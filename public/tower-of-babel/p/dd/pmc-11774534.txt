Attention to Event Segmentation Improves Memory in Young Adults: A Lifespan Study
Authors: Maverick E. Smith, Christopher S. Hall, Rachel Membreno, Daniel Quintero, Jeffrey M. Zacks
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
People spontaneously segment an observed everyday activity into discrete, meaningful events, but segmentation can be modified by task goals. Asking young adults to attend to event segmentation while watching movies of everyday actions improved their memory up to 1 month later (Flores et al., 2017). Does attending to event segmentation improve memory across the lifespan? Participants between the ages of 20 and 79 watched movies of actors performing everyday activities while intentionally encoding them for a recall and a recognition memory test 1 week (Experiment 1) or 1 month (Experiment 2) later. In addition to intentionally encoding the movies, half of the participants segmented the movies into fine-grained events. Young adults who segmented recalled more words in their recall responses than those who intentionally encoded 1 week and 1 month later. Middle-aged adults benefited from the intervention after a 1-week delay but not after a 1-month delay. Older adults over the age of 70 did not benefit from attending to segmentation. Of those who segmented, young and older adults showed similar agreement about the locations of event boundaries. Together, the results suggest that older adults are less able, compared to young adults, to maintain or retrieve well-encoded event memories after a delay. In addition, individual differences in segmentation agreement predicted memory up to 1 month later, regardless of age. These results suggest a practical and easy-to-implement intervention for improving recall of everyday events in young and middle-aged adults that is ineffective in older adults.

It is well established that aging is associated with reductions in the ability to recall details from episodic memory ( R. T.Zacks et al., 2000) and that more people in the population will be reaching the age that the changes in recall become a serious concern (Mathers et al., 2015). Considering that episodic memory is particularly sensitive to the impact of aging (Balota et al., 2000; Richmond & Burnett, 2022), there is interest in improving how people from across the lifespan use different strategies to improve how they encode new memories (e.g., Gross et al., 2012; Lustig et al., 2009; Rebok et al., 2007). Common memory encoding interventions teach people how to use mnemonic techniques such as rehearsal (Heun et al., 1997a, 1997b), visual imagery (Belleville et al., 2006; Poon et al., 1978), or the method of loci (Gross et al., 2014; Sandberg et al., 2021; Scogin et al., 1985). Older adults can use such techniques to improve memory for laboratory-friendly materials; however, benefits are likely task-specific, with minimal transfer to continuous, real-life experiences (Karbach & Verhaeghen, 2014; Rebok et al., 2007; Verhaeghen et al., 1992). Here, we tested an encoding intervention across the lifespan that was previously shown to improve memory for complex, continuous, dynamic information in young adults (Flores et al., 2017): attention to event segmentation.

Event Segmentation Theory

There is substantial evidence that people understand ongoing activity, in part, by segmenting it into meaningful events (see J. M. Zacks, 2020, for a review) and that event segmentation during encoding structures how representations are stored in long-term memory (Clewett & Davachi, 2017; Davis & Campbell, 2023; Ezzyat & Davachi, 2011; Radvansky, 2012; Smith et al., 2023). Event segmentation theory (EST) proposes that event segmentation functions as an attentional control mechanism during the formation of new event memory representations (J. M. Zacks et al., 2007). People maintain a representation in working memory of “what is happening now” in an event model, which facilitates predictions of the near future. Event models are stable and represent information that is invariant within events. If a current event model is well-fit to the actual situation, it will enable effective predictions. According to EST, prediction errors serve as a reliable cue that a current event model should be updated. When prediction error spikes, the old model is cleared from working memory, a new model is generated, and observers perceive an event boundary. Research also indicates that at such moments, information about the old event model is stored in long-term memory (Baldassano et al., 2017; Ben-Yakov & Dudai, 2011). In this way, event segmentation regulates processing resources over time and can be thought of as a form of attentional control. Specifically, it regulates the information currently active in working memory and what information is available to be consolidated in long-term memory.

Event Segmentation Structures Episodic Memory

Evidence suggests that updating event models and storing event information in long-term memory at event boundaries keeps distinctive representations formed during encoding distinct in long-term memory. Event segmentation increases the distinctiveness of successive episodes by disrupting binding across events at encoding. Consequently, temporal order memory is often affected by event boundaries (Clewett & Davachi, 2017; Clewett et al., 2020; DuBrow & Davachi, 2014, 2016; Wen & Egner, 2022), and estimates of temporal distance are longer for intervals that span event boundaries (Lositsky et al., 2016; Wang & Egner, 2022; Wen & Egner, 2022). Additionally, neural activity patterns are more similar between items within the same event than cross-event boundaries, even when people are not explicitly attending to segmentation (Baldassano et al., 2017; Ezzyat & Davachi, 2021; Sava-Segal et al., 2023).

Evidence that event segmentation increases the distinctiveness of long-term memory representations also comes from cued and free recall tests. For instance, Ezzyat and Davachi (2011) demonstrated, using a cued recall test, that sentences from a story cue retrieval of subsequent sentences better when both belong to the same event than when they cross a boundary. Last, Polyn et al. (2009) demonstrated, using a free recall test, that items within the same event cluster together. Taken together, this work suggests that episodic memories are structured around event boundaries: Episodes begin and end within the boundaries perceived at encoding.

Researchers assess how people segment movies behaviorally using the unitization/segmentation task (Newtson, 1973; J. M. Zacks, Tversky, & Iyer, 2001). Participants perform this task by explicitly dividing a movie into meaningful events by pressing a button whenever they perceive event boundaries. People tend to identify similar moments in a movie as event boundaries (Newtson, 1973; J. M. Zacks, Tversky, & Iyer, 2001), though there are stable individual differences in where people segment (Speer et al., 2003). Because event segmentation is an inherently subjective process, individual differences in segmentation ability are often characterized by assessing the degree to which the boundaries identified by an individual from the sample agree with the normative boundaries identified by the entire sample. Individual differences in segmentation agreement are functional. Observers with high agreement recall more actions from movies, describe movies with more words, and perform better on recognition memory tests than individuals with lower agreement (Flores et al., 2017; Kurby & Zacks, 2011; Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006). Presumably, effective event segmentation carves the structure of activity at its joints: It groups information from multiple timepoints with invariant structure while separating conflicting structures into different stored event models. Likewise, ineffective event segmentation leads to stored event models that jumble together features that reflect both congruent and conflicting aspects of the activity as it evolves over time, which increases memory interference.

Segmentation Agreement May Be Lower in Older Adults

Deviations from normative segmentation of activity have been observed in people with traumatic brain injury (J. M. Zacks et al., 2016; Zalla et al., 2003), posttraumatic stress disorder (Pitts, Eisenberg, et al., 2022), schizophrenia (Zalla et al., 2004), and attention-deficit disorders (Ryan & Rogers, 2021). Given that aging impacts the hippocampus and other brain structures that support event segmentation (Bailey et al., 2013; Raz et al., 1998; Reagh et al., 2020), older adults may have lower segmentation agreement than young adults. Nevertheless, studies show mixed results regarding the relation between segmentation agreement and age. J. M. Zacks et al. (2006) found that older adults segmented movies more idiosyncratically than young adults. Kurby and Zacks (2011, 2019) replicated this age-related difference and showed that older adults segment less hierarchically. In contrast, others have failed to find age-related differences in segmentation (Davis & Campbell, 2023; Magliano et al., 2012; Sargent et al., 2013). For example, Sargent et al. (2013) found that segmentation agreement and memory performance were the highest in people in their 20s and 70s and the lowest in people in their 50s. The lack of a difference between young and older adults could have been due to differences in sample characteristics. High-functioning older adults may have been more likely to participate due to biases in participant recruitment, whereas high-functioning middle-aged adults may have been less likely to participate due to family and other commitments. Alternatively, aging may leave the mechanisms involved in encoding event-level representations relatively intact (see also Radvansky, 1999; Radvansky & Dijkstra, 2007). Therefore, an open question remains as to whether event segmentation agreement is higher in young than older adults. Nevertheless, the studies that found age differences in segmentation could partially explain age-related differences in event memory with segmentation agreement.

Interventions on Event Segmentation Improve Event Memory in Young Adults

The finding that segmentation agreement predicts event memory and that group-level differences in segmentation agreement can account, in part, for age-related differences in event memory suggests that better segmentation could lead causally to better memory. Some have examined this question by altering the stimulus to increase the salience of normative event boundaries. For example, cueing event boundaries with audiovisual cues (Gold et al., 2017) or by adding commercials (Boltz, 1992) or cuts at event boundaries (Schwan et al., 2000) improves memory. Likewise, removing event boundaries impairs memory (Schwan & Garsoffky, 2004).

Another approach to testing the causal relationship between segmentation and memory is to manipulate participants’ orientation to event segmentation with instructions to segment. Initial work found better recall when people segment at a fine grain compared to a coarse grain (Hanson & Hirst, 1989; Lassiter, 1988; Lassiter et al., 1988; Lassiter & Slaw, 1991). Whether segmenting at a fine grain improves recognition memory has been a matter of debate; some studies found that segmenting at a fine grain improved both recall and recognition memory (Lassiter, 1988; Lassiter et al., 1988; Lassiter & Slaw, 1991). Others found that segmenting at a fine grain only improves recall (Hanson & Hirst, 1989).

More recently, Flores et al. (2017) manipulated whether young adults overtly segmented movies or encoded with the intention to remember them for recall and recognition memory tests administered from immediately after watching the movies up to 1 month later. In five experiments, the segmentation group outperformed the intentional encoding group on a measure of recall memory 10 min, 1 day, and 1 week after encoding. The effect was statistically significant for recognition at all delays after the immediate delay. Thus, manipulating how one imposes a self-generated structure onto a continuous activity seems to scaffold how young adults naturally encode experiences, helping them recall more.

Together, the available data indicate that event segmentation occurs spontaneously but that intervening to support it refines the mechanisms involved, resulting in better subsequent event memory. Here, we investigated whether attention to event segmentation improves event memory across the entire lifespan after a 1-week delay (Experiment 1) and after a 1-month delay (Experiment 2).

Aging, Representational Distinctiveness, and Attentional Control

It is well established that aging impairs the representational substrates that support perception and memory by making neural representations become less distinct (Carp et al., 2011; Li et al., 2001; Park et al., 2004; Pauley et al., 2023; Voss et al., 2008). Successful performance on explicit memory tests requires one to form and maintain well-defined, nonoverlapping memory representations. In functional magnetic resonance imaging, representations are commonly defined as the pattern of neural activity evoked from the stimulus and from attempts to retrieve it from memory. Several studies have compared neural patterns evoked while participants encode and retrieve laboratory-friendly materials, such as pictures (Hill et al., 2021; Kuhl et al., 2012; Xue et al., 2010) and more complex stimuli such as movies (Chen et al., 2017; Stawarczyk et al., 2020; St-Laurent et al., 2014). This work has shown that neural patterns from encoding are reinstated when information is recollected later and that people perform better on recall and recognition memory tests when neural patterns are more similar between encoding and test. Older adults show reduced representational distinctiveness during picture perception (Carp et al., 2011; Voss et al., 2008) and reduced similarity between encoding and retrieval compared to young adults (Zheng et al., 2018). Event segmentation is believed to improve recall by increasing the distinctiveness of long-term memory representations (see Radvansky & Zacks, 2017, for a review), and attention to event segmentation improves segmentation (Flores et al., 2017). Therefore, attention to event segmentation may improve memory in older adults as much or more than in young adults. Nevertheless, other studies found that reduced distinctiveness during memory retrieval is not solely due to reduced distinctiveness during perception (Hill et al., 2021; St-Laurent et al., 2014). Thus, attending to segmentation may not increase the distinctiveness of incoming events for older adults, and it may not benefit their memory as much as it does young adults.

In addition to impacting the representational substrate of memories, aging also impacts the attentional control mechanisms that regulate long-term memory encoding (McCabe et al., 2010). In fact, some have suggested that differences in attentional control may account for age- and Alzheimer disease-related differences in episodic memory (Balota et al., 1999). One possibility is that explicit event segmentation may scaffold attentional control over event encoding, leading to improvements in older adults’ subsequent memory. Some evidence, referenced earlier, supports this hypothesis. Using an extreme group design, Gold et al. (2017) found that cueing event boundaries with audiovisual cues improved recall to the same extent in young and older adults (see also Pitts et al., 2023). Providing external cues at encoding helps support how young and older adults chunk and organize incoming information at encoding. However, research also has shown that older adults have more difficulty inhibiting irrelevant information (Hasher & Zacks, 1988) and focusing attention to avoid becoming distracted (Duchek et al., 1998). They also have more difficulty maintaining current goals in working memory (Braver et al., 2001; Paxton et al., 2008). Thus, the alternative possibility is that performing the segmentation task leads to competition for cognitive resources that would be more acute in older adults, causing the intervention to be less effective in that group.

Overview of Experiments

All the participants watched movies for a later memory test. Half of them overtly segmented the movies into the smallest units that seemed natural and meaningful to them because fine-grain segmentation improves recall better than coarse-grain segmentation (Hanson & Hirst, 1989; Lassiter, 1988; Lassiter et al., 1988; Lassiter & Slaw, 1991). Participants completed memory tests after a 1-week delay in Experiment 1, and they completed memory tests after a 1-month delay in Experiment 2.

Experiment 1

Transparency and Openness

The Institutional Review Board at Washington University in St. Louis approved the studies (Improving Everyday Memory in Healthy Aging and Early Alzheimer’s Disease: Comprehension Tasks, Protocol 202003080). We preregistered the experiments before data collection; links are in the author note. We collected data for Experiment 1a in the spring of 2021. We collected data for Experiment 1b in 2021 and 2022. We collected data for Experiment 2 in the fall of 2022. Deidentified data, analyses in the programming language R (Version 4.2.2), and stimuli can be found at (Smith et al., 2024). We report how we determined the sample size, any data exclusions, all manipulations, and all the measures we recorded.

Method

Participants

We recruited 654 participants in Experiment 1a using Cloud Research (http://www.cloudresearch.com). We paid participants $6 for completing Session 1 and $8 for completing Session 2. The primary dependent measure was the count of words produced in each recall protocol divided by the number of simple actions performed by the actor (henceforth, normalized word count). To determine sample size for Experiment 1a, we ran a Monte Carlo simulation-based power analysis (Kumle et al., 2021) using the simr package (Green & MacLeod, 2016). We simulated 100 new data sets from the normalized word count data reported in Experiment 1 of Flores et al. (2017). Based on a two-tailed hypothesis test with α = .05 a and power of .80, we found we would need 86 participants per age group to detect an effect of attention to segmentation on normalized word count.

After 6 months of recruiting, we reached our goal in the group of participants in their 60s, and we were far from reaching our goal within the group of participants in their 30s and 70s with the recruiting method used in Experiment 1a. Therefore, we followed up Experiment 1a with Experiment 1b. We recruited 446 participants from the local St. Louis community and across the United States using Facebook advertisements for Experiment 1b. We paid participants $7 for completing Session 1 and $13 for completing Session 2. We determined the sample size needed to detect an effect of segmentation in Experiment 1b by running a power analysis using normalized word count from participants in their 30s in Experiment 1a. We applied the same method used in Experiment 1a to determine the sample size, except we assumed that the benefit of the segmentation task would be reduced by two thirds in older adults. Based on a two-tailed hypothesis, with the effect of the task assumed to be two thirds what it was in 30-year-olds (see Table 1), β = −0.24 × 2/3 = 0.16, α = .05, and power of .80, we found that we would need a sample of 140 within each age group. We cleaned the data, following the preregistered plan, by removing data from those who did not return for the second session or whose performance indicated a failure to understand or follow the instructions. See Supplemental Table S1 for details. We included data from 435 participants (240 intentional encoding and 195 segmentation) in Experiment 1a and 281 participants (152 intentional encoding and 129 segmentation) in Experiment 1b.

As shown in Table 2, the samples were predominantly White. Experiment 1a had an equal number of males and females. Females outnumbered males in Experiment 1b. Most young and middle-aged adults reported being employed, whereas most adults in their 60s and 70s, likely retired, were unemployed. Young adults were mostly single, while most middle-aged and older adults were married or divorced. Age groups did not appear to differ in weekly exercise, years of education, or health ratings. In addition to these demographic questions, participants in Experiment 1b also reported yearly household income. Young and older adults indicated similar wealth distributions, though more older adults indicated that they prefer not to answer the question.

Design

The study was a 2 (Task: segmentation vs. intentional encoding) × 6 (Age Group: 20s, 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, and 70s) between-subjects factorial design. We randomly assigned participants to either intentionally encode or to segment one of two different sets of three movies: (Set 1: a man setting up for a birthday party, a woman checking out a library book, and a woman making breakfast; Set 2: a man planting window boxes, a woman setting up a tent, and a woman sorting and doing laundry at a laundromat). We counter-balanced the order of the movies within each set.

Materials

As shown in Figure 1, participants watched movies of actors performing everyday activities: a man building a boat from blocks (duration = 155 s), a man decorating a dining room for a child’s birthday party (duration = 376 s), a woman checking out a book from a library (duration = 249 s), a woman making breakfast in a kitchen (duration = 329 s), a man planting two window boxes with flowers (duration = 354 s), a woman setting up a tent (duration = 378 s), and a woman sorting and washing laundry (duration = 303 s). Participants in the segmentation group watched an additional movie as part of their instructions, which depicted a man loading a dishwasher (duration = 59 s). We used the dishwashing movie to illustrate what small, meaningful units of activity could look like. A green bar appeared below the movie at moments when an independent group of participants identified event boundaries. All movies were used in prior investigations of event segmentation and memory (Eisenberg & Zacks, 2016; Kurby & Zacks, 2011, 2019; Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks et al., 2011).

Procedure

Session 1—Encoding.

We programmed the experiment using Qualtrics. Testing was conducted online using participants’ computers. Testing occurred in two sessions 1 week apart. In Session 1, participants provided the demographic information shown in Table 2. Afterward, they practiced their assigned tasks on the practice movie. Participants watched the experimental movies with the intention to remember as much as possible for memory tests administered 1 week later. Those in the segmentation group marked off the activity in the movies into the smallest units that seemed natural and meaningful to them by pressing the spacebar to identify a boundary between two small units of activity. A green bar displayed below the movie when the spacebar was pressed. We told participants that there were no right or wrong ways to identify meaningful units, but they had to press the button during the practice movie at least six times (a value unknown to participants) before they could progress to the experimental movies (J. M. Zacks et al., 2006). If the participant identified fewer than six boundaries, then we prompted the participant to identify more units until they met the requirement. Following the practice movie, participants watched one of the two different sets of movies described in Figure 1.

Session 2—Memory Tests.

Participants completed the memory tests between 5 and 7 days after they encoded the movies (Experiment 1a: MDays = 6.78 days; Experiment 1b: MDays = 7.21 days). During Session 2, participants completed the recall and then the recognition tests for each of the three movies in the order they were encoded. For the recall test, participants had 5 min to type what they remembered from each movie in as much detail as possible. We gave participants an example of what an ideal response would look like, using the practice Duplos blocks movie as the example. We scored these written responses using normalized word count, which is the number of words in the response divided by the number of fine-grained actions in the movie.

We chose to score recall as normalized word count because hand-scoring this volume of recall data was not feasible and because Flores et al. (2017) found that normalized word count correlates well with a more laborious hand-scored measure of recall memory in participants recruited from across the lifespan, r = .77 (see also Pitts, Smith, et al., 2022). We found similar correlations using data from Kurby and Zacks (2018) for both young (college students), r = 0.89 and older adults (participants between 60 and 79 years old), r = 0.70. These correlations were marginally significantly different from one another, z = 1.49, p = .07, one-tailed test, though the internal consistency of normalized word count was high for both age groups (young adults: Cronbach’s α = .94; older adults: Cronbach’s α = .93). Prior work found that older adults recall less episodic details (Levine et al., 2002), and they make more off-topic statements when recalling stories (Bluck et al., 2016). Either or both possibilities could account for the numerically smaller correlation coefficient between hand-scored recall and normalized word count in older adults.

Participants completed the recognition memory test for all the movies after completing the recall tests. On each trial, participants saw two still-shot pictures. One came from a movie they watched, and the other came from a foil movie that depicted the same actor in the same location performing similar actions. Participants selected the frame taken from the movie they watched. Participants rated their confidence on a 5-point scale, ranging from not sure (coded as a 0) to extremely confident (coded as a 4). Participants completed 20 trials for each movie. We randomly paired targets and foils for each movie with each participant. We scored recognition memory using two different measures. First, we scored it as a confidence-weighted average. We multiplied accuracy (coded as a +1 = correct, −1 = incorrect trials) by confidence (0–4) on each trial, and then we averaged the confidence weighted scores for each movie viewing. Values could range from −4 to +4. We also scored recognition memory as the proportion of correct responses for each movie, which ranged from 0 to 1. Before conducting Experiment 1, we pilot tested 30 targets and 30 foils from each movie using Cloud Research, and we retained items that were identified correctly on average by 75% of participants.

Calculation of Segmentation Agreement

We calculated segmentation agreement for each movie viewing to assess segmentation ability (Kurby & Zacks, 2011; Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks, Tversky, & Iyer, 2001). We divided each movie into 1-s bins and coded each bin for each participant as a 1 if the participant segmented during the 1-s bin and a 0 if they did not identify an event boundary in the bin. We calculated the mean of each bin (i.e., the proportion of participants who identified an event boundary within each 1-s bin) using data from the entire sample to provide an estimate of the normative event boundaries in each viewing. We then computed a point-biserial correlation between each participant’s segmentation time series and that of the sample. We scaled this correlation using the minimum and maximum possible correlation values for each viewing, based on the number of event boundaries indicated (Kurby & Zacks, 2011). Values could range from 0 to 1.

Analyses

We conducted all analyses using R. We calculated Cronbach’s α for each dependent variable by treating each movie viewing as an item in the analysis of reliability using the α function (Revelle & Revelle, 2015).

We estimated linear mixed models (LMMs) using the lmer function (Bates, 2010). We determined the random effect structure for each model we retained by comparing models with the same fixed effects but different random effects. We started by fitting the “maximal model” (Barr et al., 2013). For all the LMMs, this model included the participant and movie intercepts as random effects in addition to the age of the participants (treated continuously and centered at its mean), the encoding task, and their interaction as random slope effects with the movie. We removed a single random effect at a time, starting with the interaction term as a random slope effect, and compared the reduced model to the more complex model with a likelihood ratio test. We retained the more parsimonious model when it did not differ significantly from the more complex model (Bates et al., 2018; Matuschek et al., 2017). We repeated these steps by reducing the model further until it only contained the random intercepts of participant and movie.

Some of the p values for the fixed effects of interest were marginally significant; therefore, we deviated from the preregistered analysis plan by also reporting Bayes factors for each fixed effect. We calculated Bayes factors by comparing an alternative model, which included the fixed effect of interest to a null model that did not include the fixed effect of interest using the lmBF function (Morey et al., 2015). We used the default priors specified by lmBF. Bayes factors close to 1 represent inconclusive evidence. Bayes factors less than 1 support the null hypothesis, and Bayes factors greater than 1 support the alternative. By convention, Bayes factors between ⅓ and three represent weak evidence in favor of the null or alternative hypothesis, respectively. Values less than ⅓ or greater than three provide moderate evidence, and values greater than 10 or less than 1/10 represent strong evidence in favor of the null or alternative (Rouder et al., 2009).

Results

Recall Memory

Normalized word count was a reliable measure of recall memory in both sets of movies (Set 1: Cronbach’s α = .86; Set 2: α = .88). We used the fixed effects of the encoding task, the participants’ age (treated continuously and centered at the grand mean), and their interaction to test whether attention to event segmentation improved memory across the lifespan. The random effects selection process led to the retention of the participant and movie intercepts as random effects in Experiment 1a and to the retention of a random slope of age on movie in Experiment 1b. In Wilkinson notation, these models are described as follows: Normalized Word Count Age + Task + Age:Task + (1|Participant) + (1|Movie) and Normalized Word Count Age + Task + Age:Task + (1|Participant) + (Age|Movie) for Experiment 1a and 1b, respectively.

As shown in Figures 2A and 2B, age did not predict normalized word count in Experiment 1a, F(1, 430.01) = 2.42, p = .12, BF10 = 0.43, but it did in Experiment 1b, F(1, 20.55) = 8.75, p = .008, BF10 = 1.01, though the Bayes factor suggested there was inconclusive of a main effect of age in Experiment 1b. Replicating Flores et al. (2017), those in the segmentation group recalled significantly more than those in the intentional encoding group, Experiment 1a, F(1, 430.03) = 55.59, p < .001, BF10 > 1,000; Experiment 1b, F(1, 276.02) = 5.03, p = .03, BF10 = 2.08; though the Bayes factor associated with the effect was notably larger in Experiment 1a, which provided very strong evidence in favor of the main effect of task. The benefit of attending to event segmentation was not significantly different across age groups, as evident from the lack of an interaction between age and task on normalized word count in Experiment 1a, F(1, 430.02) = 1.54, p = .22, BF10 = 0.42, and Experiment 1b, F(1, 276.30) = 2.29, p = .13, BF10 = 1.06. This suggests that attention to event segmentation improved recall at all ages. Yet, the Bayes factors in support of the interaction effect were inconclusive, and attention to event segmentation did not appear to provide much benefit for middle-aged adults in their 40s (Figure 2A) or older adults in their 70s (Figures 2A and 2B).

For this reason and because we collected approximately an equal number of participants within each decade, we followed the planned analyses by analyzing the effect of task within each decade. We treated the participant and movie intercepts as random effects in each model. We adjusted for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni correction (Experiment 1a: α = .05/6 = .008; Experiment 1b: α = .05/2 = .025). As shown in Table 1, we found strong evidence that those in their 20s, 30s, 50s, and 60s had higher normalized word count when they attended to event segmentation, weak evidence that attention to segmentation did not improve memory for those in their 70s (BF10 = 0.37 in Experiment 1a; BF10 = 0.39 in Experiment 1b), and inconclusive evidence regarding those in their 40s (BF10 = 0.86 in Experiment 1a). We also reran the analysis after combining data from Experiments 1a and 1b in an exploratory analysis. The LMMs only included data from those in their 30s and 70s because those groups were shared across experiments. We found moderate evidence in favor of an interaction between age (treated continuously) and task, F(1, 376.36) = 8.13, p = .005, BF10 = 8.46, in the combined analysis. Attention to event segmentation improved memory for participants in their 30s, t(378) = 4.96, p < .0001, BF10 = 262.55, but not those in their 70s, t(377) = 0.62, p = .54, BF10 = 0.24. See Supplemental Materials for details.

Taken together, the finding that attention to event segmentation improved recall memory in young adults replicates Flores et al. (2017). We extend this prior work by showing that attention to segmentation improves recall in middle-aged adults after a 1-week delay. This finding is important because it suggests a novel intervention for improving memory in young and middle-aged adults. However, these results also showed that this benefit was not preserved in individuals over the age of 70. As shown in the following section, we also did not detect an age-related difference on segmentation agreement, suggesting that young and older adults segmented as well, despite not benefiting from the memory intervention. This is consistent with the proposal that aging affects the systems that support the recall of explicit memories.

Recognition Memory

Prior to running the analyses on recognition memory performance, we removed recognition memory data from the movie of the woman folding and washing laundry at a laundromat because we discovered evidence of prevalent strategic responding that compromised this movie as a tool to measure memory. We found that performance on the movie was at ceiling (Experiment 1a: M = 0.90, SD = 0.23; Experiment 1b: M = 0.88, SD = 0.24). We then found that there was a single noticeable feature that differed between most target and foil items taken from the movie. Some participants noted that feature and used it to identify the target on each trial, resulting in nondiagnostic ceiling performance (130 participants answered 100% of the trials correctly in Experiment 1). Likewise, a few participants noticed the feature but misremembered the value for that feature, leading to nondiagnostic floor performance (12 participants had less than 50% accuracy). Following the preregistration, we removed trials with reaction times under 500 ms or over 2.5 SDs from the participants’ mean reaction time (Experiment 1a: 2.8%; Experiment 1b: 1.4% of all trials).

We analyzed recognition memory as a confidence-weighted score, as mentioned previously. Confidence-weighted scores were reliable (Set 1: Cronbach’s α = .81; Set 2: α = .74). The models contained the same fixed and random effects as the models of normalized word count. In Wilkinson’s notation, they are written as follows: Confidence Weighted Score Age + Task + Age:Task + (1|Participant) + (1|Movie) and Confidence Weighted Score Age + Task + Age:Task + (1|Participant) + (Age|Movie) for Experiment 1a and 1b, respectively.

For confidence-weighted recognition, shown in Figures 2D and 2E, we did not find evidence that those in the segmentation group outperformed those in the intentional encoding group in Experiment 1a, F(1, 417.11) = 3.86, p = .05, BF10 = 0.74 or Experiment 1b, F(1, 273.75) = 2.67, p = .10, BF10 = 0.41. The Bayes factors provided inconclusive and weak evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, respectively. Further, in neither sample was there an effect of age, Experiment 1a: F(1, 416.89) = 1.63, p = .20, BF10 = 0.19; Experiment 1b: F(1, 6.68) = 3.00, p = .13, BF10 = 0.98, or an interaction between task and age, Experiment 1a: F(1, 416.89) = 3.37, p = .07, BF10 = 0.85; Experiment 1b: F(1, 274.56) = 0.48, p = .49, BF10 = 0.41. As with recall, we also examined whether attention to segmentation improved recognition memory accuracy within each decade of life (see Supplemental Table S2). We found moderate evidence that attention to segmentation did not improve recognition memory performance in any age group. We also analyzed recognition memory performance as a proportion correct score. Results were consistent with the analysis performed on the confidence-weighted score. See Supplemental Figure S1.

Segmentation Agreement

Segmentation agreement was a reliable measure of segmentation ability (Set 1: Cronbach’s α = .70; Set 2: Cronbach’s α = .86). As noted previously, some have found that older adults segment more idiosyncratically than young adults (Kurby & Zacks, 2011, 2019; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006), though this is not always observed (Sargent et al., 2013). To examine how segmentation agreement differed between age groups, we used the fixed effect of age (treated continuously) and the random intercepts of participant and movie to predict segmentation agreement. In Wilkinson’s notation, both models are written as follows: Segmentation agreement Age + (1|Participant) + (1|Movie). As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, though middle-aged and older adults had numerically lower agreement than young adults in their 20s and 30s, we did not detect a significant main effect of age in either sample, Experiment 1a: F(1, 193) = 0.46, p = .50, BF10 = 0.25; Experiment 1b: F(1, 151.31) = 2.11, p = .15, BF10 = 0.57. The Bayes factors from the two models provided moderate and weak evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, respectively. Thus, older adults seemed to have segmented as well as young adults, even though older adults did not benefit from the intervention.

Segmentation Agreement Predicting Recall Memory

Prior work found that segmentation agreement predicts event recall and recognition memory (Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006), even up to 1 month later (Flores et al., 2017). Here, we used segmentation agreement scores from those who performed the segmentation task to assess if we replicated this effect and to extend it to individuals across the lifespan. We used segmentation agreement from those in the segmentation condition, age, and their interaction to predict normalized word count, as shown in Figure 4. We treated the participant and movie intercepts as random effects in both models: Normalized Word Count Age + Segmentation Agreement + Age: Segmentation Agreement + (1|Participant) + (1|Movie). Consistent with prior work, we found moderate evidence that segmentation agreement predicted normalized word count in Experiment 1a, F(1, 575.26) = 5.90, p = .02, BF10 = 3.06, and strong evidence for it in Experiment 1b, F(1, 352.95) = 12.39, p = .0005, BF10 = 52.72. We did not detect a main effect of age in Experiment 1a, F(1, 191.12) = 3.00, p = .08, BF10 = 1.07, but we did in Experiment 1b, F(1, 122.61) = 12.00, p = .0007, BF10 = 44.36, likely due to the slightly negative slope for normalized word count and segmentation agreement in the segmentation condition, shown in Figures 2B and 3B. Interestingly, the relationship between segmentation agreement and memory held for older and young adults. This was supported by a nonsignificant interaction between segmentation agreement and age in Experiment 1a, F(1, 574.48) = 0.0002, p = .98, BF10 = 0.21, and Experiment 1b, F(1, 352.47) = 1.63, p = .20, BF10 = 0.39, which provided moderate and weak evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, respectively. Those who identify event boundaries more normatively chunk events more adaptively. As a result, normative event segmentation helps create distinct memory representations that are more durable and more likely to be retrieved later.

Segmentation Agreement Predicting Recognition Memory

The model of confidence-weighted recognition memory included the same fixed and random effects as the analysis of segmentation agreement predicting normalized word count, except that the model also included the random slope of age on movie. As shown in Figure 5 for confidence-weighted recognition memory, we found strong evidence that segmentation agreement predicted recognition memory in Experiment 1a, F(1, 462.91) = 5.01, p = .03, BF10 = 6.36. We did not find evidence that segmentation predicted recognition memory in Experiment 1b, F(1, 268.08) = 1.95, p = .16, BF10 = 0.66. In neither experiment did we detect a main effect of age, Experiment 1a: F(1, 7.12) = 1.57, p = .25, BF10 = 1.02; Experiment 1b: F(1, 114.11) = 2.82, p = .09, BF10 = 0.89, or a significant interaction between segmentation agreement and age, Experiment 1a: F(1, 456.82) = 4.01, p = .05, BF10 = 2.36; Experiment 1b: F(1, 274.84) = 4.19, p = .05, BF10 = 1.78. We reran the analysis using proportion correct as the dependent variable. Results were consistent with our findings for confidence-weighted recognition memory. In Experiment 1a, those with higher segmentation agreement performed better on the recognition memory test than those with lower agreement, F(1, 420.64) = 5.36, p = .02, BF10 = 2.78. This relationship was not significant in Experiment 1b, F(1, 233.79) = 0.55, p = .45, BF10 = 0.25. Given these mixed results, we combined data from the two experiments and reran the analysis, including data from participants in their 30s and 70s. We found no main effect of segmentation agreement for confidence-weighted recognition memory, F(1, 368.40) = 1.12, p = .29, BF10 = 0.58, or the proportion correct scores, F(1, 333.37) = 0.59, p = .44, BF10 = 0.22. See Supplemental Materials for details. Overall, these results, along with the analysis of normalized word count, suggest that normative segmentation improves recall but not recognition memory performance.

Discussion

Young adults and late middle-aged adults in their 50s and 60s had a higher normalized word count when they overtly segmented. This provides the first evidence that explicitly segmenting everyday activity into meaningful units can cause memory improvements in middle-aged individuals 1 week after encoding. Event model construction and updating are presumed to be ongoing components of everyday event understanding; however, attention to segmentation refines these mechanisms, leading to better subsequent memory in young and middle-aged adults. In contrast, individuals in their 70s did not recall more when they performed the segmentation than the intentional encoding task. This lack of an effect was surprising, given that Gold et al. (2017) found that cueing event boundaries improves memory in older adults. One possibility is that older adults can take advantage of more normative event segmentation when it is controlled by the environment, but they may either lack the self-initiated resources to benefit when orienting to how they segment (i.e., reduced attentional control) or they may lack the resources to maintain distinctive, accessible representations in long-term memory after the delay (i.e., reduced representational distinctiveness).

Another possibility is that older adults were less aroused or engaged while segmenting the movies. This possibility cannot be ruled out; however, this alternative explanation also predicts that older adults would have lower segmentation agreement than young adults. We found no such age-related differences in segmentation agreement. The lack of an age effect on agreement could also be due to differences in the participant sampling procedure, compared to prior studies that found age-related differences in agreement (Kurby & Zacks, 2011, 2019). For example, this sample was not racially diverse, and all were educated and had access to a personal computer. Alternatively, it may suggest that older adults have sufficient resources to segment continuous activities in a normative fashion (see also Kurby & Zacks, 2018; Radvansky, 1999; Radvansky & Dijkstra, 2007). Future research should examine these alternative possibilities.

Consistent with prior work, we found that participants with higher segmentation agreement had a higher normalized word count than those with lower agreement. According to EST, event models reset at event boundaries when prediction errors spike (J. M. J. M. Zacks et al., 2007). The timing of event model updating determines which representations are active in working memory and which are available to be consolidated into long-term memory (Baldassano et al., 2017; Ben-Yakov & Dudai, 2011). When people segment more normatively, features of the activity are chunked together more adaptively, resulting in more durable memory representations that are less prone to interference (see Radvansky & Zacks, 2017, for a review).

In contrast to what we found for recall, we found no evidence that orienting attention to segmentation with the overt task improved recognition memory, and there was very weak evidence that segmentation agreement predicted recognition memory performance. One possibility is that recognition tests are less sensitive than recall tests to the event models constructed during movie watching (Brewer & Dupree, 1983). We elaborate further on this possibility in the General Discussion section.

Experiment 2

The effect of an intervention on memory can be quite different depending on the delay at which memory is measured. Such delay-dependent effects can be important for theory-building and for practical applications. Nevertheless, most studies in the event memory literature have examined the relationship between segmentation and memory only a few minutes after people encode movies (Gold et al., 2017; Hanson & Hirst, 1989; Lassiter, 1988; Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006, 2011). The notable exception is Flores et al. (2017), who found that attention to segmentation improved recognition memory in young adults 1 month after encoding. The goal of Experiment 2 was to determine whether orienting attention to segmentation improved event memory after a 1-month-long delay across the lifespan. In addition, we also examined whether the relationship between individual differences in event segmentation agreement and recall extended to this longer delay.

Experiment 2 was like Experiment 1, with three substantive changes: First, the delay was increased to 1 month. Second, we recruited from a different online participant pool because we had difficulties recruiting a sufficient sample size in Experiment 1. Third, we replaced one of the movies for which recognition memory performance had been subject to strategic responding in Experiment 1.

Method

Participants

We recruited 900 participants from Prolific (https://www.prolific.co) in Experiment 2. We cleaned the data the same way we cleaned data from Experiment 1. See Supplemental Table S4 for details. The final data set included data from 623 participants (n = 345 intentional encoding and n = 278 segmentation). As shown in Table 3, after data exclusions, the sample demographics were like those in Experiment 1a. The sample was predominately White, and it included approximately the same number of males and females.

We paid participants $7 for completing the encoding session and $13 for completing the memory tests. We determined the sample size for each age group by bootstrapping a power analysis using data from Experiment 1a and custom R code. We randomly sampled normalized word count data from each age group with replacement from Experiment 1a to create 1,000 new data sets. We then used an LMM to predict normalized word count from the task condition and age. We estimated a Bayes factor from each model. We calculated the proportion of Bayes factors greater than three that provided evidence for a main effect of task and the proportion of Bayes factors less than 1/3 for the interaction between task and age. The power analysis indicated that a sample of 90 participants within each age group would be sufficient to achieve 80% power. We recruited more participants than suggested by the power analysis because we anticipated more participants would drop out between sessions.

Design, Materials, and Procedure

The design, materials, procedure, instructions, and memory tests were identical to what we used in Experiment 1, with the following exceptions: Participants completed the memory tests approximately 28 days after watching the movies. Participants could complete the experiment within a 4-day window (Mdays = 29.23 days). In Experiment 1, we found that recognition memory accuracy was very high and variable for the movie of the woman sorting and washing laundry; therefore, we replaced it with a movie of a woman folding clothes on a bed (duration = 344 s) in Experiment 2. A screenshot is shown in Figure 1H. Finally, we programmed the experiment using the Gorilla Experiment Builder (https://gorilla.sc/), and we recruited on Prolific.

Results

Recall Memory

Normalized word count was a consistent measure of recall memory (Set 1: Cronbach’s α = .87; Set 2: α = .87). The analysis of normalized word count included the fixed effects of age (treated continuously and centered at its mean), task, and their interaction as fixed effects. The model also included random intercepts of participant and movie. In Wilkinson notation, this model is described as follows: Normalized Word Count Age + Task + Age:Task + (1|Participant) + (1|Movie). As shown in Figure 2C for recall, we found a main effect of task, F(1, 619.10) = 7.68, p = .005, BF = 5.09. Unlike in Experiment 1, we also found a weak, but significant, main effect of age, F(1, 620.32) = 4.52, p = .03, BF10 = 0.58, and strong evidence in favor of an interaction between task and age, F(1, 620.43) = 13.42, p < .001, BF10 = 100.71.

We probed the significant interaction between age and task by comparing normalized word count between those who segmented and those in the intentional encoding group within each decade and adjusted for multiple comparisons with a Bonferroni correction. As shown in Table 4, we found strong evidence that attention to event segmentation improved memory 1 month after encoding in those in their 20s and weak evidence that the intervention improved memory in those in their 30s and 40s. Last, we found weak-to-moderate evidence to suggest that the manipulation did not improve memory in people over the age of 50. We also combined the data from the two experiments and evaluated whether attention to event segmentation differentially improved memory within each age group at each delay. See Supplemental Materials for details. To summarize those effects (Supplemental Table S5), we found that participants in their 20s and 30s benefited the same amount at each delay from the intervention on normalized word count. Those in their 50s and 60s benefited after a 1-week delay but not after a 1-month delay. Participants in their 40s and 70s did not benefit significantly at either delay.

Recognition Memory

We modeled recognition memory as a confidence-weighted score and as a proportion correct score. As in Experiment 1, confidence-weighted recognition accuracy was a reliable measure of recognition memory (Set 1: Cronbach’s α = .81; Set 2: α = .81). The model of confidence-weighted recognition accuracy included the main effects of age, task, and their interaction. The model included random intercepts of participant and movie. As shown in Figure 2F for confidence-weighted recognition memory, we found very strong evidence in favor of an interaction between age and task, F(1, 610.20) = 16.06, p < .001, BF10 = 255.23, but we did not find a main effect of age, F(1, 610.10) = 2.07, p = .15, BF10 = 0.09, or task, F(1, 610.04) = 0.60, p = .44, BF10 = 0.15.

We probed the interaction between age and task by examining the main effect of task within each age group. As shown in Table 5, we found weak evidence that attention to segmentation improved recognition memory after a 1-month delay in participants in their 20s and 30s. Contrary to our hypothesis, we also found strong evidence that those in the intentional encoding group in their 60s had better recognition memory than those in the segmentation group. An explanation for why participants in the intentional encoding group outperformed those in the segmentation group is unknown, and this effect warrants replication. We found analogous results when we analyzed recognition memory as a proportion correct. See the Supplemental Materials and Supplemental Figure S1 for details.

Segmentation Agreement

Segmentation agreement was a reliable measure of segmentation ability (Set 1: Cronbach’s α = .80; Set 2: α = .81). As in Experiment 1, we modeled it as a function of age for those in the segmentation condition. The model included the random intercepts of participant and movie. As in Experiment 1 and as shown in Figure 3C, we failed to find an age-related difference in segmentation agreement, F(1, 329.40) = 0.63, p = .43, BF10 = 0.20. The Bayes factor provided strong evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.

Segmentation Agreement Predicting Recall Memory

We also evaluated how segmentation agreement predicted recall across the entire lifespan. We used segmentation agreement from those in the segmentation condition, age, and their interactions to predict normalized word count. We treated the participant and movie intercepts as random effects. As shown in Figure 4, we found very strong evidence that those with higher segmentation agreement had a higher normalized word count than those with lower agreement, F(1, 695.93) = 23.26, p < .001, BF10 > 1,000. Importantly, this provides additional evidence that how an individual segments predicts how much they recall later, even after a 1-month delay (Flores et al., 2017). Further, we found that older adults in the event segmentation group recalled less than young adults, F(1, 252.16) = 17.48, p < .001, BF10 = 528.41. We also found that the effect of agreement on recall was slightly reduced in the older adults, as evident by a significant interaction between agreement and age, F(1, 702.42) = 4.69, p = .03, BF10 = 2.18, but the Bayes factor showed only weak evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis. Taken together, for the first time, we show that segmentation agreement predicts how much people recall across the lifespan after a 1-month-long delay.

Segmentation Agreement Predicting Recognition Memory

As shown in Figure 5 for confidence-weighted recognition accuracy, we found a significant main effect of segmentation agreement, F(1, 714.15) = 7.62, p = .006, BF10 = 6.93, age, F(1, 270.95) = 15.40, p < .001, BF10 = 196.00, and a marginally significant interaction between agreement and age, though the Bayes factor in favor of the interaction was inconclusive, F(1, 687.02) = 3.90, p = .04, BF10 = 1.10. We found consistent results when we analyzed recognition memory as a proportion correct without taking confidence into account. See the Supplemental Materials and Supplemental Figure S2 for details.

Discussion

The results of Experiment 2 were mostly consistent with Experiment 1. Young adults in the segmentation group had a higher word count 1 month after encoding movies than those in the intentional encoding group. Importantly, this result provides stronger evidence that attention to segmentation improves memory than Flores et al. (2017), who only found a mere trend in recall. In the present study, the effect on recall at a 1-month delay was statistically robust, and the Bayes factors suggested that there was moderate-to-strong evidence in favor of the effect in young adults. Event segmentation has been shown to be an ongoing automatic process (Baldassano et al., 2017; Kurby & Zacks, 2018; Sava-Segal et al., 2023; J. M. Zacks, Braver, et al., 2001), but attending to how one imposes a structure onto an activity may refine the mechanisms involved, improving event memory. Our results support this conclusion in young adults.

We found moderate-to-strong evidence that attention to segmentation does not improve memory in middle-aged adults or older adults in their 70s after a 1-month delay. We speculate further on why the intervention improved memory in middle-aged adults after a 1-week delay (Experiment 1) but not after a 1-month delay in the General Discussion section. The finding that orienting attention to segmentation did not improve memory in older adults over the age of 70 may result from age-related differences in either attentional control or in the systems that support the ability to recall information from long-term memory. We elaborate more on these possibilities in the General Discussion section.

We found weak evidence that the experimental manipulation improved recognition memory for young adults in Experiment 2. While it is consistent with work showing that instructions to attend to different grains of segmentation do not improve recognition memory (Hanson & Hirst, 1989), it is inconsistent with Flores et al. (2017), who found reliable evidence that segmenting improved recognition memory after a 1-month delay. While the discrepancy between the findings of the present study and Flores et al. (2017) is unknown, it is important to note that recognition and recall rely on different mechanisms and that each may be differentially sensitive to the structures formed at encoding. For instance, when performing a recognition memory test, one can use external cues and familiarity to retrieve the pertinent information, but no such cues are available when performing a free recall task (Jennings & Jacoby, 1993; Mandler, 1980; Yonelinas, 2002). Accurate recollection of prior events likely requires reinstatement of the relevant event models that were created during encoding. Thus, recall may be more sensitive to encoding interventions on event structure.

Last, we found that segmentation agreement did not differ between age groups and that it reliably predicted both recall and recognition memory performance after a 1-month delay. The finding that segmentation agreement predicted event memory after a 1-month delay replicates Flores et al. (2017). We extend their prior work by showing that the effect persists across the lifespan.

General Discussion

Neuroimaging and behavioral data suggest that people understand and remember continuous experience by segmenting it into discrete, meaningful events (Baldassano et al., 2017; Clewett & Davachi, 2017; Ezzyat & Davachi, 2021; Smith et al., 2023; J. M. Zacks, Braver, et al., 2001). The mechanisms that segment events have pervasive effects on the ability to form new memories. Those who have higher segmentation agreement have better memory than those who have lower agreement (Flores et al., 2017; Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006). Event segmentation occurs spontaneously, but asking young adults to attend to segmentation improves memory up to 1 month later (Flores et al., 2017). We investigated whether people from across the lifespan could benefit from attention to event segmentation. Participants between the ages of 20 and 79 years watched movies of actors performing everyday activities with instructions to remember the movies for memory tests 1 week (Experiment 1) or 1 month (Experiment 2) later. In addition to trying to remember the details from the movies, half of the participants also segmented the movies into the smallest events that seemed natural and meaningful to them.

Attention to Segmentation Improves Memory in Young Adults, but Not in Older Adults

Performing the event segmentation task improved recall in young adults (20–39-year-olds). This finding provides additional support that imposing a self-generated structure onto a continuous activity improves how information is encoded and later recalled in young adults. At least three mechanisms can account for how performing the segmentation task improves recall. First, the instructions to attend to segmentation may encourage viewers to devote more resources to monitoring prediction errors, which leads to more normative event segmentation and, consequently, long-term event representations that are more distinct and less susceptible to interference. A second, though not mutually exclusive, explanation is that instructions to segment encourage observers to attend more to the goals of the actors, which decreases distraction from extraneous details that are not important for completing the activity depicted in the movies. A third possibility is that forcing observers to structure information in their own way improves recall by encouraging encoding at a deeper level than what participants did in the intentional encoding condition (Mannes & Kintsch, 1987). Providing support for the link between segmentation and memory is important because it provides an avenue for improving memory in young adults that could positively impact students’ learning new skills and information.

By recruiting large lifespan samples, we were able to test whether orienting instructions to attend to event segmentation improves memory across the lifespan. We found that late middle-aged participants in the segmentation group outperformed those in the intentional encoding group on tests of recall memory after a 1-week delay but not after a 1-month delay. One possibility is that memory for the details of an experience decays at a faster rate in late middle-aged and older adults than in young adults (Wheeler, 2000). If so, then attention to event segmentation could support more adaptive encoding in this group, leading to better recall at the shorter delay, but this advantage would fade by the time of the 1-month test. Another possibility is that differences in the activities performed outside of the experiment may have differentially impacted middle-aged adults more than young adults. A higher proportion of the middle-aged adults in Experiment 2 were employed. They likely have active careers and family lives; therefore, there may have been greater interference in the middle-aged adults. A third possibility is of less theoretical interest but cannot be disregarded: The samples in Experiments 1 and 2 were recruited from different sources at different moments in time, and therefore, it is possible that they differed in some unknown characteristics. The two groups were quite similar on all the demographic measures we collected, but it is possible that some unknown difference in their characteristics could account for why the intervention was effective for middle-aged adults in Experiment 1 but not Experiment 2.

Second, we found moderate-to-strong evidence that drawing attention to event segmentation did not improve recall and recognition memory in 70-year-olds. Older adults may not be able to leverage the benefits of event segmentation to improve recall because the systems that support explicit memory contain less distinct representations (Carp et al., 2011; Hill et al., 2021; St-Laurent et al., 2014). As such, improving how older adults encode new events may not benefit their long-term retention. However, this explanation is inconsistent with at least one prior study that used a different intervention to reinforce normative event segmentation in older adults and found that intervention improved recall after a short delay (Gold et al., 2017). Gold et al. (2017) presented participants with movies that were edited to reinforce event boundaries or event middles, as well as unedited movies, and tested memory approximately 10 min later. Both age groups remembered more from the movies that were edited to highlight event boundaries. Taken together with the present study, one possibility is that older adults are able to benefit from environmental support for event structure, such as that provided by the Gold paradigm, but are unable to take advantage of attentional orienting to event structure, such as in the current paradigm.

Consistent with this possibility, older adults may not benefit from the event segmentation task manipulation because the event segmentation task increased cognitive load in a manner that was more challenging for older adults in their 70s. Participants assigned to the segmentation group were asked to remember what was happening in the movie while also remembering to press the button when a new event begins. Given that we also did not find evidence for an age-related difference in segmentation agreement, the dual-task nature of the segmentation instructions may have led to greater cognitive costs in older adults (Riby et al., 2004), who struggle more with maintaining goals in dual-task situations than young adults (Paxton et al., 2008).

A second possibility is that older adults were less aroused or less engaged while watching the movies than young adults. This possibility cannot be ruled out; however, this explanation is unlikely because we found moderate-to-strong evidence that segmentation agreement—an indicator of how well participants performed the segmentation task—did not differ between age groups and that agreement predicted normalized word count 1 week later. The analysis of segmentation agreement could suggest that young and older adults in the segmentation group encoded the movies equally as well. Older adults, as a group, may be less able to retain the information from the movies over the delay.

A third possibility that cannot be ruled out is that older adults benefited from the intervention, but the method of testing memory used here was not sensitive enough to detect potential differences between the encoding tasks in older adults. Future research could examine potential differences using an implicit memory test or additional tests of explicit memory (i.e., cued recall). In addition, we scored recall as normalized word count. Even though normalized word count correlates well with more laborious hand-scored measures of recall across the lifespan (Flores et al., 2017; Pitts, Smith, et al., 2022), it does not provide a measure of what information participants recalled. After all, prior work found that older adults recall less episodic details about past events (Levine et al., 2002) and produce more off-topic sentences (Bluck et al., 2016). Such additional statements could have added variability to the measure used here.

Segmentation Agreement Did Not Differ Between Age Groups

We did not observe age-related differences on event segmentation agreement. Prior research has sometimes found poorer segmentation agreement in older adults but sometimes not (Kurby & Zacks, 2018; Magliano et al., 2012; Reagh et al., 2020; Sargent et al., 2013; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006, 2011). For example, early work found older adults had lower segmentation agreement than young adults (Kurby & Zacks, 2011; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006), and those with mild cognitive impairment had even lower agreement than cognitively healthy older adults (J. M. Zacks et al., 2006). Likewise, functional magnetic resonance imaging data indicated that older adults show less evidence of effective event model updating at event boundaries than middle-aged and young adults (Reagh et al., 2020). Other studies have failed to find age differences for both segmentation agreement and brain activity (Kurby & Zacks, 2018; Sargent et al., 2013). A distinctive feature of the present study is that we calculated Bayes factors to assess the strength of evidence in favor of the null hypothesis of no age differences. We found moderate-to-strong evidence that young and older adults do not segment differently.

An important caution in interpreting any cross-sectional studies of age differences is selection effects. It is possible that the young and older adults differ in important features of life experience or other factors that we were not able to assay here. In addition, in interpreting both age differences and the absence of age differences, it is important to consider limits on generalization to other participants. Despite efforts to recruit a more representative sample of North American adults, our participants were not racially diverse; they were all educated, and they were all active on online platforms for recruiting participants. Future research should carefully examine differences in participant characteristics that may account for possible age-related differences in segmentation ability.

Segmentation Agreement Predicts Event Memory up to 1 Month Later

Replicating prior work (Flores et al., 2017), we found that those who had higher segmentation agreement—those whose segmentation aligned with the group norms—had a higher normalized word count 1 month after encoding. These results extend prior work by showing that the segmentation-memory relationship holds across the lifespan. Presumably, those who segment more normatively have more distinct long-term memory representations that are less susceptible to interference.

In these experiments, we observed larger effects on recall memory than on recognition. Previous studies found that attention to segmentation and segmentation agreement are reliably related to recognition memory (Flores et al., 2017; J. M. Zacks et al., 2006; see, however, Hanson & Hirst, 1989; Kurby & Zacks, 2011). We found moderate-to-strong evidence that attention to segmentation did not improve recognition memory and mixed evidence that segmentation agreement predicted individual differences in recognition memory performance. As mentioned previously, recall and recognition may be differentially sensitive to the event models constructed during the perceptual experience of ongoing activities. To perform a recall task, one must reconstruct the event models created when the information was initially encoded, whereas recognition tests can be performed using familiarity-based mechanisms (Jennings & Jacoby, 1993; Mandler, 1980; Yonelinas, 2002).

Conclusion

Memory interventions that target how information is encoded have a long history in cognitive psychology. Manipulations that encourage people to use visual imagery, survival processing, or mnemonics such as the method of loci are effective. However, such benefits are likely task- and stimulus-specific. They may help people recall lists of pictures or words, but they may not generalize to helping people encode and remember complex experiences. Prior work found that orienting participants’ attention to event segmentation improved memory in young adults and that cueing event boundaries in movies improved memory in both young and older adults (Flores et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2017). We asked whether modifying how older adults attend to event segmentation improves memory at ecologically relevant delays from 1 week to 1 month later. Young and middle-aged adults, but not older adults, who attended to event segmentation had a higher word count than those who intentionally encoded movies for a later memory test. This finding is important for theories of age differences in memory, and it is a result that would be difficult to observe without the use of complex, naturalistic stimulus materials. This result is also important for behavioral strategies to remediate age-related memory complaints. It suggests that although at least one intervention on event structure encoding has been shown to be effective in older adults at a short delay on recall tests (Gold et al., 2017), caution must be taken when deploying encoding interventions that may place demands on attentional control.
