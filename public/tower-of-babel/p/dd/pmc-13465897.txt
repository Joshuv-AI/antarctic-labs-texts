Anthropometric indices and elevated blood pressure: A record-based study among employees of a tertiary health care institute in Telangana
Authors: N Kusneniwar Govindrao, Attota Ratna Sowryaswi, Enubothula Sampath Kumar, Neeraj Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hypertension is a major global health concern and a leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease. With rising obesity rates, anthropometric indices such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) are increasingly used as non-invasive screening tools to identify individuals at risk of elevated blood pressure.Objectives:To examine the association between anthropometric indices and elevated blood pressure among employees of a tertiary health care institute in Telangana.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among employees at Tertiary Healthcare Institute, Telangana during a hypertension screening drive. Participants included healthcare and administrative staff. Data were collected on height, weight, WC, and blood pressure using WHO-recommended protocols. Hypertension and pre-hypertension were defined as per JNC VIII criteria. Statistical analysis, including logistic regression, to evaluate associations between anthropometric measures and blood pressure.Results:A significant association was observed between elevated blood pressure and increased BMI, WC, and WHtR (P < 0.05). Central adiposity, male gender, and age > 30 years were identified as significant risk factors. Pre-hypertension was found in 23% of participants, indicating a high-risk group requiring early intervention.Conclusion:Anthropometric indices are valuable tools for early detection of hypertension in occupational settings. Routine screening and workplace interventions focusing on lifestyle modifications are essential to prevent long-term cardiovascular complications.

Introduction

Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is a significant global health concern due to its role as a primary risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, including heart attacks and strokes. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that the number of adults aged 30–79 years with hypertension has increased from 650 million to 1.28 billion over the past three decades, with nearly half unaware of their condition. This silent epidemic contributes to approximately 11 million deaths annually, accounting for 19% of global fatalities.[1]

In India, the burden of hypertension is equally alarming. The National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD) has been implemented across all states and union territories to address major non-communicable diseases, including hypertension. As of July 2024, over 5.86 crore patients are under treatment for hypertension and diabetes, reflecting the extensive reach of the program.[2]

Despite these efforts, a significant portion of the population remains undiagnosed or inadequately treated, underscoring the need for effective screening tools.

Body mass index (BMI), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and waist circumference (WC) are anthropometric measures commonly used to assess obesity and related health risks.[3] Obesity is a well-established risk factor for hypertension, and these measures serve as convenient, non-invasive screening tools. BMI provides a general assessment of body fat based on weight and height, while WC and WHtR offer insights into central adiposity, which is more closely associated with cardiovascular risk.

The employees at the Tertiary Healthcare Institute Telangana represent a diverse group of healthcare professionals whose occupational stress and lifestyle factors may predispose them to hypertension. Implementing routine screening using BMI, WHtR, and WC could facilitate early detection and management of hypertension within this cohort.[4] Early identification is crucial, as hypertension often remains asymptomatic until it leads to severe complications. This study aims to evaluate the association of BMI, WHtR, and WC with hypertension among the staff of Tertiary Healthcare Institute Telangana.

Methodology

A hypertension drive was conducted under the NCD clinic in Tertiary Healthcare Institute Telangana during June 2024. Employees, including residents, nurses, administrative staff, technicians, pharmacists, and other assisting personnel, were screened for hypertension. A record-based study was conducted with employees who gave consent during January 2025. Data was obtained from the records maintained during the hypertension drive and employees with complete records were included in the study. Data collection included anthropometric measurements (height, weight, WC), blood pressure readings, and participant demographics. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, and the study adhered to ethical principles.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were conducted following a standardized protocol as suggested by World Health Organization at the hospital.[3] A trained community medicine auxiliary staff member, following an established protocol, recorded height, weight, WC, and blood pressure (BP). Measurements were taken using calibrated instruments: a portable digital weighing scale (Equinox Weighing Scale), a stadiometer (UNICEF), and a constant tension tape.[4] Sociodemographic data, including age and sex, and a history of hypertension or use of antihypertensive medication were also collected. WHtR values were categorized as follows: ‘no increased risk’ (<0.5), ‘increased risk’ (0.5–0.6), and ‘very high risk’ (≥0.6), with a cut-off value set at 0.5. For WC, thresholds were ≥90 cm for males and ≥80 cm for females. BMI classifications were based on WHO criteria: normal (18.5–22.9 kg/m²), overweight (23–24.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥25.0 kg/m²), with a BMI cut-off of 23 kg/m².

Blood pressure (BP) measurements were taken by using an aneroid sphygmomanometer with appropriate cuff and standard protocol was used as per JNC-VIII criteria.[5]

Statistical analysis

Participants were classified into three categories based on their BP readings. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP (SBP) ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic BP (DBP) ≥90 mm Hg, or the use of antihypertensive medication, regardless of current BP levels. Prehypertension (pre-HTN) was identified with SBP between 120 and 139 mm Hg or DBP between 80 and 89 mm Hg. Participants not meeting these criteria were categorized as normotensive.

The collected data were immediately entered into an Excel spreadsheet (MS Office 2010) and analysed using SPSS software version 21. Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using independent sample t-tests. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between hypertension and sociodemographic factors, as well as obesity metrics. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by including age and sex in separate models for each obesity metric.

Results

A total of 656 participants were included in the study. The mean age of participants was 29.35 ± 5.12 years, with a BMI of 23.35 ± 5.11 kg/m². The mean waist circumference was 81.6 ± 7.6 cm, and the mean waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was 0.88 ± 0.05. The mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were 122.5 ± 9.9 mmHg and 80.6 ± 7.3 mmHg, respectively. Among the participants, 36% were male (n = 236) and 64% were female (n = 420). The occupational distribution included doctors (6.6%), nurses (51.5%), paramedical staff (6.9%), pharmacy workers (7.0%), and sanitary workers (28.0%) [Table 1].

Male gender was associated with a higher risk of hypertension (AOR: 2.80, 95% CI: 1.30–6.10, P = 0.009) and pre-hypertension (AOR: 2.20, 95% CI: 1.20–3.95, P = 0.01). Central adiposity markers, such as a WHtR >0.5, were strong independent predictors of hypertension (AOR: 3.45, 95% CI: 1.65–7.15, P = 0.002) and pre-hypertension (AOR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.40–3.75, P = 0.002).

Obesity (BMI ≥23 kg/m²) was significantly associated with an increased risk of hypertension (AOR: 4.10, 95% CI: 2.10–8.00, P < 0.001) and pre-hypertension (AOR: 2.80, 95% CI: 1.65–4.75, P < 0.001). Furthermore, an increased waist circumference (≥80 cm) was a strong independent risk factor for both hypertension (AOR: 4.50, 95% CI: 2.10–9.50, P < 0.001) and pre-hypertension (AOR: 3.20, 95% CI: 1.95–5.45, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Our study identified a hypertension prevalence of 5.9%, with 23.0% of participants classified as pre-hypertensive. Hypertensive individuals exhibited significantly higher BMI, waist circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), reinforcing the well-established link between obesity and hypertension. Male participants demonstrated a higher prevalence of hypertension, a finding consistent with prior epidemiological studies, including those by Cheah WL et al.[6] and Sakurai M et al.[7].

The prevalence rate in our study aligns with the 8.2% reported by Cheah et al. (2018)[8] among university students. Differences in prevalence may be attributable to age variations, as our study population had a mean age of 29.35 years, compared to the younger participants in Cheah et al.[6] study. Gender differences further influenced hypertension risk, with a greater proportion of hypertensive males compared to females.[10] Jain J et al.[9] explained that hormonal factors contribute to this disparity, with testosterone playing a role in blood pressure regulation and estrogen providing a protective effect in females.

Obesity (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m²) was strongly associated with hypertension (AOR: 4.10, 95% CI: 2.10–8.00, P < 0.001) and pre-hypertension (AOR: 2.80, 95% CI: 1.65–4.75, P < 0.001). Increased waist circumference (≥80 cm) also emerged as a significant independent risk factor for hypertension (AOR: 4.50, 95% CI: 2.10–9.50, P < 0.001) and pre-hypertension (AOR: 3.20, 95% CI: 1.95–5.45, P < 0.001). Mankar et al.[11] similarly reported an increased hypertension risk in individuals with a BMI exceeding 25 and emphasized WHR as a superior predictor over BMI. Their findings indicated that individuals with a WHR exceeding 0.9 had a 2.73-fold increased risk of hypertension, underscoring the role of central adiposity in blood pressure regulation.

Cox et al.[12] and Brown et al.[13] reported that hypertension prevalence was significantly associated with increased BMI (P < 0.001). Harris et al.[14] found that elevated BMI was linked to increased odds of hypertension in African American and white men, while Mungreiphy et al.[15] observed that overweight/obese individuals had greater odds of developing hypertension (OR 2.42 for SBP and 3.76 for DBP). Similarly, Dua et al.[16] (OR 2.65) and Kalani et al.[17] (OR 2.86) demonstrated a strong association between elevated BMI and hypertension risk in different populations.

Beyond BMI, WHR also showed a strong relationship with hypertension. Kodali et al.[18] reported significantly higher WHR among hypertensives compared to controls. Harris et al.[14] found that elevated WHR increased hypertension odds in African American and white men, while Reddy et al.[19] observed a positive association between WHR and BP in males. Kalani et al.[17] further reported that individuals with higher WHR had a 2.39-fold increased likelihood of being hypertensive.

Male participants were disproportionately affected by hypertension in our study, with 64.1% of hypertensives being men. This gender disparity aligns with findings from Oumer et al.[20] and Mankar et al.,[11] which also reported higher hypertension prevalence in men. Potential explanations include differences in fat distribution, hormonal influences—particularly the protective effects of estrogen in women—and lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption.[212223]

Additionally, we observed a significant association between diabetes and hypertension, with 15.4% of hypertensive participants also having diabetes. This finding aligns with well-documented evidence linking metabolic syndrome components and hypertension. However, our study did not establish a statistically significant association between hypothyroidism and hypertension, suggesting the need for further investigation due to conflicting results from another research.[24]

Several independent predictors of hypertension were identified in our study, including age >30 years, male gender, high WHR (>0.5), obesity (BMI ≥ 23), and increased waist circumference (≥80 cm). These findings support previous research by Shrestha R et al.[25], Dereje R et al.[26] which emphasized the predictive value of central obesity markers in hypertension risk. Routine assessment of these anthropometric indicators in clinical settings could enhance early detection and management of hypertension.

Conclusion

This study establishes a significant association between hypertension and anthropometric indices such as BMI, WC, and WHtR among employees at Tertiary Healthcare Institute, Telangana. Central adiposity, male gender, and age above 30 years were identified as prominent risk factors for elevated blood pressure. The notable prevalence of pre-hypertension (23%) underscores the need for routine workplace screening using these simple, non-invasive measures to facilitate early detection and timely intervention. Implementing targeted workplace health programs focused on weight management, physical activity, and dietary modifications is critical to reducing the burden of hypertension in this population. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to assess the long-term impact of such interventions and to further understand the dynamics of occupational health risks related to hypertension.

Future investigations should adopt longitudinal designs to establish temporal and causal relationships between anthropometric indices and blood pressure progression, overcoming the constraints of record-based analysis. Incorporation of additional markers such as body fat percentage, visceral adiposity, and metabolic parameters may refine risk prediction. Multi-centric studies across diverse occupational groups would enhance generalizability. Interventional research evaluating workplace-based lifestyle modification programs is required to determine effectiveness in reducing pre-hypertension. Integration of digital health monitoring and periodic screening could facilitate early detection, continuous risk assessment, and targeted prevention strategies within institutional settings.

Limitations

As this is a record-based study, data available is limited and there is lack of control over variables and data collection. Causal relationship cannot be established due to nature of the study design. Limited generalizability as this is a hospital-based study.
