Age- and sex-specific normative values for reticulocyte indices and their relation to early-stage CKM syndrome
Authors: Lunhui Huang, Binbin Lin, Guoqing Zhu, Yueyi Mu, Yong Li, Yulong Fan, Guangliang Shan, Yajing Li, Min Shi, Yonghui Xia, Zhen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReticulocyte-related indices reflect erythropoietic activity and red blood cell maturation and may provide insight into systemic metabolic and inflammatory states. Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a progressive systemic disorder driven by interacting metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular abnormalities. This study aimed to establish age- and sex-specific normative values for reticulocyte-related indices and to explore their cross-sectional associations with early-stage CKM syndrome.MethodsA total of 5,692 adults aged 20–80 years from the China National Health Survey were included in this cross-sectional study for the final analysis. The Lambda-Mu-Sigma method was used to generate age- and sex-specific smoothed percentiles for reticulocyte indices, including RET# (reticulocyte count), RET% (reticulocyte percentage), LFR (low fluorescence reticulocytes), MFR (medium fluorescence reticulocytes), HFR (high fluorescence reticulocytes), IRF (immature reticulocyte fraction), RET-HE (reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent), and RPI (reticulocyte production index), with percentile estimates at P1, P5, P25, P50, P75, P95, and P99. Differences across early CKM stages were assessed using one-way analysis of variance, chi-square tests, and analysis of covariance. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to evaluate discriminative performance.ResultsReticulocyte-related indices showed significant sex-specific and age-specific variation, generally increasing in younger and middle-aged adults and then plateauing or declining in older adults. Several indices were associated with CKM stage progression. In particular, RET#, RET%, and RPI increased across CKM stages, whereas LFR decreased. ROC analysis showed that RET# provided good discrimination between CKM stage 0 and stages 1–2, with improved performance after adjustment for age and sex.ConclusionReticulocyte-related indices exhibit distinct age- and sex-specific distributions and are associated with early CKM stage status in this cross-sectional population. These findings provide normative reference data and suggest that reticulocyte indices may serve as exploratory indicators of systemic metabolic status, although prospective studies are needed to establish clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06185-x.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a progressive systemic disorder characterized by the complex interplay of metabolic dysfunction, obesity-related abnormalities, and pathological changes in the renal and cardiovascular systems, encompassing a spectrum of disease states and comorbid conditions [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of CKM syndrome is driven by a complex network of interrelated mechanisms, including excessive adipose tissue accumulation, insulin resistance, renal dysfunction, dyslipidemia, atherosclerosis, and systemic chronic inflammation, all of which contribute to its heterogeneous and multifaceted clinical presentation [3–5]. Recent epidemiological evidence has further demonstrated that CKM syndrome is strongly associated with abdominal obesity and adiposity-related risk profiles, triglyceride-glucose index–based metabolic risk stratification, and an increased risk of both cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality across CKM stages [6–8]. Adverse social determinants, such as unemployment, low income, food insecurity, limited education, and lack of health insurance; environmental pollutants, such as elevated fine particulate matter (PM2.5) levels; and psychological factors, including depression and anxiety, can increase the risk of CKM syndrome and its associated comorbidities [9–12]. Early implementation of precise screening and intervention strategies not only improves clinical outcomes for patients but also plays a significant role in reducing the burden of the disease on public health.

Reticulocyte indices, as biomarkers of bone marrow hematopoietic activity and red blood cell maturation status, have considerable potential for utilization in the early detection of hematological and metabolic abnormalities [13–15]. Our previous population-based study further demonstrated a significant association between reticulocyte indices and metabolic syndrome [16]. In parallel, systemic inflammation and chronic disease states can modulate erythropoiesis through cytokine-mediated pathways [17]. Reticulocytes are immature erythrocytes recently released from the bone marrow into peripheral blood and reflect real-time erythropoietic activity. In recent years, advancements in hematological analysis technologies have facilitated the automated measurement of reticulocyte-related indices such as reticulocyte count (RET#), LFR, MFR, HFR, reticulocyte percentage (RET%), immature reticulocyte fraction (IRF), reticulocyte production indices (RPI) and reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent (RET-HE) [18]. Under certain anemic conditions or states of stress erythropoiesis, the bone marrow releases a higher proportion of immature reticulocytes [19]. Under normal hematopoietic conditions, LFR predominate in the peripheral blood. However, during periods of intense compensatory erythropoiesis, an increased number of MFR and HFR are released into the peripheral blood, leading to a relative decrease in the proportion of LFR within the total reticulocyte population [20]. This dynamic shift provides nuanced information for the clinical assessment of hematopoietic compensation, anemia classification, and the evaluation of health risk factors [20–23].

Establishing age- and sex-specific normative values is crucial for accurate interpretation of an individual’s test results [24, 25]. Although recent studies have increasingly focused on reference intervals for reticulocyte indices [26–29], they have been limited in their comprehensiveness and ability to describe data variability, which restricts their applicability to both healthy and at-risk populations. The Lambda-Mu-Sigma (LMS) method models three indices of the data (λ, µ, and σ), capturing skewness and kurtosis of the distribution, thereby offering a more accurate depiction of how age, sex, and other factors influence physiological indices [30, 31]. The LMS method is characterized by its strong applicability, adaptability to age and sex, improved description of variability, and higher flexibility [32, 33].

This study analyzed large-scale population data obtained from multi-stage stratified cluster sampling through the China National Health Survey (CNHS) [34], to identify age- and sex-specific percentile normative values for reticulocyte-related indices and explore their growth trajectories and relationship with early stage CKM syndrome, with the aim of providing insights for the development of early and comprehensive indicators and interventions for both health outcomes and related risks.

Study design

Data source and study population

This cross-sectional study used data from the CNHS, which covered the Han Chinese population of Tianjin, China. Eligibility criteria included adults aged 20–80 years of Han ethnicity. Participants were excluded if they had a prior diagnosis of cardiovascular disease (e.g., myocardial infarction or stroke), an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² calculated using a Chinese-specific equation, or missing data on key variables, including reticulocyte-related parameters and CKM-related diagnostic indicators. After applying these criteria, a total of 5,692 participants were included in the final analysis.

Measurement of reticulocyte-related indices

The reticulocyte-related indices RET#, RET%, LFR, MFR, HFR, IRF, and RET-HE were assessed using a Sysmex XN-B3 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Japan). The reticulocyte production index (RPI) was calculated using the following formula [35]: RPI = (reticulocyte % / RBC maturation time) × (hematocrit / 0.45). Maturation time was assigned as follows: 1 d for hematocrit ≥ 40%; 1.5 days for hematocrit 30–39.9%; 2 days for hematocrit 20–29.9%, and 2.5 days for hematocrit < 20%.

For quality assurance, three-level commercial control materials (low, normal, and high concentration levels) were used throughout the reference interval investigation. Internal quality control was performed twice on each testing day, and instrument stability and analytical accuracy were required to meet predefined acceptable criteria before sample analysis. The coefficient of variation (CV) for RET# and RET% was maintained below 15%, while the analytical performance of other reticulocyte-related parameters complied with the manufacturer’s recommended specifications.

Identification of covariates

Participants’ demographic information, including age, sex, residential area, marital status, education level, and annual income, as well as lifestyle-related variables, were collected using standardized structured questionnaires administered by trained interviewers as part of the CNHS survey. Smoking status was categorized as current smoker or non-smoker, and alcohol consumption was classified as current drinker or non-drinker. Physical inactivity was defined as engaging in exercise fewer than two times per week during the previous year. These lifestyle variables were self-reported and were not externally validated; therefore, potential recall and reporting bias should be considered. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²), and obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² according to the Chinese criteria [36].

Definition of early stages of CKM syndrome

The early stages of CKM syndrome are 0–2. The definitions were adapted from those described in previous studies [3]: Stage 0 was defined as absence of metabolic risk factors, normal BMI (< 23 kg/m²), normal blood glucose (FBG < 5.6 mmol/L), normal blood pressure (SBP < 140 mmHg and DBP < 90 mmHg), normal triglycerides (TG < 1.7 mmol/L), and no evidence of CKD or CVD. Stage 1 was defined as BMI ≥ 23 kg/m² or prediabetes (FBG 5.6–6.9 mmol/L). Stage 2 was defined as the presence of any metabolic risk factor, including diabetes (FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or self-reported diagnosis or medication use), hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg or medication use), hypertriglyceridemia (TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L), metabolic syndrome (MetS), or moderate-to-high-risk CKD (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²).

MetS was diagnosed based on the following criteria [16]: (1) Hyperglycemia: Fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 5.6 mmol/L or the use of antidiabetic medication. (2) Hypertension: Diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg, systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medication. (3) Elevated triglycerides: Triglyceride levels (TG) ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or the use of lipid-lowering medication. (4) Low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C): HDL-C < 1.29 mmol/L for females or < 1.03 mmol/L for males, or the use of lipid-lowering medication. A score of 1 was assigned for each criterion met, and MetS was defined as a total score of ≥ 2.

Statistical analysis

Basic characteristics of the study population were presented as the mean ± SD or median (IQR) for continuous variables and the number (frequency) for categorical variables according to sex or CKM stage. Comparisons between sex groups were performed using Student’s t tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical variables, while comparisons among CKM stages were conducted using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables. For reticulocyte-related indices, the percentile reference intervals were determined using the LMS method after stratification by age and sex. Before model development, outliers were identified using the Tukey method, defined as values below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR and treated as missing values prior to analysis. A generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) was used to examine the relationships between each parameter and age by sex. The Box-Cox transformation was employed to account for deviations from normality. In this model, the age variable was smoothed using a cubic spline function with three degrees of freedom (cs(age, df = 3)), and the error distribution was specified as a gamma distribution (GA) to better accommodate the non-normal characteristics of the data. Following model fitting, the centiles.pred function was used to predict the 1st, 5th, 25th, 50th (median), 75th, 95th, and 99th percentiles of the reticulocyte-related indices across the age range of 20–80 years in 1-year increments, thereby providing a comprehensive depiction of the physiological distribution across stratified groups.

Missing data were observed only in several covariates included in the multivariable models, including residential area, marital status, education level, annual income, smoking history, and physical activity, with proportions of missingness < 3% for all variables. Given the low proportion of missing data, a complete-case analysis was performed, and no imputation was applied. To compare the differences among the early stages of CKM syndrome, appropriate statistical tests were selected based on the variable type and distribution. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to compare continuous reticulocyte-related indices across the early stages of CKM syndrome, adjusting for age, sex, residential area, marital status, education level, annual income, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and exercise frequency. Data are presented as least-square means ± standard errors (SE). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. P for trend was assessed by modeling CKM stages as an ordinal variable in the ANCOVA model.

For the two outcome variables (CKM stage 2 and CKM stages 1–2 combined), both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models (adjusted for age and sex) were constructed to evaluate the discriminative ability of each eticulocyte-related parameter for early CKM syndrome. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated based on predicted probabilities derived from the logistic regression models, and the area under the curve (AUC) was used to quantify discriminative performance. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results. These included (1) exclusion of participants with anemia (hemoglobin < 120 g/L in men and < 110 g/L in women), with the same modeling strategy repeated, and (2) additional adjustment for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) or white blood cell (WBC) to account for potential influence of systemic inflammation; and (3) exclusion of outliers identified using the Tukey method.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.2.2. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sex and age-specific reticulocyte-related index values

As shown in Table S1, the study of normative values included 2,143 males and 3,549 females. Among the reticulocyte-related indices, all indices, except for MFR (P-Value = 0.1136), demonstrated significant differences between the sexes (P-Value < 0.0001). Using the LMS method, we derived the normative percentile values for the reticulocyte-related indices stratified by age and sex, as illustrated in Fig. 1 and Table 1. All age- and sex-stratified subgroups contained sufficient sample sizes (minimum n = 144), supporting stable percentile estimation. The participants were grouped into three age categories: young (20–40 years), middle-aged (40–60 years), and elderly (60–80 years). All reticulocyte-related indices exhibited significant age-related trends, with values typically increasing in young and middle-aged individuals and showing either a plateau or a decline in the elderly group.

Changes in RET#, RET%, and RPI followed the same trend: they were decreased in young males but increased in females. This early divergence suggests that females experience an earlier increase in these indices compared with males. In males, RET-HE generally increased with age and stabilized after 60 years of age, whereas LFR% steadily decreased with age, with the most rapid decline occurring between 20 and 40 years of age. HFR increased slightly in the middle-aged population and declined in the elderly, particularly in males. The proportions of LFR, MFR, and HFR exhibited consistent trends across all age groups. Notably, MFR and HFR values were higher in males compared with those in females. Moreover, the LFR values in males reached a stable level during the 20–30-year age range, whereas stabilization occurred approximately 10 years later in females.

Analysis revealed substantial variability in the higher percentiles (75th, 95th, and 99th percentiles) across all indices, especially in the elderly, suggesting differences in reticulocyte function and production among individuals.

Characteristics of the early CKM syndrome stages

Table S2 showed the significant changes in the demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and metabolic indices across the early stages of CKM syndrome. Among the 5,692 participants included in the final analysis, 930 (16.3%) were classified as CKM stage 0, 994 (17.5%) as CKM stage 1, and 3,768 (66.2%) as CKM stage 2. RET#, RET%, RPI, IRF, HFR, and MFR showed significant upward trends as the CKM stages progressed, whereas LFR exhibited an inverse pattern. Although RET-HE showed only minor fluctuations, these changes were statistically significant (p < 0.0001).

Figure 2 showed that the prevalence of CKM stage 2 gradually increased with increasing age, whereas that of CKM stage 0 markedly decreased, underscoring the strong age-dependent trend in the early progression of CKM syndrome. Notably, the prevalence of CKM stage 1 remained relatively stable among middle-aged individuals but declined in the 70–80-year age group, where stage 2 became predominant. Across all age groups, females exhibited a higher incidence of CKM stage 2 than males, particularly within the 50–80-year age range, potentially reflecting an inherent sex-specific susceptibility to CKM progression.

Effects of the early CKM syndrome stage on reticulocyte-related indices

ANCOVA (Table 2) was conducted to assess the differences in the reticulocyte-related indices across the CKM stages (0, 1, and 2), whilst adjusting for age, sex, residential area, marital status, education level, annual income, smoking history, drinking history, and exercise frequency. The RET#, RET%, IRF, LFR, MFR, HFR, and RPI exhibited significant differences among the stages, with most indices displaying increasing trends. RET-HE values showed a slight but statistically significant decrease, from 34.74 ± 0.10 in CKM0 to 34.50 ± 0.07 in CKM2 (P = 0.0101). This suggests a minor reduction in the hemoglobin content of reticulocytes in the advanced CKM stages.

Predictive value of reticulocyte-related indices in the early CKM syndrome stages

The discriminative performance of reticulocyte-related indices for different stages of CKM syndrome was evaluated using ROC curve analyses (Fig. 3). In unadjusted models distinguishing CKM2 from the combined CKM0–CKM1 group, RET# and RPI showed the highest AUC values (both AUC = 0.70), followed by RET% (AUC = 0.68,, 95% CI: 0.66–0.69 ). Other indices, including IRF, LFR, and HFR, showed moderate performance (AUCs approximately 0.62–0.65), whereas RET-HE showed the lowest discriminative ability (AUC = 0.56) (Fig. 3A). After adjustment for age and sex, higher AUC estimates were observed for most indices, with RET# (AUC = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.78–0.81), RET% and RPI (AUC = 0.79) showing the highest discriminative performance, suggesting that demographic factors provide additional discriminatory information (Fig. 3B). Similar patterns were observed when distinguishing CKM1–CKM2 combined from CKM0. In unadjusted models, RET# showed the highest AUC (0.76, 95% CI: 0.74–0.77), followed by other indices (Fig. 3C). After adjustment, RET# achieved the highest AUC (0.82, 95% CI: 0.81–0.84), with overall improved discriminative performance across indices (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, sensitivity analyses excluding participants with anemia, additionally adjusting for hs-CRP or WBC, and excluding outliers identified using the Tukey method yielded largely consistent results with the primary analyses (Figures S1–S3). Similar AUC patterns and ranking of reticulocyte-related indices across analyses support the robustness of these markers for early-stage CKM discrimination.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into age- and sex-specific variations in reticulocyte-related indices and their relevance to early CKM progression. Our results demonstrated significant differences in the reticulocyte-related indices between the sexes and across age groups, with distinct trends observed in various indices, including RET#, RET%, and RPI. Importantly, these indices showed progressive changes in response to advancing early stage CKM syndrome, suggesting their potential as biomarkers for early detection and disease monitoring. These findings underscore the intricate relationship between hematopoiesis and metabolic dysfunction in CKM and emphasize the importance of considering age, sex, and disease stage when interpreting reticulocyte-related indices. The predictive ability of these indices in differentiating the early stages of CKM further strengthens their clinical utility, especially in identifying individuals at a higher risk of disease progression.

To the best of our knowledge, for the first time, this study elucidated the age-related trajectory of reticulocyte maturation across the 20–80 age range. The general distribution of reticulocyte-related indices across different age groups suggested that bone marrow hematopoietic activity peaks in early adulthood and middle age, followed by a gradual decline in older adults. The observed decrease in LFR and increase in HFR with age indicates a shift in reticulocyte maturation, likely reflecting age-related alterations in erythropoietic efficiency [19]. Previous studies had demonstrated significant age- and sex-related differences in automated reticulocyte counts and maturation fractions among pediatric patients [26, 37]. Although Fernando et al. demonstrated that the IRF and RET# in a pediatric population exhibited age-specific dependencies, sex stratification was not deemed necessary [38]. Our study identified significant sex-related differences in fluorescence subtypes, particularly higher HFR values in males, indicating sex-specific erythropoietic responses. Females exhibited earlier shifts in certain reticulocyte-related indices, which may reflect differences in erythropoietic regulation, systemic iron homeostasis, and hormonal modulation of erythropoiesis. In contrast, MFR did not differ significantly between sexes, suggesting that this intermediate maturation fraction may be relatively less sensitive to physiological sex-related variation and may reflect greater stability of intermediate reticulocyte maturation kinetics across sexes. These differences represent the sex-dependent adaptation of erythropoiesis to compensatory mechanisms, stress, and metabolic regulation [39]. These findings are consistent with age-related clonal hematopoiesis [40], which is associated with physiological aging processes and closely linked to inflammation, vascular diseases, and cancer mortality rates [22, 41, 42], particularly in the elderly population, where the decline further substantiates this finding.

Our data are consistent with those of previous reports, demonstrating that as CKM advances from stages CKM0 to CKM2, there is an increase in the prevalence of socio-economic and lifestyle factors, such as low income, limited educational attainment, smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity, indicating their association with advanced disease [11, 12]. Emerging population-based studies have also reported temporal changes in CKM prevalence, including an increase during the COVID-19 pandemic period, highlighting the influence of broader metabolic and environmental contexts on disease burden [43]. Recent studies have further expanded the CKM framework by highlighting the contributions of vascular aging, frailty, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease to disease progression. These conditions share common pathophysiological pathways with CKM syndrome, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation, suggesting that alterations in reticulocyte-related indices may reflect broader biological processes associated with systemic aging and multisystem metabolic impairment [44–47]. Notably, RET#, RET%, IRF%, HFR%, and RPI showed a significant upward trend as the CKM stage progressed, whereas LFR% decreased. Given the mathematical interdependence among LFR, MFR, and HFR, the decline in LFR should be interpreted as a complementary inverse reflection of the expansion of immature reticulocyte fractions rather than as an independent biological phenomenon. Additionally, individual reticulocyte indices have been widely utilized in recent years for predicting both benign and malignant hematologic diseases [21, 48]. Their potential role in health risk assessments should be recognized and further explored.

The results of the covariance and ROC curve analyses further substantiated the statistical significance of the differences in these indices across CKM stages, reinforcing the value of reticulocyte-related indices in early stage differentiation and risk assessment. However, the observed AUC values indicate only limited-to-moderate discriminative ability, which is insufficient for standalone clinical prediction of CKM stages. These findings suggest that reticulocyte-related indices may serve as complementary markers within multivariable risk stratification frameworks rather than independent predictive biomarkers. These findings suggest that RET# and RPI could serve as valuable biomarkers for early CKM detection, facilitating timely intervention to prevent disease progression. Additionally, age- and sex-adjusted models demonstrated improved predictive accuracy, highlighting the necessity of incorporating demographic factors into personalized risk stratification. In this context, normative age- and sex-specific reference values established for reticulocyte-related indices would be highly valuable for clinical applications.

Stage 2 CKM syndrome is typically diagnosed at a relatively late stage of the disease course. The use of reticulocyte-related parameters allows earlier detection of CKD in individuals with a higher risk of developing hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes, thus improving patient health and prognosis [12]. Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation play crucial roles in CKD [49, 50]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α and HIF-2α regulate the expression of the EPO gene in the kidney and liver, contributing to the protective response to hypoxia and promoting erythropoiesis [51]. The proliferation and survival of red blood cells are closely linked to the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway [52]. During chronic inflammation in damaged kidneys, numerous cytokines and growth factors related to the JAK-STAT signaling pathway are present [53]. The investigation of these mechanisms may provide new intervention strategies for the progression of CKM syndrome.

Despite the methodological rigor of our study, including its substantial sample size, comprehensive stratified analyses, and robust statistical approaches, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the study was based on a population-based sample using a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling design, its single-region setting in Tianjin may limit generalizability to other populations, and some selection bias may remain due to voluntary participation and exclusion of individuals with incomplete data. Second, the cross-sectional design limited our ability to capture dynamic changes in erythropoietic indices over time or to infer causal relationships between reticulocyte-related indices and CKM progression. Third, mechanistic validation was beyond the scope of this study, and the biological pathways linking erythropoiesis-related indices to CKM progression require further investigation. Finally, future multicenter longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the broader applicability of our findings and to clarify the mechanistic relevance of these associations.

In conclusion, this study systematically delineated the normative values of reticulocyte-related indices and elucidated the age- and sex-specific differences among the early CKM stages. These valuable insights support early risk assessment and the development of personalized interventions for the early CKM stages. Our results have substantial translational potential and lay a robust foundation for further exploration of early CKM pathogenesis. Future research integrating prospective follow-up studies and multicenter validations is crucial to advance the clinical application of these indices in early screening and targeted intervention strategies for early CKM syndrome.
