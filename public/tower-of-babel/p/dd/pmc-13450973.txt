ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImproving access to genetic testing has increased the number of individuals identified with cancer genetic predisposition. Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) and Lynch syndrome (LS) are key examples of high-risk hereditary cancer syndromes. Ensuring that carriers of germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (GPVs) receive evidence-based risk counseling, surveillance and risk-reducing interventions remains a global challenge. Variations in implementation and reporting of international guidelines across healthcare systems contribute to this problem.MethodsAn international workshop on cancer genetic care, held from March 12 to March 15, 2025, in Switzerland, brought together 40 experts on this topic from 10 countries. The workshop combined evidence-based presentations with expert-led discussions and considered novel strategies, which were synthesized in key discussion points.ResultsParticipants highlighted major inconsistencies in reporting age of initiation and uptake of surveillance, follow-up intervals, and uptake of risk-reducing interventions for carriers of GPVs associated with genetic predisposition to cancer between and within countries. These differences are due to variations in available technology, insurance coverage, and sociocultural attitudes that shape national clinical guidelines. Participants emphasized the need for a standardized approach for reporting surveillance practices, including clear definitions of gene-specific recommendations, timing of follow-up, and alternatives when ideal resources are limited. In addition to these reporting issues, participants also noted the need for sustained outreach for lifelong follow-up surveillance of GPV carriers through digital as well as low-tech approaches.ConclusionStandardized reporting of surveillance and risk-reducing practices across countries may improve the quality and comparability of data in cancer genetic predisposition, reveal gaps in genetic care, and inform outreach strategies for engaging GPV carriers in lifelong cancer risk management.

Introduction

Hereditary cancer syndromes account for approximately 5–15% of all cancer diagnoses and encompass diverse genetic conditions characterized by significantly elevated lifetime cancer risk [1–6]. Inheritance is autosomal dominant for most of these conditions, implying high cancer risks for successive generations [7]. Key examples of high-penetrance conditions are hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) and Lynch syndrome (LS), which contribute to a significant portion of common cancers (e.g., breast, colorectal, endometrial, prostate) and cancers often diagnosed at advanced stages (e.g., pancreatic, ovarian) [8, 9]. Advances in genetic testing technology and broader testing criteria have increased the number of patients with cancer found to carry a germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant (GPV) in a cancer predisposition gene. Systematic cascade genetic testing of at-risk relatives can lead to the identification of a growing number of unaffected individuals who are also carriers of the familial GPV and therefore eligible for guideline-recommended enhanced cancer risk management [10]. Genetic predisposition to cancer marks the initiation of lifelong comprehensive care, involving intensive cancer surveillance and/or risk reduction strategies [11]. Access to imaging, risk-reducing surgeries, and preventive medication has increased the life expectancy of GPV carriers. For example, annual magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) surveillance among females with a GPV in the BRCA1 gene has been associated with an approximately 80% lower breast cancer-specific mortality compared with those not undergoing MRI (the majority of the non-MRI group underwent at least one mammogram) [12].

International guidelines emphasize proactive, gene-specific surveillance and risk-reducing strategies for GPV carriers, highlighting the critical importance of early detection of premalignant and malignant lesions and of adapting care as guidelines evolve [13]. Evaluating whether GPV carriers receive guideline concordant care is essential for improving cancer-related outcomes [14]. Currently, studies show substantial care gaps. For example, in the USA, 35%–40% of individuals with an HBOC- or LS-associated GPV lack risk-appropriate surveillance [15–17], resulting in delayed diagnoses and poorer outcomes. Comparable findings have been reported in Europe, where despite universal health insurance coverage, 30%–40% of carriers receive inadequate surveillance [18–20], and up to 20% receive no surveillance at all [18]. These estimates are likely understated, as variations in how surveillance is measured and reported, including underdiagnosis and undertreatment (missed or delayed recommended surveillance), and overdiagnosis and overtreatment (screening or procedures performed more frequently or earlier than recommended), underestimate the true extent of care gaps.

Most national guidelines are informed by international frameworks, such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) in the USA, the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO), the European Reference Network for Genetic Tumor Risk Syndromes (ERN GENTURIS), eviQ in Australia, or the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the UK. However, each country adapts recommendations to its population risk profile(s) and healthcare resources. Cultural values and the acceptability of interventions further influence how risk is communicated, how surveillance and preventive surgeries are perceived, and how guidelines are interpreted and implemented across countries. Consequently, the type, timing, and accessibility of surveillance and preventive options can vary, even within relatively unified regions [21]. These differences complicate cross-national comparisons regarding access to surveillance and prevention methods, compliance with national guidelines, the evaluation of long-term impact on cancer prevention, and the development of effective and culturally informed care models. Without a systematic approach to integrating actionable genetic findings into clinical practice, opportunities for prevention, early detection, and education of at-risk individuals are too often missed.

Methods

An international workshop on effective models of cancer genetic care was held on 12–15 March 2025 at Monte Verità, Switzerland. Organized through the ETH Zurich (Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule, Zurich, Switzerland) – Congressi Stefano Francini collaboration, the workshop convened 40 experts from 10 countries (see online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553075 for participant information). The program combined evidence-based presentations with expert-led discussions, which were synthesized into key discussion points and reviewed by all attendees for accuracy.

The workshop addressed three areas of cancer genetic care: lifelong cancer surveillance and risk management in cancer genetic predisposition; measuring cascade testing and applying findings in clinical and public health practice; and unmet needs of individuals with a GPV in cancer predisposition genes. Discussions were documented in real time, and all participants consented to the inclusion of their contributions in subsequent reports.

This paper focuses on lifelong cancer surveillance and risk-reducing surgeries for carriers of GPVs linked to genetic predisposition to cancer. It discusses two central questions: how clinicians, researchers, and public health officials can monitor and report uptake of surveillance and risk-reducing strategies amid substantial inconsistencies in current reporting practices, and how healthcare systems can ensure sustainable, lifelong follow-up of these families in a rapidly evolving field.

Results

Question 1: Standardize Reporting to Enable Assessment of Effectiveness of Interventions to Reduce Cancer Risk in individuals with Genetic Predisposition to Cancer

Participants emphasized that inconsistencies in cancer surveillance and risk reduction practices are a major barrier to comparing data across countries and evaluating the effectiveness of these measures. An international survey of 22 high-risk cancer clinics across 16 countries for carriers of GPVs in BRCA1 and BRCA2 (hereafter BRCA) genes found substantial variation in the age of initiating surveillance, imaging methods, and follow-up intervals [22]. At the time of the study, most centers began annual breast MRI at age 25, but some centers in the UK, Italy, and Australia delayed until age 30, while Hong Kong implemented this screening as early as 18 [22].

Local factors, such as clinician attitudes, technology availability, and health insurance coverage, strongly affect how guidelines are implemented. Another international study reported that, at the time of the study, almost 100% of breast surgeons in the Netherlands and the UK supported the option of risk-reducing mastectomy for GPV carriers in BRCA genes unaffected by breast cancer, compared to 78% in France and 66% in Germany [23]. In low- and middle-income countries, where MRI technology is not widely available, breast MRI is replaced by mammography for surveillance of females with HBOC-associated variants [24]. Additional barriers in low- and middle-income countries include shortage of trained genetics professionals and fragmented health systems that impede access to genetic care for high-risk individuals [25]. Essential tools, such as breast MRI and colonoscopy are often scarce outside major cities, resulting in long-distance travel and high out-of-pocket costs, which makes guideline-recommended surveillance difficult to follow [11]. For example, insurance coverage in the Republic of Korea (hereafter Korea) does not include risk-reducing mastectomy for unaffected females with a GPV in BRCA genes [26]. In Israel, recently, free genetic testing for certain HBOC-related founder GPVs was added to the health basket services, only to females of Ashkenazi Jewish descent regardless of family history, excluding Ashkenazi Jewish males [27, 28]. Availability of disposable income for health expenses and health literacy further shape access to and use of risk management options. Indirect costs such as travel time, time off work, caregiving responsibilities, and limited financial flexibility can restrict individuals’ ability to pursue recommended surveillance or preventive interventions even when services are nominally available [29]. At the same time, variability in health literacy affects how genetic risk information, preventive options, and long-term implications are understood, discussed with providers, and acted upon [30]. Thus, without reporting contextual factors at the patient, provider, and healthcare system levels, interpretation of outcomes related to cancer surveillance and uptake of risk-reducing strategies is incomplete.

Workshop participants emphasized that in addition to standardized methods for guideline development, there is a clear need for a framework to standardize how surveillance and uptake of risk-reducing strategies for cancer genetic predisposition are measured and reported. This would enable clinicians, researchers, and public health officials to track risk management efforts consistently, facilitating meaningful cross-country comparisons despite differences in national guidelines and resource availability. Standardization in reporting can support the integration of hereditary cancer-specific interventions into practice and public health, bridging the gap between real-world data and the implementation of evidence-based healthcare [31, 32]. The workshop identified critical core elements to enable valid cross-country comparisons:1.Guideline reporting – specify the version of the national or international guidelines used to define compliance with surveillance and risk-reducing surgeries.2.Gene-specific reporting – report gene-specific and, if relevant, variant-specific surveillance and risk-reducing surgeries. For patients with GPVs in two or more cancer genes, report the gene-specific risk management strategies for non-overlapping cancers. For example, for a female with GPVs in BRCA1 and MLH1 genes, with increased risk for breast and colorectal cancer, respectively, report risk-reducing mastectomy for BRCA1 and colonoscopy for MLH1. For overlapping cancers, report risk management according to the gene with the highest cancer risk (highest penetrance). For example, for a female with increased risk for breast cancer due to GPVs in BRCA1 and CHEK2 genes, report risk-reducing mastectomy for BRCA1.3.Reporting of personal/family history-dependent surveillance – when recommendations depend on ancestry, personal, or family health history, report the percentage of the sample with those characteristics, and use only the number of individuals meeting these criteria (personal or family health history, ancestry) as the denominator when reporting compliance rates.4.Age of initiation – use the highest recommended upper age limit when reporting surveillance and risk-reducing surgeries to account for limited resources and/or flexibility in guidelines. Individuals are considered compliant until they pass the upper age limit.5.Surveillance interval – use the longest recommended interval when reporting surveillance to account for limited resources and/or flexibility in guidelines. Individuals are considered compliant until the maximum interval range has passed.6.“Next best action” principle – report alternative surveillance when ideal tools are unavailable or not widely accessible. For example, countries with limited availability or access to breast MRI can report on mammography surveillance.7.Post-diagnosis documentation – report time since genetic diagnosis and record surveillance and uptake of risk-reducing surgeries starting 1 year after the disclosure of a genetic diagnosis. Individuals often require time to organize and initiate recommended cancer risk management plans.8.Post-treatment documentation – report time since last cancer diagnosis and record surveillance and uptake of risk-reducing surgeries at least 1 year post-diagnosis to avoid overcounting procedures done as part of treatment.

Question 2: Sustaining Outreach for Cancer Surveillance and Prevention

Genetic care models, defined as the organizational structures and clinical pathways through which genetic counseling, testing, surveillance, and risk-reducing interventions are delivered, differ widely [37]. Some regions use high-risk clinics with standardized protocols, while others have informal, fragmented, or no documentation of screening and risk management uptake [38–41]. In a multicenter study of high-risk clinics, most centers offered no specific or standardized long-term surveillance following risk-reducing mastectomy and/or salpingo-oophorectomy [22]. However, carrying a GPV in a cancer genetic predisposition gene requires lifelong surveillance for cancer in several organs. For example, females with a GPV in the BRCA1 gene who undergo risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy still require ongoing breast surveillance [42], which may be overlooked in the absence of coordinated follow-up pathways. Additional surveillance for pancreatic cancer or melanoma may also be indicated, depending on personal or family history. Another international study across 10 countries reported that in the USA, Canada, and France, more than 70% of females with a GPV in the BRCA genes chose risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy, compared with around 37% in China [43]. These findings highlight differences in uptake of risk-reducing surgeries and the need for structured systems to support consistent implementation.

Healthcare systems must ensure lifelong follow-up for individuals with GPVs linked to cancer predisposition genes and develop strategies to maintain their engagement. High-risk clinics and dedicated personnel, including nurse navigators or trained genetic counselors, play a key role in coordinating and sustaining follow-up care by scheduling appointments, addressing logistical barriers, and fostering ongoing education and empowerment [15]. However, these solutions are resource intensive and are often unattainable due to lack of genetic experts or dedicated specialty hereditary cancer programs. Enhanced education and standardized protocols of nongenetic healthcare providers, such as advanced practice nurses or nurse practitioners can improve cancer prevention and reduce disparities [44]. Effective surveillance also relies on coordinated action across multiple pathways of the healthcare system, spanning services from surveillance registries and primary care to oncology care. Although implementation of national guidelines regarding follow-up does not necessarily require direct involvement of genetic specialists, genetic counseling and comprehensive risk assessment and communication remain essential for informed and shared medical decision-making. At the same time, genetics expertise is critical to ensure clinicians remain updated as guidelines evolve.

Many systems integrate genetic and genomic information into electronic health records (EHRs) to track high-risk patients. Examples include a US network for sending email reminders using real-time EHR alerts and population dashboards for individuals with LS-associated GPVs [45]. Similarly, the PennChart Genomics Initiative integrates structured genetic results into the EHR to streamline routine care [46]. The Danish Hereditary Non-Polyposis Colorectal Cancer (HNPCC) register uses a centralized national infrastructure to identify carriers through cascade testing and support long-term surveillance via systematic recall and provider-mediated follow-up [47]. Another example is the Netherlands Foundation for the Detection of Hereditary Tumors (StOET), which supports identification and long-term surveillance of individuals at increased cancer risk through a centralized recall system that sends notifications when periodic investigations (e.g., colonoscopy) are due [48]. Mobile apps, such as the SNAP for BRCA iPhone app [49], and the HomeTown mobile app [50], provide personalized surveillance schedules and reminders. However, when digital infrastructure is limited or is unavailable, low-tech and paper-based solutions can boost uptake of evidence-based practices [51], including call-recall lists at local genetic clinics, annual reminders, or newsletters.

Discussion

Carrying a GPV in a cancer predisposition gene often means lifelong engagement with the healthcare system for surveillance and risk-reducing strategies. Without structured and continuous support, it is challenging to manage individuals with heightened cancer risk, who require frequent multiorgan surveillance [52]. Coordinating multiple appointments and navigating often conflicting recommendations from multiple clinicians can also be overwhelming for carriers of GPVs [15], particularly when a decision is complex due to multiple individual and external factors [53]. Given that clinical guidelines evolve rapidly, there is a need for continued support for families with high-risk cancer predisposing genes that extends well beyond the initial genetic testing period. Relying on individuals with GPVs to independently track evolving risk management recommendations often leads to gaps in care and inconsistent follow-up [52].

Achieving standardization of reporting in real-world settings requires clear responsibility for reporting key characteristics relevant to cancer risk management. Proactive tracking and prospective data collection are considered best practices, given the need for decadal-long, regular screening [15]. Establishing a gene-specific global cancer surveillance network would enable the hereditary cancer community to ensure high-quality care through continuous learning and benchmarking [21]. Additional actions could involve replicating syndrome-specific registries, such as the Danish HNPCC register [47] or enriching existing resources, such as cancer registries, with information regarding genetic testing results in individuals with a cancer diagnosis. This could ensure consistent documentation of core elements such as the applicable guidelines, gene-specific risk management, relevant personal and family history, timing and intervals of surveillance, uptake of risk-reducing surgeries, and morbidity and mortality outcomes over time. A global, unified approach to reporting surveillance and uptake of risk-reducing surgeries is key to translating expanding genetic and genomic knowledge into tangible reductions in cancer morbidity and mortality.

The real-world evidence on current gaps in management of cancer genetic predisposition is a call to action. Surveillance and risk-reducing surgeries are cost-effective [54] and can reduce cancer morbidity and mortality [12] but only when used consistently. Effective risk management plans require stakeholders to evaluate outcomes and identify best practices by pooling comparable data from different countries and healthcare systems. A critical first step to achieve this goal is to set international standards for data reporting, including definitions of outcomes and clarification of follow-up intervals. Without robust data, opportunities to improve outcomes for carriers of GPVs may be lost. Such harmonization can enhance the quality of studies on the uptake of surveillance and risk-reducing strategies, reduce research waste, and accelerate progress in cancer genetics and related fields.

Genetic experts, researchers, health policy officials, and patients themselves play a crucial role in maintaining consistent outreach to individuals and families with GPVs connected to cancer predisposition and in facilitating the implementation of evidence-based risk management guidelines. At the same time, nongenetic experts can provide sustainable and ongoing outreach to these families and can play a meaningful role in shaping the processes and workflows that are implemented in routine care [44]. Effective approaches likely include patient registries and call-up systems that monitor carriers of GPVs and issue timely prompts to both patients and clinicians when guidelines are updated or when surveillance or risk-reducing surgeries are due [47]. Leveraging both advanced digital solutions and essential low-tech support systems can help GPV carriers remain engaged in shared decision-making for lifelong risk management.
