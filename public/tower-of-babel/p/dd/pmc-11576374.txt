A Novel Protocol for Contrast-Free Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement Evaluation and Implantation
Authors: David Elison, Barbara A. Danek, Bryce V. Johnson, Christine J. Chung, Shakirat Oyetunji, G. Burkhard Mackensen, Gabriel Aldea, James M. McCabe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to develop a transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) sizing algorithm and implantation method to facilitate safe and effective TAVR without contrast use in patients with severe chronic kidney disease (CKD) who do not yet require renal replacement therapy. Patients with CKD are a challenging patient subset to treat using standard TAVR care pathways which most usually require the use of iodinated contrast media both during gated computed tomography (CT) angiography sizing, and valve deployment. Iodinated contrast exposure may worsen kidney function in a dose-dependent fashion, and may result in a need for renal replacement therapy. Therefore, a method to eliminate, or greatly lessen, contrast exposure during TAVR is highly desirable.MethodsOne hundred sixty patients who underwent standard CT angiography and TAVR implantation were used to develop (100 patients) and validate (60 patients) an algorithm to predict balloon-expandable valve size using measurements available from noncontrast CT. Model accuracy was measured using Pearson correlation between algorithm-predicted valve size and actual size used based on annular area. As proof of concept, we then report a case series of 35 consecutive patients who underwent TAVR evaluation and implantation with moderate sedation and no contrast between May 2021 and April 2024.ResultsThe valve sizing algorithm incorporates sinus and sinotubular junction diameters, both available from a reformatted noncontrast, cardiac-gated CT. The algorithm demonstrated a strong correlation with the actual valve size used (development cohort R = 0.81, validation cohort R = 0.76). Thirty-five patients underwent “no-contrast TAVR” with noncontrast CT for valve sizing. Forty-eight percent were urgent inpatient TAVR. Technical procedural success, as defined by the VARC3 criteria, was 100%, with no patient deaths during the index procedure. Three patients had a mild paravalvular leak. No valve embolization or need for a second valve occurred. Four patients required a pacemaker.ConclusionsWe present a novel algorithm for TAVR evaluation and implantation without the use of iodinated contrast media in a high-risk patient subset with CKD.

Introduction

As transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) continues to grow in popularity, procedural techniques also continue to evolve with the goal of improving patient outcomes. Nevertheless, certain patient subgroups may be more challenging to treat. One such group is patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) not yet on renal replacement therapy. Unfortunately, CKD is a common comorbidity among patients with aortic stenosis. In the Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valve 1 and 2 trials, > 20% of patients treated with TAVR had CKD stage 3B (defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <45 mL/min/1.73 m2), and >5% of patients had CKD stage 4 (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2).1, 2, 3 Patients with CKD are at high risk of contrast-induced nephropathy,3 either during the TAVR screening evaluation with cardiac gated, computed tomographic angiography (CTA) or during the valve implantation procedure. Unsurprisingly, impaired or worsening renal function both pre- and post-TAVR are associated with worse clinical outcomes, including increased mortality.3,4 One meta-analysis of 11 studies demonstrated that preexisting CKD increases risk of short- and long-term mortality in high-surgical-risk patients (HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.22-1.88, and HR, 1.56; CI, 1.38-1.77, respectively).5 A separate meta-analysis of 43 studies showed that post-TAVR acute kidney injury (AKI), independent of preexisting CKD, increases short-term mortality (OR = 6.25) and long-term mortality (OR = 3.49); it also increases risk of early myocardial infarction (OR = 3.98) and risk of major bleeding (OR = 1.51).6 Several other studies have confirmed that in patients with baseline CKD, worsening renal function (often defined as ≥10% decrease in eGFR or initiation of hemodialysis) after TAVR is associated with increased 2-year mortality.7, 8, 9 Thus, it is imperative to minimize potential renal insults in patients with CKD undergoing TAVR, which ideally means eliminating or at least minimizing iodinated contrast exposure.

One mechanism classically used to avoid contrast associated with the screening CTA is annular sizing with 3-dimensional (3D) transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) during the TAVR implantation. Prior to the routine use of CTA for valve sizing, several imaging modalities were assessed including 2D transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and TEE, as well as 3D TEE. Although studies demonstrated that there was reasonable agreement between echocardiographic modalities, there are statistically significant differences in the obtained annular size measures between modalities, with a high degree of interobserver variability.10 3D TEE appears to perform best, though with high rates of postimplant aortic regurgitation,10,11 which suggests significant undersizing. In ex vivo analyses comparing 3D TEE, cardiac-gated CTA, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in measuring the known dimensions of a calcified ring, 3D TEE consistently had the lowest accuracy and largest variability.12 Furthermore, 3D TEE has been shown to consistently underestimate annular size when compared to CTA analyses, raising concern for paravalvular leak and valve embolization.13 In the hands of experienced operators, with considerable procedural refinement, 3D TEE measurements closely approximate cardiac-gated CTA,14 though this is neither practical nor largely generalizable. Additionally, in the modern era, in which patients rarely undergo screening or intraprocedural TEE at the time of TAVR, the introduction of an additional invasive procedure is undesirable. Lastly, although cardiac MRI is a highly accurate method for obtaining annular measurements,12 availability and expertise are lacking and, at this time is not a preferred approach.

Thus, we sought to develop a mechanism to facilitate TAVR in patients with advanced CKD, including a sizing algorithm and implantation technique that does not require contrast use. We then describe our initial, single-center experience using this approach in 35 consecutive patients with significant renal impairment.

Methods

Study design and population

All patients in this study were aged >18 years and were treated with transfemoral TAVR for tricuspid, native valve aortic stenosis using SAPIEN 3, SAPIEN Ultra, or SAPIEN Ultra Resilia (all Edwards Lifesciences) at a large academic medical center. Exclusion criteria included TAVR indicated for aortic insufficiency, prior aortic valve replacement requiring valve-in-valve TAVR, TAVR with a prosthesis other than the balloon-expandable platforms listed, and presence of a bicuspid aortic valve.

Our center had early experience in performing TAVR implantation without iodinated contrast in patients with advanced CKD starting in October 2019, and valve sizing was originally performed by expert opinion (J.M.M.) using aortic sinus and sinotubular junction (STJ) diameters from noncontrast chest computed tomography (CT). In an effort to standardize and teach this method, a valve sizing algorithm using these same features was developed and validated using 160 consecutive patients who had undergone standard CTA and TAVR from January 2020 through November 2020; this is described in further detail below. The algorithm was finalized in May 2021 and was subsequently employed for valve sizing in consecutive no-contrast TAVR patients presented in this study, who were treated between May 2021 and April 2024. In total, 35 consecutive patients judged to be at high risk for hemodialysis based on CKD or ongoing AKI underwent noncontrast CT for pre-TAVR evaluation and no-contrast TAVR. During this time, no at-risk patients were treated with TAVR using TEE, MRI, or balloon valvuloplasty-based sizing. Baseline data and procedural outcomes are presented in the results section for these patients as an aggregate group.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Washington, which granted a waiver of informed consent.

Standard and noncontrast pre-TAVR valve sizing

By convention, the current standard pre-TAVR evaluation of the aortic valve complex is typically done with a gated CTA of the chest. This study provides measurements of the aortic valve area and perimeter, 3 aortic sinus (sinus of Valsalva) diameters, and 2 STJ diameters. Aortic diameters on CTA are obtained from cross-sectional planes and from the outer-to-outer wall. For balloon-expandable TAVR, the aortic valve annulus area is the primary value used to determine the prosthesis size. Noncontrast CT of the chest is not able to effectively determine the annular area or perimeter, but can reproducibly measure outer-to-outer aortic sinus diameters and STJ diameters.

Valve sizing algorithm development and validation

To create a reproducible algorithm to predict valve size in patients undergoing pre-TAVR evaluation with noncontrast CT, we first turned to patients who underwent standard CTA. Using a subset of 100 patients who underwent standard CTA pre-TAVR (“training cohort”) where the predicted valve size by area was known, we created a multiple linear regression model for the prediction of valve size using data known to be available on a noncontrast CT. Predictor variables were selected with a 5-fold cross-validation procedure using a backward selection method that included age, sex, 2 orthogonal diameter measurements of the STJ, and 3 diameter measurements of the aortic sinuses. Predictors resulting in the best model fit (highest R2 and lowest root mean squared error) were included. We then fit a multiple linear regression model using these predictors of interest and data from the training cohort with valve size predicted by annular area as the outcome of interest. This model was further simplified to include 1 term that used the average of the STJ and aortic sinus diameters. This system does not predict a true annular diameter and thus oversizing and undersizing relative to a prosthesis size are not germane. The linear regression model provided a predicted valve size by rounding (both up and down) to the nearest available prosthesis size among SAPIEN 3/Ultra/Ultra Resilia valves. For model validation, another 60 consecutive subjects (“validation cohort”) were studied. Model accuracy was measured using a Pearson correlation between predicted valve size using our multiple linear regression model and actual valve size used during TAVR based on the annular area and standard sizing algorithm.

Standard and noncontrast arterial access site evaluation

Access site imaging is also typically done with CT angiography of the abdomen/pelvis, providing specific dimensions of iliac arteries, whereas noncontrast CT abdomen/pelvis can provide estimations of iliac arterial outer diameter based on the extent and distribution of calcification. In concerning scenarios, intravascular ultrasound of the iliofemoral vessels can be used.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Among patients who underwent no-contrast TAVR, patient demographic data, the aortic complex dimensions, laboratory data, and procedural outcomes were obtained via retrospective chart review of electronic medical records.

All data analyses were carried out using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC) with the exception of the creation of the statistical algorithm which was carried out using R (Integrated Development Environment for R. RStudio, PBC). Continuous data are shown as mean ± SD with skewed variables reported as median and IQR. For categorical variables, counts and percentages are reported. Pearson correlation coefficient and kappa statistic were calculated to evaluate the strength of association between the predicted valve size from our algorithms and the actual valve size used.

When comparing algorithm-predicted valve size with actual valve size used in the training cohort, the algorithm had a Pearson R value of 0.81 and kappa statistic of 0.63 (76% agreement, expected agreement 34.4%), undersizing 10/100 (10%) valves and oversizing 15/100 (15%) valves (Figure 1). All incorrectly predicted valve sizes were within 1 valve size of the actual one used. In the validation cohort, the algorithm had an R value of 0.76 and a kappa statistic of 0.66 (78.3% agreement, expected agreement 36.3%), undersizing 5/60 (8.3%) valves and oversizing 8/60 (13.3%) valves. Noncontrast TAVR implantation methods

The coplanar view was established using a 5F pigtail catheter in the right or noncoronary cusp and a J-tipped wire simultaneously advanced in series to the remaining cusp nadirs. Adjustments to the image intensifier angle were made to establish the coplanar view, which was subsequently modified slightly to remove the parallax from the prosthesis once it was in position across the annulus if required (Figure 2). Valve deployment was performed without aortography, to a more traditional depth to ensure stability. The pigtail catheter was kept in the noncoronary cusp during deployment to provide a reference depth. The valve was assessed by TTE following deployment. Noncontrast TAVR results

Following completion of the training and validation cohorts, a case series of 35 total patients underwent TAVR with noncontrast CT valve sizing only and no-contrast implantation. As illustrated in Table 2, 9 patients had any paravalvular leak (8 trace, 1 mild) after the procedure, and 4 patients suffered complete heart block requiring permanent pacemaker placement. There were no pericardial effusions. There were no difficulties delivering the valve transfemorally and there were no access site complications. One patient suffered a stroke during hospitalization which was managed medically with largely resolved clinical deficits. Two of the 35 patients required hemodialysis within 1 week following TAVR (one with significant preprocedural CKD of a previously transplanted kidney and the other with severe presenting cardiogenic shock and oliguric AKI) and 9 patients had a decrease in eGFR ≥2 mL/min/1.73 m2 24 hours after the procedure. However, this change was minuscule, with an average decrease in eGFR in these 9 patients of 2.3 ± 1.6 mL/min/1.73 m2, and there was no clinically significant difference between preprocedure and postprocedure creatinine and eGFR.

Discussion

In the above study, we describe a novel protocol for evaluation and valve implantation without the use of iodinated contrast in TAVR patients at high risk for contrast-induced AKI. To that end, we first derived an algorithm for TAVR valve sizing using dimensions available from a noncontrast CT, and validated it using a cohort of patients who had previously undergone TAVR with standard CT angiographic preprocedural evaluation. We then highlight a case series of patients who underwent evaluation using the noncontrast CT imaging algorithm and valve implantation with no contrast as a proof-of-concept demonstration.

In our study, when compared with patients whose prior TAVR size had been evaluated with traditional CT angiography and aortic valve area, the proposed valve sizing algorithm had a correlation of 0.81 in the development cohort and 0.76 in the validation cohort, undersizing 9.4% of patients in total and oversizing 14.4%. The implications of undersizing range from paravalvular leak to valve embolization, and those of oversizing range from progressive degrees of heart block to aortic annular rupture. However, in our proof-of-concept cohort, there were excellent clinical outcomes. Though there was an 11% pacemaker rate in our clinical cohort, there is no clear reference basis for the pacemaker rate in a population of TAVR patients with such advanced kidney dysfunction. Furthermore, this was an extreme-risk cohort in whom over 50% required inpatient TAVR performed emergently. Consistent with this urgency, the cohort was elderly, with a high symptom burden including 34% presenting with NYHA class IV symptoms and 15% demonstrating cardiogenic shock at admission. Renal impairment was significant with a baseline creatinine of 2.8 mg/dL. Despite this extreme risk, and potential concerns around undersizing or oversizing a prosthesis based on the derived calculation, there were no deaths, and the average length of stay was just over 3 days and no patients had more than trace (8) or mild (1) paravalvular leaking, with no cases of valve embolization or second valve implantation. This suggests that no-contrast TAVR is a potentially safe and viable option for patients with CKD despite this cohort’s complexity.

Other studies have also attempted to reduce or eliminate contrast in patients with CKD undergoing TAVR. Prior protocols have been developed to reduce CT contrast in pre-TAVR evaluation, focusing on improved dual-energy CT technology that requires less contrast (20-30 mL compared to 60-100 mL) for similar image quality,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 although one study showed 20% interobserver prosthesis size disagreement in patients receiving low-dose contrast in CT compared with 0% disagreement in patients evaluated with the standard dose.20 Other studies aiming to eliminate CT contrast altogether have used cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) for annulus size evaluation with good success, demonstrating near near-perfect correlation of annulus sizing and resultant valve size selection between CMR and CTA.21,22 However, CMR is often more logistically challenging to obtain than CT for routine evaluation and it is not uncommon for patients with aortic stenosis to have preexisting pacemakers, which may be incompatible with or introduce artifacts on CMR images. Study duration for MRI is also prolonged and is less likely to be tolerable for a population such as ours with advanced symptoms or shock. Despite these issues, given its overall accuracy, in situations of hemodynamic stability and local availability and expertise, CMR would be a reasonable option for valve sizing.

Echocardiography has also previously been used for valve sizing. Prior studies have shown that TTE correlates well with 3D-TEE in annular sizing (P < .01 in 1 study of 149 patients) but is thought to be subject to a higher degree of error as compared to 3D-TEE13,23; thus, TEE is the preferred option between these 2 echo modalities. Nevertheless, in addition to requiring general anesthesia, TEE also appears to be inferior to CT evaluation for valve sizing: 2 prior studies demonstrated a moderate to high correlation between TEE and CTA in terms of aortic annulus diameter (R = 0.69 and R = 0.69), perimeter (R = 0.70 and R = 0.72), and area (R = 0.67 and R = 0.69), but TEE-derived measurements are consistently smaller than those obtained with CTA.13,24 Furthermore, annular sizing with 3D-TEE is increasingly uncommon in the modern era and thus may be less consistently performed now than it was prior to the broad adoption of the “minimalist approach” to TAVR. Our study demonstrates that TAVR valve sizing using noncontrast CT has a stronger correlation with CTA results than TEE did in prior studies, and, unlike TEE, does not require additional invasive procedures. Although not performed in this analysis, a head-to-head comparator of noncontrast CT versus 3D TEE for annular sizing could be informative, and, in situations with borderline measurements or poor CT imaging, the additive use of TEE may hold value in select cases.

Another group has described an approach to noncontrast TAVR evaluation and implantation: Maffeo et al,25 recently described a protocol for contrast-free pre-TAVR screening CT scan and valve implantation in 12 patients. Their algorithm used valve cusp insertion calcifications as a reference for postimage processing to identify the valve annular plane, yielding measurements including perimeter, area, and maximum and minimum diameter of the annulus; they then used these measurements to derive an implant projection to be used in the catheterization laboratory. For patients whose TAVR size choice was borderline, the valve area was evaluated with TEE. Outcomes were overall positive, with 4/12 patients having trace and 2/12 having mild aortic regurgitation, and 2/12 patients requiring permanent pacemaker implantation after TAVR implant. Although this study demonstrated center-specific feasibility, broader applicability would need to be determined. In addition, their proposed method relies on relatively specific calcification distribution patterns for optimal outcomes. We believe our algorithm outlined above is more accessible and generally feasible for the majority of TAVR centers to employ.

Our study does have several limitations. The protocol was developed at a high-volume TAVR institution with significant expert experience. Relatedly, aortic valve dimensions in noncontrast CT were obtained by radiologists with significant expertise in cardiac CT, and radiology aptitude in obtaining these measurements may not be present at all centers. The algorithm was designed specifically for balloon-expandable intraaortic prostheses and is not validated for self-expanding supraannular prostheses. Additionally, certain patient subsets, such as patients with bicuspid aortic stenosis, were excluded. These features introduce selection bias and limit broad generalizability to all patients and all valve types at this time. Lastly, although we demonstrate the feasibility of the method in a relatively small number of patients (though the largest cohort to date), validation with a larger, multicenter registry would be needed to better understand the efficacy and outcomes of this technique.

Conclusions

We present a novel algorithm for TAVR valve sizing and implantation that does not require the use of iodinated contrast. Using this derived algorithm, we demonstrate the feasibility of performing TAVR with no contrast in a preliminary cohort. This simplistic, reproducible algorithm for pre-TAVR valve sizing and contrast-free valve implantation technique holds promise for broader adoption and safe, effective TAVR in high-risk patients with CKD or other contraindications to iodinated contrast.
