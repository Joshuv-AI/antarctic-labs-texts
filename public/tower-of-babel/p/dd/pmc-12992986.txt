Real-time drug release monitoring from acoustically responsive scaffolds
Authors: Sander Spiekhout, Bachir A Abeid, Jonathan B Estrada, J Brian Fowlkes, Mario L Fabiilli, Mitra Aliabouzar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcoustically responsive scaffolds (ARSs), composite hydrogels containing phase-shift droplets that are activated by ultrasound, enable on-demand drug delivery with spatiotemporal precision. Yet, real-time monitoring of drug release from ARSs remains limited. Here, we studied the dynamics of the ultrasound-based activation mechanism, acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV), in fibrin-based ARSs containing perfluorohexane droplets. We investigated how initial droplet concentration, acoustic pressure, and burst number affect droplet dynamics, bubble cloud evolution, acoustic emissions, and drug release efficiency. Optical imaging, at 5 million frames per second (Mfps) and 50 fps, revealed that ADV-induced bubble cloud morphologies were concentration dependent. At high concentrations and pressures, bubble clouds expanded significantly beyond the ultrasound focal region by up to 300%. ADV generated distinct low-frequency (LF) emissions that progressively decreased over repeated bursts by ≈30 dB, indicating a reduction in the number of vaporized droplets within the focal region. The burst number at which LF emissions plateaued (e.g., 51 bursts at 0.1% (v/v), 6.2 MPa) correlated with the burst number at which payload release reached its maximum value (57 bursts), demonstrating that LF emissions provide real-time, non-invasive feedback on ADV-mediated drug delivery. These results establish a direct correlation between LF emissions and ADV, and underscore their potential for real-time monitoring of drug release in ARSs.Keywords: Acoustic droplet vaporization, Drug release, Acoustic monitoring, Acoustically responsive scaffolds, Phase-shift droplets

Introduction

Up to 90% of clinical trials fail to achieve therapeutic efficacy, primarily due to unmanageable systemic toxicity, limited target specificity, and insufficient drug accumulation at disease sites [1]. Targeted and controlled drug delivery strategies address these limitations by enhancing treatment precision, minimizing off-target effects, and potentially salvaging promising therapeutics with limited half-lives and target specificity. A widely adopted approach for localized drug delivery is to incorporate therapeutic agents into biocompatible hydrogel matrices implanted directly at the intended treatment site.

The therapeutic advantages of hydrogel-based drug delivery systems are substantial. By slowing down drug release over hours to weeks, higher initial drug doses can be administrated and fewer additional doses are required. This approach is particularly beneficial for chronic conditions requiring long-term therapy, for anatomically challenging targets with poor systemic accessibility, and for post-surgical applications where prolonged local drug exposure can prevent complications [2], [3]. The release kinetics of conventional hydrogels, however, remain characterized by a high initial release rate that slows down over time. The release rate is primarily governed by passive mechanisms such as molecular diffusion and matrix degradation with limited dynamic spatiotemporal control after implantation [4]. This is particularly limiting for drugs with narrow therapeutic windows, where the gap between the minimum effective dose and toxic dose is small, such as human growth factors (e.g., VEGF, BMP-2) or chemotherapeutics [5]. Additionally, these conventional hydrogel-based drug release platforms lack integrated mechanisms for real-time feedback on the timing, location, and dosage of drug release—information that would be invaluable for optimizing therapeutic outcome. A delivery strategy that combines the inherent advantages of hydrogel-based depots with externally triggered on-demand control and real-time monitoring capabilities would therefore represent a significant advancement in precision medicine.

Externally triggered, stimuli-responsive hydrogels address these limitations by enabling dynamic, user-defined control over therapeutic release profiles [6]. Various external stimuli have been investigated, including light, temperature, or magnetic fields [7]; however, their clinical translation has been hindered by shallow tissue penetration and poor spatial precision [8]. Focused ultrasound presents a particularly attractive external stimulus since it is non-invasive, penetrates deeply into tissue (up to ≈10 cm), and offers precise spatial and temporal modulation [9], [10]. Ultrasound-triggered drug delivery systems harness acoustic energy to generate localized thermal or mechanical effects that modulate payload release. Microbubble-assisted drug delivery systems have also been reported for on-demand ultrasound-triggered release from hydrogel carriers. For example, Yamakawa et al. demonstrated that calcium alginate microspherical carriers encapsulating lipid-coated microbubbles, used as acoustic vibration enhancers, alongside fluorescent silica nanoparticles released drugs in response to ultrasound [11]. The combined approach yielded a release ratio approximately 4.5 times higher than spontaneous release in disc-shaped hydrogels, and microspherical carriers showed a ≈13-fold greater improvement over the disc-shaped format. However, diffusion-based drug delivery systems and microbubble-embedded hydrogels may face limitations for applications requiring implantation days to weeks prior to therapeutic use, as the drug payload may prematurely diffuse out of the carrier and microbubbles may dissolve within hours under physiological conditions, compromising their acoustic responsiveness over longer time [12]. A recently emerging approach integrates ultrasound-sensitive phase-shift droplets into hydrogels, creating a smart platform termed acoustically responsive scaffolds (ARSs) [13]. These droplets, composed of liquid perfluorocarbon (PFC) cores stabilized by lipid or polymer shells, can encapsulate aqueous or lipophilic therapeutic payloads within their core. Importantly, these droplets remain stable under physiological conditions. Prior studies have shown that droplets embedded within hydrogels and implanted in vivo remain stable for up to four weeks [14], [15]. Upon exposure to focused ultrasound at pressures exceeding their vaporization threshold, droplets undergo a rapid liquid-to-gas phase transition through a non-thermal process termed acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV) [16]. The volume expansion during ADV is substantial (≈125-fold increase) and the associated mechanical forces disperse the encapsulated payload from the droplet into the surrounding environment [13], [17], [18]. Unlike conventional hydrogels that exhibit fixed release profiles, release from ARSs is highly tunable through acoustic parameters (e.g., frequency, pressure) and droplet formulation properties (e.g., size, PFC composition).

ARSs have been explored in diverse pre-clinical applications, including pro-angiogenic growth factor delivery for ischemic tissue regeneration [14], osteogenesis facilitated by bubble-mediated matrix compaction [19], modulation of oncogenic signaling in cancer models [20], spatiotemporal control of myofibroblast differentiation [21], probing of local tissue mechanical properties using ADV-generated bubbles [22], and on-demand generation of microporosity within engineered biomaterials [23]. To date, however, most ARS studies have focused on maximizing therapeutic or mechanical effects by employing relatively high acoustic pressures and prolonged insonation, rather than leveraging the acoustic feedback associated with droplet vaporization. While ultrasound-triggered drug delivery from ARSs has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in preclinical models, including promotion of angiogenesis via controlled bFGF release in subcutaneous and ischemic tissue models [14], [24], these studies lacked real-time feedback mechanisms to monitor vaporization efficiency and guide therapeutic delivery. The development of acoustic monitoring techniques that correlate ADV activity with drug release represents a critical step toward clinical translation.

The success of ARS-based therapies depends critically on precise control of the vaporization process. To achieve this, ARSs are typically loaded with shelled, high-boiling point PFC liquid droplets (e.g. perfluorohexane, bulk boiling point 56 ∘C), which remain stable and do not vaporize in the absence of ultrasound. These droplets are considerably larger than the nanodroplets commonly used for ultrasound imaging (≈3-12μm compared to ≈100–500 nm) to offset the higher vaporization threshold associated with high-boiling PFC liquids [25]. To ensure a uniform activation threshold and predictable vaporization responses, droplets are manufactured with narrow size distributions, typically using microfluidic techniques [13], [26], [27]. Once vaporized by ultrasound, the generated bubbles within ARSs exhibit different behaviors depending on the acoustic parameters, PFC properties, and the surrounding environment. The bubbles may persist within an ARS as stable bubbles that continue to grow due to passive diffusion, recondense back to liquid droplets, or fragment into smaller PFC remnants [28]. Tunable post-vaporization dynamics enable unprecedented control over payload release kinetics, ranging from rapid bolus delivery to more gradual, sustained release [28], [29], [30].

Several methods exist for measuring drug release from hydrogels in vivo, including SPECT/CT imaging of radiolabeled drugs [31], and fluorescence-based monitoring approaches [32], [33]. However, the high cost, limited sensitivity, and in the case of nuclear imaging, radiation exposure restrict their clinical applicability. A modality that integrates therapy with real-time monitoring would therefore offer a significant advantage by improving treatment efficacy and safety. Both conventional microbubbles and phase-shift droplets are particularly appealing for theranostic applications because they combine advantages of minimally-invasive, localized, on-demand drug delivery with detectable acoustic emissions. In microbubble-mediated ultrasound therapy, nonlinear scattering and broadband emissions have been extensively studied for cavitation monitoring and safety assessment [34], [35]. Passive cavitation detection has been widely employed to monitor cavitation activity during ultrasound therapies, enabling real-time distinction between stable cavitation (characterized by (sub-)harmonic emissions) and inertial cavitation (characterized by broadband emissions) [36], [37], [38]. While acoustic monitoring is available for microbubble-based applications [35], [38], their adaptation to ADV-based applications requires understanding of the unique acoustic emissions associated with vaporization dynamics.

Optical and acoustic characterization of phase-shift droplets have been studied extensively. Optical investigations of ADV has focused primarily on single-droplet vaporization using ultrafast imaging, yielding key mechanistic insights into nucleation, bubble growth, oscillations, and payload release [17], [18], [39], [40], [41]. These studies typically use isolated micron-scale droplets and small fields of view and therefore do not capture the collective, cloud-scale dynamics that emerge at therapeutically relevant droplet concentrations. Expanding optical investigations to encompass the full ultrasound focal volume is needed to better understand ADV behavior under clinically applicable conditions. Additionally, acoustic characterization has been conducted predominantly to define vaporization and inertial cavitation thresholds of the generated bubbles as functions of acoustic parameters and droplet formulation [42]. Acoustic emissions generated in ARSs, where high-boiling point PFCs, high droplet concentrations, and large droplet sizes are used, remain largely understudied. Identifying ADV-specific emissions could enable real-time monitoring of drug release and pave the way for patient-specific dosing in theranostic application of ARSs.

We recently characterized the fundamental vaporization dynamics and initial payload release from single phase-shift droplets using ultra-high-speed brightfield and fluorescence imaging [18]. Building on that single-droplet foundation, the present study extends the investigation to ADV bubble cloud dynamics at clinically relevant concentrations, quantifies bulk payload release under repeated ultrasound exposures, and correlates acoustic emissions as a real-time monitoring tool for ADV-guided drug delivery. We investigated the dynamics of ADV-generated bubble clouds and associated acoustic signatures generated from perfluorohexane phase-shift droplets embedded in fibrin-based ARSs. Fibrin was chosen as the hydrogel matrix for its broad range of biomedical applications including wound healing, tissue engineering, and drug delivery [43] as its known mechanical behavior [44]. Using synchronized optical imaging and passive/active acoustic detection techniques, we studied how driving pressure, burst number, and initial droplet concentration affected bubble cloud evolution, acoustic emissions, and drug release efficiency in ARSs. Our central goal was to correlate payload release with ADV-specific emissions and advance ARSs to a real-time monitored drug delivery platform.

Methods

Preparation of phase-shift droplets and ARSs

Monodisperse, micron-sized phase-shift droplets were generated using a microfluidic flow-focusing device following established protocols [17], [29]. To enable encapsulation of water-soluble compounds, a double emulsion technique was employed [13]. Since PFC liquids are both hydrophobic and lipophobic, therapeutic payloads are typically encapsulated within a secondary aqueous phase, forming a water-in-PFC-in water (W1/PFC/W2) double emulsion structure. Perfluorohexane (PFH; Strem Chemicals, Newburyport, MA, USA) was used as the PFC core. The inner aqueous phase (W1) contained Alexa Fluor 488-labeled dextran (10 kDa; Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Life Technologies) and stabilized with a fluorinated surfactant. Alexa Fluor 488-dextran served as a fluorescent model payload for quantifying drug release. The continuous outer aqueous phase (W2) consisted of Pluronic F68 (50 mg/mL, CAS# 9003-11-6; Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in PBS. To investigate whether payload encapsulation affects the acoustic response and stability of ADV bubbles, PFH phase-shift droplets of three formulations were prepared using the same microfluidic chip: (1) double emulsions with Alexa Fluor 488-dextran in the W1 phase (payload-loaded) (see Fig. 1A), (2) double emulsions with PBS in the W1 phase (blank double emulsion), and (3) single-phase PFH droplets. To generate droplets with a nominal diameter of 6μm, flow rates were maintained at 1 μL/min for the inner channel (W1/PFC) and 10 μL/min for the outer continuous phase (W2). Droplet size distribution and concentration were measured using a Coulter Counter (30μm aperture; Beckman Coulter, CA, USA), yielding an average diameter of 6 ± 1μm and a concentration of 5.7 × 109 droplets/mL.

Sequential magnifications from left to right show droplets distributed within the fibrin matrix, payload distribution within a double emulsion droplet, and the nanoscale fibrin network structure. (B) Schematics of the experimental setup for optical-acoustical characterization and payload release quantification. A 2.5 MHz focused transducer insonified ARSs in a customized cuvette. Bubble cloud dynamics were captured via optical imaging, while acoustic emissions were recorded using a low-frequency hydrophone as well as the transducer in pulse-receive mode (P/R). (C) For payload-release experiments, a multi-well sample holder was used under similar acoustic exposure conditions.

ARSs were formulated by mixing bovine fibrinogen (10 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), dissolved in FluoroBrite Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Life Technologies), with recombinant human thrombin (2 U/mL; Recothrom, Baxter, Deerfield, IL, USA) and phase-shift droplets. To investigate the effect of initial droplet concentration, ARSs were prepared with droplet concentrations ranging from 0.005 to 0.5% (v/v), while hydrogels without droplets (0% (v/v)) served as controls. To visualize the fibrin network structure, fibrin hydrogels were prepared using Alexa Fluor 647-labeled fibrinogen (Thermo Fisher) and imaged by super-resolution confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 980 with Airyscan 2) (see Fig. 1A).

Optical-acoustical experimental setup

Experiments were conducted in a temperature-controlled water tank equipped with a motorized three-axis positioning system (AIMS III hydrophone scanning system, ONDA, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), as shown schematically in Fig. 1B. The tank was filled with degassed deionized water maintained at 37 ∘C. Fibrin hydrogels and ARSs were polymerized directly in optically transparent cuvettes (dimensions: 10 ×10 ×50 mm). The cuvettes were customized to provide acoustic access for ultrasound propagation by replacing two opposing faces with acoustically transparent membranes (Tegaderm, 3M Health Care, St. Paul, MN, USA) (attenuation coefficient per membrane: 0.12 ± 0.07 dB.MHz -1 [45]). The cuvette was confocally aligned with the axial focus of the transducer using the positioning system and pulse–echo technique. A single-element focused transducer (2.5 MHz, Model H-108, f-number: 0.8, radius of curvature: 50 mm; Sonic Concepts, WA, USA) was mounted inside the tank. Acoustic excitation consisted of 2.5 MHz, 6-cycle bursts generated by an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG1, 33210A, Agilent) and amplified using a radiofrequency amplifier (240L, Electronics & Innovation, Rochester, NY, USA). The acoustic field and focal pressure of the transducer were characterized in the water tank using a custom fiber-optic hydrophone (fiber diameter: 105μm, sensitivity: 16.6 mV/MPa [46]) and AIMS III scanning system.

ADV experiments consisted of up to 1000 sequential bursts delivered to the same location within the hydrogel at a pulse repetition frequency of 50 Hz. For a subset of experiments, temperature was monitored during ADV using a needle-type thermocouple (Type K, 0.6 mm diameter) connected to a data logger (TC-08, Pico Technology, Cambridge, UK) with 0.025 ∘C resolution. The thermocouple needle was inserted into ARSs orthogonally to the acoustic beam path and positioned 1 mm lateral to the transducer focus. Temperature was continuously recorded throughout ultrasound exposure (1000 bursts at 50 Hz PRF). Each cuvette was used for a maximum of 8 measurements, with a new spot selected for each exposure to avoid overlap with previously formed bubble clouds (5 mm from the previous exposure). ADV-generated bubble clouds were imaged using a camera (Model Chameleon 3 USB3 FLIR, Oregon, USA) equipped with a macro lens (100 mm, f/2.8), positioned to capture axial–lateral views of the focal region. Backlight illumination was provided by a continuous light source. One frame was acquired prior to insonation, followed by one frame per transmitted burst (1001 frames total, 50 frames per second).

A second arbitrary waveform generator (AWG2, 33210A, Agilent) synchronized acoustic transmission, optical recording, and data acquisition on a digitizing oscilloscope (15 bit resolution, 125 MHz sampling rate, Picoscope 5242 A). Acoustic emissions were recorded using two detection methods: (1) a low-frequency hydrophone (1089D, ITC, bandwidth: 100–400 kHz) with 20 dB amplification to capture sub-MHz emissions, and (2) the transmit transducer operated in pulse–receive (P/R) mode through a triplexer (Ritec Inc., Warwick, RI USA) to capture higher frequency emissions. To better understand concentration dependent ADV behavior over repeated excitations, vaporization dynamics were recorded at 5 million frames per second (Mfps) using an ultra-high-speed camera (HPV-X2; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a pulsed laser illumination source (400 W, 640 nm; Cavitar Ltd., Tampere, Finland) in a custom integrated optical setup that integrates a focused transducer, as described previously [18]. Droplets (0.005% and 0.05% (v/v)) were exposed to the same 6 cycle, 2.5 MHz ultrasound pulses at 6.2 MPa and 20 total recordings were captured at 30 s intervals. Bubble radii were isolated from the optical recordings using a custom MATLAB script.

Four sets of experiments were performed. First, acoustic emissions from ADV-generated bubbles were measured at peak negative pressures of 2.2, 3.3, 4.4, 5.2, 6.2, 6.6 MPa. For each pressure, 1000 bursts were delivered to a single focal spot in hydrogels containing three initial droplet concentrations of 0% (control), 0.05%, or 0.1% (v/v). Second, bubble cloud dynamics and acoustic emissions were characterized at initial droplet concentrations of 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5% (v/v). Exposures were performed at two peak negative pressures of 4.4 MPa and 6.2 MPa with up to 1000 bursts per exposure. Third, hydrogels with 0.1% (v/v) droplets were insonified with 1, 10, 100, or 1000 bursts at 6.2 MPa. After the final ultrasound burst in each case, optical imaging continued for the remainder of the 1001-frame sequence to track post-ultrasound bubble cloud evolution due to passive diffusion. Fourth, effects of burst number and initial droplet concentration on payload release efficiency were assessed as described in the following subsection.

Quantification of payload release efficiency

To quantify payload release efficiency within the generated bubble cloud, the experimental setup shown in Fig. 1C was used. A custom 3D-printed (Form 3L, Formlabs, Boston, USA) rectangular plate with a 5 × 9 array of cylindrical wells was used to hold the hydrogels. The hydrogels were polymerized directly inside the wells, which were completely filled (inner diameter: 6 mm, depth: 7 mm). The dimensions were designed to exceed the maximum lateral and axial extent of the ultrasound focal volume at the highest applied pressure (6.2 MPa), such that reflections were avoided. Prior to use, the sample holder was treated with bovine serum albumin (CAS# 9048-46-8, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min to prevent fibrin adhesion to the well. Experiments were performed at three droplet concentrations (0.01, 0.1 and 0.5% (v/v)), with each sample exposed to 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 200, 500, or 1000 ultrasound bursts at 6.2 MPa. Following insonation, hydrogels were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and digested in 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Life Technologies) overnight in the fridge to ensure complete dissolution of the fibrin matrix. Following enzymatic digestion, samples were centrifuged at 5000×g for 1 min to pellet unvaporized droplets, and the supernatant was collected for fluorescence analysis.

Release of Alexa Fluor 488-labeled dextran was quantified by measuring fluorescence emission at 525 nm following excitation at 495 nm using a fluorometer (Molecular Devices Spectramax M2e, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The percentage of payload release was calculated by a mass balance relative to the initial loading, as described previously [47]. Control samples (0 bursts) underwent identical sample preparation, trypsin digestion, and fluorescence quantification to establish baseline payload release in the absence of ultrasound exposure.

Since cylindrical hydrogel samples were considerably larger than the treated regions, the measured release represents an underestimate of the total release achievable under full insonation (e.g., by raster scanning the entire sample). To estimate payload release efficiency within the bubble cloud, measured release values from the plate reader were multiplied by a scaling factor. This scaling factor was determined as the ratio of the total sample volume to the optically estimated bubble cloud volume after 1000 bursts.

Image processing and analysis

Representative optical images of an ADV-generated bubble cloud captured following selected burst numbers are shown in Fig. 2A. This example used an initial droplet concentration of 0.1% (v/v) and was exposed to 6.2 MPa. All image processing and quantitative analysis were performed using custom scripts written in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). First, the frames acquired during ultrasound exposure (frames #2-1001) were subtracted from the pre-exposure frame (frame #1) to remove the background and make the bubble clouds appear bright. Background-subtracted images were then Gaussian filtered (σ = 3) to suppress noise and artifacts outside the sample holder. The bubble clouds were isolated by intensity thresholding the filtered images. For each experimental dataset (comprising 1000 frames from a single exposure sequence), a fixed threshold value was determined by first applying Otsu’s method to the final frame (frame #1001), which typically contained the largest and most well-defined bubble cloud. The threshold for each dataset was set to 30% of the Otsu-determined maximum intensity value to ensure sensitivity to subtle cloud growth at early bursts and to account for variations in opacity and brightness across droplet concentrations. Corresponding binarized bubble clouds are shown in Fig. 2B.

(A) Sequential optical frames showing ADV-generated bubble cloud formation in ARSs containing 0.1% (v/v) droplets exposed to 6.2 MPa, captured after 1, 10, 50, 100, 500 and 1000 bursts. The bubble cloud area was calculated as the total number of pixels in each binary mask multiplied by the squared spatial calibration factor. Cloud dimensions were determined from the cumulative pixel intensity profiles along the axial (parallel to ultrasound propagation) and lateral (perpendicular to ultrasound propagation) directions. The bubble cloud volume was estimated under the assumption of rotational symmetry about the acoustic axis. Volume was quantified by squaring the lateral width of the cloud, integrating it trapezoidally along the axial length of the cloud, and multiplying the result by π/4. The 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of these distributions were extracted to characterize cloud spread and symmetry. The maximum axial length and corresponding lateral widths at the 10th and 90th percentile heights were also recorded. From these eight coordinates the cloud boundary was reconstructed in an octagon for each frame.

Acoustic signal processing and spectral analysis

For high-frequency (HF) emissions recorded by the transducer in P/R mode, reflections from the proximal and distal Tegaderm membranes in the cuvette were identified in the voltage-time traces. A time window was then selected beginning 2μs after the first membrane reflection and ending 2μs before the second membrane reflection, such that signals originating from the focal region were isolated. For low-frequency (LF) signals recorded by the hydrophone, a fixed time window of 41μs was applied to capture the low-frequency emission components. Representative frequency spectra from hydrophone recordings are shown in Fig. 3A–D for burst numbers 1, 5, 50, and 500. Hydrophone spectra revealed strong LF components during the initial bursts (1 and 5), which decreased substantially down to −30 dB by burst 500. To quantify this reduction in LF signal amplitude with increasing burst number, the average spectral amplitude within the 15–900 kHz frequency range was computed for each burst.

Frequency spectra of acoustic emissions recorded during ADV. Low frequency (LF) spectra recorded using a hydrophone are shown for burst numbers (A–D) 1, 5, 50, and 500. High frequency (HF) spectra recorded simultaneously by the transmitting transducer in pulse-receive mode are shown for the same burst numbers (E-H).

HF spectra (Fig. 3E–H) from initial bursts showed a broad spectrum with distinct peaks corresponding to the subharmonic (1.2 MHz) and higher harmonics (3.2 MHz). As the bursts progressed, the frequency content around the fundamental frequency (2.45 MHz) narrowed and the harmonic amplitudes progressively diminished. To identify potential spectral components sensitive to bubble cloud dynamics, eight frequency bands centered at fractional and integer multiples of the driving frequency were analyzed: 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 4/5, 1, 6/5, 7/5, 8/5, and 2 × 2.45 MHz (each 300 kHz wide). These frequency components included the commonly used subharmonic (1/2), fundamental (1) and second harmonic (2), while additional fractional bands captured prominent sidebands around 3.2 MHz and ensured broad spectral coverage without overlap. To determine which harmonic bands were most sensitive to bubble cloud dynamics, averaged amplitudes in each band were logarithmically fit to the optically measured cloud size. Frequency bands centered at 1/2 (subharmonic, 1.25 MHz), 4/5 (2 MHz), 6/5 (3 MHz), and 8/5 (4 MHz) times the driving frequency exhibited the strongest correlations with bubble cloud evolution, i.e., these bands exhibited the strongest coefficient of determination when fitted to their corresponding cloud area evolution. In contrast, the fundamental frequency showed a non-monotonic trend with burst number, while other bands either lacked clear correlation or fell outside the sensitive bandwidth of the transducer. Therefore, the HF amplitudes reported throughout this paper represent the averaged of the spectral amplitudes across the 1/2, 4/5, 6/5 and 8/5 frequency bands relative to the driving frequency.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB. Normality of data distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All datasets passed the normality assumption. Differences across experimental conditions were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. The number of independent replicates (N) is provided in the corresponding figure captions. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. To quantify the rate constant (K) for bubble cloud growth as a function of burst number, cloud area data were fit to a first-order exponential model [48]. The characteristic time constant (1/K) represents the burst number at which the cloud area reached (1−(1/e))≈63% of its maximum value.

To determine the burst number at which the bubble cloud area, acoustic emissions, and payload release reached a plateau, the data were fit with a two-term power model (y = axb + c). The plateau was calculated as the burst number corresponding to where the model reached 80% of its final value, calculated as exp(log(0.2)/b).

Results

Optical ADV bubble dynamics

Low frame rate cloud dimensions and dynamics

The temporal evolution of the ADV-generated bubble cloud area measured at 4.4 MPa is shown in Fig. 4A, and for 6.2 MPa in Fig. 4C. At both pressures, ADV bubble cloud area increased with burst number, and higher initial droplet concentrations consistently produced larger clouds. For example, at 6.2 MPa, the maximum bubble cloud area in fibrin hydrogels containing 0.5% (v/v) droplets was ≈5.9-fold larger (19.4 ± 2.3 mm2) than in gels containing the lowest concentration tested, 0.005% (v/v) droplets (3.3. ± 0.8 mm2). At 4.4 MPa, the maximum ADV cloud area increased by a factor of 5.8 across the same concentration range, from 1.1. ± 0.4 mm2 (0.005% (v/v)) to 6.6 ± 1.4 mm2 (0.5% (v/v)). Comparing between pressures at the highest droplet concentration (0.5% (v/v)), the maximum bubble cloud area at 6.2 MPa was ≈2.9 times larger than at 4.4 MPa. This pressure-dependent increase is consistent with the increase in suprathreshold volume. Cloud growth kinetics (i.e., the rate of growth) also exhibited a strong pressure dependency. At 0.5% (v/v), the characteristic time constant, as defined in the statistical analysis section, was 55 bursts at 4.4 MPa compared to 28 bursts at 6.2 MPa, which indicates that clouds approached their maximum size approximately twice as rapidly at the higher pressure. A summary of maximum cloud areas and corresponding rate constants across all conditions is provided in Fig. A.13. Additionally, bubble clouds appeared visually at earlier burst numbers with higher droplet concentrations. For example, at 6.2 MPa, visible clouds formed after the first burst at 0.5% (v/v) compared to 10 bursts at 0.01% (v/v) (Fig. 4). Inertial cavitation in control hydrogels (0% (v/v) droplets) were observed in 3 out of 6 samples insonified at 6.2 MPa (visible in Fig. 4D) but not in samples insonified at 4.4 MPa (Fig. 4B). Moncion et al. reported an inertial cavitation threshold of 6.5 MPa peak negative pressure at 2.5 MHz in fibrin hydrogels [49].

To understand whether the continued increase in cloud area observed over repeated bursts resulted from new droplet vaporization or from diffusion-driven growth of the bubbles generated earlier, a series of experiments was performed in which ultrasound exposure was limited to 1 (Fig. 5A), 10 (Fig. 5B), 100 (Fig. 5C), or 1000 (Fig. 5D) bursts, all at a fixed droplet concentration of 0.1% (v/v) and a peak negative pressure of 6.2 MPa. Following the final burst (indicated by downward-facing triangles), optical imaging continued for the remainder of the 1001-frame sequence. Following a single burst (Fig. 5A), a small initial cloud size ≤1 mm2 was generated, which continued to expand after ultrasound exposure via passive diffusion. The final area of this single-exposure cloud was ≈8 mm2, approximately 50% of the maximum area obtained in response to 1000 bursts (≈17 mm2, Fig. 5D). After 10 bursts (Fig. 5B), the cloud reached 87% of the maximum area. By 100 bursts (Fig. 5C), the final cloud area was not statistically different from the maximum achieved after 1000 bursts. This indicates that all vaporization events already occurred within the first 100 bursts. Quantitative analysis of post-ultrasound growth kinetics (Fig. A.14) showed that passive diffusion-driven growth is substantially slower than ultrasound-driven growth (K: 0.007 per frame for a single burst vs 0.05 per frame after 1000 bursts). It should be noted that temperature remained at 37.0 ± 0.2 ∘ throughout the entire ultrasound exposure (1000 bursts), with no measurable temperature rise detected (ΔT ≤0.2∘) (Fig. A.15). The absence of thermal effects is attributable to the low duty cycle (0.012%) used in this study.

Discussion

Main observations

In this study, we investigated how ADV-generated bubble clouds in fibrin ARSs are impacted by repeated ultrasound exposures, initial droplet concentration, and acoustic pressure, and whether these bubble dynamics can be monitored acoustically to provide real-time feedback on therapeutic payload release. Our findings reveal distinct concentration-dependent regimes that govern both the physical evolution of ADV bubble clouds and their associated acoustic signatures.

Acoustic emissions recorded during the initial bursts exhibited a strong LF component that progressively decreased in amplitude over repeated exposures as new vaporization events became less frequent. Once no further vaporization occurred, LF amplitude reached a plateau. Notably, the plateau in LF emissions occurred at burst numbers closely matching those where payload release plateaued. In fibrin-only hydrogels without droplets, LF emissions were weak and relatively constant with burst number, even if inertial cavitation was visibly present (Fig. 4D), confirming that the strong LF emissions originated specifically from droplet vaporization rather than cavitation in the hydrogel itself.

Distinct dynamics of cloud formation and acoustic emissions were observed between ARSs with droplet concentrations <0.05% (v/v) and those with higher concentrations, as stable bubbles did not form and acoustic emissions did not plateau at the lower concentration.

Optical ADV bubble cloud dynamics

The growth kinetics, dimensions, and morphology of ADV-generated bubble clouds strongly depended on droplet concentration, acoustic pressure, and burst number; higher concentrations and pressures produced faster growth rates and larger final cloud areas. At 4.4 MPa (≤0.05% (v/v)), bubble clouds remained confined to the suprathreshold focal region even after 1000 bursts at all droplet concentrations, closely matching the −6 dB beam width of the transducer. At 6.2 MPa, however, bubble clouds appeared in triangular geometries and exceeded the suprathreshold focal region. At the highest concentration tested (0.5% (v/v)) and 6.2 MPa, bubble clouds reached maximum areas of ≈18 mm2 after 1000 bursts, approximately 380% of the focal beam area ( 4.7 mm2) (Fig. 4). This expansion occurred predominantly in the lateral direction, with clouds extending well beyond the beam profile laterally while remaining relatively confined axially (Fig. 6).

Xin et al. observed similar concentration-dependent behavior, with bubble cloud shapes transitioning from small elliptical formations at low concentrations to teardrop and triangular morphologies at higher concentrations [51]. This morphological evolution is attributed to acoustic shadowing and scattering from the proximal face of the previously formed bubbles. At high acoustic pressures, nonlinear propagation creates strong compressional fronts where peak positive pressure far exceeds peak negative pressure. When these wave fronts reflect from already-formed bubbles in the focal region, they undergo phase inversion, producing locally high tensile pressures in the proximal region [52]. These reflected tensile waves constructively interfere with the rarefactional phase of subsequent incident bursts, creating zones of amplified negative pressure that nucleate new ADV events toward the transducer. This process continues until the cloud dimensions have extended too far outside the focal beam and the combined acoustic pressures (incident and reflected) become insufficient to nucleate new ADV events. Lo et al. also demonstrated that reflections from pre-existing bubbles allowed ADV to be achieved at approximately half the driving pressure required in the absence of a bubble wall [53].

A critical question is whether the increase in cloud area with repeated bursts is caused by new droplet vaporization or growth of existing bubbles. Fig. 5, which compares the evolution of the bubble cloud following varying bursts, demonstrates no additional increase in area beyond 100 bursts. This indicates that the maximum number of vaporization events occurred within the first 100 bursts, and underscores the contribution of existing bubble growth to the overall increase in cloud area. LF emissions (Fig. 9) and payload release measurements (Fig. 11D) indicated that new vaporization events became negligible after 57 bursts (0.1% (v/v), 6.2 MPa), while optical cloud area continued increasing until 398 bursts before plateauing. This suggests that after 57 bursts, further area expansion arises from growth of already-vaporized droplets rather than from additional vaporization events.

Acoustic emissions during ADV

Strong LF components were consistently detected in ARSs, whereas fibrin-only gels showed no activity. The dominant spectral peak observed around 200 kHz in hydrophone recordings corresponded closely to the natural resonance frequency of bubbles ≈ 30μm in diameter (generated from ≈ 6μm droplets at maximum expansion), consistent with theoretical predictions from the Minnaert equation (217 kHz) [54]. LF emissions were dependent on droplet concentration and driving pressure (Fig. 8). LF emission amplitude decreased with repeated bursts for droplet concentration ≥0.05% (v/v) which we attribute to the diminishing number of new ADV events as fewer droplets remained available in the focus of the transducer due to acoustic shadowing by pre-existing bubbles. Although the number of unvaporized droplets also diminishes, this does not necessarily lead to a decrease in LF signal, as demonstrated by the ARSs with <0.05% (v/v) with continuously strong LF emissions. Therefore, we consider the shadowing effect from ADV-generated bubbles as more significant on the decreasing LF amplitude than the decreasing number of available droplets. Lo et al. also demonstrated experimentally that ADV-generated bubble clouds can effectively shield distal regions from ultrasound exposure, preventing further vaporization events [53]. Numerical simulations by Xin et al. showed that acoustic attenuation in tissue can increase substantially in the presence of bubbles —from ≈0.068 Np/cm in tissue alone, to values ranging from 5 to >90 Np/cm for bubble concentrations of 3.4 × 105-107 bubbles/mL [55]. This shadowing effect prevents subsequent ultrasound bursts from reaching distal droplets, confining further vaporization primarily to the proximal edge of the bubble cloud.

The number of bursts required for the LF amplitude to plateau correlated inversely with droplet concentration: 12 bursts for 0.5% (v/v), 39 for 0.1% (v/v), 78 for 0.05% (v/v), and >1000 for 0.01% (v/v) at 4.4 MPa. This inverse relationship indicates that higher concentrations produce bubble clouds that effectively shadow the incoming acoustic wave more rapidly. As can be seen in Fig. 9, this relationship is not linear due to shadowing, which become more pronounced as droplet density increases.

At concentrations below 0.05% (v/v), ADV generated transient bubbles during ultrasound exposure that did not form a stable cloud. No plateau was observed in LF emissions, which remained nearly constant throughout 1000 bursts with continuous LF emissions. Since no stable bubble cloud was generated (i.e., no acoustic shadowing) at these low concentrations, repeated bursts continued to access and vaporize remaining droplets throughout the focal volume.

In our experiments, HF signals progressively narrowed, with nonlinear components diminishing substantially while the fundamental peak persisted. We attribute this early broadband response to bubble oscillations and radial modulation of the incident wave. The spectral narrowing over time may thus reflect a decreasing number of vaporized droplets, similar to what was observed from the LF signals, and could serve as an indicator of vaporization activity. However, the LF amplitude exhibited greater sensitivity to ADV than HF components. LF amplitudes differed by ≈30 dB between hydrogels containing droplets and control gels, whereas HF signals differed by only ≈3 dB. The enhanced sensitivity of the LF signals to ADV compared to HF components likely reflects the fact that LF emissions originate directly from vaporization and subsequent oscillation of relatively large ADV-generated bubbles, whereas the changes in HF emissions could be due to other bubble activity in the sample such as inertial cavitation.

These findings demonstrate the feasibility of acoustic monitoring as a real-time indicator of ADV activity, which is highly relevant for safety and efficacy in drug delivery applications using ARSs. Acoustic monitoring has been extensively developed for microbubble-based drug delivery applications, where passive cavitation detection or passive acoustic mapping have been used to monitor microbubble cavitation behavior and link it to therapeutic bioeffects both in vitro and in vivo [34]. In these systems, acoustic monitoring exploits the nonlinear oscillations of microbubbles, which generate harmonics and subharmonics of the driving frequency during stable oscillation, or broadband noise during inertial cavitation. These distinct acoustic signatures enable real-time differentiation between cavitation regimes and have been used to optimize therapeutic outcomes [56]. Adapting acoustic monitoring to ADV-based systems, however, requires understanding the fundamentally different emission mechanisms associated with vaporization dynamics. Unlike the continuous oscillatory behavior of microbubbles, ADV involves a rapid liquid-to-gas phase transition that produces transient, low frequency acoustic emissions, which are stronger for larger size droplets. Our approach leverages several unique features of ADV acoustic emissions that distinguish it from conventional microbubble monitoring. The large frequency separation between the activation pulse (2.5 MHz) and the dominant low-frequency (sub-MHz) emissions from vaporization minimizes interference between the driving and detection signals, eliminating the need for complex filtering strategies required in microbubble monitoring. Additionally, low-frequency acoustic emissions experience weaker attenuation in tissue, enabling detection at greater depth with reduced susceptibility to nonlinear distortion compared to the higher-frequency harmonics typically monitored in microbubble applications [57].

Concentration dependent stable bubble formation

At low droplet concentrations (<0.05% (v/v)), no stable bubbles were formed. We speculate that vaporized droplets recondensed or fragmented into bubbles with sub-critical sizes as a result of violent collapse in the absence of neighboring bubbles. The dynamics of isolated bubbles differ fundamentally from those in dense bubble clusters, affecting their likelihood of achieving stable oscillations [58]. Isolated vaporized droplets undergo larger-amplitude expansions and more violent collapses after ultrasound, often fragmenting into multiple smaller daughter droplet/bubble fragments. If the bubbles trapped in these fragments fall below a critical radius, they recondense rapidly [59]. The resulting sub-critical remnants may re-vaporize during subsequent ultrasound bursts, explaining the sustained LF activity observed in the cases without stable cloud formation (<0.05% (v/v)). Classical nucleation theory predicts that a minimum bubble radius is required for inward gas diffusion to overcome the Laplace pressure [60], [61], [62]. For PFH at 37 °C, this critical radius is ≈ 5μm. In contrast, at high droplet concentrations, neighboring bubbles interact through their scattered pressure fields that dampen the expansion and oscillation amplitudes [63] increasing the likelihood of bubbles remaining intact during collapse. Additionally, at high concentrations, the local partial pressure of PFH vapor in the surrounding medium may be elevated by prior vaporization, further stabilizing newly formed bubbles by reducing the local concentration gradient. The high-speed imaging observations in Fig. 7 provide direct visual evidence of concentration-dependent bubble behavior that explains the differences in acoustic emissions between the two regimes. At low concentrations, droplets expand to larger maximum radii and subsequently fragment rather than forming stable bubbles. These fragments undergo repeated vaporization-fragmentation cycles, sustaining low-frequency acoustic emissions across successive bursts. At higher concentrations, more modest expansion amplitudes favor stable bubble formation; these persistent bubbles undergo small-amplitude oscillations rather than fragmentation, and consequently cease to generate low-frequency acoustic emissions — consistent with the acoustic emission patterns reported in Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10.

Payload release efficiency

The maximum cumulative payload release from 0.5% (v/v) droplet samples was only ≈40% greater than from 0.01% (v/v) samples, despite a 50-fold difference in initial droplet content (Fig. 11A). Based on the vaporization efficiency values corrected for cloud volume, only about 31% of droplets within the insonated region were vaporized in 0.5% (v/v) samples, while nearly all droplets in 0.01% (v/v) samples underwent vaporization (Fig. 11B). Higher droplet concentrations lead to stronger shadowing, as described above, reducing the droplet-to-bubble conversion efficiency. This concentration-dependent reduction in ADV efficiency aligns with previous reports [29], [45], [64].

A key finding of our study is that the burst number at which LF acoustic emissions plateaued corresponded closely to the burst number where payload release approached its asymptote (Fig. 11C–E). For example, in 0.1% (v/v) and 0.5% (v/v) ARSs, LF emissions stabilized after approximately 51 and 12 bursts at 6.2 MPa, respectively, values that aligned with the burst numbers at which drug release reached its asymptotic levels (57 and 12 bursts, respectively). This finding suggests that real-time acoustic emissions may serve as a non-invasive surrogate for drug release in ARSs at droplet concentrations of at least 0.05% (v/v). From a translational perspective, monitoring LF emission amplitude could provide real-time feedback to identify when additional ultrasound bursts no longer produce meaningful payload release. When emission levels drop to a stable baseline, the treatment could be terminated or spatially redirected to untreated regions, to optimize efficiency while minimizing unnecessary tissue exposure. For the concentrations tested, the plateau in LF emissions occurred within 5–57 bursts depending on droplet concentrations, corresponding to insonation times of approximately 0.1–1.1 s per focal spot. A real-time feedback system based on the diminishing LF amplitude could enable dynamic, adaptive treatment protocols that optimize exposure based on observed vaporization kinetics. This has the potential to improve both efficacy and safety compared to fixed-parameter protocols that do not account for inter-patient or inter-site variability in droplet distribution or surrounding medium properties.

The 10 kDa fluorescently labeled dextran employed in this study serves as a surrogate for therapeutically relevant growth factors such as bFGF (≈17–18 kDa) and VEGF (≈19–23 kDa), which are commonly used in tissue regeneration applications. Our previous work validated this approach by demonstrating successful delivery of both bFGF and fluorescently labeled dextran from ARSs under similar acoustic parameters [14], [65]. The primary focus of this work was to establish the correlation between ADV-mediated payload release from phase-shift droplets and low-frequency acoustic emissions for real-time monitoring. While post-vaporization diffusion kinetics through the hydrogel matrix will depend on molecular weight and protein-hydrogel interactions, the acoustic emissions characterized here specifically report on the droplet vaporization event and immediate payload release into the surrounding matrix. Future studies will characterize post-vaporization release kinetics for therapeutic agents of varying molecular weights. Furthermore, the 10 mg/mL fibrin gel matrix forms a nanoscale network as confirmed by super-resolution imaging (Fig. 1A) and consistent with established concentration-dependent scaling relationships [66]. This mesh size is on the order of collagen gels at 2.4 mg/mL (≈0.5μm) but substantially larger than alginate gels (0.5%–5%, ≈ 7–22 nm) [67]. Future systematic comparisons across hydrogel platforms would be valuable for determining whether matrix properties influence vaporization dynamics or only subsequent release kinetics.

Limitations and future directions

These findings demonstrate the feasibility of acoustic monitoring as a real-time indicator of ADV activity, which is highly relevant for safety and efficacy in drug delivery applications using ARSs. The cloud volume factor for 0.01% (v/v) ARSs may be overestimated (leading to ADV efficiencies >100%) due to underestimation of cloud volume from optical images. At low droplet concentrations, ADV efficiency values exceeding 100% are attributed to underestimation of bubble cloud volume from optical images. Sparse, highly transparent bubble clouds at this concentration reduce segmentation accuracy using the uniform threshold-based algorithm (see Fig. 4D), leading to overestimation of the cloud volume correction factor. While a single-element hydrophone provided sensitive detection of ADV events, it offered limited spatial information. Future work should incorporate imaging arrays to enable passive acoustic mapping to localize individual vaporization events and guide the treatment.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that LF emissions are real-time indicators of ADV and therefore payload release in ARSs. The close temporal correlation between LF emission plateau and payload release provides a non-invasive method for real-time monitoring of vaporization activity, with potential applications for drug delivery. Two distinct vaporization regimes were identified: at ≥0.05% (v/v), stable bubble clouds formed with LF emissions decreasing over repeated bursts as the number of vaporization events diminished; at <0.05% (v/v), transient bubbles were observed without forming clouds, and LF emissions remained at a constant low level. High droplet concentrations produced larger, laterally expanding clouds with reduced vaporization efficiency due to acoustic shadowing. Collectively, these findings advance the translational potential of ARSs by establishing acoustic monitoring as a viable approach for real-time control of drug delivery.
