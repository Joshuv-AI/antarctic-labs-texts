Metabolomic fingerprint of volatile and non-volatile compounds of Ganoderma vinegar, Astragalus vinegar, and Winter jujube vinegar based on integrated HS-SPME/GC-MS and UPLC-MS/MS technologies
Authors: Hongrui Lv, Shang Guo, Min Zhu, Fang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVinegar is a traditional fermented flavouring, which has been a commercial product for thousands of years. Ganoderma vinegar (GV), Astragalus vinegar (AV), and Winter jujube vinegar (WV) are new functional health vinegars. The study aimed to recognize and compare the volatile compounds (VOCs) and non-volatile compounds (NVCs) in these vinegars based on metabolomic fingerprint. A total of 2417 differentially accumulated metabolites were screened out, which were classified as 8 clustering patterns using K-means method. The relative contents of 1076, 884, 457 metabolites were highest in GV, AV, WV, respectively, indicating GV dominating the comparative profile. Most VOCs were correlated with sweet, followed by fruity, green, woody, floral, herbal, waxy, fresh, spicy, balsamic, nutty, apple. The characteristics of NVCs exhibited that GV was dominant, followed by AV and WV. The study provided reference for investigation of authenticity, quality and origin of vinegars, and contributed to medical treatment of vinegar.Keywords: Vinegar, Volatile compounds, Non-volatile compounds, Metabolomic fingerprint

Introduction

Vinegar is a kind of dietary spice and also beneficial to various ailments in daily life. Moreover, vinegar has been proved to help weight loss, and improve digestion and skin quality, which has become a commercial food product for thousands of years, especially in Asia and Europe (Budak, Aykin, Seydim, Greene, & Guzel-Seydim, 2014). Generally, it could be normally separated as grain vinegar and fruit vinegar on account of different raw materials. And solid-state and liquid-state fermentation are two familiar utilized methods for vinegar producing. In Europe, vinegar is usually produced through liquid fermentation from fruits including grapes, apples, pineapples, persimmons, and tomatoes. In Asia, vinegar is traditionally produced on account of solid-state fermentation cereals utilizing sorghum, rice, and wheat (Zhang et al., 2022). China has been generating and popularizing Chinese traditional cereal vinegars for about 3000 years. Chinese vinegars possess attractive flavor, and also have abundant nutrients and bioactive constituents, which exhibit various health care functions. Shanxi aged vinegar, Baoning vinegar, Zhenjiang aromatic vinegar, and Sichuan Sun vinegar are famous in China (Ho, Lazim, Fazry, Zaki, & Lim, 2017). However, in addition to grain or fruit vinegar, the vinegar adding other kinds of raw materials, such as fungus or traditional Chinese medicine, was rarely reported.

Ganoderma lucidum belongs to fungus, and is homologous for both medicine and food. G. lucidum contains numerous bioactive components such as terpenoids, polysaccharides, polyphenols, sterols, flavonoids, alkaloids, and benzoic acid derivatives, which is appreciated by notable properties, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antiviral, antioxidant, and antidiabetic activities (González-Díaz et al., 2024). G. lucidum also serves as an ingredient to maintain the physicochemical and oxidative stability of food (Vargas-Sánchez et al., 2024). Astragalus as a traditional and valuable Chinese medicine, contains essential amino acids, microelement and minerals, as well as flavonoids, saponins, polysaccharides, alkaloids and other functional substances, which have the effects on regulating blood sugar level, lowering blood pressure, anti-cancer, anti-radiation, anti-aging; etc. (Li, Li, Huang, Zhang, & Zhang, 2024). Therefore, the development of health care products Ganoderma vinegar (GV) and Astragalus vinegar (AV) has great prospects. In the study, another fruit vinegar, Winter jujube vinegar (WV) features deep red color, sweet fragrance, soft acidity, which was also used for comparison.

On account of the different raw materials and complex process of vinegar producing, the component composition of different vinegars was unclear and lacking comparison. Some pivotal problems in vinegar industry have not been solved, such as the production of precipitates or harmful substances. In addition, the biologically functional mechanisms of vinegar are uncertain. Therefore, it is of great concern to reveal the component composition in vinegar to guarantee the quality and provide insights into the health benefits. The constituent profile of vinegar comprises two distinct classes: volatile compounds (VOCs) that mainly mediate olfactory stimulation, and non-volatile compounds (NVCs) which not only play important roles in taste, but also modulate the volatilities of VOCs (Wang et al., 2023).

Metabolomics offer a comprehensive understanding of component composition, which contribute to the deep investigation of chemical compounds in food (Li et al., 2022). Headspace solid-phase microextraction/gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME/GC-MS) and ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS) are both powerful metabolomics tools widely used in several fermented foods, such as soy sauce, wine and tea for identifying the flavor compounds of VOCs and NVCs. Multivariate statistical analysis applies to component analysis and screening different metabolites (Eriksson, Byrne, Johansson, Trygg, & Vikstrom, 2013). Thus, this study aimed to combine metabolomics with multivariate statistical analysis to compare the volatile and non-volatile compounds and characterize the chemical distinguishing features of three vinegars, including Ganoderma vinegar, Astragalus vinegar, and Winter jujube vinegar. The study established metabolomic fingerprint as highly efficient approach to determine different types of vinegars on the basis of GC-MS and UPLC-MS/MS technologies, which is of great significance for advancing functional food engineering and preventive nutrition strategies.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Ganoderma vinegar is brewed from broomcorn bran, Ganoderma, and Daqu. Astragalus vinegar is brewed from broomcorn bran, Astragalus, and Daqu. Winter jujube vinegar is brewed from winter jujube and Daqu. Ganoderma vinegar and Astragalus vinegar were supplied by Taiyuan Ninghua Palace Yi-Yuan-Qing Vinegar Industry Co., Ltd. (Taiyuan, China). Winter jujube vinegar was purchased from Shanxi Qianmeng Agricultural Science and Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Yuncheng, China). Samples were maintained at −80 °C until analysis.

Methanol and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck (Sigma Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Formic acid was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). n-hexane was purchased from CNW (Shanghai Anpu Experimental Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The reagents were all of chromatographic purity.

Sample preparation and extraction

Volatile compounds preparation and extraction

Three times repeated of nine samples were respectively ground into a powder with liquid nitrogen. A 500 mg aliquot was aseptically dispensed into a 20 mL HS vial (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA), pre-charged with NaCl-saturated solution for enzyme activity suppression. The vial was lidded by crimp-top caps linked to TFE‑silicone HS septa (Agilent). During SPME analysis, each sample was equilibrated at 60 °C for 5 min in sealed vial, followed by HS extraction utilizing a DVB/CWR/PDMS fibre (120 μm, Agilent) under condition of 60 °C for 15 min.

After fibre exposure, the VOCs underwent desorption from the fibre through GC injection port (Model 8890; Agilent) under condition of 250 °C for 5 min. VOCs were identified and quantified by utilizing a Model 8890 GC system (Agilent) hyphenated with a 7000D mass spectrometer (Agilent). Chromatographic separation was achieved via 5% phenyl-polymethylsiloxane capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm DB-5MS). A helium carrier gas was employed at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. At the same time, the injector was maintained at a constant temperature of 250 °C. The oven temperature program was initiated at 40 °C with a 3.5 min isothermal hold, followed by a three-stage gradient: ramped at 10 °C/min to 100 °C, ramped at 7 °C/min to 180 °C, and ramped at 25 °C/min to 280 °C. Finally, it was maintained isothermally for 5 min. Electron impact ionization mode was operated at 70 eV to acquire MS data. The operational temperatures were regulated at 150 °C for the quadrupole mass detector, 230 °C for ion source, and 280 °C for the capillary transfer line. The MS system was configured in selected ion monitoring mode to achieve targeted ion acquisition for the qualitative verification and quantitative measurement. All experimental procedures were replicated thrice.

Non-volatile compounds preparation and extraction

Samples were retrieved from the −80 °C ultra-low temperature storage unit, followed by thawing until complete ice-free state, and subsequently subjected to 10 s vortex agitation for achieving homogenization. A 200 μL aliquot was precisely transferred into designated 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, followed by adding an equivalent volume (200 μL) of 70% methanol containing internal standard extraction solvent (maintaining a 1:1 (v/v) sample-to-solvent ratio). Vortex for 15 min at 4 °C and centrifuge at 12000 r/min for 3 min. The clarified supernatant underwent microporous membrane filtration (0.22 μm pore size) and transferred to the chromatographically optimized vials for following LC-MS/MS detection.

The chromatographic separation coupled with mass spectrometric detection was executed on an integrated UPLC-ESI-MS/MS analytical platform, specifically configured with an ExionLC™ AD UPLC module and Applied Biosystems 6500 Q TRAP (SCIEX, China). Chromatographic conditions were established with an SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Agilent), coupled with a mobile phase system composed of solvent A (pure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Samples were measured under optimized gradient elution parameters: initiated with 95% A, and 5% B; 0–9 min, linear transition to 5% A, 95% B; 9–10 min, 5% A, and 95% B; 10–11.1 min, adjusted 95% A, and 5% B; 11.1–14 min, kept 95% A, and 5% B. The mobile phase velocity was maintained at 0.35 mL/min, and the separation chamber was employed at 40 °C, with 2 μL of injection volume. The effluent was directly attached to ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap-MS.

The ESI source was operated under the following optimized parameters: source temperature was maintained at 500 °C to ensure efficient desolvation; ion spray voltage was calibrated at (IS) 5500 V (positive ion mode)/−4500 V (negative ion mode) for effective ionization; ion source gas I, gas II, curtain gas were regulated at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively, to enhance ion transport and desolvation efficiency; the collision-activated dissociation parameters were configured at high to promote sufficient fragmentation. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) acquisition was implemented in triple quadrupole (QQQ) scanning with medium nitrogen collision gas optimization. Declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for each MRM transition was systematically calibrated via further DP and CE optimization. A particular set of MRM transition was managed for each period in terms of the metabolites eluted in this period.

Qualitative and quantitative determination of metabolites

Determination of metabolites on GC-MS platform

A database was independently established on the basis of multi-species, literature, standards and retention index, including the specific retention time (RT), qualitative and quantitative ions in selected ion detection mode. Each metabolite corresponding to 1 quantitative ion and 2–3 qualitative ions. Dynamic acquisition parameters partitioned detection timelines according to predicted elution sequences of target ions. If the RT matches the reference, and the target ions are detected in the sample MS after background subtraction, then, the metabolite is confirmed. To ensure quantification accuracy, quantitative ions were employed in both peak integration and calibration of chromatographic data.

Determination of metabolites on LC-MS platform

Metabolite annotation was performed through the secondary spectrum on the basis of the MWDB metabolite database. Isotopic interference was computationally filtered, specifically excluded repeating signals of K+, Na+, NH4+, and repeating signal originating from other larger molecular substances with higher mass-to-charge ratios.

Quantification of metabolites was conducted in MRM through QQQ MS. In the MRM mode, the initial quadrupole performs mass-selective filtration of the precursor ions specific to the target substance, and preliminarily eliminate interference of ions matching other molecular weight substances. The isolated precursor ions subsequently undergo collision-induced dissociation in the collision cell to generate lots of fragment ions. The required characteristic fragment ion is selected through final quadrupole filtration, and the non-target ions is eliminated to ensure quantification accuracy and repeat reliability. The integration of peak area was conducted on the MS peaks of all substances based on metabolite profiling. The adjustment of integration was conducted on the MS peaks corresponding to identical metabolite across different samples.

Data processing

GC-MS data processing

For the GC-MS platform, raw MS data underwent systematic processing through MassHunter quantitative software. Chromatographic peaks integration and adjustment were conducted through selected quantitative ions. MS peaks of different metabolites were corrected based on RT and peak distribution to ensure qualitative and quantitative accuracy. The total ion current (TIC) of the pooled QC samples generated from GC-MS analysis is displayed in Fig. S1A.

LC-MS data processing

The MRM metabolite detection multimodal maps illustrated the compounds that detected in the sample, and each MS peak in different color indicated a particular detected metabolite. The characteristic ions of each compound are isolated through QQQ, and the signal intensity is acquired in the detector. The MS file of each sample is conducted by MultiQuant software. The chromatographic peaks integration and correction were performed, of which the peak area indicated the relative content of the related compound. Upon completion of chromatographic analysis, all peak area integration datasets were systematically exported and archived. Fig. S1B-C showed the TIC of the mixed QC samples on the LC-MS platform.

Multivariate analysis

Principal component analysis

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was implemented via the prcomp function in the R statistical computing environment (www.r-project.org). Prior to PCA, variables were standardized through unit variance scaling.

Hierarchical cluster analysis and Pearson's correlation coefficients

The hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) of relationships between metabolites or samples was visualized through hierarchical clustering heatmaps with dendrograms, while intergroup pearson's correlation coefficients (PCC) was algorithmically computed by the corfunction in R and visualized as heatmaps. HCA and PCC were conducted through R package ComplexHeatmap. Metabolites signal intensities normalized by unit variance scaling were visualized through a color gradient in HCA.

Differential metabolites selected

For pairwise comparison, differential metabolites were screened by variable importance in the projection (VIP) > 1, absolute Log2 fold change (FC) (|Log2FC| ≥ 1) and the significance of difference P < 0.05. Orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to derive VIP values, and implemented via MetaboAnalystR, accompanied by score and permutation plots. A permutation test with 200 permutations was implemented to prevent overfitting. In addition, we computed P values for each compound by performing t-test.

Results and discussion

Overview of the volatile and non-volatile compounds

A total of 3405 metabolites were annotated in vinegars of GV, AV, and WV according to Metware database, consisting of 1475 VOCs and 1930 NVCs (Table S1). The VOCs comprised of 304 esters, 259 terpenoids, 162 ketones, 150 heterocyclic compounds, 131 alcohols, 83 hydrocarbons, 81 phenols, 79 aldehydes, 61 acids, 49 amines, 39 aromatics, 35 ethers, 24 nitrogen compounds, 10 halogenated hydrocarbons, and 8 sulfur compounds (Fig. 1A). Among the identified NVCs, there were 467 amino acids and derivatives, 307 alkaloids, 190 phenolic acids, 145 flavonoids, 131 terpenoids, 115 organic acids, 106 lipids, 87 lignans and coumarins, 65 nucleotides and derivatives, 18 quinones, 3 tannins, and 1 steroid. In addition, 291 compounds remained unclassified (others) (Fig. 1B).

Multivariate statistical analysis

PCA was implemented to preliminary understand the holistic metabolic variability among the 9 samples within groups. The results showed that QC samples are closely clustered and each group was well separated, indicating good reproducibility. The first two principal components PC1 and PC2 respectively explained 56.70% and 13.96% of the total variance of VOCs (Fig. 2A). In the PCA diagram of NVCs, the first two principal components respectively accounted for 73.39% and 7.69% of the total variance (Fig. 2B). WV was clearly distinguished from GV and AV in PC1. In PC2, GV and AV also discriminated clearly. The biological repeat correlation of samples within group also evaluated via Pearson's correlation coefficient R, the value of which is close to 1, suggesting credible biological duplication in the study (Fig. S2). Hierarchical cluster analysis was performed for the quantitative analysis of entire VOCs and NVCs from GV, AV, WV samples. GV and AV samples clustered together and away from WV samples (Fig. 2C). K-means clustering was utilized for the quantitative analysis of 27 classes of metabolites (Fig. 2D). There was a higher quantity of alkaloids, phenolic acids, lipids and organic acids present in Winter jujube vinegar samples. Higher quantities of amino acids and derivatives, nucleotides and derivatives, lipids, organic acids and aldehyde were found in Ganoderma vinegar. Astragalus vinegar showed similar dominant classes of metabolites with Ganoderma vinegar samples. Volatile and non-volatile compounds metabolic variation in GV, AV, and WV

To screen the different components among three vinegars, the OPLS-DA model was constructed for pairwise comparison. The excellent predictability (Q2) and robust model of fit (R2X, R2Y) of the OPLS-DA models were exhibited as evidenced by comparisons between GV and AV (Q2 = 0.929, R2X = 0.589, R2Y = 1) (Fig. S3 A), WV and AV (Q2 = 0.996, R2X = 0.885, R2Y = 1) (Fig. S3B), as well as WV and GV (Q2 = 0.996, R2X = 0.891, R2Y = 1) (Fig. S3C). GV, AV, and WV samples were separated from each other in OPLS-DA models. A total of 1573, 2394, and 2569 metabolites showed VIP > 1 in comparisons of GV vs. AV, WV vs. AV, and WV vs. GV, respectively (Fig. 3A-C). These metabolites exhibited maximal covariance and correlation displacement along the S-plots, of which could serve as indicators to separate different vinegars. On account of VIP values, the top 20 differentially accumulated metabolites of each comparison group were screened, such as Ganoderic Acid J, 1H-Indene, 2,3-dihydro-4-propyl, and Ganoderenic acid K in GV vs. AV; Ziziphin, N-nornuciferine, and Cyclic 3′,5′-Adenylic acid in WV vs. AV; 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic Acid, N-nornuciferine, and Ganoderenic Acid A in WV vs. GV (Fig. 3D-F).

NVCs metabolite characteristics of the three vinegars of GV, AV, and WV

The discriminatory features in the component composition of non-volatile compounds were visualized in heatmaps. Amino acids and derivatives constitute the majority of NVCs. Among them, 338 metabolites were significantly higher in GV and AV than WV (Fig. S4). Leu-Glu-Thr, S-(2-Carboxypropyl)cysteine, Ile-Gln-Leu, β-N-Methylamino-L-Alanine, Val-Ile-Asp were the top five ranked abundant amino acids and derivatives in GV. S-(2-Carboxypropyl)cysteine is a key compound in the biosynthesis of cycloalliin that is the quantitatively most important sulfur compound in onion (Granroth, Virtanen, Thom, Stoll, & Dumanovi, 1967). S-(2-carboxypropyl)-L-cysteine is a quantitatively predominant organosulfur compounds (OSCs) in urine after black garlic intake by healthy volunteers; and the consumption of black garlic is correlated with decreasing risk of various diseases attributing to OSCs (Moreno-Ortega, Pereira-Caro, Ludwig, Motilva, & Moreno-Rojas, 2023). Ala-Ile-Leu, Ile-Phe, 4-Hydroxyhippurate, Ethyl N-acetyl-L-tyrosinate, Thr-Lys were the top five ranked abundant amino acids and derivatives in AV. Leu-Ile-Ile, Asp-Pro-Ile, Glu-Thr-Leu, L-Cystathionine, Ile-Gly-Ile were the top five ranked abundant amino acids and derivatives in WV. L-Cystathionine is the substrate for cysteine synthesis, which is involved in regulation of intracellular redox balance from bacteria to higher eukaryotes (Lv, Xu, Bo, & Wang, 2021).

Alkaloids contribute significantly to the bitterness quality of food. GV and AV comprised more alkaloids than WV. A total of 173 alkaloids showed relative higher contents in GV and AV than WV (Fig. S5). 1-[1-O-3(3,4-methylenedioxyphenyl)-2E-propenyl]-pyrrolidine, Cyclen, Caffeoylcholine, Strictosamide, Castanospermine were dominant in GV. Castanospermine was able to suppress SARS-CoV-2 (Clarke, Nofchissey, Ye, & Bradfute, 2021). 2;4-Dihydroxyquinoline; Neopterin, 2;3;5;6-Tetramethylpyrazine, Ligustrazine, 2-methylisonicotinic acid, Oxoglaucine* were dominant in AV. 2;3;5;6-Tetramethylpyrazine confers dose-dependent photoreceptor cytoprotection by suppressing endoplasmic reticulum stress (Huang et al., 2021), and improves diet-induced whole-body insulin resistance (Xiang et al., 2020). Oxoglaucine relieves hepatic fibrosis through inhibiting ROS generation (Azamov et al., 2023). Laudanine, 10;16;19-trihydroxy-5-methyl-12-methylidene-7-azaheptacyclo[9.6.2.01;8.05;17.07;16.09;14.014;18]nonadecan-3-one, Denudatine, N-Methylhigenamine*, N-nornuciferine were dominant in WV. N-Nornuciferine is one of the major alkaloids from Nelumbo nucifera leaves (Ye et al., 2018).

Phenolic acids are the main precursors of flavanols, and cooperatively amplify the astringency of food. The phenolic acids proportion was highest in AV, followed by GV and WV (Fig. S6). Helicide, O-Anisic acid (2-Methoxybenzoic acid)*, Phenoxyacetic acid*, tert-butyl (3S)-3,4-dihydroxy-4-[(4-methoxyphenyl)methoxy]butanoate, Sinapinaldehyde were the top five ranked abundant phenolic acids in AV. Phenoxyacetic acid has the fungicidal activity (Taghizadeh et al., 2024). Dibutyl phthalate*, Butyl isobutyl phthalate*, 2;4-Dihydroxybenzoic acid, Vanillic acid methyl ester, Ethyl ferulate were the top five ranked abundant phenolic acids in GV. Butyl isobutyl phthalate is the active compound against α-glucosidase for diabetes treatment (Bu, Liu, Zheng, Guo, & Lin, 2010). 2;4-dihydroxybenzoic acid belongs to food additive, which could prevent human renal dysfunction as a potential novel treatment strategy (Widmeier et al., 2019). Ethyl ferulate has the protective effect in retinal cells and retinal neovascularization against hypoxic injury (Xue et al., 2023). 4-Methoxycinnamic acid, Salicyl Alcohol, Protocatechuic acid xylosyl xyloside, Phenylacetylglycerol 2-glucuronide, Eudesmic acid (3,4,5-trimethoxybenzoic acid) were the top five ranked abundant phenolic acids in WV.

Flavonoids play important roles in taste formation. Flavonoids in GV were more abundant than those in AV and WV (Fig. S7). In particular, Tricin-5,7-O-diglucoside*, Xanthone, Apigenin-8-C-Arabinoside*, Luteolin-6,8-di-C-arabinoside, Quercetin-3-O-(2”-O-arabinosyl)rutinoside were the top five ranked abundant flavonoids in GV. Xanthone has anti-acne potential (Blicharska, Ahmed, Jackson, Rotondo, & Seidel, 2024), and with multiple roles against diabetes (Ke et al., 2024). Blumeatin, 3-(2-Hydroxy-4;6-dimethoxyphenyl)-1-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-1-propanone, Calycosin, 7;8-Dihydroxy-4′-methoxyisoflavone, 3′-Methoxydaidzein were the top five ranked abundant flavonoids in AV. Blumeatin possesses antioxidant potential, and inhibits xanthine oxidase for hyperuricemia (Altunayar-Unsalan & Unsalan, 2024). Calycosin suppresses ferroptosis and ameliorates myocardial injury induced by doxorubicin (Han et al., 2024), and modulates astrocyte hyperreactivity after spinal cord injury (Tang et al., 2025). Quercetin-3-O-rhamnoside(Quercitrin), 4-C-Glucose-1;3;6-trihydroxy-7-methoxyxanthone, Epicatechin, Afzelechin (3;5;7;4’-Tetrahydroxyflavan), Chrysoeriol-6-C-glucoside (Isoscoparin) were the top five ranked abundant flavonoids in WV. Epicatechin mediates beneficial effects on vascular function in humans (Schroeter et al., 2006).

GV has more terpenoids than AV and WV, which is consistent with G. lucidum rich in terpenoids. 64 and 92 terpenoids respectively exhibited relative higher contents in GV than AV and WV (Fig. S8). Ganoderenic acids were the most abundant terpenoids in GV. Ganoderenic acid D-loaded engineered graphene oxide was applied to site-specific treatment of cervical carcinoma (Lu et al., 2023). In HepG2/ADM cells, Ganoderenic acid B originated from G. lucidum could relieve multidrug resistance (Liu et al., 2015). Bruceine D; Brucein D was the most abundant terpenoid in AV. Bruceine D could restrain colorectal cancer tumorigenesis (Yang, Li, Li, Zhu, & Guo, 2024). Jujubasaponin V* and jujubasaponin IV* were abundant in WV. They were firstly isolated from Zizyphus jujuba MILL fresh leaves, and showed antisweet activities on the sense of taste (Yoshikawa, Shimono, & Arihara, 1992).

Organic acids are one of the crucial components acting on food flavor by regulating acidity taste and aroma. GV and AV have relative higher abundance of organic acids than WV (Fig. S9). Mevalonic Acid Glucoside, Phosphoenolpyruvate, Acetoxyacetic acid, β-Hydroxyisovaleric acid, 5-Acetamidopentanoic Acid were the top five ranked abundant organic acids in GV. 2-Hydroxyethylphosphonic acid, L-Tartaric acid, Quinic Acid, L-Malic acid*, Cis-Aconitic acid were the top five ranked abundant organic acids in AV. In rat aorta, L-Tartaric Acid suppresses diminazene-induced vasorelaxation (Amssayef, Bouadid, & Eddouks, 2024). Quinic acid has potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties relieving ulcerative colitis (Ekhtiar, Ghasemi-Dehnoo, Azadegan-Dehkordi, & Bagheri, 2025). Dietary supplementation with L-malic acid significantly reduces drip loss in broiler meat (Sun et al., 2024). 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic Acid, 6-Hydroxyhexanoic acid, 2-Hydroxy-2-methylbutyric acid, 4-Pentenoic acid, trans-2-Butene-1,4-dicarboxylic Acid were the top five ranked abundant organic acids in WV.

Lignans and coumarins concentrations were higher in GV and AV than WV (Fig. S10). 7,8-Dihydroxy-4-phenylcoumarin, Magnolignan D, Daphnoretin, 4’-Demethylpodophyllotoxin, Nortrachelogenin-4-O-glucoside were the top five ranked abundant metabolites in GV. 4′-demethylpodophyllotoxin emerged as a potent anti-cancer compound for colorectal cancer (Liu et al., 2025). Stevenin, Dalbergin, Columbianetin Glucopyranoside, Thamnosmonin, Meranzin hydrate were the top five ranked abundant metabolites in AV. Dalbergin acts as a potential antidiabetic agent (Sutrapu et al., 2024). Meranzin hydrate exerts antidepressant effects via regulating neuronal functions in hippocampus (Wu et al., 2025). 7;8-Dihydro-Buddlenol B(threo), 3;4-Divanillyltetrahydrofuran*, Divanillyltetrahydrofuran*, 3-Methyl-4;8-dihydroxy-3;4-dihydroisocoumarin, Pinoresinol* were the top five ranked abundant metabolites in WV. Pinoresinol has effect on inhibit methamphetamine addiction (Fan et al., 2025).

Lipids partly contribute to mellowness and sweetness. Respectively 48 lipids exhibited relative higher contents in GV and AV than WV (Fig. S11). Octadeca-11E,13E,15Z-trienoic acid, 1-Monomyristin, 2-Hydroxy-4-methyl-3-tridecanoyloxypentanoic acid methyl ester, (9S)-Hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid, LysoPC 18:3 were the top five ranked abundant lipids in GV. LysoPC 18:1(2n isomer), LysoPC 18:1, 9-Hydroperoxy-9Z,11E-Octadecadienoic Acid, 2-hydroxynonanoic acid, 5S-HpEPE were the top five ranked abundant lipids in AV. 1,14-Tetradecanedioic Acid, Palmitaldehyde, 10,16-Dihydroxypalmitic acid, 12-Hydroxydodecanoic acid, 9,16-Dihydroxypalmitic acid were the top five ranked abundant lipids in WV.

Nucleotides and derivatives serve as essential biosynthetic precursors for coenzymes and signaling molecules, also closely related to the umami flavor. GV included the maximum concentrations of nucleotides and their derivatives, followed by AV and WV (Fig. S12). Adenosine 2’-Phosphate, 3′-Adenylic Acid, 5’-Deoxy-5′-(methylthio)adenosine, Uridine 5′-monophosphate*, Pseudouridine 5′-phosphate* were the top five ranked abundant metabolites in GV. 1-Methylxanthine, 5-Ribofuranosylnicotinamide, 5-Methyluridine, 2-Deoxyribose-5′-phosphate*, 5-Aminoimidazole ribonucleotide were the top five ranked abundant metabolites in AV. 1-Methylxanthine could improve cognizance, memory and increase neurotransmitter levels (Jäger et al., 2025). 3′,5’-Cyclic IMP, 2’-Deoxycytidine-5′-monophosphate, Cyclic 3′,5′-Adenylic acid, Guanine, 8-Azaguanine were the top five ranked abundant metabolites in WV.

Conclusions

The metabolomics on the basis of HS-SPME/GC-MS and UPLC-MS/MS technologies was utilized to synthetically analyze and compare the component composition of Ganoderma vinegar, Astragalus vinegar, and Winter jujube vinegar for the first time. A total of 1475 VOCs and 1930 NVCs were identified through metabolomic profiling with the aid of multivariate statistics. PCA exhibited that the volatile and nonvolatile components of the three vinegars were well separated with good reproducibility. A total of 1573, 2394, and 2569 metabolites respectively showed VIP > 1 in comparisons of GV vs. AV, WV vs. AV, and WV vs. GV, of which could serve as biomarkers to trace provenance of different vinegars. The dynamic distribution map of metabolites relative content was created on the basis of the screening criteria (VIP > 1, |Log2FC| ≥ 1, P < 0.05), and 2417 differential metabolites were screened out, of which a total of 308, 2143, and 2215 metabolites were respectively significantly different in the comparisons of GV vs. AV, WV vs. AV, and WV vs. GV. Flavor analysis was conducted based on VOCs. A total of 125 metabolites were closely related to sweet, followed by fruity (119), green (89), woody (67), floral (60), herbal (47), waxy (45), fresh (44), spicy (41), balsamic (37), nutty (36), apple (21), etc. The discriminatory features in the component composition of NVCs suggesting that the differential metabolites in the three kinds of vinegars were more upregulated in GV, followed by AV and WV. The study systematically revealed the composition, flavor, as well as their efficacy characteristics of NVCs in three vinegars. The study offered a reference for the future investigation of authenticity, quality and origin of vinegars, and was conducive to the healthy and sustainable development of vinegar.
