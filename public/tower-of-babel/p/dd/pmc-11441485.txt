Enhanced effects of species richness on resistance and resilience of global tree growth to prolonged drought
Authors: Yun-Hao Bai, Zhiyao Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe effects of species richness on resistance and resilience of tree growth to drought as well as the difference between short-term and prolonged droughts remain uncertain at the global scale, which has important implications for the growth and carbon sequestration of trees. Based on tree ring data, our study reveals the enhanced effects of species richness on the stability of global tree growth to prolonged drought, highlighting the crucial role of species richness in resisting prolonged drought, and confirms the trade-off between drought resistance and resilience of trees globally. These findings provide valuable insights into understanding the effects of diversity on the stability of tree growth in the context of current and future climate change.

Anthropogenic forcing has led to changes in global temperature and precipitation patterns, increasing the frequency, intensity, and duration of drought (1), and this is likely to escalate in the future (2). In response to these increasingly severe droughts, the growth of trees, a crucial pathway for carbon sequestration in natural ecosystems (3), can be severely impacted (4–6). The duration of drought may be critical for affecting tree growth (7, 8). Prolonged drought can often weaken the ability of trees to resist drought (9) as well as reduce their postdrought recovery rates, leading to persistent declines in tree growth (7, 10). As a result, the anticipated rise in the persistence of drought is likely to pose serious challenges to tree growth and vigor.

Species richness is closely linked to ecosystem functioning (11). Communities that have higher species richness are often more insured against severe drought, buffering the impact of drought through resource partitioning, facilitation, and selection effects, thereby stabilizing the ecosystem and its components (12, 13). However, most of this work has been conducted in grasslands (14–16), and there is relatively scant research on how species richness influences the stability of tree growth under drought, mostly at limited spatiotemporal scales (17–19). In forest ecosystems, beyond tree species richness, the understory serves as a vital biodiversity reserve capable of influencing ecosystem processes and functions in various ways (20, 21). Some studies have shown that diverse understory vegetation can also provide facilitation to trees by enhancing rainfall infiltration, reducing soil evaporation, increasing soil nutrient content and soil microbial biomass, and diluting disease transmission risks (22–24). Variation in species richness has been shown to influence the resistance of vegetation to drought in roughly half of forests measured globally (25), largely influenced by the intensity of drought and the climatic water deficit. However, the impact of species richness on the ability of forests to recover after drought (i.e., resilience), has not been investigated, nor has the influence of prolonged drought, heightening the uncertainty surrounding future climate change impacts.

Remote sensing based on vegetation indices has been widely employed to explore the effects of biodiversity on the stability of forests to drought (25, 26). However, vegetation index reflects the response of ecosystem and is also influenced by other processes such as species asynchrony and changes in species composition as well as individual mortality and regeneration, besides individual tree growth (27). In comparison, tree ring width directly reflects the actual growth conditions of trees. Meanwhile, studies have demonstrated that tree ring width is a more sensitive metric of forest stability to drought than vegetation index (28). In this study, we coupled information on both short-term (i.e., a single-year drought) and prolonged (i.e., a consecutive drought with a duration of two or more years) droughts over the past 70+ years together with the standardized ring width index (RWI) from across 4,072 sites distributed globally, based on the International Tree Ring Data Bank (ITRDB). We then calculated the indices of tree resistance and resilience to short-term and prolonged droughts. We used an independent dataset of vascular plant species richness (29) to evaluate the role of species richness in mediating tree resistance and resilience in response to drought after controlling for a number of abiotic and biotic covariates. We further built structural equation models (SEM) for both short-term and prolonged droughts to explore the potential pathways by which species richness influences resistance and resilience. Finally, we explored the impacts of climatic water deficit (CWD) and drought intensity (DI) on the effects of species richness by incorporating interaction terms into models.

We specifically proposed the following hypotheses. 1) Species richness could enhance the resistance and resilience of trees by increasing tree growth during and after drought (H1). 2) The effects of species richness on the resistance and resilience of trees would be stronger under prolonged than short-term drought (H2). 3) Environmental context (i.e., water stress) could moderate the influence of species richness on both resistance and resilience to drought, and the effects of species richness would be intensified in drier conditions (H3).

Results

Spatial Patterns of Drought Resistance and Resilience.

We found that trees in higher latitude regions tended to exhibit stronger resistance to short-term drought, particularly in North America and Europe. However, the resilience showed a roughly opposite pattern, tending to be lower in northern Europe and North America, but higher in drier regions of southern Europe (along Mediterranean coast) and southern North America (Fig. 1 A and D). The patterns for both resistance and resilience under prolonged drought were similar, but the areas with high resistance or low resilience both expanded (Fig. 1 B and E). Overall, tree growth resistance was higher, while resilience was lower (Fig. 1 C and F), as a response to prolonged drought compared to short-term drought.

Effects of Species Richness under Short-Term and Prolonged Droughts.

Multiple regression analysis demonstrated that the resistance of tree growth to either short-term or prolonged droughts was higher in communities with higher species richness after controlling for other variables, but the positive effect of species richness on resistance was stronger when droughts were prolonged (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S1). Meanwhile, species richness weakened the resilience of growth to drought, and the negative effect of species richness was also stronger under prolonged drought (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S1). Further, sequential regression models and analyses based on tree species richness showed similar results (SI Appendix, Table S1 and Fig. S1). Analysis of different taxonomic groups showed that angiosperms dominated the effects of species richness on resistance and resilience of tree growth to drought (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Overall, the explanatory power of species richness on both drought resistance and resilience was the second highest, next to that of climate variables (Fig. 2C).

The SEM revealed that species richness indirectly enhanced resistance by increasing the mean RWI in years with drought (RWId) (Fig. 3 A and C). The positive effect of species richness on RWId was more pronounced in the face of prolonged drought relative to short-term drought, resulting in a stronger positive impact on resistance (Fig. 3 A and C). However, for resilience from drought, the influence of species richness differed following short-term and prolonged droughts (Fig. 3 B and D). With short-term drought, species richness indirectly weakened resilience by positively affecting RWId (Fig. 3B). The positive effect of species richness on resistance increased as the CWD or the DI decreased (i.e., the aridity increased or the degree of drought intensified) in the face of short-term drought (Fig. 4 A and C). Meanwhile, the negative effect of species richness on resilience also intensified as CWD or DI decreased (Fig. 4 B and D). However, the effect of species richness on resistance did not change significantly as CWD decreased under prolonged drought (Fig. 4E) and the trend was even reversed as DI decreased (Fig. 4 G and H).

Discussion

Our results showed that species richness enhanced the resistance of trees to short-term and prolonged droughts, which was consistent with our H1. Diversity enhances the resistance of forests to drought by a number of mechanisms (30). More diverse communities can have more facilitative interactions among species, reducing the net effects of interspecific competition, thereby mitigating the impact of drought stress on tree growth (13, 31, 32). Specifically, complex canopy structures composed of diverse species can improve microclimate conditions (temperature, humidity, etc.) of the understory and increase its diversity (33), which in turn increases soil moisture (20) that can mitigate drought effects. Likewise, belowground, differences in the distribution of root systems in more diverse communities may reduce competition for soil water (34), while some species can also redistribute water from deep soil to upper soil through their roots, thereby increasing water availability (32). At the same time, facilitation brought about by the increased species richness may enhance the availability of soil nutrients and fungal mutualists (35, 36), promote fine root growth (37), and improve the uptake and use efficiency of soil water by trees. More diverse communities may also experience lower damage from herbivores and pathogens (38), allowing them to better withstand drought. All of these are beneficial for mitigating drought stress and enhancing the resistance of trees.

Our finding that the effect of species richness on resistance was even stronger during prolonged drought is consistent with our H2 and the stress-gradient hypothesis (39). That is, when abiotic stresses are higher, the relative importance of facilitation among trees via the mechanisms discussed above should increase, while that of competition should decrease (40). Relative to more favorable environmental conditions (short-term drought), positive complementarity is more common and may have a stronger effect under higher stress (prolonged drought), further enhancing tree growth during drought.

For resilience of tree growth following drought, we found that communities with higher species richness tend to have lower resilience of trees. Compared with short-term drought, species richness not only promoted tree growth during drought, but also reduced the growth of trees following prolonged drought, and its negative effect on resilience was enhanced mainly through this pathway, reflecting a slower recovery rate. There are multiple mechanistic explanations for the negative correlation between species richness and the postdrought growth or recovery rate of trees. First, trees can show a number of trade-offs in their physiological mechanisms for water update and growth that may lead to a trade-off between the resistance and resilience of trees to drought (28, 41, 42). Species richness has the potential to modulate the functional traits of trees (43) and, consequently, may alter the preexisting trade-off relationship, tilting it toward resistance. Second, lower diversity communities have lower growth rates during drought and thus consume fewer resources such as soil nutrients, which are then available for recovery (44, 45). Meanwhile, lower diversity communities may experience higher tree mortality during drought (46), which would increase resource availability and decrease competition after drought (42), allowing survivors to recover more rapidly (47). Conversely, in higher diversity communities, competitive interactions may become more intense (48, 49), reducing postdrought recovery of trees in those communities. All of these mechanisms may become more prominent under long-term stress, either acting independently or in combination to reduce the growth of trees in high-diversity environments after prolonged drought.

The lack of significant effects of species richness on gymnosperms may be attributed to several factors. First, the increase in leaf area and transpiration rate caused by diversity can be detrimental to plant during drought (43, 50, 51). Generally, gymnosperm trees have greater hydraulic safety margins and stronger resistance to embolism, but their stomatal regulation ability is weaker (52), making them more susceptible to the negative effects of increased transpiration rates, than angiosperm trees. Second, local environmental conditions can influence the effects of species richness. The predictive model based on environmental factors indicated that the positive effects of species richness were quite weak or even reversed for ecoregions from boreal forests in the Scandinavian Peninsula, northern Canada, and Siberia (25), which weakened the effects of species richness on the response of gymnosperms to drought globally. Third, overall, the species richness at the gymnosperm sites is lower than that at the angiosperm sites (P < 0.001) and is concentrated at lower levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), making it difficult to observe the effects of diversity.

Our finding that species richness is more likely to enhance the resistance of trees in drier sites or under more severe droughts for short-term drought is consistent with our H3 and previous work based on satellite-derived data (25). When conditions are drier, there can be more positive facilitatory interactions among species according to the stress-gradient hypothesis (39, 53). However, for resilience, we found that the negative effect of species richness was intensified in drier sites or under more severe droughts. This is largely attributable to the opposing effects of species richness on the resistance and resilience of tree growth discussed earlier. However, the effects of species richness endured in drier sites but reversed with higher DI for prolonged drought. That is, under prolonged drought, when drought was more intense, the positive effect of species richness on resistance as well as the negative effect on resilience were both tempered. Here, we suggest that it is possible that as drought stress further intensifies or as multiple aspects (duration and intensity) of drought stress are superimposed, facilitation among species can be reduced or even reverse toward more intense competitive interactions (54). In this situation, species richness will have a weaker promoting effect in more severe droughts, and tree growth will face greater threats, which calls for attention.

In summary, our study used tree ring data obtained from field sampling that can directly reflect tree growth on a global scale to investigate the effects of species richness on the resistance and resilience of trees to drought. Faced with the escalating threat of prolonged drought, we conducted analyses to address the gap in this research area. Our results indicate that species richness increased the resistance of tree growth to drought, and this effect became more pronounced under prolonged drought. These findings highlight the critical role of species richness in resisting long-term drought. At the same time, however, we found that resilience of tree growth following drought was weakened in higher-diversity communities. The adverse effect of species richness on resilience, to some extent, counterbalances its positive effect on resistance, and previous studies solely focusing on resistance may overestimate the role of diversity. In addition, our results suggest that the positive facilitation among species in higher diversity communities is tempered or even reversed when drought is severe and prolonged, posing a serious threat to tree growth in scenarios where the intensity and duration of drought are expected to increase. Our findings provide valuable insights into understanding the effects of species richness on the stability of tree growth in the context of current and future climate change. However, our study is still subject to several limitations. First, although we considered both resistance and resilience, due to data limitations, we were unable to comprehensively analyze the influence of species richness on the overall response of tree growth to drought nor could we explore the specific mechanisms underlying the trade-off relationship between resistance and resilience. Second, we were unable to obtain the physiological indicators of the sampled trees and their neighboring conditions and thus could not analyze the specific mechanisms of how species richness influences drought stability. Third, considering that many tree ring samples were collected in areas with significant spatial heterogeneity, the vascular plant richness data we used at a resolution of 2.5 arcminutes may not be able to accurately reflect the diversity around these samples, potentially leading to underestimation or overestimation. In the future, it would be necessary to incorporate tree rings or tree growth observation sites with diversity information into the analysis or deploy a global biodiversity experimental network, to support further research in this area.

Materials and Methods

Tree Ring Chronologies.

We obtained the raw data of tree ring widths from the International Tree Ring Data Bank v7.21 (ITRDB; https://www1.ncdc.noaa.gov/pub/data/paleo/treering/) in September 2023. The ITRDB has collected tree ring data from multiple sites worldwide, and tree samples from the same site belong to the same species. We selected tree ring widths at 4,072 sites according to the following criteria: 1) the presence of metadata (longitude, latitude, and species code), 2) tree ring series covering at least 15 y between 1951 and 2021, 3) the availability of tree ring measurements including total ring widths, and 4) at least five tree samples for each site and year (55, 56). We detrended the raw tree ring width series using a 30-y cubic smoothing spline with a 50% frequency-response cut-off (57), and averaged the ring widths of different trees for each site and year using a bizweight robust mean. These processes resulted in standardized site-level chronologies, which were used as the standardized RWI in this study. We used the dplR package (v.1.7.4) (58) in R (59) for detrending, standardizing, and averaging tree-level series data.

Considering the significant differences in functional traits, ecological strategies, and responses to drought between gymnosperms and angiosperms (55, 60), we categorized the 4,072 sites into gymnosperm and angiosperm sites based on the corresponding tree samples. Among the 4,072 tree ring sites, there were 3,194 gymnosperm sites and 878 angiosperm sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), with 145 gymnosperm species and 69 angiosperm species.

Species Richness Data.

We used vascular plant species richness as the metric of species richness in this study, sourced from Sabatini et al. (29). This species richness dataset is based on a compilation of vegetation plot databases combined with several environmental variables to model (using boosted regression trees) global patterns of vascular plant richness at a resolution of 2.5 arcminutes. We chose the modeled species richness at a spatial grain of 1,000 m2 to extract for each site based on its spatial coordinates.

Considering the uncertainty of the model results and the limited correlation between vascular plant species richness and tree growth (r = 0.034, P < 0.05), we also obtained the records of species richness from the Global Forest Biodiversity Initiative (GFBI) dataset (61), which provides measurements of tree species richness from 773,100 permanent sample plots, to conduct supplementary analysis and verify the robustness of the results (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Specifically, we selected all GFBI plots within 0.05 × 0.05° of the tree ring sites based on their latitude and longitude coordinates. Then, we retained only the plots from national forest inventories of the United States to ensure consistent plot area (167.4 m2), accounting for 69.2% of the selected sample plots (1,405 of 2029). The tree species richness at each tree ring site was calculated as the average of the tree species richness of all GFBI plots within the range of 0.05 × 0.05°, resulting in a total of 1,278 sites with corresponding tree species richness data (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). There was a significant positive correlation between vascular plant richness and tree species richness at each site (P < 0.001, R2 = 0.39, SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

Environmental and Functional Trait Variables.

To account for the confounding effects of other variables when assessing the impact of species richness on drought resistance and resilience of trees, we considered eight environmental variables in our regression models (see below). These variables included DI, stand age (AGE), mean annual temperature (TMP), mean annual precipitation (PRE), mean annual CWD, cation-exchange capacity of the topsoil (CEC), available water-storage capacity of soil (AWC), and a compound topographic index (CTI). In addition, given that functional traits reflect the ecological strategies of plants, represent interspecific differences to some extent (62), and influence the responses of species’ growth to drought (63), we also considered two functional trait variables, specific leaf area (SLA) and wood density (WD), to account for the influences of different species (see details in SI Appendix, Table S2).

We calculated the DI as the mean monthly Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) value during drought years (see below). We obtained monthly SPEI data from the gridded SPEI dataset (SPEIbase v2.9; https://spei.csic.es/database.html) with a spatial resolution of 0.5° (64). We determined AGE at each site by calculating the mean ages of all sampled trees in corresponding drought years from the ITRDB. We derived TMP and PRE values from the Climatic Research Unit (CRU) v.TS 4.07 with a spatial resolution of 0.5° (65), calculated as the mean values from 1951 to 2021 for each site. CWD is the difference between precipitation and potential evapotranspiration from the CRU TS 4.07 dataset, which was calculated as the mean value from 1951 to 2021 for each site. We derived CEC and AWC from the Regridded Harmonized World Soil Database v.1.2 (https://daac.ornl.gov/SOILS/guides/HWSD.html), which represent the capacity of nutrient fixation and water storage in soil, respectively (66). We derived a comprehensive representation of topography, the CTI, from Marthews et al. (67) with a 15 arcsec resolution. We gathered SLA data, an important leaf economic trait closely related to plant photosynthesis and resource acquisition (62), from the trait maps developed by Butler et al. (68). Finally, we calculated WD as the dry mass divided by fresh volume from the Global Wood Density Database (69). All environmental and functional trait variables were significantly correlated with the resistance or resilience of tree growth to drought (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Identification of Droughts with Different Durations.

We identified droughts with different durations in 1951–2021 from the SPEI database (SPEIbase v2.9) (64). SPEI represents the degree of drought relative to the long-term average, with larger negative values indicating more severe drought. We used SPEI with timescale of 12 mo to identify drought because it represents the annual water balance and its time step is consistent with the tree ring (14, 55, 70). At each site, we sorted the mean monthly SPEIs for all years from 1951 to 2021 in ascending order and defined drought threshold as the lower quartile of the data distribution (mean = −0.69; SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B) for comparability with previous studies (25). We also considered the 20th and 30th percentiles as thresholds to ensure the robustness of the results (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8). Years with mean SPEI values below the drought threshold were identified as drought years.

We identified a single-year drought as a short-term drought and a consecutive drought that had a duration of two or more years as a prolonged drought. To ensure the robustness of the results, given the potential for extended recovery times from droughts lasting for more than two years, we also only considered two-year consecutive drought as prolonged drought (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). We retained only drought years when both the SPEI and tree ring data had valid records and considered only years when productivity declined in response to drought (55), and there should be no occurrence of new droughts within two years after the end of last drought. In the end, we obtained a total of 6,691 short-term droughts and 3,319 prolonged droughts available for analysis of tree resistance to, and resilience from, drought (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

In addition, considering that some regions have significant drought trends in past decades (71), we also identified droughts after detrending the raw SPEI time series. Specifically, we used the Mann–Kendall test to estimate the trend significance and used Sen’s slope estimator to calculate the linear fit slope of SPEI series for each site (72). Supplementary analysis of detrending the raw SPEI time series showed similar results (SI Appendix, Fig. S11).

Quantification of Resistance and Resilience to Drought.

Based on the obtained RWI data and identified droughts, we calculated the resistance (Rt) and resilience (Rs) of trees for each drought and site (14, 42):[1]Rt=RWInRWIn-RWId, [2]Rs=RWId+2-RWIdRWIn,

where RWIn is the mean RWI in normal years, RWId is the mean RWI in years with drought, and RWId+2 is the RWI two years after the drought. The normal years were defined as those years where the mean SPEIs were between the lower and upper quartiles of the SPEIs from 1951 to 2021, to avoid the influence of dry and wet events on tree growth. In accordance with previous findings (4), we selected a two-year period to measure tree resilience, as the impact of drought on tree growth has been shown to last for about two years. This choice also aligned with recent studies (55, 56). Through t tests, we examined the differences in tree resistance and resilience between short-term and prolonged droughts.

Statistical Analysis

Estimating the Effects of Species Richness on Resistance and Resilience.

We excluded values of resistance and resilience beyond three SD from the mean to avoid the influence of outliers. Then, we fit multiple regression models using the robustbase package (v.0.95-1) (73) in R to estimate the effects of species richness on resistance and resilience under short-term and prolonged droughts, while controlling for the potential confounding effects of environmental and functional trait covariates:[3]Rti\Rsi=θ×Si+∑j=1j=nαj×Xij+ε,

where Rti and Rsi are resistance and resilience for site i, respectively; S is vascular plant species richness; Xj is environmental or functional trait variable j, and αj is the regression coefficient of variable j and ε is the residual term. All environmental and functional trait variables were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1). We also fit the multiple regression models to estimate the effects of species richness on resistance and resilience of tree growth for gymnosperms and angiosperms under all droughts, short-term drought, and prolonged drought, respectively.

We additionally used two types of sequential regression models to explore the effects of species richness on resistance and resilience while controlling for the effects of other covariates as much as possible and test the robustness of the results. For the first type of sequential regression model, we conducted a regression with species richness as the dependent variable and all other variables as independent variables to exclude their impact on species richness. Subsequently, we regressed the residuals of species richness, together with other variables, against the drought resistance and resilience of trees. The regression coefficients of the residuals of species richness (S) can represent the effects of species richness:[4]Si=∑j=1j=nαj×Xij+ε, [5]Rti\Rsi=θ×residual(Si)+∑j=1j=nαj×Xij+ε.

The effects of species richness estimated by the first type of sequential regression models are consistent with the results of the multiple regression models (SI Appendix, Table S1). For the second type of sequential regression model, we regressed environmental and functional trait variables against drought resistance and resilience to obtain the residuals of resistance and resilience that could not be explained by these variables. Then, we separately regressed these two residuals with species richness to obtain the effects:[6]Rti\Rsi=∑j=1j=nαj×Xij+ε, [7]residualRti\residualRsi=θ×Si+ε.

Although the results of the second type of sequential regression models are different from those of the multiple regression models, their significance, effect direction, and relative magnitude between short-term and prolonged droughts remain unchanged (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Finally, based on the results of Liu et al. (25), we incorporated the interaction terms between CWD and DI with species richness into the original multiple regression models (Eq. 3) to explore how they affect the effect of species richness on drought resistance and resilience and whether these effects vary between short-term and prolonged droughts. These models were fit using the robustbase package (v.0.95-1) in R (73).

Pathway Analysis for the Effects of Species Richness on Resistance and Resilience.

We built SEM for short-term and prolonged droughts to disentangle the potential indirect effects of species richness on resistance and resilience mediated by the growth of trees (RWIn, RWId, RWId+2) through the piecewise approach. In contrast to the variance–covariance approach, the piecewise approach incorporates various distributions (Poisson, Gamma, etc.) and can model nonindependence (74). We included species richness, climate variables, and the RWI data used to calculate resistance and resilience (RWIn, RWId, RWId+2) into the models. Starting from the initial models (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), we removed all nonsignificant relationships and added all relationships with significant d-separation results to the models. We repeated this process until all relationships in the models were significant and the P-values in both the d-separation and Fisher test were greater than 0.05. The SEM was built in R using the piecewiseSEM package (v.2.1.0) (74).
