Life's Essential 8 and cerebrovascular disease among Hispanic/Latino adults: findings from the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos and Study of Latinos‐Investigation of Neurocognitive Aging magnetic resonance imaging cohort
Authors: Freddie Márquez, Wassim Tarraf, Sayaka Kuwayama, Paola Filigrana, Alexandra Rekesh, Haibo Zhou, Linda C Gallo, Tali Elfassy, Charles DeCarli, Hector M González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBetter cardiovascular health (CVH) is associated with better cognitive function, but limited data exist on its association with cerebrovascular disease (CVD) burden using the Life's Essential 8 (LE8) metric, particularly among Hispanic/Latino adults.METHODSLE8 scores (range: 0 to 100) were calculated at baseline (2008 to 2011). Brain magnetic resonance imaging volumetric outcomes (2017 to 2022) included cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) burden (infarcts, cerebral microbleeds, high white matter hyperintensity [WMH] burden, enlarged perivascular spaces), gray matter (GM), and WMH volumes. Survey‐weighted regression models examined associations between LE8, adjusting for demographic factors.RESULTSAmong 2211 participants (mean age [SE] = 57.6 [8.0] years, 58.5% female), higher LE8 score was associated with reduced cSVD burden and lower odds of infarcts and WMH burden. Higher LE8 scores were associated with greater total and frontal GM and lower WMH volumes.DISCUSSIONBetter CVH was associated with reduced CVD burden and greater GM volume in Hispanic/Latino adults.Keywords: brain structure, cardiovascular health, cerebral small vessel disease, cerebrovascular disease, epidemiology, Hispanics, Hispanics/Latinos, Latinos, neuroepidemiology, neuroimaging, population neuroscience

BACKGROUND

With an aging population, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) are an increasing public health concern in the United States. Identifying strategies for primordial and primary prevention are necessary. Life's Essential 8 (LE8), developed by the American Heart Association (AHA), is a framework for assessing cardiovascular health (CVH) based on eight key modifiable components associated with better cardiovascular and overall health. 1 These include body mass index (BMI), blood lipids, blood glucose levels, and blood pressure, as well as dietary quality, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep duration. 2 The construct of CVH shifts focus on disease treatment to health promotion and preservation across the lifecourse in populations and individuals, offering a practical tool for risk modification through lifestyle changes, with established associations with better cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes. 3 , 4 Compared to the earlier Life's Simple 7 (LS7), LE8 adds sleep and uses continuous 0 to 100 scoring with updated thresholds, allowing more sensitive assessment of CVH. Sleep is increasingly recognized as important for cardiometabolic health, stroke risk, and ADRD‐related brain changes 5 and is particularly relevant to brain health through its roles in glymphatic clearance, neuroinflammation, cerebrovascular risk, and cognitive decline. 6 , 7

The US Hispanic/Latino population constitutes the second largest ethnic or racial population in the United States and is projected to have the largest increase in ADRD prevalence of any ethnic or racial group in the coming decades. 8 , 9 As such, identifying pathogenic mechanisms contributing to cognitive decline in this population remains a challenge. Despite elevated cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk, 10 , 11 , 12 Hispanic/Latino populations remain understudied in brain health research. CVD risk factors are known contributors to cognitive decline, 13 , 14 , 15 and prior findings in the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) cohort showed that better CVH measured with LS7 was associated with better cognitive function 16 and white matter microstructure. 17 Our study extends this work by using LE8, which captures updated definitions of CVH and incorporates sleep health, to evaluate whether a more comprehensive CVH construct is related to cerebrovascular disease (CVD) burden and brain structure.

CVD increases with age and is a known contributor in the pathogenesis of cognitive decline and ADRD, 18 especially among underrepresented populations. 19 Infarcts, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), and enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVSs) are common in aging and associated with worse cognition. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 CMBs reflect hemorrhage‐prone cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD), often related to cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), and may occur adjacent to perivascular spaces (PVSs). 24 , 25 ePVS on MRI may reflect impaired interstitial fluid drainage from the brain and underlying cSVD. 26 Because CVD is heterogeneous, and individuals with mixed CVD, which is more common than single CVD, have faster rates of decline, 18 examining both macrovascular and microvascular (infarcts, WMH, CMBs, and ePVS) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) markers is important.

This study examined the association between CVH, assessed using the LE8 at baseline, with MRI‐derived (1) CVD burden and (2) structural brain volumes 10 years later among Hispanic/Latino adults. We hypothesized that higher LE8 scores would be associated with lower CVD burden and greater gray matter (GM) volumes.

METHODS

Study population

We used data from the HCHS/SOL study, a prospective multi‐site cohort study examining Hispanic/Latino adults across four US metropolitan areas (Bronx, New York; Chicago, Illinois; Miami, Florida; and San Diego, California). Details on study design and sampling have been reported elsewhere. 12 , 27 , 28 Briefly, HCHS/SOL used a stratified, multi‐stage area‐probability sampling design in which census block groups, households, and individuals were sampled within each field center. This design involved unequal probability of selection and clustering of participants within sampling units, such that analyses must account for the survey design to obtain population estimates and standard errors.

HCHS/SOL is a community‐based cohort that did not restrict enrollment to individuals free of prior stroke, cognitive impairment, or other brain conditions at baseline. During HCHS/SOL Visit 2, participants aged 50 years and older who underwent the neurocognitive tests at Visit 1 were recruited to participate in the Study of Latinos‐Investigation of Neurocognitive Aging (SOL‐INCA; 2015 to 2018), an ancillary study designed to examine neurocognitive aging. SOL‐INCA‐MRI (2017 to 2022) is a subsequent ancillary study conducted approximately 10 years after the baseline HCHS/SOL visit. For SOL‐INCA‐MRI, eligible participants were those who had completed HCHS/SOL Visit 1 and Visit 2 and were willing and able to return for an additional neurocognitive assessment and MRI.

The HCHS/SOL coordinating center generated SOL‐INCA‐MRI‐specific sampling weights that account for the original HCHS/SOL sampling design, attrition, non‐response, and selection into the MRI ancillary study. These were used to generate representative estimates for the target population of Hispanic/Latino adults aged 50 years and older in the four metropolitan areas. All analyses additionally incorporated the survey design strata and primary sampling units (clusters) to obtain appropriate variance estimates. Omitting these design estimates could bias population estimates and underestimate standard errors. All participants provided written informed consent, and protocols were approved by Institutional Review Boards at participating institutions.

MRI acquisition and processing

Structural MRI scans were conducted using GE 3T 750 scanners (three sites) and a Philips 3T Achieve TX (one site). Sequences included (1) T1‐weighted (1 mm3); (2) three‐dimensional fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (3D FLAIR; 1 × 1 × 3 mm); (3) Inversion Recovery Spoiled Gradient Echo or Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo (T2*GRE; 1 mm isotropic).

Preprocessing included skull stripping, intensity correction, tissue segmentation, and atlas‐based hippocampal quantification. MRI‐visible infarcts, WMHs, CMBs, and PVSs were defined, quantified, and processed at the Imaging of Dementia & Aging Laboratory at the University of California, Davis using previously published and harmonized protocols. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Brain imaging outcomes

Primary outcomes included cSVD burden markers and global and regional brain MRI volumetric measures. To account for inter‐individual differences in head size, all brain volumetric measures were residualized by cerebrum total cranial volume (TCV) by residualization prior to analysis. The residuals were then standardized (z‐scores) to facilitate interpretation and comparability across outcomes. Because WMH volume was right‐skewed, it was log‐transformed prior to residualization and standardization.

CVD burden was assessed using a cSVD composite score ranging from 0 to 4, calculated as the sum of four binary MRI markers: (1) high WMH volume, defined as WMH volume in the highest 10th percentile of its distribution; (2) ePVS, defined as PVS volume in the highest 10th percentile based on its distribution; (3) presence of infarcts; and (4) presence of CMBs. High WMH and ePVS burden were defined a priori as values in the upper decile of their distributions, using distribution‐based thresholds and were not derived from cognitive or clinical cutpoints. These thresholds were selected to identify individuals with the greatest burden of MRI‐visible cerebrovascular pathology for inclusion in the cSVD composite. Each marker contributed one point when present, with higher scores indicating greater cSVD burden.

The following brain MRI volumetric measures (in cubic centimeters [cm3]) were evaluated: total cerebrum GM, frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital cortical gray matter (CGM), hippocampal, WMH, and total cerebrum brain volumes.

LE8 CVH score

CVH at HCHS/SOL Visit 1 was assessed using the AHA's LE8 score. The LE8 includes eight components 1 : four health factors (blood lipids, blood glucose, blood pressure, and BMI) and four health behaviors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep). Each component was scored on a scale of 0 to 100 according to AHA criteria, 1 with higher scores reflecting more ideal CVH. The overall LE8 score was calculated as the mean of the eight component scores, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate better CVH. For categorical analyses, participants were classified into three CVH groups: Low (< 50), Moderate (50 to < 80), and High (≥80) groups. LE8 components were derived using standardized protocols, validated questionnaires, clinical measurements, and medication data available in HCHS/SOL, following AHA criteria. 1 , 10 , 38 , 39 , 40 For all components, higher scores reflected healthier behaviors or more favorable clinical profiles. Additional details on the operationalization of individual LE8 components are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Covariables

Covariates included age (continuous, at the SOL‐INCA‐MRI visit), sex (male, female), education (less than high school, high school or equivalent, greater than high school or equivalent), Hispanic/Latino background (Dominican, Central American, Cuban, Mexican, Puerto Rican, South American, other/ more than one), and field center (Bronx, Chicago, Miami, San Diego).

Analytical approach

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 17 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). All estimates accounted for the complex HCHS/SOL sampling design, incorporating ancillary study‐specific sampling weights and the strata and primary sampling units (clusters) from the parent HCHS/SOL study using Stata survey procedures. Field center was included as an adjustment covariate in all models and was not treated as the clustering unit.

We first generated weighted descriptive statistics for our target population stratified by LE8 score category (Low: < 50, Moderate: 50 to < 80, High: ≥80; Table 1). Means and standard deviations were reported for continuous variables, while percentages and standard errors were presented for categorical variables. Group differences were assessed using survey‐adjusted F tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables.

Because the cSVD composite represented a count of MRI markers (range: 0 to 4), we used survey‐weighted Poisson regression models to examine associations between LE8 and overall cSVD burden, reporting incidence rate ratios (IRRs). A generalized linear model with binomial family [n = 4 trials] and log link produced similar results. We additionally used survey‐weighted logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for individual cSVD markers, including infarcts, CMBs, high WMH burden, and ePVS burden. Associations between LE8 (continuous and categorical) and brain MRI volumetric measures were examined using survey‐weighted linear regression models. All models adjusted for age at the SOL‐INCA‐MRI visit, sex, education, Hispanic/Latino background, and field center.

We conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated all models using an alternative operational definition of LE8 based on self‐reported medication usage for blood pressure and cholesterol. This yielded a separate set of continuous and categorical LE8 scores, hereafter referred to as self‐reported LE8 (SR LE8). Second, we repeated cSVD analyses using the top 25th percentile (rather than the top 10th percentile) to define high WMH and ePVS burden.

For all models, we computed and plotted marginal estimates (mean, counts, and probabilities) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals to visualize associations across outcomes.

RESULTS

Target population characteristics

Of the 16,415 individuals enrolled in HCHS/SOL at Visit 1, 6377 were reexamined during Visit 2 as part of the SOL‐INCA ancillary study. For SOL‐INCA‐MRI, 6139 participants met criteria for phone screening, of whom 4326 completed pre‐MRI screening. Among those, 2989 were eligible and agreed to participate; 2789 provided consent directly or via proxy. After final eligibility determination, 2667 participants completed SOL‐INCA‐MRI. Of those, 2392 were aged 50 years or older and had completed the SOL‐INCA visit. Thus, this study included SOL‐INCA participants who also underwent brain MRI as part of SOL‐INCA‐MRI.

We excluded eight participants due to missing total cerebrum brain volume, eight participants due to missing covariates, and 165 participants with missing LE8 exposure data, yielding a final analytic sample of N = 2211 participants (Figure S1). Among those excluded for missing LE8 data, the most commonly missing components were sleep (n = 83), cholesterol (n = 52), and blood pressure (n = 47); these categories were not mutually exclusive, and some participants were missing more than one component.

Descriptive characteristics by LE8 score category are presented in Table 1. The mean age (SD) at baseline Visit 1 was 57.6 (8.0) years and 68.0 (8.0) years at the SOL‐INCA‐MRI visit, and 58.5% of the target population was female. Overall, 64% had completed high school or more. Participants in the High LE8 category (LE8 score ≥80) were younger (65.1 years) than those in the Moderate (68.2 years) and Low (68.9 years) categories. Hispanic/Latino background varied by LE8 group.

Unweighted counts and weighted percentages of each cSVD marker are presented in Figure S2; weighted distributions of cSVD counts included in the cSVD composite are presented in Figure S3. Among participants positive for each item, the weighted proportion of those with that marker in isolation was 40.8% for infarcts, 55.7% for CMBs, 37.7% for high WMH burden, and 66.3% for ePVS burden.

Associations with cSVD

Higher LE8 scores were associated with lower cSVD burden in adjusted models (IRR = 0.991; 95% CI: [0.983, 0.999], p = 0.034; Table 2, Figure 1). Associations for categorical LE8 scores were not statistically significant after full adjustment (Figure S4).

Sensitivity analyses

Findings were generally consistent when LE8 was recalculated using self‐reported medication use for blood pressure and cholesterol (Figures S9–S14). Results were also comparable when redefining high WMH and ePVS burden using the top 25th percentile thresholds rather than the top decile threshold.

DISCUSSION

In this study of middle‐aged and older Hispanic/Latino adults living in the United States, higher CVH scores were associated with lower WMH volume and burden, fewer infarcts, lower overall small vessel disease burden, and greater brain volumes 10 years later. Better overall CVH was associated with greater total cerebrum GM and frontal CGM volume. These findings support the importance of CVH promotion and preservation during middle‐aged and older adulthood as a modifiable target for ADRD risk reduction in this population.

Hispanic/Latino adults account for half of the population growth in the United States, experience a disproportionate burden of ADRD, yet remain underrepresented in brain‐aging research. 41 Projections indicate that this population will quadruple in size and bear the greatest increase in ADRD prevalence of any racial or ethnic group in the United States. 8 , 9 Prior research in Hispanic/Latino populations has shown that cognitive decline is associated with global GM atrophy and WMH burden. 42 Frontal GM, in particular, plays a central role in executive function and cognitive control, 43 and reduced volume in this region has been associated to poorer cognitive aging and increased ADRD risk. 44 From a biological standpoint, better CVH and less vascular brain pathology may mitigate cognitive decline through a variety of proposed mechanisms, which include decreased neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction, improvements in cerebral perfusion, and preservation of brain structure. 38 , 39

GM and WMH volume are established markers of brain aging, cognitive decline, and ADRD risk. 40 , 45 Evidence from the UK Biobank, a cohort enriched for generally healthy and predominantly non‐Hispanic individuals, also suggests that low CVH is associated with older brain age. 46 Similarly, adherence to AHA guidelines for CVH is associated with delayed white matter aging. 47 In the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, which includes non‐Hispanic White and Black individuals, poor and intermediate CVH during early midlife, assessed via LE8 scores, predicted higher WMH burden across midlife. 48 Notably, this cohort includes non‐Hispanic/Latino individuals. Similar findings have been observed in the Multiethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), which includes Hispanic/Latino individuals, but most are of Mexican or Caribbean backgrounds. In the MESA study, healthier LS7 profiles were associated with more favorable brain structure profiles in participants without stroke or dementia. 49 Importantly, this study includes a community‐based individuals from admixed ancestries and varied backgrounds, 50 strengthening its relevance for public health. Our findings using the LE8 CVH index are consistent with these earlier results and provide additional support for LE8's utility in brain health research.

Although the magnitudes of the associations per one‐point increase in LE8 were modest, a 10‐point increase in LE8 score corresponded to approximately 0.04 SD greater total GM and 0.07 SD greater frontal GM. These associations may translate into differences in brain structure comparable to several years of age‐related decline reported in prior neuroimaging studies. For example, Driscoll et al. reported that frontal GM is among the regions showing accelerated age‐related decline in cognitively normal older adults, highlighting it as a region particularly vulnerable to structural aging. 51 In that context, the positive association we observed between LE8 and frontal CGM suggests that better CVH may be associated with preservation of brain regions known to be susceptible to age‐related atrophy.

These findings extend and build upon prior research in the HCHS/SOL and SOL‐INCA cohort using both the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) and the LS7. Using the FRS, a clinical score that indexes CVD risk, medium and high CVD risk was previously associated with lower brain volumes, greater WMH volume, and greater infarcts in this population. 32 Previous studies using LS7 showed that better CVH was associated with better verbal learning and memory, verbal fluency, and processing speed/executive function, 16 as well as more favorable white matter microstructure. 17 Our findings using LE8, an updated comprehensive CVH metric, are consistent with those prior analyses and extend this work by applying the updated LE8 framework to MRI markers to brain structure and CVD.

Despite associations between CVH and other markers of cerebrovascular burden, LE8 scores were not associated with the presence of CMBs or ePVSs. Several explanations may account for these null findings. In our study, CMBs and ePVSs were more commonly isolated markers, whereas infarcts and WMHs co‐occurred more often with other cSVD indicators. While both CMBs and ePVSs are considered markers of CVD, they may precede and reflect earlier or partially distinct markers of cSVD compared to infarcts or WMH. Although these markers were not rare in our sample, CMBs and ePVSs may be more heterogeneous and more difficult to characterize reliably, particularly when modeled as high global burden indicators, which may attenuate associations. CMBs and ePVSs were more common than WMHs and infarcts in our sample, but their etiologies are complex and may also reflect partially distinct pathophysiological processes. Both have been associated with CAA and specific hypertension‐related vascular changes. 52 , 53 At the same time, the presence of ePVSs is associated with an increased risk of incident subcortical infarcts, CMBs, and WMH volume, 26 suggesting that it may represent an early marker of downstream vascular brain injury. One proposed mechanism is that impaired perivascular or glymphatic clearance through PVSs may reduce the removal of metabolic waste, including amyloid proteins, thereby accelerating vascular and neurodegenerative brain injury. 54 , 55 Similarly, in CAA, CMBs are associated with deposition of the Aβ40 fragment in the cerebral blood vessels. 56 Thus, these observations suggest that CMBs and ePVSs may reflect heterogeneous mechanisms that include vascular, AD‐related, and neurodegenerative processes, which may overlap with but are not identical to those underlying infarcts and WMHs. Prior work in SOL‐INCA‐MRI also showed that CMBs were associated with vascular risk burden and cognitive impairment, 35 and ePVSs are associated with cSVD, cortical atrophy, and cognitive impairment, supporting their inclusion as a marker or cSVD in this cohort. 37 Future work should examine associations between CVH and cSVD in individuals without established infarcts or WMH, and in combination with other markers of AD‐related pathology.

From a public health perspective, LE8 offers a modifiable and trackable metric that can be used to promote and maintain brain health and reduce ADRD risk. This is particularly important given evidence that vascular pathology contributes substantially to dementia burden. 57 The LE8 was developed using heterogenous US population data, including Hispanic/Latino participants, making it a validated and inclusive CVH framework with up‐to‐date metrics for cohorts across heterogeneous backgrounds. Given persistent disparities in CVH across racial and ethnic groups, 1 LE8 may serve as a valuable tool for early identification and intervention in clinical and public health settings.

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design limits causal inference. Second, LE8 was measured at baseline (Visit 1) and may not capture changes in CVH over the approximately 10‐year interval before MRI. Thus, our exposure reflects baseline rather than long‐term CVH, and individuals whose CVH improved or worsened over time may be misclassified with respect to their cumulative vascular risk. The time‐varying misclassification would be expected to attenuate associations toward the null, and, as a result, the relationships between sustained CVH and brain structure or CVD burden may be stronger than the estimates reported here. Third, although we applied ancillary‐study‐specific calibrated weights to account for the complex sampling design, non‐response, and selection into SOL‐INCA‐MRI, these methods may not fully eliminate residual selection bias associated with survival, continued participation, and MRI eligibility. Fourth, some LE8 components, such as sleep and physical activity, relied on self‐reported behaviors and may be subject to misclassification. Fifth, because there are no universally established cut points for MRI‐visible ePVS burden in this cohort, we used a distribution‐based approach to define high burden for composite modeling, although findings were robust in sensitivity analyses using alternate thresholds. Lastly, generalizability is limited to Hispanic/Latino individuals living in selected metropolitan areas. Despite these limitations, this study has several key strengths to consider. It is the largest neuroimaging study of Hispanic/Latino individuals living in the United States, a population underrepresented in brain health research. While technical or measurement error may also contribute to these findings, this study used evidence‐based and well‐validated CVH metrics, standardized and harmonized neuroimaging protocols, and sampling methods to improve generalizability. Sensitivity analyses with self‐reported medication use and alternative thresholds for WMH and ePVS burden enhance the robustness of the results.

Overall, better CVH metrics were associated with lower cerebrovascular pathology and greater GM volume 10 years later in a large prospective community‐based cohort of Hispanic/Latino adults. These findings highlight the importance of promoting CVH during midlife and older age as a potential primordial or primary prevention strategy for reducing CVD and preserving brain structure in this growing population.
