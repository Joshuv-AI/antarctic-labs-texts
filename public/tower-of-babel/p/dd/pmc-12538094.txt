Eosinophilia associated with nintedanib therapy
Authors: Kiana A. Banafshay, Rahul Mutneja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nintedanib (Ofev) is a widely used antifibrotic agent approved for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and other fibrosing interstitial lung diseases, with a known side effect profile that includes gastrointestinal symptoms and hepatotoxicity. However, immune-mediated eosinophilia has not previously been associated with its use. We describe the case of a 69-year-old man recently diagnosed with pulmonary fibrosis who developed marked eosinophilia and transaminitis shortly after initiating treatment with Nintedanib. An extensive evaluation excluded infectious, autoimmune, and alternative drug-related etiologies. Remarkably, both the eosinophilia and liver enzyme abnormalities resolved promptly following the discontinuation of Nintedanib. This case represents, to our knowledge, the first reported instance of Nintedanib-associated eosinophilia, raising the possibility of a delayed hypersensitivity reaction.

Introduction

Nintedanib is an antifibrotic agent commonly used in the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). While its known side effects include gastrointestinal disturbances, immune-mediated reactions such as eosinophilia have not been previously reported [1]. We present the first well documented case of nintedanib-associated eosinophilia in a patient with newly diagnosed idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

Case report

A 69 year old male with a past medical history of hypertension, diabetes, hypothyroidism, and allergic rhinitis presented to the pulmonology clinic for assessment of a newly diagnosed restrictive lung disease in the setting of a chronic cough for the last two years. Pulmonary function testing had shown an FEV1/FVC ratio of 86 and total lung capacity of 67 %. Review of systems was overall unremarkable for any other systemic symptoms. He has no prior pulmonary history, is a lifetime nonsmoker, and denies any occupational or avocational exposures. He lives at home with his wife and is very active at baseline. On physical examination, he had bilateral air entry and mild bilateral crackles. No evidence of lower extremity edema. Serological workup was sent and he was started on Nintedanib for IPF.

About twenty days after Nintedanib initiation, lab results revealed a white blood cell count of 11.3 k/uL and eosinophilia of 3.8 K/uL, which was not noted in blood work just a few days prior to starting. In addition, he had a new transaminitis with an alanine aminotransferase of 67 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase of 53 U/L, and alkaline phosphatase of 393 U/L. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and c-reactive protein were elevated to 60 mm/hr and 3.3 mg/dL respectively. Total IgG increased to 1689 mg/dL. Overall, viral and autoimmune studies, including an ANA, ANCA panel and rheumatoid factor were largely unrevealing, with the exception of IgG1 of 966 mg/dL and IgG4 of 99.8 mg/dL. Hepatitis labs were negative. The patient was promptly informed of the results. He denied any fever, rash, neck swelling, abdominal pain, nausea or vomiting. He reports no new medications, supplements, or recent travel. He was advised to stop Nintedanib. Subsequent blood work about a week after discontinuation revealed improvement in his eosinophilia and liver enzymes (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Discussion

A review of the literature suggests this is the first reported case of an allergic-type reaction to Nintedanib in a patient with pulmonary fibrosis. Nintedanib's known adverse effects primarily include gastrointestinal symptoms, which comprise roughly 76 % of its side effect profile, along with less common effects such as elevated liver enzymes, nasopharyngitis, cough, headache, fatigue, weight loss, dermal ulcers, and urinary tract infections [2]. Eosinophilia is not mentioned as a common or expected side effect.

Drug-induced eosinophilia can range from asymptomatic laboratory findings, as in this patient, to severe systemic reactions such as DRESS (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms). The latter typically occurs two to eight weeks after initiation of an offending medication and is associated with fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, and multi-organ involvement [3].Drug hypersensitivity reactions are generally classified as immediate (within 1 h) or delayed (after 1 h) and can be immunologically or non-immunologically mediated [4]. Immediate reactions are IgE-mediated (Type I), presenting with urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, or anaphylaxis, and can be confirmed via skin testing and serum tryptase. In this patient, no anaphylactic or IgE-mediated findings were observed.

Delayed hypersensitivity reactions are typically IgG-mediated (Type II) and can present across a range of clinical phenotypes, including serum sickness-like reactions, contact eczema, DRESS, toxic epidermal necrolysis, or acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis [4]. In this patient, the elevated IgG1 and IgG4 levels are highly suggestive of a delayed hypersensitivity reaction. Although no single test can definitively confirm Nintedanib as the culprit, the strong temporal association between initiation of Nintedanib and the development of eosinophilia and liver enzyme elevations, followed by resolution upon discontinuation, supports the diagnosis. Alternative causes such as infection and immunodeficiency were considered but largely excluded based on clinical and laboratory evaluation.

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of Nintedanib-associated eosinophilia suggesting a possible delayed hypersensitivity reaction. Clinicians should consider drug-induced eosinophilia in patients receiving antifibrotic therapy and monitor closely for rare but potentially significant adverse immune responses.
