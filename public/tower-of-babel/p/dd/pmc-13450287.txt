Toxoplasmosis Beyond Transplantation: Diagnostic and Prevention Challenges in a Patient Receiving Targeted Immunomodulators
Authors: Joy Mouanes‐Abelin, Christelle Pomares, José G Montoya, Morgane Pondrom, Lucille DE MARIA, Andrea J Zimmer, Carlos A Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTToxoplasmosis has long been recognized as a serious complication in immunocompromised host, particularly those with advanced HIV/AIDS, hematopoietic stem‐cell transplantation (HSCT), solid‐organ transplant (SOT), and hematological malignancies. The rapid expansion of targeted immunomodulators, including chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell (CAR‐T) therapies, monoclonal antibodies, and small‐molecule inhibitors, is creating new at‐risk populations beyond traditional transplant settings. We present a 9‐year‐old boy with high‐risk B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL), who developed prolonged fever and macrophage activation syndrome (MAS). After an extensive unrevealing workup, disseminated acute toxoplasmosis was identified incidentally on bone marrow aspirate via morphologic identification of tachyzoites and confirmed by Toxoplasma gondii PCR. This case exemplifies the emerging threat of toxoplasmosis in non‐transplant immunomodulated hosts and supports three core mitigation strategies. First, baseline Toxoplasma IgG and IgM serology should be obtained in all patients initiating targeted immunotherapy, recognizing that B‐cell depletion or hypogammaglobulinemia may render IgG unreliable, and that IgM may be falsely negative, delayed, or persistently positive in immunocompromised individuals. Second, targeted PCR from clinically relevant compartments or metagenomic next‐generation sequencing when conventional diagnostics is unrevealing should be applied early. Third, prevention requires a bundled approach: baseline screening, patient education for seronegative individuals, and trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis with or without serial qPCR monitoring for seropositive patients. Toxoplasmosis is no longer a transplant‐exclusive concern. As targeted immunomodulators reshape practice across rheumatology, oncology, neurology, and autoimmune disease, infectious diseases specialists must lead efforts to raise cross‐specialty awareness, establish guidelines, and build registries to define the true burden of toxoplasmosis in these growing populations.Keywords: biologics, CAR T‐cell therapy, disseminated toxoplasmosis, hematologic malignancy, macrophage activation syndrome, PCR, targeted immunotherapy, Toxoplasma gondii, toxoplasmosis

Case Presentation (Christelle Pomares)

A 9‐year‐old boy was diagnosed with B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) with an IKZF1 deletion 2 years prior to presentation and assigned to the high‐risk arm of the ALLTogether protocol, a European randomized treatment study for children and young adults with ALL that employs individualized risk stratification based on leukemia genetics and treatment response [1]. He underwent induction chemotherapy followed by consolidation therapy and maintenance therapy with methotrexate and 6‐mercaptopurine. He did not undergo allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). While in maintenance therapy, he was receiving monthly intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIGs) for secondary hypogammaglobulinemia, and trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (TMP‐SMX) prophylaxis, the latter of which had been discontinued in the weeks preceding presentation due to chronic diarrhea.

Eighteen months after commencing maintenance therapy, the patient developed a fever without localizing symptoms. He was evaluated by a general practitioner while in Corsica, France, without identification of an infectious source. Given that his fever persisted beyond 7 days in the context of active hematologic malignancy treatment, he was admitted to the pediatric oncology‐hematology unit of CHU de Nice, France (Day 0). On admission, laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia and liver enzyme elevation (Table 1). Initial microbiologic workup was unrevealing. Cross‐sectional imaging, including a CT scan of the sinuses, thorax, and abdomen and pelvis, as well as an abdominal ultrasound, was non‐revealing. A urine culture obtained at that time grew pan‐susceptible Escherichia coli, though this was felt to be insufficient to explain his systemic clinical picture. A bone marrow aspirate (Day +1) showed no evidence of disease relapse.

On Day +7, the patient continued with persistent fever and worsening inflammatory markers consistent with macrophage activation syndrome (MAS). Since no infectious cause had been identified at that point, oral corticosteroid therapy was initiated with prednisone. A 1 8F‐FDG PET–CT scan on Day +15 showed a mild diffuse osteomedullary hypermetabolism of uncertain significance (inflammatory vs. leukemic) without hypermetabolic lesions suggestive of a deep infectious localized focus. Given the concern for possible B‐ALL relapse, a bone marrow aspirate was performed again on Day +21 of hospitalization. The aspirate was negative for leukemic blasts but revealed extracellular organisms morphologically consistent with tachyzoites of Toxoplasma gondii (Figure 1).

Morphologic diagnosis was confirmed by positive T. gondii PCR on the aspirate and peripheral blood.

Following this unexpected finding, a diagnostic review was undertaken, including retrieval and testing of stored sera and the previously performed bone marrow aspirate specimen. Serologic testing on stored serum from Day +7—obtained at the time of MAS workup—revealed Toxoplasma IgG at low levels by one assay (Alinity, 2.5 IU/mL, cut‐off 3.0 IU/mL) and at borderline levels by a second assay, with Toxoplasma IgM negative by both platforms (Alinity 0.17; second technique 0.11). This pattern, in the context of monthly IVIG infusions, had been initially consistent with passively acquired antibodies rather than active infection. In contrast, serology performed on Day +21 demonstrated a dramatic rise in both Toxoplasma IgG (Alinity, 6.08 IU/mL, cut‐off 3.0 IU/mL) and IgM (Alinity 13 660; second technique 5.35), IgG avidity of only 14%, and IgA detection by immunoblot (bands at 30 and 40 kDa), collectively confirming acute primary Toxoplasma infection (Table 2). Toxoplasma PCR was positive on the Day +21 bone marrow aspirate and on peripheral whole blood obtained on Day +21 and Day +27, subsequently turning negative on whole blood by Day +56. Toxoplasma PCR was also performed in the bone marrow aspirate specimen obtained on Day +1—with no morphologic features of toxoplasmosis identified at that time—and returned positive, indicating that systemic parasitemia had been present from the very onset of the febrile illness, weeks before morphologic or serologic diagnosis was achievable.

Following diagnosis at Day +21, the patient was initiated on intravenous TMP‐SMX, followed by oral pyrimethamine‐sulfadiazine plus folinic acid once tolerated. Diagnostic Pitfalls and Testing Considerations for Toxoplasmosis in Immunocompromised, Hematological Malignancy, Targeted Immunotherapy, and HSCT Patients (José G. Montoya)

Modern risk‐adapted therapy for pediatric B‐ALL imposes a progressive and sustained burden of immunosuppression that accumulates across three sequential phases spanning 2–3 years [2]. Induction (∼4 weeks) aims to achieve complete remission in ∼95% of patients through a backbone of vincristine, a corticosteroid (dexamethasone or prednisone), an asparaginase preparation (pegaspargase or calaspargase), and an anthracycline, with intrathecal methotrexate for central nervous system (CNS) prophylaxis throughout [3]. In high‐risk disease, such as IKZF1‐deleted B‐ALL, targeted immunotherapy is incorporated alongside conventional chemotherapy; blinatumomab (a B‐cell CD19/CD3 bispecific T‐cell engager) is now part of frontline therapy as a post‐remission approach, and agents such as inotuzumab ozogamicin (a B‐cell CD22‐directed antibody‐drug conjugate) or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) may be used in select high‐risk or refractory settings, with induction potentially serving as a bridge to allogeneic HSCT or CAR‐T cell therapy in eligible patients [4]. Consolidation (up to ∼8 months) deepens immunosuppression through antimetabolites, including high‐dose methotrexate and 6‐mercaptopurine, along with cyclophosphamide, cytarabine, asparaginase, and continued intrathecal chemotherapy, with intensity titrated to minimal residual disease (MRD) response and risk group. Maintenance typically continues for 2–3 years, based on a backbone of daily 6‐mercaptopurine and weekly methotrexate (with periodic vincristine and corticosteroids), though the least intensive phase, sustains clinically meaningful immunosuppression as well [4]. Across this entire continuum, cumulative disruption of CD4+ T‐cell–mediated immune surveillance, compounded by the B‐cell–depleting and T‐cell–modulating effects of targeted agents, creates a persistent vulnerability to opportunistic pathogens, including T. gondii, a risk that does not resolve with the most intensive phases but extends well into maintenance.

This case illustrates three interconnected and actionable opportunities to mitigate the burden of toxoplasmosis in patients receiving modern immunosuppressive and targeted immunomodulatory regimens. The first gap is unrecognized risk: Universal baseline Toxoplasma IgG screening is not yet standard practice in this population, leaving a substantial proportion of at‐risk patients without a serological reference point from which to interpret subsequent results. Baseline serology should therefore be obtained at diagnosis, before any therapy is initiated. Seronegative patients should receive counseling on avoiding known sources of Toxoplasma infection, while seropositive patients should be considered for a preemptive monitoring strategy and/or prophylaxis during high‐risk periods of immunosuppression. Indeed, reliance on classical exposure history or clinical risk factors alone is inadequate for risk stratification. As many as 50% of acutely infected individuals report no identifiable exposure history [5, 6, 7]. Acute toxoplasmosis can occur in the apparent absence of identifiable risk factors, and the expanding use of biologics, small‐molecule inhibitors, and CAR‐T cell therapies introduces immunological vulnerabilities that are not yet systematically captured in existing prophylaxis frameworks [8, 9]. Second, missed or delayed diagnosis continues to threaten survival: Serology may be falsely negative or uninterpretable in the profoundly immunocompromised host, particularly when confounded by concurrent IVIG administration. This highlights the importance of performing baseline Toxoplasma serological testing prior to initiation of immunomodulators. In addition, clinical presentations are protean and nonspecific, encompassing CNS involvement, fever of unknown origin (FUO), ground‐glass pulmonary infiltrates that may progress and cause hypoxemic respiratory failure, bone marrow involvement, and hyperinflammatory syndromes [10] (Table 3). Furthermore, molecular diagnostics—including targeted PCR from whole blood, urine, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), ocular fluids, and tissue—are often pursued too late in the diagnostic workup, if at all, reflecting limited awareness across primary oncology, hematology, and cellular therapy teams. Third, prevention remains imperfect and inconsistently implemented. For instance, TMP‐SMX, the cornerstone of primary prophylaxis, is frequently interrupted or contraindicated in high‐risk patients due to myelosuppression or intolerance, and the awareness of toxoplasmosis risk and prophylaxis indications among the specialized teams caring for these patients remains insufficient. Toxoplasmosis may appear to be an infrequent event to primary care teams managing immunosuppressed patients. However, when unrecognized and untreated, it is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Because diagnostic evaluations for infectious etiologies often do not routinely include testing for T. gondii, and autopsy rates are now low, cases of fatal toxoplasmosis are likely underdiagnosed and underreported.

Establishing a baseline Toxoplasma serological status before initiation of immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory therapy is a pivotal first step, with direct implications for risk stratification, prophylaxis decisions, and the interpretability of future serological results. Ideally, this screening should be performed at the time of initial diagnosis of hematologic malignancy, before any treatment is initiated, rather than deferred to the pre‐allogeneic HSCT assessment, as chemotherapy, anti‐B‐cell monoclonal antibodies, and other targeted immunomodulatory agents can deplete specific IgG antibodies and render serology falsely negative. Indeed, Štajner et al. demonstrated that approximately 30% of pediatric patients who were Toxoplasma IgG seropositive at the time of their leukemia diagnosis had lost detectable IgG by the time of pretransplant evaluation, highlighting that deferring serological screening until the pretransplant period risks misclassifying previously exposed patients as seronegative, with direct consequences for prophylaxis and monitoring decisions [11]. For most patients, commercial laboratory assays are sufficient for initial screening, provided the patient retains normal immunoglobulin production. A result of IgG‐positive/IgM‐negative is the expected serological signature of past infection, indicating latent bradyzoite cysts in tissue and identifying the patient as being at risk for reactivation under immunosuppression. A seronegative result (IgG‐negative/IgM‐negative) indicates no prior Toxoplasma exposure and should prompt counseling regarding infection prevention.

When initial commercial results yield IgG‐positive/IgM‐positive, equivocal findings, or isolated IgM positivity, confirmation at a reference laboratory (e.g., in the United States, the Dr. Jack S. Remington Laboratory for Specialty Diagnostics, https://www.sutterhealth.org/patient‐resources/remington‐laboratory) is warranted to exclude acute primary infection: A diagnosis that may require treatment before immunosuppression is initiated. This distinction carries clinical urgency, as isolated IgM positivity is notoriously difficult to interpret. For instance, among patients with confirmed Toxoplasma IgM positivity at commercial laboratories, only approximately 22% represent true acute infection, while approximately 74% reflect chronic infection [12]. Importantly, the converse problem exists in immunocompromised patients, who may mount blunted or delayed antibody responses. In our case, the diagnostic IgM rise only became apparent on Day +21, more than 3 weeks into the febrile illness, while Toxoplasma PCR was already positive on bone marrow aspirate obtained early in the disease course, underscoring that molecular diagnostics should be performed early in these patients.

For equivocal diagnostic situations, reference laboratory testing offers the most informative and reliable serological characterization. The panel available at specialized centers such as the Remington Laboratory includes IgG quantification by the Sabin–Feldman Dye Test (the reference standard), the Differential Agglutination Test (AC/HS pattern), IgM detection by double‐sandwich ELISA, IgA, and IgE testing, and IgG avidity determination [13]. Of note, no single assay alone determines the timing of infection. Therefore, accurate interpretation of whether a patient has remote, chronic, or recently acquired infection requires a complete serological panel interpreted in clinical context. A high‐avidity IgG result effectively excludes primary infection acquired within the preceding 3–5 months [14], while low avidity in the setting of positive IgM raises concern for recent acquisition [15].

For immunocompromised patients with suspected toxoplasmosis, a multimodal and anatomically guided diagnostic strategy is essential. Targeted qPCR for T. gondii—preferably using the 529‐bp repeat element (∼300 copies per genome) for maximal sensitivity, or the B1 gene (∼30 copies per genome) as an alternative—should be applied to all clinically relevant specimens as dictated by the presenting syndrome: Whole blood, CSF, BAL, ocular fluid, bone marrow aspirate, urine, or fresh/paraffin‐embedded tissue samples. When conventional targeted molecular testing is negative, or the differential diagnosis is broad, as is typically the case in profoundly immunocompromised patients, agnostic hypothesis‐free platforms offer a complementary and increasingly actionable diagnostic layer. Plasma microbial cell‐free DNA metagenomic next‐generation sequencing (mcfDNA mNGS), available through platforms such as Karius–Spectrum (Karius, USA) [16, 17, 18, 19] or the DISQVER platform (Noscendo, Germany) [20], and mNGS testing in CSF by Delve Detect (DelveBio, USA) [21] can detect T. gondii DNA and establish the diagnosis in settings where conventional testing has been unrevealing. Finally, toxoplasmosis in the immunocompromised host can masquerade as hyperinflammatory conditions such as HLH or MAS, and the diagnosis may only be reached incidentally, when tissue sampling is performed for a different indication or via mNGS testing [22, 23, 24, 25].

Clinicians caring for patients receiving modern immunosuppressive or targeted immunomodulatory regimens who develop refractory fever, unexplained cytopenias, hyperinflammatory syndromes, or progressive organ dysfunction, particularly in the setting of interrupted TMP‐SMX prophylaxis, must maintain a high index of suspicion for disseminated toxoplasmosis, pursue early and broad molecular testing across multiple specimen types, and consider agnostic mNGS platforms when the diagnosis remains elusive.

Immunologic Basis of Toxoplasmosis Risk in Targeted Immunomodulation, Including CAR‐T Recipients (Andrea J. Zimmer)

Understanding why patients receiving modern targeted immunotherapies face high risk for toxoplasmosis requires revisiting the fundamental immunology of T. gondii control in human hosts. Upon oral ingestion of oocysts or tissue cysts, pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) are detected by pattern recognition receptors, principally Toll‐like receptors, on dendritic cells and other innate immune cells, triggering robust IL‐12 secretion, particularly from dendritic cell populations [26]. This IL‐12 signal is the major upstream driver of protective immunity: It drives CD4+ T helper 1 (Th1) differentiation and CD8 + cytotoxic T‐cell effector responses, as well as early IFN‐γ production by NK cells. These pathways lead to the coordinated production of IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, IL‐2, and IL‐21 [27]. Functional CD4+ T cells are not merely effectors but also act as immunological scaffolders: Through sustained IL‐2 and IL‐21 secretion, they are indispensable for the generation and long‐term maintenance of CD8+ memory T‐cell immunity. The convergent production of IFN‐γ by both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with subsequent macrophage activation, constitutes the critical barrier that prevents reactivation of latent bradyzoites [26]. From a serological standpoint, intact humoral immunity is required to produce and sustain detectable IgG. Notably, a significant depletion of B‐cells, as occurs with anti‐CD19/CD20/CD22 agents or sustained hypogammaglobulinemia, can erase this serological trace without eliminating the parasite burden. Toxoplasmosis by reactivation has been reported in B‐cell‐depleted patients [9]. In sum, durable control of T. gondii requires the coordinated integrity of innate IL‐12 signaling, Th1‐polarized CD4+ helper function, sustained CD8+ cytotoxic effector and memory responses, functional IFN‐γ production, and resilient humoral immunity [28, 29]. Any targeted therapy that substantially disrupts any node in this axis, whether by depleting T‐cell subsets, blocking cytokine signaling, or impairing effector function, creates a permissive window for reactivation or severe primary Toxoplasma infection [9].

Characterizing the Landscape of CAR T‐Cell Therapy and Its Immune Deficit

CAR T‐cell therapies are currently FDA‐approved for B‐cell hematologic malignancies and multiple myeloma and are under investigation for a widening range of indications, including autoimmune diseases and solid tumors [30]. The CAR T‐cell treatment cycle includes lymphodepleting chemotherapy (typically fludarabine and cyclophosphamide), followed by infusion of the engineered T‐cell product. The immune sequelae are profound and prolonged [31]. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS), occurring in 40%–95% of patients, and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), occurring in 20%–70%, are managed with corticosteroids, tocilizumab, and other cytokine inhibitors, all of which add further layers of immunosuppression [32, 33]. Hematotoxicity following CAR T‐cell therapy, now formally classified as immune effector cell‐associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT) [34], results in prolonged neutropenia and lymphopenia that are qualitatively distinct from chemotherapy‐related cytopenias, frequently exhibiting a biphasic course and, in a subset of patients leads to a severe aplastic phenotype refractory to growth factor support [35]. For anti‐CD19 and anti‐BCMA products, on‐target B‐cell aplasia produces hypogammaglobulinemia lasting months to years, requiring IVIG supplementation [34, 36]. The nature and depth of humoral immune deficiency differ meaningfully by CAR T‐cell target: CD19‐directed products spare long‐lived BCMA‐positive plasma cells, preserving at least partial pathogen‐specific antibody titers, whereas BCMA‐directed products deplete all plasma cell populations, causing more immediate and profound antibody deficiency [37]. CD4+ T‐cell counts remain below 200 cells/mm3 in up to 60% of patients at 12 months, further compounding susceptibility to intracellular pathogens [38]. The resulting immunologic state closely resembles that following allogeneic HSCT: Profound T‐cell lymphopenia in the early phase; defective cellular immunity extending weeks to months; and functional antibody deficiency requiring IVIG replacement.

Toxoplasma reactivation has been documented in CAR T‐cell recipients, with syndromes including disseminated disease, cerebral toxoplasmosis, and autopsy‐confirmed multiorgan involvement [25, 39, 40]. Importantly, post‐CAR T‐cell neurotoxicity (ICANS) can mimic cerebral toxoplasmosis in presentation, underscoring the need for prompt diagnostic evaluation in patients with neurologic deterioration following CAR T‐cell infusion. Beyond the canonical toxicities of CRS and ICANS, a growing spectrum of noncanonical and late complications has emerged, including secondary malignancies, coagulopathy and thrombosis, cardiac arrhythmias, and a range of delayed neurological syndromes specific to BCMA‐targeted products [37]. These noncanonical toxicities, together with prolonged immune dysregulation, underlie the burden of non‐relapse mortality (NRM) observed after CAR T‐cell therapy [41]. Indeed, in a systematic review and meta‐analysis of 7604 patients across 46 clinical trial and real‐world cohorts, the overall NRM point estimate was 6.8%, with infections accounting for more than half of all non‐relapse deaths (50.9%), far exceeding deaths attributable to CRS, ICANS, and HLH combined (cumulatively ∼11.5%). These data collectively position CAR T‐cell recipients as a high‐risk population for opportunistic infections including toxoplasmosis and underscore the critical importance of structured infectious disease surveillance and prophylaxis strategies in this expanding patient population.

Toxoplasmosis in Patients Receiving Targeted Biologics and Small Molecules

A recent systematic review by Cho et al., identified 46 toxoplasmosis cases across 18 countries linked to targeted immunotherapies [9]. The implicated agents spanned anti‐TNF‐α agents, anti‐CD20, and anti‐CD52 agents, JAK inhibitors, T‐cell co‐stimulation inhibitors, IL‐12/23 inhibitors, CAR T‐cell therapies, and others including MEK inhibitors, TKIs, and sphingosine‐1‐phosphate receptor modulators. The clinical burden in this cohort was striking: Around 50% of cases presented with cerebral toxoplasmosis, often mimicking brain abscess or tumor on neuroimaging, 33% with ocular toxoplasmosis, and 4% with disseminated disease. Four cases were fatal, and eight patients were left with permanent neurological or visual deficits, underscoring that toxoplasmosis in this population can have significant and lasting clinical consequences. Notably, both reactivation of latent infection (44%) and acute primary infection (39%) contributed to the disease burden, with three severe cases directly linked to ingestion of undercooked wild game. The above highlights that dietary counseling and behavioral risk reduction are essential components of prevention (Table 4). A sentinel case underscores that discontinuation of a biologic does not immediately restore immune competence. A woman had an acute Toxoplasma infection 6–7 months prior to conception while on chronic treatment with adalimumab for ankylosing spondylitis. Although adalimumab was discontinued 5 months before conception, vertical transmission of the parasite was documented and led to elective pregnancy termination due to severe congenital infection with CNS involvement [42]. Although the half‐life of adalimumab is approximately 2 weeks, its immunomodulatory effects clearly persisted well beyond 6 months, supporting our recommendation that pregnancy be deferred for at least 12 months in seropositive patients who have received long‐acting or deeply immunosuppressive biologics.

Our case illustrates that profound immunosuppression sufficient to allow severe, disseminated toxoplasmosis is not exclusive to transplant recipients, a point reinforced by the preceding discussion and the growing literature on non‐HSCT regimens. The discontinuation of TMP‐SMX prophylaxis, which provides dual activity against Pneumocystis jirovecii and T. gondii, removed a critical layer of protection at a particularly vulnerable moment; such decisions should prompt consideration for alternative prophylactic agents or weekly qPCR surveillance [43]. Infectious disease consultation is pivotal for optimizing pretreatment screening, guiding prophylaxis decisions, and coordinating management of active disease. The ECIL‐9 guidelines provide a robust framework for T. gondii management in allogeneic HSCT recipients, recommending universal pretransplant IgG screening, weekly qPCR surveillance in IgG‐seropositive recipients, TMP‐SMX as first‐line prophylaxis, and a combined prophylaxis–plus–preemptive–qPCR strategy given that drug prophylaxis alone may fail [44]. These principles should be extended to patients receiving CAR‐T cell therapies, targeted biologics, and small molecules, which can generate equivalent immunosuppressive depth, and where both primary infection and reactivation of latent disease must be considered [9, 45]. Finally, while the ASTCT CAR‐T infection prevention guidelines recommend Toxoplasma serology only when risk factors are present [38], we advocate for universal pretreatment screening: Behavioral and epidemiological risk factors are unreliable at the individual level, and a missed seropositive status eliminates the opportunity for targeted prophylaxis and surveillance in precisely the patients who need it most.

Take Home Messages and Recommendations (All Authors)

Building on the diagnostic and immunological frameworks outlined above, three firm recommendations emerge from this case.

Screen Universally, Not Selectively

Baseline Toxoplasma IgG/IgM should be obtained before initiation of any potent immunosuppressive regimen in every patient, regardless of reported exposures. T. gondii is a globally prevalent parasite, and the majority of human infections are asymptomatic, often occurring without recognized exposure or identifiable risk factors. A positive result mandates a formal prophylaxis and surveillance plan. A negative result is an opportunity for structured primary prevention counseling (Table 4).

Maintain TMP‐SMX Prophylaxis; Documents and Bridge Any Planned Interruption

Most published cases of disseminated or fatal toxoplasmosis in the hematological host trace back to a gap in TMP‐SMX prophylaxis. When interruption is unavoidable, it must be planned and explicitly documented, with seropositive patients bridged by intensified weekly qPCR surveillance [46]. Of note, alternative agents such as atovaquone harbor the risk of breakthrough infection [47, 48, 49, 50]. The pre‐engraftment window warrants particular attention: Pentamidine, of widespread use in allo‐HSCT programs, carries no anti‐Toxoplasma activity, yet up to 40% of post‐HSCT reactivations occur within the first 30 days, carrying mortality exceeding 70% [51].

Use qPCR as a Safety Net

Weekly whole‐blood qPCR in seropositive HSCT recipients is established practice [52] and should be extended to other deeply immunosuppressed patients (including CAR‐T recipients) during high‐dose corticosteroid exposure and any prophylactic gap due to TMP‐SMX interruption [53].

Areas of Uncertainty and Future Research

Despite meaningful advances, the field lacks the prospective infrastructure needed to fully characterize toxoplasmosis risk across the expanding landscape of targeted immunotherapy. We identify three priority research needs:

First, a multicenter prospective registry for opportunistic infections in patients receiving novel immunomodulatory agents is urgently needed. Existing pharmacovigilance databases capture adverse events inconsistently and rarely with sufficient granularity to discern baseline serology, prophylaxis status, or clinical outcomes. Pharmacovigilance efforts center around capturing and preventing serious adverse events directly related to the mechanism of action of the drug and less on the unintended consequences such as opportunistic infections. A structured registry would enable systematic collection of time‐to‐event data, mode of infection (reactivation vs. primary infection), prophylaxis exposures, qPCR kinetics, clinical presentations, treatment regimens, and outcomes.

Second, a prospective multicenter trial evaluating lower TMP‐SMX doses (e.g., 20 mg TMP–100 mg SMX daily) that would permit its use during the pre‐engraftment phase is warranted [54]. Nearly all fatal toxoplasmosis cases occur in the context of absent or ineffective prophylaxis, and the pre‐engraftment window represents a period of disproportionate mortality risk precisely because standard‐dose TMP‐SMX is withheld over myelotoxicity concerns [46, 55]. Demonstrating a safety and efficacy signal for a low‐dose regimen during this window, supported by a preemptive qPCR framework as a safety net, would address one of the most consequential and underexplored gaps in toxoplasmosis prevention.

Third, a prospective definition of optimal qPCR surveillance frequency and thresholds in non‐HSCT populations is needed. ECIL‐9 recommends weekly blood PCR in seropositive allogeneic HSCT recipients; preliminary data suggest that twice‐weekly sampling may improve early detection in the highest‐risk window [56]. Whether and how these surveillance parameters should be adapted for CAR‐T recipients, bispecific T‐cell engager recipients, and patients on other profoundly immunosuppressive biologics remains to be defined through dedicated prospective cohort studies.
