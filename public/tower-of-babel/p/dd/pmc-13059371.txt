Validation and psychometric properties of the persian version of the caregiver contribution to self-care of chronic illness inventory: a confirmatory factor analysis
Authors: Ali Aghajanloo, Azam Maleki, Mir Amirhossein Seyednazari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic illness management requires self-care. Although family caregivers are essential in helping patients, there is currently no approved tool in Iran to assess their impact. Translating and assessing the psychometric qualities of the Caregiver Contribution to Self-Care of Chronic Illness Inventory (CC-SC-CII) in Persian was the goal of this work.MethodsConvenience sampling was used to choose 334 family caregivers of patients with chronic illnesses for this methodological study, which was carried out in Zanjan, Iran. In accordance with Beaton’s recommendations, the forward-backward approach was used to translate the instrument. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was used to evaluate construct validity. Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for test-retest stability were used to assess reliability.ResultsSelf-care maintenance, self-care monitoring, self-care management, and caregiver self-efficacy were the four initial factors that the CFA endorsed. Acceptable goodness-of-fit indices were shown by the model (χ²/df = 2.8, RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.98, GFI = 0.90). Excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96) and stability (ICC = 0.96, p < 0.001) were demonstrated by the instrument. On a standardized 0–100 scale, caregivers reported poor mean scores for self-efficacy (26.32) and self-care management (29.41).ConclusionWith low management and self-efficacy ratings, the Persian CC-SC-CII is a valid and reliable tool that may be used for clinical screening and research. Clinicians should use this measure to identify caregivers who need specific educational interventions about symptom response.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

In 2021, noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) claimed 43 million lives, with low- and middle-income nations accounting for 73–82% of these fatalities. Diabetes, cancer, chronic respiratory conditions, and cardiovascular disorders are major factors [1]. These conditions place a heavy burden on patients, necessitating ongoing medical care and significant lifestyle modifications [2]. Patients’ lived experiences are significantly changed by chronic disease, which frequently leads to low self-esteem and restrictions on everyday activities. Therefore, successful management requires mobilizing personal resources and strictly following treatment regimens [3].

Taking care of oneself is essential to treating chronic illnesses. According to the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness, self-care is comprised of three core concepts: self-care maintenance (actions taken to keep the body stable, such following a diet and medication schedule), self-care monitoring (monitoring oneself for alterations in symptoms and indicators), and self-care management (proactive reactions to symptoms as they appear, such taking more medicine or seeing a doctor) [4].

Family caregivers are essential since many patients struggle to do these duties on their own because of cognitive decline or problems connected to their illness [5]. Caregivers play a crucial role in crisis management, medication adherence, and symptom monitoring. Increased caregiver self-care engagement has been associated with improved patient outcomes and fewer readmissions to hospitals, according to research [6].

Even while caregivers play an essential role, it is still difficult to accurately measure their contribution [7]. A thorough tool created by Vellone et al. to evaluate this contribution across the domains of maintenance, monitoring, management, and self-efficacy is the Caregiver Contribution to Self-Care of Chronic Illness Inventory (CC-SC-CII) [8]. There is currently no validated Persian version of this instrument, despite it being validated in other languages.

Clinical decision-making requires having access to a valid and trustworthy instrument that is adapted to the Iranian situation. Healthcare providers can pinpoint particular care gaps, including inadequate symptom monitoring or low management confidence, by appropriately evaluating caregiver contributions. This makes it easier to create customized lesson plans, makes the most use of available resources, and eventually enhances the results of sickness treatment. Therefore, the goal of this study was to translate the CC-SC-CII into Persian and evaluate its psychometric qualities among Iranian caregivers.

Method

Setting and type of study

This study employed a methodological design focusing on the evaluation of tests and measurement instruments. The research setting consisted of the inpatient wards of Valiasr (AJ) and Ayatollah Mousavi Hospitals in Zanjan, Iran. Data were collected from April 2024 to August 2024.

Participants and sampling method

All informal caregivers who help hospitalized patients with chronic diseases were included in the research population. Being the primary caregiver, having a patient with a chronic illness validated by a doctor, being ready to engage, and being literate were the requirements for membership. The Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) recommendations, which suggest a minimum ratio of 10 participants per item to ensure model stability, were used to estimate the sample size [9, 10]. The inventory has 29 items, hence a minimum sample size of 290 was needed. Convenience sampling was used to recruit 334 individuals in order to increase power and adjust for any attrition.

Data collection instruments

A questionnaire on demographics and the Persian version of the Caregiver Contribution to Self-Care of Chronic Illness Inventory (CC-SC-CII) were used to gather data. In addition to the Caregiver Self-Efficacy scale (10 items), the CC-SC-CII has 29 items divided into three subscales: self-care management (7 items), self-care monitoring (5 items), and self-care maintenance (7 items). A 5-point Likert scale is used to rate each item.

Procedure

The researcher contacted qualified caregivers in the hospital wards after receiving ethical permission. Written informed permission was acquired when the study’s goals were outlined. The surveys were self-administered by the participants in a hospital quiet room. To address any questions and make sure nothing was left unanswered, the researcher was there. Participants received assurances that their answers would be anonymous and confidential.

Translation and Psychometric Evaluation Translation: Following the recommendations made by Beaton et al., the forward-backward translation approach was used to create the Persian version [11].

Face validity

Ten caregivers assessed the items for clarity, difficulty, and relevance throughout the qualitative phase. Twenty participants assessed item significance throughout the quantitative phase. An effect score of at least 1.5 was considered appropriate [12].

Content validity

Qualitative: The questions were checked for structure, phrasing, and scale by a panel of five experts, which included experts in internal medicine, medical-surgical nursing, and community health.

Quantitative: The Content Validity Index (CVI) and Content Validity Ratio (CVR) were used to evaluate the content validity. Lawshe’s table states that a CVR value of around 0.99 is needed to guarantee statistical significance for a panel of five experts [13]. A score of > 0.79 was deemed suitable for CVI [14].

Construct validity

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using LISREL (v.8.8). Model fit was assessed using the Chi-square (χ²), χ²/df ratio (< 3), RMSEA (< 0.08), CFI (> 0.90), and GFI (> 0.90) [15]. To assess discriminant validity, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was calculated. According to the Fornell-Larcker criterion, the square root of the AVE for each construct should exceed its correlation with other constructs.

Reliability

Cronbach’s alpha was used to evaluate internal consistency (≥ 0.70). Using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) at a two-week interval (n = 20), test-retest reliability was assessed; values > 0.80 denoted high stability.

Results

Demographic characteristics

According to the results, 40.4% of caregivers were between the ages of 25 and 40, and 55.1% of them were female. In all, 74.6% were married and 76.6% had less education than a high school degree. The patient’s children served as the caregiver in 56% of the instances. The majority of caregivers (73.9%) gave care for little more than two to five hours each day. Diabetes mellitus (36.8%) and cardiovascular disease (33.8%) were the most prevalent chronic conditions among the patients, with 63.5% of them being 56 years of age or older (Table 1).

The mean score for self-care maintenance was 48.74 (SD = 16.44). The mean score for self-care monitoring was 61.18 (SD = 19.90), which was the highest among the dimensions. The lowest score was observed in self-care management with a mean of 29.41 (SD = 16.30). Face and content validity

Every item received an effect score greater than 1.5 during the quantitative face validity phase, and all were retained. Strong content validity was confirmed by the CVR values ranging from 0.94 to 1.0 and the CVI above 0.79 for every item.

Construct validity

Confirmatory factor analysis

The suggested four-factor structure (Maintenance, Monitoring, Management, and Self-Efficacy) was deemed adequate by the CFA. The standardized factor loadings for every item were statistically significant (t-values > 1.96) and varied from 0.51 to 0.89, beyond the acceptable criterion of 0.50, as seen in Figs. 1 and 2. According to Table 4, the measurement model’s goodness-of-fit indices were adequate, with χ²/df = 2.8, RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.98, and GFI = 0.90.

Convergent validity and reliability

The subscales’ Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.86 to 0.95, indicating high internal consistency. Assessed using ICC (n = 20, 2-week interval), test-retest reliability showed good stability over time (ICC range: 0.86–0.96, p < 0.001). Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) were calculated using factor loadings in order to further evaluate construct validity. Convergent validity of the instrument was supported by AVE values exceeding 0.50 and CR values over 0.70 for all constructs.

Discussion

This study aimed to translate the Persian version of the Caregiver Contribution to Self-Care of Chronic Illness Inventory (CC-SC-CII) and assess its psychometric properties among Iranian caregivers. The results show that the Persian version of the instrument has very good construct validity and reliability, which supports its use as a standard tool in Iranian clinical and research contexts.

In terms of construct validity, the initial four-factor structure of the inventory—routine caregiving behaviors/maintenance, patient monitoring, symptom management, and caregiver self-efficacy—was validated by Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with satisfactory fit indices (RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.98). These results are in complete agreement with the original structure that Vellone et al. created [8]. However, there are intriguing subtleties when comparing our findings with validation research conducted in different cultures. For example, researchers had to alter the instrument’s structure to validate the Chinese version [16]; Notably, Chinese culture regards sleep as a curative agent rather than just a health habit; hence, the item “ensuring sufficient sleep” was moved from health-promoting activities to illness-related behaviors. Similarly, Aderaj et al. (2025) found that the “monitoring” dimension was unidimensional in the Albanian version, a middle-income nation environment comparable to Iran [4]. They hypothesized that Albanian caretakers were less able to distinguish between different components of monitoring, possibly as a result of lower educational levels and a lack of preventative initiatives [4]. It’s interesting to note that the monitoring scale’s unidimensional structure was also validated in the Spanish version of the patient inventory (SC-CII-Sp), where it showed outstanding internal consistency as a single dimension [17]. The stability of the monitoring construct is strengthened by this uniformity between various languages and viewpoints (patient vs. caregiver). On the other hand, the Persian version’s preservation of the original structure implies that Iranian caregivers have a distinct and accurate understanding of the different aspects of care, as the concept of contribution to self-care in Iranian culture shares a high semantic affinity with the original theoretical framework.

The Persian version showed outstanding internal consistency in terms of dependability (total Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96). This result is substantially higher than the coefficients recorded for the Chinese version, which had alphas between 0.79 and 0.88, and the Spanish version of a comparable inventory (CC-SCODI), which varied from around 0.72 to 0.92. Additionally, compared to the stability indicated in the Chinese version (ICC between 0.72 and 0.78), the test-retest stability (ICC = 0.96) demonstrates strong repeatability of results across time. These results imply that there are no grammatical misunderstandings for the Iranian audience due to the Persian translation’s great clarity and fluency.

The pattern of scores across several variables was one of the study’s difficult conclusions. Caregivers scored lowest in self-care management (29.41) and reported a mean score of 26.32 for caregiver self-efficacy. The study conducted in Spain by Martinez-Tofé et al. likewise found same trend (high self-efficacy and poor management), with caregivers scoring lowest in self-care management [18].

Iran’s inadequate symptom management score can be explained by the culture and healthcare system of this country. First, a research on caregiver burden in Iran by Shamsaei et al. (2015) found that supporting structures for crisis management or acute symptom training at home are weak, and family caregivers are frequently left without official assistance after patient discharge [19]. As a result, caregivers lack the technical expertise or practical skills necessary to successfully react when symptoms arise. Second, according to our demographic data, 44.6% of caregivers view social media as their main information source. However, a study by Soleimaninejad et al. (2019) evaluating eHealth literacy among Iranian caregivers of the elderly found that although internet access is high, caregivers’ capacity to “judge the quality of information” and “distinguish valid from invalid sources” is poor (mean score of 26 out of 40) [20]. This disparity explains why the current study’s high social media usage did not result in higher management scores; caregivers are probably exposed to a lot of information but lack the skills necessary to convert it into sound clinical judgments in emergencies (symptom management).

Additionally, the findings showed a relationship between caregiver burden and capacity and education level, and the length of the illness. This result is consistent with research conducted in Albania by Aderaj, which shows that caregivers in middle-income nations struggle to comprehend the challenges of managing certain chronic illnesses due to their lower educational attainment [4]. There is a clear need to provide simpler educational interventions that are suited to the eHealth literacy levels of Iranian caregivers, as a substantial portion of them lack formal education.

Therefore, we suggest a strategic change in educational interventions based on the observed discrepancy between caregivers’ inadequate management skills and their monitoring capacities. To improve caregiver self-efficacy, training programs should incorporate “scenario-based training” and the modeling of acute events in addition to information exchange. Additionally, it is recommended that health authorities require the Persian CC-SC-CII to be used clinically as a screening tool before discharge. This would make it possible for hospital teams to find high-risk caregivers who are unconfident about handling symptoms at home and provide them focused support prior to their release.

Limitation

Convenience sampling was used in this study, and it was conducted in a particular area, which may limit how broadly the findings can be applied. Additionally, the instrument relies on self-reporting, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Another limitation that should be taken into account in future research is the lack of evaluation of convergent validity using a gold-standard instrument (because there isn’t a comparable Persian tool).

Conclusion

In comparison to a number of other language variants, the Persian version of the CC-SC-CII exhibits better internal consistency and structural stability, making it a genuine and trustworthy tool. The study did, however, point up a crucial gap: caregivers have low self-efficacy and manage symptoms poorly. To find high-risk caregivers, clinicians should use this assessment as a screening tool. The focus of interventions must change from passively providing information to actively developing skills so that caregivers can confidently handle acute exacerbations.
