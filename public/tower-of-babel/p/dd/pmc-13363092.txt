Association of fat-related metabolic indices with MASLD and liver fibrosis: a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2017–2020
Authors: Neng Shen, Qinghua Wei, Jiangang Zhao, Jiacheng Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) framework emphasizes the central role of metabolic disturbances in fatty liver disease. Fat-related metabolic indices integrate information on insulin resistance and adiposity; however, their associations with MASLD and hepatic fibrosis at the population level have not been fully elucidated. Thus, this study aimed to examine the associations of 6 fat-related metabolic indices with MASLD and liver fibrosis in a nationally representative U.S. population. We analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2017–2020. Six fat-related metabolic indices: metabolic score for insulin resistance, metabolic score for visceral fat, cardiometabolic index, triglyceride-glucose index, waist triglyceride index, and lipid accumulation product, were calculated and categorized into quartiles. Hepatic steatosis and fibrosis were assessed using vibration-controlled transient elastography based on controlled attenuation parameter and liver stiffness measurement. Multivariable regression analyses were performed with progressive adjustment for potential confounders. All 6 indices exhibited significant dose–response associations with controlled attenuation parameter and MASLD prevalence. Higher quartiles were consistently associated with increased odds of MASLD after full adjustment. Positive associations with liver stiffness measurement and liver fibrosis were also observed, although the magnitude of associations was attenuated after comprehensive covariate adjustment. Among the indices, metabolic indices: metabolic score for insulin resistance, metabolic score for visceral fat, cardiometabolic index and lipid accumulation product demonstrated the strongest discriminative ability for liver fibrosis. Fat-related metabolic indices reflecting insulin resistance and adiposity are closely associated with MASLD and liver fibrosis in the general population, highlighting their relevance to metabolic liver disease phenotypes.

1. Introduction

Over the past thirty years, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has become the most prevalent cause of chronic liver pathology globally, reflecting the concurrent rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus.[1] NAFLD is characterized by excessive hepatic fat deposition in individuals without significant alcohol intake or other identifiable liver disorders, encompassing a histological spectrum that ranges from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, progressive fibrosis, and cirrhosis.[1] However, the NAFLD definition has faced criticism due to its reliance on exclusionary criteria, which may obscure the metabolic underpinnings of the disease and complicate both clinical assessment and research standardization.[2] In response to these limitations, the term metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) was proposed in 2020, marking a conceptual shift toward an inclusion-based framework.[2] This updated classification emphasizes the central contribution of metabolic disturbances to disease onset and progression, improves patient risk stratification, and integrates fatty liver disease more clearly within the spectrum of cardiometabolic disorders.[3] Notably, MAFLD recognizes the systemic consequences of hepatic steatosis, framing it as a manifestation of multisystem metabolic impairment rather than a purely hepatic disorder.[3] Building on this conceptual evolution, international hepatology societies endorsed the term metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in 2023, which preserves the metabolic focus of MAFLD, removes stigmatizing language, and simplifies diagnostic criteria for broader clinical applicability.[4–6]

In parallel, several fat-related metabolic indices have been developed to estimate insulin resistance (IR), visceral adiposity, and overall cardiometabolic risk using routine clinical and biochemical parameters. Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR) is a non–insulin–based score designed to approximate peripheral insulin sensitivity, incorporating fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), body mass index (BMI), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).[7] Building on this approach, Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) extends METS-IR to better reflect visceral fat accumulation by integrating additional anthropometric measures.[8] The cardiometabolic index (CMI) combines waist-to-height ratio with the TG-to-HDL-C ratio, providing a composite measure of central adiposity and metabolic dysregulation.[9] Among biochemical-based indices, the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) serves as a simple surrogate for IR, correlating well with clamp-derived measures and metabolic risk.[10] Complementing these, the waist triglyceride index (WTI) and lipid accumulation product (LAP) merge waist circumference (WC) with TG levels to capture visceral or ectopic fat deposition and its metabolic consequences.[11,12] Collectively, these indices offer complementary insights: some primarily reflect IR, while others emphasize fat distribution and associated metabolic burden. Their relative simplicity and use of routinely collected parameters render them valuable tools for evaluating the interplay between metabolic dysfunction and steatotic liver disease in large-scale populations.

Although some investigations have explored the relationship between fatty-metabolism–related indices and MASLD, their relevance to hepatic fibrosis has not been fully delineated.[13] In particular, to date, evidence regarding the simultaneous associations of the METS-VF with both MASLD and liver fibrosis severity is limited, representing a notable gap in the existing literature. Given that metabolic dysregulation and low-grade systemic inflammation are closely linked to hepatic steatosis and fibrotic remodeling, further clarification of these associations is warranted. Vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) provides an efficient, noninvasive modality to characterize liver alterations; the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) serves as an estimate of hepatic steatosis via ultrasound attenuation, whereas liver stiffness measurement (LSM) reflects fibrotic change through the propagation speed of shear waves.[14] In this study, we extend prior research by evaluating a comprehensive panel of fat-related metabolic indices, including both widely used measures and indices that have been less extensively investigated in previous studies. By examining their cross-sectional associations with CAP-defined steatosis and LSM-assessed fibrosis in a nationally representative population, this analysis aims to provide additional evidence on the metabolic correlates of MASLD and liver fibrosis.

2. Methods

2.1. Study cohort

A total of 15,560 individuals were initially identified from the survey dataset. To achieve a well-defined analytic cohort and reduce potential sources of bias, exclusions were implemented in a stepwise manner. Participants without valid VCTE data were first removed (n = 6539). Individuals with documented hepatitis B or hepatitis C infection (n = 665) were subsequently excluded. Those reporting high levels of alcohol intake, specified as ≥3 drinks per day for men and ≥2 drinks per day for women, were also omitted from further analyses (n = 1220). Additional participants were excluded due to missing demographic or clinical information (n = 2633) or incomplete metabolic and anthropometric measurements (n = 2355). Following application of these criteria, the final study population consisted of 2168 eligible participants. A detailed flowchart describing the selection process is presented in Figure 1. This study used publicly available, de-identified National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data, which had been approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board; therefore, additional institutional review and informed consent were not required for the present analysis.

Flowchart of participant selection. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, MASLD = metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease.

2.2. Derivation of fat-related metabolic indices

A series of fatty metabolism–related indices was generated using biochemical and anthropometric data obtained from the survey. Each metric was computed according to established equations and subsequently divided into quartiles to facilitate the assessment of their associations with MASLD and liver fibrosis. The specific variable definitions and quartile cut-points are summarized in Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. The formulas used for index calculation were as follows: METS-IR = ln(2 × FPG + TG) × BMI/ ln(HDL-C); METS-VF = 4.466 + 0.011 × (METS-IR)3 + 3.239 × WC; CMI = (TG/HDL-C) × (WC/height); TyG = ln[(TG × FPG)/2]; WTI = WC × TG/ 1000; and LAP = (WC − 65) × TG for men and (WC − 58) × TG for women. All indices were subsequently categorized into quartiles to evaluate their associations with MASLD and liver fibrosis (the details could be found in Supplementary table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1).

2.3. Operational criteria for MASLD

MASLD was identified based on evidence of hepatic steatosis in the absence of substantial alcohol intake or chronic viral hepatitis. Participants were classified as having MASLD when steatosis coexisted with at least 1 metabolic risk component. These components included: excess adiposity, defined as a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or central obesity with a WC ≥ 94 cm in men or ≥80 cm in women; dysregulated glucose metabolism, reflected by FPG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, a documented history of type 2 diabetes, or ongoing glucose-lowering treatment; elevated blood pressure, indicated by systolic or diastolic values ≥130/85 mm Hg or the use of antihypertensive medication; hypertriglyceridemia, defined as TGs ≥ 1.70 mmol/L or treatment with lipid-lowering agents; and reduced HDL-C, specified as <1.0 mmol/L for men or <1.3 mmol/L for women, or active lipid-lowering therapy.[5]

2.4. Evaluation of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis

Liver fat accumulation and fibrosis severity were assessed through VCTE. All examinations were conducted after a fasting period of at least 3 hours to minimize measurement variability. For each participant, a minimum of ten reliable readings was required, and data quality was confirmed by ensuring that the ratio of the interquartile range to the median remained below 30%. Steatosis was identified using the CAP, with values of 248 dB/m or higher denoting the presence of hepatic fat infiltration based on established diagnostic cutoffs.[15,16] Fibrosis severity was quantified using LSMs, where a threshold of ≥7 kPa was applied to indicate significant fibrotic change as supported by prior validation studies.[17,18]

2.5. Classification of covariates

Information on demographic characteristics and lifestyle behaviors was obtained through a structured questionnaire. Variables included age, sex, racial/ethnic background, educational level, marital status, poverty–income ratio (PIR), alcohol consumption patterns, physical activity habits, and use of prescribed medications. To reflect socioeconomic gradients, PIR was stratified into 3 categories: <1.30, 1.30–3.50, and >3.50.[19] Physical activity was quantified using the metabolic equivalent of task (MET) framework, derived by multiplying MET intensity by weekly frequency and session duration; individuals with a calculated value of 0 were considered inactive. Consistent with international recommendations, a weekly activity level of at least 600 MET-minutes was used to denote sufficient physical activity.[20] Tobacco exposure was evaluated using serum cotinine concentrations.[21,22] Diabetes mellitus was identified by FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%, self-reported diagnosis, or active antidiabetic treatment.[23] Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mm Hg, or current antihypertensive medication use.[24] Alcohol intake was categorized as nondrinking or moderate consumption, the latter defined as up to 1 to 2 drinks per day for men and up to 1 drink per day for women.[25]

2.6. Statistical procedures

Continuous variables were summarized as means with corresponding standard deviations, whereas categorical characteristics were expressed as weighted proportions. Differences across groups were examined using survey-weighted t tests for continuous measures and chi-square tests for categorical indicators. Initial evaluations of the relationships between fat-related metabolic indices and both CAP and LSM were performed using linear regression models. To progressively control for confounding, 3 models were specified: a crude model; a partially adjusted model incorporating demographic and socioeconomic attributes (age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, PIR, physical activity, and BMI), and a fully adjusted model additionally accounting for smoking exposure, alcohol intake, diabetes status, and hypertension. Logistic regression was subsequently applied to estimate associations with MASLD and liver fibrosis, accompanied by stratified analyses according to age and sex. Nonlinear exposure–response patterns were assessed using restricted cubic spline functions. To mitigate potential selection bias, propensity score matching was implemented prior to reestimating logistic models. Discriminative capability of each metabolic index was examined via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, with area under the curve values compared across indices. Threshold analyses were further conducted to identify potential inflection points relevant to MASLD and fibrosis. All computations were carried out using R software (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Stata (version 16.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station).

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 2168 participants were included in the final analysis, and their baseline characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2. Compared with individuals without MASLD, those with MASLD were older and more often male. Ethnic composition differed, with higher proportions of Mexican American and non-Hispanic White participants in the MASLD group, while non-Hispanic Black participants were less represented. Educational attainment was lower, and a greater proportion were married or cohabitating. Lifestyle and clinical factors also varied: MASLD participants had a higher prevalence of smoking, reduced physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, BMI, and WC. Biochemically, they exhibited elevated TGs, HbA1c, FPG, and all assessed fat-related metabolic indices, including METS-IR, METS-VF, CMI, TyG, WTI, and LAP, whereas HDL-C was lower. When stratified by fibrosis status, participants with significant liver fibrosis were older, more likely to be male, and showed higher rates of diabetes, hypertension, BMI, and WC. Most fat-related metabolic indices were also elevated in the fibrosis group, with the exception of total cholesterol and METS-IR, which showed no significant difference.

3.2. Associations of fat-related metabolic indices with CAP and LSM

The associations between fat-related metabolic indices and hepatic steatosis and fibrosis are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Across all examined indices: METS-IR, METS-VF, CMI, TyG, WTI, and LAP: higher quartiles were consistently associated with increased CAP values, indicating greater hepatic fat accumulation. These associations remained statistically significant after progressive adjustment for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and cardiometabolic variables. With respect to LSM, several indices, particularly METS-IR, METS-VF, and WTI, exhibited positive associations with higher LSM values, whereas associations for the remaining indices were attenuated and did not reach statistical significance in fully adjusted models.

3.3. Associations between fat-related metabolic indices for MASLD and liver fibrosis

Associations between fat-related metabolic indices and the presence of MASLD and liver fibrosis are presented in Tables 3 and 4. Higher quartiles of all indices were consistently associated with increased odds of MASLD, and these relationships persisted after adjustment for potential confounders. For liver fibrosis, positive associations were observed for selected indices, most notably METS-IR, METS-VF, CMI, and LAP, while other indices showed weaker or nonsignificant associations. ROC analyses indicated that the overall discriminative ability of these indices was modest (Fig. 2). For MASLD, LAP showed the highest area under the ROC curve, followed by METS-VF, WTI, CMI, METS-IR, and TyG. In contrast, discriminatory performance for liver fibrosis was lower across indices, with METS-VF and LAP demonstrating slightly higher area under the ROC curve values than the others (Supplementary Table 3, Supplemental Digital Content 3). Restricted cubic spline analyses based on fully adjusted models further demonstrated nonlinear associations between several indices and both MASLD and liver fibrosis (Figs. 3–4).

3.4. Stratified analyses and robustness assessment

Results of stratified multivariable analyses are shown in Figures 5 and 6. Overall, no material effect modification was observed across strata defined by age, sex, or BMI. Threshold effect analyses supported the presence of nonlinear associations between each index and MASLD as well as liver fibrosis (all P for nonlinearity <.05), without evidence of distinct inflection points, refer to Supplementary Table 4, Supplemental Digital Content 4. To assess the robustness of the findings, propensity score matching was conducted, followed by logistic regression analyses examining the associations of fat-related metabolic indices with MASLD and liver fibrosis. The results were generally consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Tables 5, 6 and 7, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

4. Discussion

Previous population-based studies examining the associations between fat-related metabolic indices and MASLD assessed by VCTE remain limited, with most investigations focusing primarily on hepatic steatosis or traditional metabolic markers rather than fibrosis severity.[13] Moreover, several composite indices reflecting IR and adiposity, particularly METS-VF, have not been comprehensively evaluated in relation to both steatosis and fibrosis within a single nationally representative cohort. Using NHANES data with standardized VCTE measurements, we systematically assessed a broad panel of fat-related metabolic indices, including METS-IR, METS-VF, CMI, TyG, WTI, and LAP, in relation to hepatic steatosis and fibrosis. Our analyses demonstrated that higher levels of all examined indices were consistently associated with increased odds of MASLD, independent of demographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic confounders. In analyses of significant liver fibrosis, positive associations were observed for several indices, most notably METS-IR, METS-VF, CMI, and LAP. These associations were further supported by stratified and propensity score–matched analyses, underscoring the robustness of the findings. Collectively, our results suggest that disturbances in fat-related metabolic status are closely correlated with the spectrum of metabolic liver disease, extending prior population-based evidence and providing a more integrated perspective on how different metabolic indices parallel hepatic steatosis and fibrotic burden. Consistent with these findings, our recent study in the same NHANES cohort showed that several systemic inflammatory indices were also associated with MASLD and liver fibrosis assessed by VCTE. Compared with inflammatory markers, the metabolic indices examined in the present study primarily reflect IR and adiposity, suggesting complementary metabolic and inflammatory dimensions of MASLD.[26]

A central early event in the pathogenesis of MASLD is systemic IR, which is captured by composite indices such as METS-IR and TyG index, both established non-insulin-based surrogates of whole-body insulin sensitivity that integrate glycemic and lipid measures in population studies.[27] These scores reflect impaired insulin signaling and dysregulated glucose–lipid metabolism, hallmarks of metabolic syndrome that have been repeatedly linked to hepatic steatosis across diverse cohorts.[28] Mechanistically, IR promotes hepatic de novo lipogenesis while reducing adipocyte suppression of lipolysis, resulting in increased flux of free fatty acids (FFAs) to the liver and accumulation of TGs within hepatocytes; this lipogenic diversion is a key driver of intrahepatic fat deposition in MASLD.[28] Elevated circulating FFAs derived from adipose tissue lipolysis further augment hepatic lipid storage and contribute to metabolic dysfunction, as adipose IR enhances FFA release and hepatic uptake.[29] Visceral fat has been linked to MASLD and metabolic syndrome in multiple cohort studies, highlighting its central role in disease progression.[30]

As FFAs and other lipid species accumulate within hepatocytes beyond the capacity of normal metabolic disposal, they are metabolized into lipotoxic intermediates such as diacylglycerols, ceramides, and lysophosphatidylcholine, which disrupt mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) function, impair organelle homeostasis, and trigger cellular stress pathways.[31] These toxic lipid metabolites provoke unfolded protein responses and activate kinases, including c-Jun N-terminal kinase, contributing to hepatocyte injury and cell death via apoptosis and oxidative damage.[31] Lipotoxicity also induces robust reactive oxygen species (ROS) production from dysfunctional mitochondria and ER stress, which in turn activates inflammatory signaling cascades such as nuclear factor kappa B and inflammasome pathways.[32] This pro-oxidative milieu promotes the release of damage-associated molecular patterns and proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6), facilitating recruitment of immune cells, including Kupffer cells and neutrophils to the liver, and amplifying hepatic inflammation.[33] Persistent hepatic inflammation both exacerbates IR and serves as a critical driver of fibrogenic processes. Kupffer cells and other innate immune populations produce fibrogenic cytokines (such as transforming growth factor-beta) and chemokines that activate hepatic stellate cells.[34] Once activated, hepatic stellate cells transdifferentiate into myofibroblast-like cells that synthesize extracellular matrix components, including collagen I and III, the hallmark of liver fibrosis.[34] In addition to direct hepatocyte injury and immune activation, metabolic feedback loops perpetuate fibrogenesis. Insulin resistance promotes adipose tissue lipolysis and increases FFA flux to the liver, further enhancing hepatic lipid burden.[35] Dysregulated adipokine secretion from expanded visceral fat depots (e.g., reduced adiponectin and elevated MCP-1) contributes to a chronic proinflammatory state that also drives stellate cell activation.[35] Emerging evidence also implicates gut-liver axis perturbations, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation into the portal circulation, to toll-like receptor-mediated hepatic immune activation, further linking systemic metabolic dysfunction with local liver injury and fibrosis progression.[36] Collectively, this mechanistic framework illustrates how excessive hepatic lipid accumulation leads to organelle dysfunction, oxidative and ER stress, proinflammatory signaling, and fibrogenesis. It supports the concept that fat-related metabolic dysregulation, reflected in composite indices of IR and adiposity, parallels the pathophysiological cascade from steatosis to fibroinflammatory liver disease.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the associations of fat-related metabolic indices with MASLD and liver fibrosis using VCTE data from a nationally representative cohort. By incorporating indices reflecting IR, visceral adiposity, and lipid accumulation, our analysis provides a comprehensive population-level description of metabolic features accompanying hepatic steatosis and fibrosis. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should be interpreted as concurrent relationships. Second, metabolic and inflammatory states are dynamic, and single measurements of fat-related metabolic indices may not capture longitudinal changes across MASLD progression. Third, these indices are derived from routine anthropometric and biochemical parameters rather than direct assessments of ectopic fat distribution or tissue-level metabolic dysfunction. Fourth, although VCTE offers a validated noninvasive assessment of steatosis and fibrosis, the lack of liver biopsy confirmation and longitudinal follow-up limits further mechanistic interpretation. Despite these limitations, our findings highlight consistent associations between IR–and adiposity-related metabolic indices and MASLD as well as liver fibrosis in the general population. Future prospective studies are warranted to clarify the temporal relationships underlying these metabolic patterns.

5. Conclusion

In this nationally representative cross-sectional study, fat-related metabolic indices, including METS-IR, METS-VF, CMI, TyG, WTI, and LAP, were significantly associated with hepatic steatosis and MASLD as assessed by VCTE. Higher levels of these indices were consistently correlated with greater hepatic fat accumulation, and positive associations with liver fibrosis were also observed after multivariable adjustment. These findings indicate that integrated measures reflecting IR and adiposity are closely linked to metabolic liver disease phenotypes. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be made, and further longitudinal studies are required to better characterize these relationships.
