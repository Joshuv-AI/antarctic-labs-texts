Incidence of late onset bloodstream infections and use of preventive practices in very low birth weight infants in Spain: the NeoKissEs cohort surveillance experience
Authors: Jose I Pijoan, Javier Pérez-López, Marisela Madrid, Carmen Ribes, Fátima Camba, Brar Piening, Begoña Loureiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBloodstream infections (BSI), especially central line associated BSI (CLABSI) are common severe healthcare associated infections (HAI) in very low birth weight (VLBW) neonates (< 1500 g at birth) admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). However, surveillance systems are known to contribute to reducing HAI. Our objectives were firstly, to describe BSI and CLABSI incidence in the 2015–2018 period for VLBW from 40 level III NICUs reporting data back to NeoKissEs, the Spanish neonatal surveillance system, secondly, to describe NICU organizational features and level of use of a set of recommended infection prevention (IP) practices and finally, to assess their relationships with BSI/CLABSI incidence.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort of VLBW infants. Using an online survey of NICU-level IP data, analysis of surveillance of individual data and NICU-specific IP information using random intercept logistic and Poisson models was carried out. Unadjusted and risk-adjusted incidence of neonates with ≥ 1 BSI episodes and BSI/CLABSI rates were estimated.ResultsData from 5260 VLBW infants were reported. Incidence of neonates with ≥ 1 BSI episodes (1420 newborns, 27.0%) decreased significantly during the study period (18.7% reduction (p = 0.002)), BSI rates dropped by 23.5% (p < 0.001) and CLABSI rates by 18.4% (p = 0.062). Lower incidence was associated only with the existence of hand hygiene (HH) audit and feedback activities (OR 0.62 [95% confidence interval 0.41–0.96]).ConclusionsThe NeoKissEs surveillance data show a high level of BSI, a downwards time trend and wide variation among NICUs in both BSI/CLABSI incidence and the extent of use of recommended IP practices. Systematic HH performance audit and feedback activities should be prioritized. NeoKissEs provides useful data for monitoring changes in BSI incidence and related factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01736-5.Keywords: Very low birth weight infant, Healthcare associated infection, Late onset bloodstream infection, Infection prevention, Surveillance, Benchmarking

Background

Around 1–1.5% of all live births correspond to very low birth weight (VLBW) infants (birth weight < 1500 g) [1, 2]. They constitute a high-risk population, with increased mortality and likelihood of short and long-term complications and sequelae [3, 4]. Healthcare associated infections (HAI) are commonplace during their stay in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), with late-onset bloodstream infections (BSI) (those occurring ≥ 72 h after birth) constituting the most frequent nosocomial infectious complication [5, 6]. There is evidence linking BSI with impairment in early and late neurodevelopmental performance [4, 7, 8]. Most BSI are linked to the use of invasive devices, especially central vascular lines (central line-associated BSI [CLABSI]), required to provide critical life support to this vulnerable population [5, 9, 10]. The frequency of this worrisome event varies broadly, both across countries and among units in the same country [11, 12]. Surveillance with associated feedback and benchmarking is a well-established preventive measure of HAI [9, 13]. Local systems, preferably integrated in neonatal epidemiologic surveillance networks [14], may be useful for systematically monitoring incidence and outcomes of HAI using standardised methods, and for assessing risk and prognostic factors. Besides being associated with significant reductions in HAI incidence [15–17], surveillance networks also provide an appropriate framework to assess patterns of use of infection prevention (IP) strategies [18], implement preventative, quality improvement interventions and assess their effects [19, 20]. In Spain, the NeoKissEs surveillance system, derived from the German NEO-KISS system [16], started collecting data in 2014 on late onset BSI episodes for VLBW infants from 45 level III NICUs [21]. The aims of this work are: (i) to describe incidence and time trends of these HAI in the 2015–2018 period in Spanish NICUs reporting to NeoKissEs, (ii) to describe the degree of implementation at NICU level of a set of recommended IP measures and (iii) to assess the relationship of BSI with the level of use of the preventive practices.

Methods

Design

This is a retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort. Over the 2015–2018 period, 44 level III Spanish NICUs (Table S1 in Supplementary Material 1) [22] provided regular clinical, demographic and perinatal data on VLBW infants to the NeoKissEs system [21]. Validated NEO-KISS definitions and procedures are used [23]. Information on clinical signs and laboratory and microbiological findings is collected each time a sepsis/BSI episode is suspected, to categorise it either as a microbiologically confirmed BSI or as a culture-negative, clinically suspected BSI, for which antibiotics is given for at least 5 days. Data on days of use of central and peripheral lines, ventilatory support devices and antibiotics are also obtained at patient level. More detailed information on the implementation and procedures of NeoKissEs can be found elsewhere [21].

Collection of infection prevention data

In the process of designing an educational quality improvement intervention aimed at decreasing CLABSI incidence, we designed a questionnaire and administered it by email in 2019 to NeoKissEs champions of the units that agreed to participate. The aim was to update data on structural and organizational features of participating sites and to obtain baseline information on the degree of adoption of various recommended IP practices at each unit (Table 1) [24, 25]. Data were requested on nursing staffing capabilities, assessment of hand hygiene (HH) quality and compliance with WHO recommendations, educational and training activities in safe practices for the NICU professionals, and availability of written, active protocols establishing specific procedures for several aspects of the use of CVC. After receipt of the completed questionnaire from a NICU, we convened a videoconference with the unit’s champion and neonatal and hospital IP staff to clarify uncertainties and ask for missing information. We analyse here the results from the 40 units which provided full data for the whole study period, agreed to participate in the new IP intervention project and therefore filled in the online survey.

Data management and analysis

BSI rates are presented for 1000 days of stay at the NICU, and CLABSI rates are calculated for 1000 days of use of CVC. All incidence rates are estimated using Poisson distribution with 95% confidence intervals. Data on neonate-to-nurse ratio and IP procedures in place were recorded at NICU level. Perinatal, demographic, and clinical data were recorded at patient level until exit from the surveillance system (death, transfer, discharge or reaching 1800 g in weight) [16, 21].

Frequencies and percentages are used for description of qualitative variables, and means and standard deviations or medians with interquartile range for continuous variables. One-way ANOVA or Mann–Whitney tests are used for simple comparisons of continuous variables and χ2 and Fisher tests for categorical variables.

Given the hierarchical structure of the data, logistic and Poisson random intercepts models were fitted, with NICU as a random effect and its significance assessed using null models. Initially, multivariable regression models were fitted on the set of collected individual clinical and demographic features to identify and adjust for predictor variables. The year of surveillance was then added to test for the existence of any time trend. Finally, unit-level data reported in the survey were added to the adjusted models. Models were compared and selected using likelihood ratio or Wald tests. The existence of interactions (by level of care and birth weight category) was assessed through the design and testing of multiplicative variables. Birth weight was divided into three levels (< 500 g/between 500 and 999 g / ≥ 1000 g) and gestational age was also analysed in three categories (< 24 weeks/between 24 and 28 weeks/ > 28 weeks). Significance level was set to α = 0.05. Stata 18.0 for Windows (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC) was used for all analyses (see "Statistical hypotheses and modelling strategies" in the Supplementary Material 1 for further explanation).

There was a significant downward trend in incidence over the four-year surveillance period (18.7% decrease; p = 0.002), with a statistically significant reduction in the 500–999 g group (16.5% reduction) and marginal significance in the highest weight group (20.5% reduction). Incidence varied across levels of complexity of care provided, but only IIIC units showed a statistically significant decrement (reduction of 30.4% in 2018 as compared to 2015; p < 0.001) (Table S3 and Figure S1).

1706 BSI episodes were recorded, with 1386 (81.2%) classified as CLABSI. Four hundred and twenty-eight episodes (25.1%) had no microorganism isolated in fluid cultures (clinical BSI) and 258 episodes (15.1%) had more than one identified bacteria. Overall BSI incidence was 8.7 episodes/1000 NICU days (95% confidence interval: 8.3–9.1). There was a global decline over the surveillance period (23.5% reduction from 2015 to 2018; p < 0.001 for linear trend and comparison of 2018 vs 2015) with significant decreases in the two highest birth weight groups (Table 3 and Fig. 2).

BSI incidence rates (× 1000 NICU-days). BSI: bloodstream infection; NICU: neonatal intensive care unit. Data for neonates < 500 g are not shown as they are based on very small numbers

BSI rates varied across levels of care (Figure S1). The overall decrease was significant in the IIIB and IIIC units, with the largest change in the latter (34.1% reduction; p < 0.001) (Table S3). Non-CLABSI rates (per 1000 NICU days without any central vascular catheter) showed a significant drop from 3.6 in 2015 to 2.3 in 2018 (p = 0.008).

All newborns had a CVC in place at some time during the surveillance follow-up (range 1–164 days). CLABSI global incidence was 17.4 episodes/1000 days of use of CVC (16.5–18.4). There was an overall reduction over the surveillance period (18.4%) that was marginally statistically significant (p = 0.062). All categories of birth weight showed lower rates in the last year assessed as compared with the first year, but none showed statistically significant reductions (Table 3 and Fig. 3).

Again, CLABSI rates varied across levels of care with the largest drop in level IIIC units (26.4%) (p = 0.015) (Table S3 and Figure S1).

Average time in the NICU with a CVC did not change over the study time (p = 0.602) but there was a significant reduction in average days with any antibiotic, from a median of 8 days to 7 days (p < 0.001).

Half the units reported having had HH performance audit and feedback activities in the study period and 14 (35.0%) having carried out educational programmes focused on safety practices for neonatal staff. Regarding IP practices related with management of CVC, five (12.5%) units declared the use of a dedicated tray/kit to store and allow quick access to all catheter insertion and maintenance equipment. Sixteen (40.0%) were using CVC insertion protocols with their corresponding checklists. Five sites (12.5%) had active policies for early CVC removal with systematically recorded assessment on patient records. 21 units (52.5%) used aqueous 2% CHX as skin antiseptic for CVC insertion for all VLBW neonates, while nine (22.5%) used lower concentrations (0.5% or 1% aqueous CHX). Ten units (25.0%) used two different aqueous concentrations (0.5% or 1% and 2%) depending on the degree of skin maturity of the neonate and seven NICUs (1 IIIA, 5 IIIB and one IIIC) used both aqueous and alcohol-based CHX. Thirty-four units (85.0%) used intravenous needleless connectors for CVC maintenance. All six NICUs which reported not using them were IIIB units (p = 0.046). 70% isopropyl alcohol was most commonly used as the antiseptic in catheter maintenance (17 units; 42.5%). Alcohol-based 2% CHX was used in 14 NICUs (35.0%) with lower concentrations used in nine sites. There were no statistically significant differences across levels of complexity of care in frequency of use of these practices apart from the use of needleless connectors (Table 4).

Multivariable models

Individual features significantly associated with BSI and CLABSI occurrence were sex, birth weight and gestational age categories (Table 5). Level of complexity of care was also associated, and IIIC NICUs had the highest incidence rates. No significant interactions of the association of surveillance time with BSI and CLABSI incidence by gestational age or birth weight categories were found. The logistic random-intercept model, adjusted for these factors, showed a statistically significant decline in incidence of neonates experiencing at least one BSI episode in 2018 in comparison with 2015 (OR 0.75; [0.62–0.91]; p = 0.003) (test for linear time trend p = 0.004) (Table 5).

General and vascular catheter-related IP measures and association with BSI incidence

NICUs declaring HH audits and feedback activities showed a significantly lower likelihood that their neonates had at least one BSI episode (adjusted OR 0.62; [0.41–0.96]; p = 0.031), lower incidence of BSI (IRR 0.70 [0.51–0.97]; p = 0.032) and reduced CLABSI rates (IRR 0.73 [0.53–1.00]; p = 0.052) (Table 6). No other general or catheter-related reported IP practice was individually associated with a significantly reduced incidence of BSI. When the number of CVC-specifically related IP measures in use was pooled, although the occurrence pattern was consistently lower for newborns admitted to NICUs with at least one measure adopted, neither a dose–effect relationship nor a significant reduction was observed (Table 6).

Discussion

Our study shows a statistically significant and clinically meaningful decrease in the overall incidence of BSI and CLABSI over a period of four years in forty level III Spanish NICUs starting regular reporting to NeoKissEs, a standardized surveillance system for VLBW neonates (Tables 3 and 5, Figure S2). Reduction occurred at all levels of birth weight and complexity of care, although it was greatest in level IIIC units, which usually care for the sickest neonates (Figure S1). Additionally, a survey on the use of some patient safety strategies and central-line related IP practices showed low levels of adoption and wide variability among participating units (Table 4). HH audit and feedback activities in the NICU were associated with a lower risk of BSI and CLABSI. No other single general or specific reported preventative strategy was significantly associated with a further decrease in BSI frequency (Table 6).

It is generally agreed that continuous, standardized surveillance of HAI, carried out either at local level [10] or in the context of a network of neonatal units [9, 13–17, 23, 26–29] can contribute to their reduction. Systematic generation of accurate reference data on HAI along with its dissemination to healthcare professionals and managers serves to highlight the burden of neonatal acquired infections and stimulates comparative positioning (benchmarking). It also promotes the search for potentially modifiable risk factors and the design and implementation of preventive and quality improvement actions [14–18]. Observed BSI reductions in NeoKissEs in the 2015–8 period are in line with those reported by other surveillance systems [9, 15, 16] and with the 20% decrease expected according to Gastmeier et al. [26]. In our cohort we have also found a significant reduction in the incidence of BSI non associated with the use of central lines. This result is similar to that reported by Zipursky et al. [9] from the Canadian Neonatal Network, although achieved in a shorter follow-up period.

NeoKissEs BSI rates are higher than those reported by other neonatal surveillance systems [9, 10, 12, 16, 28, 30, 31]. In some cases, direct comparisons are not reliable, as different definitions of late onset sepsis/BSI are used or only culture-proven cases are recorded. Our surveillance system follows NEO-KISS validated criteria [15, 16, 23, 28, 30] that include clinical BSI, with negative cultures and antibiotic therapy administered for at least 5 days. These cases make up a quarter of all our reported episodes. The NeoKissEs incidence of BSI is still consistently well above figures published in European networks that use the NEO-KISS criteria [16, 30, 32]. Although our data show lower caesarean rates and a higher proportion of multiple gestations than published German data [16], differences in rates probably reflect different organizational and clinical practice patterns, which is well-recognized to associate with variation in BSI rates [12, 33, 34].

It is also noticeable that IIIA NICUs rates are similar to or even higher than their IIIB counterparts. No clear differential IP practices between these two levels of care are apparent from our study. It should be highlighted, however, that more than 30% of VLBW neonates attended in IIIA units have a birth weight of < 1000 g, which does not meet the recommended standards on levels of care proposed by the Spanish Society of Neonatology (Table S1).

Our survey shows a highly variable and low use of recommended, guideline-based safe IP practices [24, 25] (Table 4) and a pattern of lower BSI incidence in NICUs with more IP strategies adopted. The substantial variability in antiseptics used for skin preparation before catheter insertion should be emphasized.

There is little published evidence regarding level of implementation and impact of compliance with specific IP actions in NICUs. Zachariah et al. [34] assessed compliance with central-line-related preventive practices in 190 NICUs in the USA. They found a high rate of written policies available but only moderate adherence to them. Only very high compliance (≥ 95%) with the use of an insertion checklist and assessment of daily line necessity were significantly associated with lower CLABSI rates. Although authors asked for monitoring of HH in the process of CVC insertion, no information is provided about a potential association of its level of compliance with CLABSI rates. Mahieu et al. [32], in a study of Belgian NICUs using NEO-KISS surveillance criteria, showed a moderate adherence to guideline-based preventive practices related to CVC use. Although again a high proportion of units had written policies, there were wide differences between NICUs in compliance with specific components, such as for example the skin antiseptic used for insertion, or the use of transparent dressing for CVC maintenance. Their study shows significantly lower CLABSI rates for units with better adherence to central line insertion and quality control recommended practices. In contrast with our results, all Belgian units participating in the study had some active monitoring system aimed at collecting and reporting HH compliance data.

A higher heterogeneity and lower compliance with guidelines among NICUs in catheter maintenance practices is generally reported [32, 35, 36]. Our study also shows a remarkable absence of explicit protocols regarding systematic assessment of the need to keep the central line, and high variability in the use of antiseptics in maintenance procedures. Use of antiseptics on the skin of VLBW infants remains a controversial issue, with an unclear trade-off between efficacy and safety, especially concerning skin burns in the most premature newborns. The pilot ARCTIC trial results [36] and recent data from Pinilla-Gonzalez et al. [37] suggest that both aqueous and alcohol-based 2% CHX solutions are efficacious and safe in preterm babies when carefully administered. Our data, previous to the ARTIC results, show that NeoKissEs NICUs universally used aqueous CHX before catheter insertion, often in concentrations lower than 2% (Table 4). Only seven units (17.5%) also used alcohol-based CHX for skin preparation, a figure lower than that reported in 2018 by neonatal units in England and Wales [38]. Regarding antisepsis in routine CVC maintenance, our NICUs most often applied 70% alcohol and about a third cleaned the ports with 2% CHX vs the 52% reported in the PREVAIL study [38].

Our study has several strengths that merit comment. First, it is based on a large sample size, with standardized data collected from a surveillance network [21] which currently collects individual patient data from around three-quarters of level III Spanish NICUs. Second, BSI definitions follow the German NEO-KISS epidemiological definitions, which have been specifically designed and validated in the neonatal population [23] and are increasingly used at European level. Third, we have included in our analyses only the 40 NICUs that provided data regularly in the whole study period and agreed to answer a survey on IP practices in order to start an intervention study. Distribution of neonates across the prespecified gestational age and birth weight strata remained stable over time. In this way we could avoid the potential bias induced by calculating rates based on a variable number of sites or on populations with different baseline risks [27]. Fourth, in addition to using an online questionnaire to obtain information on local IP practices, we convened additional videoconferences with key infection and prevention staff at each unit, to increase accuracy and completeness of information.

There are also limitations in our study. First, data on VLBW neonates for the NeoKissEs system are identified and entered by each NICU´s team. There may be inconsistencies among centers in aspects such as training in identification of clinical BSI (e.g. local practices regarding the number, procedures and time schedule of cultures) which might affect their diagnostic yield. Second, data on local IP practices relied on self-reporting by the healthcare staff, with no external validation carried out. Third, our thresholds for considering that there was compliance with some specific items were mild. For instance, if a NICU had carried out HH monitoring for one year of the study follow-up or reported use of its CVC insertion protocol and checklist in at least 50% of non-urgent cases, we categorized these items as complied with. Fourth and perhaps most relevant, our survey only covered some policies and IP practices. There are quite a few other unmeasured factors (e.g. physical infrastructure of the NICU, ease of access to specific devices, breastfeeding and nutritional practices, antibiotic stewardship programmes, etc.) likely to also differ between participating units and to influence BSI/CLABSI rates. Therefore, although we adjusted our analyses for some well-known risk factors and took into account the hierarchical, correlated structure of our data, potential for confounding remains in our observational design.

Conclusions

Our study shows that level III Spanish NICUs reporting data on VLBW neonates to the NeoKissEs surveillance system in the 2015–2018 period had on average higher BSI and CLABSI incidence rates as compared with data reported by other surveillance networks. These rates declined gradually over the study period, with CLABSI decreasing at a lower rate. Many such units did not have a full set of IP protocols and strategies in place to reduce the frequency of their HAI. In addition, the degree of adherence to available protocols and/or existing guidelines was low and heterogeneous. Units which carried out HH monitoring and feedback activities showed significantly lower rates. We did not find any significant association between a series of individual general and CVC related practices, with BSI or their time trend. It is imperative that our NICUs make an (individual and collaborative) effort to identify gaps in their current HAI preventive practices and implement and assess those more likely to be effective. HH performance monitoring and feedback should be given high priority.

Systematic epidemiologic surveillance of HAI has proven to be a useful tool for assessing the frequency and nature of these infections, monitoring time changes and helping identify target practice gaps. The NeoKissEs team is currently doing interventional research on these issues. It is advisable to extend benchmarking at an international level and make surveillance indicators comparable. The current NeoIPC surveillance system in development [39] is a step forward along this path and can provide motivation to increase efforts for the prevention and control of these worrisome events.
