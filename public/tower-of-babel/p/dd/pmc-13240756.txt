Partial pupil-sparing third nerve palsy as a manifestation of cerebral toxoplasmosis in an HIV-positive patient: A case report
Authors: Hadeel Abdulrahman Aljohani, Ahmed S. Alzahrani, Abdulaziz S. Alrashid, Ismail Tuwair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeNeuro-ophthalmic manifestations are common in patients with advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection and may result from opportunistic intracranial infections. However, partial pupil-sparing third cranial nerve palsy is an uncommon presentation in this population and may be misleading, as it is often presumed to be ischemic. We report an atypical presentation of cerebral toxoplasmosis manifesting as partial pupil-sparing third nerve palsy.ObservationsWe report the case of a 37-year-old HIV-positive male with advanced human immunodeficiency virus infection who presented with headache, seizures, and binocular diplopia and was found to have a partial pupil-sparing third nerve palsy. Neuroimaging revealed multiple intracranial enhancing lesions with surrounding vasogenic edema. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated varicella zoster virus positivity, and stereotactic brain biopsy ultimately confirmed cerebral toxoplasmosis. The patient was managed with antimicrobial therapy and showed clinical improvement.Conclusions and importanceIn patients with human immunodeficiency virus infection, pupil-sparing third nerve palsy should not be presumed to be exclusively ischemic, as infectious and other opportunistic etiologies may underlie its presentation. Careful clinical assessment, detailed examination, and appropriate neuroimaging are essential for accurate diagnosis and timely management.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is associated with a broad spectrum of neurologic abnormalities due to involvement of the brain, cranial nerves, peripheral nerves, and ocular pathways.1 Opportunistic infections further contribute to neurological morbidity in patients with human immunodeficiency virus, and commonly implicated pathogens include toxoplasmosis, tuberculosis, cytomegalovirus, and varicella zoster virus.2 Neurologic complications remain an important source of morbidity in patients with human immunodeficiency virus and encompass a broad spectrum of central and peripheral nervous system disorders.3

Involvement of the oculomotor nerve can occur at multiple anatomical sites along its nuclear, fascicular, subarachnoid, cavernous sinus, and orbital pathways.4 Fascicular involvement may produce distinct neurological syndromes, while the subarachnoid portion is the most common site of isolated oculomotor nerve paresis.4 HIV-related third nerve palsy may arise from direct viral effects, opportunistic infection, inflammatory processes, or compressive lesions.2 We present a case of partial pupil-sparing third nerve palsy secondary to biopsy proven cerebral toxoplasmosis in a patient with advanced human immunodeficiency virus infection consistent with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).

Case presentation

A 37-year-old male with a known diagnosis of human immunodeficiency virus since 2016 and no other reported medical or surgical history presented with a two-month history of progressively worsening headache associated with nausea and vomiting. Three weeks prior to presentation, he experienced a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, followed by the development of binocular diplopia. In the days preceding presentation, his family noted increasing drowsiness. He denied fever, weight loss, cough, substance use, or exposure to sick contacts.

On examination, he was drowsy but arousable, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14/15. Cranial nerve examination revealed a partial right third cranial nerve palsy characterized by ptosis and limitation of adduction, elevation, and depression, with preservation of pupillary function. The right pupil measured 2 mm and was reactive to light, while the left eye demonstrated full extraocular movements and normal pupillary responses. Other cranial nerves were intact, and motor and sensory examinations were grossly normal. Ophthalmologic assessment demonstrated best-corrected visual acuity of 6/12 in both eyes, intraocular pressures of 17 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye, and color vision was assessed using Ishihara plates. Anterior segment examination and fundus evaluation were unremarkable, with no evidence of retinitis, chorioretinitis, or features suggestive of raised intracranial pressure.

Laboratory investigations confirmed HIV infection with a CD4 count of 61 cells/μL, hemoglobin levels ranging between 10 and 11 g/dL, a normal white blood cell count, and mild hyponatremia. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed no bacterial growth but was positive for varicella zoster virus on multiplex polymerase chain reaction testing. Given the diagnostic uncertainty, a stereotactic brain biopsy of the left frontal lesion was performed, which demonstrated reactive brain tissue with mixed inflammatory infiltrates and encysted organisms consistent with cerebral toxoplasmosis on histopathological examination. Following confirmation of the diagnosis, the patient was managed in collaboration with neurosurgery, infectious disease, and ophthalmology services and was treated with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, acyclovir, fluconazole, and levetiracetam. Acyclovir was initiated due to the detection of varicella zoster virus in the cerebrospinal fluid, while fluconazole was administered empirically in the context of severe immunosuppression to provide coverage for possible fungal infections. His clinical condition gradually improved, with increasing alertness and improved verbal responsiveness, and he was discharged in stable condition on trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole 300 mg three times daily for six weeks, acyclovir 550 mg three times daily for 14 days, fluconazole 200 mg once daily based on clinical response, and levetiracetam 500 mg twice daily for one year. However, detailed follow-up data, including specific neuro-ophthalmic outcomes and CD4 count changes after treatment, were not available.

Discussion

The oculomotor nerve contains parasympathetic fibers supplying the ciliary muscles and sphincter pupillae, and somatic fibers supplying the superior, medial, and inferior recti, inferior oblique, and levator palpebrae superioris.4 Lesions affecting the nerve may occur at the nuclear, fascicular, subarachnoid, cavernous sinus, or orbital levels, with fascicular involvement associated with syndromes such as Benedikt, Weber, Nothnagel, and Claude.4

HIV infection produces neurological disease either through direct viral effects on the nervous system or indirectly by predisposing affected individuals to opportunistic infections and neoplasms.2 Opportunistic infections implicated in cranial neuropathies include toxoplasmosis, tuberculosis, cytomegalovirus, and varicella zoster virus.2 Neurologic complications remain an important source of morbidity and mortality in people living with human immunodeficiency virus and encompass a broad spectrum of central and peripheral nervous system disorders.3 Histological studies have also shown substantial brain and optic nerve injury in patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome.1

In this case, severe immunosuppression consistent with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), with a CD4 count of 61 cells/μL was associated with multiple intracranial lesions and neuro-ophthalmic dysfunction. The patient presented with partial pupil-sparing third nerve palsy. Pupil-sparing third nerve palsy is classically associated with microvascular ischemia, a pattern often attributed to relative preservation of the superficially located parasympathetic fibers while deeper somatic fibers are affected.4 However, pupil sparing is not specific for ischemic etiologies and has also been described in compressive and intra-axial lesions.4 Infectious causes of pupil-sparing third nerve palsy have likewise been reported.5 In the present case, the combination of advanced human immunodeficiency virus infection, multiple enhancing intracranial lesions, and surrounding vasogenic edema raises the possibility that local lesion location or mass effect selectively involved the somatic component of the oculomotor pathway while sparing pupillomotor fibers. Given the presence of multiple intracranial lesions and surrounding vasogenic edema, a fascicular or intra-axial process affecting the oculomotor pathway is a plausible explanation for the observed pupil-sparing pattern. Thus, in immunocompromised patients, pupil sparing should not be used in isolation to exclude secondary intracranial pathology. The neuroimaging findings of multiple enhancing lesions with vasogenic edema are characteristic of cerebral toxoplasmosis but can also be seen in tuberculosis, fungal infections, lymphoma, and viral vasculitis.2,6 The detection of varicella zoster virus in the cerebrospinal fluid represents a potentially confounding finding. VZV is recognized among the infectious neurologic complications encountered in patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; however, the clinical and radiological features in this case were more consistent with cerebral toxoplasmosis. Furthermore, the diagnosis was confirmed by stereotactic brain biopsy. While VZV infection may have contributed to the clinical presentation or represented a concurrent process, the possibility of co-infection cannot be entirely excluded.

The ophthalmologic findings in this patient reflect selective involvement of the somatic fibers of the oculomotor nerve, with sparing of the superficial parasympathetic fibers responsible for pupillary constriction.4 The normal anterior and posterior segment examinations confirmed that the visual complaints were due to neuro-ophthalmic rather than primary ocular pathology.

Conclusion

Partial pupil-sparing third nerve palsy in an HIV-positive patient should prompt urgent evaluation for opportunistic intracranial infections. This case demonstrates that cerebral toxoplasmosis can manifest with neuro-ophthalmic deficits and highlights the importance of detailed cranial nerve assessment, comprehensive neuroimaging, and multidisciplinary care. Brain biopsy may be necessary in cases of diagnostic uncertainty, as illustrated in this case where definitive diagnosis required histopathological confirmation.
