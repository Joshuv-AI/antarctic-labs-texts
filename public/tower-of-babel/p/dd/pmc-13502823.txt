Scoping Cognitive Screening in Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Christopher W.P. Hopkins
Published: 2026

The recent study by Raje et al (1) is original in its approach and affords novel insights: It is one of only a handful of studies to attempt to longitudinally monitor cognitive performance in people living with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and used 2 practical cognitive screens not previously used in this context. Despite novel approaches, the monograph's conclusions regarding the viability of cognitive interrogation at the time of routine endoscopic examination raise eyebrows.

The authors propose that appointments for routine colonoscopic examination may represent a feasible time to screen for cognitive impairment in IBD. The authors acknowledge that periprocedural factors, such as bowel preparation, fasting, sleep disturbance, anxiety, and situational stress, may transiently impair cognitive performance of participants undergoing endoscopy, and people with IBD are more susceptible to these effects (2,3). To my knowledge, only a solitary study has scrutinized baseline (not immediately preprocedural) cognitive performance versus that pericolonoscopic procedure: This demonstrated an approximately 1.5 point mean decrement in overall Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) performance from the eve of colonoscopy to the day of examination itself, with cognitive rebound thereafter (3). In the original study, the substantially elevated prevalence of cognitive impairment around the time of examination—many-fold in excess of expected population levels—in a sample with a mean age of only 49 years, adds to the suggestion that there is a temporary procedure-associated state effect on cognition, rather than an enduring trait cognitive impairment or decline (4). The striking cognitive recovery of control participants in long-term follow-up adds credence to this assertion.

On the basis of the present study's findings and limited broader context, I would contend that cognitive screening at the time of endoscopic examination would represent an unfavorable juncture for accurate and informative cognitive evaluation, given the substantial risk of false-positive findings. To champion screening at the time of routine surveillance endoscopic procedure, further studies would be required specifically examining usual baseline cognitive performance with that periprocedurally and also—and missing from this study—acceptability of such screening to people undergoing colonoscopic examination: an already challenging, stressful, and uncertain period.

I concur with the authors' recommendations that more research is needed in examining cognitive function in people living with IBD, but focused work is now required specifically investigating practical cognitive screens and their validation for their use in an IBD-specific context (the authors' use of a MOCA iteration and the TabCAT Brain Health Assessment adds to knowledge here) and to correlate findings with functional performance. Thus far, only a single study has attempted to correlate practical cognitive screen (MOCA) performance with occupational function, and this did not reveal tangible functional impacts: The significance of any cognitive change in IBD is unclear, and the merits of routine screening remain moot (5). Advances in each of the aforementioned regards are needed for the scale and prognostic significance of cognitive impacts in IBD to better understand and for the scope of practical screening, whether that is in and around the time of surveillance colonoscopy or otherwise, to be established and ethically and acceptably taken forward.
