Modeling the transmission dynamics of endemic meningitis: stability analysis and long-term persistence
Authors: Samaila Jackson Yaga, Funmilayo Westnand Oshogboye Saporu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study develops and analyzes a deterministic compartmental model to understand the transmission dynamics of Neisseria meningitidis under endemic conditions. Compartment profile plots illustrate the progression of the disease to an endemic level over time. Explicit expressions for the endemic equilibrium points and the basic reproductive number are derived. The basic reproductive number is decomposed into three interpretable components: contributions from (i) carriers, (ii) carriers progressing to symptomatic cases, and (iii) direct symptomatic cases (i.e., infectives bypassing carriership), highlighting the role of both carriers and infectives in long-term disease persistence. Sensitivity analysis identifies the transmission rate, the odds in favor of carrier infectivity, and the rate of loss of carriership as critical parameters influencing the basic reproductive number. Phase-plane plots (S–C and S–I) illustrate how variation in the basic reproductive number shapes the trajectory of convergence to the endemic equilibrium. Stability analysis shows that the endemic equilibrium is unstable when the product of the basic reproductive number and the susceptible population at equilibrium exceeds unity. Using Lyapunov functions and LaSalle’s invariance principle, we further show that the long-term dynamics are fully determined by this threshold: the disease-free equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable when the basic reproductive number is less than one, whereas a unique endemic equilibrium emerges and is globally stable when it is greater than one, demonstrating the persistence or elimination of meningitis depending on the threshold.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13589-9.

Introduction

Meningitis, an acute inflammation of the meninges, remains a serious global public health concern due to its high mortality rates and severe neurological sequelae among survivors [1]. The disease can be caused by a range of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi, with bacterial meningitis posing the greatest threat due to its rapid progression and epidemic potential [2, 3]. Among bacterial agents, Neisseria meningitidis is a leading cause of meningitis epidemics, especially in the African meningitis belt, a region stretching from Senegal to Ethiopia that experiences recurrent seasonal outbreaks [4, 5].

The dynamics of meningitis transmission are influenced by a complex interplay of climatic, demographic, and behavioral factors. Seasonal variations in temperature, humidity, and dust levels have been strongly associated with increased disease incidence, particularly during the dry season [6, 7]. Furthermore, the disease’s epidemiology is modulated by age-specific susceptibility, with the highest burden observed among children under five years, adolescents, and young adults [8]. In endemic settings, asymptomatic carriage plays a critical role in maintaining transmission, with carriers serving as reservoirs for the spread of pathogenic strains [9–11].

Given the significant public health burden of meningitis, mathematical models have emerged as vital tools for understanding the disease’s dynamics and informing control strategies. Mathematical models allow for the exploration of key epidemiological factors, the prediction of disease outcomes under various intervention scenarios and the identification of critical thresholds for disease control [12, 13].

Endemic models are particularly important for characterizing the conditions under which meningitis persists within a population and for evaluating the effectiveness of long-term intervention strategies. Deterministic models have been widely employed to study meningitis dynamics in endemic settings [14–19]. Most of these studies focus on assessing the impact of vaccination strategies, often incorporating age structure into the modeling framework. More recently, Mustapha et al. [20] integrated the effects of carrier diagnosis and prophylactic treatment on meningitis transmission dynamics.

In contrast, stochastic and deterministic models developed under epidemic settings in [11, 21] excluded demographic turnover and long-term endemic behavior. These works extended the epidemic framework of Irving et al. [17] by incorporating two disease stages (infection with and without complications), allowing a fraction of susceptible individuals to transition directly to the infectious class without passing through the carrier state, and accounting for differential transmission rates between carriers and infectives.

In this study, we develop a deterministic model for meningitis endemicity, building on the framework of [11]. The primary objective is to elucidate the key mechanisms governing the transmission dynamics, stability, and long-term persistence of meningitis under endemic conditions. Specifically, we aim to:(i)establish conditions for the local and global stability of the endemic equilibrium,(ii)characterize the long-term persistence of meningitis in the population, and(iii)compare two model formulations to assess the impact of alternative infection pathways on equilibrium behavior and the convergence of solution trajectories.

The basic reproductive number

The basic reproductive number, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document}, is a fundamental threshold parameter in epidemiology, determining the conditions for endemic stability and the potential for epidemic outbreaks. It is defined as the expected number of secondary cases generated by a single infected individual in a fully susceptible population. The derivation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document} follows the next-generation matrix framework proposed by Diekmann et al. [23], which offers a robust mathematical approach for compartmental models. This method involves decomposing the system into a transmission matrix (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T$$\end{document}), representing the rates of new infections, and a transition matrix (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V$$\end{document}), describing the progression through infection compartments. Stability analysis

In theoretical epidemiology, stability analysis plays a crucial role in understanding the long-term behavior of disease dynamics. It focuses on whether solutions to dynamical system models converge to equilibrium points, indicating whether a disease will die out, persist, or reach a stable endemic state. Smooth convergence suggests a stable balance between disease transmission and control measures, while oscillatory behavior may signal periodic disease outbreaks influenced by complex population interactions or external factors. Analyzing these dynamics provide valuable insights into epidemic control strategies and the conditions required for disease eradication or sustained management.

Conclusion

In this study, a six-compartment deterministic model proposed in Yaga and Saporu (2024) was extended to investigate the transmission dynamics of meningococcal meningitis in an endemic setting. Two model variants (Model I: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi = 0.5$$\end{document} and Model II: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi = 0$$\end{document}) were examined to compare disease dynamics under differing assumptions on infection pathways.

Compartmental profile plots under varying birth–death rates demonstrated the system’s tendency to converge toward an endemic equilibrium, with Model II exhibiting faster convergence and higher endemic levels in the recovered and death compartments compared to Model I. Notably, the presence or absence of damped oscillations in these trajectories further distinguished the two models, indicating differences in temporal disease dynamics.

An explicit expression for the basic reproductive number was derived and decomposed into three interpretable components: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_C$$\end{document}, representing the contribution from carriers; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{CI}$$\end{document}, accounting for carriers progressing to symptomatic infection; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_I$$\end{document}, corresponding to direct symptomatic infections. Sensitivity analysis revealed that the basic reproductive number is most responsive to the transmission rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}), the odds in favour of carrier infectivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}), and the rate of loss from the carrier state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}). Notably, unlike epidemic settings, the endemic reproductive number also exhibits sensitivity to the carrier transmission pathway, further emphasizing the critical role of carriers in sustaining long-term disease persistence.

A detailed equilibrium analysis established the existence of both disease-free and endemic equilibrium points. Numerical investigations showed that the time required to reach the endemic equilibrium decreases as the basic reproductive number increases. Importantly, even when only a small fraction of the population remains susceptible at equilibrium, the continuous influx of new susceptibles through births ensures the persistence of the disease at low endemic levels.

Local stability analysis [13, 30], based on Jacobian matrices and eigenvalue decomposition, showed that the endemic and disease-free equilibria are asymptotically unstable when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0 s^* > 1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0 > 1$$\end{document}, respectively, indicating that small perturbations can lead to divergent dynamics in these regimes. As expected, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0 < 1$$\end{document}, the disease dies out.

Using Lyapunov functions and LaSalle’s invariance principle, we further established that the global dynamics of the model are fully governed by the basic reproductive number: the disease-free equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable when the basic reproductive number is less than one, whereas a unique endemic equilibrium emerges and is globally asymptotically stable when the basic reproductive number exceeds one. This result rigorously confirms the threshold-driven persistence or elimination of meningococcal meningitis in the population.

Overall, this work provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for understanding endemic meningitis dynamics and offers valuable insights for targeted intervention strategies, particularly those aimed at asymptomatic carriers, to achieve long-term disease control and potential elimination.
