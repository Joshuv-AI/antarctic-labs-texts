Taurine stimulates EPO production in feline renal cells through the HIF pathway
Authors: Jiaxi Li, Yan Ma, Yi Gao, Yanping Xu, Lei Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 30% of elderly felines, impairing erythropoietin (EPO) secretion and leading to anemia, which significantly compromises feline health. Betaine, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and taurine are bioactive compounds known to modulate physiological processes, exhibiting anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. This study aimed to investigate their potential effects on EPO secretion in renal cells and their therapeutic potential for hematopoietic system improvement and anemia treatment. Two feline renal cell lines, PETCC191 and PETCC3002, were cultured and treated with betaine, DHA, and taurine. EPO expression was analyzed using western blot (WB) and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). EPO secretion was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The involvement of the HIF1α pathway was examined using the HIF1α inhibitor YC-1. Among the three compounds, taurine significantly upregulated both mRNA and protein levels of EPO and enhanced its secretion in both PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells, even under hypoxic conditions. Mechanistic studies revealed that taurine-mediated EPO production in feline renal cells occurs through the HIF1α pathway. Our findings demonstrate that taurine significantly enhances the synthesis and secretion of EPO in feline renal cells, suggesting its potential as a renal protective agent and a promising bioactive compound for the treatment of renal anemia in felines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46877-0.Keywords: Taurine, Anemia, EPO, HIF1αSubject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Medical research, Nephrology, Physiology

Introduction

Anemia associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been extensively studied in both humans and companion animals1. Erythropoiesis is primarily regulated by the production of erythropoietin (EPO), a hematopoietic growth factor synthesized in response to anemia. EPO is mainly produced by peritubular interstitial cells located in the inner cortex and outer medulla of the kidney. Its synthesis is primarily stimulated by renal hypoxemia, with the production rate inversely proportional to the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity. EPO exerts its effects in the bone marrow, where it binds to receptors on erythroid progenitor cells, promoting erythropoiesis2.

The regulation of red blood cell (RBC) mass and blood oxygen-carrying capacity by EPO represents an elegant system for maintaining systemic oxygen homeostasis. This homeostatic mechanism is governed by hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), oxygen-sensitive transcriptional activators that control EPO gene expression. Under normoxic conditions, HIFs are degraded, whereas hypoxia stabilizes HIFs, enabling them to bind to the EPO gene and enhance its transcription into mRNA, ultimately increasing plasma EPO levels3.

Taurine, a sulfur-containing amino acid naturally present in various foods and commonly added to energy drinks and dietary supplements, has garnered considerable attention for its potential health benefits4,5. Extensive research has explored its physiological effects, yet its role in ameliorating anemia associated with CKD remains unclear. Therefore, this study investigates the impact of taurine on EPO secretion in renal cells.

Methods

Cell culture and treatment

Feline renal cell lines PETCC191 and PETCC3002, obtained from the Pets’ Cell Resource Center (PETCC, Tianjin, China), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium F12 (DMEM/F12) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified environment. In cell proliferation, qRT-PCR, WB, and ELISA experiments, cells were seeded in 96-well or 6-well plates and cultured until they reached 80% confluence. Unless otherwise stated, the cells were then typically treated with 80 µM taurine (MCE, HY-B0351), 20 µM betaine (MCE, HY-B0710), 20 µM DHA (MCE, HY-B2167) and 10 µM YC-1 (MCE, HY-14927) for 24 h, respectively. Subsequently, cells or cell culture supernatants were harvested for further analysis. For hypoxia treatment experiments, PETCC191 or PETCC3002 cells were incubated for 24 h in either a normoxic (20% O₂) incubator or a hypoxic (2% O₂) incubator with nitrogen supplementation, respectively.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience) and then subjected to cDNA synthesis using the 4×Reverse Transcription Master Mix (EZBioscience). The expression of target genes was detected by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using the qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (EZBioscience). All experimental procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The Ct values were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and the final results were normalized to the expression level of actin, which served as an internal control. The sequences of primers (synthesized by BioSune, Shanghai) used for qRT-PCR are shown as blow:

Actin: Forward (5’→3’): CTCACTCGCCGCAGCC.

Reverse (5’→3’): CTCTTCCATTGCGGTCGGT.

EPO: Forward (5’→3’): GAGATGGGGTCGTGCGAATG.

Reverse (5’→3’): CCCATCGTGACATTTTCGGC.

HIF1α: Forward (5’→3’): GCTGACCCTGCACTCAATCA.

Reverse (5’→3’): CACTGGGACTGGTAGGCTCA.

Western blot

After treatment, cells were harvested and lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysates were then boiled in 1× SDS loading buffer for 5–10 min and resolved on SDS-PAGE. Membranes were blocked using 5% milk in TBST buffer and probed with the indicated antibodies (Proteintech, China). The images were captured using the Tanon 5200 imaging system (Tanon, China). Western blot quantification was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA). Band intensities of EPO and HIF-1α were normalized to tubulin after background subtraction. Relative expression was calculated relative to control (set as 1.0).

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were exposed to the indicated drugs at the specified doses. Following incubation with CCK-8 reagent (Meilunbio, China) at 37 °C for 1–2 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiscan Spectrum (Thermo) to assess cell proliferation.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells were pretreated with taurine (80 µM, 24 h). Soluble levels of EPO in cells from the different experimental groups were determined using commercial assay kits (CUSABIO Technology, https://www.cusabio.com/) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data in bar graphs are represented as fold change or percentage relative to the control, with the standard deviation of three independent experiments with similar results. Student’s t test was used for comparison between two groups to determine significant differences. Matched groups (three or more) were compared using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Levels of significance are indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Taurine increases the viability of feline renal cells

To determine the appropriate and safe concentrations of the compounds, we first used the CCK-8 assay to evaluate the effects of betaine, DHA, and taurine on the viability of feline renal cells. The results showed that betaine and DHA had no significant effect on the proliferation of PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells within the concentration range of 1 to 20 µM (Fig. 1A and D). Taurine exhibited a dose-dependent trend in promoting the proliferation of PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells (Fig. 1E and F). These results indicate that within the tested concentration range, betaine and DHA did not adversely affect cell proliferation, while taurine even promoted it. Therefore, based on the CCK-8 assay results showing no reduction in cell viability, these concentrations were considered safe for subsequent experiments.

Taurine increases the viability of feline renal cells. (A–F) Cell viability was measured in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells following 24 h of treatment with betaine (A, B), DHA (C, D), or taurine (E, F) at the indicated concentrations (n = 6). ***P < 0.0001. All data are presented as the mean ± SD.

Taurine promotes HIF1α and EPO expression in feline renal cells

Next, we investigated the effects of betaine, DHA, and taurine on the expression of EPO and HIF1α in PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells under indicated concentrations. qRT-PCR experiments revealed that treatment with different concentrations of betaine and DHA had no significant effect on the mRNA levels of EPO and HIF1α in PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells. However, taurine upregulated the mRNA levels of EPO and HIF1α in both cell lines in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2A and L). Additionally, WB experiments demonstrated that only taurine treatment upregulated the protein levels of EPO and HIF1α in feline renal cells (Fig. 3A and F), consistent with the mRNA results.

Taurine upregutated HIF1α and EPO mRNA levels in feline renal cells. (A–L) qRT-PCR analysis of EPO and HIF1α gene expression in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells following 24 h of treatment with betaine (A, B, G, H), DHA (C, D, I, J) and taurine (E, F, K, L) at the indicated concentrations (n = 3). *P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001. All data are presented as the mean ± SD.

Taurine upregutated HIF1α and EPO protein levels in feline renal cells. (A–F) WB analysis of EPO and HIF1α protein levels in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells following 24 h of treatment with 5 or 20 µM betaine (A, B, G, H); 5 or 20 µM DHA (C, D, I, J); and 40 or 80 µM taurine (E, F, K, L) (n = 3).

Taurine treatment promotes activation of the HIF1α-EPO pathway under hypoxic conditions

Renal cells are among the first to be activated in response to anemia or hypoxia. During hypoxia, renal cells activate the HIF-EPO pathway to promote red blood cell production and enhance oxygen utilization. In vitro, low oxygen conditions are often used to simulate tissue or disease states. We examined the effect of taurine on the HIF-EPO pathway in PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells under hypoxic conditions. The results showed that taurine significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein levels of EPO, as well as the protein level of HIF1α, in both cell lines under hypoxia (Fig. 4A and F). These findings suggest that taurine enhances the ability of renal cells to synthesize EPO under low oxygen conditions, indicating its potential protective effect on renal cells.

Taurine promoted HIF1α-EPO pathway under hypoxic conditions. (A–D) qRT-PCR analysis of EPO and HIF1α gene expression in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells treated with taurine for 24 h under hypoxic or normoxic conditions (n = 3). (E, F) WB analysis of EPO and HIF1α protein levels in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells treated with taurine for 24 h under hypoxic or normoxic conditions (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001. All data are presented as the mean ± SD.

HIF1α inhibitor YC-1 blocks taurine-induced EPO expression

To confirm the role of HIF1α in taurine-induced EPO expression, we used YC-1, a selective HIF1α inhibitor. The results demonstrated that YC-1 not only inhibited EPO expression in PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells but also blocked the upregulation of EPO mRNA and protein levels induced by taurine and hypoxia (Figs. 5A and F and 6A and B). These findings indicate that the promoting effect of taurine on EPO expression is mediated by HIF1α.

HIF1α inhibitor YC-1 blocks taurine’s promotion of EPO expression. (E, F) WB analysis of EPO and HIF1α protein levels in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells treated with taurine for 24 h in the presence of YC-1 (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01. All data are presented as the mean ± SD.

Taurine promoted the secretion of EPO. (A, B) WB analysis of EPO protein levels in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells treated with taurine for 24 h under hypoxic or normoxic conditions in the presence or absence of YC-1. (n = 3). (C, D) Measurement of EPO content by ELISA in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells treated with taurine, betaine, and DHA for 24 h, respectively (n = 3). (E, F) Measurement of EPO content by ELISA in PETCC2607 and PETCC3002 cells treated with taurine for 24 h under hypoxic or normoxic conditions in the presence or absence of YC-1 (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. All data are presented as the mean ± SD.

Taurine promotes the secretion of EPO

EPO is a secreted protein synthesized by renal cells and released into the extracellular space, where it binds to receptors to exert its effects. To further investigate the effect of taurine on EPO secretion, we used a commercial ELISA kit to measure EPO levels in the cell supernatant. Taurine not only promoted EPO secretion in PETCC191 and PETCC3002 cells under baseline conditions but also enhanced EPO secretion under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6C and F). The HIF1α inhibitor YC-1 effectively blocked taurine-induced EPO secretion in both cell lines (Fig. 6E and F).

Discussion

These in vitro findings suggest that taurine could serve as a potential candidate for managing renal anemia in felines with CKD. By demonstrating that taurine directly enhances EPO production in feline renal cells (PETCC2607 and PETCC3002) under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, our study provides a mechanistic rationale for this therapeutic possibility. The results align with the growing body of evidence highlighting the multifaceted physiological roles of taurine, including its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties, which collectively support its potential as a renal protective agent6,7. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our in vitro approach. Further in vivo studies and clinical trials are essential to confirm the efficacy and safety of taurine in the complex physiological environment of animals with CKD.

Of note, and in contrast to taurine, neither betaine nor DHA significantly affected EPO expression or secretion in feline renal cells under the conditions tested. This observation is particularly interesting given the well-documented anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of these compounds in other systems8,9. While betaine and DHA have been shown to confer renal protection in various models, our results suggest that their protective mechanisms may be independent of direct EPO modulation in renal parenchymal cells. However, this underscores the specificity of taurine in modulating the HIF1α-EPO pathway. The concentration-dependent upregulation of EPO and HIF1α by taurine, even under hypoxic conditions, highlights its ability to enhance renal adaptive responses to low oxygen levels. This is particularly relevant in CKD, where hypoxia-induced EPO deficiency is a major contributor to anemia. The ability of taurine to promote EPO production under such conditions suggests its potential to mitigate the adverse effects of renal hypoxia, thereby improving systemic oxygen homeostasis.

The mechanistic insights provided by the use of the HIF1α inhibitor YC-1 further validate the role of the HIF1α pathway in taurine-mediated EPO regulation. The inhibition of taurine-induced EPO expression and secretion by YC-1 confirms that taurine exerts its effects primarily through HIF1α stabilization and activation. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the central role of HIF1α in oxygen sensing and EPO gene regulation10. The ability of taurine to enhance HIF1α activity, even in the presence of hypoxia, suggests that it may potentiate the endogenous hypoxia response, offering a novel approach to treating renal anemia. The promotion of EPO secretion by taurine, as evidenced by ELISA, further supports its therapeutic potential. EPO secretion is a critical step in the regulation of erythropoiesis, and the ability of taurine to enhance this process under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions suggests its potential relevance in managing anemia associated with CKD.

While the results are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in vitro using feline renal cell lines, which may not fully replicate the complex microenvironment of the kidney in vivo. Future studies should validate these findings in animal models of CKD to assess the translational potential of taurine as a therapeutic agent. Second, the study focused on short-term effects of taurine treatment. Long-term studies are needed to evaluate the sustained efficacy and safety of taurine supplementation in felines with CKD. Additionally, the specific molecular mechanisms by which taurine stabilizes HIF1α remain to be elucidated. Further research could explore whether taurine modulates HIF1α through direct interactions or via upstream signaling pathways.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that taurine enhances EPO synthesis and secretion in feline renal cells through the HIF-1α pathway, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for managing renal anemia in felines with CKD. In contrast, under the same experimental conditions, betaine and DHA showed no significant effects on HIF-1α expression or EPO production in feline renal cells. The findings highlight the specific role of taurine as a bioactive compound with both renal protective and erythropoietic effects. Future research should focus on translating these findings into clinical applications, including the development of taurine-based therapies for feline CKD and associated anemia, while further investigating the distinct mechanisms of action of betaine and DHA in renal health.
