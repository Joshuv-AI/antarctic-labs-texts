Clinical and radiological response of benign dural arteriovenous fistulas after stereotactic radiotherapy
Authors: Withawat Vuthiwong, Kittisak Unsrisong, Bongkot Jia-Mahasap, Jirapong Vongsfak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs) are abnormal connections between dural arteries and venous sinuses or veins, commonly occurring at the transverse-sigmoid sinus. This study evaluates the clinical and radiological outcomes of benign DAVFs treated with hypofractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (HSRT).Methods: A retrospective review was conducted to assess the clinical and radiological responses of patients with benign DAVFs who underwent HSRT at a single center from January 2015 to December 2022.Results: A total of 242 patients with DAVFs were treated, of which 115 patients underwent endovascular treatment. Eight patients who were diagnosed with benign DAVFs (75% females and 25% males), predominantly located in the transverse-sigmoid sinus, were treated with HSRT from January 2015 to December 2022. The cohort had an average age of 52.75 years. All patients presented with pulsatile tinnitus, with one also experiencing headaches. Complete symptomatic recovery was observed in 75% of patients within a mean of 9.7 months’ post-HSRT. Partial recovery was reported in 25% of patients. Radiographic obliteration was achieved in 85.7% of patients within a mean of 22.3 months, with one patient exhibiting stable disease. Focal alopecia and hyperintense changes on imaging were reported as transient side effects.Conclusion: HSRT provides effective and safe treatment with high rates of symptomatic recovery and radiological obliteration in benign DAVFs at the transverse-sigmoid sinus. This method provides an attractive, minimally invasive treatment option for these patients who suffer from pulsatile tinnitus.

INTRODUCTION

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs) are rare conditions characterized by an abnormal connection between dural arteries and dural venous sinuses or leptomeningeal veins, comprising 10–15% of all intracranial arteriovenous shunts.[4] The exact etiology of DAVFs remains unclear, although associations have been reported with venous thrombosis, prior surgery, and trauma.[13] The clinical presentation, natural progression, and prognosis of DAVFs are influenced by the venous drainage pattern and their anatomical locations.[17]

DAVFs lacking cortical venous reflux (CVR) are typically considered benign, with symptoms determined by the fistula’s location and flow pattern, typically present with non-threatening symptoms such as pulsatile tinnitus and they rarely result in severe neurological complications.[18] In contrast, aggressive DAVFs are defined by the presence of CVR.

CVR refers to the retrograde flow of arterialized blood from the fistula into cortical veins, which predisposes patients to venous hypertension, venous congestion, and rupture of fragile cortical veins. This hemodynamic disturbance can result in serious complications such as intracranial hemorrhage, seizures, altered consciousness, or progressive neurological decline.[11] This mechanism differs from the hemorrhagic risk in brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), as DAVFs lack a true nidus but still pose a significant risk when retrograde venous drainage is present. Unlike brain AVMs, DAVFs lack a true nidus, but those with CVR still carry a significant risk of bleeding due to the aberrant venous drainage. The annual risk of hemorrhage in DAVFs with CVR is approximately 8.1%, with a 6.9% risk of non-hemorrhagic neurological deficits and a reported annual mortality of 10.4%.[20]

These fistulas most commonly manifest in regions such as the cavernous sinus (CS), transverse-sigmoid sinus, tentorium/torcula, or cerebral convexities with drainage into the superior sagittal sinus. DAVFs without CVR generally follow a benign course with a low risk of intracranial hemorrhage. However, benign DAVFs, particularly those located in the transverse sigmoid sinus, often present with disabling tinnitus, significantly impacting quality of life and being an indication for treatment.[6]

The Cognard classification system, based on the site of venous drainage and flow pattern in the sinuses with or without CVR, categorizes DAVFs into five groups. Cognard types I and IIa are typically considered benign with a low risk of intracranial hemorrhage or neurological deficits.[5] Cognard types IIb and higher are considered aggressive types. Endovascular embolization has emerged as the first-line definitive treatment for the immediate closure of fistulas in all aggressive DAVFs. In complex cases or cases in which there is a residual shunt after embolization, studies have reported satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes for aggressive DAVFs following a combination of glue embolization and hypofractionated radiotherapy.[21]

However, there is limited evidence supporting the effectiveness of stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) in treating patients with benign DAVFs.[2,7,19,22] This study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of hypofractionated SRT (HSRT) for the treatment of benign DAVFs located at the transverse-sigmoid junction, based on the experience at a center where HSRT is the primary available radiosurgical modality.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient data collection

This retrospective study was conducted covering from January 2015 to December 2022.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) had a confirmed diagnosis of benign DAVF (Cognard type I or IIa), (2) had DAVFs located at the transverse-sigmoid sinus junction, and (3) were treated with HSRT. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) DAVFs involving other anatomical locations (e.g., CS, tentorium), (2) CVR beyond Cognard IIa, (3) prior surgical or radiosurgical treatment at the same site, or (4) inadequate follow-up data.

The focus on DAVFs located at the transverse-sigmoid sinus was based on the clinical observation that these lesions frequently cause debilitating pulsatile tinnitus and present challenges for curative endovascular embolization due to complex arterial anatomy. In addition, this location is anatomically distant from critical neurovascular structures such as the cranial nerves and optic apparatus, reducing the risk of radiation-induced complications seen in locations like the CS. By selecting a homogeneous lesion site with favorable safety characteristics, the study aimed to reduce anatomical confounders and better assess the efficacy of HSRT for this specific subgroup.

Participants, variables, and data sources

The study aimed to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of patients with benign DAVFs. Demographic information of the patients was obtained from the electronic medical records. The data collection process included gathering information on sex, age, presenting symptoms, location of the shunt, DAVFs grading, clinical response, duration of response, and any side effects following HSRT treatment.

Ethic approval

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (Study Code: SUR-2567-0214, Research ID 7566) and the present study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All study participants provided written informed consent.

HSRT

Before 2019, HSRT treatment plan was established using the Hi-Art treatment planning system (TomoTherapy®, Accuray, Madison, USA). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated by a radiation oncologist and was confirmed by a neurointerventionist. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by a 3-mm isotropic expansion from GTV. The dosimetric evaluation was accepted as 100% of PTV was covered by at least 95% of the prescription dose (V95 = 100%). The HSRT dose schedule was 36 Gy in 6 fractions (Fx) administered every other day for 2 weeks. Subsequently, the HSRT dose regimen was rescheduled to deliver 28 Gy in 4 daily Fx. The dosimetric assessment was accepted as 100% of the prescription dose coverage, with at least 99% of PTV (D99% = 100%).

Response evaluation

Symptomatic responses following HSRT were categorized into four groups: Complete recovery, partial recovery, no recovery, and progression of symptoms. Symptomatic response was assessed through patient-reported improvement during the follow-up visits. These assessments were determined through clinical interview and examination by the treating physician.

Radiological responses were classified as total obliteration, subtotal obliteration (indicating >90% regression of DAVFs), partial obliteration (indicating >50% regression of DAVFs), stable disease, and disease progression. “Stable disease” was defined as persistent arteriovenous shunting without significant progression or regression on imaging for a minimum of 24 months post-HSRT. Fistula volume regression was assessed qualitatively based on reduction in size and flow void on magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) or computed tomography angiography (CTA). No 3D volumetric software was used; instead, two radiologists estimated percentage change based on maximum dimensions in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes compared to baseline imaging.

Follow-up protocol

Clinical evaluations were performed at 6 weeks’ post-HSRT, followed by scheduled visits every 2 months during the 1st year and every 6 months thereafter. Radiological surveillance included magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with MRA or CTA at 6-month intervals for the first 2 years, then annually. Imaging was conducted using 3T MRI (SIGNA Pioneer, GE Healthcare) or multi-detector CTA (SOMATOM FORCE, Siemens). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was reserved for patients with clinical deterioration or suspected radiological progression. The primary clinical endpoints were symptom resolution, stability, or progression. The radiological endpoint was complete, partial, or stable obliteration of the fistula as assessed by cross-sectional imaging. Follow-up duration ranged from 14 to 36 months.

Worsening clinical symptoms included intolerable pulsatile tinnitus, new-onset headache, or neurological deficits such as cognitive decline or sensorimotor impairment.

RESULTS

During the study, a total of 242 patients with DAVFs were treated, of which 115 patients underwent endovascular treatment. The selection criteria for those who received HSRT included the presence of benign DAVFs located at the transverse-sigmoid sinus associated with intolerable clinical symptoms (pulsatile tinnitus).

Eight patients with benign DAVFs were treated with HSRT in the study period. The age of the patients ranged from 29 to 68 years, with an average age of 52.75 years. The cohort included six women (75%) and two men (25%), resulting in a female-to-male ratio of 3:1. The most commonly affected location of DAVFs was the transverse-sigmoid sinus (n = 7, 87.5%), followed by the condylar confluence (n = 1, 12.5%). All patients presented with pulsatile tinnitus, and one patient also experienced headaches.

Complete symptomatic recovery was observed in 75% of patients (6 out of 8) within 6–12 months (mean = 9.7 months) after HSRT. Partial symptomatic recovery was reported in 25% of patients (2 out of 8). Follow-up MRI and MRA were performed in seven patients. The total radiographic obliteration rate was 85.7% (6 out of 7 patients) within 14–34 months (mean = 22.3 months), while one patient (14.3%) exhibited stable disease. One patient (12.5%) experienced focal alopecia as a transient side effect of HSRT and post-radiation imaging demonstrated hyperintense changes on T2/FLAIR in the right cerebellar hemisphere in one asymptomatic patient (12.5%) [Figure 4].

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of HSRT on benign DAVFs primarily at the transverse sigmoid junction. A complete symptomatic recovery was observed in 75% of cases, with a high obliteration rate of 85.7% after follow-up and a low complication rate without clinically significant adverse effects.

The management strategy for DAVFs should be individualized, depending on the location of the shunt, clinical presentation, and angioarchitecture of the DAVFs, weighing the benefits and risks of each treatment modality. For benign DAVFs without CVR and no neurological deficit, intervention may not be necessary unless the patient’s symptoms, such as pulsatile tinnitus, are intolerable. Spontaneous regression of DAVFs is commonly seen in the CS location; however, it is rare (<5%) in the transverse sigmoid location. Although endovascular embolization can provide immediate symptomatic relief, achieving complete obliteration for benign DAVFs (Cognard type I and IIa) through transarterial embolization can be challenging due to the complex and tortuous arterial supply. Transvenous scarification of the dural sinus may cause persistent sinus hypertension or local ischemia and increase the expression of vascular growth factors. A less invasive approach, such as HSRT treatment with a low complication rate, could therefore be considered for treating these patients. Conventional radiotherapy typically delivers lower radiation doses per fraction over a prolonged treatment period (e.g., 1.8–2 Gy per fraction over 5–6 weeks). In contrast, HSRT delivers higher doses per fraction in fewer sessions (e.g., 6–9 Gy per fraction over 3–6 sessions) with sub-millimeter precision. HSRT uses stereotactic localization and conformal treatment planning, allowing greater biologically effective dose to the target while minimizing dose to surrounding tissues, thus offering greater efficacy and convenience for well-defined lesions such as DAVFs.

Radiotherapy has demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of intracranial vascular malformations, particularly brain AVMs, where it is used to induce gradual obliteration of abnormal vessels while minimizing damage to surrounding brain tissue. Its application has been well established in cases where surgical or endovascular options are limited due to lesion location or patient comorbidities. These therapeutic principles can be extended to the management of DAVFs, offering a noninvasive alternative with favorable safety and efficacy profiles.

Radiotherapy techniques for DAVFs require a stereotactic process using stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) or HSRT. The pathomechanism of radiosurgery targets both dural feeder arteries and fistulas, causing radiation-induced vascular hypertrophy, leading to the occlusion of the fistula. The complete obliteration rate is reported to be approximately 44–87%, depending on the type and location of DAVFs.[9,14,16] Venous ectasia and CVR are important predictors of failure in DAVFs obliteration after radiosurgery.[2] Several studies have reported favorable outcomes of SRS in DAVFs, particularly in benign subtypes. Chen et al., conducted a systematic review showing that SRS achieved complete obliteration in approximately 63% of cases, with better outcomes in lesions without CVR.[2] Similarly, Van et al. (2002) and Söderman et al. reported effective results for transverse-sigmoid DAVFs, though many of their patients had aggressive features or underwent prior embolization.[19,20] In contrast, our study focuses exclusively on benign DAVFs treated with HSRT alone, demonstrating comparable obliteration rates and symptom resolution, supporting HSRT as a safe and effective alternative in selected patients.

SRS and HSRT are both precision radiotherapy techniques used to treat vascular malformations. SRS delivers a single high-dose radiation treatment in a single session, while HSRT delivers comparable biological effectiveness through multiple high-dose Fx (e.g., 3–6 sessions). HSRT may offer radiobiological advantages by balancing efficacy with reduced toxicity to surrounding normal tissues. In our center, HSRT was selected due to the proximity of DAVFs to critical neurovascular structures and the desire to minimize radiation-induced complications such as edema or necrosis. Compared to SRS, HSRT allows for better dose conformity and is considered safer in cases with larger target volumes or higher risk of toxicity. Both approaches aim to achieve obliteration of abnormal arteriovenous shunting through radiation-induced vascular hyalinization and closure, but HSRT offers enhanced flexibility in dose modulation and fractionation.[3,8]

Radiotherapy is not recommended as a first-line treatment for aggressive DAVFs due to their natural history and the long latency period, which can be as long as 6–12 months.[10] In contrast, benign DAVFs typically exhibit a more favorable treatment outcome with a higher occlusion rate when compared to malignant types (75% vs. 56%).[15] This study shows complete symptomatic recovery in 75% of cases, with a high obliteration rate of 85.7%. Regarding SRS for DAVFs involving the transverse-sigmoid sinus, one study on gamma knife SRS treatment of DAVFs involving the transverse-sigmoid sinus showed complete obliteration of the fistula in 44% of cases, with 67% experiencing complete recovery from symptoms. Importantly, 67% of patients included in this study had aggressive DAVFs, and only four patients were treated with SRS alone.[1] Our study also demonstrates the benefit and safety of HSRT for DAVF type IIa (Cognard’s classification), which had retrograde sinus reflux with a high clinical response and high-rate obliteration without neurological complications during the latent period. Moreover, the gradual obliteration of DAVFs after radiosurgery can avoid complications such as venous hypertension or infarction that can occur after endovascular embolization or surgery. Despite the promising efficacy and safety findings reported in this study, a notable consideration with radiosurgery is the potential risk of secondary radiation-induced malignancies, necessitating long-term follow-up in DAVFs cases post-radiation treatment.

The time from treatment to DAVF occlusion in this study aligns with literature reviews, occurring within a 1–3-year latency period after SRS.[2,12] Only one patient in our study experienced partial recovery of symptoms and increased tinnitus during the follow-up.

This study’s limitations include its small sample size and retrospective, single-center design, which may affect generalizability. While MRI/MRA provided a noninvasive method for radiological follow-up, the use of DSA in future studies could enhance diagnostic precision. In addition, the absence of a control group limits direct comparisons with other treatment modalities. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up durations are necessary to validate these preliminary findings and to better define the role of HSRT in the comprehensive management of benign DAVFs.

CONCLUSION

HSRT provides effective and safe symptomatic recovery and radiological obliteration in benign DAVFs at the transversesigmoid location. The method provides a minimally invasive therapeutic treatment option for patients who suffer from debilitating symptoms from low-grade DAVFs.
