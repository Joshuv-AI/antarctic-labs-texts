Estimated Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Grip Strength: Independent and Combined Associations With Multimorbidity Risk and Patterns in a Large‐Scale Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Xuezhen Chen, Kunfeng Su, Chunting Chen, Yongsheng Yang, Xi Chen, Shiwen Liu, Jie Yi, Jinman Zhuang, Haomin Yang, Fei He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLittle is known about the associations between cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), grip strength (GS), and their combined effect on multimorbidity. This investigation aimed to identify diseases associated with estimated CRF and GS, evaluate the associations among estimated CRF, GS, and their combination with multimorbidity.MethodsThis analysis was based on data from 63 177 individuals who provided valid CRF and GS data in UK Biobank. Phenome‐wide association studies identified diseases associated with both CRF and GS, defining multimorbidity. Cox proportional hazard models, logistic regression, and quasi‐Poisson mixed effects models were used to investigate the effects of CRF and GS on multimorbidity.ResultsA total of 32 diseases were included to constitute the multimorbidity indicator. During a median follow‐up of 11.58 years, at least 2 diseases developed in 21 275 participants. Compared with their lowest tertiles, the highest categories of estimated CRF and GS showed hazard ratios (HR) of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.74–0.80) and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.76–0.81) for multimorbidity, respectively. The HR for the combination of the highest estimated CRF and GS was 0.62 (95% CI, 0.59–0.66). Low estimated CRF and GS were associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, leading to related complications.ConclusionsHigh estimated CRF and GS were associated with low multimorbidity risk. Maintaining both estimated CRF and GS may be more effective than targeting either alone. Proactive cardiovascular health management is crucial for individuals with low estimated CRF and GS to prevent multisystem multimorbidity.

Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of ≥2 chronic diseases in the same individual 1 , 2 and is often associated with a decline in quality of life and an increased risk of mortality. 3 , 4 , 5 Several factors are associated with multimorbidity, including age, income, educational level, smoking status, drinking status, loneliness, and physical activity. 6 The burden of multimorbidity on individual well‐being and health care systems is substantially greater than that of a single disease. 7 Multimorbidity is generally associated with adverse health outcomes, such as impaired physical and mental health, elevated mortality risk, increased frailty, premature death, high hospitalization rates, and long hospital stays. 3 , 4 , 5

Moderate physical activity is associated with a low risk of multimorbidity. 8 , 9 , 10 In addition, associations have been found between physical activity and hypertension, 11 diabetes, 12 dementia, 13 and depression. 14 Notably, given the potential for bias in self‐reported physical activity data and influence of various demographic characteristics, emerging evidence suggests that cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and grip strength (GS) may be more sensitive metrics than self‐reported physical activity in predicting health risk. 15 , 16 , 17

CRF reflects the integrated ability to transport oxygen from the atmosphere to the mitochondria to perform physical work. 18 , 19 This ability relies on a series of physiological processes; thus, CRF is widely considered a reflection of physical fitness. 20 CRF can be objectively assessed using direct or indirect methods, with maximal or submaximal exercise protocols employing various modalities. 21 Evidence indicates that incorporating CRF as a clinical vital sign could improve patient management by better identifying individuals at high risk of adverse outcomes. 20 High CRF is associated with less visceral fat, improved insulin sensitivity, low blood lipid levels, a favorable lipoprotein profile, and low blood pressure. 22 , 23 , 24 Individuals with high CRF tend to have lower levels of C‐reactive protein and other inflammatory markers than those of individuals with low CRF. 25 , 26 Moreover, research has linked cardiac risk factors to the incidence of certain conditions, such as type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, stroke, and depression. Additionally, high CRF may improve prognosis in patients with chronic conditions. 21 However, current research on CRF has generally focused on individual chronic conditions, and little is known about its relationship with multimorbidity.

GS is conventionally considered an indicator of muscle strength and is associated with various adverse health events, particularly in the aging population. 17 GS may also serve as a biomarker for other physiological systems. 27 GS is a complex trait influenced by various factors. Neuromotor control, muscle fiber composition, tendon mechanics, and blood flow work together to generate gripping force. 28 Additionally, health, nutrition, and psychological factors can significantly affect GS. 28 A reduction in muscle strength, as gauged by GS, can lead to various health consequences across all population groups, including increased mortality, hospitalization, morbidity, functional disabilities, and impaired psychological health. 17 Although some studies have examined the relation between GS and multimorbidity, they were generally cross‐sectional or limited to a single organ system. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32

Moreover, considering the benefits of resistance training on glycemic control, lipid levels, and the reduction of obesity and type 2 diabetes risk—all recognized determinants of mortality—and evidence from animal models indicating a causal relation between low CRF, unfavorable cardiometabolic outcomes, and shortened lifespan, a combined approach targeting both CRF and GS may offer a greater reduction in all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality risk than improving either CRF or GS independently. 33 , 34 Current public health guidelines recommend that both men and women engage in aerobic and muscle‐strengthening activities throughout their lifespan, 35 suggesting that combined improvements in CRF and GS may be particularly beneficial. Therefore, it is critical to examine the combined effects of CRF and GS on multimorbidity risk in adults. Furthermore, disease patterns and trajectories associated with the co‐occurrence of low CRF and GS remain largely unexplored.

The primary aims of our study were to identify diseases associated with estimated CRF and GS, define multimorbidity based on these associations, assess the individual and combined associations of estimated CRF and GS with multimorbidity, and explore the multimorbidity patterns and trajectories of estimated CRF and GS.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from UK Biobank. Restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under license for this study. Data are available to all researchers upon making an application.

Study Design and Participants

The UK Biobank is a population‐based prospective cohort study. Between 2006 and 2010, >500 000 participants aged 40 to 69 years were recruited. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who retained the right to withdraw at any time. At baseline, participants underwent comprehensive data collection, including various physical measurements, biological sample collection, and questionnaires assessing prevalent morbidities, sociodemographic factors, family history and early‐life exposure, lifestyle, and environmental factors. Health‐related outcomes were obtained through regular linkages to multiple national databases. Inpatient hospital data for participants from England, Scotland, and Wales were updated using their respective national databases: the Hospital Episode Statistics database for England, Scottish Morbidity Record for Scotland, and Patient Episode Database for Wales. 36 Inpatient hospital data were assumed to cover all UK Biobank participants since 1997. 36

There were 502 366 participants in the UK Biobank. A total of 63 254 participants had available CRF and GS data. Participants without data on the Townsend Deprivation Index were excluded. Multimorbidity was determined using phenome‐wide association analysis. The final data set for the main analysis comprised 63 177 individuals, including 6510 with multimorbidity at baseline and 56 667 without it (Figure 1).

Exposure

Estimated Cardiorespiratory Fitness

Estimated CRF was assessed in a subsample using the heart rate response to a submaximal ramped cycle ergometer test individualized according to participant characteristics. The protocol for measuring fitness is described in detail in the UK Biobank Cardio Assessment Manual. 37 Briefly, participants were categorized into 4 risk groups based on a risk assessment questionnaire: the minimal and small risk groups completed an individualized ramp test; medium risk group performed a flat test cycling at a constant power level for 6 minutes; and high risk group underwent a resting ECG protocol. Individuals at high risk of adverse events were not eligible for the general CRF protocol and were therefore excluded from the study sample. The test protocol was specified according to participants’ age, sex, height, weight, and resting heart rate. The individualized ramp test protocol included an initial 15‐second seated‐rest period, followed by a 2‐minute phase at a constant power output (30 W for women and 40 W for men), and concluded with a 4‐minute ramp phase during which power increased linearly from the initial constant power to the individually determined peak power, corresponding to 50% and 35% of the predicted maximal workload for the minimal and small risk groups, respectively. During the test, heart rate response was monitored and recorded using a 4‐lead ECG device. The heart rate was monitored preexercise, throughout the activity, and during recovery using a 4‐lead ECG. The work rate at the estimated maximal heart rate was estimated by extrapolating the preexercise heart rate (ie, at a work rate of 0 W) and heart and work rates at the end of the test to the age‐predicted estimated maximal heart rate (208–0.7 × age), 38 assuming a linear relation. Estimated maximal oxygen uptake (ie, at estimated maximal heart rate) was evaluated from the regression equation for the relation between work rate and oxygen uptake (oxygen uptake [in mL·kg−1·min−1] = 7 + (10.8 × work rate [W]) / body mass [kg]). 39 To account for differences in body size, CRF was expressed as the maximal power per kilogram of fat‐free mass (ie, body mass minus fat mass), 33 which was measured using bioimpedance analysis with a Tanita BC‐418MA device. After quality control, which excluded individuals who had not completed the submaximal bicycle test, those with invalid or incomplete data, and those with no fat‐free mass or a maximum workload >20 W per kg of fat‐free mass, 33 63 416 participants had available CRF data.

Grip Strength

The GS was measured using a Jamar J00105 hydraulic hand dynamometer. 40 The participants were seated upright with their elbow positioned at their side and fixed at a 90° angle such that their forearms faced forward and rested on an armrest. A single trial measuring maximal handgrip strength was conducted for each hand, with the participants selecting the most comfortable grip position from 5 available options. Consistent with prior research, 41 , 42 we used GS measurements of the self‐reported dominant hand. If hand dominance was ambidextrous or unavailable, the maximum GS of either hand was used.

Outcome

The UK Biobank provides linked follow‐up data on participants’ health outcomes, including primary health care records, cancer registrations, death records, and hospitalization records. Diagnoses were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10). We excluded diagnoses related to pregnancy, childbirth, perinatal conditions, and unclassified symptoms or signs and restricted the analyses to chapters 1–14 of the ICD‐10. We used the 3‐digit ICD‐10 codes for medical condition identification and combined the conditions with clinical or biological similarities (Data Set S1). For conditions identified in multiple records for the same individual, only the first record and date of the initial hospital visit were used as the dates of diagnosis. After merging and transforming ICD‐10 codes, we identified 448 distinct health outcomes. We then examined the associations between the estimated CRF and GS and these health outcomes using separate Cox proportional hazards regression models. These models excluded individuals with the outcome disease before the start of follow‐up (defined as 6 months after baseline assessment) and were adjusted for age, sex, and the Townsend Deprivation Index. In these analyses, follow‐up started at baseline and continued until the onset of the disease of interest, loss to follow‐up, death, or end of the follow‐up period (December 31, 2021), whichever occurred first. Estimated CRF and GS were treated as continuous variables, and hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated per 1 SD difference in the estimated CRF and GS using age‐ and sex‐specific Z scores. Only diseases with a P value below the Bonferroni‐corrected threshold and an HR < 1 related to both the estimated CRF and GS were considered components of multimorbidity. Participants with ≥2 of these diseases were classified as having multimorbidity.

Covariates

Covariate information was obtained during baseline assessment. The missing data for these covariates were treated as separate categories. Sociodemographic characteristics included sex, age, race, employment status, and the Townsend Deprivation Index. The lifestyle factors included smoking and drinking status, fat‐free mass, dietary score, physical activity, and baseline disease status. Age was categorized into 3 levels: ≤50 years, 51 to 60 years, and >60 years. Race was categorized as White, non‐White, or missing. Employment status was grouped as working (paid employment or self‐employed), unemployed, other (looking after home and family, unable to work because of sickness or disability, doing unpaid or voluntary work, full‐ or part‐time student), or missing (prefer not to answer). The Townsend Deprivation Index is used to measure social and economic status and is categorized into quartiles. 43 Smoking status was categorized as never smoked, former smoker, current smoker, or missing smoking status. Drinking status was divided into never, previous, current >3 times per week, current <3 times per week, and missing. The diet score comprises 7 components: fruits, vegetables, fish, processed meats, unprocessed red meats, whole grains, and refined grains. For each participant, the diet score was calculated by summing the points for each of the 7 food components, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 7 points. 44 The fulfillment of each component was scored as 1 point, whereas nonfulfillment was scored as 0. In our study, the diet score was categorized into 3 levels: low (0–1 point), intermediate (2–5 points), and high (6–7 points). 45 Physical activity was measured as total metabolic equivalent minutes per week and categorized into quartiles. Participants with at least 1 disease included in the multimorbidity definition at baseline were considered to have the baseline disease.

Statistical Analysis

The participants’ characteristics were summarized based on the occurrence of multimorbidity during the follow‐up period. For the comparison of differences between groups, variance analysis, Student’s t test, or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables. The chi‐square test was used to analyze categorical variables. For the main analysis, we examined the association between different estimated CRF and GS values and multimorbidity in 3 steps. First, we determined the relation between estimated CRF and GS and each disease through phenome‐wide association analysis using Cox proportional hazards models to determine the composition of multimorbidity. Second, logistic regression models were used to estimate the association between estimated CRF and GS scores and the presence of multimorbidity at baseline. Furthermore, after excluding individuals with multimorbidity, Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between estimated CRF and GS and the incidence of multimorbidity. In addition to the first 2 diseases, the onsets of third and fourth diseases were also determined. Two diseases refer to simple multimorbidity, and ≥3 chronic diseases refer to complex multimorbidity. 46 Joint associations of estimated CRF and GS with multimorbidity were evaluated using low GS and low estimated CRF as the common reference group, and the multiplicative interaction between estimated CRF and GS was tested. For the aforementioned analyses, estimated CRF and GS were treated as continuous variables, and HRs were calculated per 1 SD difference in estimated CRF and GS using age‐ and sex‐specific Z scores. In addition, HRs and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated for a 1‐metabolic equivalent difference in estimated CRF and 5 kg difference in GS.

Age‐ and sex‐specific tertiles for estimated CRF and GS were derived (Table S1‐S2), and HRs were calculated using the lowest tertile as the reference category. For each of the approaches described previously, we ran 3 incremental models that included an increasing number of covariates: (1) adjusted for sex and age; (2) adjusted for sex, age, employment status, Townsend Deprivation Index, and race; and (3) adjusted for sex, age, employment status, Townsend Deprivation Index, race, smoking status, drinking status, fat‐free mass, diet score, physical activity, baseline disease, estimated CRF (for GS models), and GS (for estimated CRF models). Considering competing mortality risks, a competing risk model was used. In the Cox proportional hazards models, follow‐up started from baseline and continued until multimorbidity, loss to follow‐up, death, or the end of the follow‐up period (December 31, 2021), whichever occurred first. Log–log plots supported the proportional hazards assumptions. Further subgroup analyses were performed, and the results were stratified by sex. Third, quasi‐Poisson mixed‐effects models were used to estimate the impact of estimated CRF and GS on the number of new‐onset diseases during follow‐up.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) including only those individuals who did not have any of the defined diseases at baseline; (2) excluding individuals who developed multimorbidity within the first 2 years after baseline; (3) imputing missing covariates with means for continuous variables and modes for categorical variables; and (4) excluding participants with the highest or lowest 1% of estimated CRF or GS values.

In addition, to analyze multimorbidity patterns of diseases without any a priori hypotheses, we used a hierarchical agglomerative clustering approach to create dendrograms to identify patterns. We employed a binary distance measure (Jaccard coefficient) to assess similarity and generate a distance matrix, resulting in more distinctive clusters compared with other proximity measures. 47 For the dendrogram analysis, we used the Ward method to identify compact clusters and minimize within‐cluster variance. 47 The optimal number of clusters was determined by visual inspection and the elbow method. Associations between estimated CRF and GS and multimorbidity patterns were also estimated. Considering repeated measurements, P values were adjusted for each model. To further explore the temporal sequence of these diseases, we conducted a trajectory analysis. This analysis focused on multimorbidity that exhibited a significant positive association with estimated CRF and GS (HR >1). Therefore, this approach was used in populations with low estimated CRF and GS values. Briefly, the trajectory analysis consisted of 3 interrelated steps. First, we identified all possible disease 1 (D1) and disease 2 (D2) pairs if the co‐occurrence of the 2 diseases was observed in at least 1% of these individuals. We then calculated the relative risk (RR) of observing disease pairs in the same individual and used the Pearson correlation between these diseases as an indicator of co‐occurrence strength. The calculations were performed according to the following formula 48 : RRij=CijNijCiCj Φij=CijNij−CiCjCiCjNij−CiNij−Cj Where Cij is the number of individuals with both diseases, Nij is the total number of individuals, and Ci and Cj are the number of individuals affected by diseases 1 and 2, respectively. The significance of RR = 0 and Φ=0 can be determined using a Z test (given the large sample size in our study). The Z scores for the corresponding RR and Φ‐correlation are calculated using the following formulas 48 , 49 : zijRR=lnRRij1Cij−1Nij+1CiCj/Nij−1Nij zijΦ=ΦijmaxCiCj−21−Φij2 P values were calculated using the Z score and adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction. Only pairs with RR >1.0 and a positive Φ‐correlation, indicating significant co‐occurrence strength (Bonferroni‐adjusted P value <0.05 for both metrics), proceeded to the next step.

Second, a binomial test was applied to examine whether the probability of one disease (D2, disease outcome) occurring following another (D1, disease exposure) was significantly greater than 50% among individuals with low estimated CRF and low GS with both D1 and D2 diagnoses. We identified the initial D1 and D2 diagnoses for patients with both conditions and counted the number of patients with each order of diagnosis based on temporal information: ND1 where D1 occurred first, ND2 where D2 occurred first, and Nsame where D1 and D2 were diagnosed on the same day. Using 2 binomial tests, we examined whether ND1 and ND2 were significantly larger than the binomial distribution (ND1 +ND2 + Nsame samples, with a probability of 50%). Only disease pairs that demonstrated a significant direction in the binomial test, as indicated by a Bonferroni‐adjusted P value <0.05 (with significance in only 1 of the tests), were eligible for the subsequent step.

Third, we used a nested case–control design and conditional logistic regression to confirm and assess the magnitudes of these associations. For each included disease pair, we constructed a nested case–control data set by considering D2 as the outcome and D1 as the exposure. In each case–control data set, at most 4 controls were matched to each case based on sex and age of individuals with low estimated CRF and low GS. Individuals with low estimated CRF and low GS and a history of D1 or D2 diagnosis before follow‐up were not included in the analysis. We used conditional logistic regression to assess the magnitude of the associations after adjusting for confounders. We adjusted for diet scores, employment status, race, smoking status, drinking status, Townsend Deprivation Index, physical activity, and fat‐free mass. The significant disease pairs (D1 → D2) identified—characterized by an OR >1.0 and Bonferroni‐adjusted P value <0.05—were employed to construct a network of disease trajectories. For disease pairs that remained statistically significant (Bonferroni‐corrected threshold) after the step 3 analysis, but in which a substantial proportion of individuals with low estimated CRF and low GS had the same D1 and D2 diagnosis date (>20%), we further confirmed their temporal associations by reviewing the underlying biological or clinical mechanisms.

Restricted cubic spline models were used to evaluate the dose–response relation between the estimated CRF and GS and incidence of multimorbidity, with 3 knots selected based on the lowest Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion. Quasi‐Poisson mixed‐effects models were used to estimate the impact of the estimated CRF and GS on the number of new‐onset chronic conditions during follow‐up. We adjusted for GS in the estimated CRF models and for estimated CRF in the GS models.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Significance was evaluated at the 0.05 level using 2‐sided tests.

RESULT

All participants were followed up from 6 months after the date of baseline assessment until the occurrence of 448 diseases, death, loss to follow‐up, or the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2021). Among these, we identified significant negative associations between 61 diseases and GS and between 49 diseases and estimated CRF (Figure 2). Both estimated CRF and GS were associated with infectious, nutritional and metabolic, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, musculoskeletal, connective tissue, and urinary system diseases. Among diseases with HR <0.8 for both estimated CRF and GS, nutritional and metabolic diseases were common in estimated CRF, and mental, behavioral, and neurodevelopmental disorders were common in GS. Estimated CRF was most strongly associated with osteomyelitis (HR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.47–0.78]) and sarcoidosis (HR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.49–0.75]), whereas GS was most strongly associated with vascular dementia (HR, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.50–0.72]). After stratifying by sex, among men, ulcer of the lower limb was most strongly associated with estimated CRF (HR, 0.56 [95% CI, 0.47–0.67]), whereas vascular dementia remained most strongly associated with GS (HR, 0.55 [95% CI, 0.44–0.69]). Among women, diabetes was most strongly associated with estimated CRF (HR, 0.71 [95% CI, 0.67–0.75]), and autoimmune arthritis was most strongly associated with GS (HR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.62–0.73]) (Figure S1). We selected 33 diseases associated with both estimated CRF and GS as outcome indices for our main analysis. Considering the clinical similarities, iron‐deficiency anemia and other anemia types were combined into a single category of anemia. Therefore, we identified 32 diseases that constituted multimorbidity (Table S3).

Overall, we identified 21 275 participants with multimorbidity during a median follow‐up of 11.58 years (Table S4). During the follow‐up period, the 3 chronic diseases with the highest incidence rates were other functional intestinal disorders, primary hypertension, and lipoprotein metabolism disorders and other lipidemia (Figure S2).

Participants who had multimorbidity at baseline or developed at least 2 diseases during follow‐up were more likely to be men, older, smokers, drinkers, unemployed, have low physical activity, high fat‐free mass, baseline diseases, low GS, and low estimated CRF (Table 1, Table S4).

Basic Characteristics of Participants by Multimorbidity Status at Baseline

High estimated CRF and GS levels were associated with a low risk of multimorbidity (Table 2). The ORs and HRs for estimated CRF and GS in relation to multimorbidity were similar and remained consistent after stratification by sex (Table S5‐S7). When multimorbidity was defined as the occurrence of ≥3 diseases, estimated CRF and GS were also associated with multimorbidity, with stronger associations observed for GS than for estimated CRF (Table S8‐S11). In addition, the sensitivity analyses—including only those individuals who did not have any of the defined diseases at baseline, excluding individuals who developed multimorbidity within the first 2 years after baseline, imputing missing covariates with modes for categorical variables, and excluding participants in the highest or lowest 1% of estimated CRF or GS—produced results similar to the main findings (Table S12‐S15).

DISCUSSION

In this large prospective study, we extensively explored diseases associated with estimated CRF and GS for the first time, as well as the RRs of multimorbidity linked to estimated CRF, GS, and their combination. High estimated CRF and GS were associated with a low risk of multimorbidity and fewer diseases. In all comparisons, individuals with the highest levels of estimated CRF and GS had the lowest risk of multimorbidity compared with those with the lowest levels. Additionally, we identified multimorbidity patterns and trajectories associated with estimated CRF and GS.

Previous longitudinal studies have found associations between CRF and various diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and dementia. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 In our study, we found significant associations between estimated CRF and infectious and digestive diseases. The results of the disease pattern analysis further supported this finding. To the best of our knowledge, these associations with CRF have not been previously reported. Regular moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity is associated with a reduced risk of infectious diseases and enhances the first line of defense of the immune system. 55 A Mendelian randomization study indicated that moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity physical activity was associated with a reduced risk of digestive diseases. 56 Physical exercise has beneficial effects on improving CRF. 34 Therefore, improving CRF may reduce the risk of infectious and digestive diseases. Moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity has also been strongly and linearly associated with a reduced risk of diabetes. 57 Estimated maximal oxygen uptake influences the pathogenesis of endocrine and metabolic disorders by modulating energy metabolism, inflammatory responses, endocrine regulation, mitochondrial function, and gene expression. 58

In addition, associations with GS have been observed in many other diseases, except infectious and digestive diseases. 17 , 28 , 59 , 60 , 61 To the best of our knowledge, this has not been reported in previous studies. These associations may be due to the lack of adjustment for other confounding factors. However, to avoid reverse causality, we excluded individuals who experienced outcomes within the first 6 months of follow‐up. Similarly, in our analysis, we identified disease patterns that included infectious and digestive system diseases. These patterns also included lens disorders and arthritis. High physical activity and a less sedentary lifestyle have been associated with a low prevalence of diabetic retinopathy, whereas associations with other eye diseases have not been statistically significant. 62 This differs from our findings, and racial and population differences may be potential explanations. To some extent, reduced GS also reflects increased age or poor physical condition, resulting in diminished immunity or digestive system disorders. 17 , 63 However, the mechanisms underlying the coexistence of these diseases require further investigation. Greater muscular strength has been associated with improved brain health, whereas diminished muscle strength is correlated with reduced gray matter volume in the hippocampus and fusiform gyrus. 42 , 64 Furthermore, skeletal muscle plays a critical role in the production and secretion of numerous cytokines. 42 , 64 Consistent with previous research, our findings indicate that low GS was associated with decreased cognitive function and an elevated risk of mental disorders and dementia. To the best of our knowledge, limited research has explored the relation between CRF and multimorbidity. CRF was measured using estimated maximal oxygen uptake. 39 The key mechanism linking disease and health is how effectively the body metabolizes oxygen. 65 Rodents with high aerobic capacity have been shown to have long lifespans and low incidence rates of disease risk factors. 65 , 66 Physical inactivity independently contributes to a decline in CRF, shortens health span, and lowers the age at which the first chronic disease appears. 67 This, in turn, reduces quality of life, increases health care costs, and elevates mortality risk. 67 Low levels of physical activity are associated with a high risk of multimorbidity in older adults, 68 and high physical activity intensity is an effective measure for preventing cardiometabolic multimorbidity and mortality in the early stages of cardiometabolic multimorbidity development. 69 In our study, we also found an association between estimated CRF and cardiometabolic multimorbidity. However, multimorbidity patterns also included lens disorders, joint diseases, and digestive system diseases. Understanding the complexity of these multisystem diseases has deepened our understanding of CRF.

Several studies have identified an association between GS and multimorbidity. Cardiometabolic disease counts and combinations have been associated with GS, 70 similar to our results. A study in Taiwan indicated that individuals with 2 diseases, rather than 1 or 3, 71 had low GS. By contrast, our study found that high GS was associated with fewer diseases. This difference may be due to variations in ethnicity, sample size, or age of the study population. GS had a greater impact on men over 60 years of age 72 ; however, in our study, the benefits appeared to be greater for women. In addition, GS, which is often used as an indicator of frailty, also showed a protective effect against multimorbidity in a longitudinal study. 73 , 74 A decline in muscle mass leads to multimorbidity, reduced functionality, and a substantial negative impact on quality of life. 75

CRF and GS are strong independent predictors of mortality. 33 Additionally, the combination of low CRF and GS is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, 76 , 77 , 78 whereas our results showed associations with a broader range of diseases. A cross‐sectional study conducted in the United States also demonstrated an association between combined aerobic and resistance training and multimorbidity. 79 Multiple intervention studies have shown that aerobic training alone or combined resistance and aerobic training—but not resistance training alone—improved composite cardiovascular disease risk status compared with the control group. 80 , 81 , 82 Resistance training promotes muscle hypertrophy and glycogen storage, whereas aerobic exercise enhances mitochondrial function and glucose use. 83 These adaptations can synergistically improve glycemic control by reducing insulin resistance and enhancing glucose metabolism. 83 Additionally, resistance and aerobic exercises can effectively reduce the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and enhance anti‐inflammatory responses. 84 These findings suggest that the combined effect is greater than that of either factor alone. The results of the aforementioned intervention studies were based on clinical trials that are difficult to conduct in the general population. The findings of our study were based on a small sample size. Further prospective epidemiological investigations in broader populations are required to confirm these results. Low estimated CRF and GS may be early warning indicators of cardiovascular disease and anemia, suggesting that a decline in these 2 physical function indicators may precede the occurrence of specific diseases. Given that low CRF and GS may indicate physical frailty, a potential link with the occurrence of anemia warrants consideration. 85 However, the correlations between these factors remain unclear. The association between combined CRF, GS, and cardiovascular diseases has been documented. 76 , 77 , 78 Therefore, interventions aimed at improving cardiopulmonary function and muscle strength may offer preventive strategies against these diseases. Heart failure acts as a critical bifurcation point in numerous disease trajectories, potentially leading to multisystem complications; these diseases share the same disease patterns. Diseases grouped within a shared multimorbidity pattern show an increased tendency to occur simultaneously. Consequently, heart failure should be viewed not only as a consequence of cardiovascular events but also as a potential trigger for systemic dysregulation. Therefore, comprehensive monitoring of multiple organ function is warranted in patients with heart failure. Chronic ischemic heart disease represents a significant pathway in the development of heart failure, with sustained cardiovascular ischemia potentially leading to direct cardiac dysfunction. 86 Therefore, early intervention in patients with chronic ischemic heart disease may delay or prevent progression to heart failure, thereby mitigating the risk of subsequent multisystem disease development. Additionally, heart failure may also indirectly cause pneumonia via pulmonary congestion or decreased immune function. 87 Furthermore, pneumonia may predispose individuals to multisystem diseases. Therefore, future research and clinical practice should prioritize cardiovascular risk assessment in individuals with low CRF and low GS, with the aim of preventing multisystem diseases.

The strengths of the current study lie in its prospective design, the adjustment for a large number of confounders, the verified disease diagnoses from clinical records, and the extensive analysis of diseases associated with estimated CRF and GS, which expanded the scope of the disease profile compared with previous studies focusing on select conditions. This further confirmed the combined effect of estimated CRF and GS on multimorbidity. A sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of our results. In addition, we investigated the multimorbidity pattern and correlation between estimated CRF and GS and multimorbidity patterns and trajectories, which is helpful for prevention in those with low estimated CRF and low GS.

Our study has some limitations. First, a single measurement of estimated CRF and GS did not account for changes in these variables over time during the follow‐up period. If such changes occurred, the observed results may have been underestimated. Importantly, residual confounding is still a possibility, even after adjusting for known confounders in the primary and sensitivity analyses. Second, despite the exclusion of participants who developed multimorbidity within 2 years of follow‐up, reverse causality remains possible. Third, participants who completed the submaximal ramped cycle ergometer test were in better physical condition than the general population; therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, a gold standard method for assessing CRF was not used in this study. Furthermore, the study was limited to British White participants, and this selection bias may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. Finally, because of the limited number of participants in the submaximal ramped cycle ergometer test, we did not include all individuals who participated in the GS test. Future studies should explore the role of GS in relation to multimorbidity using larger sample sizes.

CONCLUSIONS

High estimated CRF and GS values were associated with a reduced risk of multimorbidity and tendency toward complex multisystem disease patterns. The lowest multimorbidity risk was observed in adults exhibiting high estimated CRF and GS. Prioritizing cardiovascular health monitoring and management in individuals with low estimated CRF and GS is crucial for preventing multisystem comorbidities. A combined approach targeting improvements in both estimated CRF and GS may represent the most effective behavioral strategy for mitigating multimorbidity risk, surpassing interventions focused on either factor alone.
