Nanoimprinted polyamide membranes for ultrafast and precise molecular sieving with low fouling
Authors: Pengrui Jin, Zhao Yang, Frederik Ceyssens, Jiakuan Yang, Shushan Yuan, Huanting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polyamide membranes with ultrahigh permeance and exceptional solute selectivity present a significant opportunity to reduce energy consumption in desalination, pharmaceutical purification, and solvent recovery. We report a nanomolding phase inversion strategy for constructing high-resolution pillar-array patterns on a nanofibrous Kevlar hydrogel support, enabling controlled interfacial polymerization (IP) of polyamide active layers with pillar-arrayed structures. The rigid polyamide layers preserve pillar textures under pressurized filtration, increasing permeable area, while the nanofibrous Kevlar regulates amine diffusion to enhance polyamide layer homogeneity. The resulting nanoimprinted composite membranes with thin, structurally homogeneous, highly negatively charged polyamide layers demonstrate a water permeance of 53.9 L m-2 h-1 bar-1 with 98.1% Na2SO4 rejection, high Cl-/SO42- selectivity (45), and improved antifouling properties. In active pharmaceutical ingredients enrichment, they achieve one order of magnitude faster methanol transport, e.g., 31.3 L m-2 h-1 bar-1, than commercial membranes. Our nanoimprinted strategy may inspire advanced membrane designs for diverse high-value separations.

Introduction

Traditional equilibrium separation techniques in the chemical industry, which rely heavily on energy-intensive phase transitions, account for approximately 15% of global energy consumption1. Compared with conventional methods such as distillation and adsorption, membrane technology, driven by a distinctive rate-governed separation mechanism, offers tenfold improvements in energy efficiency2,3. A notable example is advancing desalination and solvent recovery through polyamide thin-film composite (TFC) membranes, achieved by the precise separation of solutes or ions4–9. The critically functional layer of a TFC is a highly crosslinked polyamide active layer, which is synthesized in situ on a porous substrate via IP between diamine monomers such as m-phenylene diamine (MPD) and piperazine (PIP) in the aqueous phase and acid chloride monomers such as trimesoyl chloride (TMC) in a nonpolar organic solvent10,11. This layer is dense enough to separate salt ions from water and high-value molecules from organic solvents, but thin enough to have a low resistance to the transport of water and organic solvents. While these TFC membranes demonstrate superior permselectivity relative to others, specific attributes of the properties of the active layer and the manufacturing process present intrinsic constraints8,12,13.

The heterogeneity of the substrate underlying the active layer leads to irregular diffusion of diamine monomers and an erratic reaction boundary. This yields a selective layer with notable roughness, thickness, and uneven pore distribution. Therefore, refining the substrate’s properties and reaction conditions is critical for precisely controlling the IP process, which is vital for producing nanofiltration membranes with high permeance and precise molecular sieving capabilities11,14,15. Intensive research has focused on improving the permeance of active layers without compromising selectivity through the manipulation of various parameters, including varying monomer species and concentrations2,5,16, adding functional additives13,17,18, and optimizing fabrication conditions8,19,20. These interventions are designed to yield ultrathin, homogeneous polyamide nanofilms with significantly enhanced transport channels, achieved by either retarding the reaction kinetics or spatially restricting the reaction zone. However, further enhancing membrane permeance by reducing the active layer thickness yields diminishing returns owing to the formidable challenges in fabricating defect-free active layers that surpass the thinness of current benchmarks. Additionally, few strategies effectively enhance both permeance and selectivity through a straightforward and cost-effective process, while most do not sufficiently address fouling in real-world applications.

Nanoimprinted lithography is an advanced technique for high-throughput and high-resolution patterning of nanostructures on a substrate21–23. During nanoimprinting, a hard mask mold containing nanoscale surface-relief features is pressed into a soft polymeric resist, resulting in a conformal replica of the pattern of the mask on the polymeric resist24. This characteristic led Nagpal et al. to use this technique to make ultrasmooth patterned metals for plasmonics and metamaterials25. Other researchers have used this technique to make bright-field prints with resolutions up to the optical diffraction limit26, single-junction polymer solar cells exceeding 10% power conversion efficiency27, and large-area flexible three-dimensional optical negative metamaterials28.

Kevlar fibers with amine monomer (PIP/MPD) distribution behavior regulating and confining functions can control the IP reaction to prepare a polyamide active layer with an ultrathin and uniform pore size distribution29. For our approach, pillar patterns were constructed on the TFC membrane by using a nanofibrous Kevlar hydrogel support membrane with high-throughput and high-resolution pillar-array patterns as the support. The resulting pillar-patterned structure is expected to increase the available area for water or solvent permeation per projected area on the support; undoubtedly, increasing the effective permeable area of the ultrathin polyamide active layer can further increase water or organic permeance and overcome the thickness reduction limitation. More importantly, compared with conventional flat membranes, engineered membranes are expected to have enhanced antifouling properties because of amplified hydrodynamic forces on pollutants, which are influenced by the pillar-patterned topography disrupting fluid flow.

Results and discussion

Nanopatterning of Kevlar hydrogel substrates and polyamide active layers

This approach is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. A wafer mold with an array of holes was fabricated via standard microelectronic photolithography, ensuring high-resolution and reproducible patterning. The size and topography of the holes on the mold are predefined, which eventually determines the diameter and array of the pillars on the hydrogel support membrane. Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2 show the dimensions and microtopography of the hole patterns in detail. The hole diameter and depth are 6.2 ± 0.1 μm and 3.2 ± 0.1 μm, respectively, with a center-to-center spacing of 10 μm between adjacent holes. Kevlar dope was cast onto a wafer mold to fill the holes, forming a thin layer. To prevent air bubble entrapment that could distort the pillar morphology, the dope viscosity was kept low to ensure smooth flow. Additionally, the casting speed was carefully controlled to allow the solution to displace air within the mold gradually without causing turbulence or trapping air. This layer was solidified via phase inversion in water, resulting in a Kevlar hydrogel membrane with a pillar-array structure, which was then peeled off. The microtopography of the membrane surfaces was confirmed via SEM, as shown in Fig. 1a. The pillars on the hydrogel membrane are “negative” replicas of the holes in the wafer mold. These pillars have well-defined shapes and symmetrical patterns without noticeable deformations. Specifically, the shape of the pillar is more like a frustum of a cone with a radius of 3.6 ± 0.1 μm at the top and 6.2 ± 0.1 μm at the bottom, whereas the height of the pillars is 2.1 ± 0.1 (Fig. 1a1). These dimensions are slightly smaller than those of the holes in the wafer mold, which is due mainly to shrinkage during phase inversion. Furthermore, limited diffusion of the dope solution into the deeper regions of the holes may also contribute to this variation. The distance between adjacent pillars is still 10 μm because the pillars were fixed in the holes during the phase inversion (Fig. 1a). Notably, the hydrogel membrane and pillars are composed of nanofibers with a diameter of approximately 50 nm (Fig. 1a2 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4), with a porosity of 96.5%, which is much greater than that of other reported ultrafiltration membranes30. This nanofibrous structure and high-porosity normally favor water or organic solvent permeance when used as a support membrane for the synthesis of a TFC membrane because of the low resistance of water to pass through. In addition, the whole pattern also appears homogenous and extends over the entire membrane surface (Supplementary Fig. 5, low magnification), demonstrating the efficiency of the microimprinting method, with more than three million pillar structures per square centimeter (3.46 × 106/cm2). Conventional polymers like PES and PSf shrink or wrinkle during nonsolvent-induced phase separation, distorting surface patterns and compromising suitability for IP. In contrast, Kevlar’s rigid backbone, strong hydrogen bonding, and low swelling preserve structural fidelity, enabling high-resolution, distortion-free free-standing membranes. Only Kevlar retained patterned integrity under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6). A patterned thin polyamide film is believed to greatly increase the effective surface area for water or organic solvent transport, thereby significantly improving permeance while maintaining rejection during nanofiltration. Here, we use a pillar-patterned Kevlar hydrogel membrane as the porous support to obtain a pillar-patterned thin polyamide layer. As shown in the scheme of IP in Supplementary Fig. 8, the surface of the patterned Kevlar hydrogel membrane was first wetted with diamine solution (PIP 0.02 wt% or MPD 2 wt%). The excess large solution drops on the membrane surface were then evaporated by placing the membrane in a fume hood for a few minutes. The interfacial reaction was initiated by adding the TMC (0.1 wt%) organic phase to the membrane. After reacting for 1 min, a TFC membrane was obtained. The obtained pillar patterns of the Kevlar hydrogel not only provide additional surface area for the thin polyamide layer to attach but also result in microspaces between neighboring pillars. Owing to capillary action, such microspaces tend to contain excessive diamine solutions, which react with the TMC organic phase. This leads to the formation of a polyamide layer at the interface between the excess diamine and the organic TMC rather than on the surface of the pillars (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b), resulting in the formation of less patterned or even flat membranes. To decrease the amount of excessive diamine solution in microspaces, controlling the evaporation time is critical. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a, the membrane synthesized from a commonly used evaporation time of 4 min has a flat surface. When the time was increased to 8 min, the obtained membrane presented a hump array pattern (Supplementary Fig. 8b). This hump structure results from partial evaporation of the diamine solution from the gaps between neighboring pillars of the Kevlar hydrogel membrane. When the evaporation time was further increased to 12 min (Supplementary Fig. 8c), the pillar-patterned structures were eventually obtained.

As shown in Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 9, a dense layer of PIP-based polyamide film covers the porous pillars of the Kevlar hydrogel membrane, confirming the successful preparation of the pillar-patterned TFC membrane. Two types of patterned polyamide membranes were synthesized: one was synthesized from the reaction between PIP (0.02 wt%) and TMC (0.1 wt%), and the other was fabricated from MPD (2 wt%) and TMC (0.1 wt%). The two types of membranes both consist of well-aligned and uniform conical pillars spanning the whole surface (Fig. 1a–c and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10), with the same density (3.46 × 106/cm2) as the pillars on the Kevlar hydrogel membrane. However, the shapes of the pillars on the two types of membranes are different. The PIP-based membrane pillars are similar to the pillars on the Kevlar support membrane. The edge of the pillars is sharp, indicating the close combination of the polyamide film and the Kevlar membrane. In addition, the side face of the polyamide pillars is plicated rather than smooth; six ridges connecting neighboring pillars can be easily spotted. These ridge structures are attributed mainly to capillary action, which results in the accumulation of a reactant diamine solution at the narrow gap between two neighboring pillars (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 8c). The plicated surface structure of the pillar is expected to further increase the surface area of the polyamide film to improve the permeance. Furthermore, the distinct surface morphologies of the polyamide layer arise from local variations in water content during IP reaction. On the patterned Kevlar substrate, extended drying (~12 min) was applied to ensure uniform polyamide layer formation. However, due to geometric confinement, the valleys between pillars retain more residual water than the pillar tops. The drier pillar surfaces promote faster, diffusion-limited IP, resulting in nodular structures. In contrast, the water-rich gaps resemble free aqueous environments, where more uniform monomer distribution leads to smoother polyamide layer morphology. The MPD-based polyamide TFC membrane has a different shape of pillars with relatively low heights and round edges, resulting in a curved surface. This surface is very different from the pillar-patterned Kevlar membrane, which suggests that the MPD-based polyamide film is not closely attached to the nanoporous pillar of the Kevlar membrane. This is mainly due to the relatively high rigidity and thickness (Supplementary Fig. 11) of the MPD-based polyamide film compared with those of the PIP-based polyamide film. The high rigidity and thickness endow the polyamide with high stiffness, making bending to stick on the pillars of the Kevlar support membrane difficult. In addition, typical nodule (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 12)-like and leaf (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 13)-like structures resulting from the Kevlar fiber in the aqueous phase reduced the diffusion of the monomers on the surface of the membrane, implying that the polyamide film was not influenced by the patterns of the support membrane.

The specific topography and dimensions of these pillars are demonstrated via SEM and AFM measurements. As shown in Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 12, the radius of the conical pillar on the PIP-based polyamide membrane is 5.6 μm at the top and 8.5 μm at the bottom, whereas the height is 1.7 μm and the distance between neighboring pillars is 10 μm. The dimensions of the pillars on the MPD-based polyamide membrane were also obtained. It has a diameter of approximately 5.2 μm at the top and 7.7 μm at the bottom and a height of 1.5 μm (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 13). The polyamide layer heights (1.7 μm for PIP-based and 1.5 μm for MPD-based) are slightly lower than the pillar height (2.1 μm) on Kevlar substrate, AFM profiles show that PIP-based polyamide layers follow the substrate topography better. The lower height of the MPD-based polyamide layer may result from nanobubbles (~0.3 μm) present at the surface, as observed in Supplementary Fig. 15d, rather than indicating poor contact with the substrate. Assuming that the surface of the pillars is smooth, the increased surface area compared with that of the flat membrane could be calculated on the basis of the above data related to the dimensions of the pillars. As shown in Fig. 1d, the whole membrane surface can be divided into numerous equilateral triangles with an area of 52.09 µm2, which is much larger than the area of a flat equilateral triangle (43.3 µm2). This suggests that the surface area increased by 20.3% after being patterned with pillars. This significant increase in surface area is expected to greatly improve the water or solvent permeability of the prepared membrane. By using a similar calculation, the MPD-based polyamide membrane would have a 16.4% surface area enhancement.

Polyamide active layer on the pillar-patterned Kevlar hydrogel membrane

To investigate the thickness and mechanical robustness of the polyamide active layer, these layers were detached from the Kevlar substrate by immersing the TFC membranes in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for more than 24 h. Figure 2a and Supplementary Fig. 11 illustrate the successful transfer of the isolated, free-standing active layers onto alumina substrates, which notably maintained their integrity without macroscopic defects. Moreover, the discernible morphology of the alumina substrates corroborates the ultrathin nature of the polyamide layers. To evaluate whether the substrate geometry affects the apparent polyamide layer thickness, we compared films formed on flat and patterned Kevlar substrates. The active layers were transferred onto a silicon wafer to precisely confirm the thickness via atomic force microscopy (AFM, Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 14). AFM height profiles revealed that the F-PIP-TMC (~12 nm) and P-PIP-TMC (~17 nm) layer thicknesses were both less than ~20 nm, whereas the F-MPD-TMC (~60 nm) and P-MPD-TMC (~75 nm) layer thicknesses were both less than ~80 nm. The AFM results reveal that the PIP-TMC and MPD-TMC layers on the patterned Kevlar substrates are thicker than those on the flat substrates. This difference stems from the increased surface area of the patterned substrates, which, after the active layers are transferred onto the silicon wafers, lead to folding and overlapping, thereby increasing the overall thickness of the layer. To confirm whether these differences were due to actual variations in polyamide formation or merely artifacts from film transfer, we conducted cross-sectional TEM imaging. Subsequent investigations into the thickness of the active layer, as depicted in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 15 through cross-sectional TEM images, reveal that both flat and patterned TFC membranes exhibit identical active layer thicknesses. Specifically, the thickness for both the F-PIP-TMC and P-PIP-TMC configurations is approximately 15 nm, whereas the F-MPD-TMC and P-MPD-TMC configurations are approximately 60 nm thick. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the chemical composition of the polyamide active layers. Analysis of the XPS spectra of both the F-PIP-TMC and P-PIP-TMC active layers revealed identical chemical compositions, with equal abundances of C, N, and O (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 16). The degree of crosslinking of the polyamide active layers was calculated on the basis of the O/N elemental ratio from the XPS spectra. The degrees of crosslinking of polyamide active layers prepared from Kevlar substrates without and with patterned structures are 59.3% and 55.1%, respectively (Fig. 2d and Table S2), suggesting that the presence of patterned structures has no significant influence on the crosslinking of the polyamide active layer. The degree of crosslinking observed in this study is notably lower than that of polyamide films produced through traditional methods, which typically exhibit a degree of crosslinking of more than 70%13,20,31. The 3D nanofibrous configuration of the Kevlar hydrogel may decelerate amine molecule diffusion to the organic phase boundary during the diffusion-driven growth stage. This deceleration results in a polyamide active layer with a lower degree of crosslinking, a modification that is beneficial for increasing water permeability.

In addition, the molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of polyamide nanofiltration membranes prepared with and without patterned structures was measured to investigate the effect of the degree of crosslinking. Our results show that the MWCO for F-PIP-TMC is 385 Da and that the MWCO for P-PIP-TMC is 379 Da (Fig. 2e). These findings indicate that the two membranes have similar MWCOs. A narrow pore size distribution with a pore radius of 0.364 nm for F-PIP-TMC and 0.372 nm for P-PIP-TMC is achieved, which lies right between the sizes of hydrated Cl- ions (0.332 nm) and SO42− ions (0.379 nm) (Fig. 2e). Moreover, compared with other reported polyamide membranes, both the P-PIP-TMC and F-PIP-TMC membranes have relatively low rejections of MWCO-200 (equivalent Stokes radius of 3.2 Å) but comparable rejections of MWCO-400 (equivalent Stokes radius of 4.7 Å), thereby resulting in a large rejection difference and precise ionic recognition. Upon analyzing the high-resolution spectra for deconvoluted C1s, O1s, and N1s peaks (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Table S3), it was observed that the O-C = O content (attributable to carboxyl groups, –COOH) within the polyamide active layer presented herein exceeds that found in most other nanofiltration membranes13,20,31. This elevated concentration correlates with a reduced degree of crosslinking, which aligns well with the observed larger pore sizes in the polyamide active layers. The increased availability of free carboxyl groups promotes the formation of a highly ionized membrane. Consequently, both membrane surfaces become negatively charged at pH >3, with the P-PIP-TMC membrane demonstrating a slightly stronger negative charge, as shown in Fig. 2f. Importantly, their isoelectric points fall below pH 3, distinguishing them from the isoelectric points typical of most nanofiltration membranes (>3.3), owing to the TMC in the reactions that leave higher amounts of terminal carboxyl groups on the surface of active layers, significantly improving their selectivity toward Cl−/SO42− 32.

Polyamide layer generation involves complex reaction‒diffusion, with a 3D Kevlar hydrogel potentially slowing amine (taking PIP as an example) monomer diffusion to the interface. MD simulations at the water/hexane interface with and without Kevlar were used to evaluate the movement of PIP through the mean square displacement (MSD) and self-diffusion coefficient (D) in the z direction. The results in Fig. 2g indicate that the MSD and D of PIP in the Kevlar system (D = 4.2 × 10−6 cm2 s−1) are approximately one-third those in the Kevlar-free system (D = 1.2 × 10−5 m2 s−1), indicating reduced PIP diffusivity with Kevlar. This reduction in the self-diffusion coefficient led to fewer PIP molecules at the interface, as shown in Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 17. The PIP number density (ρN) distribution peak at ~37 Å indicates amine monomer accumulation in the reaction zone. With Kevlar, the reaction zone (z = 32–42 Å) is narrower than that without (z = 29–44 Å), promoting a thinner polyamide layer in the presence of Kevlar (Fig. 2i).

To assess the uniformity of diffusion, the quantity of PIP molecules that transition from the aqueous phase to the interface was quantified. The increased number of interfacial PIP molecules (∆N) was more pronounced in the Kevlar-free system, as depicted in Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 18, indicating that Kevlar’s barrier effect on PIP molecule diffusion. The fluctuations in ∆N in the absence of Kevlar suggested nonuniform diffusion, leading to a polyamide layer with heterogeneous polymerization and a widely distributed free volume pore size. Conversely, the presence of Kevlar ensured uniform PIP diffusion, favoring the development of structurally consistent polyamide layers. Within the reaction zone incorporating Kevlar, the observed quantity of interfacial PIP molecules (~10) was markedly lower than the number of molecules noted in the absence of Kevlar (~30) (Fig. 2h). This 66% reduction in interfacial PIP molecules in the Kevlar-containing system further confirmed the diffusion-retarding impact of Kevlar. Due to computational limitations, micrometer-scale MD simulations are not available to directly capture the influence of the patterned Kevlar substrate (~5 μm feature size). However, since IP occurs at the nanometer scale and the resulting polyamide layer thickness is ~10 nm, nanoscale simulations are sufficient to capture molecular interactions and reaction behavior during polyamide layer formation.

Performance of nanopatterned nanofiltration membranes

The desalination separation performance of the pillared patterned membranes is investigated and presented in Fig. 3, where much higher water permeance is desired. The PIP-based polyamide membrane has a water permeance as high as 53.9 L m−2 h−1 bar−1, which is 26.1% higher than that of the flat membrane, whereas 98.1% of the Na2SO4 in water is rejected. The superhigh water permeance is attributed mainly to the significantly increased surface area of the polyamide layer due to the patterning of the pillar structures. Moreover, compared with those of all other reported TFC nanofiltration membranes, the performance of pillar-patterned membranes exceeds that of these membranes by a large margin. Notably, the water permeability improvement (26.1%) is greater than the increase in surface area (20.3%), which is attributed mainly to the plicated structures on the side faces of the pillars and the gaps between two neighboring pillars (Fig. 1d). These plicated structures further increase the effective surface area of the PIP-based membranes. Furthermore, Fig. 3a shows that the F-PIP-TMC and P-PIP-TMC membranes exhibit consistent salt rejection patterns, with significantly higher rejection rates for Na2SO4 and MgSO4 than for MgCl2, CaCl2, NaCl, and LiCl, possibly due to the combined effects of the strong Donnan effect and size sieving specific to sulfate ions. The observed slight decline in rejection of MgSO₄ in sulfate salts dominated by divalent anions aligns with typical behavior of negatively charged nanofiltration membranes. In contrast, chloride salts are less affected by Donnan exclusion and are mainly governed by size exclusion. As a result, MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ exhibit higher rejection than NaCl, due to the larger hydration radii of Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. Notably, both membranes achieve over 98% rejection for Na2SO4; compared with less than 10% for NaCl, the separation factor of Cl−/SO42− is as high as 45, underscoring the superior performance across nanofiltration applications and highlighting the exceptional ability of these membranes to separate monovalent and divalent anions (Fig. 3b Supplementary Table 4). The high Cl⁻/SO₄²⁻ selectivity arises from combined Donnan and dielectric exclusion. The negatively charged polyamide layer repels SO₄²− due to electrostatic interactions, while the dielectric environment within the membrane amplifies repulsion via hydration energy penalties, especially for highly hydrated multivalent ions. The patterned membrane increases the surface area for water transport, but this alone does not account for the high permeance observed, indicating that other factors are involved. A thinner polyamide layer further increases the permeance. Conventional TFC membranes face performance limitations due to the “funnel effect,” which is caused by low substrate porosity (< 10%)33. As shown in Fig. 3c, the water transport distance (lr) is greater than the polyamide layer thickness (l), increasing the hydraulic resistance. To counter this, the use of a high-porosity (~95%) Kevlar substrate reduces the transport distance in the rejection layer. The patterned polyamide membrane combines a reduced layer thickness, optimized pathways, and greater surface area, synergistically enhancing water permeance. Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20 reveal the polyamide layer tightly adhered to the Kevlar substrate with some crumpled structure in the inter-pillar regions, while these do not indicate delamination or suspension. Supplementary Fig. 21 shows the continuous filtration of Na₂SO₄ solution over 120 h, during which the P-PIP-TMC membranes maintained stable permeance and rejection. Dynamic fouling tests with bovine serum albumin (BSA) and humic acid (HA) on the F-PIP-TMC and P-PIP-TMC membranes, as reported in Supplementary Fig. 22, revealed different fouling behaviors. The water flux of F-PIP-TMC decreased to 13.8% and recovered to 93.1% after washing with BSA, whereas that of P-PIP-TMC was limited to 10.8%, with a recovery rate of 94.6%. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis revealed that the mean fluorescence intensity of the flat membrane, indicative of fouling, was greater than that of the patterned membrane, which suggests a greater fouling propensity (Fig. 3d). After physical crossflow cleaning, the patterned membrane demonstrated a lower fluorescence intensity, indicating easier foulant removal and superior fouling resistance (Fig. 3d). Engineered surface features on patterned membranes increase shear stress and turbulence. These features disrupt water flow, generating vortices that reduce foulant deposition by preventing adhesion near the membrane surface (Fig. 3e). Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations shed light on this effect, showing that the shear stress at the pattern apexes significantly exceeds that within the valleys (Fig. 3f), thus mitigating fouling through enhanced shear forces at these high points. Furthermore, these surface patterns disrupt the hydrodynamic boundary layer, lessening the interaction force and contact area with foulants and improving fouling resistance. Supplementary Fig. 23 indicate that, owing to the no-slip condition, fluid flow remains parallel to flat surfaces, maintaining a velocity close to zero at the interface. In contrast, at the apexes of patterned membranes, the velocity significantly exceeds zero, as evidenced by the altered flow direction. This phenomenon significantly impedes foulant ingress into the valleys, a primary factor contributing to decreased fouling. Compared to state-of-the-art nanofiltration membranes fabricated via IP, the patterned P-PIP-TMC membrane exhibits markedly enhanced antifouling performance (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 5). Its flux maintenance ratio (1 − FDR) significantly surpasses the typical 40–80% range reported in the literature. Notably, while most membranes achieve low fouling only at modest initial fluxes, P-PIP-TMC sustains its ultralow fouling behavior even at high initial permeance. This highlights its superior resistance to organic fouling and underscores its performance advantage over leading nanofiltration membranes.

Owing to its remarkable stability and high separation performance, the P-MPD-TMC membrane is thus applied for determining the concentration of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs). As a demonstration, the membrane was employed for the separation of sorafenib (464.8 g/mol, 0.52 nm × 0.82 nm) from a mixture of 4-chloro-3-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl isocyanate (CTPI, 221.6 g/mol, 0.9 nm × 1.7 nm) and sorafenib (Fig. 4e). The HPLC results in Fig. 4f and Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28 demonstrate retention times of 5.6 and 6.8 for sorafenib and CTPI, respectively, with sorafenib nearly disappearing, whereas the peak for CTPI shows slight changes. These results indicate that sorafenib can be rejected when CTPI passes through the membrane. Figure 4g depicts the consecutive concentration of feed solution for 12 h, and the P-MPD-TMC membranes presented one order of magnitude greater methanol permeance than did the commercial membranes. Moreover, after 12 h of operation, sorafenib was successfully purified and separated from the feed mixture, which initially contained equal proportions of sorafenib and CTPI, resulting in a nearly pure single component of sorafenib (Fig. 4h). These results demonstrate the great potential of the fabricated membrane in pharmaceutical production.

Furthermore, nanoimprinting technology has undergone significant advancements through the integration of roll-to-roll (R2R) technologies, establishing it as a powerful method for high-throughput, large-scale manufacturing34. Although silicon molds were used in this study, future work will focus on developing flexible nanomolding platforms to further enhance industrial feasibility. As illustrated in Fig. 5, R2R technology employs rollers or flexible nanoimprint molds to continuously transfer nanoscale patterns onto a doped flexible substrate, optimizing the imprinting efficiency. Drawing inspiration from traditional industrial roller printing, this approach enables the high-speed production of nanoimprinted membranes with uniform quality, making it particularly advantageous for membrane fabrication. Overall, we developed a straightforward and versatile method to design high-resolution pillar-array patterns on polyamide TFC membranes by employing a nanoimprinted strategy while maintaining stoichiometric balance at the interface of the IP reaction. The Kevlar substrate with pillar-array patterns templated a polyamide layer, increasing the specific surface area and the fraction of the highly permeable polyamide skin layer. The crumped morphology and surface area can be fine-tuned by adjusting the pattern density and size. Additionally, the aramid nanofiber network confines and regulates the diffusion of amine monomers, ensuring a uniform flux across the water‒oil interface, leading to spatially homogeneous polymerization and a polyamide layer with greater structural homogeneity. As a result, this approach increases the effective permeable area, ensures nanoscale uniformity, and preserves surface charge properties, resulting in polyamide TFC membranes with ultrahigh permeance and highly selective separation performance for both nanofiltration desalination and organic solvent nanofiltration. Moreover, the patterned polyamide TFC membranes demonstrated a reduced fouling propensity, as the pattern structures enhanced the hydrodynamic properties (shear stress, velocity, and turbulence) effectively deterring foulant adhesion. This study provides a strategy for the precise control of membrane structures at both the microscale and the nanoscale, providing opportunities for fast and precise separation.

Methods

Preparation of the wafer mold

The wafer mold fabrication procedures (Supplementary Fig. 29) were conducted in air in a cleanroom facility (Class 1000). The photomask was created via KLayout software and patterned via direct laser writing. A low-reflectivity chromium mask blank coated with 500 nm AZ1505 photoresist was exposed to a 405 nm wavelength laser at a dose of 15 mJ/cm² via an LW405 laser writer (Micro-Tech), providing a patterning precision of approximately 1–2 µm. After 1 min of development in a 25% diluted Microposit 351 developer (Dow Chemical), the chromium layer was etched with CR-14 etchant (Transene Co., Inc.) for 1 min. The remaining photoresist was removed by immersion in acetone, followed by rinsing with isopropanol and water.

A standard (100) silicon wafer (Siltronix) was then spin-coated with ma-N 1410 negative photoresist (MicroResist GmbH) at 3000 rpm and baked at 100 °C for 90 s. The photoresist was exposed to 450 mJ/cm² of broadband UV light via an EVG620 contact aligner and developed for 60 s in a 50% Microposit 351 developer solution. Silicon etching was performed via reactive ion etching (Plasma Technology PT100) at 30 mTorr, 100 W RF power, and a 20 sccm SF6 gas flow. Finally, oxygen plasma was used to strip the resist, and the pattern thickness was measured via white-light interferometry (Sensofar).

Fabrication of pillar-patterned Kevlar hydrogel support membranes

The preparation of Kevlar dope solution was reported in our previous study. In brief, 2.5 × g of water and 2 × g of KOH were first added to a glass bottle. When the KOH was completely dissolved in the water, 100 ml of DMSO was then added to the mixture with stirring to obtain a homogeneous mixture. KOH abstracts the labile hydrogen from NH groups, weakening hydrogen bonding between polymer chains and facilitating the breakdown of Kevlar fibers into nanofibers. Afterward, 1.5 × g of bulk Kevlar 69 was added to the mixture and stirred for 2 days at ambient temperature, resulting in a dark red Kevlar solution. To remove impurities, the solution was centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 10 min.

The patterned Kevlar hydrogel support membrane was synthesized from a modified nanoimprinting process by negatively replicating the patterns on the wafer mold. The Kevlar solution was cast on the wafer mold by a casting knife with a gap of 200 μm at room temperature, during which the holes on the wafer were filled with the polymer solution. The doped solution was subsequently solidified by immersing the wafer with the doped solution in water for phase inversion. As a result, the pillar shapes of the doped solution in the hole of the wafer mold were frozen, forming pillars patterned with Kevlar hydrogel membranes. The support membrane was stored in deionized water for further IP. A flat Kevlar hydrogel support membrane was prepared via a similar procedure, except a flat glass plate rather than a wafer mold was used.

Synthesis of patterned TFC membranes via IP

The TFC membrane was synthesized via a typical IP of TMC with MPD or PIP. Briefly, a patterned Kevlar hydrogel membrane was first wetted with 6 ml of diamine monomer solution (2 wt% MPD, 0.02 wt% PIP) at a certain concentration for 10 min. Prior to the reaction, the redundant solutions on the membrane surface were evaporated in the fume hood for a certain min. The reaction was then initialized by adding TMC/n-hexane solution (0.1 w/v%) to the surface of the diamine monomer-saturated Kevlar hydrogel membrane. After reacting for 1 min, the TMC n-hexane solution was removed, and the prepared TFC membrane was cured in an oven for 6 min at 60 °C. The obtained membranes were stored in deionized water until use.

Separation performance evaluation

The water permeance and salt rejection of the pillar-patterned membranes were measured via a cross-flow setup with an effective area of 22.9 cm2 at a pressure of 4 bar and a cross-flow velocity of 30 L h−1. Membrane permeability and rejection were measured with deionized water before nanofiltration experiments using feed solutions containing 1000 ppm various salts (Na₂SO₄, MgSO₄, MgCl₂, CaCl₂, NaCl, LiCl). The OSN performance of the TFC membrane was tested with a dead-end filtration apparatus (Sterlitech HP4750) at 5 bar with constant stirring at 600 rpm to minimize concentration polarization. For the single-solvent filtration system, solvents, including n-hexene, toluene, IPA, NMP, DMSO, ethanol, DMF, methanol, and acetone, were used. OSN experiments for dye (Table S5) filtration were used to measure permeance and rejection after achieving steady-state permeance. The concentrations of the feed, permeate, and retentate were determined via ultraviolet‒visible (UV–vis) absorption. UV spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer within the 200–800 nm range.

Dynamic fouling tests

To evaluate the antifouling properties of the membranes, the flux recovery rate, reversible fouling rate, and irreversible fouling rate on the membrane surface were measured via synthetic, heavily polluted sewage containing a suspension of BSA and HA over two filtration cycles. Additionally, the adhesion of foulants on the membrane surface was examined via CLSM to assess the resistance of the membrane to organic fouling. The hydraulic conditions near the membrane surface, with both flat and patterned structures, were characterized by velocity distribution and vector mapping via CFD simulations to analyze how surface structures influence antifouling behavior.

Separation and concentration of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs)

A synthetic feedstock mixture containing two APIs, sorafenib (100 mg/L) and CTPI (100 mg/L), was prepared in methanol (MeOH) for diafiltration experiments aimed at enriching sorafenib. The transmembrane pressure was maintained at 5 bar, with the temperature held constant at approximately 25 °C (room temperature). Before the experiment, the commercial organic solvent nanofiltration membrane (AMS Memtech SN-1H) was soaked in pure ethanol overnight to eliminate any residual conditioning preservatives. The permeate was collected for analysis, while the retentate was recirculated into the feedstock solution. Pure methanol (MeOH) was added as needed to maintain a constant feedstock volume throughout the experiment. An Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column was used to analyze the concentrations of sorafenib and CTPI. The mobile phase consisted of 1% v/v formic acid in water (40%) and acetonitrile (60%), with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C.
