Loaded and coated: Outer membrane vesicles get a critical boost from a lipopolysaccharide adjuvant payload in a rationally designed anti-Helicobacter pylori nanoparticle
Authors: Jesse A Jones, Dmitri Simberg
Published: 2026

Helicobacter pylori remains one of the most prominent and formidable human pathogens worldwide. Infecting roughly 50% of the global population, it is well recognized as the primary etiological cause of several notable morbidities, including peptic ulcers, chronic gastritis, and gastric cancers such as mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma and gastric adenocarcinoma.1, 2, 3 Though H pylori was discovered in the early 1980s, it remains a highly resilient pathogen. Because of the broad emergence of multidrug-resistant strains, the modern “gold standard” therapeutic regimen for treatment-naïve H pylori patients now consists of optimized bismuth quadruple therapy that includes a proton pump inhibitor, bismuth subcitrate or subsalicylate, tetracycline, and metronidazole for 14 days.4

In addition to the onerous treatment profile required for H pylori, various comorbidities and treatment-related complications often arise, such as frequent eradication failures, persistent infections and reinfections, dysbiosis, and even severe iatrogenic complications such as Clostridioides difficile colitis from extended broad-spectrum antibiotic use.5 The need for alternative therapeutic options, including an effective H pylori vaccine, is therefore largely undisputed. Nonetheless, the pursuit of effective immunization has thus far been plagued by unsuccessful attempts. In a recently published The Journal of Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics article, however, Ye et al6 illustrate an innovative and novel preclinical advance via a rationally designed biomimetic nano-vaccine capable of addressing major deficiencies in previous vaccine platforms, including standard outer membrane vesicle (OMV) vaccines, by integrating OMV antigenic payloads with structural nanoparticle scaffolding and synergistic adjuvant boosting.

OMVs are membranous blebs shed by Gram-negative bacteria that carry components of the bacteria’s natural proteomic fingerprint, such as key H pylori cytotoxin-associated gene A and vacuolating cytotoxin A antigens, among others.7 OMVs are naturally immunogenic and considered promising for vaccine development, but have been found to be mostly ineffective on their own due to pharmacological obstacles such as aggregation, poor structural stability, heterogeneous vesicle sizes causing inconsistent dendritic cell uptake and incomplete immunogenicity, and toxicity from high levels of components such as exposed lipopolysaccharide (LPS) leading to unwanted reactogenicity instead of immunogenicity.8

Ye et al rationally address these issues by leveraging a solid core scaffold comprised of “dendritic mesoporous organosilica nanoparticles” (DMONs). DMONs represent next-generation organic-inorganic advancements over traditional mesoporous silica nanoparticles, offering 3-dimensional nanochannels and branching pores. They are designed for triggerable biodegradability, possess large mesopores with high relative surface area, and offer facile functionalization.9 They also provide the structural rigidity that biological OMVs lack. In an innovative act, Ye et al modified DMONs by loading them with purified LPS directly from H pylori to serve as a specifically tuned adjuvant (LPS@DMON). This is opposed to LPS from Escherichia coli, which possesses characteristically high toxicity.10 Lastly, the group coated the LPS@DMON scaffold with a bilayer of H pylori outer membrane vesicles (HM) via standard physical extrusion methods. This completed the “loaded and coated” LPS@DMON@HM nanoparticle capable of transforming a promising yet fragile biological vesicle into a robust and practical pharmacological nano-vaccine prototype.

Although the practical hallmark of the study by Ye et al is the rational design of the LPS@DMON@HM platform, the supporting preclinical toxicity and immune response studies are the early evidence of its potential in addressing a major hurdle associated with H pylori vaccine development. Apart from the pathogen’s robust nature and unique ability to establish a foothold in the harsh environment of the human gastric mucosa, H pylori elicits a multifaceted and skewed immune response that keeps the host immune system “off balance.”11 Usually, dendritic cells respond to initial bacterial infection by presenting antigens to naïve CD4+ T cells in a manner that triggers appropriate activation and differentiation into a dynamic yet effective balance of different T-helper (Th) cells that regulate the immune response.12 H pylori leverages unique biological tools to manipulate the host infection site, such as vacuolating cytotoxin A, which interferes with interleukin-2 (IL-2) activity and inhibits proper Th cell activation and proliferation, effectively weakening the immune response. Vacuolating cytotoxin A also promotes differentiation toward Foxp3-positive T-regulatory cells in a directly proportional manner, which further promotes a tolerogenic environment skewed away from Th1/Th17 cells and toward T-regulatory cells, a notable feature that supports persistent infection.13

Initially, H pylori infection typically causes a notable polarization of CD4+ T cells toward a heavy Th1 cell phenotype via the Tbet transcription factor, discernible via the substantial dendritic cell production of IL-12, which also regulates interferon gamma expression. This is opposed to relatively lower amounts of the Th2-respective cytokine, IL-4, again representing an abnormal Th1/Th2 disequilibrium during initial infection. This initial bias toward Th1 pathway activation is notable as Th1 cells primarily function in response to infection from intracellular pathogens.12 Th2 cells, on the other hand, primarily function as a response to extracellular pathogens and allergic insults via humoral immunity, typically serving to mitigate inflammatory damage to the gastric mucosa. If the patient is unable to clear the infection, Th2 cells eventually become over-activated via IL-4, with GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3) being the specific master transcription factor inducing Th2 cytokines. Additionally, Th17 cells have been shown to serve an important role in early mucosal defense by recruiting neutrophils to the lamina propria via IL-17A and promoting epithelial cells to produce antimicrobial peptides via IL-22. Th17 cell count has also been positively linked to acute inflammatory responses in young H pylori patients, indicating Th17 cells are critical to clearing the infection.14

Historically, traditional H pylori vaccine candidates have failed because they elicited subtherapeutic or skewed immune responses—often favoring Th2 activation without effectively priming the Th1/Th17 activation needed for mucosal defense and clearance. The LPS@DMON@HM construct by Ye et al appears to address this problem. Murine data suggest balanced activation across all three helper T-cell subsets (Fig. 1a). The Th1 response was indicated by elevated interferon gamma and supports critical macrophage activation and intracellular immunity. The Th2 response was characterized by IL-4 production and heightened IgG titers, supporting the humoral response necessary for neutralizing bacteriotoxins and combating the pathogen’s mucosal attachment. The Th17 response was showcased by elevated IL-17A and heightened IgA titers (anti-Hp SIgA) in vaginal secretions and gastric mucosa, evidencing potential for an appropriate mucosal response needed to clear the H pylori infection.

Ye et al also showed that the nano-vaccine construct elicited no notable toxicity at the tested doses via cytotoxicity assays and murine model studies. This is significant as classical LPS is known for causing cytokine storms and is not used clinically as an adjuvant, although the detoxified analog monophosphoryl Lipid A is used in several major vaccines and, therefore, would be of interest in future studies regarding this platform.10 Here, both the integrity of the HM coating and the relatively high proportion of payload per nanoparticle afforded by the DMON core appear to have yielded favorable pharmacodynamic effects. The data suggest the HM coating adequately sequesters the LPS load well enough to successfully leverage the adjuvant and deliver it to the host’s antigen-presenting cells with enough specificity to avoid broad, systemic exposure. However, Ye et al did not objectively analyze this and, therefore, the possibility unfortunately remains largely speculative at this point. The high load-to-carrier ratio also potentially helps mitigate the chance of the carrier material itself “distracting” and overwhelming the immune system, though future studies must address this, perhaps by systematically and rationally varying the load-per-carrier amounts to ascertain exposure responses, release kinetics, and the resultant LPS-induced Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pharmacological effects. Furthermore, the actual pharmacological mechanism by which the internally LPS-loaded LPS@DMON@HM nanoparticle engages TLR4, which is predominantly recognized as an extracellular component, is of profound interest here.15 As such, future research must address whether native LPS from the HM component activates extracellular TLR4, triggering endocytosis of the receptor, where the LPS@DMON@HM particle is then degraded within the endosome and the LPS payload is released, thereby allowing the added LPS to engage with TLR4 in an amplified manner, leading to the increased cytokine response observed by Ye et al (Fig. 1b). Until this future work is done, this again remains speculative and key translational, immunogenic, and pharmacologic questions remain regarding the mechanism and efficacy of the LPS@DMON@HM platform.

Although indeed promising, it is important to address the fact that the study by Ye et al focuses on the “proof of concept” biocompatibility and immunogenicity elicited by LPS@DMON@HM, not actual prophylaxis or therapeutic capabilities against an active H pylori infection, which future directions, of course, also need to address. The study was designed to analyze interferon gamma, IL-4, and IL-17A cytokine profiles indicating respective active Th1/Th2/Th17 responses, which were all significantly enhanced; antibody production, which showed a 1:12,800 IgG titer, roughly 16 times higher than the free HM vaccine; macrophage uptake studies, showing improved phagocytosis of the designed nanoparticles as compared with free HM; and histological safety profiling showing the nanoparticles did not cause significant target organ damage under the studied conditions. It is, however, potentially simplified from an overall vaccinology and pharmacology perspective to rely entirely on a Th1/Th2/Th17 framework as it remains undetermined if the vaccine could prevent or effectively mitigate the H pylori-mediated T-regulatory cell induction that contributes to persistence; again, a notable limitation of the respective work by Ye et al and an assuredly critical future direction. Moreover, the main studies were carried out in a murine model, which has historically translated poorly to human trials, therefore potentially warranting future primate studies. Additionally, Ye et al used subcutaneous injections for their study, and, as H pylori is a gastric mucosa pathogen, results from oral or intranasal administration would be of interest, which again prompts a significant question regarding the intended delivery method and what the platform is practically capable of in vivo. Moreover, significant genetic variation exists for H pylori. The Sydney Strain 1 H pylori lab strain used by Ye et al is genetically adapted to colonize the murine gastric mucosa and is the standard strain used for in vivo H pylori pathogenesis, vaccine, and drug studies.16 Although Sydney Strain 1-derived OMVs may not guarantee broad coverage against wild-type pathogen strains found worldwide, Sydney Strain 1 is nonetheless an ideal candidate for preclinical proof of concept studies.

As a novel organic/inorganic nanoparticle platform, significant concerns remain regarding preclinical production. This includes scalability and long-term biocompatibility of the organosilica-based mesoporous nanoparticles. DMONs often require complex kinetic-controlled synthesis strategies, such as biphasic oil/water stratification methods.17 Although DMON synthesis has been proven to work well under small, controlled laboratory conditions such as those used by Ye et al, replicable industrial-scale batch syntheses capable of maintaining the uniform dendritic structural stability and overall monodispersity still need to be decisively proven for clinical use.18 Moreover, one of the main advantages DMONs possess over inorganic nanoparticles is their biocompatible nature and ability to degrade in vivo. Although beneficial, this may prove difficult to calibrate uniformly at scale if the bridge moieties, such as disulfide or tetrasulfide bonds, are degraded prematurely and release the intended payloads too early.19 This is unlikely to be problematic here due to the HM coating, though scrutiny is nevertheless warranted. An arguably more likely concern is if the particles still do not degrade fast enough or consistently, leading to bioaccumulation and long-term toxicity. Long-term in vivo studies are therefore needed to ensure the designed degradability of DMONs works as intended and any persisting fragments, such as silica, do not accumulate and cause a significant degree of chronic inflammation or renal damage. Consequently, further half-life as well as degradation component and time course studies would be highly useful.

Despite these concerns, this research nonetheless represents an innovative and forward-thinking approach to the development of a novel and much-needed H pylori vaccine platform. By leveraging the engineerability of DMON nanotechnology with native LPS and the inherent variability and complexity of OMV microbiology, Ye et al have created a synergistic nano-vaccine platform prototype showcasing a composite whole that is indeed greater than the sum of its individual parts. Although still early in development, this study provides proof of concept for using biomimetic nanoparticles to effectively combat the rising threat of multidrug-resistant pathogens. As we exit the “golden era” of traditional antibiotic drug development, novel pharmacological, biosynthetic, and bioengineering tools are of the utmost importance. In general, the ostensible safe and balanced Th1/Th2/Th17 response elicited by the LPS-loaded and HM-coated LPS@DMON@HM nanoparticles is a promising example of how nano-bioengineering methods might be integrated and used in therapeutics. And in specific, the framework provided here by Ye et al may very well provide a much-needed advancement in the pursuit of an effective H pylori vaccine, potentially overcoming the pathogen’s long-lasting hold over our best immunological efforts.
