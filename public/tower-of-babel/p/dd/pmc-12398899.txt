Alternate wetting and moderate soil drying (AWMD) enhances the physicochemical properties of rice starch by promoting grain-filling in inferior grains of rice
Authors: Tianyang Zhou, Chengcheng Ge, Yuguang Zang, Kuanyu Zhu, Weiyang Zhang, Hao Zhang, Lijun Liu, Zhiqin Wang, Junfei Gu, Jianchang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater scarcity is the major challenge to sustainable rice production. This study examines how alternate wetting and moderate soil drying (AWMD), a widely adopted water-saving practice, influences grain-filling dynamics and starch physicochemical properties in both superior grains (located on apical primary branches, flower earlier) and inferior ones (located on proximal secondary branches, flower later). Results showed that AWMD enhanced grain-filling in inferior grains: it increased the mean (Gmean) and peak (Gmax) filling rates by 26.9 % and 26.3 % respectively, while shortening the filling duration (D) by 14.1 %. These changes ultimately enhanced carbohydrate supply and starch accumulation. Biochemically, AWMD promoted a short-chain amylopectin structure, increasing amorphous polymer content, short-range disordered molecular arrangements, and reducing relative crystallinity. These structural modifications enhanced starch pyrolysis rates, viscosity, breakdown, and adhesiveness, while decreasing phase transition temperature, gelatinization enthalpy, and hardness. The study demonstrates that AWMD not only reduces irrigation demand but also improves starch quality, especially in low-quality inferior grains, through optimized grain-filling kinetics. These findings suggests that AWMD is an effective strategy for the sustainable production of high-quality rice starch for food use.Keywords: Alternate wetting and moderate soil drying; Starch structure, starch physicochemical properties; Inferior grains; Superior grains

Introduction

China is the largest producer and consumer of rice in the World, and rice is the staple food for more than 65 % of the population in China and accounts for 27 % of cropping area, contributing nearly 40 % of total calorie supply. Continuous advancements in rice production are essential for ensuring food security and improving living standards both domestically and globally (Gu et al., 2025). Water shortage is a major challenge to rice production in many regions of China (Qi et al., 2022), driven by unpredictable annual rainfall, uneven distribution during the growing season, and insufficient precipitation in key rice-producing areas. These factors not only limit water availability during critical growth stages but also increase rice vulnerability to environmental stresses (Zhang, 2007). With nearly 20 % of the global population and only 6 % of renewable freshwater resources, China faces severe water scarcity, with 1.6–2.0 million hectares of rice annually affected by seasonal droughts over the past two decades (Pan et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2019).

To address this issue, various water-saving regimes have been developed, including the aerobic rice system (Nie et al., 2012), system of rice intensification (SRI) (Thakur & Uphoff, 2017), non-flooded mulching cultivation (Zhou et al., 2023), and alternate wetting and drying (AWD) irrigation (Yang et al., 2017). While these methods significantly reduce irrigation water and improve water use efficiency (WUE), they often incur a “yield penalty,” particularly in aerobic rice and SRI systems, which struggle to maintain yields comparable to traditional flooded systems (Yang & Zhang, 2023).

Among these technologies, alternate wetting and moderate soil drying (AWMD) irrigation has emerged as one of the most widely adopted practices in China, implemented on over 12 million hectares annually (Cheng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2007). AWMD alternates between flooding and controlled soil drying during the rice growth period, with drying intervals typically lasting 3–10 days, depending on soil type and climate (Yang et al., 2017). Carefully managed drying periods—maintaining soil water potential above −15 kPa at 15–20 cm depth or midday leaf water potential around −0.80 MPa—can support plant growth without impairing photosynthesis (Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). AWMD offers significant benefits, including a 23–33 % reduction in water use, a 45–90 % decrease in greenhouse gas emissions, and a 17–40 % improvement in WUE (Li et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2023). This makes it a promising solution for balancing water conservation and rice productivity in water-scarce regions.

Although several studies have explored the mechanisms by which AWMD simultaneously increased grain yield and WUE, the underlying processes are not yet fully understood. Various ecophysiological factors may contribute to this, including changes in hormone levels, a higher percentage of productive tillers, improved canopy structure, enhanced root growth and development in deeper portions of the soil, higher root activities, and improved remobilization of carbohydrates for grain-filling (Ishfaq et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2007; Zang et al., 2024). During the soil drying phase, abscisic acid (ABA) levels increase significantly, serving as a key signaling molecule that enhances sugar signaling and facilitates the remobilization of assimilates to seeds (Yang et al., 2001; Dodd et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2017). Elevated ABA activates critical enzymes, including SPS, SuS, AGP, StS, and SBE (Yang & Zhang, 2006; Yu et al., 2024). This activation stimulates sucrose resynthesis and accelerates carbon remobilization, ensuring the continuous flow of photo assimilates to developing grains (Zang et al., 2024). Therefore, AWMD influences the grain-filling processes and starch metabolism, ultimately impacting grain quality, which may also influence starch structure and starch physicochemical properties. However, there is limited information available regarding these aspects.

Starch structure and physicochemical characteristics are crucial in determining the grain quality (Zhou et al., 2020), yet most research has focused on the impact of water stress on processing, appearance, eating quality, and nutrition (Xiong et al., 2021). Changes in components of grains, such as the contents of amylose, amylopectin, and protein, and the distribution of chain length of amylopectin, can influence the crystallinity, thereby significantly influence starch physicochemical characteristics, including its thermal and pasting properties under various water management practices (Zhou et al., 2022). Some studies reported that AWD increased milling recovery, appearance, tasting quality (Darzi-Naftchali et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2007). Xiong et al. (2021) reported that AWD increased peak viscosity and breakdown, reduced gelatinization enthalpy, starch granule size, and amylopectin short chains, which may enhance thermal stability and pasting viscosity. During grain filling, the superior spikelets flower earlier, fill more rapidly, resulting in larger grains. In contrast, inferior one flower later, fill more slowly or remain sterile due to limited source, sink size, or low enzymes activities (Wang, Xu, et al., 2012). A limited supply of carbohydrates can negatively impact grain quality (Wei et al., 2023). However, it remains unclear how AWMD influences starch structure and physicochemical properties, leading to changes in thermal and pasting characteristics, or whether its effects differ between superior and inferior grains.

Widespread adoption of AWMD in rice production contribute greatly to increasing yields, improving resource efficiency, and ensuring environmental sustainability, offering substantial benefits to both farmers' incomes and ecological health. Previous studies have shown that soil water content played a crucial role in shaping the physicochemical characteristics of starch, as well as the physiological processes involved in starch endosperm cell development (Xiong et al., 2021; Yang & Zhang, 2019). Therefore, we hypothesize that water irrigation management of AWMD affects the starch synthesis during grain-filling, which in turn alters the structure and physicochemical properties of starch. The results have the potential to significantly contribute to the production of high-quality rice starch for food use, while ensuring efficient use of water resources.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

There was a split-plot design, with the main plots for different water regimes including continuous irrigation (CI) and AWMD, and the sub-plots for two rice cultivars. The CI regime maintained continuous flooding in the plot with a water depth of 2 to 3 cm until one week before harvest. In contrast, the AWMD regime, implemented 10 days after transplanting and continued until harvest, involved withholding irrigation until tensiometer readings at a soil depth of 15–20 cm reached −15 kPa (Fig. 1). In sub-plot, two rice (Oryza sativa L.) cultivars Yongyou 2640 (Y2640) and Wuyunjing 24 (W24) were planted. These two cultivars are commonly used in rice production in this region. A shelter was constructed at the experimental site to protect against rain, and it can be closed when it is raining. In all treatments, 270 kg N ha−1 was applied with ratio of 4:2:2:2, at pre-transplanting, early tillering, panicle initiation, and initial of spikelet differentiation. Phosphorus (30 kg ha−1 as single superphosphate) and potassium (40 kg ha−1 as KCl) were incorporated into soil before transplanting as basal fertilizer. Rice seeds were pre-germinated and sown in a seedbed between May 25. Then the 25-day-old seedlings were then transplanted into a paddy field with a hill spacing of 0.107 m × 0.13 m, with two seedlings per hill for two rice cultivars. The control of diseases, pests, and weeds were carried out following local high-yield practices. Plants were harvested on 20 October. Grain yield and yield components were determined from a harvest area of 6.0 m2 in each plot (excluding the border ones) and adjusted to 14 % moisture. For the physicochemical measurement, 20 panicles were sampled from each replicate and divided into superior and inferior grains for following measurements. Superior grains were considered to be the grains on the top three primary branches on the upper part of the panicle, and inferior grains were the grains on the three bottom second branches in the lower part. The harvested grains were naturally sun-dried and then put into sealed plastic bags. The grains were then stored for 2 months at 5 °C and 50 humidity in a dark environment before the measurement of rice and starch quality.

Isolation of starch, and the morphology and size of starch granules

The protein was removed from starch following the methods outlined in our previous study (Zhou et al., 2020). For observing amyloplast, the superior and inferior grains were snapped in half transversely using two pairs of forceps. Halved grains were mounted on aluminum stubs with the fractures facing up. The starch samples were uniformly dispersed into anhydrous ethanol. Ethanol solution (2 μL) was dropped into an aluminum foil film and dried at 40 °C for 4 h. After that, all grain and starch samples were coated with gold, and imaging via scanning electron microscopy (GeminiSEM 300, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Particle size distribution (0.1 to 2000 μm) was measured using a laser diffraction analyzer (Master 2000, Malvern, England).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

XRD and FTIR measurements and calculations followed the method of Dankar et al. (2018). XRD profiles of starch were obtained using a D8 advanced X-ray diffractometer (Bruker-AXS, Germany) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The 2-θ range was 5° to 40°, scanned at 0.02°/s. The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2-θ range of 5° to 40° with a scanning speed of 0.02°/s. FTIR spectra of starch samples were obtained using a 670-IR + 610-IR spectrophotometer (Varian, USA), covering a range of 400–4000 cm−1.

Starch thermogravimetric analysis

The thermogravimetric analysis of rice starch was used Pyris 1 TGA (Perkin Elmer, USA) according to the method of Tian et al. (2011). The samples were analyzed at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, over a temperature range of 30 °C to 800 °C with Al2O3 as the reference material under N2.

Starch solid-state NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) spectra

Following the method of Jing et al. (2021), high-resolution solid-state 13C CP/MAS NMR of starch was performed at B0 = 9.4 T using a Bruker AVANCE III 400 WB spectrometer (Bruker Company, Germany).

Determination of pasting properties and thermal properties

According to the methods of Zhou et al. (2020), the viscosity properties of starch was measured by rapid viscosity analyzer (Model 3D, Newport Scientific, Australia) while the thermal properties of starch was performed by DSC8500 (Perkin Elmer, USA).

Fluorophore-assisted carbohydrate electrophoresis (FACE)

Following the methodology Zhou et al. (2022), starch samples were debranched using isoamylase and subsequently labeled with 8-aminopyrene-1, 3, 6-trisulfonic acid as the fluorophore. The pretreated samples were analyzed for amylopectin chain length distributions (CLDs) using the PA-800 Plus FACE System (Beckman-Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), which was equipped with a solid-state laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) detector and an argon-ion laser as the excitation source.

The contents of protein, starch, and apparent amylose contents

The rice flour samples were dried at 40 °C to a constant weight, and their nitrogen content was determined using the Kjeldahl method. Protein content was calculated by multiplying the nitrogen content by a conversion factor of 5.95 (Wang et al., 2024). The starch content was read by a colorimetric method with the anthrone reagent at 620 nm. The apparent amylose content (AAC) was measured using the iodine-binding colorimetric method, based on the absorbance ratio at 680–520 nm (Nithya et al., 2024). The content of amylopectin was the difference between starch content and apparent amylose content.

Typical texture profile analysis (TPA)

The TPA of cooked rice was analyzed using bench-top TA-XT2 Texture Analyzers (Texture Technologies Corp, Scarsdale, NY), following the method described in our previous report (Zhou et al., 2020).

Swelling power and solubility of starch

The swelling power and solubility of the starch were determined following the procedure outlined by Colussi et al. (2020). A 1.0 g sample was homogenized with 50 mL ultrapure water in a centrifuge tube. The mixture was heated at 90 °C for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and then centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 20 min. The supernatant was dried in a 110 °C oven to constant weight to measure the soluble fraction. The solubility and swelling power were expressed as the percentage of the dried soluble fraction and sediment.

Statistical analysis

In all the figures and tables, the values were the averages from triplicate. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine statistically significant differences. Multiple comparison tests were performed using Tukey's test at a significance level of P < 0.05 in SPSS 16.0. To analyze the differences between various irrigation regimes and grain positions, the ‘gmodels’ package in R 4.0.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/gmodels/index.html) was utilized for principal component analysis. Structural equation modeling was conducted using the ‘lavaan’ package in R version 4.0.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/lavaan/index.html). The findings were subsequently visualized using the ‘ggplot2’ package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/ggplot2/index.html).

Results and discussion

Yield, grain size, and their relationships with grain filling process

The AWMD treatment increased yield in rice cultivars Y2640 and W24, by 16.3 % and 11.0 % (data not shown), respectively, due to a significant improvement in grain-filling parameters of Gmax and Gmean. Compared to inferior grains, superior grains had significantly greater length, width, and 1000-grain weight. However, the length-to-width ratio followed the opposite trend, with both cultivars showing the same pattern (Table 1). Compared with CI, AWMD increased the sizes of superior grains (e.g., grain length and grain width), although these differences were not statistically significant (Table 1). In contrast, the improvements in the sizes of inferior grains were statistically significant. For example, the length of inferior grains in Y2640 and W24 increased by 2.25 % and 2.59 %, respectively, and the grain width increased by 3.33 % and 3.24 %, respectively. AWMD could greatly promote whole plant senescence and enhances the translocation of stored carbon and nitrogen from vegetative tissues, such as leaves and stems, to the developing grains (Yang & Zhang, 2010). In our study, this mechanism could explain the observed increase in grain size, especially for inferior ones.

We also studied the grain filling process in both genotypes (Fig. 2). Grain-filling started earlier, and Gmean and Gmax was higher in superior grains compared to inferior ones in both Y2640 and W24. However, the value of D was shorter in superior grains than in inferior ones. For example, under the CI treatment, the values of D of superior and inferior grains in Y2640 was 16.56 and 27.33 days, respectively, while the Gmean was 1.50 and 0.71, and Gmax was 2.42 and 1.12, respectively. The adoption of AWMD significantly decreased D and increased both Gmean and Gmax, especially for inferior grains. For instance, compared to CI, AWMD decreased D by 4.63 %, increased Gmean by 2.88 %, and increased Gmax by 1.93 % for superior grains of Y2640. Compared to CI, AWMD decreased D by 14.11 %, increased Gmean by 26.89 %, and increased Gmax by 26.27 % for inferior grains of Y2640 (Fig. 2 E-G). The similar results were observed for genotype W24. The superior spikelets demonstrated a competitive advantage in carbohydrate allocation over the inferior ones (Yang & Zhang, 2006; Zhang et al., 2021). Studies have shown that AWMD can increase non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) accumulation in the vegetative organs before flowering, enhance NSC remobilization for grain-filling, particularly in later flowered inferior spikelets (Zang et al., 2024). This is why the grain sizes of inferior grains under AWMD were larger.

The chalkiness in superior and inferior grains

The reduced starch accumulation in the endosperm leaded to a loosening of amyloplasts, resulting in larger intercellular spaces between them, which was the primary factor contributing to chalkiness (Deng et al., 2021). The percentage of chalky kernels was lower in superior grains, and AWMD significantly improved rice appearance quality by reducing chalky kernels (Table 1). The formation of chalkiness is closely linked to starch synthesis (Ashida et al., 2009). In the study of Zhou et al. (2020), six levels of N fertilizer were applied, varied from 0 to 500 kg N ha−1, the results indicated that excessively N application would result in poor grain filling and loose arrangement of starch granules. In this study, AWMD promoted grain-filling, particularly by significantly increasing Gmean and Gmax and shortening D value of inferior grains. This resulted in reduced chalkiness and a significant improvement in the appearance quality (Table 1 and Fig. 2 E-G).

Overall, AWMD reduced chalkiness in inferior grains by promoting faster and more complete grain filling, which optimized amyloplast arrangement and minimized intercellular gaps, thereby improved rice appearance quality.

Apparent amylose content (AAC), protein content and gel consistency

Protein content of inferior grains was significantly higher compared to superior grains, whereas the contents of ACC, amylopectin, and starch exhibited the opposite trend. Both cultivars exhibited the same pattern (Table 2). Compared to CI, AWMD increased starch and amylopectin content, as well as ACC, particularly in inferior grains (Table 2). Zang et al. (2024) reported that AWMD could enhance the amylase activities (e.g., α-amylase and β-amylase) in stems during grain filling, thereby promoting the translocation of non-structural carbohydrates (NSCs) to grains. This mechanism likely explains the increased starch content observed in grains under AWMD treatment in this study (Fig. 3).

Gel consistency was strongly correlated with amylose content, starch content, and protein content. High ACC and protein content tend to increase rice hardness, reducing gel consistency and glossiness (Zhou et al., 2022). The gel consistency of superior grains was significantly higher than that of inferior grains, with both cultivars exhibiting a similar trend. Compared to CI, the AWMD treatment led to a decrease in protein content and an increase in starch and amylopectin contents, which improved gel consistency and enhanced cooking and eating quality, particularly in inferior grains (Table 2).

In conclusion, AWMD increased starch and amylopectin contents while decreased protein content in inferior grains, which improved gel consistency and enhanced cooking and eating quality, with more notable effects in inferior grains compared to superior grains.

The morphology of starch granules

The structure of endosperm amyloplasts, including their size and arrangement, is closely linked to grain filling. Superior grains, which flower early, typically have high filling rates and compactly arranged amyloplasts. The amyloplasts in superior grains were larger and tightly packed in the endosperm, compared with inferior grains (Fig. 3 A-D). There was minimal variation in the size of the amyloplast particles, with few remnants of endosperm cells and narrow gaps between the amyloplasts in superior grains. AWMD treatment enhanced the tight arrangement, reduced gaps, and increased the plumpness of amyloplasts, especially in inferior grains. The denser packing of amyloplasts minimizes chalkiness caused by excessive voids and light refraction in the endosperm (Sawada et al., 2016). This likely explains why the inferior grains treated with AWMD in this study showed much less chalky kernel and better appearance quality.

Both superior and inferior starch granules under CI and AWMD treatment displayed irregular polygonal shapes, with the surface of superior grain starch granules being smoother (Fig. 3 E-H). By analyzing the diameter of starch granules, a bimodal distribution was observed. Superior grains under AWMD treatment had the highest proportion of large-size starch granules, whereas inferior grains under CI treatment had the lowest (Fig. 3 I and J). AWMD promoted grain filling and starch synthesis, reduces the accumulation of small and medium starch granules, and resulted in smoother granules (Xiong et al., 2021). Compared to CI treatment, the average starch granule diameter of superior grains in Y2640 and W24 increased by 2.27 % and 2.96 %, respectively, while for inferior grains, the increase was 4.53 % and 7.57 %, respectively, under AWMD treatment.

To summarize, AWMD promoted plumper, more tightly packed starch granules by enhancing grain filling, reducing small granules and intergranular gaps, and improving starch granule morphology, especially in inferior grains, which contributed to better grain quality.

The chain length of amylopectin

Amylopectin is the major component of starch and is a highly branched polysaccharide. In Fig. 4 A-D, the amylopectin chain length distribution reached its peak at a degree of polymerization (DP) of 12, regardless of the grain position or treatments. Based on DP values, the starch chains can be categorized into ‘A' (DP 6–12), ‘B1’ (DP 13–25), ‘B2’ (DP 26–37), and ‘B3’ (DP >37) chains (Zhou et al., 2020). The different chain lengths and their interactions are crucial for the formation of crystalline and amorphous regions of starch, which in turn affect the starch's properties, such as its gelatinization temperature, solubility, and retrogradation. Compared to superior grains, inferior grains had significantly lower levels of ‘A' and ‘B1’ chains, and higher levels of ‘B2’ and ‘B3’ chains. AWMD treatment increased the percentage of short-chain, especially for ‘A' chains, but decreased the percentage of long-chain, particularly in inferior grains, when compared to CI treatment (Fig. 4 E-H). Wei et al. reported that by regulating the source strength and sink size, adequate supply of carbohydrates supply for grain-filling would result in higher expression of genes associated with short-length amylopectin, such as SSΙΙa, SSΙΙΙa and BEΙb (Wei et al., 2023). Similarly, in this study, AWMD was found to promote the translocation of NSCs to support grain-filling, thereby providing a higher carbohydrate supply for grain development, particularly in inferior grains. The AWMD treatment significantly enhanced the expression of genes encoding short-chain enzymes. This upregulation promoted greater accumulation of short-length amylopectin during grain filling (Patindol et al., 2015; Xiong et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2016). The ‘A' and ‘B1’ chains contribute to the development of a starch structure that is highly branched and exhibits lower crystallinity, contributing to better digestibility and a smoother texture. In contrast, the ‘B2’ and ‘B3’ chains are less soluble, more crystalline, and contribute to starch's resistance to digestion, which can influence the firmness and texture of rice after cooking. Long chains (‘B2’ and ‘B3’) are often linked to reduced eating quality and lower digestibility (Cameron & Wang, 2005; Li & Gilbert, 2018).

In short, AWMD increased the proportion of short amylopectin chains (A and B1) and decreased long chains (B2 and B3) in inferior grains, which would favor the formation of a highly branched, low-crystallinity starch structure and improve digestibility and texture.

Starch crystallinity and molecular structure

Both superior and inferior grains under CI or AWMD treatments exhibited an A-type diffraction pattern in the XRD analysis (Fig. 5 A-B). Compared to CI, the relative crystallinity of grain starch was significantly reduced under AWMD treatment, with a more pronounced decrease observed in inferior grain starch (Fig. 5 E). Specific absorption peaks in the FTIR spectrum correspond to the different crystalline and amorphous regions of starch (Fig. 5 C—D). The ratios of the bands at 1022:995 cm−1 and 1045:1022 cm−1 are used to quantify the content of short-range disordered and ordered molecular structures, respectively (Capron et al., 2007; Warren et al., 2016). The IR ratio at 1022:995 cm−1 was significantly higher for inferior grain, but the IR ratio at 1045:1022 cm−1 was higher for superior ones. Compared to CI, AWMD reduced the IR value at 1045:1022 cm−1 and increased the ratio at 1022:995 cm−1 for both superior and inferior grains, with a greater variation observed in inferior grains (Fig. 5 F and G).

The stability of the crystalline structure is influenced by the DP values of amylopectin external branch chains. The content of short-chain amylopectin negatively correlates with crystal stability, while long-chain amylopectin (‘B2’ and ‘B3’ chains) positively correlates with it (Li & Gilbert, 2018; Zhou et al., 2020), consistent with our findings (Fig. 4 E-H, Fig. 5 E-G, Fig. 8 A). The crystalline fraction in rice starch suppresses starch swelling and pasting, and AWMD treatment reduced both crystallinity and the 1045:1022 cm−1 ratio, enhancing gelatinization and stickiness properties.

In inferior grains, AWMD maintains tightly packed amyloplasts (reducing chalkiness) while reducing crystallinity via increased short-chain amylopectin (DP 6–12), which disrupts ordered crystalline structures, enhancing digestibility and adhesiveness. AWMD has similar effects on high-quality grains, yet these effects are less pronounced. Improves amyloplast packing (by accelerating grain-filling rates and shortening duration) and simultaneously lowers their inherent high crystallinity (linked to long-chain amylopectin) by promoting short-chain synthesis, thereby bridging structural and functional gaps between grain types. This optimization enhances grain translucency (macroscale) and starch functionality (molecular scale), such as lower gelatinization temperatures and higher viscosity, demonstrating the capacity AWMD for holistic quality improvement in water-saving rice production.

Overall, AWMD reduced starch crystallinity and enhanced short-range molecular disorder by increasing short amylopectin chains particularly in inferior grains. This, in turn, improved starch gelatinization and stickiness, and narrowed the quality gap between inferior and superior grains.

Solid-state NMR spectra of starch

In the spectrum, each carbon atom of the glycosyl unit within starch is assigned a unique peak (Fig. 6 A and B). For A-type starches, the peak of carbon atom C-1 with chemical shift around 100–103 ppm is a triplet, the weak peak around 80–82 ppm was associated with C-4, the large signal of 68–78 ppm was correlated with the C-2, C-3, and C-5 sites, the resonances value of 61–62 ppm represented C-6 (Warren et al., 2016; Zhu, 2017). The NMR spectra of superior and inferior grain starch under CI and AWMD treatments exhibited similar polymorphic patterns (Fig. 6 A and B). Solid-state NMR provides insights into starch molecular order and crystallinity. It can distinguish between amorphous and crystalline regions by analyzing chemical shifts and relaxation times (Zhu, 2017). NMR complements XRD by avoiding interference from lipids or proteins (Zhu, 2017). Compared to superior grain starch, inferior grain starch showed lower amorphous content and higher single and double helices, indicating greater starch crystal stability and the results were consistent with the results of starch crystallinity (Fig. 5 E-G, Fig. 6 C-E). Under AWMD treatment, both cultivars exhibited a decrease in ordered helical structure and an increase in amorphous content in grain starch. The higher amorphous content in inferior grain starch under AWMD treatment compared to CI can be attributed to the altered amylopectin chain length distribution. Specifically, AWMD increased the proportion of short amylopectin chains (A and B1; DP 6–25) while reducing long chains (B2 and B3; DP ≥26) in inferior grains (Fig. 4). Short chains are less capable of forming ordered double helices and crystalline lamellae, leading to a more disordered amorphous structure. In contrast, long chains stabilize crystalline regions by forming tightly packed double helices. By promoting grain-filling rates (Gmean and Gmax) and carbohydrate supply, AWMD likely enhanced the activity of starch synthases (e.g., SSIIa, SSIIIa) and branching enzymes (e.g., BEI) in inferior grains, favoring the synthesis of shorter amylopectin branches. This shift disrupted the crystalline-amorphous balance, reducing relative crystallinity (Fig. 5) and increasing amorphous content (Fig. 6), which aligns with the observed lower gelatinization enthalpy and thermal stability (Table 3). It has been reported that shorter amylopectin chains form smaller crystals, leading to a reduced ordered crystal structure (Zhou et al., 2022).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

There was a two-stage weight loss in TGA (Fig. 7 A and B). The first stage, occurring between approximately 30 °C and 200 °C, was attributed to moisture evaporation (around 8 %). The second stage, spanning from 200 °C to 800 °C, corresponded to the depolymerization and pyrolytic decomposition of the polysaccharide backbone (Zhu et al., 2013). The pyrolysis rate indicated that the inferior grain starch exhibited a significantly lower weight loss compared to the superior grain starch (Fig. 7 E). However, AWMD increased the mass loss, especially for inferior grain starch. The derivative thermogravimetric curves indicated that superior grain starch had a higher rate of mass loss, with AWMD treatment further accelerating this rate. (Fig. 7 C and D). This indicates that breaking down the granules of inferior grain starch demands more energy, while the AWMD treatment decreases this energy requirement. The increase in the amorphous region facilitates the thermal decomposition of starch granules. When the starch has a higher amorphous content and lower crystallinity, its thermal stability is at its lowest (Wang et al., 2018). In TGA, higher crystallinity slows down mass loss. This is because the ordered structures in the starch are more resilient to thermal breakdown (Wang, Xie, et al., 2012).

AWMD preferentially influences inferior grains, due to their inherent limitations in carbohydrate partitioning and starch metabolism. By promoting short-chain amylopectin synthesis, it reduces crystallinity and enhances amorphous content, thereby improving their functionality to align closer to superior grains. In contrast, superior grains—already optimized under CI—show structural and functional inertia to AWMD. This differential response highlights AWMD's potential to homogenize grain quality while conserving water, particularly benefiting inferior grains that traditionally contribute to post-harvest quality loss.

To summarize, AWMD accelerated starch pyrolysis rates by increasing amorphous content and reducing crystallinity especially in inferior grains. This made starch more readily decomposable and enhanced its thermal processing characteristics.

Starch swelling power and solubility

The starch swelling power and solubility of inferior grains were much lower in both cultivars (Table 2). These properties depend on crystallinity and molecular structure. Highly crystalline starches exhibit lower swelling due to strong hydrogen bonding, while amorphous regions enhance water absorption (Zhang et al., 2025). The reduction in swelling power and solubility of inferior grain starch is due to increased crystallinity, which limits hydration, strengthens interactions between starch molecules, and makes it more difficult for amylose to dissociate and diffuse during swelling (Da Rosa Zavareze & Dias, 2011; Zhu, 2018). AWMD treatment significantly increased the swelling power and solubility of starch in both superior and inferior grains, with a more pronounced effect on inferior grain starch. This enhancement was attributed to an increase in the short chains of amylopectin, which led to a more disordered arrangement of double helices within the granules and a notable decrease in crystallinity (Fig. 4 E-H, Fig. 5 E-G). These changes reduced the interactions between starch functional groups, promoting the formation of more disordered amylose–lipid complexes within the granules (Li & Zhu, 2017; Zhu, 2018).

In short, AWMD enhanced starch swelling power and solubility, especially in inferior grains by reducing crystallinity and increasing short amylopectin chains. This, in turn, facilitated water absorption and enhanced starch functionality during processing.

Typical texture profile analysis (TPA)

Hardness and adhesiveness are crucial texture characteristics that determine rice palatability. Adhesiveness is defined as the energy required to break the sticky forces, and a larger absolute value indicates greater adhesiveness (Leonard et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). The absolute value of adhesiveness ranged from 600 to 900 g. Compared to superior grains, the absolute value of adhesiveness of inferior grains decreased by 17.9 % for Y2640 and 16.4 % for W24 (Table 2). Compared to the CI treatment, the absolute value of adhesiveness of superior grains increased by 5.2 % and 3.7 % for Y2640 and W24, respectively, while the absolute value of adhesiveness of inferior grains increased by 11.6 % and 10.7 % under AWMD treatment. The hardness quantifies the force required to compress the rice grains (Zhou et al., 2020). In contrast to the results of adhesiveness, the hardness of inferior grains significantly increased compared to superior grains. Compared with CI, AWMD decreased the hardness of superior grains by 2.72 % and 3.01 % for Y2640 and W24, respectively, while decreased the hardness of inferior grains by 4.48 % and 3.16 % (Table 2). The leaching of amylopectin, the content of short amylopectin chains, and the molecular size of the leached amylopectin influence rice adhesiveness and hardness (Li et al., 2020).

Overall, AWMD reduced hardness and increased adhesiveness in cooked rice by modifying amylopectin chain length distribution particularly in inferior grains, thereby improving palatability.

Pasting and thermal properties of starch

Superior grain starch exhibited significantly higher peak, hot, and final viscosity, and breakdown value compared to inferior grain starch. In contrast, inferior grain starch had lower setback and pasting temperature values (Table 3). While AWMD improves cooking quality in both superior and inferior grains. Superior grains inherently have higher viscosity and breakdown values compared to inferior grains under both CI and AWMD (e.g., Y2640 superior peak viscosity = 3138.5–3247.0 cP vs. inferior = 2638.0–2727.0 cP). This is due to superior grains' earlier flowering, faster grain-filling, and more compact starch granule packing (Fig. 3). However, the relative improvement from AWMD is meaningful for both, especially for inferior grains, which start from lower quality baselines (e.g., higher chalkiness, lower starch content, Table 1, Table 2). The higher starch viscosities and breakdown observed in superior grains under AWMD treatment may be attributed to their larger starch granule size, more disordered structure, and increased content of short-chain amylopectin (Kim et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2013). Like superior grains, inferior grains also show increased starch viscosities and breakdown under AWMD treatment due to changes in starch granule size, structure, and short - chain amylopectin content. These changes are beneficial for improving the cooking and eating quality of inferior grains, which is important for overall rice quality improvement.

The higher gelatinization temperature of starch indicates an increased energy requirement for the onset of gelatinization (Fitzgerald et al., 2009). This temperature is mainly determined by the contents of amylose and the structure of amylopectin. The presence of amylose and long-chain amylopectin limit starch swelling and gelatinization, leading to an increased gelatinization temperature (Zhang et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2020). This conclusion is consistent with our findings, as a higher ratio of long branch chains was observed in inferior grain under CI treatment (Table 3). Consequently, the To, Tp, and Tc were also highest in inferior grains under CI treatment. The increased ratio of short amylopectin chains under AWMD treatment (Fig. 4) played a crucial role in starch gelatinization. This increase disrupted the double helix, decreased crystallinity, and promoted water absorption and interaction within the amorphous regions, resulting in lower gelatinization temperatures and enthalpies.

In conclusion, AWMD enhanced starch pasting properties—marked by higher viscosity and breakdown—and lowered starch gelatinization temperatures and enthalpies (with a more pronounced effect in inferior grains). These changes, driven by an increase in short amylopectin chains and a reduction in crystallinity, collectively improved cooking quality.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and structural equation modeling of starch structure and physicochemical properties

Previous studies (Kong et al., 2015; Zhu, 2018) have shown that the branch chain length of amylopectin is crucial in determining the overall texture and cooking characteristics, significantly influencing its stickiness and firmness. In this study, AWMD treatment increased the percentage of short-chain amylopectin (such as ‘A' chains with DP 6–12 and ‘B1’ chains with DP 13–25) in both superior and inferior grains, especially in the latter. Short-chain amylopectin contributes to a more highly branched starch structure. This structure has a lower crystallinity, which allows for better water absorption during cooking. As a result, it enhances the stickiness of the cooked rice. For example, the increase in short-chain amylopectin in inferior grains under AWMD treatment led to an increase in adhesiveness, as shown by the TPA analysis. The more disordered structure formed by short-chain amylopectin also promotes higher starch viscosities and breakdown values during cooking, improving the overall cooking quality. Conversely, long-chain amylopectin (like ‘B2’ with DP 26–37 and ‘B3’ with DP >37) is less soluble and more crystalline. It can make the rice firmer after cooking and may lead to lower viscosities and breakdown values. The negative correlation between crystallinity and breakdown, short-chain content, peak, and final viscosity shown in PCA implies that as the crystallinity of starch decreases (which occurs with an increase in short-chain amylopectin under AWMD treatment), the cooking quality of rice improves in terms of viscosity-related parameters (Fig. 8 A and B). A higher ratio of short-chain amylopectin leads to reduced crystallinity and a more disordered structure, which in turn improves the tasting quality. Gmean and Gmax were positively correlated with short chain contents and negatively correlated with long chain contents. In contrast, D was negatively correlated with short chain contents and positively correlated with long chain contents. This suggests that AWMD enhances grain filling, modifies the length of amylopectin chain, thereby improves starch quality.

The structural equation model indicated that AWMD significantly influenced the process of grain-filling, especially inferior grains. Starch physicochemical properties were primarily determined by the ‘A' chain and ‘B3’ chain contents and were predominantly influenced by grain-filling parameters (i.e., Gmean and Gmax and D) (Fig. 8 C and D). The adoption of AWMD accelerate the remobilization of NSCs, and increased the Gmax and Gmean and decreased D, which promoted grain filling and increased the accumulation of short chain, but decreased the accumulation of long chain of amylopectin. Lower ‘B3’ chain and higher ‘A' chain contents were beneficial to improve the physiochemical properties of starch, such as reducing relative crystallinity, reducing ∆Hgel and increasing adhesiveness. Notably, AWMD had a more pronounced effect on the starch of inferior grains compared to that of superior grains. The positive effect of AWMD on Gmax and Gmean (r = 0.61) in inferior grains was significantly larger than its impact on superior ones (r = 0.14). The negative effect of AWMD on D (r = −0.91) in inferior grains was much stronger than the decrease observed in superior grains (r = −0.15).

Conclusion

Two high-yielding rice varieties were cultivated under two different water management practices. The results showed that AWMD treatment significantly improved the physicochemical properties of rice starch, particularly in inferior grains. Under AWMD, amylopectin showed a higher proportion of short branch chains and a lower proportion of long branch chains, accompanied by reduced relative crystallinity, a lower degree of single and double helix formation, and an increased amorphous structure in the outer regions of the starch granules. Additionally, the starch pyrolysis rate increased, while the phase transition temperature decreased significantly. Pasting properties were also affected, with increased peak and hot viscosity, and the value of breakdown, while setback and pasting temperature decreased. The decrease was observed in gelatinization temperature, gelatinization enthalpy, retrogradation enthalpy, and retrogradation percentage, whereas there was an increase in adhesiveness. The adequate carbohydrate supply and faster grain-filling rate, which is modeling the grain-filling dynamics, influenced starch synthesis and resulted in enhanced structure and physicochemical properties of rice starch. This makes AWMD a valuable approach for modifying rice starch for various food and industrial applications.
