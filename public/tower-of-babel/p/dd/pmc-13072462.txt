Analysis of factors influencing patient delay in tuberculosis patients in Northeast China: surveillance data from 2015 to 2024
Authors: Yuchen Gao, Jingxiao Li, Jiying Ma, Zihui Yu, Xiaohui Ren, Siwei Chao, Nan Wang, Jie Liu, Yu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a serious challenge to China’s public health, and timely detection and diagnosis of play key roles in preventing disease spread and deterioration. The aim of this study was to understand the delay in consultation for TB patients in Shenyang City, Liaoning Province, and to determine the factors that influence this delay.MethodsDelay in consultation was analysed retrospectively in Shenyang City, Liaoning Province. Basic information on TB patients from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024, was extracted from the China Tuberculosis Information Management System (TBIMS). The median and interquartile spacing were used for statistical description, and the chi-square test and binary logistic regression model were used to analyse the influencing factors.ResultsA total of 38,928 TB patients were included. The median duration of delay in consultation was 18 (6,36) days, and 22,051 (56.65%) patients experienced a delay in consultation, with an increasing trend in the rate of delay in consultation in Shenyang City from 2015 to 2024 (χ2 = 2235.857, P < 0.001). The study revealed that women (OR = 1.185, 95% CI: 1.130 − 1.243), patients aged ≥ 60 years old (OR = 1.218, 95% CI: 1.018 ~ 1.458), patients from ethnic minorities (OR = 1.409, 95% CI: 1.252 − 1.585), and patients who were positive for pathology (OR = 1.296, 95% CI: 1.194 − 1.407) had a greater risk of delay. Among the population categories, farmers had a greater risk of delayed visits. Intracity mobility (OR = 0.573, 95% CI: 0.540 − 0.608) and patient origin as a Physical examination (OR = 0.468, 95% CI: 0.276 − 0.794) were protective factors for visit delay.ConclusionThe rate of delayed visits for TB in Shenyang City from 2015 to 2024 generally increased, and delayed visits were more common. Intervention strategies should be implemented for risk factors and key populations to reduce patients’ suffering and economic losses.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12838-1.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis that is transmitted through the respiratory tract. The disease is predominantly a pulmonary infection, but it can also involve multiple organ systems, such as the skin, joints and lymph nodes [1]. Despite advances in antituberculosis drug development and the implementation of prevention and control strategies, TB is a major global public health challenge that remains endemic worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the incidence and mortality rates remain persistently high. According to the latest statistical data released by the World Health Organization (WHO), there were approximately 10.8 million new TB cases globally in 2023, resulting in an estimated 1.25 million deaths. TB has surpassed COVID-19 to once again become the leading cause of death from a single infectious disease and poses a severe threat to human health [2]. China is one of the 30 countries globally with a high burden of tuberculosis. In 2023, China reported 741,000 new TB cases, accounting for 6.8% of the global total, with 36,000 deaths attributed to the disease that year. The burden of disease in China is the third highest in the world, lower only than India and Indonesia, and prevention and control are critical [3].

The high transmissibility and insidious course of TB make its prevention and control difficult, and timely diagnosis is crucial in its prevention and control. In the early stage of the disease, the symptoms do not prevent people from performing daily activities, and they may persist in a mild form for several months. Some patients with tuberculosis may not even have symptoms, so it is easy for patients to unknowingly transmit the disease to other people [4, 5]. When TB patients experience delays in seeking treatment, this not only increases the risk of infection in the population and affects TB control but may also lead to further deterioration of the patient’s condition, posing a serious threat to the patient’s health and public health safety [6]. The WHO’s “end TB” strategy clearly states that the chain of transmission should be interrupted through the triple prevention and control model of “early diagnosis—standardized treatment—contact screening”. Therefore, early and accurate detection of cases, prompt initiation of treatment and adherence to effective treatment are essential for preventing the spread of tuberculosis.

While China has made significant progress in tuberculosis prevention and control by implementing the National TB Prevention and Control Plan and improving patient management protocols, according to the Fifth National Epidemiologic Survey, 53.2% of TB patients who present with early symptoms still fail to seek timely medical attention, and the rate of delay in seeking medical attention is high [7]. A study in Lishui City, Zhejiang Province, reported that delays in consultation occurred in 2,268 patients from 2011 − 2021 with a delay rate of 71.10%, with a median of 36 days delayed for tuberculosis patients [8]. Another study reported a correlation between delay in consultation and factors such as age, area of residence, and occupation, suggesting that socioeconomic factors and access to health services are key factors in TB prevention and control [9].

As a core city of the old industrial base in northeastern China, Shenyang has a high population density and frequent mobility. It is therefore valuable to explore the basic epidemiological characteristics of TB in Shenyang City as well as patients’ delay in consultation and its causes to achieve the goal of ending tuberculosis. In this study, we collected data from the Shenyang TB registry to analyse the current status of TB patients’ delay in consultation and the factors that influenced it from 2015 to 2024. This study provides data to support improvements in patient detection strategies and the optimization of TB prevention and control strategies.

Methods

Geographic information

Shenyang is one of 14 prefecture-level cities under the jurisdiction of Liaoning Province.Shenyang, it is located in southern Northeast China and the central part of the province. It lies between 41°11’–43°02’ N latitude and 122°25’–123°48’ E longitude. The city spans a total administrative area of 12,948 square kilometers and administers 13 districts, counties, and county-level cities, including Heping, Shenhe, Dadong, Huanggu, Tiexi, Sujiatun, Hunnan, Shenbei New District, Yu Hong, Liaozhong, Xinmin, Kangping, and Faku. According to the Shenyang Statistical Yearbook 2024, Shenyang has a permanent resident population of 9.204 million. Benefiting from its strategic position at the core of Northeast China and the Liaozhong Urban Agglomeration, Shenyang not only serves as a central city in the northeastern region but also holds a pivotal role in the national geographical and economic landscape as the heart of the Northeast Economic Circle. It functions as a key hub driving the development of the entire Northeast region.

Data sources

Data on all TB cases reported in Shenyang City, Liaoning Province, from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024, were collected using the Chinese Tuberculosis Information Management System (TBIMS), and delays in TB patient visits were retrospectively analysed.

Data collection

The data for this study were provided by the Shenyang City Centre for Disease Control and Prevention. The data included general characteristics of TB patients, such as age, sex, ethnicity, population classification, type of current address, type of household address, source of the patient, time of onset of current symptoms, time of first consultation, and diagnosis. Most information was collected and recorded by TB health care workers during TB counselling at the time of registration(supplementary file).

Operational definitions

Time of Medical Consultation: The time interval (day) between the onset of the patient’s main symptoms of TB and the first visit to a comprehensive healthcare facility [10].

Patient delay: Based on the WHO recommendation that patients with a cough lasting more than 14 days should seek medical consultation for tuberculosis screening, we defined a patient delay as an interval exceeding 14 days (2 weeks) between the initial onset of tuberculosis-related clinical symptoms and the first medical consultation [11].

Data analysis

This study is a retrospective cohort study employing descriptive epidemiological methods (cross-sectional survey) to analyze the demographic characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), whereas categorical variables are expressed as ratios and proportions. This study employed a binary logistic regression model because the dependent variable “delay in seeking medical care” is a dichotomous variable (yes/no). This model effectively analyzes the strength of association between various factors and this binary outcome. Specifically, univariate analysis was employed for preliminary screening of variables associated with delayed medical care, while multivariate logistic regression identified independent risk factors and protective factors influencing delayed medical care after controlling for other confounding variables. This stepwise analytical approach ensured the scientific rigor and reliability of the research findings. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 software. The chi-square test and multivariate logistic regression model were used to study the relationships between influencing factors and delay in consultation in TB patients, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

There were 38,928 cases of tuberculosis diagnosed in Shenyang City from 2015 − 2024. These patients had a male-to-female sex ratio of 2.45:1 and were predominantly Han Chinese (96.51%). The median age of the patients was 52 (35, 63) years, with 42.9% of patients in the 35- to 59-year-old age group and 32.8% in the > 60-year-old age group. The population classification was dominated by domestic and inactive workers (73.53%), followed by farmers (14.26%). The type of household address was mainly local (60.73%), the diagnostic results were mostly indicative of positive pathology (53.10%), the treatment was categorized mainly by primary treatment (87.26%), most patients were referrals (42.81%), and the first diagnosis commonly occurred in the city (99.0%) (Tables 1 and 2).

Univariate results

The median visit time was 18 (6, 36) days, and 22,051 (56.65%) patients experienced delays in visits. The univariate analysis of delays in visits for TB patients in Shenyang from 2015 to 2024 revealed that gender, age, ethnicity, population categorization, type of current address, type of household address, patient origin and diagnosis showed differences in the rate of delay in consultation that were statistically significant (P < 0.05). In contrast, the difference in the rate of delay in consultation was not statistically significant for treatment classification or the area of the first diagnosis. The delayed visit rates were higher for females, patients ≥ 60 years old, ethnic minorities, farmers, current address type in the county, cases that were pathogenically positive, and patient source from direct access and referral (Table 2).

Multifactorial results

Using delay in consultation with TB patients as the dependent variable (nondelay = 0, delay = 1), independent variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the binary logistic regression model analysis. The results revealed that gender, age, ethnicity, population group classification, type of current address, type of civil address, patient origin and diagnosis were statistically associated with delays in consultation for TB patients in Shenyang City. Compared with male patients, female patients had a greater risk of delayed consultation (OR = 1.185, 95% CI: 1.130 − 1.243). With respect to age, patients ≥ 60 years old had a slightly greater risk of delayed visits than patients ≤ 17 years old (OR = 1.219, 95% CI: 1.253 − 1.583); the difference between the other age groups and the baseline group was not statistically significant. The risk of delay in consultation was significantly greater in ethnic minority patients than in Han Chinese patients (OR = 1.409, 95% CI: 1.253 − 1.586). In the population categorization, with farmers as the base group, children and students (OR = 0.375, 95% CI: 0.326 − 0.431), manual worker (OR = 0.730, 95% CI: 0.600 − 0.890), nonmanual labourers (OR = 0.521, 95% CI: 0.448 − 0.607), houseworkers and nonengaged workers (OR = 0.556, 95% CI: 0.519 − 0.597), retired workers (OR = 0.562, 95% CI: 0.473 − 0.667), and the “unknown, other” category (OR = 0.475, 95% CI: 0.389 − 0.581) had a lower risk of delayed visits (all P < 0.001), whereas the risk for service workers was not significantly different (P = 0.639). The risk of delayed consultation was lower among those whose current address type “other” (OR = 0.561, 95% CI: 0.460 − 0.684). In the household address category, the risk of delayed consultation was significantly lower for intracity migrants than for local residents (OR = 0.573, 95% CI: 0.540 − 0.608), whereas the risk was significantly greater for those with a household address type of “other” (OR = 1.345, 95% CI: 1.214 − 1.491). With respect to patient origin, the risk of delayed consultation was greater for those who were referred (OR = 3.176, 95% CI: 1.929 − 5.228) and for those who were seen directly (OR = 3.537, 95% CI: 2.145 − 5.833) (both P < 0.001), but it was not statistically significant for those who were tracked (P = 0.420). In terms of diagnostic results, the risk of visit delay was greater among those with a positive pathology (OR = 1.296, 95% CI: 1.194 ~ 1.407). The above results suggest that there is significant heterogeneity in the effects of different population characteristics on visit delay, as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

Analysis of factors affecting delayed medical care

This study revealed that the median patient delay for tuberculosis patients in Shenyang from 2015 to 2024 was 18 days. Compared with international studies, these findings revealed a shorter delay than in India [12](25 days), Portugal [13](33 days), and northern Australia [14](53 days). Compared with domestic studies in other regions, the median number of days of patient delay for tuberculosis patients in Shenyang was higher than that in Wuhan [15] (10 days), the Qingpu District of Shanghai [16] (10 days), and Beijing [17] (11 days) but lower than that in Lishui City [8] (36 days) and similar to that in Zigong City [18] (20 days). The rate of patient delay for tuberculosis patients in Shenyang was 56.64%, which was similar to but slightly higher than that reported in Wuhan [15] (52.5%), Changsha [19] (53.5%), Guiyang [20] (53.67%), and Ethiopia [21] (50.42%) but lower than that reported in Huaibei City, Anhui Province [22] (68.57%). Patient delay for tuberculosis patients in Shenyang is relatively common and should be taken seriously. The rate of patient delay for tuberculosis patients in Shenyang from 2015 to 2024 generally showed an increasing trend, which was consistent with the results from Zigong City [18] and Changsha [19] but inconsistent with the findings of other studies [20]. The reasons for this increasing trend might be an insufficient or ineffective public health publicity, patients’ insufficient awareness of the early symptoms of tuberculosis, and a lack of understanding of the tuberculosis prevention and control system, leading to patient delay. A previous study in Ethiopia demonstrated that knowledge of tuberculosis reduced the delay by 68% [21]. Patients with insufficient knowledge of tuberculosis are more likely to experience patient delay [23]. During the global pandemic of novel coronavirus infection, the progress of tuberculosis-related services generally stopped or reversed worldwide. Some designated tuberculosis hospitals suspended the reception of tuberculosis patients; concurrently, the fear of COVID-19 prompted patients to postpone medical consultation, which further affected the rate of patient delay for tuberculosis [24]. Additionally, the home quarantine measures implemented during the pandemic prevented some patients from seeking medical care at hospitals [25].The rate of patient delay decreased slightly from 2021 to 2024 because residents paid more attention to their respiratory symptoms during the regular prevention and control of the epidemic and were more willing to seek medical consultation [26].

This study has identified common factors associated with tuberculosis patient delays, including: residing in rural areas, failure to undergo sputum smear testing [27]; advanced age, young migrant populations, cases identified through referral and contact tracing [15]; children, newly diagnosed patients, and others [10]. The results of the binary logistic analysis revealed that gender, age, nation, crowd classification, current residence type, household registration address category, patient source, and diagnosis were all factors that influenced delayed medical consultation. This study revealed that female patients had a greater risk of patient delay for tuberculosis, which was similar to the results of studies from northwest China [28] and Ethiopia [21]. As education level increases, more women participate in social activities. To balance family and social activities, women are likely to neglect their own health. Seeking medical consultation requires taking leave or stopping work, which may affect family livelihoods, leading women to prefer to endure symptoms. In addition, women may mistake tuberculosis symptoms (such as coughing) for common problems such as fatigue, resulting in a greater risk of patient delay for women [29].

The results revealed that age ≥ 60 years old was a risk factor for patient delay. With increasing age in China, the proportion of people aged ≥ 60 years has increased. The symptoms of tuberculosis in elderly individuals are usually nonspecific, such as fatigue, loss of appetite, and weight loss, which are easily mistaken for ageing or chronic diseases, leading to a missed diagnosis. In addition, elderly individuals may lack knowledge of tuberculosis and mistake coughing and low fever for the “common cold” without giving it sufficient attention. For these reasons, elderly individuals are likely to experience patient delay [30]. Efforts should be made to promote health checks for elderly individuals and strengthen publicity about tuberculosis and its prevention and control measures [28].

Among different crowd classifications, farmers are likely to experience patient delay, a finding that is consistent with a meta-analysis in Ethiopia [31]. Children and students, people who perform household chores, unemployed people, manual workers, non-manual workers, and retirees have a lower risk of patient delay, suggesting that farmers may constitute a high-risk group for patient delay, which is consistent with the results from Zigong City [18]. This phenomenon is mainly due to the low quality of rural health services. Farmers generally have a lower educational level, lack knowledge about tuberculosis, and have limited understanding of the national free TB diagnosis and treatment policy, all of which increase the risk of patient delay [32]. Additionally, most farmers have poor economic conditions, and life and work constraints make them more likely to delay medical treatment. Studies have confirmed that the accessibility of medical resources affects the control of tuberculosis, and patients living far from medical institutions are more likely to experience patient delay [33, 34]. Therefore, greater attention should be given to remote rural areas, where the allocation of medical resources should be optimized. Additionally, policy of promoting tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment should be strengthened to reduce patient delay.

Considering the household registration status, individuals who relocate within the city exhibit a lower risk of delayed medical care, which may be attributed to the fact that the migrant population is predominantly composed of young and middle-aged adults (64.84% aged 18–59). Young and middle-aged people generally have better health literacy and are more sensitive to tuberculosis symptoms, and symptoms such as coughing and fever are more likely to be taken seriously and treated promptly. Moreover, people who move within the city usually live or work in core urban areas, which are closer to comprehensive hospitals, disease control centres, and other medical institutions, reducing the risk of patient delay due to inconvenient transportation.

Among the sources of patients, those diagnosed with tuberculosis through physical examination had the lowest rate of patient delay. Compared with physical examination, patients with direct medical consultation or those who were referred had a greater risk of patient delay. This result is consistent with studies from Beijing [17] and Zigong [18]. This phenomenon may indicate that patients who undergo physical examination have greater initiative to seek medical treatment, resulting in the detection of tuberculosis in a timely manner and a shorter interval between symptom onset and actual medical treatment. However, patients who utilize direct medical consultation or are referred often initially visit non-designated tuberculosis hospitals such as primary care institutions. The first doctor must refer the patient to a designated tuberculosis hospital after identifying tuberculosis symptoms. During this period, patients may not seek medical treatment in a timely manner for various reasons, such as ignoring early symptoms of tuberculosis, having low initiative to seek medical treatment, and lacking awareness and diagnostic ability for tuberculosis, thus increasing the risk of patient delay [35].

This study further revealed that patients who were pathologically positive for tuberculosis had a significantly greater risk of patient delay. This phenomenon may be related to the fact that the early symptoms of some patients with a positive pathology (such as low fever and fatigue) lack specificity and are easily misdiagnosed as common respiratory infections, reducing their likelihood of seeking medical treatment. Mycobacterium tuberculosis multiplies in the body for a long time before developing into pathology-positive disease. From the appearance of symptoms to actual medical treatment, there is often a long time span, and patients seek medical treatment due to a decline in quality of life [36]. In addition, community hospitals may lack rapid pathology testing equipment, and patients must be referred multiple times to specialized hospitals for testing. Moreover, the cost of pathology testing (such as molecular biology tests like GeneXpert and sputum culture) is relatively high, which reduces patients’ willingness to undergo testing and prolongs the time to diagnosis. The utilization of rapid diagnostic tools such as GeneXpert should be further optimized to improve pathological detection and the timely diagnosis of tuberculosis [37].

Characteristics of patient delay in key populations

This study identified several key populations at significantly higher risk of patient delay, including farmers, women, individuals aged ≥ 60 years, ethnic minorities, and pathologically positive patients. Farmers demonstrated the highest risk of delay, which may be associated with limited access to rural healthcare, insufficient knowledge of tuberculosis (TB), and financial constraints. This underscores the need to enhance grassroots-level screening and strengthen the dissemination of relevant policies. Women, potentially due to overlapping social and family roles that lead to neglect of their own health, would benefit from gender-sensitive health education initiatives. Older adults often present with atypical symptoms that are easily mistaken for age-related conditions or other chronic diseases; thus, integrating TB screening into routine elderly health management and reinforcing active case-finding in communities are recommended. Ethnic minority patients face barriers related to cultural and linguistic differences, as well as uneven distribution of healthcare resources, necessitating the provision of multilingual health promotion and accessible screening services. Furthermore, pathologically positive patients are more prone to delay due to nonspecific early symptoms and limited diagnostic capacity at the primary care level. Expanding the use of rapid molecular diagnostic tools such as GeneXpert could improve early detection rates. In contrast, physical examination served as a protective factor against delay, suggesting that incorporating TB screening into standard health check-ups in workplaces and communities could facilitate earlier identification and intervention.

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study is based on standardized surveillance data from the National Tuberculosis Information Management System of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, ensuring authoritative data sources and reliable statistical results. Covering a ten-year observation period from 2015 to 2024, the research comprehensively includes data from both before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. It systematically elucidates the adverse effects of patient delays on tuberculosis transmission, disease progression, and healthcare burden, thereby providing direct empirical evidence for formulating TB prevention and control strategies.

Academically and practically, the study incorporates multiple variables including gender and age, focusing on Shenyang—a core city in Northeast China’s old industrial base with high population density and frequent mobility—thus filling a regional research gap. It identifies high-risk groups such as farmers and ethnic minorities, reveals health checkups as a protective factor, provides targets for precision intervention research, and offers scientific support for optimizing control strategies (e.g. screening through health examinations for high-risk populations).

This study has the following limitations. Firstly, As the self-reporting method used in data collection reliedon personal recall, this may have led to recall bias. Furthermore, the scope of this study is limited, as it is a retrospective study design based on medical information system reports, and therefore unable to comprehensively assess the impact of patients’ socioeconomic characteristics and cultural factors on delayed medical visits. Although the sample size was adequate, it may not be generalizable to the entire population.

Currently, a delay in seeking care among tuberculosis patients is predominantly defined as “more than 14 days from symptom onset to the first medical consultation,” a criterion also adopted in this study. They neither specified the proportion of patients with delayed consultation who were ultimately diagnosed with tuberculosis, nor distinguished between patients with other comorbidities and those presenting with long COVID-related symptoms. This limitation is primarily due to constraints associated with data collection conditions. Accordingly, we recommend that clinical practice place greater emphasis on clarifying the proportions of confirmed tuberculosis cases, patients with other comorbidities, and individuals with long COVID-related symptoms among those with delayed consultation, thereby enhancing the relevance of related research to clinical practice guidance. Our findings also reveal significant heterogeneity in the duration of healthcare-seeking delays. Therefore, we recommend that national disease control and prevention authorities and other leading tuberculosis (TB) control agencies in China develop a more refined, stratified definition of healthcare-seeking delay by leveraging large-scale surveillance datasets—such as those from the Tuberculosis Information Management System. The establishment of such a stratified criteria system is critical for accurately identifying at-risk populations, raising risk awareness among the general public and healthcare providers alike, and optimizing targeted intervention strategies.

Conclusion

The rate of patient delay for tuberculosis patients in Shenyang City from 2015 to 2024 was shows an overall increasing trend. Effective interventions should be taken for risk factors and key populations to reduce the rate of delayed patient visits.

This study found that delayed diagnosis of tuberculosis patients in Shenyang was common between 2015 and 2024, with an overall upward trend in the delay rate during this period. Female patients, patients aged 60 years or older, ethnic minority patients, and farmers were found to have a higher risk of delayed diagnosis. Health check-ups and residents of the city’s floating population were identified as protective factors for reducing the risk of delayed diagnosis. These findings suggest that socioeconomic factors, access to healthcare services, and residents’ health awareness are key factors influencing timely diagnosis among tuberculosis patients in the Shenyang region. To effectively reduce the rate of delayed diagnosis, it is imperative to develop and implement more targeted public health intervention strategies tailored to the identified high-risk populations and risk factors, thereby lowering the rate of delayed diagnosis among patients.
