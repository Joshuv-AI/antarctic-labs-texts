Biogenic chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposite surface coating inhibits MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm establishment on orthopedic implants
Authors: Bushra Uzair, Zukhra Abbasi, Fehmida Fasim, Shoaib Alam, Apori Samuel Obeng, Furong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrthopedic implant-associated infections (IAIs), predominantly caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, represent a major clinical challenge due to antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation, which together contribute to their chronic nature. This research presents a biogenic biofilm-resistant chitosan encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocoating (CS-Cu/ZnO NC) synthesized using Euphorbia thymofolia metabolites and applied on orthopedic implant surface (Tubular bone locked stainless steel plates). The synthesized CS-Cu/ZnO NCs were characterized physicochemically (UV–Vis, FTIR, XRD, SEM) and biologically (antibacterial, antibiofilm, antivirulence, hemolysis and cytotoxicity). Microstructural analysis using UV–Visible Spectroscopy (UV–Vis), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) confirmed the successful synthesis, crystallinity and integration of Cu/ZnO into the chitosan matrix. SEM confirmed that a thin uniform coating was formed by the layer-by-layer method compared to the conventional dip coating method. CS-Cu/ZnO NCs at MIC level (4 µg/mL) reduced virulence traits of MDR P. aeruginosa, such as motility behavior, pyocyanin production, and biofilm production by 90%, which is important for pathogenicity. These coatings demonstrated remarkable antibacterial efficacy (34 ± 1 mm), achieving a 90% reduction in P. aeruginosa populations within 4 h with 5% of Cu/ZnO NCs while exhibiting minimal cytotoxicity (< 5%) on osteoblast cell lines at the concentration range (6–10 µg/mL). Additionally, this coating stops the adhesion of P. aeruginosa biofilms on implant surfaces due to the adhesive and antimicrobial properties of chitosan and Cu/ZnO. This study demonstrates the potential of biogenic CS-Cu/ZnO NCs nanocoatings as an antibiofilm surface engineering strategy to address current limitations in preventing MDR biofilm-associated orthopedic implant infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44446-026-00098-8.Keywords: Antibiofilm surfaces, Nanocoating, Cu/ZnO, Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Introduction

Orthopedic implants are indispensable medical devices used globally to address degenerative disorders in the geriatric population, support fractured bones, enhance joint function, and manage cardiac conduction abnormalities. Despite their critical role in modern healthcare, the long-term durability of these implants is undermined by infections associated with biofilm-producing multidrug-resistant microorganisms. These biofilms pose significant challenges to treat with conventional antibiotics and often result in the rapid resurgence of infections. Gram-positive bacteria, such as Staphylococcus epidermidis and Streptococcus sanguinis, and Gram-negative bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Escherichia coli, are among the most common pathogens implicated in implants associated infections (Chen et al. 2023).

Particularly, Pseudomonas aeruginosa has gained attention due to its remarkable ability to adhere to orthopedic implant surfaces and form biofilms, resulting in persistent infections that are difficult to treat (Reynolds and Kollef 2021). Approximately 80% of clinical infections caused by the genus Pseudomonas are attributed to Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Süer 2021). Biofilm formation on implant surfaces not only shields the bacteria from antibiotics but also facilitates rapid reinfection, necessitating repeated antibiotic treatments or implant revision surgeries. These conventional approaches are associated with challenges such as antibiotic resistance, cytotoxicity, and poor patient outcomes, further emphasizing the urgency to develop alternative and effective strategies (Akay and Yaghmur 2024). Current treatment options to eradicate biofilms embedded on medical implants include implant replacement and prolonged antibiotic courses, which often fail to eradicate biofilms and come with serious drawbacks such as antibiotic resistance, cytotoxicity, delayed bone regeneration, and increased economic burden. These challenges are exacerbated by the global rise in orthopedic procedures, with the orthopedic implants market projected to surpass USD 20.70 billion by 2033, growing at a CAGR of 4.9% (The Brainy Insights 2024). Such infections not only double the cost of treatment but also increase the risk of severe complications, including pain, amputation, or life-threatening outcomes (Chen et al. 2023).

Previous studies have witnessed a growing interest in the development of robust and reliable strategies for the prevention and treatment of IAIs (Akay and Yaghmur 2024). In particular, there is a growing interest in alternative antibacterial strategies to combat biofilm infections by functionalizing or modifying implant surfaces. Nanoparticles offer a highly promising avenue for biofilm therapy, as they have demonstrated a remarkable ability to directly eliminate bacteria or release bactericides by precisely modulating their chemical composition, size, and surface charge (Al-Wrafy et al. 2022). In recent decades, the functionalization of implant surfaces using nanotechnology has emerged as a promising approach to combat biofilm-associated infections. Nanoparticles, particularly metal oxide nanoparticles, exhibit unique physicochemical (size, surface charge, chemical composition) that enable them to penetrate biofilms precisely, disrupt bacterial membranes, and induce cell death (Sahoo et al. 2022). Among the most used antibacterial metal oxides, after silver are copper and zinc, and have garnered attention for their antimicrobial properties, osteogenic potential, biocompatibility, and cost-effectiveness as these are essential trace elements required for proper body functioning (Lv et al. 2022). Furthermore, medicinal plant-based synthesis of nanoparticles offers an eco-friendly and less toxic alternative to conventional physical and chemical methods (Chowdhury et al. 2024). Chitosan, a natural biopolymer, has demonstrated significant potential for biomedical applications, including its use as an antimicrobial coating material for orthopedic implants. Its intrinsic antimicrobial properties, combined with its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ability to promote wound healing, make it an ideal candidate for implant surface modifications (Savdenbekova et al. 2024). When combined with metallic nanoparticles, chitosan enhances antibacterial activity through synergistic mechanisms, such as cell wall disruption and DNA complexation due to its positively charged amino group, making it particularly appealing for combating infections caused by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Additionally, its anti-inflammatory properties and osteoconductivity further accentuate its suitability for implant surface modifications (Savdenbekova et al. 2024; Oliveira et al. 2024). The realization of antimicrobial coatings through the incorporation of active agents into a polymer matrix is a promising alternative to the direct addition of antimicrobials into the implant surfaces (Sahoo et al. 2022). To this aim, the goal of this work was to develop a sustainable, antibacterial coating with high functional performance, based on Cu/ZnO nanocomposites as bioactive agent.

Although chitosan-metal oxide hybrid antibacterial coatings and phyto-mediated synthesis are widely reported, their applications for implant-associated infection control remain limited. Therefore, the study developed a novel chitosan-based antimicrobial nanocoating (CS-Cu/ZnO) for a bone locked tabular plate using Euphorbia thymifolia (extremophile) extract as a reducing and stabilizing agent. The biosynthesized CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoatings were applied to orthopedic implants infected with MDR biofilm-forming P. aeruginosa using a layer-by-layer coating method. Results demonstrated that the composite coating effectively eradicated biofilms, offering a robust alternative to conventional strategies for managing IAIs. Furthermore, in vitro cytotoxicity assessment confirmed the biocompatibility of the synthesized coatings and paved the way for preliminary in vivo studies. This work provides a significant advancement in the development of eco-friendly, cost-effective, and biocompatible coatings for orthopedic implants, which could contribute to improved clinical outcomes and address the growing challenge of antibiotic resistance, in addition to implant-associated infections.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Copper sulphates (CuSO4), Zinc oxide (ZnO), Chitosan (degree of deacetylation DD: 87%; molecular weight 50–2000 kDa), Acetic acid, Tannic acid, glutaraldehyde, Methanol, Ethanol were used for the synthesis of chitosan encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposite coating. Growth media (Mueller–Hinton broth and Mueller–Hinton agar) were used for bacterial growth. Oxidase agent, Hydrogen peroxide, Blood agar, Iodine, urease, Kovac’s reagent, Barritt’s reagent A were used for biochemical tests. Orthopedic bone locked tabular plate (Ortho-Grip Gujranwala) was used to access biofilm formation. Triton-X and PBS were used as positive and negative controls in hemolysis assay. Crystal violet and congo red dye were used for biofilm formation analysis. DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl), methanol and ascorbic acid were used in radical scavenging activity (Antioxidant activity). Penicillin–Streptomycin, MTT reagent, Dulbecco Modified Eagle's Medium (cell culture medium), formazan dye, FBS (fetal bovine serum) were used in an in-vitro cytotoxicity assay on osteoblast cell lines. All the chemicals used were from Sigma Aldrich USA and of analytical grade.

Collection, identification and virulence determination of clinical MDR P. aeruginosa

Bacterial strain (MDR P. aeruginosa) was collected from the National Institute of Health (NIH, Islamabad and subjected to morphological and biochemical confirmation tests. Biochemical tests include citrate utilization test, TSI Fermentation and oxidase activity test. All the biochemical tests were performed in specific media according to the standard methods described in the Microbiology Laboratory Manual (Cappuccino et al. 1996; Karah et al. 2020). Virulence factors including the MIU (motility and urease) test, pyocyanin and biofilm production, were determined.

Resistance index of P. aeruginosa

Antibiotic susceptibility of the tested strain was observed by disc diffusion assay (Kirby-Bauer technique) as per CLSI guidelines 2025, and the antibiotics involved are ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ceftazidime-erythromycin-sulfamethoxazole and amikacin disulphate. The bacteria resistant to at least three antimicrobial drugs were considered as multidrug-resistant strains. Subsequently, the collected strain was subjected to the Analytical Profiling Index (API-20NE) system to ensure fast, reliable and efficient diagnosis.

Collection and identification of medicinal plant Euphorbia thymofolia

Fresh leaves of Euphorbia thymofolia were collected from Islamabad (I-10) in July, washed with distilled water and grinded into fine powder after being shade-dried for 1 week at room temperature. Further, the collected plant was identified by a taxonomist, Dr. Abrar Shinwari, Associate Professor at the Department of Biological Sciences, International Islamic University, Islamabad.

Bioformulation of Cu/ZnO nanocomposites

Synthesis of CuO, ZnO and Cu/ZnO nanocomposite was done using the reported methodology (Bourang et al. 2024). The aqueous extract of Euphorbia thymifolia was prepared by suspending 20 g of plant powder in 100 mL of autoclaved distilled water and incubating it on a shaker at 150 rpm for 24 h to extract plant metabolites. Separately, 1 M CuSO₄ and ZnO solutions were prepared in 50 mL of autoclaved distilled water. Each precursor solution was mixed with 50 mL of plant extract and stirred at 50 °C for 3 h on a magnetic hotplate to induce nanoparticle synthesis, as indicated by a color change to dark green. The suspensions were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and the pellets were washed with distilled water and ethanol. Purified nanoparticles were dried and stored for characterization.

Formulation of chitosan encapsulated Cu/ZnO surface nanocoating

Chitosan encapsulation of Cu/ZnO NCs with chitosan was performed by modifying the previous methodology (Lin et al. 2021). Chitosan solution (Solution A) was prepared by dissolving 1.5 g of chitosan in 100 mL of 1.5% (v/v) acetic acid in deionized water at 60 °C for 5 h. Separately, 5 g of Cu/ZnO nanocomposite was dispersed in 50 mL of distilled water (Solution B). Solution A was added dropwise to Solution B under continuous stirring at 150 rpm for 6 h, followed by the addition of glutaraldehyde and tannic acid as crosslinking agents. The resulting viscous layer of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried for subsequent analysis. Schematic illustration of Euphorbia thymofolia-mediated synthesis of CS-Cu/ZnO NCs as an antibiofilm surface coating material for orthopaedic implants

Surface engineering of tubular bone locked stainless steel plates with CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

Tubular bone locked stainless steel orthopedic plates (5 cm2 area, 1 cm thickness) were polished using grinding paper to achieve a uniform surface and segmented into 1 cm2. Residual metal oxide particulates were removed by ultrasonic cleaning in a solution of acetone, distilled water, and methanol for 1 h to activate the implant surface. The cleaned segments were dried in a vacuum desiccator and sterilized under UV light (300 µW/cm2) for 10 min on each side. Subsequently, layer by layer (Abbas et al. 2024) and dip coating method (Hasani-Sadrabadi et al. 2021) were used to coat chitosan-based Cu/ZnO nanocoating on the implant surface as reported. Briefly, a nanocomposite suspension was prepared in distilled water, and the solution was deposited dropwise on the implant surface, the coated discs were air-dried in a sterile biosafety cabinet for 24 h to ensure complete evaporation of residual solvents.

Physico-chemical characterization of synthesized pristine and CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

The optical properties of CuONPs, ZnO NPs, Cu/ZnO NCs, and CS-Cu/ZnO NCs were analyzed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (HITACHI Model U 2800) across the wavelength range of 200–800 nm to confirm the successful synthesis of nanoscale formulations. Furthermore, interaction of biological molecules in green extract with nanoparticles was confirmed by FTIR. Crystallite size, structural phase, and nature of these bio-synthesized pristine nanoparticles and nanocomposites were determined using X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD; Model D8 BRUKER, Germany) equipped with a LynxEye™ detector, operated at 60 kV and 60 mA. The crystallite size calculation was done using the Debye–Scherrer equation (Kiani et al. 2023):

The surface morphology and particle size of the nanoparticles were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Hitachi SU8230, Tokyo, Japan). Before imaging, a thin layer of gold was sputter-coated onto the samples to enhance conductivity. The samples were analyzed under an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, and elemental composition was confirmed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

Assessment of antibacterial potential of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

The antibacterial potential of the synthesized nanoformulation was analyzed against MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa using a standardized well diffusion method (Abbas et al. 2024). Overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 10ˆ8 CFU/mL) by diluting in 1:50 and uniformly swabbed onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates to ensure consistent microbial coverage. Pristine and CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating solution (20 µg/mL) was prepared and carefully poured into the wells (5 mm), including a blank distilled well as the negative control and ciprofloxacin as a positive control. Same experiment was performed 3 times and antibacterial efficacy was quantified by measuring the diameter of clear inhibition zones (mm) surrounding each well after overnight incubation at 37 °C.

Evaluating surface colonization potential of P. aeruginosa on CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoated implant

Congo red assay

Biofilm formation capacity of the collected MDR P. aeruginosa strain was evaluated using culture on Congo-red agar (CRA) and microtitre method (CV assay) (Furtuna et al. 2018). Briefly, Congo red agar was prepared using tryptic soya agar, sucrose 50 g/L, and Congo red indicator 0.8 g/L. Subsequently, the tested strain was streaked on CRA plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C; after overnight incubation, the plates were observed for colony color. Black colonies with a dry crystalline appearance are a characteristic of biofilm structure. CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite-treated plate was used to check the biofilm inhibition potential of synthesized nano-coating.

Crystal violet assay

The biofilm-inhibitory potential of the pristine and CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite was assessed using a 96-well microtiter plate method at their minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs), ½ MIC, and ¼ MIC (Rasheed et al. 2024). Briefly, P. aeruginosa was cultured overnight on Mueller–Hinton agar, then colonies were inoculated into sterile wells containing Mueller–Hinton broth with the nanocomposite at MIC and sub-MIC levels. The positive control included Mueller–Hinton broth with bacteria, while ciprofloxacin-treated bacteria served as a standard for validation. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 48 h under static conditions to promote biofilm formation. After incubation, the supernatant was gently removed, and the wells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic cells. Biofilms on the well surfaces were stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 30 min. Excess stain was rinsed off with PBS, and the wells were air-dried. The crystal violet was dissolved in 95% ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Additionally, SEM analysis was used to confirm biofilm formation on CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoated implants. Pyocyanin production

Bluish-green pigment production by P. aeruginosa was confirmed by culturing it on Cetrimide agar medium at 37 ºC for 48–72 h.

MIC of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

McFarland standard (0.5) of P. auroginosa was prepared by suspending 2–3 fresh bacterial colonies (24 h) in normal saline and confirmed using a densitometer at OD 600 1.0. Serial dilutions of Cu, ZnO, Cu/ZnO, and chitosan-coated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (20 µg/mL, 30 µg/mL, 40 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 60 µg/mL) were prepared. Bacterial inoculum (10 µL) was introduced into Mueller–Hinton broth containing different concentrations of the nanocomposite dilutions in a 96-well microtiter plate. Experimental setup also includes positive growth control and negative control groups for optimization. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the minimum concentration that resulted in a clear suspension, indicating inhibition of bacterial growth, was recorded as the MIC (Rasheed et al. 2024).

Time kill kinetics assay of synthesized CS-Cu/ZnO surface nanocoating

The time-kill kinetics of CuONPs, ZnONPs, Cu/ZnONPs, and chitosan-coated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites were evaluated against MDR P. aeruginosa following the reported methodology described by (Uzair et al. 2017). Briefly, P. aeruginosa cultures were grown to the exponential phase and then diluted to a final concentration of 10^7 CFU/mL in PBS. Bacterial suspensions were exposed to varying concentrations of nanocomposites (0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 μg/mL) in Mueller–Hinton broth. The positive control was maintained without any antibacterial agent to assess baseline bacterial growth. At predetermined intervals (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h), optical density (OD) measurements at 600 nm were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer to monitor bacterial growth. The resulting OD values were plotted to assess the bactericidal activity and the extent of bacterial inhibition over time using GraphPad Prism software.

DPPH radical scavenging activity of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

The free radical scavenging activity (RSA) of chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (CS-Cu/ZnO NCs) was evaluated using the DPPH assay, as described by (Dauthal and Mukhopadhyay 2013). The assay utilized DPPH, a stable free radical, to assess the radical scavenging potential of the CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating. DPPH solution (0.24 mg/mL) was prepared in methanol, and its absorbance was adjusted to 0.973 at 517 nm. CS-Cu/ZnO NCs were tested at various concentrations (0.04, 0.08, 0.12, and 0.16 mg/mL), and ascorbic acid was used as a standard reducing agent for comparison. Upon reaction with the nanocomposites, the deep violet color of the DPPH solution gradually changed to pale yellow and eventually became nearly colorless, indicating free radical scavenging. Quantitative analysis was performed by measuring the optical density of the reaction mixtures at 517 nm after 30 min of incubation at room temperature in the dark. In vitro biocompatibility analysis of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

In-vitro hemolytic studies of as synthesized coated material (CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite) followed the protocol of Abbas and her team. Freshly isolated 5% blood sample from the healthy volunteer in the EDTA tube was washed three times with PBS and centrifuged at 10000 g for 5 min to collect pure red blood cells. 200µL of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite was transferred into tubes containing RBCs and incubated at 37℃ for 4 h to evaluate hemolysis. Triton X-100 (positive control, PC) and PBS (negative control, NC) were also employed to make a comparison (Abbas et al. 2024).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay of CS-Cu/ZnO nano-coating

The cytotoxic potential of chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (CS-Cu/ZnO NCs) was assessed using the MTT assay, following the methodology described by (Pehlivanoglu et al. 2023). Biochemical tests and virulence characterization of purified P. auroginosa colonies are presented in Fig. 2. UV–Vis absorption spectra of the synthesized CuO-NPs, ZnO-NPs, Cu/ZnO NCs, and CS-Cu/ZnO NCs, measured in the 200–800 nm range (Fig. 3a). High-resolution fourier transform infrared spectroscopy of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

FTIR spectra of synthesized nanoparticles (CuO NPs, ZnO NPs, Cu/ZnO NCs, CS-Cu/ZnO NCs), confirming the successful incorporation and interaction of functional groups in the synthesized CS/Cu/ZnO nanocomposite as illustrated in Fig. 3b. Characteristic absorption peak is observed at a wavenumber of 589 cm⁻1, attributed to Cu = O, while peaks at 552 cm−1 and 533 cm−1 correspond to Zn = O and Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (NCs), respectively. For the CS/Cu/ZnO nanocomposite, a similar peak appears at 551 cm−1. For CuO nanoparticles, an additional peak at 860 cm−1 is assigned to C = C stretching vibrations. Additionally, a broad absorption peak in the range of 3400–3450 cm−1 indicates O–H stretching vibrations, likely due to moisture content in the sample. The band near 2916 cm⁻1 corresponds to the C–H group, while a vibrational band between 1400–1700 cm⁻1 is attributed to C = O stretching modes. The slight shifts in wavenumber and variations in peak intensity suggest minor interactions between functional groups, specifically O–H, C–H, and C = C, within the chitosan, copper, and zinc oxide nanocomposite.

X-ray crystallographic analysis of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

Crystalline structure, size, and phase purity of Euphorbia thymofolia leaf extract-mediated CuO, ZnO, Cu/ZnO, and CS-Cu/ZnO nanoparticles as analyzed through X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) in the 2θ range from 20° to 80° as depicted in Fig. 4. The XRD pattern of ZnO nanomaterials reveals a hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure with prominent peaks at 2θ values of 32.07°, 34.74°, 36.55°, 47.83°, 56.85°, 63.13°, 66.58°, 68.19°, 69.35°, and 77.70°, corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), (201), and (202) crystallographic planes, respectively (JCPDS 36–1451). The XRD patterns of the composite material Cu/ZnO exhibit additional peaks at 32.84°, 35.84°, 39.12°, 48.64°, 53.30°, 61.75°, 66.38°, 68.40°, 72.40°, and 75.16°, corresponding to the (110), (002), (111), (−202), (020), (−113), (−311), (220), (222), and (311) planes of monoclinic CuO, as per standard references (JCPDS 48–1548). These peaks coexist with those of the ZnO phase, confirming the wurtzite structure of ZnO and verifying the purity and crystallinity of the synthesized nanoparticles. Notably, the absence of peaks corresponding to crystalline impurities demonstrates the purity of the materials used during synthesis. The diffraction peaks observed for CS-Cu/ZnO NCs (JCPDS 85–1326) display a diminished intensity for some ZnO and CuO peaks, attributed to the lower concentration of these components in the composite compared to their bulk counterparts (JCPDS 36–1451 and JCPDS 48–1548). The crystallite size was calculated from the highest intensity peak, yielding ranges of 26–30 nm, which align with typical ranges observed in green-synthesized nanoparticles, highlighting the role of Euphorbia thymofolia extract in controlling nanoparticle growth.

Antimicrobial evaluation of synthesized pristine and CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

Resistance landscape of Pseudomonas aeruginosa

The antibacterial susceptibility testing results, interpreted according to CLSI guidelines (2025), provide insights into the efficacy (sensitive/resistant) of tested antibiotics against the isolated P. auroginosa microbial strain. Ciprofloxacin demonstrated the highest efficacy with a zone of inhibition of 35 mm, categorizing the strain as sensitive (S). Polymyxin B also exhibited strong activity, with a zone of inhibition of 32 mm, confirming its classification as sensitive (S). Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was found to be resistant (R) with a 12 mm zone of inhibition. Likewise, piperacillin, tazobactam and amikacin showed no inhibition zones, consistent with acquired resistance (R) for piperacillin, tazobactam and amikacin disulphate. These results suggest ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole as the most effective antibiotics, while amoxicillin-clavulanic acid might require higher doses or combination therapy for efficacy. Piperacillin-tazobactam and amikacin are also unsuitable for treating infections caused by this strain due to resistance. In conclusion, according to internationally accepted definitions (ECDC/CDC), Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibiting non-susceptibility to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial classes was classified as a multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolate.

Antibacterial potential of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating against MDR P. aeruginosa

The antibacterial activity of Euphorbia thymofolia extract, synthesized pristine nanoparticles (CuO NPs, ZnO NPs, and Cu/ZnO NCs) and chitosan (CS)-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites against biofilm forming Pseudomonas aeruginosa demonstrated promising results, as evidenced by the zones of inhibition (ZOI) (Fig. 8b, c, and f). Dark black color production on congo red plates indicates strong biofilm formation by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Fig. 8d), whereas the CS/Cu/ZnO NCs treated plate showed weak biofilm formation as indicated by pink color (Fig. 8e). Notably, the chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites exhibited significantly enhanced antibacterial efficacy (34 ± 1 mm) compared to their pristine counterparts 24 ± 1 mm (Fig. 8b and c). This enhanced activity can be attributed to the synergistic effects of chitosan’s inherent antimicrobial properties and its role in stabilizing and increasing the bioavailability of the Cu/ZnO nanoparticles. The positively charged amine groups of chitosan interact with the negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, disrupting their structural integrity and increasing nanoparticle penetration.

MIC of CS-Cu/ZnO NCs nanocoating

The MIC of the synthesized nanocomposites was determined using the broth dilution assay, which identifies the lowest concentration of a material that completely inhibits the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Concentration-dependent antibacterial effect was observed, as evidenced by the progressive decrease in absorbance with increasing concentrations (ranging from 10–1 to 104 μg/mL) of the tested materials. Among the tested nanomaterials, pristine ZnO nanoparticles exhibited an MIC value of 103 μg/mL, reflecting their moderate antibacterial activity. In contrast, pristine CuO nanoparticles and pristine Cu/ZnO nanocomposites demonstrated significantly (p < 0.001) enhanced activity, with MIC values ranging from 100 μg/mL to 101 μg/mL. Notably, the chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (CS-Cu/ZnO) exhibited superior antimicrobial efficacy (p < 0.0001), with MIC values also observed between 100 μg/mL and 101 μg/mL (Fig. 8g-h). The positive control group showed increased O.D., indicating bacterial cell viability, while the negative control group showed an O.D. of approximately zero, confirming optimal experimental conditions.

Surface colonization potential of P. aeruginosa on CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoated implant

The antibiofilm potential of the synthesized nanocomposites was evaluated utilizing a 96-well microtiter plate assay (Fig. 9a) and scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 9b-c). The findings indicated a substantial inhibition of biofilm formation by chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (CS-Cu/ZnO NCs), with a reduction exceeding 90% on the implant surface, in comparison to the untreated control (p < 0.001) at MIC and sub-MIC concentrations. Conversely, ciprofloxacin demonstrated only 45% antibiofilm activity (p > 0.001). The efficacy sequence for antibiofilm activity was as follows: Ciprofloxacin < Cu/ZnO NCs < CS-Cu/ZnO NCs. Furthermore, CS-Cu/ZnO nano-coated implants resisted the adhesion and growth of P. aeruginosa on their surfaces (Fig. 8b), whereas uncoated implants exhibited dense bacterial aggregates and extensive biofilm formation (Fig. 9c).

The results demonstrated that the bacteriostatic activity of the tested nanoparticles was concentration-dependent, with higher concentrations exhibiting greater antibacterial effects (Fig. 10a-b). Notably, after 4 h of incubation at their respective MIC values, CuO nanoparticles, Cu/ZnO nanocomposites and CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposites significantly inhibited the growth of biofilm-producing P. aeruginosa (p < 0.0001). Among these, CS-Cu/ZnO NCs exhibited the most potent activity, completely halting bacterial growth within the specified timeframe. Free radicals scavenging activity of CS-Cu/ZnO nanocoating

The antioxidant potential of chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites (CS-Cu/ZnO NCs) was assessed using the DPPH assay, which measures the scavenging of DPPH free radicals, as shown in Fig. 11a-b. The results revealed a dose-dependent radical scavenging activity, with CS-Cu/ZnO NCs exhibiting performance comparable to the standard antioxidant, ascorbic acid. DPPH dye exhibited a purple color due to free radicals, while ascorbic acid neutralized the radicals, resulting in a clear, transparent solution. The CS-Cu/ZnO NCs demonstrated a concentration-dependent color change from intense yellowish to light yellowish, indicating progressive radical scavenging. At a concentration of 0.14 mg/mL, CS-Cu/ZnO NCs achieved 68% radical scavenging activity compared to the standard.

Discussion

Implant-associated infections remain a major clinical challenge, largely due to the ability of opportunistic pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa to form persistent biofilms and develop multidrug resistance. The phenotypic characteristics observed in this study, including pigment production, motility, hemolytic activity, and strong biofilm formation, are well-recognized virulence attributes of P. aeruginosa that contribute to chronic wound and implant-related infections. These features facilitate bacterial adhesion to biomaterial surfaces and protect the pathogen from host immune responses and antibiotic therapy, thereby increasing the likelihood of persistent infection and implant failure (Holse et al. 2015). The resistance profile observed in the present study further reflects the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance in clinical P. aeruginosa isolates, which has been widely documented in hospital settings (Kachare et al. 2024).

The physicochemical tests confirmed the successful synthesis of CuO, ZnO, and Cu/ZnO nanocomposites, which were then encapsulated with chitosan. A red shift in the UV–visible spectra and a decrease in bandgap energy after forming the composites suggest stronger electronic interactions between the metal oxides and the polymer matrix. This bandgap reduction, previously linked to improved surface reactivity and antimicrobial activity in metal oxide nanocomposites (Shanmugam and Jeyaperumal 2018; Yusof et al. 2019), indicates enhanced functionality. FTIR spectra showed interactions between chitosan functional groups and nanoparticle surfaces, pointing to effective encapsulation and stabilization. Similar spectral shifts in chitosan-ZnO systems, where amino and hydroxyl groups aid nanoparticle stabilization, have been reported (Aouadi et al. 2024; Nzilu et al. 2023). XRD confirmed the crystalline structure of the nanomaterials, with crystallite sizes typical for plant-mediated synthesis. Variations in peak intensity and size likely result from phytochemicals in the plant extract acting as natural reducing and capping agents, governing nanoparticle nucleation and growth (Faisal et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2025; Hafeez et al. 2022). Green synthesis methods like these are known for producing stable nanoparticles with controlled sizes and enhanced biological functions. SEM analysis revealed distinct structural features: CuO nanoparticles had a flower-like shape, while ZnO and Cu/ZnO nanocomposites showed heterogeneous structures, indicating successful formation and co-synthesis. After encapsulation with chitosan, the nanocomposites formed a cloudy, aggregated network, probably due to polymer coating on the surface. These structural changes could affect bacterial interactions by increasing surface contact and disrupting membranes. Similar structures have been observed in chitosan-modified metal oxide nanoparticles for antimicrobial use (Ferraris et al. 2022). Additionally, SEM imaging of coated implants showed that layer-by-layer deposition produced a uniform, defect-free coating, unlike dip-coating, which produced thicker, uneven layers. Nanoscale, uniform coatings are especially important in biomedical implants as they ensure stable antimicrobial coverage and surface integrity (Aktug et al. 2021; Shbaita et al. 2023).

The antimicrobial evaluation revealed that chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites exhibited significantly enhanced antibacterial activity compared with pristine nanoparticles. This improvement can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between chitosan and the metal oxide nanoparticles. Chitosan possesses inherent antimicrobial properties due to its positively charged amino groups, which interact electrostatically with negatively charged bacterial membranes, thereby destabilizing membranes and increasing permeability. This interaction facilitates greater nanoparticle penetration and enhances their bactericidal activity. Similar synergistic effects between chitosan and metal oxide nanoparticles have been reported in previous studies investigating antimicrobial nanocomposites against Gram-negative pathogens (Akhtar et al. 2024; Javed et al. 2020).

The superior antimicrobial activity of the CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite was further supported by MIC and time-kill analyses, which demonstrated strong concentration-and time-dependent inhibition of P. aeruginosa. Composite nanomaterials often exhibit enhanced antimicrobial efficiency due to increased surface area, improved stability, and the potential generation of reactive oxygen species that damage bacterial cellular components (Abbasi et al. 2024).

In addition to antibacterial activity, the synthesized nanocomposites exhibited remarkable antibiofilm properties. Biofilm formation is a critical virulence strategy employed by P. aeruginosa to resist antimicrobial treatment and persist on implant surfaces. The significant reduction in biofilm formation observed in the present study indicates that the CS-Cu/ZnO nanocomposite effectively interferes with aquaporins and stop bacterial adhesion and early biofilm development. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that chitosan-based nanomaterials can disrupt extracellular polymeric substances and inhibit biofilm maturation in pathogenic bacteria (Kachare et al. 2024). Beyond antimicrobial effects, the nanocomposite demonstrated favorable biological properties relevant to biomedical applications. The observed antioxidant activity suggests the ability of the nanocomposite to neutralize free radicals and reduce oxidative stress, which may contribute to improved tissue healing in infected environments. Furthermore, hemolysis assays indicated minimal erythrocyte damage, confirming good hemocompatibility, while cytotoxicity evaluation showed enhanced viability of MG-63 osteoblast-like cells. Such findings indicate that the nanocomposite not only inhibits bacterial growth but also supports cellular compatibility, an essential requirement for orthopedic implant materials (Javed et al. 2020).

Overall, the present findings highlight the potential of chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposites as multifunctional antimicrobial coatings capable of preventing bacterial colonization, inhibiting biofilm formation, and maintaining biocompatibility. These properties suggest that such nanocomposite coatings could provide an effective strategy for reducing implant-associated infections and improving the long-term performance of orthopedic biomaterials.

Conclusion

This study successfully synthesized Euphorbia thymofolia L. metabolites mediated chitosan-encapsulated Cu/ZnO nanocomposite (CS-Cu/ZnO NCs) as an antimicrobial coating material to address multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa-associated implant infections. Physicochemical characterization confirmed the formation of a stable and pure nano- coating. In vitro study depicted the potent antibacterial activity of nano-coating (CS-Cu/ZnO NCs) (p < 0.0001), demonstrated by significant inhibition of biofilm formation, suppression of pigment production, and reduction in bacterial motility against P. aeruginosa. Biocompatibility assessments revealed a hemolysis rate below 5% and high cell viability 96% within the concentration range of 6–10 µg/mL, accentuating the suitability of this coating material for biomedical applications. This facile and efficient coating strategy provides a promising approach for imparting antimicrobial and therapeutic properties to implant surfaces without requiring external chemical agents or implant replacement to control IAIs. Future studies are essential to comprehensively evaluate the clinical potential of this nano-coating through in vivo studies, focusing on its antibacterial efficacy, biocompatibility, and long-term performance under physiological conditions.
