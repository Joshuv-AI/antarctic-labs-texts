Combined Prognostic Value of Follow‐Up Ejection Fraction and Natriuretic Peptide Measurements in Heart Failure With Reduced Ejection Fraction Following Initiation of Pharmacotherapy
Authors: Nandan Kodur, Paul R. Gunsalus, Alex Milinovich, Jarrod E. Dalton, W. H. Wilson Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFew studies have directly compared the prognostic value of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) in patients with heart failure (HF) with reduced EF who have initiated guideline‐directed medical therapy. This study sought to assess the combined prognostic value of follow‐up LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements taken following initiation of guideline‐directed medical therapy in patients with HF with reduced EF.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study (2009–2024) conducted at a large, integrated health system included 6168 patients who had a diagnosis of HF with reduced EF (LVEF ≤40%) along with available LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements at ~9 months following diagnosis, which was defined as the study baseline. A composite outcome of HF hospitalization or all‐cause death was assessed.ResultsA large majority of patients were on at least 2 guideline‐directed medical therapy medications at baseline. The median LVEF was 36% (interquartile range, 27–46), and the median NT‐proBNP was 2161 pg/mL (interquartile range, 610–7350). Higher NT‐proBNP levels were, in general, associated with a higher adjusted 1‐year risk of the composite outcome across the spectrum of LVEF, with even mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels (125–1000 pg/mL) associated with incremental risk. Both LVEF and NT‐proBNP offered independent prognostic information, but NT‐proBNP had a much stronger association with the composite outcome and a much greater ability to discriminate between patients based on risk than LVEF (Δconcordance index: +0.129; 95% CI, 0.118–0.141), with LVEF offering only minimal prognostic value beyond NT‐proBNP.ConclusionsAmong patients with HF with reduced EF who have initiated guideline‐directed medical therapy, NT‐proBNP may offer much greater prognostic value than LVEF, with even mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels portending meaningful incremental risk.

For patients with heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), defined by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤40%, echocardiographic measurement of LVEF is currently among the primary diagnostic tools for assessing prognosis and guiding management of therapy. 1 However, over the past few years, there has been growing recognition that LVEF, when considered alone, may not sufficiently encapsulate the heterogenous nature of HFrEF, including the unique biological phenotypes with differential clinical trajectories. 2 , 3 Indeed, even patients with HFrEF who achieve full recovery of LVEF (≥50%) remain at considerable risk of relapse. 4 Natriuretic peptides such as NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) could be used to further risk stratify patients with HFrEF beyond LVEF. 2 , 5 , 6 Importantly, leveraging NT‐proBNP to identify residual risk in patients with HFrEF with improved or recovered LVEF could allow for further intensification of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) to improve clinical outcomes. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 Yet, although current guidelines recommend routine measurement of LVEF at 3 to 12 months following initiation of GDMT to guide further management (eg, consideration of device therapies), 1 no such recommendations are made regarding NT‐proBNP, presumably owing to scant data regarding the utility of NT‐proBNP at this time point. Moreover, few studies have examined the combined prognostic value of follow‐up LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements in patients with HFrEF who have initiated GDMT, particularly in a large contemporary cohort. Herein, we evaluate the combined prognostic value of follow‐up LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements taken at ~9 months following diagnosis of HFrEF in a large contemporary cohort, with the hypothesis that NT‐proBNP offers meaningful prognostic value beyond LVEF in patients who have initiated GDMT.

METHODS

Data Availability

Our institutional review board imposes restrictions on data availability for this project because we do not have patients' authorization and consent to share their clinical data outside our institution. The authors have full access to all study data, take responsibility for its integrity and the accuracy of the analyses, and may agree to provide aggregate data upon reasonable request and approval by the institutional review board.

Study Design and Population

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted using data from the electronic medical record at multiple hospitals across Northeast Ohio and Florida in the integrated Cleveland Clinic Health System. A Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology diagram depicting the process for selection of the study cohort is shown in Figure 1. Inclusion criteria were as follows: International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) or Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code for diagnosis of HF anytime from 2009 to 2024 (ICD‐9 codes: 402.X1, 404.X1, 404.X3, 404.9X, 428.XX; ICD‐10 codes: I50.XXX, I09.81, I11.0, I13.0, I13.2); age ≥18 years; at least 1 echocardiogram with an LVEF ≤40% (indicating diagnosis of HFrEF); an available LVEF measurement at 9 ± 3 months following diagnosis of HFrEF; an available NT‐proBNP measurement at 9 ± 3 months following diagnosis of HFrEF; and available outcomes data beyond 9 months following diagnosis of HFrEF. Exclusion criteria were congenital heart disease, cardiac transplant, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Baseline for the study cohort was defined as the date at 9 months following diagnosis of HFrEF. This study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board, with informed consent waived given the retrospective nature of the study.

Baseline Characteristics and Outcomes Data

Baseline demographics, comorbidities, and vitals data were recorded as the closest available data to the date of the HFrEF diagnosis. LVEF measures, as well as laboratory values such as NT‐proBNP and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), were recorded as the closest available data to the date at 9 months following the HFrEF diagnosis. Medication data were recorded as of the date at 6 months following the HFrEF diagnosis (given that GDMT is typically initiated by this time). A composite outcome of HF hospitalization or all‐cause death was assessed, with HF hospitalization encoded using the pertinent ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes (ICD‐9 codes: 402.X1, 404.X1, 404.X3, 404.9X, 428.XX; ICD‐10 codes: I50.XXX, I09.81, I11.0, I13.0, I13.2). Time zero for the composite outcome was defined as the date at 9 months following HFrEF diagnosis.

Categorization of Patients Based on LVEF and NT‐proBNP Measurements

To assess incremental risk of outcomes across the full spectrum of LVEF and NT‐proBNP levels, patients were categorized into different groups based on clinically relevant LVEF and NT‐proBNP thresholds. 1 , 8 LVEF strata were as follows: ≤40%, 41% to 49%, and ≥50%. NT‐proBNP strata were as follows: 0 to 124, 125 to 299, 300 to 599, 600 to 999, 1000 to 1999, 2000 to 4999, and ≥5000 pg/mL.

Statistical Analysis

For descriptive analyses, continuous numeric data are represented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical data are represented as count and percentage. To compare baseline characteristics between the different LVEF groups, the standardized mean difference was computed.

To compare the composite outcome of HF hospitalization or all‐cause death across LVEF and NT‐proBNP groups, Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed, with comparisons assessed using the log‐rank test. In this analysis and all other survival analyses, patients who did not experience the composite outcome during available follow‐up were censored at the date of their most recent clinic visit.

To estimate the adjusted 1‐year risk of the composite outcome based on LVEF and NT‐proBNP strata, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed. A complete‐case approach was used with inclusion of the following covariates: age, sex, relevant comorbidities (atrial fibrillation, anemia, coronary artery bypass graft, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, left bundle branch block, myocardial infarction, obesity, peripheral vascular disease, stroke, supraventricular tachycardia, valvular heart disease, and ventricular arrythmia), body mass index (BMI), mean arterial pressure, eGFR, and GDMT medications (beta blockers, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [MRA], and SGLT2 [sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2] inhibitors). The estimated 1‐year risk of the composite outcome based on LVEF and NT‐proBNP strata was computed at mean values for numeric covariates and mode values for categorical covariates, with the exception that full use of contemporary GDMT was assumed (ie, use of a beta blocker, angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor). Risk estimates are reported along with the 95% CI. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals.

The adjusted 1‐year risk of the composite outcome was also estimated when both LVEF and NT‐proBNP were modeled as continuous variables. NT‐proBNP data were first log‐transformed to achieve a normal distribution. A generalized additive model incorporating thin plate regression splines was then used to model both LVEF and NT‐proBNP while adjusting for the aforementioned covariates of interest in a complete‐case analysis. This model was subsequently stratified by age, sex, atrial fibrillation status, BMI, and eGFR to evaluate for potential effect modification across these variables.

To compare the prognostic value of LVEF and NT‐proBNP with respect to the composite outcome, several univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models incorporating LVEF and NT‐proBNP modeled as continuous variables were created and compared. NT‐proBNP data were first log‐transformed to achieve a normal distribution. LVEF and NT‐proBNP were then modeled separately as well as together using linear terms, and were further modeled using natural cubic splines, with 3 interior knots placed at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles. Interaction between LVEF and NT‐proBNP was assessed. The partial likelihood ratio test statistic (χ 2) was evaluated for each model, with Δχ 2 and the likelihood ratio test used to assess improvement in model fit across nested models. The concordance index (C‐index) was also evaluated for each model, with ΔC‐index used to assess improvement in risk discrimination across models; the 95% CIs for ΔC‐index were computed using bootstrapping methods. 9

P<0.05 was deemed statistically significant for all analyses. All analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.7) and R (version 4.3.1).

RESULTS

Study Cohort Characteristics

Among 49 144 consecutive patients with HFrEF identified from 2009 to 2024, 6168 patients had available LVEF, NT‐proBNP, and outcomes data at 9 months following diagnosis of HFrEF (Figure 1); patient characteristics at the time of HFrEF diagnosis for those who had available data versus those with missing data are shown in Table S1, with patients in the former group having a slightly higher comorbidity burden. Baseline characteristics for the overall study cohort as well as 3 different LVEF subgroups are shown in Table 1, with baseline representing the time at 9 months following diagnosis of HFrEF. Among patients in the overall cohort, the median age was 68 years (IQR, 58–77), 62.8% were male, 43.0% had atrial fibrillation, the median BMI was 28.4 kg/m2 (IQR, 24.5–33.4), and the median eGFR was 59 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR, 39–80). A large majority of patients were on at least 2 GDMT medications at baseline, with 94.1% on a beta blocker, 84.5% on a renin‐angiotensin system inhibitor, 45.9% on an MRA, and 13.9% on an SGLT2 inhibitor.

Risk of Adverse Outcomes Based on LVEF and NT‐proBNP Measurements Taken Following Initiation of GDMT

In the overall study cohort, the median follow‐up duration for the composite outcome of HF hospitalization or all‐cause death was 15.0 months (IQR, 3.6–39.2), with 3522 patients (57.1%) experiencing the composite outcome during available follow‐up. Across all LVEF groups, patients with higher NT‐proBNP levels were, in general, less likely to remain free from the composite outcome during follow‐up compared with patients with lower NT‐proBNP levels (Figure 3). The adjusted 1‐year risk estimates of the composite outcome based on LVEF and NT‐proBNP strata are shown in Figure 4, with estimates adjusted for relevant covariates using a complete‐case approach (n=5811) and computed assuming full use of contemporary GDMT (i.e., use of a beta‐blocker, angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor). Higher NT‐proBNP levels were, in general, associated with higher risk of the composite outcome across all LVEF strata, with even mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels (125–1000 pg/mL) associated with incremental risk (Figure 4). Indeed, across all LVEF strata patients with an NT‐proBNP of 600 to 999 pg/mL were estimated to have a 5% to 10% higher absolute risk of the composite outcome compared with patients with an NT‐proBNP of 0 to 124 pg/mL (Figure 4). Moreover, patients who had an LVEF of ≥50% but an NT‐proBNP of ≥125 pg/mL were in fact estimated to be at higher risk of the composite outcome than patients with an LVEF of ≤40% but an NT‐proBNP of 0 to 124 pg/mL (Figure 4).

Comparing the Prognostic Value of LVEF Versus NT‐proBNP

Comparing a multivariable Cox regression model containing both LVEF and NT‐proBNP with univariable models containing either LVEF alone or NT‐proBNP alone revealed that both variables offered independent prognostic information with respect to the composite outcome (Table 2). However, NT‐proBNP contributed to model fit to a much greater degree than LVEF (Δχ 2: +1555 for NT‐proBNP versus +26 for LVEF), indicating a much stronger association between NT‐proBNP and the composite outcome than between LVEF and the composite outcome (Table 2). Moreover, the univariable model containing NT‐proBNP had a much greater ability to discriminate between patients based on risk compared with the univariable model containing LVEF (ΔC‐index, +0.129; 95% CI, 0.118–0.141), with no meaningful improvement in risk discrimination observed for the multivariable model containing both LVEF and NT‐proBNP (Table 2). A multivariable model in which both LVEF and NT‐proBNP were modeled using natural cubic splines to account for potential nonlinear relationships had better model fit compared with the original multivariable model, albeit marginally and without a corresponding meaningful improvement in risk discrimination (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective cohort study evaluating the combined prognostic value of follow‐up LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements taken following initiation of GDMT in a large contemporary HFrEF cohort revealed a couple of key findings. First, NT‐proBNP provided substantial incremental prognostic information beyond LVEF with respect to the composite outcome of HF hospitalization or all‐cause death, with higher NT‐proBNP levels associated with higher risk, even at mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels (125–1000 pg/mL). Importantly, this relationship was largely consistent across the spectrum of LVEF—even at values ≥50%—as well as across age, sex, atrial fibrillation status, BMI, and eGFR. Second, although both LVEF and NT‐proBNP provided independent prognostic information with respect to the composite outcome, NT‐proBNP had a much stronger association with the composite outcome and a much greater ability to discriminate between patients based on risk compared with LVEF. Indeed, LVEF provided only marginal prognostic value beyond NT‐proBNP. Taken together, these findings suggest that NT‐proBNP measurements may offer much greater prognostic value than LVEF measurements in patients with HFrEF who have initiated GDMT, with even mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels portending meaningful incremental risk.

Our study is among the first to date to provide rigorous quantitative comparison (eg, 1‐year risk estimates, risk contour maps, model statistics) between follow‐up LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements taken following initiation of GDMT in a large contemporary HFrEF cohort. Our analyses demonstrate that, although both follow‐up LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements offer independent prognostic information—which has also been suggested by previous findings 10 —NT‐proBNP may provide much greater prognostic value than LVEF. Indeed, even mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels (125–1000 pg/mL) appear to offer incremental prognostic information beyond LVEF. For example, across the spectrum of LVEF in our study cohort, patients with an NT‐proBNP of 600 to 999 pg/mL were estimated to have a 5% to 10% higher absolute 1‐year risk of the composite outcome compared with patients with an NT‐proBNP of 0 to 124 pg/mL. Importantly, our findings suggest that patients with fully recovered LVEF (≥50%) but persistently elevated NT‐proBNP levels (≥125 pg/mL) might in fact be at greater risk of adverse clinical outcomes than patients with persistently reduced LVEF (≤40%) but normalized NT‐proBNP levels (0–124 pg/mL). These findings are consistent with a previous retrospective cohort study that found that the trajectory of BNP (B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels was a stronger prognosticator of survival than the trajectory of LVEF in 1158 patients with HF with reduced or mildly reduced LVEF. 11 Additionally, a separate retrospective cohort study that included 9847 patients with HF from across the entire spectrum of LVEF found that, among all patients, the risk of adverse clinical outcomes was more strongly predicted by whether NT‐proBNP levels were elevated (>1428 pg/mL) than by which specific LVEF category a patient belonged to. 12 Moreover, a prior prospective cohort study consisting of 615 patients with HF found that the risk of adverse clinical outcomes was similar between patients with preserved LVEF and patients with reduced LVEF (LVEF >40% and LVEF ≤40%, respectively) at any given BNP level. 13

Upon initiating GDMT in patients with newly diagnosed HFrEF, clinicians often rely primarily on echocardiographic measurement of LVEF, as well as other imaging features (eg, LV dimensions and volumes, valvular function), to assess prognosis and guide further management. 1 , 14 Over the past few years, however, there has been growing recognition that imaging features such as LVEF, when considered alone, may not adequately detect subclinical myocardial dysfunction. 2 , 3 , 6 , 15 Indeed, even patients who achieve full recovery of LVEF (≥50%) still have a notable risk of relapse and adverse outcomes. 2 , 3 , 4 , 6 , 16 Our findings add to this growing body of literature by suggesting that LVEF alone does not sufficiently risk stratify patients with HFrEF, and that NT‐proBNP offers substantial incremental prognostic value across the spectrum of LVEF, including by identifying residual risk in patients with improved or recovered LVEF. Hence, our findings may make the case for routine measurement of NT‐proBNP levels following initiation of GDMT in patients with HFrEF to better inform prognosis and potentially guide further management. For example, patients who have achieved full recovery of LVEF (≥50%) but have persistently elevated NT‐proBNP levels (specifically ≥300–900 pg/mL) could potentially benefit from starting GDMT medications such as finerenone (a nonsteroidal MRA) or dapagliflozin (an SGLT2 inhibitor), as suggested by recent secondary analyses of clinical trials. 17 , 18 , 19 Notably, despite the null findings of the GUIDE‐IT (Guiding Evidence Based Therapy Using Biomarker Intensified Treatment in Heart Failure) trial, 20 , 21 reduction of NT‐proBNP levels to <1000 pg/mL with intensification of GDMT has been shown to improve clinical outcomes in patients with HFrEF, 22 , 23 , 24 albeit this has yet to be prospectively validated in patients with improved or recovered LVEF. Although achieving an even lower NT‐proBNP target goal (eg, 125 or 300 pg/mL) could theoretically further improve clinical outcomes, this also has yet to be prospectively validated. Nevertheless, our findings make the case that the overall therapeutic goal for patients with HFrEF should potentially entail normalization of not only LVEF but also NT‐proBNP.

Our findings also provide evidence to support current guideline recommendations that routine serial measurement of LVEF is not warranted in many patients with HFrEF, with repeat assessment of LVEF being recommended only in patients who have experienced worsening clinical status, have been recently started on therapeutic interventions expected to meaningfully affect cardiac function, or are being considered for device therapies or invasive procedures. 1 Given our observation that LVEF appears to offer only minimal prognostic value beyond NT‐proBNP in patients with HFrEF who have initiated GDMT, it can be argued that, in the absence of the aforementioned exceptions, routine serial assessment of NT‐proBNP may be sufficient, with routine serial assessment of LVEF potentially being unnecessary; granted, prospective studies may be needed to confirm whether this is true. In this context, it should be noted that echocardiography resources are limited and costly, whereas natriuretic peptide testing is more widely available and relatively low‐cost. 25 , 26

Our study additionally sheds light on the underutilization of natriuretic peptide testing in patients with HFrEF. The fact that roughly half of all patients (52%) with available LVEF data in our database had missing NT‐proBNP data suggests that clinicians may underappreciate the ability of natriuretic peptides such as NT‐proBNP to further risk stratify patients beyond LVEF.

Prospective studies are warranted to confirm our findings. Moreover, there may be justification for prospective studies assessing whether serial measurement of NT‐proBNP levels could serve as a feasible primary diagnostic strategy for monitoring disease course and guiding management in patients with HFrEF. 21 , 27 There may also be justification for clinical trials assessing whether achieving an NT‐proBNP target goal of even lower than 1000 pg/mL, such as 125 or 300 pg/mL, might be feasible and could improve clinical outcomes. Additionally, there is a need to better define appropriate NT‐proBNP cutoffs for assessing chronic HFrEF based on known effect modifiers such as age, sex, atrial fibrillation status, BMI, and eGFR. 8 , 28

The limitations of our study are as follows. Given the retrospective nature of this study, LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements were not obtained at standardized intervals and, consequently, a large proportion of patients had missing follow‐up LVEF or NT‐proBNP data at our time point of interest and were in turn excluded from the study cohort, potentially introducing selection bias. Although patients who were included in the study cohort appeared to have a slightly higher comorbidity burden compared with those who were excluded, the available baseline characteristics were overall relatively similar between both groups, albeit there could of course be important differences in unmeasured variables. Potential reasons for this large amount of missing data include the following: first, our health care system sees a high volume of referrals for HFrEF, with some follow‐up care often occurring at other hospitals; and second, individual providers may assess LVEF and NT‐proBNP at varying follow‐up times based on patient‐specific factors (eg, earlier assessment for patients with high‐risk features, delayed assessment for patients requiring extra time for GDMT optimization). Also owing to the retrospective design, we did not have granular GDMT data such as dosage data, thus precluding us from computing GDMT scores to assess optimization. We also cannot be definitively sure that every single patient in our cohort was initiated on GDMT by study baseline, as this was not an explicit inclusion criterion in our cohort. Nevertheless, we can at least be assured that a large majority of patients in our cohort were on at least 2 GDMT medications at baseline. Additionally, because we assessed LVEF and NT‐proBNP measurements at only a single time point, caution should be taken when extrapolating these results to other time points. In a similar vein, given the inherent limitations of this retrospective study, it is not possible to conclude based on our results that serial NT‐proBNP‐guided therapeutic strategies improve clinical outcomes; prospective studies are ultimately needed to determine whether this may be the case. Moreover, our analyses may not have adjusted for some relevant covariates (eg, use of device therapy, heart rhythm status at the time of NT‐proBNP measurement among patients with atrial fibrillation), which could potentially limit the validity of our findings. Lastly, our study was conducted using data from a single integrated health system in which patients had relatively high GDMT uptake, had access to tertiary care, and were predominantly White or Black, all of which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other patient populations and clinical settings.

CONCLUSIONS

Among patients with HFrEF who have initiated GDMT, NT‐proBNP may offer much greater prognostic value than LVEF with respect to adverse clinical outcomes, with LVEF appearing to contribute only minimal prognostic value beyond NT‐proBNP. Higher NT‐proBNP levels are associated with a higher risk of adverse clinical outcomes across the spectrum of LVEF, with even mildly to moderately elevated NT‐proBNP levels (125–1000 pg/mL) portending meaningful incremental risk. These findings suggest that NT‐proBNP can further risk stratify patients with HFrEF beyond LVEF, including by identifying residual risk in patients with improved or recovered LVEF, thereby potentially allowing for intensification of GDMT to improve clinical outcomes.
