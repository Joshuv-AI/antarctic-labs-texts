The association of the composite dietary antioxidant index with the risk of cardiometabolic disorders: findings from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study
Authors: Hamid Ahmadirad, Hossein Farhadnejad, Niloufar Saber, Mitra Kazemi Jahromi, Farshad Teymoori, Parvin Mirmiran, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectiveDietary antioxidants have been suggested to play a protective role against chronic diseases. This prospective study aimed to examine the association between the composite dietary antioxidant index (CDAI) and the risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension, and abdominal obesity.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 7357 adults aged ≥ 18 years who participated in the third and fourth phases of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 168-item food frequency questionnaire, and CDAI scores were calculated accordingly. Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity across quartiles of CDAI.ResultsDuring the follow-up period, the incidence rates were 12.5% for T2D (mean follow-up: 10.8 ± 3.64 years), 23.4% for hypertension (mean follow-up: 10.0 ± 4.09 years), and 33.5% for abdominal obesity (mean follow-up: 10.1 ± 4.29 years). After adjustment for age, sex, physical activity, energy intake, education, marital and occupational status, smoking, and outcome-specific baseline clinical variables, higher CDAI scores were associated with a lower risk of T2D (HR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.48–0.96; P for trend = 0.025), hypertension (HR = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.52–0.83; P for trend = 0.014), and abdominal obesity (HR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.42–0.68; P for trend < 0.001).ConclusionsHigher adherence to a diet with greater antioxidant capacity, as reflected by higher CDAI scores, may be related to a reduced risk of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity among Iranian adults.Keywords: Dietary index, Antioxidant, Type 2 diabetes, Hypertension, Obesity, Adults

Background

Cardiometabolic disorders represent a global public health challenge, as reflected by the rising prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension, and obesity [1]. These conditions impose a substantial financial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [2] and, as major cardiometabolic risk factors, contribute to increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. In 2022, the global prevalence of diabetes reached 13.9% among women and 14.3% among men [5]. The prevalence of hypertension was also estimated at 32% in women and 34% in men in 2021 [6]. Obesity prevalence is projected to increase from 14% in 2020 to 24% by 2035 [7]. In Iran, the prevalence of T2D, hypertension, and obesity has been reported as 10.8%, 26.2%, and 10.9%, respectively [8–10].

Both genetic and environmental factors contribute to the development of cardiometabolic disorders [11]. Among modifiable environmental factors, diet plays a central role in the prevention and progression of these conditions [12, 13]. Recent evidence highlights the potential role of dietary antioxidants in modulating the risk of chronic diseases, particularly T2D and hypertension [14, 15]. Antioxidant-rich diets may help reduce oxidative stress, a pathway implicated in the pathogenesis of T2D and hypertension, thereby potentially lowering disease risk [16, 17].

In 2004, Wright and colleagues introduced the composite dietary antioxidant index (CDAI) as a simple and cost-effective measure of overall dietary antioxidant capacity, based on the intake of six antioxidants: vitamins A, C, and E, selenium, zinc, and manganese [18]. Several studies conducted in developed countries have examined the association between CDAI score and the risk of T2D, hypertension, and obesity [16, 19–25]. Evidence suggests that higher CDAI scores are associated with a lower risk of T2D [16, 19], reduced risk of hypertension [20], and a lower odds of obesity [21–25]. However, no observational study has evaluated these associations in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, particularly in Iran.

Given the role of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity as major risk factors for cardiovascular disease, and considering the potential protective effect of higher CDAI scores, this study aimed to examine the association between CDAI and these cardiometabolic disorders among Iranian adults. This study addresses an important evidence gap by providing data from a population in which this relationship has not been previously investigated.

Materials and methods

Study population

This prospective cohort study was conducted within the framework of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), an ongoing population-based study designed to investigate non-communicable disease risk factors among residents of Tehran, Iran. During the third TLGS examination (2006–2008), 3568 of 12,523 participants were randomly selected for dietary assessment. In the fourth examination phase (2009–2011), dietary data were collected from 7930 newly enrolled participants. For the present analysis, participants with complete dietary data from either the third examination or newly recruited participants from the fourth examination were included.

Participants with implausible baseline energy intake (< 800 or > 4,200 kcal/day) were excluded (n = 225). Individuals who were excluded in the third examination due to implausible energy intake but had plausible intake data in the fourth examination (n = 98) were included, with dietary data from the fourth examination considered as baseline. After excluding newly enrolled participants from the fourth examination with implausible energy intake (n = 261), a total of 8845 participants with valid dietary data remained eligible for analysis.

For all analyses, participants aged ≥ 18 years were included (n=7357). Individuals with a history of myocardial infarction or stroke (n = 70), cancer (n = 16), or pregnancy or lactation (n = 185) at baseline were excluded. Participants with missing data for the outcome of interest or with the respective cardiometabolic condition at baseline were also excluded. Some participants met more than one exclusion criterion.

For the type 2 diabetes analysis, 6125 participants were eligible at baseline after excluding individuals with prevalent diabetes (n = 603) or missing diabetes-related data (n = 398). During follow-up, 312 participants were lost, resulting in 5,813 participants included in the final analysis (follow-up rate: 94.9%).

For the hypertension analysis, 5866 participants were included at baseline after excluding individuals with prevalent hypertension (n = 1,197) or missing blood pressure data (n = 82). After excluding 263 participants lost to follow-up, 5603 participants remained for analysis (follow-up rate: 95.5%).

For the abdominal obesity analysis, 4137 participants were included at baseline after excluding those with prevalent abdominal obesity (n = 2961) or missing waist circumference data (n = 125). During follow-up, 210 participants were lost, leaving 3927 participants for the final analysis (follow-up rate: 94.9%).

Dietary intake assessment

Dietary intake at baseline was assessed using a validated semi-quantitative 168-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) designed for the Iranian population [26], which was administered by trained and experienced nutritionists in face-to-face interviews. The interviews captured participants’ usual dietary intake over the previous year. Reported consumption frequencies were converted to daily intakes, and nutrient and energy intakes were calculated using the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) food composition tables. For traditional Iranian foods not available in the USDA database, nutrient values were obtained from the Iranian food composition table (FCT) [27].

CDAI calculation

The CDAI was calculated based on the method proposed by Wright et al. (18) to estimate overall dietary antioxidant capacity. The CDAI incorporates six antioxidant nutrients: vitamins A, C, and E, selenium, zinc, and manganese. Physical activity assessment

Physical activity was assessed using the Modifiable Activity Questionnaire (MAQ), which has been validated in the Iranian adult population [28]. Participants reported the frequency and duration of leisure-time and occupational physical activities performed during the previous year. Physical activity levels were expressed as metabolic equivalent task hours per week (MET-h/week).

Demographic, anthropometric, and clinical assessment

Demographic characteristics were collected at baseline using a pretested questionnaire, including age, sex, smoking status, educational level, medication use, and medical history. Anthropometric measurements were obtained at baseline by trained personnel following standard protocols. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated digital scale (SECA, model 707; Seca Corporation, Hanover, MD, USA), with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured to the nearest 1 cm using a stadiometer, with participants standing upright without shoes. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Waist circumference (WC) was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm at the midpoint between the lower margin of the rib cage and the iliac crest using a non-elastic tape, without applying pressure.

Biochemical and clinical measurements were performed at baseline. After a 12–14 h overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected between 07:00 and 09:00 h, processed within 30–45 min, and analyzed at the TLGS research laboratory using a Selectra 2 auto-analyzer (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, the Netherlands). Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was measured using an enzymatic colorimetric method based on glucose oxidase (intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation: 2.2%). A standard 2-h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was conducted using 82.5 g of glucose monohydrate (equivalent to 75 g anhydrous glucose), except in participants with known diabetes receiving glucose-lowering medication. Serum triglycerides (TGs), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods (Pars Azmun, Tehran, Iran). Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 1.6% and 0.6% for TGs, 2.0% and 0.5% for TC, and 2.0% and 0.5% for HDL-C, respectively.

Blood pressure was measured by trained staff after participants rested in a seated position for at least 15 min. Two measurements were obtained from the right arm using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer, with a minimum interval of 30 s between readings. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was defined by the first Korotkoff sound, and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) by the disappearance of Korotkoff sounds. The mean of the two measurements was used for analysis.

Outcome definitions

T2D was defined according to the American Diabetes Association criteria as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL following a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test, or use of glucose-lowering medication [29].

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication [30].

Abdominal obesity was defined as WC ≥ 88 cm in women and ≥ 102 cm in men [31, 32].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using histograms and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Baseline characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed variables, and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in baseline characteristics by outcome status were evaluated using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent two-sample t-tests for continuous variables.

Participants were categorized into quartiles according to their CDAI scores. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity across CDAI quartiles, with the lowest quartile as the reference category.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using plots of log(− log(survival)) versus log of survival time, and the assumption was considered satisfied as the lines in these plots were approximately parallel.

For T2D and hypertension, three models were fitted: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2, adjusted for age, sex, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), and total energy intake; and Model 3, further adjusted for education level, marital status, occupational status, smoking status, and baseline outcome-specific values (fasting plasma glucose for T2D and systolic blood pressure for hypertension).

For abdominal obesity, Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, physical activity, smoking status, total energy intake, and baseline WC; and Model 3 was further adjusted for education level, marital status, and occupational status.

Linear trends across CDAI quartiles were assessed by entering the median value of each quartile as a continuous variable in the Cox models. In all multivariable Cox models, baseline BMI (for T2D and hypertension) and baseline WC (for abdominal obesity) were included as confounders, given their strong predictive role for cardiometabolic outcomes and their close association with dietary patterns. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To minimize potential reverse causation, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding participants with less than 2 years of follow-up, regardless of outcome status. Cox proportional hazards models were then re-estimated using the same adjustment strategy as in the primary analyses.

Results

During follow-up, the incidence rates were 12.5% (724 incident cases) for T2D (mean follow-up: 10.8 ± 3.64 years), 23.4% (1,312 incident cases) for hypertension (mean follow-up: 10.0 ± 4.09 years), and 33.5% (1,315 incident cases) for abdominal obesity (mean follow-up: 10.1 ± 4.29 years).

Baseline demographic, anthropometric, and clinical characteristics of participants according to cardiometabolic disorder status are presented in Table 1. Participants who developed T2D were older and had higher BMI, WC, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TGs), total cholesterol (TC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) compared with those without T2D. They also had lower HDL-C levels, were less likely to have academic education, and were more likely to be married.

Compared with normotensive participants, individuals with hypertension were significantly older, more likely to be male and married, and had higher BMI, WC, FPG, TGs, TC, SBP, and DBP, but lower CDAI scores, HDL-C levels, and educational attainment.

Participants with abdominal obesity were more likely to be younger, female, employed, and married, and had higher BMI, WC, FPG, TGs, TC, HDL-C, SBP, and DBP. However, they had lower physical activity levels, were less likely to smoke, and had lower educational attainment compared with those without abdominal obesity.

For abdominal obesity, Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, physical activity, smoking status, energy intake, and baseline WC) showed a significant inverse association between CDAI score and abdominal obesity risk (HR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.42–0.69; P for trend < 0.001). This association persisted in Model 3 after additional adjustment for education, marital status, and occupation status, with a 47% lower risk of abdominal obesity observed across CDAI quartiles (HR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.42–0.68; P for trend < 0.001).

In sensitivity analyses excluding participants with less than 2 years of follow-up, the associations between CDAI and the risk of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity remained unchanged, with similar hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals across CDAI quartiles.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, higher CDAI scores, indicative of greater dietary antioxidant capacity, were linked to a lower risk of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity in Iranian adults independent of effects of potential confounding factors.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between CDAI and cardiometabolic disorders in the Middle East and North Africa region. Previous evidence on this topic has been largely derived from NHANES-based studies conducted in Western populations. Our findings are consistent with these reports, which have shown inverse associations between CDAI scores and the risk of T2D [16, 19] and hypertension [20], as well as lower obesity-related indicators, including BMI and WC [21–25]. The present results extend these observations to a non-Western population with distinct dietary patterns and lifestyle characteristics.

The observed inverse associations between CDAI scores and the risk of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity may be explained by the role of dietary antioxidants in reducing oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation, which are central to the development of cardiometabolic disorders [33]. Excess oxidative stress and inflammation contribute to insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and adipose tissue dysregulation, thereby increasing susceptibility to metabolic and vascular abnormalities [34]. Diets with higher overall antioxidant capacity, as reflected by the CDAI, may help attenuate these processes through combined effects on redox balance and inflammatory signaling [35].

In relation to T2D, improved antioxidant status may be associated with enhanced insulin sensitivity and preservation of pancreatic β-cell function [36, 37]. For hypertension, antioxidant-rich dietary patterns have been linked to improved endothelial function and vascular homeostasis, partly through modulation of nitric oxide bioavailability and inflammatory pathways [38–40]. The inverse association with abdominal obesity may reflect the influence of antioxidant-related dietary patterns on adipose tissue function, lipid metabolism, and chronic low-grade inflammation, partly through attenuation of oxidative stress [41].

The CDAI reflects the combined intake of multiple dietary antioxidants rather than the effects of individual nutrients. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as indicators of overall dietary antioxidant capacity. Given the observational nature of the study, causal relationships cannot be inferred, and further experimental and mechanistic studies are warranted.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first prospective investigations to evaluate the association between CDAI and the incidence of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity in the Middle East and North Africa region. The prospective design minimizes the risk of reverse causation and supports temporal inference. Additional strengths include the large sample size, extended follow-up, and the use of validated dietary and physical activity assessment tools adapted for the Iranian population. Finally, additional analyses without adjustment for baseline BMI and WC yielded similar results, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Some limitations should also be acknowledged. The CDAI is a population-standardized index, and its absolute values are not directly comparable across different populations. Thus, the results should be interpreted in terms of relative ranking within the study population rather than universal cut-off values. Although this limits direct generalizability, this approach captures real-world variability in dietary antioxidant intake and has been widely used in previous studies. In addition, the assessment of dietary intake and physical activity using questionnaires is subject to measurement error due to its reliance on participants’ memory, leading to inevitable recall bias. While we mitigated this by using validated instruments and conducting interviewer-administered assessments instead of self-reporting, some degree of measurement error is unavoidable and should be acknowledged. Nutrient and energy intakes were estimated primarily using the United States Department of Agriculture food composition tables because of incomplete Iranian food composition data, which may have led to some degree of misclassification. Although the prospective design and long follow-up reduce the likelihood of reverse causation, changes in dietary patterns during early or preclinical stages of disease cannot be entirely excluded. Moreover, the study population consisted of urban Iranian adults participating in the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to rural populations, other regions, or populations with different dietary patterns and sociodemographic characteristics. Finally, although a wide range of potential confounders was accounted for, residual confounding due to unmeasured or unknown factors remains possible, such as overall diet quality, dietary changes over time, and certain socioeconomic indicators.

Conclusions

The findings of our study suggest that higher CDAI scores may be associated with a lower risk of T2D, hypertension, and abdominal obesity. Further large-scale prospective studies with longer follow-up and in more diverse populations, particularly within the Middle East and North Africa region, are warranted to confirm these associations and to further elucidate the role of dietary antioxidants in cardiometabolic health.
