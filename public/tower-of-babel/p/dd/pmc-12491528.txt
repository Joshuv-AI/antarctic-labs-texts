Numerical investigation to improve thermal performance of a water-cooling PVT collector with novel cooling subchannels
Authors: Zainab Mohammed Sellab Al-Mamoori, Enes Kilinc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Increasing the thermal performance of PVT systems through cooling channels is a crucial concern to improve the efficiency of the PV panels by lowering their temperature. Recent studies on PVT systems’ cooling channels demonstrate notable improvements in both the design and number of the cooling channels. However, research on dimensional optimization of cooling channels to enhance flow and heat transfer characteristics with various subchannel dimensions and Re numbers is still lacking. In this study, PVT system models with four novel cooling subchannels are generated to investigate how subchannel dimensions affect the temperature distribution and the average temperature of the PV module, velocity distribution in the subchannels, pressure drop through the channels, and the figure of merit of the cooling channels with respect to varying Re numbers. Temperature distribution results indicate that the average temperature of the PV module decreases with increasing subchannel dimensions and increasing Re number. Therefore, the lowest average PV module temperature is obtained as 32.63 °C for the subchannel dimension of 20 × 20 mm2 as Case (4) which is 8% lower than the literature value indicating higher thermal performance for the PV modules. As a result, subchannel dimensions of the PVT system are optimized at 20 × 20 mm2 for Re number of 2275 in terms of the lowest PV module temperature and the lowest pressure drop.

Introduction

Many researchers worldwide are interested in studying renewable energy sources which don’t produce residues contributing to climate change and environmental pollution1. Photovoltaic (PV) panels convert solar energy, among the renewable energy sources, into electricity. Nowadays, many studies are being carried out to increase the thermal performance and accordingly the conversion efficiency of the PV panels2,3. The thermal performance of the PV panels is significantly impacted by the PV panel operating temperature4. Since an increase in temperature causes the PV panels to heat up and reduce conversion efficiency, maintaining the operational temperature of PV panels is crucial5. To prevent a reduction in conversion efficiency, overheating must be avoided. Airflow6air-flow through the porous medium7wind stream with metallic fins8jet impingement9liquid immersion10liquid/water11heat pipe12thermoelectric13phase change materials14–17microchannel18–20 and mist nozzle21 applications are used on the back side of PV panels using cooling channels, ducts or pipes which constitute the photovoltaic/thermal (PVT) systems to cool the PV panels and prevent overheating22.

PVT systems use PVT collectors to control the PV panel’s operating temperature and simultaneously generate electricity and recover heat energy from solar energy23. Many studies investigating the thermal and electrical performance of PVT systems have been carried out in the literature24,25. Mahdi et al.26 combined air and water cooling to generate a hybrid solar collector to enhance the thermal efficiency of the PVT collector. The combined PVT system improved the total thermal efficiency from 43.2 to 56.3% compared to the conventional system. Boumaaraf et al.27 combined the computational modeling and experimental analysis for a water-glazed PVT system and compared it to a typical PV panel. According to the findings, the PV panel’s thermal efficiency was 18.43%, whereas the PVT system’s efficiency could reach 74.20%. A new dual oscillating absorber copper pipeline flow that relies on the PVT collector was created by Misha et al.28 to increase the overall efficiency by computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation and experimental investigation. Results have shown that the highest thermal efficiency of the PVT system was 59.60%. Engin et al.29 created a numerical model in order to examine the electrical and thermal efficiency of a hybrid PVT collector. The electrical efficiency was increased by employing a thermal collector to lower the PV module’s temperature. By creating a new PVT system design, Rejeb et al.30 increased the PV collector’s electrical and thermal efficiency. According to the numerical results, when the suggested PVT collector is compared to the basic PVT system, it achieves higher electrical and thermal efficiency of 15.4% and 73%, respectively. In another study, Basuhaib et al.31 conducted an experimental study to investigate the effects of different parameters on the performance of PVT collectors in a tropical climate, in Malaysia. They specified that the best results were obtained at a flow rate of 0.5 LPM, with an outlet water temperature of 45.8 °C and a maximum thermal efficiency of 67.4%. Salvaraj et al.32 investigated front-flow cooling PV/T systems using the same PV panels. At a flow rate of 0.8 LPM, the maximum thermal efficiency for monocrystalline and polycrystalline PVT was 41.26% and 47.71%, respectively. The highest electrical efficiency is found in monocrystalline PVT, whereas the highest thermal efficiency is found in polycrystalline PVT. These studies indicate that the efficiency of the PV panels is enhanced by PVT collector system applications.

The PVT system investigations to increase their efficiency focus on the design of the PVT collectors. Rosli et al.33 carried out CFD simulations of different absorber tube designs for PVT collectors to compare their efficiencies. The findings indicated that the u-flow, serpentine, and spiral configurations of the PVT collector achieved peak thermal efficiencies of 21.02%, 22.62%, and 22.96%, respectively, at a mass flow rate of 0.005 kg/s. The u-flow design acquired the highest electrical efficiency of 11.78% among the three designs at a mass flow rate of 0.005 kg/s. At the same mass flow rate, the spiral, serpentine, and u-flow configurations indicated the highest total conversion efficiency of 34.63%, 34.29%, and 32.80%, respectively. Park et al.34 conducted a numerical and experimental study to investigate the effect of different pitch sizes on the thermal efficiency of a water-based serpentine PVT system. The results indicate that the thermal efficiency of the PVT system decreased from 80.9 to 27.6% for pitch sizes of 40 mm and 120 mm, respectively. Selimli et al.35 achieved a comparison of PVT collectors grounded in the first and second laws of thermodynamics. The findings revealed that the electrical, thermal, and overall conversion efficiency values for the PVT serpentine collector were 9.74%, 29.08%, and 38.82%, respectively, whereas the PVT channeled collector exhibited values of 49.68%, 10.19%, and 59.88%, respectively. Khelifa et al.36 introduced a numerical model to increase the efficiency of a PV panel for electricity generation in conjunction with a thermal system for water heating using a sheet and tube heat exchanger. The findings demonstrated that the fluid’s output temperature in the system attained 39 ℃ illustrating the produced thermal energy. Salem et al.37 conducted experimental research on the efficiency of the PVT collector utilizing an aluminum cooling plate featuring helical and straight channels. According to the findings, electrical efficiency increased from 17.70 to 38.4% in comparison to the non-cooled PV panel for 0.25 L/min to 1 L/min of the volume flow rate range. For both straight and helical channels, the thermal efficiency rose from 31.60 to 47.20% and 34.6–57.9%, respectively. The water pump’s efficiency in both configurations did not surpass 3.30% of the converted electrical power.

The studies in the literature indicate that the key issue to enhance PVT system efficiency is to decrease the PV panel temperature by examining how cooling channels affect the PV panel temperature and studying the PV module’s temperature distribution. For this purpose, Zhou et al.38 developed a three-dimensional finite element model to study the temperature distribution of a serpentine tube-equipped water-cooled PVT collector. Based on the outcomes, reducing the distance between the serpentine tubes is the most efficacious method to improve the consistency of temperature distribution on the PV module. The PV/T collector’s temperature, velocity, and pressure distributions were examined numerically by Yu et al.39 in relation to the grooves inside the cooling channels. Based on the findings, PV modules with recurrently enlarged grooves have a mean temperature that is approximately 4 K lower than those with smooth channels. Moreover, convective heat transport is improved by lengthening the groove. Shen et al.40 developed a numerical model for PVT collectors featuring several parallel cooling channels to assess the impact of these channels on the heat transfer performance of PVT collectors. The findings showed that an increase in the diameter ratio (D/d) of the channels leads to a reduction in the PV module’s temperature resulting in a more uniform temperature distribution. The figure of merit of the cooling channels has a diameter ratio of 4 exceeding that with a diameter ratio of 2 by 50%.

According to our pioneering investigation, increasing the thermal performance of PVT systems through cooling channels is a crucial concern to lower the PV module temperature and enhance their effectiveness. Contemporary research on cooling channels of PVT systems highlights significant advancements in their design and number of channels. However, there is still a lack of studies on the dimensional optimization of the cooling channels to optimize the flow and heat transfer characteristics with different channel dimensions with respect to different Re numbers. From this vantage point, the enhancement in heat transfer performance of cooling channels according to various channel dimensions and Re numbers is still restricted. This study addresses these gaps and contributes to the existing literature in terms of improving the thermal performance of PVT collectors by optimizing the channel dimensions and Re numbers. In line with these gaps, numerical models of four different dimensional cases for the PVT system’s cooling subchannels were applied to figure out their impact on the PVT system’s heat transfer achievement. As a result, the influence of subchannel dimensions and the Re number on the PV module’s temperature distribution, the PV module’s average temperature, velocity distribution in the subchannels, pressure drop through the channels, and the cooling channels’ figure of merit were discussed.

Methodology

Physical configuration

Four different cooling subchannel models were designed within the scope of this work to evaluate the effects of different channel dimensions on the PVT system’s heat transfer performance. The PVT system modeled in the study includes a glass cover, a photovoltaic module, an absorber plate, and a cooling channel constructed from two main channels that collect ten cooling subchannels between them as illustrated in Fig. 1. The dimensions of the model are 630 mm x 1305 mm in width and length, respectively, and the total thickness of the model is 11 mm without cooling channels. The main channels and the subchannels in the models are rectangular and the width and the length of the main channels are constant at 40 mm and 630 mm, respectively. However, the thickness of the main channels differs with respect to the dimensions of the subchannel. Subchannel dimensions varied as 10 × 10 mm212.5 × 12.5 mm215 × 15 mm2and 20 × 20 mm2 generating the four cases in this study as illustrated in Fig. 2. The dimensions of the subchannels were specified regarding the manufacturing of the PVT collector. The PVT collector can be manufactured using square and rectangular section tubes manufactured from circular copper pipes in the dimensions specified in this study. Copper tubes and distilled water used in the collector will prevent corrosion and maintenance. The tubes can be connected using copper solders to prevent leakage in the piping and joints and the manufactured collector can be easily integrated with electrical or thermal components due to its basic structure. Subchannel length and the channels’ wall thickness are assigned as 1225 mm and 1 mm, respectively. Copper is used for the cooling channels, while aluminum is used for the absorber plate. Numerical solution and boundary conditions

A three-dimensional PVT model is developed in this work to examine the PVT system’s thermal performance. Governing equations given in Eq. (1) to Eq. (5) are solved depending on the finite volume analysis by Ansys Fluent. The SIMPLE method addresses pressure-velocity coupling solutions43. The velocity and temperature fields are uncoupled in incompressible flows which means that the velocity components and pressure can be solved first using continuity and momentum equations given in Eq. (1) to Eq. (4) without using energy equation. When the velocity components and pressure are solved, temperature distribution can be obtained using the energy equation given in Eq. (5). Iterations are accepted as converged when the residuals decrease under 10−9.

Water serves as the coolant within the PVT system’s cooling channels. Steady, incompressible, and laminar flow is accounted for in the model since the Re number is varied as 920, 1200, 1520, 1600, 1850, and 2275 representing laminar flow. Water enters the cooling channels at 300 K temperature and atmospheric pressure is applied at the channel exit. The convection and radiation on the glass surfaces of the PVT collector are combined and applied as a constant heat flux of 1000 W/m2. The insulation boundary condition is applied on the lateral surfaces of the glass layer, PV layer, absorber layer and the cooling channel in Fig. 1. In addition, the bottom surfaces and the surfaces between the cooling channels in Fig. 2 are also defined as insulated boundaries. In terms of key assumptions and boundary conditions, the numerical PVT model generated in this study has also some limitations. It is known that the power generation using PVT systems is heavily affected by climate changes and seasonal effects. In a study, it has been concluded that the PVT system was more effective at solar irradiation of 500 W/m2 and above and the heat flux applied on the glass surface of the PVT system in this study is applied regardless of climate changes and seasonal effects.

Model validation and grid independence

The grid independence test is conducted to validate the precision of the numerical analysis results. The meshing of the model is modified by increasing the number of elements until it reaches less than 1% relative inaccurate results where the change is negligible. The PV module’s average temperature (Tpv) is investigated concerning the number of mesh components to specify the grid independence. Figure 3 demonstrates that as an increase in the quantity of mesh elements occurs, Tpv increases first, and then remains almost constant. According to the figure, the optimum number of the mesh elements is specified as 3,953,596 for the model to ensure exact results and reduce the calculation time.

Tpv values obtained from the numerical findings in this investigation are juxtaposed with the results of reference40 to verify the PVT model constructed in this study. Using the same intake and outlet configuration for the cooling channels, the same number of subchannels and the same physical parameters and boundary conditions as in reference40Tpv values are compared and validated. Figure 4 illustrates the average temperature results of the PV module for this work and the findings of reference40 according to the Re number. The maximum error of the results between the reference work and this work is obtained as 4.69% for the Re number of 1600 which is acceptable for the numerical analysis. Therefore, the numerical findings and the PVT model in this research are accordingly validated. The validation between the current research and the previous work translates the optimization of cooling subchannel dimensions in PVT systems to real-world applications with respect to different Re numbers. This translation allows researchers to perform potential experimental studies on the optimized results of this numerical study without testing all parameters which will save time and labor force and prevent problems occurring during the experimental studies. These experimental results will lead to the real-world applications of the PVT systems optimized in this study.

Results and discussion

Temperature distribution among the PV panel and the cooling channels

Figures 5 and 6 illustrate the impact of different subchannel dimensions of 10 × 10 mm212.5 × 12.5 mm215 × 15 mm2and 20 × 20 mm2 as Case (1), Case (2), Case (3), and Case (4), respectively, on the temperature distribution among the PV module and the cooling channels in the PVT collector. According to Fig. 5, the PV module’s temperature is lower at the fluid inlet region where it is higher at the outlet region, and it has maximum values at the outlet region of the subchannels. The cause is because the water’s temperature is constantly rising from inlet to outlet and the heat transfer rate decreases due to the temperature difference between the fluid and the cooling channel40. The temperature distribution among the cooling channels in Fig. 6 confirms this result. Figure 6 illustrates that the cooling channel temperature rises from the entrance to the outlet region across all cases. The PV module’s temperature distributions for all cases in Fig. 5 illustrate that the surface temperature of the PV module lessens as the subchannel dimensions boost, indicating the lowest PV module temperature in Case (4) compared to the other cases. In parallel to this result, the temperature of the cooling channels in Fig. 6 decreases as the subchannel dimension increases indicating that the cooling channel temperature is the lowest in Case (4) compared to the other cases. Besides having the lowest surface temperature for Case (4), the PV module’s temperature distribution in Fig. 5 exhibits further uniformity compared to the other cases. The reason for the lowest PV module temperature in Case (4) is the increased convection and conduction heat transfer area among the cooling channel and the water and among the cooling channel and the absorber layer as the subchannel dimension grows. The results indicate that the heat transfer rate among the cooling channels and the PV module in Case (4) is bigger than in the other cases.

Average PV module temperature

Figure 7 illustrates the average PV module temperature (Tpv) for varying subchannel dimensions with respect to the Re number ranging from 920 to 2275. It is recognized from the curves that Tpv decreased from 41.45 °C to 36.43 °C when the Re number is boosted from 920 to 2275 in Case (1). As the Re number rises from 920 to 2275, Tpv in Case (2) differs between 40.1 °C and 34.58 °C. For Case (3), Tpv varies between 38.53 °C and 33.61 °C upon raising the Re number from 920 to 2275. Tpv for Case (4) shifted between 37.28 °C and 32.63 °C in a decreasing behavior in the same range of Re number. The decrease in Tpv with increasing subchannel dimensions is attributed to the increment in the heat transfer area between the water and the cooling channel and between the cooling channel and the absorber layer which improves the heat transfer performance. In addition to the subchannel dimension, Tpv reduces with the increment in the Re number for all cases. Re number is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Re=\frac{\rho\:V{D}_{h}}{\mu\:}$$\end{document} where ρ, V, and µ represent the fluid’s density, velocity, and absolute viscosity, respectively, and Dh denotes the hydraulic diameter of the channel44. Accordingly, when the Re number increases, convection heat transfer also increases as the water velocity increases40. As a result, Tpv for Case (4) has the lowest value of 32.63 °C for the Re number of 2275 which is 8% lower than the value of Shen et al.40. Tpv in Case (4) is 10.4% lower compared to Case (1). Tpv results in this study are compared to other PV cooling techniques. Kumar et al.45 established an experimental PVT setup with a combined phase change material and obtained an average PV panel temperature of 33 °C with the combined phase change material which is close to Tpv for Case (4) in this study. In another study, Aspetakis et al.46 obtained an average PV panel temperature of 49 °C for the blank channel at Re number of 2000 and an average PV panel temperature of 32 °C for top V-baffles at Re number of 7000. When compared to this air-cooling PVT system, Tpv of 32.63 °C for Case (4) at Re number of 2275 in this study is much lower than the blank channel at Re number of 2000 and approximately the same with the top V-baffles at Re number of 7000. This result indicates that the same PV panel temperature is obtained with the optimized water-cooling channels in this study at lower Re numbers which also affects the pressure drop and decreases the pump power. When compared to the heat pipe PVT systems average PV panel temperature in this study remains lower than the study of Ma et al.47. In their study, they obtained an average PV panel temperature of 58 °C with 14 numbers of heat pipes under solar radiation conditions of 800 W/m2 and an inlet velocity of 2 m/s.

Velocity distribution inside the cooling channels

The velocity distribution inside the cooling channels in relation to different subchannel dimensions is depicted in Fig. 8. For constant inlet mass flow rate, the velocity gradient in all cases elucidates that when the subchannel dimensions increase, the velocity of water inside the subchannels decreases due to the increase in the cooling channels’ hydraulic diameter described as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{h}=\frac{4ab}{2\left(a+b\right)}$$\end{document}, where a and b denote the channel’s width and height, respectively48. When the hydraulic diameter of the cooling channels increases, the velocity inside the channels decreases in steady-state conditions. Additionally, the velocity of water at the main channel’s inlet and outlet is noticeably beyond that of the subchannels indicating a velocity gradient. Although the velocity gradient inside the subchannels is almost constant, Case (4) has a reduced velocity than other cases due to the bigger channel dimensions which leads to less flow resistance. The hydraulic diameter of the cooling channels and the velocity are inversely related; thus, an increase in the hydraulic diameter corresponds to a decrease in the velocity.

Pressure drop among the cooling channels

Figure 9 displays the pressure distribution among the main channels and the subchannels for different subchannel dimensions of 10 × 10 mm212.5 × 12.5 mm215 × 15 mm2and 20 × 20 mm2. In the figure, the pressure drop (ΔP) within the channels diminishes as the subchannel dimensions rise, attributable to the reduction in water velocity. According to the pressure distributions, the ΔP is the lowest for Case (4) among the cases, where the dimensions of the subchannels are the biggest. The velocity of water in Case (4) is smaller than that of other cases resulting in less ΔP. Conclusions

In this study, numerical models of four novel cooling subchannels of the PVT system are created to optimize the flow and heat transfer characteristics with different subchannel dimensions with varying Re numbers. The results contribute to the existing literature in terms of improving the thermal performance of PVT collectors by optimizing the channel dimensions and Re numbers as a gap in the literature.
