Asymptotic Confidence Interval, Sample Size Formulas and Comparison Test for the Agreement Intra‐Class Correlation Coefficient in Inter‐Rater Reliability Studies
Authors: Abderrahmane Bourredjem, Hervé Cardot, Hervé Devilliers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe agreement intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICCa) is a suitable statistical index for inter‐rater reliability studies. With balanced Gaussian data, we prove the explicit form of ICCa asymptotic normality (ASN), valid both with analysis of variance (ANOVA), maximum likelihood (ML), or restricted ML (REML) estimates. An asymptotic confidence interval is then derived and its performances are examined by simulation compared to the most commonly used methods, under small, moderate and large sample size designs. Then, we deduce sample size calculation formulas, for the number of subjects and observers needed, to achieve a desired confidence interval width or an acceptable ICCa value test power and give concrete examples of their use. Finally, we propose a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to compare two ICCa's from two distinct subpopulations of patients (or raters) and study by simulation its first order risk and power properties. These methods are illustrated using data from two inter‐rater reliability studies, one in physiotherapy with 42 patients and 10 raters and the second in neonatology with 80 subjects and 14 raters. In conclusion, we made recommendations to employ the proposed confidence interval for medium to large samples combined with the quantification of the minimal required sample size at the planning step, or the posterior‐power at the analysis step, using simple dedicated formulas. Furthermore, with sufficient sizes, the proposed LRT seems suitable to compare inter‐rater reliability between two patient subpopulations. Used wisely, this proposed methods toolbox can remedy common current issues in inter‐rater reliability studies.Keywords: agreement intra‐class correlation coefficient, asymptotic normality (ASN), balanced two‐way crossed random effect model, confidence interval (CI), likelihood ratio test (LRT), sample size

Background

Reliability and Agreement Intra‐Class Correlation Coefficient

In biomedical research, it is well known that the development of new measures is a major challenge in diagnosis, prognosis and health strategy assessments. Generally, measurement systems are based on a technological solution (e.g., in pathology, radiology and imaging), an interview grid (e.g., in clinical psychology) or a self‐questionnaire (e.g., in patient‐reported outcomes of pain, fatigue, depression, physical symptoms, and health‐related quality of life). All measuring tools are subject to errors and must be validated before being used. Measurement's reliability refers to its reproducibility when it is randomly repeated on the same subject and is a key metrological property. Repetitions are possible levels in a population such as human observers, research labs, multiple devices (meant to measure the same concept), or simply times (reflecting possible measurement circumstances).

For quantitative or ordered categorical outcomes, intra‐class correlation coefficients (ICCs) are the principal measurements of reliability [1, 2]. At least 10 kinds of ICCs are described in the literature depending on the design and the conceptual intent of the study (see Appendix C). Our article deals only with the ICC(2,1) of agreement, we referred to as intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICCa), described in the paragraph bellow. Indeed, in a typical inter‐rater reliability study, we assume that a random sample ofn subjects is rated byk raters. Concerning a sample of subjects, it must be representative of the population of all possible subjects who may have to be evaluated in practice. According to rater considerations, Shrout and Fleiss [1] described three different designs corresponding to three closed linear mixed models. From these, they derived three main ICC types, whose analysis of variance (ANOVA) estimators were noted ICC(1,1), ICC(2,1) and ICC(3,1). Indeed, there are situations where the rater can be seen as an abstract entity to some extent, and other situations where the rater must be a concrete entity. For example, when discussing the reliability of medical devices, our study will clearly be limited to those devices, and any reliability quantification will directly refer to them. In this case, we should use the ICC(3,1) with the design 3 in which each subject is measured by each of the samek raters which are assumed to be the only possible raters of interest. However, for example when we explore the reliability among radiologists testing a tumor malignancy, then we would like to have our sight over all radiologists who are potential examiners now or in the future. In this setting, two cases are possible. We could use the ICC(1,1) with the design 1 in which each subject is rated by a different set ofk raters, randomly selected from a larger population of raters. Since the design 1 is often difficult to implement, we should use the ICC(2,1) with the design 2 in which a unique random sample ofk raters is selected from the population of all possible raters, and each rater measures each subject, that is, each rater ratesn subjects altogether. Further, McGraw and Wong [3] give more details on these ICCs inference and distinguish two broad classes of reliability from an interpretation point of view: agreement (or concordance) and consistency (or coherence) between raters. Agreement concerns the fact that different raters assign the same score to the same subject, whereas consistency concerns whether the raters' scores to the same group of subjects are correlated [3]. From a mathematical point of view, as a ratio of variances, ICCs resemble other statistical parameters such as the Variance Partition Coefficients (VPC) whose interpretation is not directly linked to the inter‐rater agreement [4].

This work considers the setting of the agreement ICC, calledICCagreement in [5] and whose ANOVA estimator denoted by ICC(2,1) in [1] and ICC(A,1) in [3] (A for agreement and 1 because it is the reliability of a single rate). We will abbreviate this ICC to ICCa. This ICCa is derived from a crossed two‐way random effects model without interaction (the Case 2A in [3]) which corresponds to situations with one observation per patient and rater, where the interaction term is not identifiable (see Section 2.1 for a mathematical definition). This setting arises frequently in various application fields of biomedical research and with a considerable number of subjects and raters. Such recent inter‐reliability studies are for example: in physiotherapy [6] with 42 patients and 10 raters, in neonatology [7] with 80 subjects and 14 raters, in orthopedics [8] with 100 subjects and 18 raters, in pathology [9] with 170 subjects and 18 raters or in radiology [10] with 39 subjects and 29 raters. Data from the two studies [6, 7] will be used in the present article for illustrative purposes.

Indeed, ICCa is equivalent to a version of weighted kappa [2] and can be used with ordinal categorical data. Additionally, ICCa directly measures agreement in comparison to its counterpartICCconsistency, which measures consistency and not agreement in major practical cases [3, 5]. On the other hand, despite its robustness, the concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) measures the relational agreement among fixed raters [11] and does not allow generalization of the reliability results to the entire rater population, as does the ICCa. For all these reasons, ICCa is recommended in the regulatory environment [12].

ICCa Thresholds and Confidence Interval Methods

ICCa values vary from zero (no agreement) to one (perfect agreement). Shrout [13] reported the following revised agreement ranges: values of 0–0.10 represent virtually none; 0.11–0.40 slight; 0.41–0.60 fair; 0.61–0.80 moderate; and 0.81–1.00 substantial. However, existing ICCa estimators are known to be biased [1]. In particular, the agreement cut‐offs must be compared to the lower bound of the 95% ICCa confidence interval (CI) and not to its point estimate [14].

Several confidence intervals (CIs) have been proposed for ICCa. Fleiss and Shrout (F&S) [15] proposed a large sample CI based on a Satterthwaite's two‐moment approximation to a linear combination of chi‐square sum of squares. It is a two stage first order approximation, which is asymptotically valid under the data normality assumption. This method was implemented in the most popular statistical software (R, SAS, SPSS, Stata …) and remains one of the most used in practice. Another ICCa CI is given by the modified large sample (MLS) approach proposed by Cappelleri and Ting [16]. Authors show by simulation that the F&S method is too liberal and the MLS method provides generally at least the stated coverage on two‐sided bounds. One year later, Tian and Cappelleri [17] applied the generalized confidence interval (GCI) approach to the ICCa. The GCI method is based on the process of generalized inference for testing hypotheses when exact methods do not exist. This method did not offer a closed formula for the ICCa confidence bounds, which are thus approximated using Monte Carlo simulations. Authors conducted a simulation study to investigate the coverage probabilities of the GCI approach relative to the MLS one. For a two‐sided interval, both methods generally maintain the stated confidence coefficient under the data normality assumption, and the two approaches provide comparable results in most cases. Nevertheless, the GCI method is computationally much more intensive.

The most recent work on ICCa CI methods is by Ionan et al. [18], who evaluate the contribution of a Bayesian method with a non‐informative prior distribution (NIB) to the MLS and CGI methods. The authors showed that no method works well when the number of subjects OR raters is limited. More specifically, the MLS and GCI methods outperform the NIB method when one of the factors has a limited number of levels and the data are normally or nearly normally distributed. Moreover, in the more applied literature, many papers report the inadequacy of small‐sized reliability studies (e.g., [19, 20]) and recommend the use of large samples. These observations have led us to propose an ICCa CI asymptotic methods with specific formulas to quantify the minimal required size.

ICCa Sample Size Formulas, Comparison Tests, and the Present Work

Given the complex forms of the existing ICCa CI bounds, there is no straightforward way to calculate analytic sample size formulas based on these CIs. Saito et al. [21] used a first order Taylor approximation oflog(ICCa) estimate variance, and they proposed to determine the sample size that minimizes this variance approximation. This requires further approximations using optimization algorithms. They used simulation to compare the efficiency of different designs but did not present any explicit formula to determine sample size at the planning step.

Furthermore, a recent systematic review of agreement studies [22] points out that, despite the importance of a sample size calculation, two‐thirds of agreement studies did not provide justification. Currently, in a reference software of sample size calculation such PASS 2023 (http://www.ncss.com/software/pass/pass‐documentation/#Correlation), no method is proposed for the ICCa. The only available methods for the ICCs concern the ICC(1,1) (issued from the one‐way random effect model) or the ICC(3,1) and CCC (issued from the two‐way mixed effect model) and, in the best case, studies using the ICCa are wrongly planned, based on these formulas. This is mainly due to the complexity of the existing methods for the ICCa sample size calculation. Our paper proposes a simple alternative that could easily be used in practice. To our knowledge, no explicit form for the ICCa asymptotic normality (ASN, a law convergence form, defined mathematically in Section 2.4) has been established so far. Thus, we aim to develop this approach, which will offer a CI centered around the ICCa estimate and simplify the derivation of sample size formulas.

Finally, in many studies, as a secondary objective, we need to compare ICCa's from two distinct subpopulations of patients or raters (e.g., [6, 8]). The aim, for example, is to verify if the agreement is better among experimented raters (vs. novice ones) or for younger patients (vs. elderly ones). Here again, to our knowledge, no statistical test has been published for the ICCa. In practice, such analyses rely on marginal estimates and CIs to answer this question, which is not sufficient. We therefore propose an appropriate likelihood ratio test (LRT) to accomplish this task.

To summarize, the present paper is organized as follows: Section 2.1 shows the mathematical definition for ICCa from the balanced two‐way crossed random effects model and its hypothesis, Section 2.2 details the ICCa estimation procedures: ANOVA, maximum likelihood (ML) and restricted ML (REML), Sections 2.3 and 2.4 (with their proofs in Appendices A and B) present the explicit form of ICCa ASN, valid both with ANOVA, ML and REML estimates. An asymptotic confidence interval is then derived in Section 2.5 and simple sample size calculation formulas are proposed in Section 2.6. In Section 2.7, we introduce an appropriate likelihood ratio test (LRT) to compare two ICCa's issued from distinct subpopulations of patients (or raters). In Section 3.1, we examine by simulation the performance of our CI in relation to the two most commonly used CI methods (F&S and MLS). In Section 3.2, numerical examples of sample size determination are given. In Section 3.3, we studied by simulation the LRT first order risk and power. Real applications are presented, using data from two inter‐rater reliability studies [6, 7] in Section 3.4. Finally, Section 4 presents a discussion of the results, practical recommendations and ideas for future work.

ICCa Comparison Between Two Distinct Subpopulations of Patients or Raters

In many studies, we need to compare ICCa's from two distinct subpopulations of patients or raters (e.g., [6, 8]). The aim, for example, is to verify if the agreement is better among experimented raters (vs. novice ones) rating the same patients or for younger patients (vs. elderly ones) rated by the same raters.

Let us focus on the raters (for the patients, the reasoning will be similar, we will come back to this at the end). In this case, we have two distinct subpopulations of raters: P1 and P2, rating the same patients. In our sample, raters representing P1 will be indexed byj from1tok1 and those representing P2 byj fromk1+1 tok1+k2, andk=k1+k2 is the total number of raters participating in the study. Let (M2) be the two‐way random effect model representing these data. (M2) is identical to the (M1) model, except that:

b1,…,bk1 will have a varianceB1 while,b1+k1,…,bk will have a varianceB2

Thereby, we will deal with two ICCa's,ρ1 quantifying agreement among P1 raters andρ2 among P2 raters, defined by:

The objective is to test the following hypothesis:H0:ρ1=ρ2versusH1:ρ1≠ρ2.

We can easily see that this formulation is equivalent to:H0:B1=B2versusH1:B1≠B2.

UnderH0, the two agreements are the same, corresponding toρ1=ρ2=ρ=AA+B+E whereB=B1=B2 is the common inter‐rater variability. In this case, the two‐way random effect model representing the data will be identical to the (M1) model.

UnderH1, the (M2) model will be different than (M1), but (M1) is nested in (M2). We can then use the famous likelihood ratio test (LRT) comparing these two nested models (H0: reduced model (M1) with the same inter‐rater variabilityB=B1=B2, and H1: full model M2 with two different rater variance components according to the rater subpopulation).

Results

Comparative Simulation Study of the ICCa CI Coverage and Width

A simulation study has been conducted to evaluate the ICCa ASN CI recovery rate and mean width. Our ASN based method was compared to the F&S and MLS methods [15, 16]. The GCI method was not included since it was shown equivalent to MLS in terms of recovery rate and CI width but computationally much more intensive [17]. The NIB method was not included since it does not improve the MLS [18]. Five rectangular designs (i.e., withn>k) were studied: small sample size withn=30,k=5, moderate sample size withn=40,k=10 thenn=60,k=15 and large sample size withn=115,k=15 thenn=150,k=15. Indeed, in practice, the number of repetitionk rarely exceeds 15 as the used devices are often costly and raters are usually practitioners who have limited time for research activities. Nevertheless, to assess the theoretical contribution of a squared design withn=k (c=1), five parallel designs were added withn=k=10, 20, 30, 40 and 50. So that, the total observation number (nk) for each rectangular design is equivalent to one parallel squared design (e.g.,20∗20=40∗10=400,30∗30=60∗15=900,40∗40=1600 ∼ 1725=115∗15, and50∗50=2500 ∼ 2250=150∗15). Based on the Shrout [13] reliability thresholds, ICCa values of interest were 0.55 for fair agreement, 0.65 then 0.75 for moderate agreement and 0.85 for substantial agreement. Without loss of generality, the total variance was set to20=A+B+E so that values ofA=11, 13, 15, and 17 yields ICCa = 0.55, 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85 respectively. For each case, 1000 data sets are simulated under the normal distribution for (ai), (bj) and (eij). The simulations were conducted using the R software (version 4.1.0). The F&S method was implemented using the R psych::ICC() function (psych package version 2.1.6). The MLS method was implemented as in Ionan et al. [18]. The ASN‐based method was implemented easily from the two‐way crossed random effect model variance components ANOVA estimates using the anova(lm()) R base function. The ML and REML estimates were also implemented using the lme4::lmer R function and examined to verify the results similarity. Programs are available from the corresponding author upon request. For each simulation, a 95% bilateral ICCa CI was derived using the three methods (F&S, MLS and ASN). Overall the 1000 simulations, the recovery rate (or coverage probability, estimated by the percentage of simulations when the CI contain the true ICCa value) and average interval width (upper bound–lower bound; mean over the 1000 simulations) were calculated.

Results are shown in Table C2 for the five rectangular designs and in Table C3 for the five parallel squared designs. The F&S method recovery rate is around 90% for all designs including whenn=150,k=15 orn=k=50. The MLS method recovery rate is about 95% for all designs but with a mean width systematically larger than that of the ASN method. For the five rectangular designs (withn>k andk<=15), our ASN method performs better than the F&S in all cases, except in the small size design (n=30,k=5) where the two methods were close with a coverage rate about 90%. Hence, our ASN method is not recommended for a small sample size (at least ifn<=30 ork<=5). For the moderate sample size, the coverage rate of our ASN method is about 93% with a mean width lower than the MLS method. The expected coverage of 95% is closely approximated forn=115,k=15 and achieved forn=150,k=15, still with a mean width lower than the MLS method. The five parallel squared designs (withn=k) would improve neither the recovery rate nor the CI width, except in the case ofn=k=50 (nk=2500) which yields results equivalent to the design withn=150 andk=15 (nk=2250). Nevertheless, a design withn=k=50 is very rarely possible in practice.

Simulation Study of the ICCa LRT

As far as we know, the proposed ICCa LRT has never been evaluated yet in inter‐rater reliability studies. We conducted a simulation study to explore its trueα level and power in the case of comparing two ICCa between two subpopulations of patients rated by the same raters. Hypotheses are formulated as:H0:A1A1+B+E=A2A2+B+EversusH1:A1A1+B+E≠A2A2+B+E (which is equivalent to:H0:A1=A2versusH1:A1≠A2 (when the ICCa is not equal to 1).

For theα risk, we use the same scenarios as for the CI: five rectangular designs and five squared parallel designs with ICCa = 0.55, 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85. For each case, 1000 data sets were simulated underH0:A1=A2=A with patient subsamples of equal sizesn1=n2=n/2 (except wheren=115, thenn1=58 andn2=57). Then, for each design, the power is evaluated to show the three following differences:A1=17≠A2=9,A1=17≠A2=7 andA1=17≠A2=5.5 corresponding to the cases were:ρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.75,ρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.7 andρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.65. Also, for each case, 1000 data sets are simulated underH0:A1=A2=A with patients subsamples of equal sizesn1=n2=n/2 (except wheren=115, thenn1=58 andn2=57). The nominal level of the test rejection rule was fixed at5%. The simulations were conducted using the SAS software (version 9.4). The LRT was implemented using the SAS proc Mixed. Programs are available from the corresponding author upon request. Results are shown in Table C6 for theα risk (α LRT columns) and in Table C7 for the power (LRT power columns).

The results presented in Table C7 show that the empirical test levels are all close to 5%, including in the case of small samples (n=30,k=5, andn=k=10). The levels provided by the square designs (n=k) are less good than in the cases wheren>k, except for large samples (fromn=40,k=40). The power of the test depends clearly on the sample size and on the ICCa difference between the two subpopulations. In the case of square designs (n=k), the power is smaller except whenk=n=50 (n1=n2=25) and the difference to detect is large (ρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.65). The power of the rectangle designs is always better. This power is satisfactory for a small difference (ρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.75) whenk=15 andn=150 (n1=n2=75), for a medium difference (ρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.70) fromk=15 andn=115 (n1=58,n2=57), and for large difference (ρ1=0.85≠ρ2=0.65) fromk=15 andn=60 (n1=n2=30). For the ICCa LRT, we do not have to decrease the nominal level to less than 5% because this will not improve the true test level (which is already close to 5%) but will considerably deteriorate the power.

Real Data Analysis

We illustrate the proposed ICCa ASN CI, sample size and posterior power formulas and the ICCa LRT on data from the following two inter‐rater reliability studies. The analyzed datasets are available from their corresponding authors on reasonable request. In these illustrative examples, we have used the data at our disposal, which may suffer from an insufficient number of patients or raters. Other data in the literature are more suitable for the methods developed in the present article, such as: in orthopedics [8] with 100 subjects and 18 raters, in pathology [9] with 170 subjects and 18 raters, in endoscopy [32] with 633 subjects and 22 raters, or in radiology [10] with 39 subjects and 29 raters.

Hermet et al. Study (BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders, 2018)

The aim of this study [6] was to assess the agreement among physiotherapists in rating patient performance during exercises recommended for chronic low‐back pain (LBP). Forty‐two patients were videotaped when performing exercises recommended for LBP. Among them, 25 were doing strengthening exercises and 17 were doing stretching exercises. Then, 10 physiotherapists viewed the 42 videos preceded by clinical instructions and rated patient performance from zero (worse performance) to ten (excellent performance) by considering the position and duration of the contraction or stretching. ICC estimates were calculated based on a single measurement, consistency, two‐way mixed‐effects model (ICC (3,1)). This ICC considers observers as fixed effects and does not permit a generalization of the estimations into the total observer population. The confidence intervals were computed using a cluster‐bootstrap technique, resampling the patient vectors as implemented in the “icc” function of the psy R package software. This technique is justified in the case of the ICC(3,1), since observers are fixed effects [33, 34]. This is not the case for ICCa where the observers are random effects. No test was performed to compare ICCs between the two patient groups (strengthening vs. stretching exercise). The estimated ICC reported in this paper is 0.48 [95% CI 0.33, 0.56] but was better for stretching exercises (0.55 [0.35, 0.64]) than strengthening exercises (0.42 [0.20, 0.52]).

For ICCa, ANOVA estimations with F&S 95% confidence intervals are 0.46 [95% CI 0.35, 0.60] overall, 0.52 [0.35, 0.73] for stretching exercises and 0.36 [0.23, 0.55] for strengthening exercises. The ASN 95% CIs are [0.35, 0.57] overall, [0.36, 0.69] for stretching exercises and [0.23, 0.498] for strengthening exercises. F&S CI is about 4% wider overall, 13% for stretching exercises and 16% for strengthening exercises. The Bootstrap interval lengths are very close to the ASN ones. However, their bounds are shifted to the left relative to the ASN intervals, corresponding to lower ICC estimates. New analyses are detailed in Table C8.

For the LRT comparing ICCa between strengthening and stretching exercises, p‐value was of 0.08, indicating a slightly significant difference between the two patients groups. This borderline LRT result may be due to a lack of power since there were only 25 patients in the strengthening group versus 17 in the stretching group.

Concerning the overall power of this study, withρ^=0.46, an estimatedû=0.107=0.372/3.479=B^/Â andĉ=n/k=42/10=4.2, the posterior‐power is equal to 0.72 forH0:ρ=0.6 and 0.19 forH0:ρ=0.4. Evidently, we need more power to prove a fair agreement. Nevertheless, given the relatively small range of ICCa acceptable values, our posterior‐power formula seems to be sensitive to the hypothesis specification.

Brenne et al. Study (PLoS ONE, 2023)

In this inter‐rater reliability study [7], 14 raters assessed the respiratory distress of preterm infants using the Silverman and Andersen index (SA index). Ten nurses, one neonatologist and three senior consultant neonatologists with more than two years of experience scored 80 videos. The scores suffered from missing values due to the difficulty for junior raters to score certain items of the SA index especially the nares dilation and expiratory grunting. Even if the REML method has the potential to handle unbalanced or missing data [31, 35] we have remained faithful to the ANOVA method used by the authors and we have analyzed the SA3 score (total score without the items nares dilation and expiratory grunting) presented in the article with a negligible rate of missing values (6%). Authors reported an ICCa estimated at 0.32 with an F&S CI of (0.21 to 0.44). They concluded a poor inter‐rater reliability and recommended a formal raters training to improve it.

We propose here to re‐analyze these data using our methods. For a purely illustrative purpose, we compare the reliability of the three senior neonatologists to the other 11 raters. However, it is important to note that the subpopulation of senior neonatologists is under‐represented with only three raters, which is not adapted to our LRT as seen in its power simulation study.

The ASN 95% CIs that we computed are [0.206, 0.415] for all raters, [0.373, 0.595] for the senior neonatologists and [0.181, 0.421] for less‐experienced raters. This is very close to the MLS and F&S CI, except for the senior neonatologists where the MLS is about 94% wider than the ASN CI, which is not a minor difference. In this case, the F&S interval higher bound is lightly shifted to the right, relative to the ASN interval, corresponding to higher expected values for the ICCa. Results are detailed in Table C9.

For the LRT, the p‐value was of 0.126, indicating that the observed difference between senior neonatologists and less‐experienced raters is not statistically significant. This LRT result may be due to the small number of raters in the senior neonatologists group, which supports the authors recommendation of raters training to improve the inter‐rater reliability.

In the senior neonatologists subpopulation of raters, the estimatedu was ofû=0.213=0.186/0.874=B^/Â. This is useful to plan a novel inter‐rater reliability study on this raters population. For a ratioĉ=n/k=6, an expected ICCa ofρ=0.61 and a required confidence interval widthL=0.2, it is needed to include 72 subjects and 12 raters. Discussion and Conclusions

Measurement reproducibility is a key metrological property and ICCa is an essential parameter for its assessment. Nevertheless, small scaled inter‐rater reliability studies are inadequates especially when the sample size is arbitrary [18, 19, 20, 22]. It is therefore necessary to provide the means in terms of number of patients and raters to carry out these studies. The proposed methods in this article remain asymptotic but are accompanied by simple formulas to determine the minimal required size.

The current paper confirms the asymptotic equivalence of the ANOVA, ML, and REML methods for ICCa inference. This asymptotic normality could be verified by simulation on finite samples, as shown by [4] for the finite sample distribution of the Variance Partition Coefficients (VPC), which is a statistical parameter similar to the ICC.

The ICCa ASN CI has a better coverage than the popular method of F&S and is more accurate (provide a shorter CI) than the MLS method. This can be explained by the fact that F&S involves two stage approximation and the wideness of the MLS CI was already reported [16, 18]. Thus, in view of our simulations results, we suggest the following recommendations: do not use ASN CI for small samples (n<30 andk<10) where the MLS remains the best method; for moderate sample size (30<n<100 and10<=k<=15), the MLS remains preferable but we can use the ASN CI with a nominal level at 96% or 97% (α=4% or 3%) and take the most accurate interval (the shorter) between MLS and ASN. For large samples (n>100 andk about 15), the ASN achieves the required coverage rate and has the advantage of being more accurate (shorter) than the MLS. ICCa ASN CI requires a large number of patients (n) and also a large number of raters (k). Ifn=k (squared design), our simulations show that the asymptotic level is achieved betweenn=k=40 to 50. Nevertheless, in practice,k is limited andk<n (rectangular designs). In this case, our simulations show that largen values (n>100) could compensate limitedk values (k=15). It seems thatn is more important thank, becausen reflects the convergence speed of the inter‐patient variability, which is present both in the numerator and in the denominator of the ICCa, unlikek, which reflects the convergence rate of the inter‐rater variability, present only in the ICCa denominator.

The ICCa ASN‐based method also has the advantage of being provided with simple formulas to estimate the minimal required sample size (to achieve the confidence interval width or an acceptable ICCa value test power) at the planning step or the posterior‐power at the analysis step. At the planning step, for the desired fraction betweenn andk, the optimal fraction could not be determined analytically. Indeed, as noted in Section 2.5, the ICCa asymptotic variance takes the form of2nρ41ρ−12+nku2=2ρ41n1ρ−12+1ku2. This is a sum of two terms. The first has an1/n order and the second is in1/k. Thus there is no way to minimize this variance with respect ton andk or deduce an optimal finite sample fractionĉ=n/k. Nevertheless,1ρ−1=u+EA>u. So that the weight of1/n is more important than that of1/k. In other words, ICCa estimation precision is increased more by increasingn than by increasingk. Moreover, in terms of confidence interval coverage probability and the comparison test alpha risk and power, under all scenarios, our simulations show that takingĉ between 4 and 10 (corresponding to the case wheren>k, qualified by rectangular designs) performs systematically better thanĉ=1, qualified by squared designs withn=k (see Sections 3.1 and 3.3). Furthermore, takingĉ<1 (i.e.,n<k) is not common in practice since the raters are usually practitioners who have limited time for research activities and it would be difficult to have more raters than patients. For all these reasons, we recommend choosingĉ>1, for example from 4 to 10, according to the investigator's constraints, while at the same time ensuring a raters numberk between 10 and 15, which is a realistic number of raters and a range that could be used in practice. What's more, given the asymptotic nature of our methods and the practical constraints, especially for the raters numberk, we advise retaining designs with the maximal possiblek, then taking the correspondingn>30. As shown in our examples, this is quite possible in practice with some reasonable assumptions. Nevertheless, the sample size formula for a test power needs to specify both the value ofρ0 andρ˜1 (whereρ˜1 is a specific underlying value of ICCa under H1), which may be difficult to choose. Thus, the approach based on CI width could be less questionable.

There are several studies where the main (and often the only) objective is to assess the inter‐rater reliability (see e.g., [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]). Thus, in such inter‐rater reliability studies, where the primary objective is to quantify the inter‐rater agreement with an accurate estimation under design 2, the sample size should be based on the ICCa. In other cases, where the inter‐rater reliability is not the main objective of the study (for example in studies validating the use of quantitative criteria in diagnostic procedures, for which sensitivity and specificity can be used in relation to a gold standard), it is not the required precision for an ICC estimation that should be the main concern for the sample size calculation.

To compare two ICCa's obtained in distinct subpopulations of patients rated by the same raters (or raters rating the same patients), our LRT is the first of its kind proposed in this context. This test seems conservative enough, and has no problem with theα risk, but rather with the power. It is therefore necessary to be careful with the non‐significance of this test, especially when the number of subpopulations is small. This is rather the case when comparing two subpopulations of raters. The ICCa LRT is therefore recommended with rectangular designs (n>k) when comparing two subpopulations of patients.

Finally, it is worth keeping in mind that our simulations may not be fully exhaustive. The normality deviation, where the MLS method failed [18], deserves to be investigated in light of the work of Ionan et al. [18]. This could be in favor of the ASN approach since the REML estimator is known to be robust to the non‐normality of data [36, 37]. With unbalanced data or with missing values, the ML and REML methods are recommended, especially if the unbalanced pattern is severe [31, 35]. This may be another advantage for our ASN ICCa CI and LRT methods but it should be confirmed by a full simulation study. Moreover, sample size formulas could be used as in Saito et al. [21] to compare optimal design allocation patterns, and more scenarios should be detailed for the LRT simulations. Furthermore, the ICCa ASN result gives a simple expression for its asymptotic variance. A stabilization transformation of this variance could improve the properties of the confidence interval on small samples, especially if this transformation is accompanied by a suitable resampling strategy for the two‐way crossed random effect model. This point was already shown for the ICC(1,1) with the one‐way random effect model [33, 34]. Thus, more research is needed on variance stabilizing transformation and resampling procedures for the ICCa and this article gives a solid foundation to get there.
