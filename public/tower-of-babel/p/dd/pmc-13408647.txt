Cytotoxic effect of 3D-printed and thermoformed clear aligner materials on human oral fibroblasts: An in vitro study.

- Korean J Orthod 2025;55:477-486

With the growing adoption of clear aligner therapy and the emergence of direct three dimensional (3D) printing as an alternative to conventional thermoforming workflows, ensuring the biocompatibility of newly introduced printable resins has become increasingly important. Because aligners are medical devices that remain in close contact with the oral mucosa for prolonged periods, even subtle material- and exposure-dependent cytotoxic responses may carry clinical relevance. In this context, the authors’ systematic comparison of three commercially available 3D-printed aligner resins with a widely used thermoformed material, using artificial-saliva conditioning and human oral fibroblast-based CCK-8 assays, is timely and meaningful. I appreciate the authors’ valuable contribution and would like to raise the following questions.

Q1. The authors appropriately noted that the static immersion model lacks physiological rinsing and mechanical processes as a limitation. However, clinical aligner therapy essentially involves intermittent removal for meals and oral hygiene, creating cycles of exposure and non-exposure that may facilitate mucosal recovery. Considering this intermittent usage, I would like to ask the authors’ perspective on whether the current static model might overestimate the cytotoxic risk compared to the dynamic clinical environment. Furthermore, I am curious how they define the scope of clinical applicability and the specific limitations of their findings in this context.

Q2. The study reported no significant difference in cytotoxicity between the 7-day and 14-day immersion periods. Under a static immersion model, however, this may reflect an early plateau in elution rather than true temporal material stability. Notably, Sagen et al.1 reported that the elution of methacrylate monomers in artificial saliva peaked at 24 hours. In this context, do the authors interpret the non-significant difference as reflecting inherent material stability or methodological saturation, and how does this affect the scope of the conclusions regarding 7-day versus 14-day clinical wear protocols?

Q3. In this study, the control was complete culture medium, whereas the test conditions used artificial-saliva extracts at final concentration ranging from 10% to 30% in culture medium. This design may introduce confounding effects related not only to material-derived leachables but also to the artificial saliva-medium mixture itself, including differences in ionic composition, osmolality, and pH, as well as medium dilution. Would the authors agree that a concentration-matched vehicle control consisting of artificial saliva mixed with culture medium at the same ratios but without material exposure could have strengthened the attribution of cytotoxicity to material leachables, and how might its absence affect the interpretability of the conclusions?

A1. We thank the reader for raising this important point and for emphasizing the differences between static in vitro models and the dynamic clinical environment of clear aligner therapy.

We fully agree that intermittent aligner wear, together with continuous salivary flow, mechanical forces, and regular oral hygiene, may reduce actual biological exposure and allow periods of mucosal recovery. Consequently, a static immersion model may represent a conservative, worst-case scenario and could overestimate cytotoxic risk relative to real clinical conditions.

Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that direct 3D printing of clear aligners represents a relatively new and still evolving field within orthodontics. Manufacturers of 3D-printed aligner materials face a particularly demanding challenge: achieving adequate, stable mechanical properties necessary for predictable tooth movement while simultaneously maintaining sufficient transparency and esthetic acceptability. These competing requirements often necessitate complex photopolymer resin formulations, which may increase the likelihood of residual monomers or other leachable components, even when production and post-processing protocols are carefully followed.

In this context, there is growing clinical awareness of occasional adverse soft-tissue reactions associated with certain 3D-printed aligner materials. While such reactions have not yet been systematically investigated or formally confirmed as allergic responses in controlled clinical studies, and therefore cannot be classified as established material allergies, their anecdotal occurrence highlights the need for caution. Importantly, these clinical impressions are supported by limited prospective studies reporting infrequent, generally mild mucosal reactions, including transient swelling, burning sensations, or minor soft-tissue irritation, even when manufacturers’ instructions were followed.2,3 Together, these findings suggest that material-related biological interactions in directly 3D-printed aligners remain incompletely understood.

Our findings should be interpreted as comparative and precautionary, aimed at identifying materials that may pose a higher biological risk under controlled, concentrated exposure conditions. Importantly, the absence of significant differences between the simulated 7-day and 14-day wearing protocols suggests a certain degree of material stability, even under conservative testing conditions.

From a regulatory perspective, the need for conservative preclinical evaluation is reinforced by current international medical device frameworks. In the European Union (EU), clear aligners are regulated under the Medical Device Regulation (EU) 2017/745, which requires a structured biological risk assessment throughout the device life cycle.4 Comparable regulatory expectations exist in other major jurisdictions, including the United States and China, where biocompatibility testing and risk management are integral components of medical device approval and post-market surveillance.5,6 Together, these frameworks underscore the importance of robust preclinical testing, complemented by well-designed in vivo studies, to ensure the long-term safety of novel aligner materials.

To conclude, although dynamic oral conditions and intermittent aligner wear may mitigate biological effects in vivo, the novelty of directly 3D-printed aligner materials, together with the inherent challenge of balancing mechanical performance and esthetics, justifies a conservative in vitro approach. Carefully planned in vivo studies, designed in accordance with international regulatory requirements, are essential to establish the clinical relevance of these findings and to ensure the long-term safety of this emerging technology.

A2. We thank the reader for this methodological observation. We agree that, within a static immersion model, the absence of statistically significant differences between the 7-day and 14-day immersion periods may reflect an early plateau in the elution of leachable components rather than definitive long-term material stability. It is plausible that a substantial proportion of biologically relevant release occurs during the initial phase of exposure, after which further elution stabilizes under static conditions.

In this context, extending the immersion period from 7 to 14 days may not lead to a measurable increase in cytotoxicity once an early saturation phase has been reached. Therefore, the observed non-significance should not be interpreted as evidence of complete material inertness over time, but rather as a limitation inherent to static extraction models when assessing time-dependent effects.

Importantly, clinical clear aligner therapy is based on the sequential replacement of aligners, which are typically changed every 7, 10, or 14 days depending on treatment complexity, planned tooth movement, and patient-related factors. Each aligner introduced intraorally represents a newly manufactured device and therefore reinitiates a potential early exposure phase. From a biological perspective, this suggests that clinical exposure may involve repeated short-term exposure peaks rather than cumulative effects driven solely by prolonged wear of a single aligner.

Within this framework, the lack of difference between the simulated 7-day and 14-day protocols in our study indicates that, once early elution has occurred, extending the wear duration of an individual aligner does not proportionally increase cytotoxic effects under conservative in vitro conditions. However, our experimental design was not intended to model repeated aligner replacement or cumulative exposure over an entire course of treatment.

Consequently, the scope of our conclusions regarding clinical wear protocols is limited to the observation that prolonged static exposure of a single aligner did not exacerbate cytotoxicity beyond early time points. More clinically relevant dynamic in vitro models and well-designed in vivo studies are required to clarify the biological implications of sequential aligner replacement and to better inform clinical wear protocols.

A3. We thank the reader for this comment and acknowledge that the inclusion of a concentration-matched vehicle control: artificial saliva mixed with culture medium at identical ratios without material exposure could provide additional insight into the relative contribution of the extraction medium itself. While such a control may represent a useful methodological refinement, the primary objective of the present study was to evaluate the biological effects of substances released from the tested materials under standardized extraction conditions.

The selected extract dilutions (10%, 20%, and 30%) were based on examples from the available literature and are consistent with commonly applied practices in in vitro cytotoxicity testing of dental and biomedical materials. Although International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards do not prescribe fixed dilution percentages, in accordance with ISO 10993-5, in vitro cytotoxicity testing may be conducted using the original extract and/or a series of extract dilutions to assess concentration-dependent effects under physiologically compatible conditions.7

Artificial saliva was intentionally used as the extraction medium to simulate intraoral conditions in a controlled and standardized manner. Its use enables a stable experimental environment and reproducible procedures, as commonly applied in pharmacological and bioengineering research. The selected artificial saliva formulation mimics the chemical composition and physical characteristics of human saliva and is based on established pharmaceutical literature, particularly studies on drug dissolution and drug delivery through the oral mucosa. Importantly, artificial saliva is not expected to exert cytotoxic effects on cells representative of the oral mucosa, allowing its use as an extraction medium without introducing inherent biological toxicity.

To maintain experimental clarity and focus, complete culture medium was used as the control condition, representing optimal cell culture viability. While a vehicle-only control could further isolate the influence of the extraction medium, several aspects support the interpretability of the present findings. Cytotoxic effects were material- and concentration-dependent rather than uniform across conditions, and identical extraction and dilution protocols were applied to all tested materials, enabling valid comparative assessment of their relative cytotoxic potential.

In conclusion, although additional controls may further refine mechanistic attribution in future studies, the current experimental design, adherence to ISO guidance, use of a well-characterized and non-toxic artificial saliva, and consistency with existing literature allow cautious and meaningful interpretation of the results within the defined scope of this study.
