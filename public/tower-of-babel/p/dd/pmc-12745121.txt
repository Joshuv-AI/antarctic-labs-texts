Long-term survival for preoperative versus postoperative treatment of N2 lung cancer: The importance of complete adjuvant therapy for occult N2 disease
Authors: Fatemehsadat Pezeshkian, Chandana Adhikarla, Emanuele Mazzola, Hao Li, Carla Vega, Anupama Singh, Fatemeh Hooshmand, Miles McAllister, Rafael Ribeiro Barcelos, Elias Bou Farhat, Abby A White, Matthew M Rochefort, Raphael Bueno, Desiree Steimer, Matthew J Pommerening, Paula A Ugalde Figueroa, Michael T Jaklitsch, Scott J Swanson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnsuspected (defined here as occult) N2-positive lung cancer impacts overall survival (OS), and treatment patterns likely influence outcomes. We aimed to evaluate factors that could improve the long-term outcomes of patients with occult N2 disease compared to biopsy-proven, pre-resectional N2 disease.MethodsOur institutional prospective database was queried for N2 non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) at presentation (biopsy-proven) prior to surgical resection or postoperatively diagnosed N2 (occult N2), excluding stage IV disease or patients with insufficient follow-up. In occult N2 group, postoperative therapy was stratified into complete (≥3 cycles), incomplete (1-2 cycles), or no adjuvant treatment. Propensity score weighting adjusted for age, comorbidities, tumor size, and N2 involvement. Kaplan-Meier analysis assessed 5-year overalls survival (OS).ResultsBetween 2006-2023, 483 patients met inclusion criteria, stratified into 325 patients (67.2%) with biopsy-proven N2 and 158 (32.8%) with occult N2. The biopsy-proven N2 group primarily received neoadjuvant therapy (chemo/chemoimmunotherapy ± radiation). In the occult N2 group, 86 patients (54.4%) received full-dose adjuvant therapy, 31 (19.6%) received incomplete adjuvant therapy, and 41 (26%) received no adjuvant therapy. After propensity score weighting, the occult N2 group had significantly lower OS (P = .03); however, when the cohort was stratified by completeness of adjuvant treatment, those who received full adjuvant therapy had OS comparable to that of the biopsy-proven group (hazard ratio [HR], 0.98; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.65-1.48; P = .9). Incomplete therapy provided some survival benefits but was associated with worse OS compared to complete therapy (HR, 1.54; 95% CI, 0.9-2.6; P = .1). The no-therapy group had the lowest OS.ConclusionsPatients with occult N2 treated with adjuvant treatment may achieve improved survival rates compared to those not fully treated. Neoadjuvant treatment is optimal to ensure delivery of therapy as prescribed, and thus every effort should be made to identify N2 disease prior to resection.Key Words: adjuvant therapy, neoadjuvant therapy, non–small cell lung cancer, occult N2, postoperative N2, propensity score

Comparable survival in adjuvant-treated occult N2 and neoadjuvant-treated biopsy-proven N2.

Central Message

In patients with occult N2, complete adjuvant therapy yields comparable survival to biopsy-proven N2 disease treated with neoadjuvant. Partial therapy still offers survival benefits over no treatment.

Perspective

This study highlights the importance of complete adjuvant treatment in patients with occult N2, showing that those receiving full adjuvant therapy have survival rates comparable to biopsy-proven N2 patients. Incomplete therapy provides some benefits, but neoadjuvant therapy remains optimal to ensure proper treatment delivery. These findings can guide clinical decision making in lung cancer management.

Lung cancer is the most common cause of cancer and cancer mortality worldwide, with 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths annually.1 Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the predominant type, with 20% to 35% in stage III, a heterogeneous group comprising a variety of tumor sizes and patterns of hilar and mediastinal lymph node involvement.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Although survival in all stages has improved over the past decade,6 patients with N2-positive NSCLC have a 5-year survival ranging from 12% to 58%, similar to stage IVA on one end of the range and stage IIB on the other.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 This wide variation is likely based on the extent and pattern of mediastinal disease burden, tumor size, thoroughness of preoperative staging, biological and geographic differences, and different perioperative therapeutic strategies.4,12, 13, 14, 15

Thorough investigation and preoperative evaluation to determine the N2 disease burden is essential for delivering the best stage-appropriate therapy.14,16 However, despite aggressive preoperative evaluation, between 6% and 30% of patients have surprise, unsuspected, or occult N2 disease, diagnosed intraoperatively or postoperatively.7,17 These patients are traditionally known to have worse survival, having missed a chance at induction therapy, compared to those who are diagnosed with N2 disease prior to resection and receive neoadjuvant treatment.7,18 Recent studies have reported better survival outcomes, albeit small in retrospective series and contingent on a narrowly selected group of patients with unsuspected or occult N2 disease.9,10,19

Consensus on the optimal treatment for stage III N2 disease, especially when unsuspected, remains an area of ongoing debate, with its inherent heterogeneity providing significant opportunity for research.4,7,13,20, 21, 22 In this study, we aimed to evaluate and describe the outcomes of patients diagnosed with occult N2 NSCLC after surgical resection, particularly as they relate to the postoperative treatment regimen, and compare them with the outcomes of patients with biopsy-proven preoperatively diagnosed N2 NSCLC.

Methods

This is a retrospective review of the prospectively collected thoracic surgery cases performed at a single tertiary facility. The Institutional Review Board or equivalent Ethics Committee of the Brigham and Women's Hospital approved the study protocol and publication of data (protocol 2020P000145; approved February 13, 2020). Informed consent was waived, because data were drawn from an existing database.

Our institutional database was queried for patients with biopsy-proven N2 NSCLC (8th TNM classification) at presentation or intraoperatively/postoperatively diagnosed N2 (occult N2) NSCLC between January 2006 and December 2023. Patients with stage IV disease or insufficient postoperative follow-up were excluded. Patient demographics, procedural details, clinical and pathologic characteristics, and long-term outcomes were extracted from the database and audited in September 2024.

Patients with occult N2 NSCLC were stratified into 3 groups based on postoperative therapy, which were those who received complete adjuvant therapy (≥3 cycles), incomplete adjuvant treatment (1-2 cycles), or no adjuvant therapy.

Long-term outcome data, including overall survival (OS) and locoregional recurrence-free survival (L-RFS), were measured from the time of surgery, with death as a competing event for locoregional recurrence. Locoregional recurrence was defined as recurrence in the same lobe or in ipsilateral hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes confirmed by histology or computed tomography (CT) imaging interpreted by a thoracic radiologist. Patients surviving ≥5 years were censored at that time point.

Postoperative follow-up included physical examination and CT surveillance for at least every 6 months for the first 3 years postoperatively and at least annually thereafter.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data are summarized as number (%) and compared between groups using the Fisher exact test. Continuous variables are expressed as mean (SD) and median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Propensity score weighting for the average treatment effect was performed to control for possible differences in clinical characteristics influencing the choice of procedure using logistic regression. The model was adjusted for the following variables, selected based on clinical reasoning: age, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), imaging tumor size, and number of positive N2 stations (N2a/b, based on clinical diagnosis for the biopsy-proven N2 group and pathologic diagnosis for the occult N2 group) involved. A Love plot was inspected to ensure a reasonable effect of the weights. Survival analysis for OS used Kaplan-Meier estimators compared with log-rank tests. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Stratified log-rank tests and Cox models with robust variance estimators were used for the weighted cohort. Survival analysis for L-RFS was performed in a competing-risk setting, assuming death as a competing event for the occurrence of locoregional recurrence. Both the cause-specific (weighted and unweighted) and subdistribution (Fine-Gray) hazards (only unweighted) were calculated with corresponding 95% CIs, and cumulative hazard plots were provided.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). P < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 483 patients with NSCLC who underwent lung resection met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 325 (67.2%) had biopsy-proven-N2 disease and 158 (32.8%) had occult N2 disease. The occult N2 group was significantly older, with a median age of 69.4 years versus 63.2 years in the biopsy-proven N2 group (P < .001). Other preoperative demographics and comorbidities were not significantly different between the 2 groups.

The imaging tumor size in the occult N2 group was significantly smaller, with a median size of 2.8 cm versus 3.2 cm in the biopsy-proven N2 group (P < .001). This was reversed in the pathologic setting, with having significantly smaller tumor size in the biopsy-proven N2 group (2.5 cm vs 3.2 cm; P < .001), likely a result of the response to neoadjuvant therapy. The tumor avidity measured on positron emission tomography (PET) scan was significantly lower in the occult N2 group compared to the biopsy-proven N2 group (median, 7.7 vs 9.8; P = .02). The location of the tumor varied between the 2 groups and was seen frequently in the right upper lobe in the biopsy-proven N2 group and in the left upper lobe in the occult N2 group (P < .001). Additionally, lymphovascular invasion (LVI) was observed more frequently in the occult N2 group (P < .001).

The frequency of invasive mediastinal staging differed significantly in the 2 groups, performed in 320 (98.5%) of the biopsy-proven N2 group (187 with cervical mediastinoscopy, 114 with endobronchial ultrasonography, and 19 with both) versus 107 (67.7%) of the occult N2 group (88 with cervical mediastinoscopy and 19 with endobronchial ultrasonography) (P < .001). The number of stations biopsied in the clinical setting was higher in the biopsy-proven N2 group (3 vs 2; P < .001). Node-specific false-negativity was noted in 45 patients (39.4%) with occult N2 who underwent invasive mediastinal staging. Twenty-three out of 51 patients with occult N2 disease who did not receive invasive staging had small peripheral tumors without hilar or mediastinal lymph node enlargement or PET-avidity, with no indication for invasive mediastinal staging. Multistation N2 (N2b) involvement was more frequent in the biopsy-proven N2 group compared to the occult N2 group (28.3% vs 15.5%; P = .002). Figure 1 depicts the heat map of lymph node positivity in both groups. Complete mediastinal lymph node harvest (defined as dissecting at least 3 N2 stations and 1 N1 station) was achieved in 146 patients (44.9%) of the biopsy-proven N2 group and 58 patients (36.7%) of the occult N2 group (P = .1). The number of resected lymph nodes on final pathology was significantly lower in the occult N2 group compared to the biopsy-proven N2 group (9 vs 11; P = .002). A similar trend also was observed for the number of stations resected, but this trend did not reach statistical significance. However, the proportion of R (un) was significantly higher in the occult N2 group (78.4% vs 67%; P = .01).

Heatmap of N2 station lymph node positivity across the biopsy-proven N2 versus the occult N2 group.

The use of sublobar resection and minimally invasive surgery was significantly higher in the occult N2 group (P < .001), while more pneumonectomies were performed in the biopsy-proven N2 group (P < .001). The rates of postoperative adverse events were comparable in the 2 groups except for atrial fibrillation, which was observed more often in the biopsy-proven N2 group (13.8% vs 5.7%; P = .008). The rates of 30-day and 90-day mortality and disease recurrence were not significantly different between the 2 groups.

The biopsy-proven N2 group received neoadjuvant therapy in the form of chemotherapy with or without radiation (n = 276 of 325; 84.9%) or chemoimmunotherapy (n = 49 of 325; 15.1%). Adjuvant treatment was administered in 117 patients (74%) of the occult N2 group in the form of chemotherapy with or without radiation (n = 113 of 117; 96.6%) or chemoimmunotherapy (n = 4 of 117; 3.4%). The remaining patients 41 (8.5%) in the occult N2 group did not receive any postoperative treatment, owing mainly to significant postoperative morbidity. The breakdown of treatment types based on single versus multistation N2 disease showed that a higher rate of multistation N2 in chemoimmunotherapy recipients compared to chemoradiation recipients (35.9% vs 23.4%; P = .05).

The median duration of follow-up was similar in the occult N2 and biopsy-proven N2 groups at 32.9 and 33.5 months, respectively (P = .48). The rate of recurrence was not statistically significantly different between the groups, with a total recurrence rate of 55.1% (n = 179) in biopsy-proven N2 group and 50.6% (n = 80) in the occult N2 group (P = .36). The median unweighted duration of OS was 33 months in both groups (P = .31), but the 5-year OS rate was lower in the occult N2 group (50.6% vs 59.7%; P = .06).

Table 1 summarizes all the demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics along with short- and long-term outcomes in the study cohort. Based on the timing and completeness of treatment administration, 4 groups were identified: biopsy-proven N2 group who received neoadjuvant therapy (n = 325; 67.3%), occult N2 group with complete (≥3 cycles) adjuvant therapy (n = 86; 17.8%), occult N2 group with incomplete (1-2 cycles) adjuvant therapy (n = 31; 6.4%), and occult N2 group with no adjuvant therapy (n = 41; 8.5%). The baseline characteristics were not significantly different among the groups; However, patients who did not receive adjuvant treatment experienced significantly more postoperative adverse events. Length of stay was notably longer in the no-treatment group, with a median of 8 days, compared to 4 days in the complete and incomplete adjuvant treatment groups (P = .004), consistent with the higher rate of postoperative complications observed in that group (P = .03). Among specific postoperative events, respiratory failure occurred significantly more frequently in the no-treatment group (P = .004). Additionally, the 90-day mortality rate was highest in the no-treatment group (P = .04). A more detailed comparison of preoperative and postoperative variables is presented in Table 2.

On further assessment, we identified several reasons for not completing systemic therapy, including disease progression, patient refusal, and treatment-related adverse events, such as ototoxicity and neurotoxicity.

Propensity weighting adjusted for age, CCI, imaging tumor size, and number of positive N2 stations (n2a/b) involved was applied (Figure E1). The biopsy-proven N2 group had significantly better 5-year OS (P = .03) (Figure 2); however, when stratified by the completeness of adjuvant treatment, patients who received full adjuvant treatment had comparable OS to the biopsy-proven N2 group who had received neoadjuvant treatment (HR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.65-1.48; P = .9). In the occult N2 group, patients who did not receive any adjuvant treatment had significantly worse OS compared to those who received complete adjuvant treatment (HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.23-0.81; P = .008). Compared to complete treatment, incomplete adjuvant treatment appeared to provide some survival benefits for up to 3 years; however, this did not reach statistical significance (HR, 1.54; 95% CI, 0.9-2.65; P = .11). Discussion

Over a span of 17 years, our analysis of N2 NSCLC compared the long-term outcome of the patients with biopsy-proven N2 disease receiving neoadjuvant treatment with patients with occult N2 disease who received various adjuvant treatment regimens. Biopsy-proven N2 disease was associated with survival advantages over occult N2 in an unadjusted comparison; however, when stratified by the completeness of adjuvant treatment in patients with occult N2 disease, those who received complete adjuvant therapy had survival outcomes comparable to biopsy-proven N2 patients. Additionally, partial adjuvant treatment provided approximately 3 years of survival benefit compared to no treatment in patients with occult N2 disease (Graphical Abstract).

The prevalence of occult N2 disease has been reported to range from 6% to 30%, with some studies reporting a rate as low as 4% in a narrowly selected patient group compared to as high as 48% in older studies.10,12,17,23, 24, 25 This variability could be attributed to the selection criteria used to define occult N2 classification, such as excluding larger tumors, extent of preoperative evaluation, staging changes, study settings, exclusion of larger tumors, and geographical differences.4,7,13,17,20,25, 26, 27 Additionally, owing to patient factors such as frailty or other comorbidities, the occult group might not have had invasive staging for reasons that may have led to worse outcomes. In our study, 32.8% of patients were postoperatively diagnosed with occult N2.

Patients with occult N2 disease are likely to be older (mean age, 68 years) with an adenocarcinoma histology. No difference in histology was noted in older studies by Van Klaveren, Goldstraw, and others,11, 12, 13,21 while recent studies in the West and Asia have shown an increase in the prevalence of adenocarcinoma in patients with occult N2 disease.10,19,20,24,25,27 The latest Asian studies also have reported patients with occult N2 as younger (mean age, 60 years), female, and nonsmokers.19,20 Differences based on sex and smoking status have been inconclusive. These differences probably reflect demographic changes among Asian and non-Asian populations, although more research is required. While we observed that our occult N2 group was older (median, 69 years), no differences based on sex, smoking status, or histology were found.

Most studies regarding occult N2 disease report smaller-sized, centrally located tumors, with a propensity for the left side, greater LVI, and maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) > 4.10,12,17,19,20,24,27,28 In concordance with previous studies, our occult N2 group had clinically smaller tumors (2.8 cm), with an average SUVmax of 7.7 on PET scan, greater frequency of LVI, and preponderance for the left upper lobe compared to the biopsy-proven group. Notably, the pathologic tumor size in our study was larger in the occult N2 group due to tumor shrinkage by neoadjuvant therapy in the biopsy-proven N2 group. Surgically, the biopsy-proven N2 group had higher rates of thoracotomy and pneumonectomy, which might have contributed to their increased incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation compared to the occult N2 group.

Patients with N2-positive stage III NSCLC are reported to have a 5-year survival ranging from 12% (large central primary, multistation, or bulky N2 and poor performance status) to 58% (peripheral small primary, single-station N2, low tumor burden, and good performance status).4,17,29 Survival has been significantly better in patients who received neoadjuvant therapy and were downstaged.4,27,30, 31, 32 The consensus from large randomized controlled trials (eg, CALGB, SWOG, EORTC) recommends that induction therapy (chemotherapy ± radiation from prior era) followed by surgery serve as the standard of care for resectable biopsy-proven stage III N2 disease.16,32,33 In the current era, chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy is the standard neoadjuvant treatment regimen; however, the best management regimen for occult N2 disease remains controversial. The reported 5-year survival of evident-N2 disease of 35% to 45% is generally higher compared to the 20% to 30% in occult N2.7,9,18,21,27 A subset of patients who have occult N2 disease despite extensive mediastinal staging appear to have significantly higher survival than postoperatively diagnosed N2.13,17,27 This may indicate lower tumor burden and the presence of nodal micrometastases in occult N2 disease, which may favorably influence their survival.13,17,27 In our study, multistation N2 was significantly lower in patients with occult N2 disease compared to those with biopsy-proven N2 (15% vs 28%), indicating the likelihood of lower tumor burden in occult N2 disease.

In this decade, despite controversies, the introduction of immunotherapy to the neoadjuvant regimen has improved the survival of biopsy-proven N2 patients significantly. The addition of adjuvant immunotherapy for patients with occult N2 disease is currently being explored. In our study, 85% of the patients with preoperatively diagnosed N2 disease received chemoradiotherapy and 15% received chemoimmunotherapy. Among the patients with occult N2 disease, 70% received chemoradiotherapy and 3% received chemoimmunotherapy. After a median follow-up of 33 months, 5-year OS was significantly higher in the patients with biopsy-proven N2 disease (59.7% vs 50.6%). Regarding the rate of recurrence and RFS, we did not find any differences in locoregional recurrence or RFS between the groups. Our findings are supported by other single-institution studies.9,27,28 Some Asian studies have found a significant difference in RFS between their preoperatively diagnosed and unsuspected N2 groups.19,20 Our 5-year OS values of 59.7% in the biopsy-proven N2 group and 50.6% in the occult N2 group are both higher in their respective categories compared to some of our contemporaries. It is to be noted that factors pertaining to low performance status and higher comorbidity in some patients with occult N2 disease may explain both the inability to receive adjuvant therapy as well as lower survival. This good survival rate in our study reflects the strength of the diagnostic and surgical expertise, as well as improved treatment regimens provided to our patients.

Finally, we strove to identify a subset of patients with occult N2 disease that were better survivors. Occult N2 disease, irrespective of radiotherapy, was distinguished based on whether they received ≥3 full cycles of cisplatin-based chemotherapy, partial (1-2 cycles) chemotherapy, or none. Our findings suggest that when stratified by the completeness of adjuvant treatment, those who received full adjuvant therapy had an OS comparable to the biopsy-proven N2 group receiving neoadjuvant therapy. Patients who received incomplete adjuvant therapy had approximately 3 additional years of survival compared to those without adjuvant therapy. However, beyond this, their survival was lower compared to those who completed full adjuvant therapy. Patients who received no adjuvant therapy had the lowest OS. These findings exemplify the heterogeneous nature of occult N2 disease. Patients with occult N2 disease who are adequately treated with adjuvant therapy have survival similar to patients with biopsy-proven N2. Our findings are also supported by Thomas and colleagues in their 2018 analysis of occult N2 from the National Cancer Database, with a 5-year OS of 41.5% for occult N2 versus 41.2% for preoperative N2.27 A meta-analysis of 9387 patients found a 5-year survival rate of 44% in the unsuspected N2 group who underwent complete resection.10 Moreover, the significantly higher rates of postoperative morbidities and adverse events observed in the incompletely treated patients likely contributed to their inability to complete the prescribed adjuvant regimens. This finding supports the idea that neoadjuvant therapy should be a more effective strategy, allowing patients to receive systemic treatment at a more robust stage when they are better able to tolerate it. This would further mandate pre-resection pathologic staging to achieve conclusive results. Intraoperative frozen section analysis is an alternative strategy but has a lower probability of yielding a conclusive pathologic diagnosis. In cases of occult N2 findings, it is essential to adopt a more diligent approach to ensure that patients recover smoothly from the operative stress. This enhanced focus on postoperative care is crucial for maximizing the likelihood of patients completing their adjuvant treatment regimen.

Our study has some limitations. Because the cohort was derived from a prospective surgical database, patients who progressed during neoadjuvant therapy or became medically ineligible for surgery were not included, potentially enriching the neoadjuvant group with individuals of better prognosis. Similarly, in the occult N2 group, the absence of adjuvant therapy in some patients might reflect underlying comorbidities or frailty not fully captured by the CCI, which could have influenced overall survival outcomes. To mitigate this limitation, we performed a propensity score–weighted analysis to account for measurable baseline differences between the occult N2 and known N2 groups. We included patients from different eras of therapeutic regimens, differential TNM staging systems, and different diagnostic techniques for mediastinal evaluation. Further longitudinal studies in large populations are needed to identify individualized characteristics linked to successful treatment completion and predictive of the greatest benefit, enabling more personalized and effective decision making strategies.

Conclusions

Although survival outcomes in patients with occult N2 disease have remained relatively limited over the last decade, controversies around their management reflect their inherent heterogeneity. Patients with occult N2 disease can have reasonable OS rates comparable to those of patients with biopsy-proven N2 if complete adjuvant therapy is instituted. Neoadjuvant therapy remains the recommended strategy, as it ensures that patients receive systemic treatment at a time when they are more likely to tolerate it, highlighting the need for diligent mediastinal assessments in patients. In our cohort, several patients were unable to complete adjuvant therapy owing to perioperative morbidities. With neoadjuvant therapy combined with minimally invasive surgery, the future appears improved for patients with occult N2 disease; however, a focus on identifying N2 disease prior to surgery remains paramount.
