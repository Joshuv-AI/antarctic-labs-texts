Comparative Cross-Kingdom DDA- and DIA-PASEF Proteomic Profiling Reveals Novel Determinants of Fungal Virulence and a Putative Druggable Target
Authors: Brianna Ball, Arjun Sukumaran, Jonathan R Krieger, Jennifer Geddes-McAlister
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and reliable detection of fungal pathogens presents an important hurdle to manage infections, especially considering that fungal pathogens, including the globally important human pathogen, Cryptococcus neoformans, have adapted diverse mechanisms to survive the hostile host environment and moderate virulence determinant production during coinfections. These pathogen adaptations present an opportunity for improvements (e.g., technological and computational) to better understand the interplay between a host and a pathogen during disease to uncover new strategies to overcome infection. In this study, we performed comparative proteomic profiling of an in vitro coinfection model across a range of fungal and bacterial burden loads in macrophages. Comparing data-dependent acquisition and data-independent acquisition enabled with parallel accumulation serial fragmentation technology, we quantified changes in dual-perspective proteome remodeling. We report enhanced and novel detection of pathogen proteins with data-independent acquisition-parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (DIA-PASEF), especially for fungal proteins during single and dual infection of macrophages. Further characterization of a fungal protein detected only with DIA-PASEF uncovered a novel determinant of fungal virulence, including altered capsule and melanin production, thermotolerance, and macrophage infectivity, supporting proteomics advances for the discovery of a novel putative druggable target to suppress C. neoformans pathogenicity.Keywords: mass spectrometry-based proteomics, DDA-PASEF, DIA-PASEF, macrophages, Cryptococcus neoformans, Klebsiella pneumoniae, virulence, druggable targets

Introduction

The clinical landscape of infectious diseases is continuously evolving and expanding with the emergence of novel pathogens, rising rates of antimicrobial-resistant strains, and increasingly at-risk populations (e.g., immunosuppressive therapies and elderly populations).1 It is vital to have effective screening and early detection strategies for optimal patient management. Moreover, understanding the interactions between the host and pathogen is critical for treating the infection’s establishment, progression, and outcome. However, pathogen diagnosis and treatment challenges arise when pathogen levels fall below the limit of detection, multiple active infecting pathogens are present (e.g., shifted virulence factor elaboration), and the host immune response is altered (e.g., microbiome dysregulation).2 Thus, the ability to accurately diagnose and treat resulting infections becomes confounded, especially with the lack of biomarkers and surveillance for rare or emerging pathogens.1 For example, perpetually neglected fungal pathogens have recently emerged as a staple within infectious disease research, with the World Health Organization announcing a priority list of pathogens raising public awareness, including Candida albicans, Candida auris, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Cryptococcus neoformans.3,4

Currently, there are newer, less understood risk factors for susceptible populations to these invasive fungal pathogens, including heightened risk of a fungal coinfection for individuals hospitalized with respiratory infections.3 For instance, the reported incidences of COVID-19-associated fungal coinfections range from 4 to 35%, attributed to invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, mucormycosis, cryptococcosis, and candidiasis.5 At the same time, inflammatory disorders, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, are risk factors for pulmonary aspergillosis and cryptococcosis.6−8 A predominant and widespread pulmonary-associated fungal pathogen, C. neoformans, is responsible for causing life-threatening meningitis, impacting 220,000 immunocompromised individuals annually.9,10 This opportunistic pathogen features prolonged lung residency due to its dormancy capabilities, increasing the likelihood of lung microbiota interactions.11C. neoformans has reported synergistic and antagonistic interactions with bacterial pathogens identified in vitro, including enhanced melanin pigmentation during coculture with Klebsiella aerogenes(11,12) and altered catalase production during coculture with Klebsiella pneumoniae;13 however, the scope and magnitude of such interactions remain unresolved. These concerns highlight the requirement for advances to delineate coinfection interactions within the host for a better understanding of virulence establishment, pathogenicity, and infectivity.

Innovative technological approaches for multipathogen–host interaction studies show promise for elucidating novel virulence factors and adaptation strategies.13−15 Mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics enables high-resolution unbiased protein profiling from complex host–pathogen samples.16 For instance, our recent proteomic analysis of cross-kingdom interactions of C. neoformans with the nosocomial pathogen K. pneumoniae revealed global protein-level host immune adaptation dependent on pathogen exposure and incubation times, supporting novel fungus- and bacteria-specific modulation of the host.13 However, significant technological and bioinformatic challenges that limit our biological discoveries in infectious disease research need to be addressed, including high-resolution identification and quantification of low abundant pathogen components within a host system, consistent precursor selection in complex samples, and prolonged separation techniques necessary to reduce multispecies complexity.17 Moreover, as infection models of invasive fungal and bacterial pathogens are often centered upon engulfed and replicating pathogens, detectable levels of pathogen proteins depend on the abundance and complexity of the host proteome and the ability of the pathogen to survive within the hostile environment of the host. Thus, prior expensive and time-consuming enrichment steps are often used to achieve sufficient depth of profiling the pathogen proteome.15

Current MS technologies have revolutionized how we study host and pathogen proteomes simultaneously, while maintaining sufficient depth, sensitivity, and speed, and mechanisms to leverage MS approaches to drug discovery.18 For instance, the emergence of data-independent acquisition (DIA) overcomes reproducibility issues and stoichiometric limitations of the traditional data-dependent acquisition (DDA) methods.19,20 DDA is generally biased toward selection of the most intense peptide signals, consequently limiting the quantification of low-abundant peptides, whereas DIA fragments all ions, enabling detection of low abundant peptides within complex samples; however, widespread adoption of DIA depends on the development of robust analysis tools, as the resulting MS/MS spectra are highly complex.21 Recent developments, including dual-trapped ion mobility (IM) separation [trapped ion mobility separation (TIMS)] combined with the MS scan mode, parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) has showcased robust, high-sensitivity, and ultrafast MS/MS acquisition to enable 4d-proteomics.22−25 The extension to DIA-PASEF provides comprehensive precursor selection with ultrasensitivity and speed for an advantageous tool for infectious disease research that prioritizes the distinguishment of low-abundant and cofragmented peptides.26,27

Here, we aimed to establish an innovative workflow to improve the identification and quantification of low-abundance fungal and bacterial proteins directly from complex coinfection samples. We explored a range of multiplicities of infection (i.e., MOI; ratio of the pathogen to host cells) based on the elegantly described pathogenic potential concept that incorporates fulminancy, time, and virulence into the disease state of a host.28,29 We investigated the dynamics of C. neoformans with the bacterial pathogen K. pneumoniae, which is capable of causing respiratory and urinary tract diseases in immunocompromised individuals.30,31 The pathogens of interest display similar mechanisms of pathogenicity, including the production of a polysaccharide capsule, intracellular replication in host immune cells, and initial interactions with alveolar macrophages upon pulmonary colonization.31,32 As the incidences of fungal and bacterial coinfections rise, expansion of analyses to include these multiple pathogen perspectives that influence the diagnostics and treatment of at-risk individuals is important.33,34 Our data comprehensively compare DDA- and DIA-PASEF for single- and dual-pathogen–host samples across a continuum of pathogen burden levels, revealing concentration-dependent proteome shifts. The qualitative and quantitative performances of DIA-PASEF revealed new insights into factors influencing fungal pathogenesis, including the identification of a novel virulence-associated protein, CNAG_05997, which is vital for production of a suite of C. neoformans virulence factors. Our DIA-PASEF proteomic analysis of a highly complex cross-kingdom coinfection demonstrates the technology’s potential to advance infectious disease research through discovery of novel putative druggable targets to improve our capacity to diagnose and treat fungal infections.

Materials and Methods

Microbial Strains, Growth Conditions, and Media

For fungal growth, C. neoformans var. grubii strain H99 (serotype A) was used for all analyses and as a reference strain for mutant construction. Wild-type strain was maintained on YPD medium (2% dextrose, 2% peptone, and 1% yeast extract) at 30 °C unless otherwise stated. For macrophage infection, C. neoformans was grown overnight in YPD media at 37 °C and subcultured in YPD to mid log phase. For bacterial growth, K. pneumoniae 52.145 (K2 serotype) was maintained on Lysogeny Broth (LB) agar plates. K. pneumoniae was grown overnight at 37 °C in LB, subcultured into LB, and grown to mid log phase.

Macrophage Infection Experimental Setup

BALB/c WT-immortalized macrophages (generously provided by Felix Meissner, Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Germany) were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 2 mM Glutamax, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% l-glutamine, and 5% penicillin–streptomycin (pen-strep). Macrophages were seeded in six-well plates at 0.3 × 106 cells/well and grown for 48 h until ∼70–80% confluency was reached (i.e., 1.2 × 106 cells/mL).

Fungal and bacterial cell preparation and coculture with macrophages were performed as previously described with some modifications.13 Briefly, C. neoformans cells were grown to mid log phase in YPD at 37 °C, collected at 1,500g for 10 min, washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in DMEM without pen/strep. K. pneumoniae cells were grown to mid log phase in LB at 37 °C, collected at 3,500g for 10 min, washed twice in PBS, and resuspended in DMEM without pen/strep. For single-pathogen cocultures, macrophages were infected with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) gradient consisting of 10:1, 25:1, 50:1, and 100:1 (pathogen/macrophage cells) for 90 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for both fungal and bacterial pathogens. At confluence, we presume approximately 1.2e6 macrophages in each well, and therefore, to achieve the desired MOI, approximately 1.2 × 107, 3 × 107, 6e,7 and 1.2e8C. neoformans and Klebsiella pneumoniae cells were used for the inoculum, respectively. For dual-pathogen infection assays, both C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae were simultaneously cocultured together with macrophages following the outlined MOI gradient consisting of 10:10:1, 25:25:1, 50:50:1, and 100:100:1 (fungal/bacterial:macrophage cells) for 90 min. Control samples of uninfected macrophages were maintained in DMEM without a pen/strep for 90 min to correspond with the pathogen coculture experiments. Following coculture, infected macrophages were washed twice with PBS and immediately collected for proteomic sample processing. Experiment was performed in biological quadruplicate.

Proteomics Sample Preparation

Samples were prepared as we previously described.35 Briefly, samples were collected in 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.5) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by addition of sodium dodecyl sulfate ( 2% final concentration) and probe sonication (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dithiothreitol (10 mM final concentration) and iodoacetamide (5.5 mM final concentration) were added, followed by acetone precipitation overnight at −20 °C. Samples were collected, washed, and resuspended in 8 M urea/40 mM HEPES for protein concentration measurement using a bovine serum albumin tryptophan assay.36 Samples were diluted in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, normalized to 50 μg, and digested with LysC/trypsin (Promega, protein/enzyme ratio, 50:1). To stop digestion, 10% v/v trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added, and peptides were purified using C18 Stop And Go Extraction tips.37

LC–MS/MS

LC–MS/MS was performed on an NanoElute (Bruker Daltonik) system coupled online to a hybrid TIMS-quadrupole TOF mass spectrometer38 (Bruker Daltonik timsTOF Pro, Germany) via a nanoelectrospray ion source (Bruker Daltonik Captive Spray). Next, 200 ng of peptides were separated on a PepSep column 15 cm × 75 μm ID, 1.9 μm reversed-phase column (Bruker Daltonics) at a flow rate of 500 nL min–1 in an oven compartment heated to 60 °C. To analyze samples from whole-proteome digests, we used a gradient starting with a linear increase (solvent B: 80% acetonitrile and 0.5% formic acid) from 4% B to 30% B over 42 min, followed by 95% B in 10 s which was held constant for 2 min. Column was equilibrated using 4 volumes of solvent A (0.1% formic acid).

In DIA-PASEF studies, the method covers the m/z range from 475 to 1000 and the IM [1/K0] range of 0.85–1.27. Three IM windows were included in the DIA-PASEF scan, and the isolation widths were fixed at 25 Da. Cycle time was estimated at 0.95 s. Collision energy was set on a linear ramp as a function of across the IM range: 27ev at 0.85 Vs/cm2 to 45ev at 1.30 Vs/cm2. For the DDA-PASEF acquisition mode, 1 survey TIMS-MS and 10 PASEF MS/MS scans were performed per acquisition cycle. We analyzed an IM range from 1/K0 = 0.85 to 1.3 Vs/cm2 using equal ion accumulation and ramp time in the dual-TIMS analyzer of 100 ms each. Suitable precursor ions for MS/MS analysis were isolated in a window of 2 Th for m/z < 700 and 3 Th for m/z > 700 by rapidly switching the quadrupole position in sync with the elution of precursors from the TIMS device. The collision energy was lowered stepwise as a function of increasing IM, starting from 27 eV for 1/K0 = 0.6 Vs/cm2 and 45 eV for 1/K0 = 1.6 Vs/cm2. In both acquisition modes, we made use of the m/z and IM information to exclude singly charged precursor ions with a polygon filter mask and further used “dynamic exclusion” to avoid resequencing of precursors that reached a “target value” of 20,000 au. The IM dimension was calibrated linearly using three ions from the Agilent ESI LC/MS tuning mix (m/z, 1/K0: 622.0289, 0.9848 Vs/cm2; 922.0097, 1.1895 Vs/cm2; and 1221.9906, 1.3820 Vs/cm2).

Data Processing

DDA was searched using ProluCID39 on the PaSER (now Bruker ProteoScape) platform, version 2023b. For the search engine, the following standard settings were used: a precursor tolerance of 20 ppm, a fragment tolerance of 30 ppm, 2 missed trypsin cleavages, a fixed modification of +57.0214 on cysteines, and a variable modification of +15.995 on methionine. False discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using DTASelect,40 where protein FDR was held to 1% with peptide and spectrum FDR also held at 1%. The TIMScore mobility predictor was employed with enhanced discriminant analysis. Label-free quantification was performed using the Census algorithm on the PaSER platform. DIA was conducted using Spectronaut v18.5 (Biognosys, Switzerland) in directDIA + mode using the default settings, including search for fully tryptic peptides, and an FDR set to 1%. Cross-run normalization was disabled, and modifications were set similar to the DDA-PASEF settings. In both DIA-PASEF and DDA-PASEF searches, databases used were as follows: C. neoformans reference proteome UP000002149 (6734 entries); K. pneumoniae reference proteome UP000007841 (5126 entries); and Mus musculus reference proteome UP000000589 (17,126 entries). Notably, different algorithms are used to calculate protein intensities for DDA- and DIA-PASEF experiments, and y-scales are adjusted to capture the full range of intensities for the relative comparison.

Bioinformatics

Statistical analysis and data visualization of the proteomics data were performed using Perseus (version 1.6.2.2).41 Data was prepared by filtering for reverse database matches, contaminants, and proteins only identified by site, followed by log2 transformation of LFQ intensities. Filtering for valid values (3 of 4 replicates, in at least one group) was performed, and missing values were imputed from the normal distribution (width: 0.3 and downshift: 1.8 standard deviations). Significant differences were evaluated by Student’s t-test (p ≤ 0.05) with multiple hypothesis testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg42 FDR cut off at 0.05 with S0 = 1. STRING analysis was performed;43 notably, as “CNAG_” identifiers are not currently available within STRING, we matched orthologs from Cryptococcus gattii (possessing “CNBG_” identifiers) using FungiDB44 to define protein interaction networks. For STRING clustering, we set the k-mean value to 5 and the medium confidence interaction score to 0.400. Plots were generated with GraphPad v.10.

Microbial Cell Count

To determine the intracellular burden of the two pathogens across the MOI inoculum, the pathogen coculture with macrophages was performed as described previously.45 Macrophages were lysed with 0.5% Tween 20-PBS for 15 min and serially diluted prior to plating. Lysates were plated on LB supplemented with 34 μg/mL fluconazole and YPD supplemented with 32 μg/mL chloramphenicol to isolate K. pneumoniae and C. neoformans cells, respectively. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and 30 °C for 48, for followed by bacterial and fungal enumeration, respectively. Experiment was performed in biological quadruplicate and technical duplicate.

Mutant Construction

Generation of all mutant and complementation strains was obtained by biolistic transformation as previously described.46,47 Briefly, gene deletion constructs were generated by double-joint PCR adjoining upstream and downstream homologous regions (∼1000 bp) of CNAG_05997 to the Nourseothricin (NAT) resistance cassette (generously provided by Dr. J.P. Xu, McMaster University). Stable transformants were selected on YPD-NAT (100 μg/mL) agar and confirmed by diagnostic PCR, and two independent mutants were verified for the correct genomic deletion by southern blot or whole-genome sequencing.

Complementation PCR constructs were first generated by Gibson assembly (NEB) joining the CNAG_05997 promoter (∼1000 bp upstream) and coding region with a 1x-FLAG tag added to the 3′ end of the open reading frame, terminator (∼500 bp downstream), hygromycin B (HYG) resistance cassette, and 3′ downstream region (∼1000 bp) into pSDMA58 (generously provided by Dr. James Fraser, University of Queensland). Transformation constructs were amplified from the resulting plasmid. Stable transformants were selected on YPD-HYG (200 μg/mL) agar, confirmed by PCR, southern blot, and western blot. Primers and plasmids are provided (Table S1).

Fungal Virulence-Associated Phenotypic Assays

Polysaccharide Capsule

Polysaccharide capsule production of fungal strains was assessed following fungal growth overnight in YPD, followed by overnight subculture in yeast nitrogen base with amino acids (YNB; BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ) supplemented with 0.05% dextrose; cells were washed twice in low-iron media (LIM; prepared as described previously48) and inoculated into LIM for 24 h at 37 °C. Capsule production was visualized with India ink dye (Hardy Diagnostics) on a Zeiss Axiovert microscope 200 M equipped with a Hamamatsu C10600 Orca R2 camera at 100× magnification under differential interference contrast. Capsule thickness was quantified from a minimum of 50 cells across three biological replicates per strain using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html).

Melanin Pigmentation

Melanin pigmentation of fungal strains was assessed following growth overnight in YPD, followed by a 1:100 subculture in YNB overnight, and a final 1:100 subculture in minimal media (MM; Chelex 100-treated (Bio-Rad) dH2O containing 29.4 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM MgSO4–7H2O, 13 mM glycine, 3 μM thiamine, and 0.27% dextrose) overnight at 30 °C. Cells were spot-plated (106 cells/5 μL) on MM agar plates supplemented with 1 mM l-DOPA (Sigma-Aldrich). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for up to 7 days, with images taken every 24 h. The percentage of melanin pigmentation was quantified as previously described.49 Briefly, each image was 8 bit grayscale-converted, and the gray value (0–255) of colonies was calculated from a set area (50 × 50 pixels) on ImageJ software and converted into a percentage of pigment using the formula [(255 subtracted by the mean grayscale value for each spot)/255] x 100. Experiments were completed in biological and technical triplicate.

Thermotolerance

Thermotolerance of fungal strains was assessed following growth in YPD overnight, followed by normalization of cultures to OD600nm 0.02 in YNB and incubated at either 30 or 37 °C. Fungal growth OD600nm measurements were obtained on a BioTek HM1 plate reader every 15 min over 72 h. Experiment was completed in biological quintuplicate and technical duplicate.

LDH Quantification

Host cell cytotoxicity assays were completed by coculture of immortalized BALB/c macrophages with fungal strains as previously described.47 Briefly, macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates at 0.05 × 106 cells/well and grown until ∼70–80% confluency following 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in DMEM pen/strep. Fungal cells were grown to mid log phase in YPD at 30 °C, collected at 1,500g for 10 min, washed twice in PBS, and resuspended in DMEM without pen/strep. Fungal cells were opsonized with mAb18B7 (1 μg: 106 cells) for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Macrophages were infected at a MOI of 10 (fungal/macrophage cells) for 90 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Following coculture, infected macrophages were washed with PBS, and fresh DMEM was added for the remainder of the assay. To quantify macrophage death, the culture supernatants of infected macrophages were collected at 3, 18, 27, and 48 h postinoculation (hpi), and the percentage of cell death was quantified by LDH release with the CytoTox96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Experiment was performed in biological triplicate and technical duplicate.

Statistical Analysis

To assess statistical differences between samples [e.g., protein identification numbers and colony-forming unit (CFU) counts], we performed a paired Student’s t-test. Significance values presented as ns = nonsignificant. *p-value <0.01, **p-value <0.001, ***p-value <0.0001, and ****p-value <0.00001.

Results

DIA-PASEF Profiling Provides Increased Depth of Pathogen Proteome Coverage

A critical limiting factor of infectious disease research is the detection of low-abundance pathogen-associated proteins within complex matrices of the host. Under these conditions, confirming host response to infection is routine and robust; however, understanding how the pathogen is adapting to its environment and identifying potential novel virulence factors driving the infection are limited. Such undetectable pathogen proteins that influence virulence are prime examples of antivirulence candidates that may serve as novel druggable targets to disarm the pathogen and enable the host to clear the infection.50−53 To explore the dynamic relationship between a host and pathogen during infection with consideration of burden and time (Figure 1A), and to expand upon our previous findings of coinfection modulation of host response,13 we developed an innovative proteomics workflow (Figure 1B). This workflow provides the first comparison of DDA- and DIA-PASEF profiling during macrophage coculture of C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae, along with a dual species comparison, to provide new insights into how the host and pathogens adapt during infection.

Single- and coinfection workflow for MS-based proteomics profiling. BALB/c macrophages were cocultured with C. neoformans H99, K. pneumoniae, or a combination of the pathogens across a MOI range of 10:1, 25:1, 50:1, and 100:1 for 90 min. Nonphagocytosed cells were removed, and samples were collected and subjected to our total protein extraction protocol, including cell lysis, protein precipitation, digestion, and purification. Peptides were measured on a mass spectrometer using the DDA- or DIA-PASEF technology. Experiment was performed in biological quadruplicate. Figure generated with Biorender.com.

Using a traditional DDA approach enabled with PASEF technology, we identified an average of 4515 proteins compared to a DIA-PASEF approach, which identified an average of 5819 proteins across the experiment (Figure 2A). Next, we defined the distribution of proteins across the distinct biological systems and reported 88% host (M. musculus), 11% C. neoformans, and 0.5% K. pneumoniae via DDA-PASEF (Figure 2B) compared to 85% host, 14% C. neoformans, and 1% K. pneumoniae via DIA-PASEF (Figure 2C). With consideration of pathogen burden across the experiments, we reported an increase in pathogen-associated proteins correlating with increased MOI during fungal infection of macrophages in both DDA- and DIA-PASEF modes with more pathogenic proteins associated with DIA-PASEF (Figure 2D,E and Table S2). In particular, we observed a 2-fold increase in our ability to detect bacterial proteins within the complex host system using DIA-PASEF. This increased depth of coverage for both fungal and bacterial pathogens within the complex host system is a promising early observation for DIA-PASEF profiling.

DIA-PASEF Reveals a New Infection-Associated Proteome of C. neoformans

Across the dynamic MOI range for C. neoformans and macrophage, we confirmed a correlative significant increase in fungal burden via CFU counts correlating with initial inoculum preparations (Figure 3A). These findings support our experimental design and ability to recover C. neoformans following phagocytosis by macrophages within the coculture model. Importantly, we detected significantly more fungal proteins across the range of evaluated MOIs using DIA-PASEF compared with DDA-PASEF profiling (Figure 3B). These results demonstrate the ability of DIA approaches to detect 10-fold lower fungal burden yields within complex mixtures, a critical consideration for disease diagnostics.54 In support of the improved detection of fungal proteins in the presence of macrophages, we tracked the protein intensity of all fungal proteins within the sample using DDA-PASEF (Figure 3C) and DIA-PASEF (Figure 3D) measurements. Notably, we observe a significant increase in protein intensities from the DIA-PASEF samples that aligns with the fungal burden loads quantified by CFU counts and the initial inoculum cell counts, further supporting the sensitivity and specificity of DIA-PASEF, especially with respect to protein quantification. Finally, we collated all fungal proteins detected from the single pathogen infection of macrophages and across a continuum of MOIs and reported a core C. neoformans proteome consisting of 630 proteins shared by the analytical methods with an additional 118 proteins uniquely defined via DIA-PASEF that were absent from the DDA-PASEF measurements (Figure 3E). Importantly, of these 118 newly defined proteins, we detected several proteins associated with virulence, including phospholipase B (important for dissemination to the central nervous system55), Sec14 (required for secretion of phospholipase B156), glyoxal oxidase (detected in the blood following cryptococcal infection in mice57), and alpha-1,3-glucan synthase (required for proper polysaccharide capsule anchoring to the cell wall58), supporting the value of DIA-PASEF in the discovery of novel putative antivirulence candidates. Importantly, for the highly abundant host proteins, we defined a core proteome between PASEF profiling methods of 5235 proteins with an additional 329 proteins detected by DDA and an impressive 994 (>3-fold higher) uncovered exclusively by DIA (Figure S1A). Together, we reveal a previously undetected proteome of C. neoformans from complex host–pathogen profiling, with putative functional roles during macrophage infection and potential targets for antifungal activity.

DIA-PASEF Increases Detection of Low Abundant Bacterial Proteins

As with C. neoformans above, we confirmed a correlation in MOI values and bacterial burden via CFU counts, along with initial culture preparations, during a single pathogen coculture (Figure 4A). A significant increase in CFU counts between the MOI 50:1 and MOI 100:1 was reported. These findings further support our experimental design and ability to recover K. pneumoniae following phagocytosis by macrophages within the coculture model. A comparison of bacterial protein identifications between DDA- and DIA-PASEF showed a significant increase with the latter approach; however, in the context of the highly abundant host proteome, detection of K. pneumoniae proteins was very low regardless of the MOI and the corresponding initial inoculum cell counts (Figure 4B). Next, we profiled the intensities of all detected bacterial proteins following DDA-PASEF (Figure 4C) and DIA-PASEF (Figure 4D) measurements. We observed a consistent trend between CFUs and protein identifications using DIA-PASEF with a significant increase between MOI 50:1 and MOI 100:1. Notably, we observed consistent levels of protein abundance across DDA with a drop in bacterial intensities at MOI 50:1 using DIA. This observation may be correlated with bacterial cell death or reduced phagocytosis at these elevated burden levels, and further investigation into host proteins produced exclusively during at MOI 50:1 may provide new biological insights into the relationship between bacterial burden and host defense efficacy. Alternatively, given the low abundance of bacterial proteins within the host proteome, our setting of 1% FDR may also influence the K. pneumoniae identifications with a lower FDR (i.e., 5%) perhaps showing the anticipated pattern. Finally, we compared all bacterial proteins detected from the single pathogen infection of macrophage across a continuum of MOIs and reported a core K. pneumoniae proteome of 18 proteins with an additional nine and 49 proteins uniquely measured using DDA- versus DIA-PASEF, respectively (Figure 4E). Of these 49 proteins, we detected a sensor histidine kinase (EnvZ; a sensor of the two-component regulatory system59), outer membrane proteins (OmpC; associated with susceptibility to ß-lactams60), and a UTP-glucose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (GalF; involved in polysaccharide production60), confirming detection of virulence-associated bacterial proteins using DIA-PASEF. As reported above, for the highly abundant host proteins, we defined a core proteome between PASEF profiling methods of 5767 proteins with an additional 257 proteins detected by DDA and an impressive 1358 (>5-fold higher) uncovered exclusively by DIA (Figure S1B). Together, DIA-PASEF of K. pneumoniae-macrophage coculture provides new insights into bacterial regulation via protein production during infection.

Dual-Infection Profiling Confirms Host and Pathogen Proteome Remodeling and Increased Pathogenic Depth Using DIA-PASEF

Building upon our previous discoveries of the interplay between C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae during macrophage infection,13 we performed dual-pathogen coculturing with immortalized macrophage across a continuum of MOIs. As observed during single-pathogen infections, the burden loads for both pathogens increased with a higher MOI; a nonsignificant increases was observed for K. pneumoniae, whereas a significant increase at each MOI was observed for C. neoformans (Figure 5A).13,61 As with single infections, we observed a significant increase in the detection of both fungal and bacterial proteins using DIA-PASEF compared with DDA-PASEF methods (Figure 5B). Notably, for host protein intensities, the average abundance levels show a slight decrease across increasing MOIs for DDA-PASEF (Figure 5C) and consistent intensities for DIA-PASEF (Figure 5D) measurements. We observed a core host proteome of 4315 host proteins between the analytical methods with an additional 370 and 927 uniquely defined via DDA- or DIA-PASEF, respectively (Figure 5E). Host proteins unique to DIA-PASEF included well-characterized immune-associated proteins such as interleukins (e.g., IL16), cytokine receptors (e.g., IL2), toll-like receptor (e.g., Tlr9), and tumor necrosis factor receptor (e.g., Traf2), supporting the capacity of our workflow to broadly define the host response to infection.

For the pathogens, we defined protein intensities for C. neoformans using DDA-PASEF (Figure 5F) or DIA-PASEF (Figure 5G) and observed a similar stepwise pattern with DIA measurements during the dual-pathogen infection as with only C. neoformans. A significant increase in protein intensities for DIA-PASEF was observed between MOIs of 25:1 vs 50:1 and 50:1 vs 100:1, correlating with the CFU counts, whereas with DDA-PASEF, this alignment was only observed at the higher MOI comparison. In addition, we observed a core fungal proteome of 248 proteins common to both measurement methodologies with 37 and 242 proteins uniquely defined via DDA- or DIA-PASEF, respectively (Figure 5H). Although the depth of fungal proteome coverage during the dual pathogen infection was less than the single infection, we consistently detected proteins across experiments (e.g., glyoxal oxidase), as well as additional well-defined virulence-associated proteins, including superoxide dismutase (roles in virulence and macrophage survival62), catalase (involved in antioxidant defense63), and alpha-amylase (associated with glucan production and virulence64).

For K. pneumoniae, we observed similar protein intensities across MOIs via DDA-PASEF (Figure 5I) but a marked correlation between protein intensities and CFU counts with an increase in both as the MOIs increased using DIA-PASEF (Figure 5J); this latter observation was more pronounced during the dual-pathogen infection than that with K. pneumoniae alone. All measurements showed a nonsignificant change across MOIs. Moreover, we defined a small core proteome of K. pneumoniae consisting of only nine proteins common to the analytical methods, whereas 10 and 26 additional proteins were uniquely defined via DDA- and DIA-PASEF, respectively (Figure 5K). Similar to the single bacterial infection, we detect EnvZ and OmpC unique to DIA-PASEF profiling, along with additional outer membrane proteins (e.g., major outer membrane lipoprotein, a receptor for antimicrobial peptides65) and a transcriptional regulator (e.g., LacI, regulator of motility, virulence, and antibiotic production in other bacteria66). Interestingly, we do not observe the same reduction in protein identifications at MOI 50:1 during bacterial coinfection vs single infection, despite similar total bacterial identifications. This observation supports further investigation into host biological drivers of bacterial reduction at MOI 50:1 compared with the other burden loads. Alternatively, assessment of differences at the peptide level to provide more data points may support observation of the anticipated differences. Taken together, DIA-PASEF continues to provide deeper coverage of the host and pathogen proteomes, including the detection of critical proteins driving pathogen virulence and the associated immune response.

Proteome Remodeling of C. neoformans upon Macrophage Coculture Defines Unique Burden-Associated Responses

Given our previous findings in the identification of novel antifungal targets of C. neoformans,47 our observations of cryptococcal modulation within macrophages,13 and our discovery of over 100 fungal proteins associated with single infection only detected using DIA-PASEF, we aimed to better define and characterize remodeling of the fungal proteome during cryptococcal infection of macrophages. Here, a principal component analysis revealed separation between the infected (MOIs 10:1, 25:1, 50:1, and 100:1) and uninfected samples (component 1, 48.3%), followed by distinct responses based on fungal burden (i.e., MOI) (component 2, 11.9%) (Figure 6A). Next, we evaluated proteome remodeling of each biological system by identifying proteins with significant changes in abundance as the fungal burden increased (Figure 6B). At a MOI of 25:1 (compared to the lowest fungal burden of a MOI at 10:1), we did not observe any significant changes in protein abundance. As the fungal burden increased (i.e., MOI of 50:1), we observed 373 fungal proteins (and 13 host proteins) with a significant increase in abundance compared to four fungal proteins (and seven host proteins) with a significant decrease. Finally, at a MOI of 100:1, we observed substantial remodeling of the fungal proteome with 724 fungal proteins (and 37 host proteins) with a significant increase in abundance compared to five fungal proteins (and 84 host proteins) with a significant decrease in abundance compared to a lower MOI of 10:1.

Next, focusing on the 118 uniquely defined C. neoformans proteins that were exclusive to DIA-PASEF profiling, we highlighted 80 fungal proteins with significant increases in production as the fungal burden rose (Figure 6C). To gain better insights into the functions of these fungal proteins, we explored their interactions using the STRING database.43 We defined five clusters (by K means clustering) to reveal protein interactions across the fungal proteome associated with signaling and translation (19 proteins), metabolism and biosynthesis (14 proteins), nuclear and transcription (nine proteins), the proteosome (eight proteins), and uncharacterized (3 proteins); the remaining 27 proteins were not available for mapping in the STRING database (Figure 6D; Table S3). Together, these findings support fungal proteome remodeling during macrophage infection with increased pathogen protein detection and distinct proteome profiles as fungal burden increases.

DIA-PASEF Profiling Defined a Novel Virulence Determinant and Putative Druggable Target in C. neoformans

Based on our previous findings that inhibition of the ubiquitin proteosome pathway reduced capsule production in C. neoformans,67 suggesting a novel drug-repurposing strategy to combat the pathogen, we explored the role of a putative new druggable target uncovered using DIA-PASEF. We focused on the proteasome-associated proteins that were exclusively identified by our DIA-PASEF profiling and highlighted an uncharacterized protein (i.e., CNBG_4739, an orthologue of CNAG_05997) for further investigation (Figure 7A). We tracked the production of the protein across the MOI continuum and confirmed an increase in abundance upon elevation of the fungal burden (Figure 7B). Next, to characterize the role of CNAG_05997 in the production of classical cryptococcal virulence factors,68 including the polysaccharide capsule, melanin, growth, thermotolerance, and macrophage infectivity, we constructed two independent gene deletion strains (confirmed by southern blot; Figure S2).

Given the importance of the polysaccharide capsule in evasion of host defenses (e.g., phagocytosis) and environmental survival (e.g., desiccation),69,70 we performed differential interference microscopy to detect changes in capsule production upon disruption of CNAG_05997. Excitingly, we observed a significant reduction in capsule production in the absence of CNAG_05997 compared with WT C. neoformans (Figure 7C,D). Next, based on the importance of melanin production by C. neoformans for protection from ultraviolet light within the environment and reactive oxygen species production in the host,71 we assessed an impact on melanin production in the absence of CNAG_05997. As mentioned above, we observed a significant reduction in melanin production in the absence of CNAG_05997 compared to that in WT C. neoformans (Figure 7E). We also profiled changes in fungal growth in the absence of CNAG_05997 at 30 °C and observed a delay in growth, followed by a recovery to WT and gene complementation levels. Area under the curve (AUC) assessment of the C. neoformans strain growth profile reported an overall significant reduction in mutant growth despite the mutants’ ability to recover to WT cell density levels (Figure 7F,G). We extrapolated this observation to assess an impact of CNAG_05997 on the thermotolerance of C. neoformans, a critical factor influencing the ability of the fungus to survive and proliferate at human physiological temperature.72 Here, we observed a significant reduction in fungal growth in the absence of CNAG_05997 compared to the WT and complemented strains (Figure 7H,I). Finally, given our experimental design of C. neoformans coculture with macrophages, we evaluated the impact of the CNAG_05997 deletion strains on the cytotoxicity of macrophages through quantification of LDH release.73 We observed a significant reduction in virulence of the deletion strains compared with WT-infected macrophages with similar levels of cell death compared to the uninfected control (Figure 7J,K). Overall, DIA-PASEF profiling of the relationship between C. neoformans and macrophages across a continuum of fungal infection identified a novel putative druggable target that was not previously characterized or explored in the context of disease.

Discussion

In this study, we extensively compared DDA- and DIA-PASEF technologies on a timsTOF Pro2 with single-infection and coinfection models. We compared the capabilities of the technologies to detect and quantify low-abundance bacterial and fungal proteins within a complex macrophage system across a broad inoculum continuum. Our experimental MOI inoculum was corroborated by quantified fungal and bacterial intracellular burdens that reflect the DIA-PASEF protein intensity values for single-fungal and dual-fungal and bacterial infections. We provide novel insights into the production of virulence-associated pathogen proteins facilitated by DIA-PASEF sensitivity that was not detected in DDA-PASEF measurements, providing opportunities to deepen our knowledge of host–pathogen interactions. Our data complement and advance the current knowledge of low-abundance protein identification and indicate the capacity of DIA-PASEF to innovate infectious disease research.

We report the increase in the detection of unique pathogen proteins by DIA-PASEF technology compared to DDA-PASEF at lower (MOI 10:1) and higher (MOI 100:1) ratios of pathogen to host. Specifically, we observed a DIA-PASEF increase of unique protein identification of 15.8 and 52.7% from single and 38.9 and 35.6% from dual coculture of C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae with macrophages, respectively. Despite this impressive increase in pathogen protein detection, the distribution of pathogen proteins identified across the distinct biological systems is below 16% of total protein IDs, with K. pneumoniae minimally represented by less than 1% of global proteome coverage. Moreover, observations of total protein identifications from DDA-PASEF (5045 host, 638 fungal, and 31 bacterial proteins) and DIA-PASEF (6027 host, 987 fungal, and 70 bacterial proteins) indicate an improvement with the adoption of DIA-PASEF technology; however, these values highlight the limited number of pathogen proteins detected from infected macrophages across both approaches. In comparison, a cross-kingdom analysis of C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae at an MOI of 5 and 100, respectively, reported detection of 2292 host, 126 fungal, and 163 bacterial proteins on an Orbitrap Exploris 240 instrument operated in the DDA mode.13 Furthermore, the investigation of spleen-associated cryptococcal biomarkers from infected mice reported 2373 host and 746 fungal proteins with DDA and tandem mass tag labeling on an EvoSep One interface with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 instrument.16 These studies highlight our noticeable DIA-PASEF boost in coverage for both host and fungal proteins yet demonstrate the shortcomings of bacterial protein detection.

Possible explanations for differences in protein identifications across pathogens may be due to the disparate cellular sizes of the species (macrophages, ∼21 μm; C. neoformans, ∼5 μm; and K. pneumoniae, ∼2 μm) and the corresponding cellular mass contributing to protein identification as well as the capabilities of the macrophage to engulf and contain a high inoculum of two pathogens. This underscores the need to continue the technological pursuit of improving the sensitivity for low-abundant pathogen detection by MS, as many more proteins remain to be identified below the current limit of detection. For instance, our analysis captured a small portion of the pathogen proteomes at the highest MOI for both DDA- and DIA-PASEF; thus, to sufficiently measure the pathogen proteome within the highly abundant host proteome with current technologies, a nonphysiologically relevant level of pathogen inoculum would be needed to for increased pathogen proteome coverage. However, artificially elevated pathogen inoculums may alter the pathogens’ capacity to cause disease.29 For example, the pathogenic effect of C. neoformans features diminishing returns with increasing inoculum toward Galleria mellonella, in which each individual cell contributes less pathogenicity than at a lower inoculum.28 Thus, we require continued improvement of MS technology and bioinformatics tools to ensure realistic observations of infectious niches. Additionally, we did not assess for pathogen-specific differences of infection rates between C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae and during single vs coinfections. Briefly assessing the CFU counts of both pathogens following single vs coinfections, we do observe a slight reduction at the lower MOI during single infection compared to coinfection, along with a reversal of this observation at higher MOIs. This observation suggests that further investigation into infection rates between the pathogens and across infection states may provide new insights into pathogenesis and host interplay during infection.

Coinfections and secondary microbial infections wreak havoc on healthcare systems, and these infections are mainly produced by opportunistic pathogens, often resulting in more severe clinical complications and outcomes than a single infection. Therapeutic interventions and resulting prognosis become greatly hampered by late or incorrect diagnostic decisions; thus, accurate diagnosis of coinfections is of critical importance.74 The difficulty in diagnosing an invasive fungal infection contributes to the high mortality rate (>50%) in which an estimated 80% of patients could be saved with universally available diagnostics and treatments.75 These fungal diagnostic challenges arise from the nonspecific nature of symptoms, often resulting in misdiagnosis and the dependence on conventional methods requiring laborious culturing and invasive sample collection, substantially increasing the time to diagnosis.76 MS-based clinical proteomics was recently highlighted for its potential to detect and characterize potential secondary infections from COVID-19 patients measured from nasopharyngeal swabs, from which C. neoformans and K. pneumoniae were present pathogens.77 Our study provides an opportunity to enhance the sensitivity and accuracy of current MS-based clinical proteomics and overcome the challenges of fungal and bacterial coinfection diagnostics with DIA-PASEF. This technology offers capabilities to efficiently improve early pathogen recognition of primary and imminent secondary infections, thus drastically reducing diagnostic time and diverting the precious “golden hours” toward therapeutic intervention. Our comparison of DDA- and DIA-PASEF highlights the beneficial power of DIA approaches to measure pathogen proteins that accurately reflect the MOI inoculum. The expanded DIA-PASEF coverage depth gained a 2-fold increase in K. pneumoniae protein identification and revealed a new infection-associated proteome of C. neoformans. Additionally, this technology shows promise for fungal diagnostics based on its capability to detect 10-fold lower fungal burden yields within complex mixtures compared with a traditional DDA approach.

The perpetual neglect of fungal diseases and shared human homology of potential fungal drug targets have engendered an insufficient arsenal of antifungal therapeutics. To date, there are only four major classes of antifungal drugs, all of which have promoted the emergence of antifungal-resistant strains, often resulting in treatment failure.78 These dire circumstances emphasize the necessity to invigorate research toward discovering and developing new therapies and interventions. We report the identification of a new C. neoformans infection-associated proteome consisting of 118 proteins with a notable subset of 80 fungal proteins displaying significant increases in protein intensity in proportion to rising fungal burden levels. Prioritization of pathogen proteins featuring increasing production signatures correlated to fungal burden provides a favorable circumstance for inhibitor-based studies, as the druggable protein of interest is consistently produced regardless of burden level, allowing efficient target inhibition proportional to the abundance of the invading pathogen. Furthermore, C. neoformans maintains interactions with macrophages (i.e., monocytic-derived and tissue-specific) throughout multiple stages of cryptococcosis, providing an optimal niche for investigating host–fungal interactions.61 Thus, our described workflow for drug target selection based on infection-associated pathogen proteins overcomes the challenge of many proposed antivirulence drug targets that are dependent on protein inhibition during a specific infection stage, such as the inhibition of metalloprotease, Mpr1, to prevent C. neoformans invasion of the central nervous system.79

Finally, of the 80 DIA-PASEF-exclusive fungal proteins, we prioritized an uncharacterized proteasome-associated protein, CNAG_05997, for functional characterization of classic C. neoformans virulence factors. Remarkably, the deletion of CNAG_05997 resulted in a complete disturbance of virulence factor elaboration, including decreased capsule and melanin production, thermotolerance, and reduced macrophage death during infection. An in silico search of CNAG_05997 revealed a putative fungal specific oxidoreductase subunit (24.7% identity to human homologues; blastp search) associated with the mitochondrial respiratory chain, indicating its potential for enzymatic inhibition. These exciting findings motivate future investigations into CNAG_05997 and its druggability. Commonly, target-based drug discovery is initiated with a small molecule screening campaign, often resulting in minimal fully validated drug targets requiring additional screening;80 however, integration of proteomics profiling and advanced bioinformatics tools supports computer-aided drug discovery for identifying potent inhibitors, beginning with identification of protein target subunits featuring high inhibitory potential, followed by screening of an in silico compound library for docking and binding affinities to rapidly prioritize a list of optimal inhibitors.81 This rational approach was recently used to explore potential inhibitors for the C. neoformans virulence factor, urease.82 We continue our investigation of CNAG_05997 in the pursuit of potential inhibitors via computer-aided drug discovery.

Conclusions

Using comparative proteome profiling of single and dual infection of macrophages with DDA- vs DIA-PASEF methods, we report increased pathogen proteome coverage with an emphasis on the value of a newly discovered fungal infection-associated proteome. Our findings are exciting and clearly demonstrate the power of new analytical methods to explore dynamic biological systems within an infectious disease context to reveal new biological insights that may positively impact our ability to treat fungal disease in the future. Overall, the increase in diaPASEF precision and accuracy holds promise for the future of host–pathogen research and the discovery of novel druggable targets with critical roles in pathogen virulence.
