Blazing a Trail for Fire Management and Smoke Health Collaboration: Takeaways From the 2024 Rocky Mountain Wildfire Smoke Symposium
Authors: Taylor Stewart, Savannah D’Evelyn, Jennifer Faiella, Fernando Sanchez, Kerri Mueller, Luke Montrose, Ethan S. Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWildfire activity in the United States is increasing due to climate change, land management practices, and human ignitions, reversing decades of air quality progress. Wildfire is an essential process in fire‐adapted ecosystems, but fine particulate matter (PM2.5) from wildfire smoke poses significant health risks both near the fire source and in communities far from fire‐prone areas. Public health and forest management are often viewed as having conflicting goals—reducing smoke exposure versus restoring fire to ecosystems—but opportunities for collaboration exist. We analyzed an interdisciplinary panel discussion from the 2024 Rocky Mountain Wildfire Smoke Symposium (RMWSS) using thematic analysis and the RADaR technique to identify such opportunities. Four major themes emerged: (a) coordinated communication between stakeholders, (b) barriers and facilitators to bridge building across disciplines, (c) impacts of climate change and (d) priorities and perspectives across disciplines. Additionally, we synthesized the panel discussion and audience polling data into a figure that categorizes solutions by perceived investment, impact, and stakeholder responsibility. High impact objectives included advancing climate resilient community infrastructure, expanding resource sharing, and securing full‐time equivalent (FTE) funding for smoke specialists and communication liaisons. Collaboration across disciplines, combined with long‐term policy that reduces barriers for safe fire management while investing in clean air will be critical to addressing the wildfire crisis.

Introduction

In recent decades, there have been drastic improvements in overall air quality in the United States (US) through advances in policy, technology, and clean energy (US EPA & OAR, 2015). In contrast, ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations have increased and reversed air quality progress across most of the contiguous US due to increasing wildfire activity as a result of climate change, forest management practices, and unplanned human‐related ignitions (Abatzoglou & Park Williams, 2016; Burke et al., 2023; McClure & Jaffe, 2018). In part, climate change is extending the length of wildfire seasons (Flannigan et al., 2013; Senande‐Rivera et al., 2022) and leading to more extreme fire events, such as the California wildfires in January 2025 and the record breaking 2020 and 2023 wildfire season in the US and Canada, respectively (Jain et al., 2024). Humans also play a direct role in increasing wildfire activity, with human‐started wildfires accounting for the majority of all wildfires in the US and resulting structure loss (Balch et al., 2017; Higuera et al., 2023). Furthermore, development in wildfire prone areas, such as the wildland urban interface (WUI), is increasing, directly exposing more people to fire and smoke impacts (Guo et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2025; Radeloff et al., 2018). And yet, health impacts of people who do not live in the WUI or wildfire prone areas are often overlooked, even though they can be exposed to long‐range wildfire smoke and PM2.5 that contains more oxidative and proinflammatory components (Wong et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2023). The worsening wildfire crisis underscores the need for wildfire and smoke preparedness that extends beyond traditional wildfire seasons and covers a broader range of locations.

PM2.5 is associated with adverse health outcomes including respiratory and cardiovascular disease, all‐cause mortality (Liu et al., 2015; Reid et al., 2016), and decreased mental health (Eisenman & Galway, 2022; Humphreys et al., 2022). By 2090, climate models predict there will be a 60% increase in premature mortality from wildfire smoke emissions, costing an estimated health burden of 36–43 billion US dollars per year (Neumann et al., 2021). Due to the serious health risks of PM2.5, public health and environmental agencies at local, state, Tribal, and national levels have developed strategies to disseminate information and resources on air quality, health risks of PM2.5 exposure, and actions people can take to reduce exposure, including creating clean indoor air spaces and reducing PM2.5 emissions where controllable.

In contrast to public health efforts (including local air quality programs, environmental regulatory agencies, and researchers), where reducing smoke exposure is a primary goal, in forest management, fire is seen as a natural and necessary ecosystem process that inevitably creates smoke. As a result, forest managers often see efforts to eliminate smoke and suppress fires as exacerbating the fire deficit and creating more extreme fires now and in the future (S. M. D’Evelyn et al., 2022; Kreider et al., 2024). Land managers intentionally introduce prescribed fire to fire‐adapted ecosystems to reduce wildfire severity, foster ecological resilience and biodiversity (Davis et al., 2024; Ryan et al., 2013), and reduce hazardous fuels, especially in the WUI (Florec et al., 2019). While prescribed fires do produce smoke, when all goes to plan, they may produce less smoke compared to wildfires (Jaffe et al., 2020; Marsavin et al., 2023), though emission amounts vary. Unlike wildfires, prescribed fires are planned; both fire management and air pollution control agencies work together to conduct prescribed fires on days with conditions optimal for smoke dispersion, with the goal of reducing impacts of smoke emissions on nearby communities.

As the wildfire crisis continues, land management and public health are often perceived to have opposing interests in solving it. However, the increasing frequency of large wildland fires, amount of people at risk, and need for more fire on the landscape demonstrate the necessity of stakeholder collaboration rather than opposition. It is important for professionals in the fields of public health and land management to engage in interdisciplinary efforts to dually reduce smoke exposure, while improving forest management with prescribed burns. Toward this goal, since 2020, the Rocky Mountain Wildfire Smoke Symposium (RMWSS) has been held as an opportunity for collaboration across disciplines. In this paper, we analyze an interdisciplinary panel discussion from the 2024 RMWSS, which focused on collaborative solutions to the wildfire crisis.

Background

In the spring of 2019, not realizing that 2020 would bring about one of the worst US fire seasons in recorded history, the idea for a Rocky Mountain region‐specific wildfire smoke conference was born. The vision for the meeting was to provide a platform for information sharing and collaboration across disciplines (e.g., academics, industries, non‐profits, government personnel and community partners) that would specifically address challenges faced in the Rocky Mountain region relating to wildfire smoke and health.

Since the inaugural RMWSS in 2020, four consecutive symposia have been organized. These meetings were held in a variety of formats and rotated between different host institutions. The scope of the symposium and evolution of topics over five years is summarized in Figure 1, as well as the breakdown of 2024 attendees. The 2024 RMWSS included attendees across eight self‐described industries, including 19 in education, 48 in government, two in healthcare, two in news, 10 in non‐profit, 23 in public health, 35 in research and nine in other. Audience members represented 12 different states and the District of Columbia in the US, in addition to two international attendees from Canada and Chile.

Methods

Thematic Analysis

The primary contribution of this manuscript is a thematic analysis of an interdisciplinary panel discussion that was held on the final day of the 2024 RMWSS. This panel was organized as an opportunity to amplify ideas from the prior RMWSS sessions and considered the question: How can we better coordinate to have shared messages and comprehensive strategies (infrastructure, tools, policies, etc.) to protect the public from smoke? The panel was moderated by a trained facilitator and consisted of six people including: a fire ecologist, an air quality specialist, a pulmonologist, a wildland firefighter, a federal smoke management coordinator, and a professor in environmental and Native American studies. A transcript of the panel was recorded verbatim and analyzed by a qualitative research team using thematic analysis and the five steps of the Rigorous and Accelerated Data Reduction (RADaR) technique (Watkins, 2017). To begin, the author organized transcripts (Step 1) and transferred them into an all‐inclusive table for each qualitative research team member (TS, SD, JF) to individually read (Step 2). Next, researchers took notes, made preliminary codes and reduced data according to the overarching research question (Step 3). The qualitative research team met regularly to further reduce information, form deeper interpretations of the data, and develop a shared coding scheme with descriptions (Step 4). After a shared coding scheme was created, researchers coded all data independently. Researchers met several times afterward to talk through differences in codes to resolve discrepancies and ensure consistent interpretation of the data. Each code was discussed and added into the results if there was majority agreement on the explanation (Step 5). Finally, codes were organized by the research team into higher level themes and deliverables. The key recommendations of this analysis were summarized in Table 1.

Synthesis of Panel Discussion Themes, Polling Data, and Symposium Sessions

The secondary contribution of this manuscript is a synthesis of the RMWSS panelist discussion, polling data, and symposium sessions. While the panelists were experts intentionally selected to represent the different disciplines involved in managing wildfires and smoke, a wider pool of 46 audience members were also asked to interact and offer input during the panelist discussion. Due to this, the panel was able to build off of additional expertise and collaborations in the room. After accessing a link to the polling platform, audience members were able to submit and read anonymous responses, as well as vote on ideas they agreed with. Questions included: What is one thing people assume about your discipline, what is one thing you assume about other disciplines, and what other ideas do you have for combined efforts to better prepare for the future of smoke? Although we did not do a formal thematic analysis of these responses, some of the answers to these questions were written in the results and discussion section, to deepen interpretations of the thematic analysis from the panel discussion. Finally, the authors met to synthesize insights from the polling responses, panel discussion themes and session presentations to develop Figure 2. Solutions incorporated in Figure 2 were identified from author discussions and collaboratively categorized according to their perceived level of investment and impact. Specifically, solutions were grouped as (a) high investment, high impact, (b) high investment, low impact, (c) low investment, high impact, (d) low investment, low impact. This categorization was used to help with prioritization of solutions discussed during the symposium. In addition to characterizing solutions by investment and impact, stakeholders were identified in Figure 2 to highlight responsibility and interests across fields.

Results and Discussion

We identified four major themes including (a) coordinated communication between stakeholders, (b) barriers and facilitators to bridge building across disciplines, (c) impacts of climate change, and (d) priorities and perspectives across disciplines. These themes were inclusive of all the participants on the panel and were reflective of the presentations during the symposium, broadly. Each theme contained codes or “categories” and subcodes or “subcategories” to help maintain data richness. Definitions of categories and subcategories are provided in Table 1. The panel data was summarized into recommendations, to capture important discussion points that were left out of key quotes. We provide examples of the data in text and in Table 1. Following the table, the results are organized by theme and inclusive of data from the panel discussion, audience poll, symposium sessions, and the literature.

Coordinated Communication Between Stakeholders

Coordinated communication between stakeholders was the most referenced theme throughout the panel discussion. Similarly, “cross agency communication” was one of the top responses in the audience poll. Panelists talked about the challenges of coming up with a common message and distilling jargon between fields to gain public trust so that “regardless of who you're hearing from, you're hearing the same thing again and again.” Audience poll responses expanded on this idea, mentioning the need to work collaboratively but put information out with the most trusted partner and direct the public to a common website or app for information. Literature shows that trusted sources of wildfire smoke communication differ depending on the community (Sandoval et al., 2025); our panelists mentioned how collaborating with the community and co‐developing plans provide major opportunities to build trust. Shared messaging and holistic thinking incorporate perspectives of other fields, and the understanding that wildfire acts as a system that impacts forest and human health. For example, one panelist stated that they could improve on “addressing the fact that we need fire on the land” when talking about air quality and wildfire smoke. Complementarily, a different panelist mentioned “There is an opportunity for me or my agency to use [public health] in both our burn plan writing and in our outreach.” Professional advocacy was also mentioned as an important communication tool. Poll responses echoed panel discussion in this matter, emphasizing the need for more healthcare provider education on patient counseling related to the health impacts of wildfire smoke.

Notably, panelists mentioned that although it is important to create a common and shared message, fire and smoke professionals should recognize that “from a diversity standpoint, when it comes to communication, there is not a one‐size‐fits‐all thing.” Panelists recommended that smoke messaging be dispersed to a variety of channels and recognize that how people take action to protect themselves depends on social determinants of health. For messaging to be actionable, panelists recommended incorporating resources on air quality and communication about “what do you do right now with the smoke exposure that you have.” Additionally, actionable messaging should include accessible options such as sheltering in a public clean air space or building a box fan filter. Research has demonstrated how social determinants, including education and income, are associated with higher levels of implementation of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems and air filters, as well as a higher amount spent on air filters (Castillo et al., 2024). Recent reviews of wildfire smoke communication mentioned similar communication strategies to those discussed by panelists, highlighting the need for messages with specific actions to avoid smoke exposure, concentrating efforts to address gaps in messaging specific to at‐risk populations, and understanding how communication impacts behavior change (Coker et al., 2024; Vien et al., 2024).

Barriers and Facilitators to Bridge Building Across Disciplines

Siloing, local capacity to do the work, and research cycles and timescales were all mentioned as barriers to bridge building across disciplines. Opportunities to connect in person, including conferences and invitations to participate in or observe a prescribed burn can help foster professional relationships across disciplines. As one panelist put it, “getting on the ground and working with the people you make these decisions for is so important and really helps both sides to understand each other better.” Nevertheless, there have been limited meetings in the US to engage interdisciplinary stakeholders on issues at the intersection of wildfire and smoke (Davison et al., 2021; S. M. D’Evelyn et al., 2023; Errett et al., 2019). Community resources and local capacity to do the work were also mentioned as a major gap in both conducting prescribed burns and protecting public health during the panel discussion. One qualitative study including 54 interviews from land managers and air quality regulators across 11 western states found that adequate capacity and funding were the most commonly cited barriers to increasing the application of prescribed fire and that obtaining air quality permits was not the primary barrier land managers face (Schultz et al., 2019). Barriers to prescribed burning in the western US bear resemblance to other fire‐adapted, forested ecosystems in North America (Ryan et al., 2013; Williams et al., 2024) and similar places where prescribed burning is underused, globally (Fernandes et al., 2013). However, barriers across the US may vary since permitting processes and liability laws for prescribed burns vary, complicating institutional coordination (Smithwick et al., 2024). Additionally, many public health departments and federal agencies are under‐resourced and understaffed, making it difficult to hire a dedicated smoke specialist or coordinator.

Panelists were encouraged by federal funding sources that provided opportunities for special collaboration, attention, and resources dedicated to the rising threat of wildfires. However, when collaborating on grants, it is important to note the differences in timescales by which research and management occur. As summarized by the panel facilitator, “Land managers and wildland fire fighters make decisions in minutes, right? When we're thinking about forestry decisions, sometimes that's hundreds of years where we're planning ahead. And then things that are maybe more interagency thinking about funding is within 10 to 20 years. We need to think about how that may change what our collaborations are going to look like.” Due to these challenges, the success of interdisciplinary collaborations often depends on effective leadership and team‐building across disciplines, as well as institutional commitment (National Academy of Sciences, National Academy of Engineering and Institute of Medicine, 2005). Collaborative governance and processes to support resource sharing among federal, state, and non‐governmental partners, as well as investment in positions for staff member liaisons between agencies are integral to the success of wildfire and smoke management moving forward (Schultz et al., 2019). One example of this mentioned during a presentation in the RMWSS was the expansion of the Montana Department of Environmental Quality's monitoring network. The network plans to balance monitoring requirements and needs against available resources using a tiered approach to measuring PM2.5, including personal air quality sensors and federal equivalent method (FEM) monitors (Montana Department of Environmental Quality, 2024).

Climate Change Impacts

Another theme mentioned during the panel discussion was adapting and preparing for the impacts of climate change. Panelists discussed the need to recognize that wildfire and smoke are inevitable, one stating “I often think of [climate change] as pushing us into a corner… it used to be that we could say, well, we want no smoke and we're going to eliminate fires. But increasingly, we're kind of boxed into this corner where we have to figure out how much smoke is acceptable and what are the impacts of that smoke.” Recent management practices and research have advocated for prescribed burning as a mechanism for restoring forests that were impacted by years of fire suppression, as well as a tool for climate adaptation. Indigenous peoples have incorporated burning for a wide range of reasons, including land management and cultural practices for millennia (Kimmerer & Lake, 2001; Roos et al., 2021, 2025; Taylor et al., 2016). However, fire of any kind increases the frequency of smoke and requires prioritizing and protecting vulnerable communities and individuals. One panelist brought up the point “if people don't have the resources and tools to take the actions that we're telling them to, then we're stuck, right? Or some people will be able to, and other equity issues will just become worse.” Equitable investment in resources and climate adaptation and mitigation activities are opportunities to address health disparities, as health costs are rising under climate change (Clarke et al., 2023; Romanello et al., 2024).

Priorities and Perspectives Across Disciplines

The final theme of the panel discussion was priorities and perspectives across disciplines. Panelists emphasized the need to protect communities from smoke and fire before a wildfire occurs and how prescribed fire is an opportunity to practice coordinated messaging. The West Bend Prescribed Fire Pilot on the Deschutes National Forest in Oregon in 2024 is an effective example of an inter‐agency effort to implement wide‐scale prescribed burns, while centering public health and risk‐reduction efforts (Huber‐Stearns & Downey, 2024). Panelists also talked about the need to protect life, property, and public lands from fire, while protecting health from smoke. Local wildfires and long‐range wildfire smoke may have different impacts, but the consequences of both are important to consider. As one panelist shared, “a lot of our attention is going to protecting homes in the urban interface,” another panelist noted “although those [East Coast] communities are not receiving smoke every summer, that smoke in Connecticut, that could be the smoke episode that sends someone to the hospital. And so, I think on a more national scale…we need to prepare for wildfire smoke.” Solutions moving forward should involve both wide‐scale smoke ready messaging recognizing short‐term risks of exposure and creating defensible spaces for homes in the growing WUI, especially as more firefighting resources are being used to protect these homes. Finally, addressing the wildfire crisis requires continuous work and an expanded idea of fire seasonality. Best summarized by a panelist, “Much like fire, I think is relentless, we've got to be relentless in our mission to work with it, live with it.”

Limitations

We purposely selected a diverse representation of experts from different fields in wildfire management and smoke health, to deepen the panel discussion and broaden potential solutions; however, this analysis is based on a single regional symposium and there is potential for bias related to panelist selection. Although panelists were based entirely out of the Rocky Mountain region, this region can offer significant contributions to inform public health and fire management, more broadly, due to its high percentage of public land, wildfire smoke emissions, and experts in the field. Another limitation of our panel selection was that we did not have representation from any Indigenous groups or community members, in part due to a scheduling conflict. Despite these limitations, panelists discussed the importance of wide‐scale smoke communication and bottom‐up approaches to fire management. Additionally, while we provided Figure 2 to help conceptualize where priorities are shared and what high‐impact collaborations are possible, we did not do a formal economic analysis to quantifiably determine the investment and impact of these actions. Rather, Figure 2 serves to synthesize panel recommendations, audience poll responses, RMWSS sessions and research into identifiable solutions for prioritization by practitioners.

Conclusion

While few papers have attempted to engage interdisciplinary stakeholders and conduct qualitative analyses to identify research needs and practitioner recommendations on issues at the intersection of fire and smoke in the US (S. M. D’Evelyn et al., 2023; Errett et al., 2019; Schultz et al., 2019), we advance this work by synthesizing our findings in a way that is action and impact oriented. Moving forward, and in consideration of federal, state, and Tribal funding constraints, areas of high impact and low investment should be prioritized to address the wildfire crisis, as represented in Figure 2. Notably, most solutions discussed during the symposium fell within the category of high impact and low investment, including cross‐agency education and shared communications, advocating for accessible solutions to reduce smoke exposure, and expanding networks of cross‐disciplinary collaborators. In spirit of expanding interdisciplinary networks, a handful of collaborations developed as a result of this symposium, including a commentary on the need for longer‐duration and repeated smoke exposures (Sacks et al., 2025) and an ongoing fire‐health science collaboration at the University of Montana.

Beyond working across disciplines, fire management requires integration of top‐down and bottom‐up approaches. For example, top‐down institutions and government agencies may contribute research, monitoring and modeling resources, and policies. On the other hand, bottom‐up community and Indigenous knowledge may reveal observations and concerns that are not present in scientific data or policymaking and help adapt fire management and models to a particular place. Strategic guidelines for engaging communities and Indigenous people in fire management have been identified nationally and globally (FAO, 2024; USDA Forest Service, 2022). While increasing awareness about smoke exposure and health is a common goal of researchers, public health professionals, and clinicians, it has relatively low reach compared to other more actionable and integrated fire management approaches.

Other high impact objectives, such as climate resilient community infrastructure, resource sharing and full‐time equivalent (FTE) funding for smoke specialists and communication liaisons should not be overlooked due to high investment. The West Bend Prescribed Fire Pilot on the Deschutes National Forest in Oregon in 2024 is demonstrative of how investment in inter‐agency collaboration and FTE can lead to safe implementation of larger prescribed burns on expedited timelines (Huber‐Stearns & Downey, 2024). Finally, long‐term policy that reduces barriers for safe fire management while investing in clean air will play an immense role in solving the worsening wildfire crisis and protecting health for generations to come.
