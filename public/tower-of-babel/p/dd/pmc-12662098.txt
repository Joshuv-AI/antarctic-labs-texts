Crosstalk between liver gene expression and enteric microbiome regulates laying performance and lipid metabolism in different breeds of layer hens during growing and pre-laying periods
Authors: Xi Bai, Yuhua He, Yixuan Ge, Shennan Gao, Anjian Li, Taozong Guo, Hong Hu, Hongbin Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present work was conducted to investigate the interaction between liver gene profile and enteric microbiome on regulation of laying performance and lipid metabolism in different breeds of layer hens during the growth and pre-laying periods. A total of 192 7-w-old laying hens (six replicates per breed, 16 hens per replicate), including 96 Jingfen-1 (JF-1) and 96 Jingfen-2 (JF-2) hens, were selected and fed the same feed under identical environments. The experiment lasted from 7 to 20 w-of-age. JF-1 initiated egg production at 16 w, whereas JF-2 started at 18 w. From 17 w, JF-1 exhibited higher egg production rates than JF-2 (P < 0.05). ​ Compared with JF-2, egg weight, Haugh unit, egg shape index, albumen height, and yolk mass were higher in JF-1 eggs (P < 0.05). Hepatic triglyceride, cholesterol, fatty acid synthase, and acetyl-CoA carboxylase contents were higher, whereas lipoprotein lipase and total lipase levels were lower in JF-1 (P < 0.05). There were 363 downregulated and 385 upregulated genes in the liver of the JF-2. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis indicated that pathways enriched in the downregulated genes of JF-2 hens included fatty acid biosynthesis, elongation, and metabolism and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor signaling. Lipid synthesis-related genes such as FASN, ACACA, ELOVL2, and ACSL5 were upregulated in JF-1 hens. The cecal Firmicutes abundance in JF-1 was higher, whereas the Bacteroidetes abundance was lower than that in JF-2 hens (P < 0.05). Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in the cecum of JF-1 hens was higher than in JF-2 hens (P < 0.05). Phocaeicola, Lactobacillus, Mediterraneibacter, and Phascolarctobacterium were more abundant (P < 0.05) in the cecum of JF-1 hens. Correlation analysis suggested that higher Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in the cecum might activate the liver gene expression associated with lipid synthesis, and then improved egg quality in JF-1 hens. Summary, the liver genes and intestinal flora coordinated to promote laying performance and egg quality by modulating physiological fat deposition in JF-1 during the growing and pre-lay periods.Keywords: Breed, Laying performance, Lipid metabolism, Liver gene expression, Enteric microbiome

Introduction

Layer production is a vital component of modern animal husbandry and plays an irreplaceable role in safeguarding food security, driving economic development, and preserving ecological equilibrium (Yang, 2021). As an efficient core industry in poultry farming, layer hens are characterized by a short cycle, fast turnover, low investment, and continuous and stable provision of a high-quality animal protein source (eggs) (Yang, 2021; Arulnathan et al., 2024). Rich in high-quality protein, vitamins, and minerals, eggs are highly cost-effective nutritional carriers, which is of far-reaching consequence in improving dietary structure and enhancing physical fitness in people (Puglisi and Fernandez, 2022; Gautron et al., 2022). The health of layer flocks is a core element for evaluating production performance and directly reflects the quality of feed management in chicken farms. Currently, production entities generally attach importance to chick rearing and laying period management, whereas the refinement of management in the growing period and pre-laying period is insufficient.

The growing and pre-lay periods are critical stages in the physiological development of layer hens and determine egg production, egg quality, feed conversion ratio, and culling period during the laying phase (Wu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2022). Lipid metabolism which reflects the nutritional intake and health status of laying hens is closely associated with their growth, development, and reproductive performance. Thus, it is vital for the development of layer hens during the rearing and pre-laying periods (Van Eck et al., 2023). Liver, the largest and most functionally-diverse glandular organ in laying hens, is involved in multiple physiological activities, such as bile secretion and hormone synthesis, and undertakes important metabolic processes involving carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids (Tang et al., 2023). The liver synthesizes approximately 90 % of the fat and serves as the main site of fat metabolism in hens (Wang et al., 2021). Therefore, the liver of the layer has a substantial impact on health status and productive performance. For hens in the growing and pre-lay periods, whose growth and development are not yet complete, the demand for lipids is relatively high, with frequent turnover of lipid metabolic pathways and remarkable enhancement of the fat-synthesizing function of the liver.

The gastrointestinal tract of laying hens harbors a complex and dynamic microbial community (Geng et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2024a). Intestinal microbial flora play a crucial role in host digestion and absorption, metabolism, and immune regulation (Wu et al., 2021). In recent years, research on the differences in the intestinal microbial characteristics of different poultry breeds has become increasingly in-depth (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Different host breeds and strains have a substantial effect on the diversity of intestinal microorganisms and the composition of dominant microbial flora (Ricke and Rothrock, 2020). Analyzing the influence of the host genotype on the intestinal microorganisms is conducive to formulating reasonable feeding plans for production or regulating the ecological balance of intestinal microorganisms through precise nutritional intervention to improve the productive performance of poultry.

Layer hens at different growth stages exhibit distinct physiological and developmental characteristics, necessitating precise, stage-specific feeding management tailored to their developmental needs (Edwards and Hemsworth, 2021; Bryden et al., 2021). Jingfen-1 (JF-1) and Jingfen-2 (JF-2) are high-performance laying hen breeds characterized by high egg production rates, low mortality rates, and high stress resistance (Zhang et al., 2021). Current research on laying hens focuses on the egg-laying period, whereas studies on lipid metabolism and gut microbiota dynamics during the growing and pre-lay periods are limited. The developmental patterns of JF-1 and JF-2 during these stages have rarely been reported. The goal of this research was to explore variations in laying performance, egg quality, and lipid metabolism in these two breeds. Transcriptomics was used to analyze differences in hepatic gene expression and 16S rRNA sequencing was used to assess variations in cecal microbiota. An integrated analysis was subsequently conducted to investigate the crosstalk between hepatic transcriptional regulation and gut microbiota dynamics and elucidate their combined effect on egg quality and fat metabolism. From the perspective of commercial egg production, the present study provides a scientific basis for the feeding and nutritional management of laying hens during the growth and pre-lay periods.

Materials and methods

Experiment design and sample collection

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yunnan Agricultural University (202202001). A total of 96 healthy (six replicates per breed, 16 hens per replicate), 7-w-old JF-1 and JF-2 laying hens (Yunling Guangdayukou Poultry Industry Co., Ltd., Yunnan, China) were selected for this experiment. Diets (Table 1) were designed following Chinese Chicken Feeding Standards (NY/T 33-2004). Layer hens were maintained under the same conditions from weeks 7–20, with ad libitum access to water. At the end of week 20, 36 eggs per group (six eggs per replicate) were collected for egg quality analysis. 12 hens/group (2 hens/replicate) were slaughtered, and serum, liver, and cecal digesta samples were collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Productive performance and egg quality determination

The laying rate was calculated as the daily egg production (the number of eggs per replicate was recorded daily) divided by the number of layer hens per replicate. The egg mass (g) and yolk mass (g) were recorded using an analytical balance (Sartorius ME204E, Germany) with 0.1 mg sensitivity. Eggshell parameters, including thickness (mm), longitudinal length (cm), and transverse diameter (cm), were determined using digital calipers with 0.01 mm sensitivity. The egg shape index (length-to-width ratio) was calculated from the biometric measurements. Ovalbumen content (%), albumen height (mm), and Haugh unit (HU) were quantified using an automated egg analyzer (Robotmation EMT-7300, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with laser micrometers.

Measurement of total cholesterol (T-CHO), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)

Total protein concentration (the bicinchoninic acid assay) and the TC and TG levels in serum and liver homogenate samples were measured using spectrophotometric analysis with commercial assay kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Commercial kits from Comin (Suzhou, China) were used for the quantification of LDL-C and HDL-C.

Determination of hepatic lipid metabolism-related enzymes

Lipoprotein lipase (LPL), lipase (LPS), hepatic lipase (HL), total lipase (TL), fatty acid synthase (FAS), and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) levels were measured using commercial kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). The enzymatic activities of lipoprotein lipase (LPL), hepatic lipase (HL), lipase (LPS), and total lipase (TL) were quantified using colorimetric assays with commercial kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS) activity were assayed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

Hepatic gene expression profile

Total liver RNA (n = 12/group) was extracted from individual liver samples using a silica membrane-based RNA purification system (Tiangen, China). After quality checking, the cDNA library was constructed according to the method of Hu et al. (2024b), followed by 150-bp, paired-end sequencing using the NovaSeq 6000 system. Sequencing data quality was determined according to the method described by Hu et al. (2024b). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 v1.42.0 with fold change (FC)≥2.0 and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment of adjusted p-values (FDR <0.05) for transcriptome-wide significance assessment. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KOBAS-intelligence.

Cecal microbiome detection

Total DNA of cecal chyme (n = 12/group) was extracted and amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the hypervariable V1–V9 region of the 16S rRNA gene (forward primer 27F: AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG; reverse primer 149R: CRGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT). The PCR amplification was performed by the ABI GeneAmp ® 9700 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) with TransStart® Fastpfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) The detailed PCR mixture and reaction procedure were consistent with those reported in the study of Gao et al. (2022). The PCR products were then recovered using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AXYGEN, San Francisco, CA, USA) and qualified by the QuantiFluor ® ONE dsDNA system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The PCR products from each sample were then equally mixed to construct the SMRTbell Express TPK 2.0 sequencing library, in accordance with the protocol provided by PacBio (Menlo Park, CA, USA). However, it was observed that only 14 of the 20 wild samples and 15 of the 20 captive samples satisfied the standards for PacBio high-fidelity sequencing strategy. Amplicon sequencing was accomplished by a PacBio Sequel platform in Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

PacBio raw reads were processed to obtain demultiplexed circular consensus sequence (CCS) reads using the SMRT Link Analysis software (v9.0) Subsequently, raw reads were processed through the SMRT Portal to filter sequences for length (> 1000 or < 1800 bp) and quality. Subsequent filtration of the sequences was conducted by the removal of the barcode and primer sequences utilizing the LIMA pipeline (Pacific Biosciences demultiplexing barcoded software, https://lima.how/). Following the assignment of CCS reads to specific samples according to their unique barcode, the reads were qualified, assembled and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with 98.65 % similarity cutoff using UPARSE version 10. All OTUs were assigned to a taxonomy based on the “SILVA release 138 database”. Singletons (i.e. number of tags in a specific OTU = 1) were discarded in order to avoid potential sequencing errors.

Statistical analysis

The comparative analysis of data was statistically validated using a Wilcoxon test and SPSS 18.0 software. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Associations between the variables were assessed using ​​Spearman's rank correlation analysis.

Liver transcriptome

The transcriptome sequencing of JF-1 and JF-2 yielded clean reads ranging from 34,743,412 to 79,356,084 bp (Table S1). The Q20 base distribution ranged from 99.14 % to 99.38 %, and the Q30 base distribution ranged from 97.37 % to 98.20 % (Table S1). The percentage of reads mapped to the chicken genome was 88.11–91.35 %, indicating a high sequencing quality (Table S1). The 24 samples from JF-1 and JF-2 exhibited good clustering performance, with strong intragroup reproducibility and distinct intergroup differentiation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4a). Compared to JF-1, JF-2 had 363 downregulated and 385 upregulated genes in the liver (Fig. 4b). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that pathways significantly enriched in JF-2-downregulated genes included fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid elongation, fatty acid metabolism, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling (Fig. 4c). Lipid synthesis-related genes, FASN, ACACA, ELOVL6, ELOVL2, ACSL5, and FADS2 were upregulated in JF-1 hens (Fig. 4d).

Cecal​ bacteria

The 16S rRNA sequencing of JF-1 and JF-2 yielded sequences and bases ranging from 30,065 to 34,639 and 43,927,678 to 50,421,089 bp, respectively (Table S2). The average sequence length was 1452.36–1462.38, and the proportion of optimized sequences (1401–1500) reached 98.28 % (Table S2). Simpson’s alpha diversity analysis indicated differences (P < 0.05) in cecal microbiota diversity between JF-1 and JF-2 hens (Fig. 5a). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) indicated that the microbiome of JF-1 and JF-2 groups were clustered separately (P < 0.05, Fig. 5b). The cecal microbiota of JF-1 and JF-2 were predominantly composed of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Deferribacteres. The dominant phyla were Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria (Fig. 5c). At the phylum level, Firmicutes abundance in JF-1 ceca was higher (P < 0.05), whereas Bacteroidetes abundance was lower (P < 0.05) than that in JF-2 (Fig. 5c). Consequently, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in JF-1 ceca was higher (P < 0.05) than in JF-2 (Fig. 5d). Key genera such as Phocaeicola, Lactobacillus, Mediterraneibacter, and Phascolarctobacterium were more abundant (P < 0.05) in the JF-1 ceca than in the JF-2 ceca, whereas Muribaculum, Bacteroides, Christensenella, Lawsonibacter, and Parabacteroides were more abundant (P < 0.05) in the JF-2 ceca (Fig. 5e).

Crosstalk between liver genes and cecal microbial community in liver fat metabolism and egg quality

Modulatory interactions between liver genes and cecal microbial community in relation to liver fat metabolism and egg quality are shown in Fig. 6. Based on the strong correlations identified, we inferred the potential mechanisms underlying egg quality and lipid metabolism in JF-1 and JF-2. The F/B ratio in the cecum (by increasing Lactobacillus and Phascolarctobacterium in the Firmicutes phylum, and decreasing Muribaculum and Bacteroides in the Bacteroidetes phylum) was higher in JF-1 hens, which activated the liver gene expression associated with lipid synthesis. This gene expression upregulated the activity of ACC, FAS, and LPS enzymes in the liver and downregulated LPL enzyme activity, thereby promoting the synthesis and accumulation of fatty acids in the liver. This resulted in elevated TG and TC levels in the liver, along with increased TG levels in the blood, and ultimately improved egg quality (eggshell thickness, HU, albumen height, and yolk weight) in JF-1 hens.

Discussion

Egg production rate serves as an important indicator of laying hen performance, directly influencing farm profitability (Mollenhorst et al., 2006). Age at the onset of lay substantially affects the timing and duration of peak egg production (Tůmová and Gous, 2012; Tůmová et al., 2017). Although a 5 % egg production rate is commonly used as the threshold for defining the onset of lay, variations in age at the onset of lay among breeds lead to differences in peak period initiation and sustainability. Breed-specific patterns in egg production traits have been reported (Shi et al., 2023; Weng et al., 2019). Tůmová et al. (2017) observed differences in laying pattern and egg weight among six layer strains under identical conditions. Jung et al. (2024) reported divergent growth curves for the egg production rate, egg weight, and keel length across breeds. In the current study, JF-1 hens exhibited an earlier age at onset of lay and substantially higher egg production rates than the JF-2 hens, indicating accelerated progression into the peak laying period for JF-1.

Egg quality is a critical indicator of avian production performance and directly influences economic returns in layer production, adaptability for food processing, and consumer acceptance (Rondoni et al., 2020). The key evaluation indices of egg quality include HU, egg weight, eggshell thickness, egg shape index, and albumen height. HU and albumen height reflect egg freshness, grading, and albumen viscosity, demonstrating a strong correlation with egg shelf life (Narushin et al., 2021; Quan et al., 2021). Eggshell quality is the most important external indicator of egg quality, and shell thickness and strength directly determine breakage rates during storage and transportation (Zhang et al., 2021). The egg shape index also affects hatchability in parent flocks, and optimal values can effectively reduce breakage and enhance packaging efficiency (Alasahan and Copur, 2016). Generally, egg quality is positively correlated with HU, eggshell thickness, egg shape index, and albumen height (Narushin et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Alasahan and Copur, 2016). Genetic background has been consistently identified as a major determinant of egg quality variation across different layer strains (Preisinger, 2018; Yang et al., 2025). This study identified statistically significant differences in egg quality between JF-1 and JF-2 hens, with JF-1 displaying better overall egg quality.

The rate of hepatic lipid metabolism directly influences the speed of yolk formation and, consequently, egg production rates in laying hens (Ding et al., 2021). TG, T-CHO, HDL, and LDL synergistically regulate lipid metabolism in laying hens, collectively maintaining lipid homeostasis, and playing vital roles in metabolic regulation, health maintenance, and productive performance (Li et al., 2024). TG, the primary storage form of body fat and energy source, is hydrolyzed into fatty acids and glycerol for β-oxidation to generate cellular energy (Chitraju et al., 2017). This contributes to yolk synthesis, in which most yolk fat exists in triglyceride form, directly affecting reproductive performance and egg yield. TC serves as a core component of lipid metabolism and is ubiquitously present in cell membranes to maintain fluidity and stability (Casares et al., 2019). TC regulates reproductive capacity and egg production via hormone synthesis and bile acid formation in layer hens. LDL mainly functions to carry cholesterol produced in the liver to peripheral tissues (e.g., the ovaries), supplying the substrate required for yolk formation (Van Eck et al., 2023). In contrast, HDL mediates the reverse cholesterol transport mechanism, recycling peripheral cholesterol back to the liver for metabolism, thereby maintaining cholesterol homeostasis and reducing lipid deposition in the vascular and ovarian tissues (Ouimet et al., 2019). Variations in physiological and biochemical parameters across different layer strains exhibit stage-dependent differences, ultimately resulting in disparities in productive performance and egg quality (Kumar et al., 2022). In the current study, JF-1 serum TG, hepatic TG, and T-CHO contents were substantially higher than those of JF-2, suggesting that the lipid synthesis capacity of JF-1 exceeded that of JF-2.

Lipid metabolism in laying hens is a complex process regulated by multiple enzymes including ACC, FAS, HL, LPS, LPL, and TL (Hu et al., 2024a). These enzymes jointly regulate the synthesis, decomposition, transportation, and utilization of fat through synergistic or antagonistic effects, which are of key significance for performance in lay, yolk formation, and energy balance (Hu et al., 2024b). Fatty acid synthesis is cooperatively catalyzed by FAS and ACC (Svensson et al., 2016). Specifically, ACC drives the production of malonyl-CoA from acetyl-CoA, and FAS subsequently employs this intermediate to generate long-chain fatty acids with acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA as substrates (Svensson et al., 2016). TL, including LPL and HL, catalyzes the hydrolysis and synthesis of fatty acids, thereby regulating fatty acid levels and maintaining lipid balance in organisms by regulating lipid synthesis and decomposition (Shang and Rodrigues, 2021). The lipid metabolism pattern in the liver of poultry is strongly influenced by genetic background (Cui et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2018). In the liver of JF-1 hens, the enzymatic activities of FAS and ACC were higher, whereas the enzymatic activities of HL, LPL, and TL were lower than those of JF-2. Kang et al. (2022) suggested that the expression profiles of lipogenic genes (ACC and FAS) were modulated by species and age, suggesting a hierarchical relationship in orchestrating lipid biosynthesis and storage mechanisms. Based on these findings, JF-2 may mediate hepatic lipid deposition by modulating enzymes involved in lipid metabolism.

Transcriptome sequencing comprehensively analyzes mRNA in specific cells or tissues using high-throughput sequencing (Stark et al., 2019). PCoA of the sample relationships indicated that the JF-1 and JF-2 groups were clustered separately, indicating that JF-1 and JF-2 hens had distinct hepatic gene expression profiles. In total, 748 DEGs were identified between the JF-1 and JF-2 hens. Relative to JF-1, JF-2 displayed 385 upregulated genes and 363 downregulated genes. The expression levels of ACACA, FASN, ELOVL6, ACSL5, ELOVL2, PLN2, FADS2, ME1, and PCK2 in the livers of JF-1 hens were substantially higher than those in JF-2 hens. ACACA and FASN are key rate-limiting enzymes in de novo fatty acid synthesis, regulating fatty acid biosynthesis (Liu et al., 2023, 2025). ACACA catalyzes the carboxylation of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA, whereas FASN mediates de novo synthesis of long-chain palmitic acid (palmitate) from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA (Liu et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024b). Their enzymatic activities directly dictate the liver's capacity for fat synthesis and are crucial for regulating adipose accumulation traits in animals.

Palmitic acid serves as a key substrate for ELOVL6, ELOVL2, and ACSL5, which are key regulatory factors in lipid metabolism that participate in fatty acid synthesis, activation, and subsequent utilization through synergistic interactions (Blaess et al., 2024). ELOVL6 primarily mediates the elongation of palmitic acid into long-chain saturated fatty acids (Sunaga et al., 2016). ELOVL2 specializes in extending long-chain fatty acids, mainly by converting medium-long-chain fatty acids (C20-C24) into very-long-chain fatty acids (C26-C30) (Vaz et al., 2025). Some long-chain saturated fatty acids generated by the elongation of ELOVL2 and ELOVL6 serve as substrates for ACSL5, which converts them into acyl-CoA (Blaess et al., 2024). This process creates a metabolic flux within the lipid metabolic network, which collectively influences the intracellular lipid homeostasis and functional diversity. Additionally, PLN2, FADS2, ME1, and PCK2, which are associated with the PPAR signaling pathway, exert critical regulatory effects on lipid synthesis (Jester et al., 2016). The upregulated expression of genes associated with lipid anabolism in the liver tissue of JF-1 suggests that the pathways associated with the above genes, fatty acid biosynthesis, elongation, and metabolism and PPAR signaling pathway, play critical roles in lipid metabolism in the livers of both JF-1 and JF-2 hens.

The intestine is the main site for poultry to digest and absorb nutrients, and is closely related to growth and development (Celi et al., 2017). The intestinal microbiota is an important indicator of animal health (Khan et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2022). The PCoA analysis indicated statistical differences in the intestinal microbiota between JF-1 and JF-2 hens, which may be one of the reasons for the differences in egg quality, lipid metabolism, and liver gene profiles (Dai et al., 2022). The cecal microbiota of JF-1 and JF-2 were composed predominantly of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes. Firmicutes (increased Lactobacillus and Phascolarctobacterium) in the ceca of JF-1 hens was higher than that of JF-2, whereas Bacteroidetes was lower than that of JF-2. Firmicutes enhance energy extraction by secreting digestive enzymes, activating fat synthesis genes, and promoting fat absorption and storage (Magne et al., 2020). Bacteroidetes (decreased Muribaculum and Bacteroides in this study) inhibit fat absorption and synthesis by regulating bile acid metabolism and enhancing fatty acid oxidation (Zhang et al., 2024). An increase in the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes indicates enhanced lipid anabolism, which is conducive to lipid deposition (Chen et al., 2019; Magne et al., 2020). In this study, the increase in the F/B ratio in the ceca of JF-1 hens suggests that liver lipid metabolism may regulate the liver gene expression profile through dynamic changes in F/B, promoting liver lipid deposition and consequently improving egg quality in JF-1 hens.

Conclusions

JF-1 had an earlier age at first egg than JF-2, and the laying rate was greater than that in JF-2. Additionally, JF-1 exhibited stronger lipid synthesis capacity in the liver than JF-2. Liver genes and intestinal flora synergistically promoted egg production and egg quality by modulating physiological lipid deposition in JF-1 during the growing and pre-lay periods. The present study suggests that liver fat deposition and an optimal gut F/B ratio in pullets are conducive to improved egg production.
