Low-intensity stimulation drives macrophage efferocytosis via ACSL4 lipid remodeling and CCL9-CCR1 signaling for tendon-bone healing
Authors: Juncheng Yao, Yuhao Wu, Xuan Wang, Jiexin Zhang, Yan Shao, Huabin Chen, Haiyan Zhang, Haobin Li, Jianying Pan, Yitao Zhao, Daozhang Cai, Denghui Xie, Chun Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mechanisms underlying exercise rehabilitation–induced tendon-bone healing remain unclear. Using a mouse model of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, we found that low-intensity mechanical stimulation promoted macrophage M2 polarization, phagocytosis, and efferocytosis at the tendon-bone interface via acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)–mediated lipid metabolism reprogramming. Acsl4 silencing reduced fatty acid oxidation and efferocytosis, impairing exercise rehabilitation–induced tendon-bone healing. Notably, the CCL9-CCR1 axis contributed to bone marrow stromal cell homing following macrophage efferocytosis. In addition, engineered CCL9-expressing exosomes accelerated tendon-bone repair.

INTRODUCTION

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is a common sports injury that, if untreated, can severely impair knee function, accelerate osteoarthritis onset, and reduce quality of life. In severe cases, it may lead to joint disability, imposing a substantial burden on patients, families, and health care systems (1). Surgical reconstruction is the primary treatment to restore knee function, with an estimated 165,000 ACL reconstructions (ACLRs) performed annually in Europe (2). However, 3 to 7% of patients require secondary surgeries (3), and in the US alone, more than 10,000 undergo revision procedures each year (4). The predominant cause of these revisions is graft failure, with technical errors (most commonly nonanatomic tunnel placement) accounting for 40 to 60% of cases and biologic failure (poor graft-to-bone integration) contributing 20 to 30%—an important yet modifiable factor—followed by recurrent trauma (5). Therefore, enhancing postreconstruction recovery to prevent reinjury remains a critical challenge.

Successful ACLR depends on effective graft integration within the bone tunnel (6, 7). Early postoperative exercise improves knee function and tendon-bone healing, in part by promoting macrophage M2 polarization to reduce the intensity of inflammatory response at the repair site (8). Bioimplantable materials, such as magnesium-ion–treated periosteum and bone marrow stromal cell (BMSC)–derived exosomes, further facilitate healing by suppressing M1 macrophage polarization and enhancing M2 polarization (9, 10). Two macrophage populations contribute to tendon-bone interface remodeling: resident tissue macrophages and monocyte-derived infiltrating macrophages. Hays et al. (11) reported that macrophages accumulate at the interface postreconstruction and differentiate into distinct subtypes, suggesting that their polarization directly influences tendon-bone healing (11). Mechanical stress has been identified as a key regulator of macrophage function (12, 13). Tan et al. (14) demonstrated that low-intensity treadmill exercise enhances tendon–bone healing and M2 polarization at the Achilles tendon insertion in mice (14). In addition, in the field of oncology, it has been reported (15) that mechanical stress loads act on the Piezo1 channel, up-regulating genes associated with M2-like macrophages in orthopedic and soft tissue repair models. Similarly, Chen et al. (16) reported that primary cilia mediate mechanical stimulation to regulate the osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation potential of nestin-positive BMSCs through the actin-Hippo/YAP pathway, thereby promoting the healing process at the bone-tendon interface (16). However, the mechanisms by which mechanical stress modulates macrophage function during tendon-bone healing remain unclear.

Macrophage activation is tightly linked to metabolic reprogramming. M1 macrophages rely on aerobic glycolysis and fatty acid biosynthesis to sustain inflammation and pathogen clearance, whereas M2 macrophages shift to fatty acid oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation to promote tissue repair and resolve inflammation (17). Efferocytosis—the clearance of apoptotic cells—provides macrophages with fatty acids for oxidation and directly influences their functional state (18). Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) is a pivotal regulator of fatty acid metabolism, playing a vital role in metabolic processes (19). It esterifies CoA to specific polyunsaturated fatty acids, remodels cell membrane phospholipids, regulates steroidogenesis, and balances eicosanoid biosynthesis (20–22). However, the role of ACSL4 in macrophage efferocytosis and tendon-bone healing remains unknown.

In this study, we demonstrate that low-intensity treadmill exercise promotes tendon-bone healing after ACLR by up-regulating ACSL4, which enhances macrophage lipid metabolism reprogramming, M2 polarization, and apoptotic cell clearance via efferocytosis. This process reduces inflammation at the tendon-bone interface and, through the CCL9/CCR1 axis, facilitates BMSC homing, accelerating tendon-bone integration. These findings underscore the potential of engineered CCL9-expressing exosomes as a therapeutic strategy for improving tendon-bone healing outcomes.

RESULTS

Low-intensity treadmill exercise promotes tendon-bone healing after ACLR

To evaluate the impact of rehabilitation intensity on tendon-bone healing, we established an ACLR model in C57BL/6 mice and subjected them to treadmill exercises of varying intensities (Fig. 1A and fig. S1). Exercise parameters are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging confirmed the sagittal positioning of the reconstructed ACL, with the area outlined by the red dashed line representing the region of interest (ROI) for both micro-CT scanning and histological analysis (Fig. 1B). Mechanical loading at the tendon-bone interface was analyzed under different rehabilitation protocols, revealing that higher running speeds increased loading intensity, particularly at the tendon-bone entry point (fig. S2). Tendon-bone healing was assessed at 4 and 8 weeks after the surgery using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson, and Safranin-O and Fast Green staining (Fig. 1C). Histological scores indicated that low-intensity treadmill exercise markedly enhanced healing, characterized by increased cellularity, a more continuous and integrated tendon-bone interface with dense Sharpey-like fiber penetration, and greater fibrocartilage formation. In contrast, medium- and high-intensity exercise delayed healing, as evidenced by a discontinuous interface with gaps and fibrous tissue interposition, lower cellular density, absent fibrocartilage, and bone resorption. Statistical analysis confirmed significantly higher histological scores in the low-intensity group compared to all other groups (Fig. 1D and fig. S3A).

To assess knee joint function, biomechanical testing was performed to evaluate the mechanical strength of grafts. Only instances of graft dislodgement or loosening were included in the statistical analysis, while graft ruptures within the joint cavity were excluded (Fig. 1E). At 8 weeks, the low-intensity treadmill group exhibited significantly greater maximal load and stiffness than the control group, whereas the medium- and high-intensity groups showed no significant differences from controls at either time point. These findings suggest that low-intensity treadmill exercise improves the mechanical performance of the reconstructed graft within the ACLR model. However, because native ACL mechanical properties were not included as a benchmark in the present study, these data should be interpreted as relative improvement rather than evidence of full restoration of native ACL function (Fig. 1F and fig. S3B).

Further micro-CT analysis of bone tunnel remodeling revealed that at 8 weeks, the low-intensity treadmill group had a significantly smaller bone tunnel area (Fig. 1G) and higher bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) values, indicative of increased bone formation (Fig. 1H and fig. S3C), compared to controls and other exercise groups. The medium- and high-intensity groups exhibited larger bone tunnel areas than the low-intensity group but no significant differences compared to controls. Collectively, these results demonstrate that low-intensity treadmill exercise improves structural and biomechanical healing of the reconstructed graft and promotes bone formation at the tendon-bone interface in mice.

Low-intensity mechanical stimulation enhanced macrophage polarization toward M2 and improved phagocytic function

To investigate the mechanism by which low-intensity treadmill rehabilitation accelerates tendon-bone healing, we isolated BMDMs and BMSCs from mice and cultured C3H/10T1/2 fibroblasts. These cells were subjected to three different mechanical stimulation intensities (5, 10, and 20% strain; 0.5 Hz; 24 hours) using the Flexcell system (Fig. 2A). Before cyclic loading, BMDMs were allowed to fully adhere to the flexible silicone membrane and displayed a spread-adherent morphology without an obvious uniform preferential orientation. Because continuous live-cell imaging was not performed during loading, the instantaneous cell configuration during each stretch cycle was not quantified. Immediately after the 24-hour stimulation period, the cells remained adherent and were collected for downstream analyses. The strain levels were selected to approximate in vivo tendon-bone interface loading profiles: 5% for low-intensity physiological conditions, 10% for moderate rehabilitation, and 20% for high-intensity overload, based on reported tendon strains in human and animal models (23). mRNA expression of Piezo1 and Yap1 in BMDMs and BMSCs was significantly up-regulated in BMDMs and BMSCs following 5% tensile stimulation, indicating an early mechanotransduction response in macrophages (Fig. 2B). Given that macrophage infiltration has been shown to enhance graft biomechanical properties after ACLR (24), these findings suggest that macrophages are key mediators of tendon-bone interface remodeling. To explore this further, BMDMs were subjected to varying mechanical stress intensities. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) results showed that 5% mechanical stress suppressed the mRNA levels of proinflammatory genes, including Tnf, Il1b, and Il6, while promoting M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 2C and fig. S4A), indicating that low-intensity stress reduces inflammation and shifts macrophages toward a reparative phenotype. Western blotting (WB) confirmed this, demonstrating increased CD206 expression and decreased NOS2 expression in macrophages under low-intensity stress (fig. S4B). To assess the functional effects of this polarization, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on macrophages after 24 hours of low-intensity mechanical stimulation (fig. S5, A to D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant up-regulation of genes associated with the “Phagosome” pathway, suggesting enhanced macrophage phagocytic function (Fig. 2D). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated fewer apoptotic cells at the tendon-bone interface in the low-intensity treadmill group, supporting the in vivo enhancement of macrophage phagocytosis (Fig. 2E). To validate these findings, we quantitatively analyzed macrophage phagocytic activity under different mechanical stimuli using immunofluorescence and flow cytometry. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that macrophages subjected to low-intensity mechanical stimulation exhibited significantly greater phagocytic activity toward apoptotic cells (Fig. 2F). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the macrophage phagocytic ratio under low-intensity mechanical stimulation compared to other groups (fig. S4C). Collectively, these results indicate that low-intensity mechanical stress enhances M2 macrophage polarization and markedly improves macrophage phagocytic function.

Enhanced M2 macrophage polarization improves phagocytic function

To further assess the impact of macrophage polarization on phagocytic function, we examined macrophages in the low-intensity treadmill rehabilitation group. Immunofluorescence staining revealed increased expression of CD206 and MerTK at the tendon-bone interface, suggesting that low-intensity mechanical stimulation promotes M2 macrophage polarization and enhances phagocytosis and efferocytosis of apoptotic cells in vivo (Fig. 3, A and B). Furthermore, we stimulated BMDMs using the Flexcell system in vitro and cocultured them with apoptotic cells for a duration of 2 hours. Axl and MerTK are TAM family receptor tyrosine kinases that play an important role in macrophage efferocytosis (25). We found that the mRNA levels of Axl and Mertk were significantly higher in the group subjected to low-intensity mechanical stimulation (fig. S6). This suggests that low-intensity mechanical stimulation promotes phagocytosis and efferocytosis of apoptotic cells by macrophages in vitro.

To further investigate this mechanism, we used transgenic mice with enhanced M2 polarization (Rheb1KO) and enhanced M1 polarization (TSC1KO), together with age-matched wild-type controls (Fig. 3C). Growth analysis showed no significant difference in body weight among the three groups at 8 weeks of age, indicating that the genetic modifications did not overtly affect development (fig. S7). After ACLR, immunofluorescence analysis showed that Rheb1KO mice exhibited an increased proportion of CD206-positive macrophages and a reduced proportion of NOS2-positive macrophages at the tendon-bone interface compared with wild-type controls, whereas TSC1KO mice showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 3D). Cleaved caspase-3 staining at 1 and 2 weeks after surgery was also lower in Rheb1KO mice, consistent with more efficient clearance of apoptotic cells. Consistently, WB analysis of polarized BMDMs showed higher CD206 expression in Rheb1KO cells and higher NOS2 expression in TSC1KO cells (Fig. 3, E and F). Flow cytometry further showed that macrophages from Rheb1KO mice had a greater phagocytic capacity than those from wild-type or TSC1KO mice (Fig. 3G). Together, these findings support the conclusion that an M2-biased macrophage state is associated with enhanced phagocytosis and efferocytosis during tendon-bone healing.

Macrophage phagocytosis and efferocytosis enhance tendon-bone healing

To further investigate the impact of macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and efferocytosis on tendon-bone healing, we established the ACLR model in three mouse strains and assessed tendon-bone healing at 4 and 8 weeks postsurgery (Fig. 4A). Histological staining at 4 weeks revealed that Rheb1KO mice, with enhanced phagocytosis and efferocytosis, exhibited increased cell growth, a more compact tendon-bone interface, and early fibrocartilage formation, whereas TSC1KO mice, with reduced phagocytic and efferocytic activity, displayed a less dense interface. By 8 weeks, Rheb1KO mice showed abundant fibrocartilage formation, whereas TSC1KO mice exhibited fewer fibrocartilage connections compared to the other groups (Fig. 4B). Histological scoring and quantitative analysis confirmed that Rheb1KO mice had significantly higher tendon-bone healing scores, suggesting that enhanced phagocytosis and efferocytosis accelerate tendon-bone healing (Fig. 4C). Biomechanical testing was performed to assess graft strength in Rheb1KO and TSC1KO mice. At both 4 and 8 weeks, Rheb1KO mice exhibited higher maximal load and stiffness in grafts, whereas TSC1KO mice showed lower maximal load and stiffness at 8 weeks, indicating that enhanced phagocytosis and efferocytosis improves graft mechanical properties and accelerates functional recovery of the knee joint (Fig. 4, D and E). Micro-CT analysis of bone formation in the bone tunnel further revealed that Rheb1KO mice had smaller bone tunnel areas and higher BV/TV values, indicating enhanced bone formation and ingrowth at the tendon-bone interface (Fig. 4, F and G). In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that enhanced macrophage phagocytosis and efferocytosis promote tendon-bone healing by improving graft mechanical properties and facilitating bone formation, ultimately supporting the functional recovery of the knee joint.

Low-intensity mechanical stress promotes M2-like macrophage polarization through ACSL4-dependent lipid metabolic remodeling

To investigate how low-intensity mechanical stimulation promotes M2 macrophage polarization, we performed metabolomics analysis and identified marked lipid metabolism reprogramming, including enhanced fatty acid oxidation, in macrophages following stimulation (Fig. 5A and fig. S8A). To further investigate, we induced differentiation of BMDMs. After applying low-intensity mechanical stimulation (5%, 0.5 Hz, 24 hours), we observed a significant up-regulation in the mRNA expression levels of fatty acid oxidation–related genes (Cpt1a, Cpt1b, Hadh, and Acadm) (fig. S8B). These findings suggest that lipid metabolism reprogramming is a key mechanism by which mechanical stress modulates macrophage function. To further explore this regulatory network, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that Acsl4 expression was negatively correlated with lipid metabolites, suggesting that ACSL4 plays a critical role in mechanical stress–induced lipid metabolism reprogramming in macrophages (Fig. 5B). We confirmed the up-regulation of Acsl4 mRNA and ACSL4 protein in macrophages following mechanical stimulation using qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 5, C and D). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated increased ACSL4 expression at the tendon-bone interface in mice subjected to low-intensity treadmill exercise (Fig. 5E). In addition, we administered PRGL493 (HY-139180, MedChemExpress LLC, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; purity >98%, confirmed by high-performance liquid chromatography) via intraperitoneal injection to mice following ACLR for 8 weeks (250 μg/kg body weight) or vehicle. Histological staining later showed that the enhancement of tendon-bone healing by low-intensity exercise rehabilitation decreased in PRGL493-treated mice, indicating that low-intensity mechanical stress affects tendon-bone healing via ACSL4 (fig. S9, A and B). To determine whether ACSL4 regulates fatty acid oxidation, we applied low-intensity mechanical stress to macrophages for 24 hours and silenced Acsl4 using small-interfering RNA (siRNA; Fig. 5F). qRT-PCR and WB analyses demonstrated down-regulation of Cpt1a mRNA, CPT1A protein, and CD206 protein, while Cpt1b and Hadh mRNA levels were also down-regulated, suggesting reduced fatty acid oxidation and impaired M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 5, G and H, and fig. S8, C and D). We next investigated whether ACSL4 influences macrophage phagocytosis. After 24 hours of low-intensity stimulation, Acsl4 silencing significantly reduced macrophage phagocytosis, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis (Fig. 5, I and J). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that Acsl4 silencing led to a significant decrease in the phagocytic macrophage population (Fig. 5, K and L). These results indicate that mechanical stress regulates macrophage fatty acid oxidation via ACSL4, thereby promoting M2 polarization and enhancing macrophage phagocytosis.

Macrophage efferocytosis accelerates tendon-bone healing by promoting BMSC homing

To investigate how macrophage efferocytosis accelerates tendon-bone healing through the clearance of apoptotic cells, we administered MRX-2843 by intraperitoneal injection to inhibit macrophage recognition of apoptotic cells, thereby reducing their phagocytic and efferocytic activity (Fig. 6A) (26). First, using Rheb1KO mice, we assessed tendon-bone healing following MRX-2843 injection. Histological staining revealed that inhibited macrophage phagocytosis resulted in tissue loosening, decreased cell density, and reduced fibrocartilage formation, leading to lower histological scores (Fig. 6B). Similarly, in the low-intensity treadmill exercise group, MRX-2843 treatment led to disorganized tissue structure, reduced fibrocartilage formation, and lower histological scores (Fig. 6C). We conducted biomechanical testing to assess the impact of MRX-2843 injection on graft performance in two groups. The results showed that in the MRX-2843–treated group, both the maximum load and stiffness of the grafts decreased, suggesting that inhibiting macrophage efferocytosis slowed tendon-bone healing (fig. S10, A and B). To confirm this, we cocultured BMDMs with apoptotic cells in the presence or absence of MRX-2843 and assessed its effect on stem cell migration using Transwell assays (Fig. 6D). Migration assays revealed that BMSC migration was impaired after inhibiting efferocytosis, as shown by reduced crystal violet staining at 6, 12, and 24 hours (Fig. 6E). In addition, scratch wound assays demonstrated that BMSC migration speed was slowed, and wound closure was reduced after blocking macrophage efferocytosis (Fig. 6F). MRX-2843 was administered to Rheb1KO mice and to mice in the low-intensity exercise intervention group after ACLR surgery. To characterize the homing behavior of BMSCs at the tendon-bone interface, we examined the expression levels of the BMSC surface markers CD44 and CD29 at this interface (27). Subsequent immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis showed that blockade of macrophage efferocytosis reduced the number of CD44- and CD29-positive stromal/progenitor-like cells at the tendon-bone interface, suggesting impaired BMSC homing and stem/progenitor cell recruitment. (Fig. 6, G and H, and fig. S11, A and B). These findings suggest that macrophage efferocytosis facilitates BMSC homing, thereby contributing to accelerated tendon-bone healing.

Macrophage efferocytosis up-regulates CCL9 expression and activates CCL9-CCR1 signaling

To further elucidate the mechanism by which macrophage efferocytosis promotes the homing of BMSCs, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data obtained from the tendon-bone healing interface in mice (Fig. 7A and fig. S12A). After filtering and quality control, we identified two macrophage populations: tissue-resident macrophages and monocyte-derived macrophages, consistent with previous findings by Fujii et al. (Fig. 7B) (28). Lahmar, Qods et al. (29) further classified these macrophages into subtypes: tissue-resident macrophages, which act as inflammatory mediators, and monocyte-derived macrophages, which migrate from the blood vessels and contribute to tissue repair. scRNA-seq revealed a progressive increase in the number of monocyte-derived macrophages over time, consistent with previous studies (Fig. 7C). To assess phagocytic function, we applied gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using the Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) phagocytosis gene set, which confirmed that monocyte-derived macrophages exhibit stronger phagocytic activity, making them the primary efferocytosis-active cells at the tendon-bone interface (Fig. 7D). Further CellChat analysis predicted that macrophages communicate with other cell types in the healing microenvironment primarily through CCL chemokine signaling, with CCL9 identified as a prominent predicted ligand-receptor pathway (Fig. 7, E to G). Monocyte-derived macrophages, which were the predominant efferocytosis-active cells, were inferred to interact with other cell types mainly via the CCL9-CCR1 axis, consistent with their markedly elevated Ccl9 expression (Fig. 7, H to J).

To further investigate the effects of macrophage efferocytosis in vitro, we cocultured BMDMs with apoptotic cells and conducted transcriptomic sequencing (Fig. 7K and fig. S12, B and C). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of “cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions” and phagocytic function (fig. S12, D and E). Heatmap analysis of enriched genes confirmed a marked increase in Ccl9 expression (Fig. 7L). In vitro, BMDMs were extracted, subjected to low-intensity intervention, cocultured with apoptotic cells, and treated with MRX-2843. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and qRT-PCR were used to measure changes in CCL9 protein and Ccl9 mRNA expression after efferocytosis. After efferocytosis, CCL9 protein secretion and Ccl9 mRNA levels were significantly increased, whereas inhibition of efferocytosis reduced both CCL9 protein secretion and Ccl9 mRNA levels (fig. S12G). These findings suggest that CCL9 plays a key regulatory role in macrophage efferocytosis both in vivo and in vitro. Similarly, in the low-intensity treadmill rehabilitation mouse model, immunofluorescence staining revealed increased CCL9 expression in macrophages. However, after MRX-2843 injection and PRGL493 treatment to inhibit efferocytosis and ACSL4 expression, respectively, CCL9 expression was decreased, supporting a functional association between macrophage efferocytosis and CCL9 production (figs. S12F and S13A). Injection of PRGL493 and AF463 (a CCL9-neutralizing antibody) diminished the enhancement of tendon-bone healing induced by low-intensity treadmill exercise and inhibited BMSC homing in mice (figs. S14, A and B, and S15, A and B). These findings indicate that low-intensity treadmill exercise influences the tendon-bone healing microenvironment by enhancing macrophage-mediated CCL9 secretion following efferocytosis, thus promoting BMSC homing and tissue repair.

Characterization of CCL9-enriched engineered exosomes

To facilitate the clinical application of CCL9, we used a lentiviral plasmid system to generate a BMSC line overexpressing Ccl9 and producing high levels of CCL9 and subsequently isolated exosomes from this cell line for tendon-bone interface research (Fig. 8A). To characterize the engineered exosomes, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed typical cup-shaped or spherical vesicular morphology, and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that most particles were within the 100- to 200-nm range (Fig. 8, B and C). The measured zeta potential was −14.1 mV (Fig. 8D), indicating a relatively stable exosome suspension. Further, WB confirmed the successful isolation and high purity of the exosomes. The exosome markers CD63 and CD81 were strongly expressed in the exosome (EXO) sample, while the endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin, used as a negative control, was detected in the BMSC sample but not in the EXO sample (Fig. 8E). In addition, CCL9 was detected in the EXO-CCL9 sample, confirming the successful enrichment of CCL9 in engineered exosomes (Fig. 8F).

In vivo retention of exosomes was monitored using a small animal fluorescence imaging system, which revealed a significant decrease in the DiD-only group from days 7 to 21. However, exosomes loaded in the sustained-release hydrogel showed prolonged in vivo retention. Quantitative analysis confirmed that hydrogel-loaded exosomes exhibited longer in vivo retention (7 to 21 days) compared to DiD injected alone, demonstrating the superior sustained-release effect of the exosome-loaded hydrogel (Fig. 8K). These results indicate that loading CCL9-enriched engineered exosomes into the hydrogel prolongs their in vivo retention at the tendon-bone interface and enables more sustained CCL9 release.

CCL9-enriched engineered exosomes accelerate tendon-bone healing

These exosomes were then injected into the tendon-bone interface of mice, along with a hydrogel-only control group (Fig. 9A). The in vivo effects of CCL9-engineered exosomes were examined. Histological staining showed that the hydrogel-only group did not significantly affect tendon–bone healing (fig. S16). Comparing the effects of CCL9 overexpression versus no overexpression on tendon-bone healing, histological staining revealed that, 4 weeks postinjection, the tendon-bone interface in mice treated with CCL9-engineered exosomes exhibited enhanced tissue growth, increased cellularity, and tighter tissue connections, while the exosome-only group showed comparatively fewer effects. At 8 weeks, there was substantial fibrocartilage and bone formation, and histological scores were significantly higher in the CCL9-engineered exosome group compared to both the control and exosome-only groups (Fig. 9, B and C). To assess the mechanical properties of the grafts, we performed biomechanical testing. At both 4 and 8 weeks postinjection, the CCL9-engineered exosome group demonstrated significantly higher maximal load and stiffness compared to the other groups, with statistically significant differences (Fig. 9, D and E). Micro-CT analysis of the bone tunnel revealed that mice treated with CCL9-engineered exosomes had smaller bone tunnel areas and higher BV/TV values compared to the other groups, indicating enhanced bone formation (Fig. 9, F to H). In addition, we examined the effects of CCL9 exosomes and control exosomes on the osteogenic, chondrogenic, and tenogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vivo. We measured the expression of key transcription factors RUNX2 (for osteogenesis), SOX9 (for chondrogenesis), and SCX (for tenogenesis), finding that CCL9 exosomes primarily enhanced RUNX2 expression compared to control exosomes (fig. S17). This suggests that CCL9-engineered exosomes more effectively promote bone formation than the exosome-only treatment. Further immunofluorescence staining demonstrated elevated expression levels of the BMSC-associated surface markers CD44 and CD29 at the tendon-bone interface in the CCL9-engineered exosome-treated group, suggesting enhanced chemotactic activity and increased recruitment of stem cells in vivo in mice compared to the group treated with exosomes alone (Fig. 9I and fig. S11C). Similarly, in the CCL9-engineered exosome group, CCR1 expression was significantly higher compared to the exosome group (Fig. 9J). In addition, we supplemented the tendon-bone interface of low-intensity exercise group mice with CCL9 exosome hydrogel, further enhancing tendon-bone healing (fig. S18). These findings suggest that CCL9-engineered exosomes promote BMSC homing via the CCL9/CCR1 axis and accelerate tendon-bone healing, highlighting their potential for clinical application (Fig. 10).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that low-intensity treadmill exercise enhances macrophage lipid metabolic reprogramming via ACSL4, thereby improving macrophage phagocytosis and efferocytosis. This metabolic shift enhances apoptotic cell clearance at the tendon-bone interface, mitigating inflammation caused by apoptotic cell accumulation and optimizing macrophage metabolic states, ultimately fostering tissue repair. Furthermore, our findings indicate that following efferocytosis, macrophages secrete the chemokine CCL9, which facilitates BMSC migration and homing by activating CCR1 on BMSCs, thereby accelerating tendon-bone healing. Together, the results support a stepwise model in which low-intensity loading first reprograms macrophage lipid metabolism, then enhances efferocytosis, and finally promotes BMSC recruitment through CCL9/CCR1 signaling, leading to improved tendon-bone healing. These results reveal the molecular mechanisms through which low-intensity exercise rehabilitation programs promote tendon-bone healing and provide a strategy for clinical translation via the construction of engineered exosomes that overexpress CCL9.

Previous studies suggest that appropriate mechanical loading is essential for tendon graft development and physiological function (30). Mechanical stimulation maintains tendon cell morphology, regulates extracellular matrix protein expression, and supports tendon graft growth and healing (31–33). Consequently, postoperative rehabilitation programs are designed to align with mechanobiological principles to optimize recovery (34, 35). Camp et al. (36) further demonstrated that prolonged mechanical unloading in a murine ACLR model significantly reduces the maximum tensile strength of tendon grafts (36). In this study, we observed that low-intensity treadmill exercise in mice after surgery enhanced the biomechanical performance of the tendon grafts, promoted growth at the tendon-bone junction, and increased bone density at the bone tunnel, along with a marked increase in M2-type macrophages at the tendon-bone interface, all of which indicate that low-intensity treadmill exercise is beneficial for the tendon-bone healing process after ACLR in mice.

Recent studies indicate that macrophages detect mechanical signals from their environment via the Piezo1 cation channel receptor, triggering intracellular metabolic changes essential for converting physical stimuli into electrochemical signals (37). Research has shown that following radiation injury, bone marrow macrophages (BM-Mφ) respond to alterations in the vascular niche, suggesting Piezo1 as a potential therapeutic target for hematopoietic recovery. Mechanical stimulation, such as bone marrow ablation, has been demonstrated to up-regulate Piezo1 expression, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and establishing a positive feedback loop that enhances mechanical signal transmission (38). Clinical observations further link Piezo1 allele variations to increased plasma iron levels in African populations (38), underscoring the role of macrophages in mechanotransduction and iron metabolism (39). These findings collectively highlight the capacity of mechanical stress to modulate macrophage metabolic activity. In this study, experimental evidence demonstrated that mechanical stretching stimuli induce lipid metabolic reprogramming in macrophages, characterized by enhanced fatty acid oxidation and up-regulated ACSL4 expression, alongside a marked increase in M2 macrophage polarization. Combined RNA-seq and lipidomics/mass spectrometry analyses revealed a strong correlation between ACSL4 and lipid metabolic reprogramming. In the low-intensity treadmill training mouse model, we observed elevated ACSL4 expression at the tendon-bone interface. Further in vitro experiments confirmed that ACSL4 knockdown impaired fatty acid oxidation, reduced M2 macrophage polarization, and validated ACSL4 as a critical regulator of mechanical stress–induced macrophage metabolic reprogramming.

Tendon-bone healing is a complex biological process, with an appropriately regulated early inflammatory response playing a crucial role in recruiting macrophages and other cell populations essential for repair (40). However, excessive or prolonged inflammation impairs healing by promoting collagen degradation, inhibiting fibrocartilage regeneration, and increasing osteoclast activity (41–43). In our study, we observed a substantial accumulation of apoptotic cells during the early stages of tendon-bone healing in the mouse ACLR model. According to Gerlach et al. (44), excessive apoptotic cell accumulation leads to the excessive secretion of inflammatory molecules, potentially triggering a cytokine storm and secondary tissue damage (44). Our findings indicate that low-intensity treadmill exercise enhances macrophage efferocytosis at the tendon-bone interface, thereby promoting M2 macrophage polarization, reducing M1 macrophage activity, and suppressing inflammatory mediator secretion. Consequently, this process accelerates tendon-bone healing. In summary, the effective regulation of the inflammatory response and timely apoptotic cell clearance is essential for tendon-bone healing. The core regulatory mechanism underlying this process is the promotion of M2 macrophage polarization and function.

Macrophages play a central role in phagocytosis and efferocytosis, recognizing and clearing phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cell surfaces through surface molecules such as BAI1, TIM4, and GAS6. M2 macrophages are particularly critical in this process (45). Studies have shown that promoting M2 polarization and enhancing efferocytosis can protect the liver from ischemia/reperfusion injury (46), while macrophage-specific Akt2 deficiency increases M2 polarization and efferocytosis capacity, thereby inhibiting atherosclerosis progression (47). These findings underscore the regenerative role of M2 macrophages and their contrast with M1 macrophages, which are closely associated with inflammation. During the early stages of ACLR, the tendon-bone interface is dominated by CX3CR1+CCR2+ macrophages expressing proinflammatory markers and CD9+IL1+ macrophages that activate interferon responses (28). This inflammatory environment can impair collagen synthesis, fibroblast proliferation, and bone remodeling, ultimately hindering tendon-bone healing. To further investigate this, we developed a transgenic mouse model to enhance M1 and M2 macrophage polarization. While excessive M1 polarization initially promoted fibroblast and inflammatory cell activity, it ultimately led to poor bone integration and slower healing. Conversely, enhanced M2 polarization, facilitated by efficient apoptotic cell clearance, markedly improved tendon-bone healing.

In addition, macrophages in healing tissues exhibit a proliferation increase following efferocytosis, a process termed efferocytosis-induced macrophage proliferation (48). Notably, proinflammatory macrophages lack this proliferative capacity, suggesting a metabolic transition following efferocytosis. In our study, monocyte-derived macrophages recruited to the tendon-bone interface exhibited enhanced phagocytosis and efferocytosis while secreting the chemokine CCL9. Yan et al. (49) previously demonstrated that in the tumor microenvironment, the CCL9/CCR1 pathway recruits bone marrow progenitor cells to intestinal tumors, facilitating invasion (49). However, our findings reveal that in the early stages of tendon-bone healing, the CCL9/CCR1 axis plays a crucial role by promoting BMSC migration and homing to the tendon-bone interface, thereby accelerating healing. The successful application of engineered CCL9-overexpressing BMSC-derived exosomes in tendon-bone injury models further supports this mechanism.

While this study provides mechanistic insight into how low-intensity mechanical stimulation promotes tendon-bone healing, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was performed in a murine ACLR model, which does not fully recapitulate the complexity of human postoperative healing. Second, although biomechanical testing showed relative improvement in graft maximal load and stiffness under selected conditions, we did not include an uninjured native ACL group as a biomechanical benchmark. Therefore, the present data define improvement within the ACLR model but do not establish the extent to which graft mechanics approach native ACL function. Third, our mechanistic analysis focused primarily on the ACSL4-associated lipid remodeling pathway and the CCL9-CCR1 signaling axis and, thus, does not exclude the contribution of additional molecular and cellular pathways involved in tendon-bone repair. Fourth, because the study included multiple experimental modules designed to connect rehabilitation, macrophage biology, exosome-based intervention, and translational validation, some comparisons were necessarily exploratory in nature. Future studies using larger cohorts, longer follow-up, direct comparison with native ligament properties, and validation in larger preclinical models will be important to define the functional magnitude, mechanistic breadth, and translational relevance of these findings more precisely.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Extraction of engineered CCL9-BMSC exosomes

BMSCs were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (50, 51). The cell pellet was resuspended in complete alpha-minimal essential medium (α-MEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), and incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Lentiviral plasmids overexpressing CCL9 were constructed using the technology provided by Qingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). For transfection, P3-generation BMSCs were cultured up to 50% confluency. The lentiviral plasmid (15 μg) and lentiviral packaging plasmid mix (15 μg) were dissolved in Opti-MEM serum-free medium and gently mixed, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min, a second mixing step, and an additional incubation at room temperature for 20 min. The resulting mixture was added to the cell culture dish and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 10 hours. After 12 hours, the medium was replaced with 10 ml of fresh culture medium. After 48 to 72 hours, the culture supernatant was filtered through a 0.45-μm filter to remove cell debris and dead cells. Following the generation of the LV-CCL9 stable cell line, BMSCs were cultured until they reached 80 to 90% confluency. The cells were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with the Serum Replacement Solution (PeproTech, USA) for 24 hours in the incubator. The BMSC culture supernatant was then collected. The culture medium greater than 150 ml was transferred into a 50-ml centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 4°C, 3000g for 30 min to remove the cells. The supernatant was transferred into a fresh 50-ml centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 4°C, 16,000g for 20 min to remove the cell debris. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22-μm filter into a fresh 50-ml centrifuge tube. The exosome-enriched supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 110,000g for 70 min to collect the exosomes. The supernatant was carefully discarded, and 5 ml of PBS was added to resuspend the exosomes via gentle pipetting. The sample was then subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 110,000g for 70 min. After centrifugation, a pale-yellow pellet was visible at the bottom of the tube. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS to achieve the desired volume. A third ultracentrifugation was performed at 110,000g for 70 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The exosomes were then transferred into a 15-ml EP tube and stored at −80°C for further use. The exosome size and concentration were determined via NTA, and exosome morphology was observed by transmission electron microscopy. BMSC exosomal proteins were extracted, and WB analysis was performed to analyze the expression of exosome markers and CCL9, confirming the high expression of CCL9 in the engineered exosomes. For the control group, exosomes were extracted from P3-generation BMSCs without CCL9 overexpression. The exosomes were labeled with DiD dye. The dye was dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, USA) and mixed with the exosome solution to a final concentration of 2 μM. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Labeled exosomes were purified using polyethylene glycol precipitation to remove unbound dye and then resuspended in PBS for subsequent in vivo imaging experiments.

Formulation of a protein slow-release hydrogel containing exosomes

Fibrinogen (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. F8630) was dissolved in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 50 mg/ml. Thrombin (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. T4648) was reconstituted in 40 mM CaCl2 to 10 U/ml. Exosomes [200 μg of total exosomal protein, as determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit; Beyotime] isolated from parental BMSCs or CCL9-overexpressing BMSCs were gently resuspended in 50 μl of the fibrinogen solution. Hydrogel formation was initiated by rapidly mixing this 50 μl of exosome-containing fibrinogen solution with 50 μl of thrombin solution [final concentrations: fibrinogen (25 mg/ml), thrombin (5 U/ml), and exosomes 100 μg of protein per 100 μl of hydrogel]. Polymerization was allowed to proceed at 37°C for 30 min. In vitro release studies were performed by placing each hydrogel (100 μl) in 500 μl of PBS supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) BSA and incubating at 37°C on an orbital shaker (60 rpm). At predetermined time points (days 0 to 14), the supernatant was collected and replaced with fresh buffer. Released CCL9 was quantified using a mouse CCL9 ELISA kit (R&D Systems), and cumulative exosomal protein release was determined using the BCA assay kit (Beyotime).

Isolation of BMDMs

BMDMs were isolated from 2-month-old C57BL/6 mice and cultured following the experimental guidelines described by Murray et al. (52). The mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation under sterile conditions, and their femurs and tibias were harvested. The surrounding tissues were carefully removed, and the epiphyses were excised. The bones were flushed with 5 ml of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS to collect the bone marrow, which was then transferred into a sterile 50-ml conical tube. The bone marrow suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer (BD Falcon, USA) to remove larger debris and bone fragments. The collected cell suspension was centrifuged at 300g for 5 min to pellet the cells. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The suspension was cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 humidified incubator for 24 hours, after which the nonadherent cells were collected. Subsequently, recombinant murine macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; 50 ng/ml) was added to the medium to promote the differentiation of bone marrow cells into macrophages. M-CSF–derived BMDMs were collected after 7 days of culture in M-CSF–containing medium. Afterward mature BMDMs were cultured on a flexible silicone membrane (Bio-Flex plate; Flexcell International Corp., Hillsborough, NC, USA) and subjected to mechanical stress (5, 10, or 20%) using the Flexcell tension system for 24 hours. Before stimulation, cells were allowed to attach and spread on the membrane surface. Continuous live-cell imaging was not performed during cyclic loading; therefore, cell shape and orientation during each loading cycle were not assessed dynamically. Immediately after stimulation, adherent cells were directly processed for RNA extraction, protein analysis, immunofluorescence staining, and phagocytosis assays. Lipopolysaccharide (10 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) and IL-4 (20 ng/ml; PeproTech) were used to induce M1 and M2 macrophage polarization, respectively, and to maintain their respective phenotypes. Following the mechanical stimulation, the culture medium was removed, and the total RNA and protein were extracted for qRT-PCR or WB. Other mechanically stimulated cells were used for immunofluorescence and flow cytometry experiments for the calculation of the phagocytic efficiency.

Thymocyte isolation and macrophage efferocytosis assay

The macrophage efferocytosis assay was performed as described by Heo et al. (53). BMDMs were cultured on Bio-Flex plates and subjected to mechanical stimulation using the Flexcell tension system at varying intensities for 24 hours. Thymocytes were isolated from 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. The mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation, and the thymus was carefully dissected under sterile conditions. The thymus was placed in a sterile culture dish containing cold PBS, and the thymus capsule was gently removed. The tissue was then cut into small pieces with sterile scissors. The minced tissues were mechanically dissociated through a 70-μm cell strainer (BD Falcon), and the resulting single-cell suspension was transferred into a sterile 50-ml conical tube. The suspension was centrifuged at 300g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator for 1 hour to remove the nonadherent cells. For carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) labeling, thymocytes were incubated with 5 μM of CFSE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37°C for 10 min in 1 ml of PBS. The reaction was terminated by adding an equal volume of RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS. The cells were then washed twice with PBS and resuspended in a fresh medium. To induce apoptosis, CFSE-labeled thymocytes were cultured in the presence of 1 μM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 hours. CFSE-labeled apoptotic cells were then added to BMDMs at a ratio of 10:1 for 2 hours. Unphagocytosed apoptotic cells were thoroughly washed three times with PBS. In the control group, an equivalent volume of the culture medium without apoptotic cells was added. The phagocytosis rate of macrophages was quantified by immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry. Total RNA was extracted from the cells exposed to 5% tensile stimulation both before and after efferocytosis and used for RNA-seq analysis. The phagocytosis rate of macrophages was calculated according to a method reported previously by Zihao et al. (54).

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell assays. The experiments were performed with 24-well Transwell chambers (Corning, USA) with an 8-μm pore size in the upper chamber. To evaluate the migratory capacity of BMSCs after macrophage phagocytosis or coculture with engineered exosomes overexpressing CCL9, P3-passage BMSCs were collected and resuspended in a serum-free medium, with the cell concentration adjusted to 2 × 105 cells/ml. A 200-μl aliquot of the cell suspension was then added to the upper chamber. Following low-intensity mechanical stress stimulation of BMDMs, the cells were placed in the lower chamber at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/ml, without or with coculture of apoptotic cells containing MRX-2843. In exosome-related experiments, exosomes (50 μg/ml) were added to the lower chamber. All chambers were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. After incubation, the cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently wiped away with a cotton swab, and the migrated BMSCs in the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Postfixation, the cells were stained with crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) and counted under a microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 10× magnification. The number of cells was quantified from three random fields per chamber. The experiment was repeated at least thrice, and the data were presented as the means ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the t test, with P < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Cell scratch assay

Macrophage phagocytosis–induced BMSC migration was assessed using the cell scratch assay. BMSCs were seeded in a six-well plate at the density of 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured in the complete medium until reaching the confluence. Once confluent, a sterile 200-μl pipette tip was used to create a uniform scratch across the cell monolayer. After scratching, the cells were washed with PBS to remove debris. The supernatant from the coculture of macrophages and apoptotic cells was mixed in a 1:1 ratio with serum-free medium and added to the wells. In some conditions, MRX-2843 (20 ng/ml) was added for further incubation. Cell migration was monitored and captured at 0 and 24 hours postscratch using an inverted microscope (10× magnification). Wound closure was quantified by measuring the width of the scratch at each time point, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated by comparing the scratch width at each time point to the initial width (t = 0 hours). The results were presented as the mean percentage of wound closure from three independent experiments.

ScRNA-seq data processing

scRNA-seq data from the tendon-bone healing interface following ACLR in C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the GEO database (GEO Data no. GSE171480) and analyzed using the Seurat R package (v. 4.3.2). Quality control was performed to filter out low-quality cells based on the following criteria: nFeature_RNA >400, nFeature_RNA <4000, and percent.mt <5%. (28). The gene expression matrix was normalized using the “NormalizeData” function, and 1500 variable features were identified using the “vst” method. Principal components analysis was applied to reduce dimensionality, and the batch effects were removed using the “harmony” package. The JackStrawPlot and ElbowPlot functions were applied to determine the top 30 significant principal components for clustering. Clustering and t-SNE projection were conducted, with macrophages identified using Adgre1 and Cd68 markers, which encode F4/80 and CD68, respectively. The CellChat R package was used to analyze and map intercellular regulatory relationships, and visualization was performed using the ggplot R package.

RNA-seq data processing and analysis

One experimental group comprised BMDMs subjected to 5% cyclic mechanical stress for 24 hours, with unstressed BMDMs serving as controls. A second experimental group involved BMDMs exposed to identical mechanical stress conditions followed by a 2-hour phagocytosis assay with apoptotic cells, which were then compared against stress-treated BMDMs without any subsequent phagocytosis. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was verified before library preparation, with all samples yielding ≥1 μg of high-quality RNA (RIN >8.0) for subsequent processing. Strand-specific mRNA sequencing libraries were constructed using the DynaPro Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (DynaPro Biotechnology), generating 150–base pair paired-end reads. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0) with significance thresholds set at |log2FC| >1 and adjusted P < 0.05. Pathway enrichment analyses were conducted through Oebiotech’s cloud platform (https://cloud.oebiotech.cn/) using both the KEGG and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis algorithms.

Liquid chromatography–MS/MS analysis and data processing

BMDMs were either treated with 5% cyclic tensile strain or left untreated before collection for analysis. The samples were separated on a 2.1 mm–by–100 mm ACQUITY UPLC BEH 1.7-μm column (Waters, Ireland) by hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), followed by MS acquisition. The instrument was set to a range of 25 to 1000 Da, with a scan accumulation time of 0.05 s per spectrum. Raw MS data were converted to MzXML files using ProteoWizard MSConvert and subsequently imported into the XCMS software for processing. Isotope and adduct annotations were performed using the Collection of Algorithms for Metabolite Profile Annotation (CAMERA). Complex metabolite identification was performed by comparing mass/charge ratio values (with an accuracy of <10 parts per million) and tandem MS (MS/MS) spectra against authentic standards in the internal database. Sample identification and data analysis were conducted by Omicshare (Guangzhou, China).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

BMDMs generated as described above were seeded into a six-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. The control group was not subjected to any mechanical stress, whereas the experimental group was exposed to 5% tensile mechanical stress for 24 hours. Afterward, apoptotic cells were added at a 10:1 ratio for 2 hours, or were not added, followed by the removal of unphagocytosed apoptotic cells. The cells were further cultured for 24 hours, and the culture supernatant was collected. The CCL9 levels in the macrophage culture supernatant and exosomes extracted from BMSCs were determined using the CCL9 ELISA kit (Signalway Antibody, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the CCL9 protein levels were calculated with reference to a standard curve.

siRNA-mediated knockdown of ACSL4 in mouse BMDMs

To silence Acsl4 expression in mouse BMDMs, siRNA targeting Acsl4 was used. BMDMs were isolated and cultured as previously described. Once the cells reached 60 to 70% confluence, they were transfected with siRNA targeting Acsl4 (siACSL4) or a nontargeting control siRNA (siControl) using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 pmol of siRNA was diluted in 100 μl of Opti-MEM (Gibco), and 3 μl of Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 100 μl Opti-MEM. The two solutions were then incubated for 5 min at room temperature, after which the siRNA-lipid complex was added to the cells and the mixture was incubated for 24 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After transfection, the medium was replaced with a fresh culture medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 48 hours. The knockdown efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and WB. For qRT-PCR, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Vazyme, China). Acsl4 expression was quantified with specific primers (table S2) and normalized to Gapdh.

Quantitative RT-PCR

After 24 hours of mechanical stress stimulation, total RNA was extracted from BMDMs with the Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme). cDNA synthesis was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative gene expression was quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The primer sequences used for the qRT-PCR analysis are listed in table S2.

WB analysis

Macrophages were subjected to different intensities of mechanical stress (5, 10, and 20%) using the Flexcell tension system for 24 hours. In the Acsl4 knockdown experiment, macrophages were first exposed to 5% mechanical stress for 24 hours, followed by Acsl4 silencing with specific siRNA. After 24 hours of mechanical stress exposure or ACSL4 knockdown, the cells were lysed with the lysis buffer [62.5 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 50 mM dithiothreitol, and 0.01% bromophenol blue] at 96°C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis for 100 min and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA) using the wet transfer method. The membranes were first treated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C on a shaker, followed by incubation with specific secondary antibodies (table S3). The protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Proteintech, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animal study design and surgical procedure

All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (approval no. SMUL2021087). Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Southern Medical University. LysM-Cre, Tsc1flox/flox, and Rheb1flox/flox mice were bred and identified following the protocols described previously by our research group (55). Mice with myeloid-specific deletion of Tsc1 were designated as TSC1KO, while Rheb1-knockout mice were referred to as Rheb1KO. Littermates carrying Tsc1flox/flox or Rheb1flox/flox without Cre recombinase were used as wild-type controls. All experimental mice were housed in a specific pathogen–free facility under controlled conditions of a temperature of 23°C and relative humidity of 40% to ensure their health and well-being. Surgical procedures were performed as described previously (36, 56). Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), and the flexor digitorum longus tendon was harvested and maintained in sterile saline until transplantation. Following exposure of the knee joint cavity, the native ACL was carefully transected. The tibial and femoral bone tunnels were drilled using a 0.6-mm diameter drill bit. Subsequently, 10 μl of the engineered exosomes, either expressing or not expressing CCL9 from cell sources, were injected into the bone tunnels. Each mouse in the in vivo imaging experiment was injected with approximately 200 μg of labeled exosomes encapsulated in a sustained-release hydrogel. The control group was injected with hydrogel containing only DiD dye. The tendon graft was then pulled into the bone tunnels using a guidewire and securely fixed with sutures. Both the ends of the tendon were tightly secured, and the joint capsule and skin were meticulously sutured in layers to close the incision site. For postoperative care, 40,000 units of benzylpenicillin were administered intraperitoneally daily for 3 days to prevent infection. The rectal temperature was monitored daily, and the mice were regularly assessed for signs of postoperative distress. The overall health of the mice was closely monitored. All mice underwent a 5-day treadmill adaptation period before their surgery (Table 1). During the first 3 days of surgery, their activity was restricted, and they were provided with ample food and water. Subsequently, the mice in the nontreadmill intervention group were kept with restricted activity within their cages, while those in the treadmill intervention group were placed on the treadmill daily for forced running, set for 35 min at a speed of 5 to 15 m/min, which included a 5-min warm-up period (Table 2). Each group consisted of five mice. At 1, 2, 4, and 8 weeks of surgery, the mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 to 200 mg/kg) and euthanized via cervical dislocation. The knee joint samples were collected for a series of analyses, including immunofluorescence to assess the protein expression, histological staining to evaluate tissue morphology, micro-CT for structural analysis, and biomechanical testing to assess the graft strength. In vivo imaging was performed using a small animal fluorescence imaging system (IVIS Lumina Series III, Caliper Life Sciences, USA) at designated time points (1 to 21 days) postinjection. Real-time fluorescence imaging was conducted using the appropriate fluorescence channel (Ex/Em 645/670 nm). To ensure accuracy, imaging was performed under identical environmental conditions. Fluorescence signal intensity was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer, USA). The details of the mouse model construction are provided in fig. S1.

Biomechanical testing

To compare the mechanical strength of grafts under different treatment conditions, the mice from the exercise-rehabilitation group, exosome-treatment group, and the TSC1KO and Rheb1KO groups were selected. Five mice from each group at the 4-week and 8-week time points were included in the analysis. The distal tibia and proximal femur were dissected, and the surrounding tissues and ligaments of the knee joint were carefully removed, leaving only the reconstructed ACL. The specimens were placed in a testing machine and stabilized with cushioning cotton. The thread-knot fixation at both ends of the reconstructed ligament was removed to prevent any interference during biomechanical testing. The maximum load tension at which the graft was either pulled out of the bone tunnel or fractured was measured using a high-precision mechanical testing system (Electroforce 3200, TABOSE, USA). Biomechanical failure mode was predefined before analysis. Only specimens in which failure occurred through graft pullout or loosening at the graft-bone construct were included in the final biomechanical comparison, whereas intraarticular midsubstance rupture was excluded because it does not directly reflect tendon-bone interface fixation strength in this model. The testing system was calibrated to zero, with a loading speed of 0.5 mm/s (equivalent to 30 mm/min), which was selected to maintain quasi-static testing conditions and is within the range commonly used in biomechanical testing of ligaments and reconstructed grafts (57–59). A load-displacement curve was generated, and the stiffness of the graft was determined by calculating the slope of the linear region of the load-displacement curve. (58).

Micro-CT analysis

To assess bone formation in the bone tunnels of mice from the exercise-rehabilitation, exosome-treatment, TSC1KO, and Rheb1KO groups, samples were collected at 4 and 8 weeks after ACLR surgery, with five mice selected from each group. The microstructures of the femoral and tibial tunnels were analyzed, and three-dimensional (3D) image reconstruction and quantitative analysis were performed using image analysis software. The scanning parameters were set as follows: layer thickness of 15 μm, voltage of 55 kV, current of 145 μA, and energy of 8 W. A prescan of the entire specimen was conducted, followed by a detailed scan of the selected ROI. The scanned images were analyzed to capture the femoral tunnel from the entrance to the articular cavity. The BV/TV and bone mineral density were calculated by using image analysis software.

Finite element model analysis

The finite element model was constructed using ANSYS software (version 19.2) based on a 3D geometry of the post-ACLR mouse knee joint, derived from micro-CT scans of C57BL/6 mouse specimens (resolution: 15 μm). The model incorporated the tibia, femur, reconstructed ACL graft, and bone tunnels, with material properties assigned as follows: bone as linear elastic (Young’s modulus E = 10 GPa, Poisson’s ratio ν = 0.3), tendon graft as hyperelastic (Mooney-Rivlin model with C10 = 0.5 MPa, C01 = 0.1 MPa), and interface as frictional contact (coefficient 0.15) (60). Boundary conditions simulated treadmill exercise: fixed distal tibia, applied displacement at the proximal femur (0 to 2 mm, corresponding to knee flexion), and relative loads (1 to 4 N) to mimic dynamic loading from running speeds (0 to 15 m/min). Mesh consisted of tetrahedral elements (average size 0.1 mm), with convergence achieved at <5% stress variation. Stress distributions were computed at the tendon-bone entry point, revealing peak stresses of 0 to 26.826 MPa under higher speeds, as shown in fig. S2.

Histological evaluation and scoring

To evaluate tendon-bone interface healing after the different interventions, knee joints were harvested at 4 and 8 weeks after ACLR, fixed in formaldehyde, decalcified, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3 μm along the sagittal plane. Histological evaluation was performed using H&E, Masson’s trichrome, and Safranin O–Fast green staining. Tendon-bone healing was assessed using the semiquantitative scoring system described by Zhao et al. (61). Five independent evaluators scored each specimen, and the final histological score for each specimen was calculated as the mean of the scores assigned by the five evaluators.

The scoring system included five parameters, each graded from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating better healing: cellularity, interface separation, fibrocartilage formation, interface transition, and tidemark formation. Cellularity was assessed semiquantitatively within a standardized tendon-bone interface region on H&E-stained sections based on the predominant cell morphology and relative cell density rather than by absolute cell counting. Lower scores indicated sparse fibrovascular tissue, whereas higher scores indicated fibrocartilage populated by rounded mature cartilage-like cells. Interface separation was scored according to the extent of visible gap formation between the tendon graft and surrounding bone along the interface, with higher scores indicating less separation and a more continuous tendon-bone attachment. The criterion “fibrocartilage with ≥50% mature cartilage cells” referred to a fibrocartilage region in which at least half of the cells displayed a rounded, lacuna-containing chondrocyte-like morphology on H&E staining; Safranin O–Fast green staining was used as supportive evidence for cartilage matrix formation in the corresponding interface region. Fibrocartilage formation was scored according to the extent of fibrocartilage surrounding the tendon graft, interface transition according to the continuity of tissue transition from bone to tendon, and tidemark formation according to the visibility of the mineralization front (fig. S19).

Immunofluorescence staining

Mouse knee joint samples were collected at 1, 2, 4, and 8 weeks after surgery and sectioned into 4-μm paraffin slices. These sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating the sections in TE9.0 buffer and heating them in a microwave at 96°C for 2 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS for 1 hour to reduce nonspecific binding. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibodies (diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA). The next day, the sections were washed thrice with PBS and incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate– and phycoerythrin-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, away from light (table S3). After washing three times with PBS, the sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:1000 dilution) for 5 min to label the cell nuclei. The sections were mounted with a mounting medium and observed under an Olympus fluorescence microscope. The proportion of positive cells at the tendon-bone interface was quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

For in vivo endpoint analyses, each mouse was treated as one independent biological replicate. A sample size of five mice per group per time point was used for the predefined histological, micro-CT, immunofluorescence, and biomechanical assessments in this ACLR model. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Nonnormally distributed data (P < 0.05) were analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis H test for multiple-group comparisons, followed by Dunn’s test for pairwise comparisons. For normally distributed data, homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Two-group comparisons were performed using the two-sample Student’s t test, while multiple-group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD test for all pairwise comparisons. Analyses were conducted in SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Normally distributed data are presented as means ± SD and nonnormally distributed data as median (interquartile range). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. (ns: nonsignificant, *P < 0. 05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).
