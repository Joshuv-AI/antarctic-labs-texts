The protocol for a randomized, sham-controlled trial of transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation for chronic pain and opioid withdrawal symptoms during a 4-day opioid taper for adults in the United States
Authors: Chris J Pierson, Navid Khodaparast, Melanie A McWade, Yong-Fang Kuo, David C Houghton, Sophia L Rodriguez, Victoria L Korschgen, Kathryn A Cunningham, Denise M Wilkes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReducing opioid use is challenging due to limited evidence-based weaning methods and a lack of interventions to mitigate withdrawal symptoms. An emerging intervention using transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation (tAN) is being developed to reduce opioid withdrawal symptoms, but its mechanisms of action are not yet well understood. This is a clinical trial performed to investigate the mechanisms of tAN in managing pain and opioid withdrawal during opioid taper in adults with chronic pain.MethodsThis is a single-site, randomized, double-blind, and sham-controlled superiority framework trial during an inpatient opioid taper for participants on long-term opioid therapy for chronic pain. Participants are recruited for an inpatient stay at a large, academic medical center in the United States. Included participants are adults between 18 and 75 years of age who have the presence of pain more than half of the days in the past 6 months, are prescribed opioid medication, have a willingness to taper the opioid dose by at least 10%, and have a urine drug screen positive for the prescribed opioid but negative for illicit drugs and nonprescribed opioids. Participants are excluded with a condition affecting their safety of participation (e.g., epileptic seizures, current suicidal ideation, current abuse of illicit drugs or alcohol, pregnancy), a condition that precludes fMRI assessment (implanted medical device, claustrophobia), or a status affecting pain medication intake (e.g., surgery in the past month, opioid prescription dose > 200 morphine milligram equivalents per day, history of neurological diseases or traumatic brain injury, active treatment for cancer).Participants are randomized to receive either active tAN (n = 20) or sham tAN (n = 20). Both groups undergo a mild-to-moderate opioid taper on day 1 and are maintained at the reduced level for 4 days under inpatient medical supervision. The primary outcome measure, brain functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), is used to measure BOLD signals and resting functional connectivity (z-value) of pain networks. Secondary outcome measures are self- and clinician-observed opioid withdrawal scales, behavioral assessment questionnaires, and quantitative sensory testing (QST) data.The first subject enrollment was completed from July 25 to 28, 2023. The total enrollment count was set to 40 with two arms of equal ratios. Randomization stratification by gender at birth was performed. The study physician, intervention-providing staff member, and outcome-assessing study coordinator each perform recruitment, and each is blinded to treatment group assignment.Safety and harm measures of opioid withdrawal will be assessed with the Clinical and Subject-reported Opiate Withdrawal Scores. Vital signs will be assessed three times per day, and adverse events will be recorded and reported as necessary.DiscussionUnderstanding the mechanisms of action of tAN will lead to the development of more effective future non-pharmacologic treatments in mitigating withdrawal while gradually tapering participants off prescription opioid management.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov, NCT05555485. Registered on 15 September 2022.Keywords: Chronic pain, Opioid tapering, Opioid withdrawal, Vagus nerve, Trigeminal nerve, Neuromodulation, Transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Worldwideconsumption of prescription opioids has increased globally since the 1990s, with annual consumption led by the Americas (144 mg/person), Oceania (132 mg/person), and Europe (98 mg/person) [1]. Use of prescription opioids during the previous 12 months was reported by 1.9% of US adults in 2019–2020 [2]. This National Health Interview Survey report indicates an increased prevalence of prescription opioid use related to pain, with 29.3% of those patients with chronic pain and 41.2% of those with high-impact chronic pain [2]. The 2020 National Survey on Drug Use and Health estimated that 9.5 million people aged 12 and older reported non-medical prescription opioid use in the past year, with 9.3 million identified as misusing prescription pain relievers [3]. As such, providers face a challenging clinical dilemma in adequately controlling pain in patients [4] while limiting opioid use [5–8].

Current clinical guidelines highlight the unfavorable risk/benefit ratio associated with long-term opioids for chronic pain, encouraging providers to consider reducing doses or eliminating opioids for those who no longer experience clinically significant benefits [9]. Unfortunately, long-term opioid use causes physiological dependence, which results in a withdrawal syndrome upon tapering and discontinuation. Opioid withdrawal includes many noxious symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, arthralgias, and possible increases in pain [10, 11]. Many patients fear experiencing withdrawal symptoms, which limits the successful initiation and completion of an opioid taper [12, 13]. To mitigate the intensity of withdrawal symptoms, slow tapering is recommended [14]. Our understanding of opioid withdrawal symptoms during tapering in long-term opioid use is still limited [14]. Observational studies have warned that tapering without assistance can lead to patient destabilization, uncontrolled pain, increased incidence of overdose, increased incidence of mental health crises, and termination of care [15–18]. Additional factors in the clinical management of pain challenge providers, including shortages of opioid pain medications and the refusal of over 40% of primary pain clinics to accept new patients on chronic opioid therapy [19]. Given this landscape, there is a clear need for both systematic opioid tapering protocols and innovative pain treatments that cannot be abused or diverted.

Neurostimulation, one type of neuromodulation, of cranial nerves has been used for various applications, including cluster headaches and migraines [20–36]. Interest in neurostimulation techniques has recently increased due to their non-invasive application at neck and ear sites [37]. Using a transcutaneous neurostimulation device for pain relief may be an effective alternative to opioid therapy [38, 39] and a useful method for tapering off opioids [40, 41], without any need for invasive surgery, risk for liability for opioid use disorder (OUD), or known pharmaceutical contraindications. Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS), which is stimulation specifically to the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (ABVN), has shown effectiveness for various indications, including migraine [42, 43] chronic pelvic pain [44], chronic gastrointestinal disorders [45], esophageal pain [46], and post-hysterectomy pain [47]. Auricular stimulation of the nearby trigeminal nerve has also shown promise in migraine [32, 33], craniofacial pain [34], and ocular pain [35].

Transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation (tAN) is an emerging neurostimulation modality that stimulates both the ABVN and the auriculotemporal nerve (ATN), a branch of the trigeminal nerve. A clinical study demonstrated that tAN mitigated withdrawal symptoms in opioid-exposed newborns, demonstrating an average length of morphine treatment of 13.3 days [40]; compared to the 2004–2011 US major children’s hospitals average length of morphine treatment of 23 days [40]. Moreover, a recent randomized clinical trial published in 2022 investigated the use of tAN for opioid withdrawal in adults with OUD following abrupt opioid discontinuation [41]. Enrolled participants had a pre-treatment Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS) average (SD) of 14.9 (2.7), indicating this population had on average “moderate” withdrawal severity [48]. Mean COWS reduction in the active tAN group after 30 min of treatment was 6.3 (41.7%) compared to the sham group’s 3.7 (24.1%) [41]. Notably, during the open-label period, the average COWS reduced during days 2–5 by 61.0% in the intent-to-treat population and by 74.7% in not imputed data [41]. This clinical trial led to tAN becoming an FDA-cleared (K230796) treatment for adult opioid withdrawal symptoms [49].

Opioid withdrawal is associated with increased disruptions to mood [50] and sleep [51]. Furthermore, the effect of long-term opioid use appears to worsen these comorbidities [52–55]. Neurostimulation also offers potential benefit in targeting symptoms of mood disorders such as major depression and anxiety, which are highly comorbid with chronic pain [56]. A growing body of evidence supports taVNS for ameliorating depression [57, 58], anxiety [59], and insomnia [60]. Withdrawal severity is linked to increased anxiety and depression in patients treated with prescription opioids for long-term treatment of pain [50], suggesting additional benefits of tAN in this patient demographic.

The goal of our study is to elucidate the mechanisms of tAN in managing pain and opioid withdrawal during an opioid taper in adults with chronic pain based upon the hypothesis that tAN increases parasympathetic activation and the release of endogenous opioids [40]. This hypothesis is based on fMRI studies showing activation of the nucleus tractus solitarius, locus coeruleus, spinal trigeminal nucleus, parabrachial area, periaqueductal gray, amygdala, and nucleus accumbens by ABVN stimulation [37, 61–64]. Brainstem activity has also been shown to increase during ABVN stimulation [65] followed by increases in higher-order structures such as the basal ganglia, thalamus, limbic cortex, cingulate cortex, insula, and prefrontal cortex [37, 61, 66, 67]. Trigeminal afferents, similar to the vagus nerve, stimulate the periaqueductal gray [68] and increase the release of endogenous opioids [69, 70]. We hypothesize that vagal and trigeminal stimulation during tAN therapy will synergistically promote endogenous opioid release in the brainstem and midbrain, exerting modulatory effects on higher-order brain structures within the pain network [37, 61, 65–67].

People with chronic pain exhibit alterations both within and between these pain networks, while opioid-maintained pain patients exhibit physiological dependence and maladaptive neuroplasticity upon long-term drug exposure [71–76]. From molecular to cellular levels, tolerance due to prolonged exogenous opioid use leads to homeostatic dysregulation and decreased pain inhibition through endogenous opioid activity, which occurs in both pain-managed opioid users without OUD [77] and persons with OUD [78, 79]. Targeting endogenous opioid transmission and/or pain network activity during opioid tapering is therefore a promising approach. We hypothesize that participants who receive active tAN stimulation will demonstrate increased fMRI signal activation in vagal afferents within the brainstem as compared to sham. We hypothesize that participants who receive active stimulation will demonstrate comparatively less fMRI signal activation in the cortical and subcortical pain networks compared to sham. As an exploratory goal, we hypothesize that tAN-based alterations in brain activation may correlate not only to measures of pain and withdrawal, but also to changes in affect, depression, and anxiety.

Objectives {7}

This protocol describes the study undertaken for the third aim of the funded HEAL project grant, which focuses on the analysis of tAN as a safe and effective treatment for pain and opioid withdrawal, the elucidation of neurophysiological mechanisms of tAN, and the potential to lead to treatment optimization and precision implementation in real-world applications. The primary objective of this study is to describe differences between active tAN and passive sham tAN (no stimulation) in fMRI signal activation before and after an opioid taper and 3 days of stimulation treatment. The secondary objectives are to describe differences in patient-reported pain severity, patient- and clinician-reported opioid withdrawal severity, patient-reported mood and sleep quality, and QST to pressure pain and thermal pain. We hypothesize the following: as compared to chronic pain participants who receive sham stimulation, and while controlling for baseline Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) signal, those who receive tAN will demonstrate increased BOLD signal activation in vagal afferents within the brainstem; as compared to those who receive sham stimulation, and while controlling for baseline BOLD signal, participants who receive tAN will demonstrate decreased BOLD signal activation in the cortical and subcortical pain networks; tAN-based changes in brain activation will correlate with measures of pain, opioid withdrawal, and negative affect to specifically delineate the overlay and distinguishing factors between the brain signatures of analgesia, reduced withdrawal, and improved depression and anxiety. Two other concurrent studies funded by this project grant at our partner institution, the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC), are determining the neurochemical mechanism of tAN (NCT05490134) and direct brain effects of various tAN parameters in healthy volunteers (NCT05487664), outcomes which complement the study described herein.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Trial design {8}

This is a clinical mechanistic trial employing randomization, double-blinding, and sham-control. Each of the 40 participants is individually randomized into one of two parallel groups, such that each group (sham or active tAN) has complete data (n = 20 each) after accounting for attrition. There will be a 1:1 allocation ratio (stratified by gender) with the two groups with a superiority framework. The study will consist of only one phase for each participant, a single 4-day inpatient stay. The trial design is depicted in the CONSORT diagram (Fig. 1).

Study setting {9}

This study takes place at a single urban academic medical center in the southern United States. Our hospital system has over six hospitals with a total of 800 beds, and over 100 clinics spread across east and southeast Texas, serving over 59,000 annual patient visits at our clinics. Study participation entails a 4-day inpatient stay in the University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB) Clinical Research Center after each participant has provided informed consent with the signed form. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the UTMB has approved the study (22–0187).

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators (6b)

The comparator to active tAN stimulation is passive sham tAN (no stimulation). A new pharmacological treatment must produce beneficial effects that outweigh placebo effects, biases, and fluctuations in symptoms to be approved by the FDA. These rigorous requirements are not yet widely applied to neurostimulation treatments [80]. It is imperative for the control group to adequately represent the same procedural placebo effects, biases, and fluctuations in symptoms [80]. Thus, the inclusion and exclusion criteria are the same for all participants, regardless of treatment allocation. Other chronic pain studies using devices employ the same blinding paradigm, such as spinal cord stimulation [81–83] and implanted axillary nerve stimulation [84].

Sham stimulation consists of passive control. Following randomization, all participants are told that stimulation may or may not be perceived and that the tAN device will deliver the stimulation. Participants in the active tAN group receive therapy at the preset programmed stimulation settings. Participants in the sham group wear a tAN device that appears visually identical to the active tAN device but delivers no stimulation.

Intervention description {11a}

The Sparrow Ascent System (Spark Biomedical, Dallas, TX) received 510(k) clearance (K230796) and is indicated as a transcutaneous nerve field stimulator intended for use in mitigating opioid withdrawal in patients. The broad FDA-cleared indication for the Sparrow Ascent device for patients in opioid withdrawal makes this device an ideal candidate to investigate the mechanism of action for device-based pain relief in chronic pain patients experiencing pain and withdrawal symptoms while tapering off opioid medications during this study.

The system has three components provided to the participants: an earpiece with hydrogel electrodes, the cable, and the patient controller (Fig. 2). The parameters of stimulation (besides intensity controlled by the patient controller) are set using a specialized Sparrow Ascent Clinical Tool App on a Bluetooth-connected tablet. The system is intended to transcutaneously stimulate branches of the vagus and trigeminal nerves in three regions on and around the ear.

Sparrow ascent (A) earpiece, (B) cable, and (C) patient controller. A Sparrow ascent earpiece. B Earpiece placement on each region

The subcutaneous tissue in stimulation region oneis innervated by the ABVN, also known as Arnold’s nerve [85]. Region two ascends the auriculotemporal nerve (ATN), which is the branch of the mandibular division of cranial nerve V (trigeminal nerve). Region threebranches from the posterior auricular nerve (arising from cranial nerve VII) and branches of the lesser occipital nerve terminate behind the auricle [86]. The electrodes in region three are located such that the branches of both the lesser occipital and posterior auricular nerves are subcutaneous to the electrodes.

tAN will be administered for 4 h per day for three consecutive days. Typically, stimulation will be delivered in four blocks of 1 h administration, but participants will be allowed to alter this schedule if needed, as long as they receive four total hours of tAN per day. Stimulation time will be recorded by study personnel. A depiction of the regions can be found in Fig. 2. In the active stimulation group, two individual stimulation frequencies are set: 15 Hz at the Cymba Concha (Region one/Channel one; vagal innervation) and 100 Hz at the Tragus (Region two/Channel two; trigeminal innervation). Region threeis a return site which does not provide stimulation. The pulse duration is set to 250 µs. The stimulation intensities (mA) are set to 1.0 and 1.4 (for Regions 1 and 2, respectively) based on the median values observed in previous clinical studies [41]. If the participant states that the stimulation intensity is discomforting or imperceivable, the researcher gradually decreases/increases intensity until a comfortable stimulation intensity is achieved.

During day one of admission, participants review and sign the consent form and complete a UDS to confirm prescription opioid use and rule out illicit substances or non-prescribed medications. The participant brings all over-the-counter and prescribed medications. The study physician starts by completing a history and physical and discussing the opioid tapering process. All prescribed medications related or unrelated to pain are reviewed and recorded, and administered by the nursing staff, except for over-the-counter vitamins, acetaminophen, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, topical liniments, and diabetic medications and supplies such as insulin and glucose monitoring equipment. The opioid medication is carefully tapered at a level greater than 10% of their immediate acting opioids, under full medical supervision to ensure that participants do not compensate for a reduction in opioids by increasing other medications. This level of monitoring requires an inpatient stay, in which nursing staff administer prescription and allowable medications. The baseline questionnaires are administered on the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system allowing participants to complete them independently. The quantitative sensory testing battery and fMRI assessments, detailed in the Outcomes section, are then conducted. These assessments take place on day one, prior to the onset of opioid withdrawal.

During the second and third days of admission, we expect to detect the onset of minimal opioid withdrawal symptoms. Neurostimulation intervention, whether active or sham, will be conducted for a total of 4 h each day. We assess pain scores, vital signs, and opioid withdrawal symptoms (as evaluated by both clinician and participants) three times per day. Throughout this period, the study physician and nursing staff monitor for all side effects. Additionally, the nursing staff monitor excessive withdrawal symptoms, particularly exceeding heart rates over normal limits (100 beats per minute).

On the fourth day of the hospital stay, the opioid reduction level is maintained. Stimulation begins in the morning and the testing battery, which includes questionnaires, QST, and fMRI, is completed after the stimulation session ends. After the testing battery is concluded, the participant undergoes a debriefing to assess their experience and their perception of receiving sham or active stimulation is obtained.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants will be discontinued from this study if they are unable to complete study procedures, demonstrate one or more criteria for a mental health crisis, or display one or more criteria of severe opioid withdrawal. Completion of study procedures includes staying for the full 4-day study admission, using the device as instructed, completing both fMRI measures, and completing all secondary outcome measures. Mental health crisis criteria include a self-reported onset of suicidal ideation including plan or intent, self-harm behavior such as cutting or refusal to eat, significant verbal aggression, or any physical aggression toward study personnel or research nurses, or the onset of psychotic symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions, or thought disorganization. Severe opioid withdrawal will be identified with a score on the COWS over 36, blood pressure elevated to 180/120 or higher, tachycardia (> 100 beats per minute) that fails to remit within 30 min, fever, or behavioral signs of clinically significant withdrawal (severe agitation, insomnia, vomiting, or sweating).

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Participants undergo treatment in a controlled clinical research setting while staying overnight for three nights. Opioid medications are dispensed by staff, ensuring control of dosing. Neurostimulation protocols are saved and monitored for quality assurance.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Any participant who reports significantly elevated craving for opioids, illicit opioid consumption, or serious psychiatric/medical symptoms will result in research staff notification, physician notification, and referral as needed to outpatient treatment resources or the Emergency Department. Participants are recommended to follow up with their pain medication prescribing provider no later than 2 weeks after study completion. Compensation for injury or illnesses resulting from participation is not available from the UTMB at Galveston.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome measure

The neurophysiological response to tAN is measured with fMRI. The protocol consists of structural and resting state functional scans. From the neuroimaging data, regions involved in the vagal afferent network, pain matrix, and connected regions will be identified. We will perform a correlational analysis of brain areas activated during resting state with changes in objective and subjective pain tolerance, opioid withdrawal, and negative affect. Intrinsic functional connectivity between these regions will be quantified to determine the neurophysiological effects of tAN-based analgesia during prescription opioid tapering. Each network’s functional connectivity (z-value) and BOLD will be assessed separately, without combining outcomes. The imaging protocol will be performed once at baseline (before tapering) and repeated after the taper and treatment.

Key secondary outcome measures

Secondary outcome measures include both patient and clinician-reported outcome measures, with timelines of each depicted in the Table of Sect. 13. The COWS assessed by a trained study coordinator will assess the severity of opioid withdrawal symptoms. The COWS contains 13 physically observable signs that are rated as either present or absent [87]. These are observed during either a 10-min duration or throughout the time taken for the subject to fill out other questionnaires, whichever is shorter. The minimum possible score is 0 and the highest possible score is 13, with higher scores indicating greater withdrawal symptoms [87].

The Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS) is provided by the participant rating of opioid withdrawal. The SOWS is a self-rated questionnaire consisting of 16 items reflecting the common symptoms of opioid withdrawal. Participants rate each symptom on a scale from 0 to 4, with higher numbers indicating greater symptom severity at the time of testing [87]. The minimum possible score is 0 and the highest possible score is 64, with higher scores indicating greater withdrawal symptoms. The validity of SOWS is assessed by administering both scales before and after opioid withdrawal symptom-modifying pharmacological intervention. Agreement between COWS and SOWS measures is above 75% during an opioid taper for opioid withdrawal management [88] and will inform treatment intervention outcomes in the present study.

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is a nine-item, self-administered questionnaire designed to assess the severity of depression [89]. Each item is scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with higher scores indicating more severe depression. The minimum possible score is 0 and the highest possible score is 37, with higher scores indicating greater depression severity. In people with substance use disorders, the PHQ-9 has been shown to have convergent validity with a correlation of 0.88 to the Beck Depression Inventory-II [90].

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-7) is a seven-item self-rated questionnaire developed as a screening tool and severity indicator for generalized anxiety disorder [91]. Each item is assessed with response options “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” or “nearly every day.” These correspond to scores of 0, 1, 2, or 3, respectively, with higher summed scores indicating greater anxiety severity [91]. The minimum possible score is 0 and the highest possible score is 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity. Repeated measurements in this population exhibit high temporal stability with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.85, and high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha 0.91) [92]. In people with substance use disorders, the GAD-7 has been shown to have convergent validity with the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory. The correlation with state anxiety (STAI-S) was 0.72 and trait anxiety (STAI-T) was 0.77 [90].

The Post-traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) is a 20-item self-report questionnaire assessing symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder. Each item is scored on a Likert scale with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity [93]. The sum of each item provides a total symptom severity score with a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 80. PCL-5 test scores demonstrate strong internal consistency (α=0.96), test-retest reliability (r= 0.84), and convergent and discriminant validity [94].

Measures recommended by NIH helping to end addiction long-term (HEAL) initiative

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) was originally created by the NIH to evaluate and monitor over 122 different domains. The Short Form Sleep Disturbance 6a (PROMIS-S) is a six-item self-report questionnaire assessing sleep quality [95]. Each item is scored from 1 to 5 (range 6 to 30), with higher scores indicating better sleep. The PROMIS Physical Function (PROMIS-PF) is a six-item self-report questionnaire that assesses a broad range of general physical function activities. Each item is scored from 1 to 5 (range 6 to 30), with higher scores indicating greater disability.

The Pain, Enjoyment of Life, and General Activity scale (PEG-3) is a three-item scale assessing the average severity of pain in the past week, pain interference with enjoyment of life, and pain interference with general activity [96]. Each question is scored from 0 to 10, (range 0 to 30) with higher scores indicating greater impairment. The PEG-3 was derived from the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) [97], validated in patients with chronic non-cancer pain [98, 99]. This scale is intended to be shorter than the BPI and better suited for implementation in clinical use, while being more informative than single-question pain screening forms.

Psychophysical pain assessment

Additional outcome measures use two measures of QST: pressure pain (kPA) and thermal pain (°C) threshold and tolerance. The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) defines pain threshold as “the minimum intensity of a stimulus that is perceived as painful.” [100]The IASP defines pain tolerance level as “the maximum intensity of a pain-producing stimulus that a subject is willing to accept in a given situation.” [100].

Sample size {14}

Power analyses were conducted with emphasis on the primary objective, changes in BOLD response between active and sham treatment conditions. Few studies have been completed with a clinical pain population using resting-state neuroimaging from which to derive an expected effect size. A prior study assessing electroencephalogram potentials in patients receiving VNS for migraine found a large effect size (f) for the time by treatment interaction (f = 1.22) [42], and a study of auricular VNS for migraine also showed a large effect size (f= 0.73) [43]. For this aim’s proposed sample size of 40 with a medium to large effect size (f = 0.35), the study has a power of 0.87 to detect significant treatment effects (active tAN vs. sham), and the interaction effects between time (baseline vs. post-treatment) and treatment on the outcome of BOLD based on a repeated measures ANOVA with a 0.05 significance level. The power calculation was computed using PASS 2024.

Recruitment {15}

The study team prescreens potential participants through multiple sources, including the UTMB Pain Management Clinics, UTMB Electronic Health Records, TrialFacts online patient recruitment platform, and the UTMB community daily email announcements. Flyers and brochures are placed in UTMB Pain Management waiting rooms and exam rooms. UTMB physicians who often treat chronic pain and prescribe opioids have been briefed on the protocol and inclusion/exclusion criteria and routinely provide referrals for prescreening.

If a participant is identified as meeting preliminary prescreening criteria, study personnel contact the patient by phone to arrange an in-person screening visit. During this visit, participants undergo a structured patient interview, evaluation of current medication regimen, and a urine drug screen (UDS) to confirm study eligibility. Each participant who meets the screening requirements will be consented to the specific opioid reduction and scheduled for the study admission.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The UTMB Office of Biostatistics (OBIOS) generated the randomization scheme using the PROC PLAN procedure from SAS Software Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). The total enrollment count was set to 40 with two arms of equal ratios. Stratification by gender at birth was performed. We will recruit up to 60 participants to account for a 33% dropout rate.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The allocation sequence based on the randomization scheme is stored in REDCap software, which is accessible only to the OBIOS biostatistician and a designated unblinded research staff member who programs the neurostimulation device for each participant. The remaining key personnel and staff do not have access to the allocation sequence nor know device programming, and this information is forbidden from discussion.

Implementation {16c}

Study staff will enroll participants and admit them to the Clinical Research Center. Upon a new participant enrollment, the designated unblinded staff member assigns the participant to a condition based on the allocation sequence.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

After enrollment, each participant is blinded and randomized to receive either active stimulation or sham stimulation. The device is programmed to provide either sham or active stimulation from the Sparrow Ascent Clinical Tool App on a Bluetooth-connected tablet. One unblinded study staff member is responsible for randomization, programming the app on the tablet, and recording. The unblinded staff member hands the programmed device (but not the tablet containing information regarding condition assignment) to the blinded study coordinator administering the intervention.

The unblinded staff member has no further roles in the study, such as data collection or administering the intervention. To maintain the blind, the participant communicates all questions and concerns only to the blinded coordinator. The device, earpiece, and patient controller are indistinguishable across groups.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Unblinding would occur in the event of a severe or life-threatening adverse event. Severe events are events that interrupt a participant’s usual activities of daily living and may require systemic drug therapy or other treatment. Life-threatening events are any adverse events that, in the investigator’s view, pose an immediate risk of death or harm to the participant.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

The fMRI scans will be performed at baseline Day 1 (prior to the appearance of opioid taper withdrawal symptoms) and Day 4 (after three sessions of tAN/sham). The protocol consists of a structural scan and resting state functional scan. All MRI imaging is performed using a Siemens 3 T Skyra scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. All MRI scans are conducted in the UTMB Radiology Clinic. Each MRI scan session includes a high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan (whole brain sagittal acquisition, 224 slices, TR = 2530 ms, TE = 3.65 ms, TI = 1100 ms, 1 mm thick slices, FOV = 256, 256 × 176, flip angle = 7 degrees), followed by a T2-weighted functional scan during resting state (whole brain acquisition, 136 axial slices, 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm voxel size, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 204 mm, multi-band factor = 3).

Structural MRI data are processed through the FreeSurfer software environment, allowing fMRI data to be projected onto individual cortical surface anatomy. The following preprocessing will be applied for each subject: the first 4 volumes will be deleted to account for magnetization stabilization, motion correction, B0 field map unwarping, slice-timing correction, non-brain removal, spatial smoothing (4-mm full-width half-maximum kernel), grand mean scaling, and band-pass temporal filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). Data segments with a high level of head motion (> 1 mm) will be removed using the scrubbing technique [101].

Functional MRI data are analyzed at the single-subject level using a parcellation technique called Sparse Noise Modeling (SNM), which employs an iterative learning approach to accurately model noise in each individual subject’s fMRI signal. Functional networks localized using this technology have been validated by invasive cortical stimulation mapping in surgical patients [102, 103]. The individualized, fine-grained parcellation method will be applied to map each individual participant’s cortical and subcortical pain networks.

Patient-reported outcome measures

The COWS, PEG-3, and vital signs will be assessed three times per day from 8 am to 8 pm on Days 2 and 3 by trained medical staff. The remaining survey measures are completed privately by the participant on a tablet computer via RedCap on Days 1 and 4.

Psychophysical pain assessment

QST is assessed at baseline Day 1 (prior to opioid tapering) and Day 4 (after 3 days of tAN/sham). For each measure, an average of all trials is assessed as the change from baseline used for analysis. Pressure pain tolerance is assessed on the left forearm using an Algomed algometer (Medoc, Ramat Yishay, Israel). The blinded study coordinator begins with no pressure and then increases the pressure at a consistent rate of 35 kPa/s. The study coordinator will continue to add pressure until pressure pain tolerance is reached and remove the stimulus once the participant presses the button. A 5-s rest is provided before repeating the trial. After one practice trial, five trials of pressure pain tolerance are recorded.

Thermal sensory detection, thermal pain threshold, and thermal pain tolerance are assessed using a Thermal Sensory Analyzer II thermode device (Medoc, Ramat Yishay, Israel). The thermode is affixed to the same location on the left forearm, with the switch again held in the right hand. The thermode will start with no stimulation and increase heat at a rate of 0.5ºC per second. Once the switch is pressed, the thermode rapidly returns to room temperature. The participant is instructed to press the switch as soon as detecting the heat, for five trials of thermal sensory detection. Next, they complete five trials pressing the switch as soon as the thermal pain threshold is reached. Last, participants complete five trials pressing the switch as they reach their thermal pain tolerance, with the device stopping if they do not press the switch before reaching the maximum temperature of 50 °C. A 10–15 s rest is provided before repeating each trial.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

If a participant chooses to leave the study after enrollment but before completion of the 4-day study, they will receive time-prorated renumeration. Completion of follow-up is not applicable as the participants engage in only 4-day study visit.

Data management {19}

For the primary outcome measure, the fMRI imaging data are collected via a password-protected external hard drive, which is then stored in a locked cabinet in a locked office. From this hard drive, neuroimaging data are transmitted securely to MUSC via a HIPAA-compliant cloud-based file sharing service and are analyzed in the MUSC Computational Imaging Core. At the halfway point of data collection (n = 20), a formal assessment of data values was performed by a neuroimaging scientist, and results were presented to the study monitoring committee.

For secondary outcome data, a secure REDCap database on the UTMB server is used. For clinician-assessed data such as the COWS and QST values, study staff enter the values directly into the REDCap form. For patient-reported outcome measures, the participant directly types their responses into the REDCap form.

Confidentiality {27}

Personal information is stored in a secure REDCap database on the UTMB server. All data are de-identified and uploaded to the NIH HEAL Data Sharing Platform and exported from the REDCap database for statistical analysis. After the trial is completed, de-identified data will remain on the NIH HEAL Data Sharing Platform. Data will be stored at UTMB for a minimum of 10 years before participant identifiers are deleted.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

Biological specimens are not collected in this trial for data analysis. The urine drug screen for study eligibility purposes is discarded and the results are not included in the final data analysis.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

To investigate whether tAN induced brain changes, first-level brain activation maps of “Posttreatment-Baseline” will be estimated for each single participant. The mean, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval (CI) of second-level brain activation changes across the whole brain for the active tAN group and sham group will be calculated and compared by a two-sample t-test (two-tailed). Furthermore, to explore whether tAN modulates functional connectivity (FC) within the vagal afferent and extended pain networks, intrinsic FC among these regions will be quantified before and after treatment. The mean, standard deviation, and 95% CI of BOLD signal activation at each brain region for both groups at baseline and post-treatment will be calculated. A 2 × 2 (time by treatment) repeated measures ANOVA will then compare baseline and post-treatment FC in both active and sham groups. We hypothesize that participants who receive tAN will demonstrate increased BOLD signal activation in vagal afferents within the brainstem. We also hypothesize that participants receiving active tAN will demonstrate decreased BOLD signal activation in the cortical and subcortical pain networks. The assumptions of repeated measures ANOVA, including normality, homogeneity, and outliers/influential values, will be examined. If violations of assumptions are found, a non-parametric Friedman test based on rank will be conducted. Secondary outcomes will be analyzed by performing correlational analysis of brain areas activated during resting state with changes in behavioral and self-report outcome measures, including PHQ-9, GAD-7, PROMIS-PA, PROMIS-SD, TAPS-1, and PCL-5. All analyses are intent-to-treat and conducted using SAS/STAT software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc). A more detailed statistical analysis plan, specific for each set of outcome measures, will be included in each results manuscript.

Interim analyses {21b}

No formal interim analyses will be performed. At the halfway point of data collection (20 participants completed), the study biostatistician will conduct descriptive analyses for all enrolled participants together to ensure the quality of data. The Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) has access to the descriptive results. The determination to terminate the trial can be made by the investigative team, the sponsor, and/or the DSMB.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

A correlational (regression) analysis of the relationship between changes in secondary outcomes and changes in brain activation (measured by fMRI) will be performed. A subgroup analysis including subjects with the dosage of opioid at baseline above or below median will be conducted. Other subgroup analyses will be based on the score of SOWS and PEG-3. If we find there is a significant difference in the outcomes associated with tAN by gender (the stratifying variable), we will perform an analysis conditional on gender.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

We will conduct per-protocol analyses and exclude participants who discontinue intervention (non-adherence). We do not expect that participant non-adherence is systematic or not at random. The comparison of non-adherence by baseline measurements between two groups will be reported. Missing or spurious values will not be imputed as the number of missing values is expected to be low. The count of missing values will be included in the summary tables and listings.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data and statistical code {31c}

Group-level study data and the full study protocol, including statistical analysis code, will be shared on the NIH HEAL Data Ecosystem. This platform is a secure environment where users can search for NIH HEAL-funded studies and access their data. Individual participant-level study data and individual identifiers will not be shared.

Composition of the coordinating centre and trial steering committee {5d}

The coordinating site center team at UTMB consists of the study investigators, study postdoctoral fellow, project manager, and research coordinator. This team meets weekly for running the trial day-to-day. The investigator team, consisting of investigators from each of the three sites, meets once monthly. The investigator team also meets with the NIH Program Officer quarterly.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

The DSMB is composed of four experts not otherwise affiliated with the proposed studies who are highly experienced in clinical trials, brain stimulation, fMRI, pain, and opioid use. The DSMB does not have an independent statistician. The DSMB provides independent, continuous, and impartial oversight of processes and data monitoring to ensure scientific validity, safety, and overall integrity of the clinical trials. The DSMB meets bi-annually online, at which time study progress, recruitment, withdrawals, etc., and recommendations for changes are discussed. A summary of the DSMB reviews is compiled. The UTMB Research Integrity and Regulatory Affairs (RIRA) office assists with research regulatory compliance, resources, ClinicalTrials.gov registration, and training and education.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Monitoring of the study and reporting of any adverse events or harms to the UTMB IRB is the responsibility of the investigators as a component of UTMB IRB standard procedures which require reporting within 48 h for serious adverse events and 5 days for unanticipated adverse events. As data are collected, all protocol deviations and adverse events will be identified, graded for severity and assigned causality, and reported to the required entities, and compiled for periodic review. After assigning causality, the investigators will determine the course of action for the study participant. The PIs will evaluate every adverse event and decide the potential impact on the risk/benefit ratio of the study and whether modifications of the protocol or informed consent are required. An annual report summarizing all AEs will be prepared and reported to the DSMB, IRB and NIH. Reports of adverse events, protocol deviations, and any other relevant safety information (dropouts, device malfunctions, etc.) are reviewed by the research team on an ongoing weekly basis at regular meetings, and the research team discusses whether the study should continue as planned, if changes are needed, or if the study should be stopped.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

The clinical trial is monitored by quarterly reviews by the DSMB, annual reviews by the IRB, and annual progress reports to NIH.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g,. trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

All significant protocol changes will be communicated to the IRB, DSMB, NIH, and clinical trial registries. Changes that would affect the risk/benefit ratio of the study and impact prior participants will be communicated to participants.

Dissemination plans {31a}

Data will be shared on the NIH HEAL Data Sharing Platform. All results from analyses will be published in open-access, peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

The present study is the first to study tAN stimulation in the adult population with chronic pain experiencing a medically managed opioid taper. This study may provide evidence to support the FDA approval for managing opioid withdrawal symptoms during an opioid taper protocol in patients with chronic pain. Preliminary work in 17 healthy adults found that active stimulation showed BOLD changes measured by fMRI in the contralateral postcentral gyrus, bilateral insula, frontal cortex, and left cerebellum [104]. A recent open-label study on focused ultrasound neurostimulation to the bilateral nucleus accumbens, a similar target to our study’s, provided highly promising results, with seven of eight participants remaining abstinent from opioids 20 days post-intervention [105]. Our results could show the neuroimaging and QST findings of analgesia, improvements in behavioral outcome measures, and improvements in opioid withdrawal symptoms during a controlled medication taper.

The study’s strengths include the fact that participants are admitted for inpatient stays, which minimizes the impact of environmental changes, lifestyle changes, differences in physical activity, and other factors that may influence pain or opioid withdrawal. The medication dosages are managed by the research physician and nursing staff. Additionally, there is a robust blinding protocol, with fully blinded study personnel applying identical devices that are indistinguishable between stimulation and sham settings. With the data we are collecting on both healthy subjects and in patients with chronic pain, we can compare fMRI outcomes between the two groups.

The limitations of the study include the fact that the activities, foods, and environment of the clinical research center do not reflect the participants’ real lives. Chronic pain patients often have poor health, and many potential participants are not able to enroll due to the presence of pacemakers, implanted non-MRI compatible metal implants, or medical instability. Additionally, many chronic pain patients have higher rates of substance use, which can alter their brain function, leading to their exclusion from the study.

The challenges we have encountered in conducting the study are mostly issues related to fMRI claustrophobia. We had to exclude one participant because they were unable to complete fMRI assessments. To address this, we’ve implemented pre-screening questions to identify participants who may be uncertain about undergoing the fMRI. We now offer mock MRI training, which consists of participants lying in a similarly sized cardboard cutout with their heads secured to a backboard for the same duration as the real fMRI session. Additionally, we reproduce the loud sounds of the MRI to enhance the authenticity of the experience. Moreover, we have faced difficulties in enrolling male participants. This may be attributed to several factors, including the higher prevalence of chronic pain disorders in females, greater reliance on male chronic pain patients by their families, and higher refusal rates among potential male participants. Enrollment in our study requires participants to provide consistent, timely, and accurate reporting during QST tests and to complete a high volume of patient-reported outcome measures. An age-related decline in cognition affects many individuals with persistent pain, rendering some of our screened participants ineligible for participation. Additionally, fMRI contraindications prevent us from enrolling participants with implanted stimulation devices, pregnancy, implanted pumps, or other magnetic implants.
