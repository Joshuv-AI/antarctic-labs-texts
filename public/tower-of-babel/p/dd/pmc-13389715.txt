Successful emergency Hartmann procedure for perforated sigmoid colon cancer requiring splenic flexure mobilization under combined spinal-epidural anesthesia: a case report
Authors: Takuya Mishina, Akihiro Yamada, Satoshi Kobayashi, Atsushi Sekimura, Takehiro Takagi, Takashi Maeda, Akihiro Hori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: We report a case of an emergency Hartmann procedure with splenic flexure mobilization performed under combined spinal-epidural anesthesia (CSEA) in a patient with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).Patient and Methods: An 88-year-old man with severe COPD requiring home oxygen therapy presented with abdominal pain, constipation, and vomiting. Computed tomography revealed an obstructing sigmoid colon tumor with a mesocolic abscess, suspected proximal perforation, diffuse ascites, and synchronous hepatic metastases. Previous spirometric assessments demonstrated the following findings: vital capacity (VC), 2.77 L; %VC, 91.4%; forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), 0.75 L; %predicted FEV1, 33.5%; and FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC), 35.2%. CSEA was selected because general anesthesia posed a high risk of postoperative pulmonary complications.Results: Hartmann procedure with splenic flexure mobilization was successfully completed under CSEA. The operative time was 144 min, and blood loss was 92 mL. Mild shoulder pain occurred during upper-abdominal manipulation; however, conversion to general anesthesia was not required. No postoperative respiratory complications occurred and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 31. The patient died 15 months after surgery because of the progression of colon cancer.Conclusion: CSEA may be a feasible alternative for carefully selected high-risk patients undergoing emergency colorectal surgery, even when splenic flexure mobilization is required.

Introduction

Emergency colorectal surgery in elderly patients with severe respiratory dysfunction presents a major perioperative challenge because general anesthesia may increase the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) in these patients1). Although laparotomy is often unavoidable in patients with generalized peritonitis due to colorectal perforation, alternative anesthetic strategies may be required in carefully selected, high-risk patients. Combined spinal-epidural anesthesia (CSEA) has been reported for selected colorectal procedures; however, its application in open colorectal surgery requiring upper-abdominal manipulation remains uncommon2, 3). We report a case wherein a successful emergency Hartmann procedure with splenic flexure mobilization was performed under CSEA in a patient with perforated sigmoid colon cancer and severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

Case Presentation

An 88-year-old man presented with a 2-day history of abdominal pain, constipation, and vomiting. His medical history included severe COPD with home oxygen therapy (1 L/min at rest and 2 L/min with exertion), vertebral compression fractures, laparoscopic cholecystectomy for cholelithiasis, and right hemicolectomy for diverticulitis 20 years previously. His height, weight, and body mass index were 161 cm, 41 kg, and 15.8 kg/m2, respectively.

On examination, his temperature was 38.1°C, and he exhibited marked left lower quadrant tenderness with guarding. Laboratory investigations revealed the following findings: white blood cell (WBC) count, 10,570/μL; C-reactive protein (CRP) level, 6.28 mg/dL; procalcitonin level, 46.31 ng/mL; and renal dysfunction (blood urea nitrogen level, 32.8 mg/dL; creatinine level, 1.66 mg/dL). The patient was diagnosed as showing generalized peritonitis secondary to the sigmoid colon cancer obstruction. The patient’s respiratory function had been evaluated in previous tests but was likely further compromised at the time of presentation. Given the high risk of PPCs, including prolonged mechanical ventilation, pneumonia, and the potential need for tracheostomy associated with general anesthesia, a multidisciplinary team opted for CSEA in preparation for possible conversion to general anesthesia if required.

An epidural catheter was placed at the T9–T10 interspace, and spinal anesthesia was administered at L3–L4. For spinal anesthesia, 2.8 mL of hyperbaric bupivacaine, 10 µg of fentanyl, and 0.1 mg of morphine were administered as a bolus. Continuous epidural infusion of 0.2% ropivacaine was initiated at a rate of 4 mL/h. Before skin incision, the sensory block level was confirmed up to T4, and this level was maintained and extended to T3 during the procedure. To maintain adequate blood pressure, continuous intravenous norepinephrine (0.04–0.15 μg/kg/min) was required, which was supplemented by intermittent boluses of phenylephrine and ephedrine as needed.

Midline laparotomy was performed. Adhesions from the previous right hemicolectomy were identified and divided. The tumor was palpable in the sigmoid colon, and a fecal abscess was found along with purulent ascites in the descending mesocolon. The mass was also palpable in segment 5 of the liver. To ensure adequate infection control, parts of the descending and sigmoid colon were resected together with the mesocolonic membrane containing the abscess. Mobilization of the splenic flexure was required to facilitate the construction of an end colostomy in the left upper quadrant and to remove the abscess within the descending mesentery, and the Hartmann procedure was performed (Figure 2Figure 2Schematic illustrations of the surgical procedure. Histopathological examination revealed a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma of the sigmoid colon (type 3, 50 × 40 mm) with transmural invasion (pT4a) and regional lymph node metastasis (pN2a). Meropenem was administered postoperatively until postoperative day (POD) 5. The patient was able to ambulate and resumed oral intake by POD 3. The patient did not show pneumonia or other respiratory complications. Mild delirium and paralytic ileus (Clavien–Dindo grade I) were observed; however, after rehabilitation for postoperative deconditioning, the patient was discharged on POD 31.

Follow-up CT examinations revealed stable hepatic metastases. Molecular analyses confirmed the wild-type status of RAS/BRAF. After careful discussion, and in accordance with the patient’s wishes, no chemotherapy was administered, and the patient was observed while living at home.

Eleven months after surgery, the patient was hospitalized because of dyspnea, and imaging revealed progression of hepatic metastases with newly developed multiple pulmonary metastases. Although the patient’s Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status was 3, panitumumab monotherapy was initiated at the patient’s request. Temporary disease control was achieved; however, the disease subsequently progressed, and the patient died 15 months after surgery because of colon cancer progression.

Discussion

General anesthesia is considered the standard anesthetic approach for colorectal surgery because of the extensive nature of the procedure, frequent requirement for upper-abdominal manipulation, and the need for adequate muscle relaxation and respiratory control. However, in patients with severe respiratory compromise, general anesthesia may pose substantial risks, including PPCs, prolonged ventilator dependence, pneumonia, and the potential need for tracheostomy.

Spinal anesthesia or CSEA is typically reserved for surgeries limited to the lower abdomen and perineum, such as anorectal or low anterior resection. In colorectal surgery, previously reported applications of neuraxial anesthesia, including CSEA, primarily involved selected colorectal procedures, including low anterior resection and right colectomy2, 3). Reports describing open colorectal resections requiring splenic flexure mobilization under CSEA remain uncommon. Notably, mobilization of the splenic flexure is generally regarded as a colorectal surgical maneuver that requires extensive upper-abdominal manipulation and often involves traction and dissection near the diaphragm. From an anesthetic perspective, such upper-abdominal manipulation poses particular challenges for neuraxial anesthesia because the visceral and diaphragmatic afferent pathways may not be fully blocked, increasing the risk of intraoperative discomfort and hemodynamic instability. In this context, the successful completion of the Hartmann procedure with splenic flexure mobilization under CSEA in the present case highlights the potential feasibility of this anesthetic strategy with careful planning.

Preoperative pulmonary function tests are useful for perioperative risk assessment in patients with severe respiratory dysfunction. Parameters such as VC, %VC, FEV1, and the FEV1/FVC ratio reflect the respiratory reserve and may help estimate the risk of PPCs. Although no universally accepted cutoff values are available to define operability for colorectal surgery4), markedly reduced FEV1 values and impaired ventilatory function are generally associated with increased perioperative risk, especially in elderly patients undergoing emergency laparotomy1, 5,6,7). In the present case, the patient’s FEV1 was markedly reduced despite the preservation of %VC, indicating severe obstructive impairment rather than restrictive dysfunction. Together with home oxygen dependence, COPD, emergency laparotomy, and the need for upper-abdominal manipulation, these findings indicated an extremely high-risk pulmonary profile. In such patients, avoiding airway instrumentation and positive-pressure ventilation may be advantageous. Savas et al. reported that abdominal surgery, including segmental colectomy, could be safely performed under regional anesthesia alone in patients with severe pulmonary disease, supporting the rationale for a neuraxial-only approach in such high-risk patients8). Moreover, evidence from meta-analyses suggests that epidural-based analgesia can reduce postoperative pneumonia after abdominal or thoracic surgery in comparison with systemic opioid-based strategies, supporting the rationale for neuraxial techniques in patients with high pulmonary risk9). Thus, regional anesthesia and analgesia remain important components of perioperative management, particularly in high-risk colorectal surgery.

Nevertheless, recent advances in anesthetic management, including short-acting agents, lung-protective ventilatory strategies, and multimodal analgesia, have considerably improved the safety profile of general anesthesia, even in patients with severe respiratory dysfunction. General anesthesia with a laryngeal mask airway may be considered an alternative approach to avoid tracheal intubation in selected patients. However, emergency laparotomy for generalized peritonitis carries a substantial risk of aspiration and may require secure airway protection, stable ventilation, and rapid conversion to more invasive respiratory support. Therefore, in the present case, the multidisciplinary team judged that the avoidance of tracheal intubation and positive-pressure ventilation by means of CSEA, with readiness for immediate conversion to general anesthesia, if necessary, represented the most appropriate strategy.

In this case, the anesthetic challenge was compounded by the patient’s severe COPD with a markedly reduced pulmonary reserve. Thoracic epidural anesthesia supplemented with spinal anesthesia provided sufficient analgesia and muscle relaxation to complete the procedure, despite the requirement for splenic flexure mobilization. However, an important limitation of CSEA for procedures extending into the upper abdomen is incomplete coverage of the visceral and diaphragmatic afferent pathways. In our case, referred shoulder pain occurred during upper-abdominal manipulation, suggesting that analgesia may not have been complete despite adequate neuraxial block for surgical progress. Such shoulder pain is commonly attributed to diaphragmatic irritation transmitted through the phrenic nerve, and may occur even when epidural analgesia is otherwise effective10). This observation underscores the importance of explicit preparedness for conversion to general anesthesia if analgesia is inadequate or if physiological instability develops.

From a surgical perspective, the Hartmann procedure was selected because the patient had obstructive sigmoid colon cancer with proximal perforation and generalized peritonitis. In cancer-related perforations, leaving the tumor in situ carries the risk of persistent contamination and inadequate source control. Current emergency colorectal cancer guidelines emphasize life-saving strategies and source control tailored to patient physiology and contamination severity11). Primary anastomosis was considered inappropriate given the patient’s advanced age, systemic inflammation, and peritonitis, which are widely recognized as high-risk conditions for anastomotic failure in emergency left-sided colectomy12, 13). In a nationwide Japanese cohort of 8,500 patients with colorectal perforation of various etiologies including cancer, Tsuchiya et al. demonstrated that the Hartmann procedure was associated with significantly lower 30-day mortality than primary anastomosis12). Accordingly, the Hartmann procedure represents a pragmatic surgical strategy that prioritizes sepsis control and the avoidance of anastomotic complications.

Another potential surgical option is to construct a transverse loop-end colostomy without splenic flexure mobilization. However, in the present case, adequate infection control required resection of the perforated segment along with the mesocolonic abscess, and simple diversion alone would likely have provided insufficient source control. Furthermore, because the patient had previously undergone right hemicolectomy, the remaining colonic length was limited, and mobilization of the splenic flexure was considered necessary to achieve a tension-free end colostomy.

With the increasing proportion of elderly patients with complex comorbidities, individualized strategies that integrate multidisciplinary anesthetic planning with appropriate surgical approaches are becoming increasingly relevant. The findings in this case suggest that CSEA may be a feasible alternative for carefully selected high-risk patients even when upper-abdominal manipulation, including splenic flexure mobilization, is required, provided that teams acknowledge its limitations, maintain clear criteria for conversion, and remain prepared for conversion to general anesthesia.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that CSEA is a feasible anesthetic option for emergency colorectal surgery requiring upper-abdominal manipulation, including splenic flexure mobilization, in carefully selected high-risk patients. Multidisciplinary planning and appropriate surgical strategies enabled safe completion of the Hartmann procedure while avoiding general anesthesia-related respiratory complications.
