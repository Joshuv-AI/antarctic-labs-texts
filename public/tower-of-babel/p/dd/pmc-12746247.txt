Utility of the prostate health index density for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer in patients with negative magnetic resonance imaging findings
Authors: Kazuhiko Oshinomi, Shota Kikuchi, Hirotaka Kishi, Anju Hayashi, Sho Okada, Masahiro Kurokawa, Toshiki Mugita, Yoshiki Tsunokawa, Tatsuki Inoue, Motoki Yamagishi, Yoshihiro Nakagami, Masakazu Nagata, Takashi Fukagai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used in prostate cancer diagnosis; however, a significant proportion of clinically important cancers can be missed in patients with negative MRI findings. Although attempts have been made to analyze prostate-specific antigen (PSA)-related parameters to improve the specificity of PSA testing, no evidence-based cutoff values have been established. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the prostate health index (PHI) and PHI density (PHID) in detecting clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) in the MRI-negative population.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 339 MRI-negative patients with PSA levels of 3–15 ng/mL who underwent PHI testing and MRI between September 2022 and July 2024. MRI-negative patients were defined as those with Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) category 1 or 2 lesions. Biopsies were performed in 181 patients. The diagnostic accuracies of PSA, % free PSA, PHI, PSA density, and PHID were assessed using the receiver operating characteristic curves.ResultsAmong the 47 patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2 who underwent biopsy, 24 (51.1%) had prostate cancer, of whom 16 (66.7%) had csPCa. The PHID showed the highest diagnostic performance (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.8832 for any cancer; AUC = 0.7843 for csPCa). Two patients with a PHI <27.2 were diagnosed with low-risk cancer (Gleason 6).ConclusionsThe PHI and PHID may serve as valuable adjuncts to MRI for the detection of csPCa and may help avoid unnecessary biopsies in patients with negative MRI findings.Keywords: Clinically significant, Diagnostic, Magnetic resonance imaging, Negative, Prostate cancer

Introduction

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening is widely used for prostate cancer detection; however, its limited specificity leads to unnecessary biopsies and overdiagnosis. Attempts have been made to analyze PSA-related parameters to improve the specificity of PSA testing. The free-to-total PSA ratio, which takes advantage of the low rate of free PSA in patients with prostate cancer; PSA density (PSAD), which takes into account the PSA level elevation due to benign prostatic hyperplasia; and PSAD of the transition zone are PSA-related parameters that may improve the specificity of PSA testing, but no evidence-based cutoff values have been established for either.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 The Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) is a standardized reporting and evaluation system for the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) diagnosis of prostate cancer. It aims to standardize the imaging, interpretation, and reporting of prostate MRI and contribute to improving the accuracy of detection of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa). Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) is primarily used to evaluate the risk of clinically significant prostate lesions. It is scored from 1 to 5, with a score of ≥4 indicating a high likelihood of csPCa. However, a score of 1 or 2 indicates a low likelihood of significant cancer.6 Although mpMRI has improved the ability to detect cancer, the interpretation of negative MRI findings remains controversial, and there is a possibility that important cancers may be overlooked. Recently, it has been reported that an index related to [−2]proPSA (p2PSA), a precursor of PSA, has a high diagnostic accuracy as an auxiliary marker for prostate cancer. Among the p2PSA-derived indices, the prostate health index (PHI) is calculated as PHI = (p2PSA/free PSA) × √(total PSA).7, 8, 9 Several studies have demonstrated that the PHI has excellent sensitivity and specificity for csPCa detection. PSA screening has limitations in terms of specificity, particularly in the diagnostic gray zone (PSA <10 ng/mL). The PROPHET study demonstrated that p2PSA-derived indices, especially the PHI, significantly improved csPCa detection in this population.9 We hypothesized that the PHI might contribute to the detection of csPCa in patients with negative MRI findings. This study aimed to assess the utility of the PHI for detecting csPCA in patients with negative MRI findings, thereby evaluating its potential to safely reduce unnecessary biopsies.

Materials and methods

The participants were patients who visited our hospital with an elevated PSA level (4.0–10.0 ng/mL) between September 2022 and July 2024. We retrospectively studied 339 patients whose PSA levels were reinvestigated at our hospital and had a level of 3–15 ng/mL. Free PSA levels, the PHI, MRI findings, and the clinical course, including biopsy status and results, were also investigated. The initial inclusion criterion for the patients was a PSA level of 4–10 ng/mL; however, some patients who met this range at the time of referral from the primary care or health check-up centers presented with PSA levels slightly outside this range (e.g. <4 or >10 ng/mL) at their initial visit to our institution. To account for this variability, the inclusion range was revised to 3–15 ng/mL. The prostate volume was measured using MRI in all patients. The cutoff PHI value was 27.2 with a sensitivity of 90%.9

All patients underwent prostate MRI, including both multiparametric and noncontrast MRI. Patients who did not undergo MRI were excluded from the analysis. During the study period, no changes were made to MRI acquisition protocols. All MRI scans were interpreted using PI-RADS version 2.1 by two board-certified radiologists with expertise in genitourinary imaging, and all interpretations were double-checked. Although central review was not performed, interpretations were made according to the institutional guidelines, any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved in multidisciplinary meetings. The PHI density (PHID) was calculated as the PHI divided by the prostate volume, with the prostate volume measured from MRI images in all cases to ensure reproducibility.

We examined whether significant cancer was detected in patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2 (MRI-negative findings). Data on PSA, free PSA, and the PHI values were collected, and the PSAD and PHID were calculated using the prostate volume. Clinically significant cancer was defined as cancer that met any of the following three criteria: (1) lesion with a Gleason score of 7 (including 3 + 4) or higher, (2) number of positive biopsies of ≥20% of the total, and (3) maximum cancer length of the biopsy core of ≥5 mm.10

The indication for prostate biopsy was determined by the treating urologist based on the PSA level, digital rectal examination, elevated PHI or PSAD, or a combination of these. Biopsies in patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2 lesions were primarily performed via an ultrasound-guided transrectal systematic 12-site approach; however, a transperineal approach was used in patients with anal diseases. In selected cases, additional cognitively targeted biopsies were performed for PI-RADS 3–5 lesions. Only patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2 lesions were included in this study. Histopathological evaluation and Gleason grading were performed by a board-certified pathologist in accordance with the 2014 International Society of Urological Pathology consensus.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro15 software (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and two-sided pvalues <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves comparing the areas under the curve (AUC). The optimal PHID cutoff for csPCa prediction was determined by maximizing Youden's index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) from the ROC analysis.Multivariable logistic regression analysis was additionally performed to evaluate whether the PHID was an independent predictor of csPCa. Covariates included age, PSA density, and the PHI, selected based on clinical relevance and potential confounding. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were reported.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. 22-208 B).

Informed consent was obtained through an opt-out approach.

Results

Of the 339 cases, 153 (45%) were PI-RADS 1 and 20 (6%) were PI-RADS 2, indicating that approximately half of the total cases were PI-RADS1 and 2 with negative MRI findings (Fig. 1).

The percentage of abnormal PHI values (≥27.2) was 57.1% (102/173 cases) in PI-RADS 1 or 2, 74.1% (43/58 cases) in PI-RADS 3, and 86.1% (93/108 cases) in PI-RADS 4 or 5. Of the 183 patients who underwent biopsy and the 47 patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2 who underwent biopsy, 24 were diagnosed with prostate cancer, of whom 16 were clinically significant. The biopsy-positive rate was 45.5% (24/47 cases) in patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2, 47.4% (18/38 cases) in those with PI-RADS 3, and 76.0% (73/96 cases) in those with PI-RADS 4 or 5. The proportion of significant cancers was 66.7% (16/24) for PI-RADS 1 or 2, 66.6% (12/18) for PI-RADS 3, and 83.6% (61/73) for PI-RADS 4 or 5. When comparing the biopsy-positive and -negative groups, significant differences were observed between the two groups except for age and PSA values. PHI classification in patients who underwent biopsy with PI-RADS 1 or 2 is shown in Table 2. The sensitivity and specificity of the PHI in our patients were 91.7% and 34.8%, respectively. In the ROC curves for the prediction of any prostate cancer, the AUC for the PHID (0.8832; 95% CI, 0.7642–0.9606) was significantly larger than that for the PHI (0.7563; 95% CI, 0.5837–0.8779) (p = 0.0340), with an optimal cutoff value of 1.09. This cutoff yielded a sensitivity of 87.5% and a specificity of 78.3%. In the ROC curves for the prediction of csPCa, the AUC for the PHID (0.7843; 95% CI, 0.6331–0.9022) was the largest; however, it was not significantly different from that for the PHI (0.6935; 95% CI, 0.5187–0.8419) (p = 0.0810). The optimal cutoff value of the PHID for csPCa prediction was 1.23, with a sensitivity of 75.0% and a specificity of 50.8% (Fig. 2).

In the univariable logistic regression analysis, the PHID was significantly associated with the detection of csPCa (OR, 2.556; 95% CI, 1.228–5.319; p = 0.0126). However, in the multivariable analysis, the PHID was not identified as an independent predictor (OR, 1.706; 95% CI, 0.3390–8.587; p = 0.5170).

Discussion

In recent years, large-scale cohort studies and randomized controlled trials have shown that performing mpMRI before biopsy and targeted biopsy in patients with positive MRI findings increases the detection of clinically significant cancers, reduces the detection of clinically insignificant cancers, and avoids unnecessary biopsies. However, some clinically significant cancers cannot be detected using MRI, which is a clinical issue.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 If a biopsy is not performed in cases with negative MRI findings (PI-RADS 1 or 2), typically 5–20% of significant cancers may be missed.15,16 There is no consensus on how to safely avoid biopsies when mpMRI results are negative.

In this study, we demonstrated that the PHI and PHID are useful for detecting csPCa in patients with PI-RADS 1 or 2 on MRI. In particular, the PHID showed high diagnostic performance compared with other biomarkers, with AUCs of 0.8832 (all cancers) and 0.7843 (csPCa). These results suggest that the PHID can be an effective auxiliary index for detecting csPCa that may be missed on MRI alone. Previous studies have reported on the usefulness of the PHI and PHID. Stephan et al. reported that the PHID is more useful than the PHI in detecting prostate cancer; however, there was no difference between the two in detecting csPCa.17 However, Jeon et al. showed that the PHI and PHID were effective in detecting prostate cancer in patients with no clear lesions on mpMRI.18 Druskin et al. also reported that the PHID is superior to other PSA-related indices in diagnosing csPCa and that the diagnostic accuracy improves when combined with MRI.19 Furthermore, Tosoian et al. reported that the use of the PHID could reduce unnecessary biopsies by 38%, and the rate of missed csPCa by 2%.20 Applying a PHID cutoff of 1.23 would have avoided biopsy in 27 patients, missing four csPCa cases (25% of all csPCa cases). All missed csPCa cases were of Grade 2 or 3, with no Gleason ≥8 tumors. This suggests that, while some intermediate-grade cancers may be missed, high-grade disease would not be overlooked, which is acceptable in selected MRI-negative patients when integrated with other clinical factors. Nevertheless, this sensitivity–specificity balance highlights the need for individualized decision-making and external validation of the cutoff. Our findings align with those of the PROPHET study,9 which highlighted the superior diagnostic performance of the PHI over traditional PSA metrics in detecting csPCa among patients with PSA levels <10 ng/mL. Incorporating the PHI into clinical practice may reduce unnecessary biopsies while maintaining a high sensitivity for significant cancers.

The results of this study are consistent with those of previous studies and support the usefulness of the PHID in detecting csPCa, particularly in patients without clear lesions on MRI. Furthermore, the use of the PHID may make it possible to avoid unnecessary biopsies and minimize the risk of overlooking csPCa. In addition, combining the PHID with MRI may enable a more careful consideration of the indications for biopsy, especially in older patients and those with comorbidities, potentially reducing the risk of overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Our findings align with those of previous studies highlighting the value of the PHID in optimizing biopsy strategies. Kim et al. proposed a PHID threshold to avoid unnecessary biopsies in PI-RADS 3 cases while maintaining high sensitivity for csPCa.21 Although our study focused on MRI-negative patients, similar principles may apply, as Arulraj et al. demonstrated that negative MRI alone cannot reliably exclude csPCa.22 Moreover, prospective data from Chen et al. support the integration of the PHID with mpMRI findings to improve diagnostic accuracy across different PI-RADS categories.23 These results collectively suggest that the PHID could serve as a robust adjunct to MRI, particularly in settings where imaging findings are inconclusive or negative.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings. The decision to perform a prostate biopsy may have been influenced not only by serum biomarkers such as the PHI and PSAD in clinical practice but also by age and comorbidities. Therefore, selection bias may be a potential limitation. Furthermore, the cutoff values obtained from this cohort may not be generalizable to a broader population. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to externally validate the diagnostic performance of the PHID and PHI in MRI-negative patients. Second, prostate biopsies were performed based on the attending physician's clinical judgment, possibly introducing selection bias, as elevated PHI or PSAD values may have influenced the decision to perform a biopsy. Third, mpMRI was interpreted by multiple radiologists without central review, potentially leading to interobserver variability in PI-RADS classification. Fourth, the cutoff values for the PHID were derived from this cohort and have not been externally validated, limiting their applicability to other populations. Fifth, prostate-volume measurements used to calculate the PHID and PSAD may be subject to inter-reader variability, affecting reproducibility. Sixth, although the PHID demonstrated superior diagnostic performance in ROC analysis, it was not an independent predictor of csPCa in the multivariable logistic regression model. This may be partly explained by its correlation with other PSA-derived parameters, such as the PSAD and PHI, which are mathematically and biologically related. Consequently, the incremental value of the PHID may be limited compared with that of established markers when these parameters are included simultaneously in predictive models. Finally, pathological assessments were performed at a single institution, which may limit the consistency and generalizability of the histopathological grading. Further verification of the usefulness of the PHID through multicenter prospective studies is required to expand the possibility of its clinical application. In conclusion, in patients with negative MRI findings (PI-RADS 1 or 2), the PHI, especially the PHID, showed excellent diagnostic performance in detecting csPCa. The PHID showed the highest AUC among all markers evaluated and may be a useful indicator to complement MRI in biopsy decision-making. Incorporating the PHID into clinical practice is expected to help avoid unnecessary biopsies without compromising the diagnostic accuracy of csPCa.
