Revealing interfacial failure mechanism of silicon based all solid state batteries via cryogenic electron microscopy
Authors: Jingming Yao, Zhixuan Yu, Jun Ma, Zhangran Ye, Congcong Du, Jun Zhao, Jingzhao Chen, Hongjun Ye, Qiushi Dai, Hui Li, Yong Su, Jitong Yan, Dingding Zhu, Zaifa Wang, Xuedong Zhang, Zhaoyu Rong, Qiang Yu, Ziang Guo, Hailong Qiu, Zhenyu Wang, Lingyun Zhu, Yongfu Tang, Jianyu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterfaces are the critical components of all-solid-state batteries, and it is generally believed that high interfacial impedance is the major culprits of battery failure. In this study, the interface impedance has been found not to be a major issue in the batteries comprising Si negative electrode, Li10GeP2S12 and Li10Si0.3PS6.7Cl1.8 electrolytes and LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 positive electrode. Instead, it is the sustainable interfacial reaction that depletes the active lithium source, causing continuous capacity decay. The interphase layer at the Si/Li10Si0.3PS6.7Cl1.8 interface comprising nanocrystalline Li2S dispersed in an amorphous matrix is thin (with a thickness < 200 nm) and stable, and the battery maintains a good cyclability. In contrast, the interphase layer at the Si/Li10GeP2S12 interface is thick with a thickness of 10 μm. Couter-intuitively, despite the thick interfacial layer comprising mainly needle shaped Li2S, the interfacial impedance does not increase dramatically, suggesting that interfacial impedance is not the main issue, rather, it is the chemically/electrochemically continuous reaction of negative electrode with Li10GeP2S12 that consumes the active lithium source from positive electrode and causes the capacity decay. This study provides atomic-scale interface structures of sulfide based batteries, which have important implications for the design of stable interfaces for high performance batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Cryoelectron microscopy, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) are potential post lithium energy storage technology. Compared to traditional liquid electrolyte batteries, ASSBs with solid state electrolytes (SSEs) are expected to offer better safety, longer cycle life, and higher energy density1. However, achieving high-performance ASSBs, especially those based on sulfide SSEs, still faces many challenges, and one crucial aspect of which is the construction and optimization of electrode/SSE interfaces2,3. The structure and composition of the electrode/SSE interfaces in ASSBs impact not only the complex charge transfer but also the chemical/electrochemical, electrochemomechanical stability of the interfaces4,5. For instance, robust interfaces in oxide-based ASSBs can ensure uniform lithium deposition in the negative electrode6,7, while in halide-based ASSBs, they can ensure the cycling stability of the positive electrodes8. However, lithium tends to react with both sulfide and halide SSEs due to their limited chemical/electrochemical stability windows, and the interfacial reactions are usually detrimental to battery performance9–11. Therefore, understanding the interface failure mechanism is the prerequisite towards constructing stable electrode/SSE interface and achieving good performance of ASSBs12.

Lithium and silicon (Si) are both promising negative electrode candidates with high specific capacity, understanding their stability with sulfide SSEs is critical for the development of high energy density ASSBs13,14. It is widely believed that the Si negative electrode can alleviate the side reaction between the negative electrode and electrolyte due to the higher redox potential (0.3 V versus Li/Li+) for the alloying/dealloying reaction15. However, due to the volume expansion during lithiation, the continuous solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) growth between the organic liquid electrolyte and the Si negative electrode hinders the commercialization of Si negative electrodes in liquid electrolyte lithium-ion batteries16. Nevertheless, applying silicon negative electrodes in ASSBs may effectively circumvent these issues17. However, there are numerous investigations in the Li/sulfide SSE interfaces18–20, while studies of the Si/sulfide SSE are scarce. Moreover, atomic scale imaging the negative electrode/SSE interfaces in sulfide or halide-based ASSBs is very challenging due to both the air sensitivity of the sulfide and halide SSEs and the irradiation damage of electron/ion beam during the preparation and characterizations of interfaces21–23. Nevertheless, understanding the interface structure/composition, especially the difference between the Si/SSE and Li/SSE, is of paramount importance in the development of the prevalent promising Si negative electrode based ASSBs.

In this study, we developed a technology that combines cryogenic focus ion beam (cryo-FIB) and cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) to study the electrode/sulfide SSE interfaces in ASSBs, and characterized the micron silicon (μ-Si) negative electrode/sulfide SSE interface at the atomic scale, thus offering a comprehensive understanding of the μ-Si negative electrode/sulfide SSE interfaces from micron to atomic scale. Cryo-FIB was employed for sample preparation of the Si/SSE interfaces involving two different sulfide electrolytes, i.e., Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS) and Li10Si0.3PS6.7Cl1.8 (LSPSC) with LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) as the positive electrode. Using cryo-TEM, we revealed two distinct interfacial structures, i.e., a thin and sharp interphase layer at the Si/LSPSC interface with a thickness of about 100–200 nm comprising nanocrystalline Li2S dispersed in an amorphous matrix, and a thick interphase layer at the Si/LGPS interface with a thickness of 10–20 μm comprising of massive nanometer needle-shaped Li2S nanocrystals dispersed in the LGPS substrate, and scattered LiGe precipitates which have definite orientation relationship with the Li2S nanocrystals, which is apparently caused by the reaction of lithium-ion and electron with LGPS at the Si/LGPS interface. Moreover, isolated reaction regions (IRRs) featuring similar contrast to the interfacial reaction layer (IRL) and dispersing in the non-reacted LGPS substrate were also observed. The IRL can be understood as a side reaction occurring at the Si/SSE interface. The distinct interface structure has grand impact on the battery performance. Namely, the Si|LGPS | NMC811 ASSB (hereafter refers to ASSBLGPS) suffers from rapid capacity decay, while the Si|LSPSC | NMC811 (ASSBLSPSC) maintains a stable cycling for over 300 cycles. Counter to the conventional understanding that the capacity decay in ASSBs is mainly attributed to the high interfacial impedance, we show that it is not the interfacial impedance but the sustainable interfacial reaction is the culprit for ASSB failure, as the interfacial reaction depletes the active lithium source from positive electrode and causes continuous capacity decay. This study provides the atomic-scale understanding of the complex interfaces in the μ-Si negative electrode and sulfide SSE based ASSBs via cryo-TEM, offering valuable insights for the development of high-performance ASSBs.

Results and discussion

To understand the interface stability of the μ-Si negative electrode in ASSBs, two representative sulfide SSEs, i.e., LSPSC and LGPS, were chosen for the assembly of ASSBs with NMC811 as the positive electrode. During cycling, the capacity (calculated based on the positive electrode mass loading, hereafter the same) of the ASSBLGPS continuously decreases from 120.6 mAh/g to 11.4 mAh/g, retaining only 9.5% capacity after 300 cycles (Fig. S1b). For comparison, the ASSBLSPSC shows stable electrochemical performance with the specific capacity decreases from 123.7 mAh/g at the initial to 100.9 mAh/g after 300 cycles (Fig. 1a, Fig. S1a), with a capacity retention of 81.5%. The average coulombic efficiency (CE) of the ASSBs confirms the higher electrochemical stability of the ASSBLSPSC (with the CE being 99.7%) than that of the ASSBLGPS (CE 99.2%).

To further understand the interfacial reaction in ASSBs, in-situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) test and distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis were conducted to monitor the impedance evolution of the ASSBs with different Si/SSE interfaces24. Comparing with EIS, DRT analysis can effectively decouple different relaxation processes with different relaxation times, providing a semi-quantitative understanding to the evolution of electrochemical interfaces25. The ASSBLGPS (Fig. 1b–d) and ASSBLSPSC (Fig. 1e–g) were cycled between 2.6 V and 4.3 V, with impedance tests conducted at 0.1 V intervals. During the initial charging process in the ASSBLGPS (Fig. 1b), the charge transfer impedance increases from 8 to 21 Ω. It indicates that the lithiation of the μ-Si negative electrode increased the charge transfer impedance due to the reaction between the Li-Si alloy and sulfide electrolyte. And during the discharge process, the charge transfer impedance further increases from 20 to 32 Ω (Fig. 1c). The impedance increasing in both the charge and discharge processes may be attributed to the interface evolution rather than the μ-Si negative electrode. For comparison, the ASSBLSPSC shows stable variations in interface impedance in the first cycle (Fig. 1f). The semicircle gradually expands at the first charging cycle, representing an impedance increase from 14 to 22 Ω. Then the semicircle gradually stabilizes as the discharge process, with the charge transfer impedance slightly increases from 16 to 18 Ω, suggesting the formation of a SEI during the charge and discharge processes.

As shown in Fig. 1d, g, the relaxation time (τ) of 10−6 s corresponds to the grain boundaries in the SSE, where both ASSBs exhibit increasing peaks intensity during cycling, with the grain boundary impedance associated with LGPS showing a more pronounced increase. The peaks corresponding to the relaxation times from 10−5 s to 10−3 s relate to the contact losses caused by significant volume contraction of μ-Si negative electrode. And the peaks with relaxation times from 10−3 to 10−1 s correspond to Li diffusion in the SEI layer at the Si/SSE interface. Significant increasing of this peak during the discharge process with LGPS indicates the interface reaction. The peaks from 10−1 s to 1 s correspond to the charge transfer impedance (Rct) during Li-Si alloying. The distinct peak variations of Rct indicate that the interfacial instability leads to significant resistance to lithium-ion transport at the Si/LGPS interface. However, it is interesting that the interface impedance of both ASSBs was eventually stable after long cycling (Fig. 1h, i). And the X-ray diffraction (XRD) result show that the diffraction intensity of Li2S generated at the Si/LGPS interface is much higher than that at the Si/LSPSC interface, indicating that more severe degradation occurs at the Si/LGPS interface (Fig. S2). This contrasts with the differences in capacity retention, indicating that interface impedance does not seem to have a direct correlation with the capacity decay in the ASSBs.

The in-situ EIS and DRT analysis reveal that the ASSBLSPSC exhibits a stable electrode/SSE interface with relatively low impedance and minimal degradation over cycling, while the ASSBLGPS shows slight increase in impedance and capacity decay. Additionally, compared to the lithium metal negative electrode-based ASSB, the μ-Si negative electrode-based ASSB demonstrates a more stable capacity retention and smaller impedance change during electrochemical cycling (Figs. S3, S4). The use of μ-Si negative electrode significantly suppresses the interface degradation between the negative electrode and the sulfide electrolyte (Figs. S5, S6). The results assert that the SSE has a significant impact on the stability of the Si/SSE interface and the performance of the μ-Si negative electrode based ASSBs. When LSPSC was introduced as an intermediate layer into ASSBLGPS (Fig. 1a, purple line), both the average CE (99.6%) and the capacity retention (69.1% after 300 cycles) were higher than those of the ASSBLGPS (99.2% per cycle, 9.5% after 300 cycles). Moreover, after 0, 100, 200, and 300 cycles, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests for both ASSBs were conducted to investigate the impedance evolution during cycling. It is interesting that the impedance changes for both ASSBs are small after 200 cycles, suggesting that both the Si/LGPS and Si/LSPSC interfaces achieves a stable structure after a long time cycling (Fig. S7), which is different with those with the lithium metal negative electrodes18.

To further investigate the effect of the Si/SSE interface on the battery performance, both ASSBs were disassembled and transferred to a cryogenic scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM) for analysis (Fig. S8). The morphology and elemental distribution of the Si/SSE interface were analyzed through secondary electron (SE) images, central backscattered electron (CBS) images, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. The total thickness of the full cell was approximately 700 μm, comprising a ~ 30 μm μ-Si negative electrode, a ~ 570 μm sulfide SSE and a ~ 100 μm LSPSC/NMC811 (m/m = 3/7) composite positive electrode (Figs. S9, S10). CBS image and EDS mapping reveal that the μ-Si negative electrode in ASSBLGPS gradually transfroms into pillars during cycling (Figs. S11, S12), likely due to the alloying of Si and Li. Notably nearly periodic cracks appeared in the μ-Si negative electrode as well, which is similar to the cracks observed in the μ-Si negative electrode of the ASSBLSPSC, suggesting that these cracks were related to the large volume change of Si (Li-Si) during cycling (Figs. S13, S14). Due to the constraints of the battery mold, radial volume changes were limited. However, volume changes occur throughout the cycling process of the battery. The presence of these cracks inevitably affects lithium-ion transport and consequently impacts the performance of the ASSBs. The oxygen signal between the Si pillars suggests little lithium deposition with cell cycling, attributing to the different migration rates of lithium ions between Si (LixSi) and the electrolyte26. The deposition of Li in the μ-Si negative electrode is confirmed by the presence of numerous nearly periodic arranged cracks in the μ-Si negative electrode. These cracks initiate from the Si/SSE interface, and then propagate towards the interior of μ-Si negative electrode. The crack opening is much larger near the interface, which then narrows down to the sharp crack tip. EDS mapping of the cracks indicates that the cracks are rich in oxygen, suggesting the little Li deposition (including dendrites) along these cracks, as the deposited Li is oxidized in the FIB chamber (Fig. S15). However, when statistically analyzing the area of the O signal in the EDS mapping, the area distribution of the O signal at the Si/LSPSC interface is generally higher than that at the Si/LGPS interface (Fig. S16). This suggests that the interphase layer of pure ionic conductor seems to suppress the chemical/electrochemical degradation of sulfide electrolyte (Fig. S17). Comparing with the interphase layer with pure ionic conductivity at the Si/LSPSC interface, the interphase layer with mixed conductivity of the Si/LGPS interface exhibits a more dispersed distribution of cracks.

In the ASSBLGPS full cell, CBS image results reveal the presence of an approximately 10 μm thick IRL in the LGPS electrolyte near the μ-Si negative electrode surface after 100 cycles, which exhibits darker contrast than the non-reacted sulfide SSE (Fig. 2a). The IRL is formed in the LGPS layer rather than in the μ-Si negative electrode, and it starts from the LGPS surface near the μ-Si negative electrode and propagates into the LGPS interior with a nearly uniform thickness of 10 μm (Fig. 2a, dash line). Note that beyond the 10 μm uniform IRL, isolated reaction regions (IRRs) featuring similar contrast to the IRL and dispersing in the non-reacted LGPS substrate are also observed (Fig. 2a, dash line). The IRRs propagate from the bottom of the IRL to the entire LGPS electrolyte. The central backscattered electron image and its corresponding EDS line scan showed that the content of S/P/Ge near the negative electrode is relatively low (Fig. 2b), indicating the formation of an IRL of about 10 μm due to electrolyte decomposition near the negative electrode.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis demonstrates the appearance of new Li2S, Li3P, and Li-Ge species at the Si/LGPS interface after 100 cycles under the argon-protection. The S 2p spectrum at the Si/LGPS interface exhibits new Li2S double peaks at 161.7 electron volts (eV) and 160.6 eV, in addition to the existing peaks at 163 eV and 161.7 eV (Fig. 2c). The two peaks in the P 2p spectrum at 133.1 eV and 132.1 eV attributed to PS43- tetrahedra negatively shift to 130.5 eV and 131.4 eV (Fig. 2d), respectively, with additional double peaks at 127.1 eV and 126.1 eV assigned to Li3P. The Ge 3 d spectrum at the Si/LGPS interface shifts from a single peak at 31.2 eV to a double peak at 31.2 eV and 29.5 eV, indicating the formation of reduced Ge species (Fig. 2e). These decomposed products are attributed to the electrochemical reaction between LGPS and Li-ion, as reported previously10,27,28. It is worth noting that the chemical and electrochemical stability of the μ-Si negative electrode and IRL product is stable than that of the lithium negative electrode to LGPS electrolyte (Fig. S18). Indeed, the μ-Si negative electrode ended abruptly at the interface, suggesting Si is stable to the LGPS SSE. In conclusion, the Si/LGPS interfacial reaction is mainly caused by the electrochemical reaction of Li+ coming from the positive electrode and e- coming from the negative electrode with the LGPS electrolyte. Moreover, the use of μ-Si negative electrode, as opposed to metallic Li negative electrode, can greatly suppress the interfacial reactions at the negative electrode/electrolyte interface, thereby slowing down the loss of active lithium from positive electrode in the sulfide based ASSB29–31. The atomic structure of the interphase layer was investigated using cryo-TEM. Cryo-FIB was employed to fabricate the Si/LGPS interface samples from the Si|LGPS | NMC811 ASSBs before and after 100 cycles (Figs. S19–21). For a better observation of the Si/sulfide interface, electron beam dose testing was conducted to confirm the reliability of atomic-scale characterization via cryo-TEM. The pristine LGPS electrolyte and Si/LGPS interface was observed in Cryo-HRTEM with the increasing electron beam dose. And it could be stably imaged at 16 e-/Å2, with gradual amorphization takes place with the increasing dose rate, and ultimately full amorphization occurrs at 177 e-/Å2 (Figure S22). During the amorphization process, it was observed that the edges of the LGPS particles changed from sharp to blurred (Fig. S23). Similarly, the LGPS/IRL interface could be stably imaged at 20 e-/Å2, with the gradual appearance of voids in LGPS at an electron beam dose of 150 e-/Å (Fig. S24).

In this case, the original Si/LGPS interface exhibits a tight contact and a clear interface with low-dose HRTEM at 10 e-/Å2 (Fig. S25). This demonstrates that the μ-Si negative electrode is stable with the LGPS electrolyte. The interface samples after 100 cycles were subsequently transferred into a cryo-scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) operating at liquid nitrogen temperature. As shown in the annual dark field (ADF) images, there was a distinct thick reaction layer propagated from the Si/LGPS interface to the LGPS interior with a thickness of 10 μm (Fig. S26a). The IRL contains numerous nearly horizontal, kinked or slanted cracks and nanoscale bright needle like precipitates (Fig. S26b, c). The cracks are generated during the electrochemical reaction of lithium with LGPS SSE (Fig. S6e, g), as revealed in our previous in-situ FIB-SEM experiments32. High resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image and its corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis indicates that the needle-like structures are Li2S (detailed characterization is shown later).

Cryo-STEM electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was used for further structure analysis of the IRL. In the ASSBLGPS, a 10 μm dense IRL comprising of decomposed products was observed (Fig. 2f), and the IRL contains nanostructured needle-like Li2S dispersed in the undecomposed LGPS electrolyte substrate. Similarly, the color gradient from red to purple corresponds to the EELS line scan from the electrolyte to the negative electrode (Fig. 2g). By comparing the standard spectra of LGPS, Li3P, Li2S, and LixSi (Fig. 2h), it is found that the IRL mainly comprised of Li2S, but undecomposed LGPS is also present. The micron-scale uneven IRL forms in the Si/LGPS interface caused degradation of electrochemical performance and cycle life in the ASSB. The plasmon loss peak positions of the negative electrodes at the two solid-state interfaces are both located at 16.7 eV (Fig. S27), while the plasmon peak position of the μ-Si negative electrode shifts to the left during lithium-ion insertion, indicating that the negative electrodes of both ASSBs are in a lithium-extracted state. The active lithium loss originated from the interfacial reaction induced continuous decomposition of the LGPS electrolyte during subsequent charge-discharge cycles resulted in capacity loss in the battery.

In the cryo-TEM image of the Si/LGPS interface (Fig. 3a), the μ-Si negative electrode exhibits lower crystallinity after cycling (Fig. 3b) compared to that at the Si/LSPSC interface (Fig. 5c). The thickness of the IRL is approximately 10–20 μm, and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis shows that it mainly consists of Li2S, Li3P, and Li-Ge (Fig. 3c). Due to the high electronic conductivity of Li-Ge alloy33, the presence of Li-Ge confirming the electrical conductivity of the IRL in ASSBLGPS. Unlike the uniform crystallinity at the Si/LSPSC interface, there is gradient in the crystallinity and grain size of Li2S at the Si/LGPS interface (Fig. S28). The weak crystallinity of Si and the gradient distribution of crystallinity and grain size of Li2S in the IRL indicate that the Si/LGPS interface deterioration occurred during the battery cycling (Figs. S29, S30). Moreover, the Li2S particles after 30 cycles are larger than 30 nm near the μ-Si negative electrode, which differs from that after 100 cycles, indicating that Li2S in the IRL is gradually refined during continuous electrochemical cycling (Fig. S31). Therefore, the uneven distribution of Li2S in the IRL derives from the electrolyte fatigue induced by electrochemical cycling. And the weak crystallinity indicates incomplete delithiation of the Si negative electrode34. Although the LGPS electrolyte exhibits high crystallinity with sharp diffraction rings (Fig. 3d), lattice distortions have been discovered through the [001] zone axis at the decomposition front of the electrolyte (Fig. S32). The IRL contains numerous cracks, which should be related to the stress generated by the volume expansion/contraction of μ-Si negative electrode during charge/discharge cycles and the electrolyte decomposition. The SAED shows that the IRL is not the completely decomposed electrolyte but containing some undecomposed LGPS electrolyte (Fig. S33). This volume increase due to electrolyte decomposition introduces the stress, leading to more cracks and disrupting the ion transport pathway (Fig. S34).

Cryo-HRTEM of the Si/LGPS IRL using low-dose electron beam imaging at low temperatures reveals the formation of highly crystalline Li2S featuring needle-shaped morphology dispersed in an amorphous matrix with a length over 140 nm and a width varying from 12.7 to 73.7 nm (Fig. 3e). The amorphous matrix is related to compounds of P and S as analyzed by cryo-STEM EELS (Fig. S35). The Li12Ge7 phase dispersed in the Li2S substrate is also observed in the cryo-HRTEM images. The Li12Ge7 and Li2S phases maintain a definite orientation relationship: i.e., (1¯11)[110]Li2S//(1¯24)[2¯5¯2]Li12Ge7 (Fig. S36a–d). Li9Ge4 phase is also observed, and it has definite orientation relationship with Li2S as well, i.e., (1¯11)[110]Li2S //(1¯05)[511]Li9Ge4 (Fig. S36e–h). The size of Li2S exceeding 100 nm may be related to its orientation relationship with Li-Ge. Additionally, as shown in the cryo-HRTEM image, dispersive Li3P nanocrystals with sizes of 10–20 nm are also observed in the IRL (Fig. S37). Note that Li3P and Li-Ge are electronically conductive35,36, rendering the interfacial reaction sustainable (Fig. S38), leading to continuous interfacial reaction (Figs. S39–41) and continuous electrolyte decomposition. The persistent growth of the IRL results in constant lithium loss and finally causes the capacity loss in the ASSB.

The IRL of the ASSBLSPSC full cell was also analyzed for comparison. Note that the thin IRL was not visible in the SEM image (Fig. 4a). In the full cell with an LSPSC electrolyte, after 100 cycles, no apparent IRL is observed between Si and the SSE, and both the μ-Si negative electrode and the LSPSC terminate abruptly at the interface, suggesting that the Si/LSPSC interface is stable. The CBS images and its corresponding EDS line scans at the interface exhibit a continuous decrease of Si and a gradual increase of S/P/Cl contents from the Si/LSPSC interface to the Si interior (Fig. 4b). XPS of the Si/LSPSC interface after 100 cycles shows the additional Li2S double peaks at 161.6 eV and 160.5 eV besides the existing peaks at 163 eV and 161.7 eV (Fig. 4c). The peak positions of P and Cl remain unchanged (Fig. 4d, e). It indicates the formation of a Li2S based SEI at the Si/LSPSC interface, which may contribute to stable cycling of the Si|LSPSC | NMC811 ASSB. Unlike the IRL at the Si/LGPS interface, less decomposition products were observed in the IRL at the Si/LSPSC interface.

To gain further understanding to the Si/LSPSC interface, cryo-FIB-TEM was also employed to characterize the Si/LSPSC interface before and after cycling (Figs. S42–44). The LSPSC electrolyte could be stably imaged at 100 e-/Å2 and became amorphous at 300 e-/Å2, with noticeable deformation after irradiation damage (Fig. S45). And the LSPSC/IRL sample exhibites significantly higher electron beam tolerance than the LGPS/IRL sample. At 1100 e-/Å2, LSPSC fully amorphized, while the IRL remained stable (Fig. S46). As shown in the HRTEM of the Si/LSPSC interface which was obtained at 80 e-/Å2, the absence of an interfacial layer between the pristine Si and the LSPSC electrolyte demonstrates the chemical stability between Si and the sulfide electrolyte, which is crucial for the stable operation of the μ-Si negative electrode in sulfide-based ASSBs (Fig. S47). And after electrochemical cycle, the Si/LSPSC interface is sharp and rough with the μ-Si negative electrode exhibiting brighter contrast while the LSPSC displaying darker contrast. No distinct IRL is observed at the Si/LSPSC interface in low magnification (Fig. S48). By analyzing the Li K-edge spectra at different region, we observes the formation of a uniform IRL with a thickness of 100–200 nm on the surface of LSPSC (Fig. 4f). The colored circles correspond to the variations of the Li K-edge, P L-edge, and S L-edge intensity from the SSE to the μ-Si negative electrode (Fig. 4g). By comparing the peak positions to the standard spectra of LSPSC, Li2S, and μ-Si negative electrode (Fig. 4h), it is found that the IRL comprised of mainly Li2S at the LSPSC surface during the cycling of the Si|LSPSC | NMC811 batteries.

The microstructure of the Si/LSPSC interphase layer in the ASSB after 100 cycles was investigated by cryo-TEM. An IRL of 150 nm between Si and LSPSC was observed through bright-field (BF) image (Fig. 5a). SAED and FFT analysis indicate that the μ-Si negative electrode maintained good crystallinity after 100 cycles (Fig. 5b, Fig. S49). The SAED of the IRL corresponds to Li2S (Fig. 5c), and the LSPSC also maintains the integrity of its lattice structure (Fig. 5d). The uniform and thin IRL prevents further interfacial reactions and stabilizes the Si/LSPSC interface. To investigate how Li2S, which is an ionic conductor but electronic insulator37, affects interface evolution, cryo-HRTEM at low dose was conducted for atomic-scale characterization of the Si/LSPSC interface (Fig. 5e). It reveals an IRL consisting of Li2S nanocrystals dispersed in an amorphous matrix with sizes of 10–20 nm (Fig. 5f, Fig. S50), as being confirmed by the corresponding FFT (Fig. 5g). The amorphous matrix is related to compounds of P and Cl as analyzed by cryo-STEM EELS (Fig. S51). The Li2S rich IRL ensures the uniform insertion/extraction of Li ions between the μ-Si negative electrode and the LSPSC electrolyte, and prevents the further interfacial reaction between Li and LSPSC, thereby maintaining stable capacity of the ASSB over long cycles.

To directly demonstrate the interfacial reactions in the two sulfide SSEs, cryo-FIB processing was conducted for three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the Si/SSE interfaces. The spatial structural differences in the two Si/SSE interfaces during the charging/discharging processes are clearly visualized in the 3D reconstructed images. At the Si/LSPSC interface, no clear IRL at the mesoscale was observed (Fig. S52). For comparison, a thicker IRL with 10 μm is observed at the Si/LGPS interface (Fig. S52b). The differences in the IRL have direct impacts for the electrochemical performances of the two corresponding ASSBs. Unstable interphase layer arising from continuous chemically/electrochemically interfacial reaction in Si/LGPS interface led to continuous capacity decay over time (Figs. S53, S54), while the stable IRL at the Si/LSPSC interface renders stable electrochemical cycling of the corresponding ASSBs. And this unstable negative electrode/electrolyte interface reaction will be magnified during electrochemical cycling after the introduction of electrolyte into μ-Si negative electrode. This demonstrates the importance of a pure μ-Si negative electrode for the stable operation of sulfide electrolytes (Figs. S55, S56). Based on this knowledge, a LSPSC interlayer could be introduced between μ-Si negative electrode and LGPS to construct a stable interface (Fig. S57). The LSPSC interlayer inhibits the LGPS decomposition and the IRL formation in LGPS (Figs. S58, S59). Consequently, a stable cycling of the Si|LGPS | NMC811 ASSB (Fig. S60) is achieved.

Based on the cryo-TEM characterizations, some profound insights are obtained (Fig. 6a–j). The main decomposition product of both the ASSBLGPS and ASSBLSPSC is Li2S. However, there are differences between the two in terms of IRL thickness, morphology, and the microstructure of the decomposition product. The Si/LGPS interface features an IRL with a thickness 10 μm containing undecomposed LGPS electrolyte, Li3P nanocrystals of 10–20 nm, Li-Ge and Li2S nanocrystals with definite orientation relationship, and the IRL has many cracks. In contrast, the Si/LSPSC interface features an IRL with only 150 nm thick consisting of 10–20 nm Li2S nanocrystals dispersed in an amorphous matrix. The thin layer of dense Li2S nanocrystals block electron transport, thereby suppressing further interfacial reactions at the Si/LSPCL interface.

Based on the interfacial layer thickness and the reaction products, the amount of lithium consumed in the IRL can be quantitatively estimated. The molar lithium-ion required to form a 10 μm thick Li2S layer is 285.5*10-7 mol, which is significantly higher than the capacity loss of the ASSBLGPS calculated based on the capacity-voltage profile (Fig S1b, 67.1 × 10−7 mol), suggesting that a significant amount of non-reacted LGPS in the IRL. In contrast, the 150 nm thick Li2S layer in the IRL of the Si/LSPSC interface consumes 4.3 × 10−7 mol lithium-ion, and the molar amount of lithium-ion loss calculated through coulombic efficiency is 23.1 × 10−7 mol. This indicates that 18.6% of lithium-ion was consumed in the formation of IRL, while the remaining 81.4% capacity loss may be attributed to the formation of dead lithium in the μ-Si negative electrode and the trace amounts of irreversible lithiation of the ultra-thin oxide layer on μ-Si negative electrode surface (Figs. S61, S62). The uniform 150 nm thick IRL consisting of densely packed Li2S nanocrystals (Fig. 6h–j) effectively suppresses further interfacial reactions, reducing the loss of lithium-ion at the Si/LSPSC interface, thus ensuring the stable capacity retention of the ASSB.

In summary, the degradation mechanism of the μ-Si/sulfide SSE interface in ASSBs is unveiled through cryo-TEM. It is observed that the Si/LSPSC interphase layer of ASSBLSPSC is comprised of nanoscale Li2S dispersed in an amorphous matrix, forming a uniform and dense 150 nm thick IRL. In contrast, the Si/LGPS interphase layer of ASSBLGPS is an uneven, crack-filled 10 μm thick IRL, consisting of predominantly needle shaped Li2S with minor Li-Ge alloy/Li3P nanocrystals, and undecomposed LGPS debris. The thick IRL at the Si/LGPS interface do not cause significant impedance increase, suggesting that interface impedance is not the dominant factor that caused capacity decay in the ASSBLGPS. Instead, it is the formation of Li2S in the IRL that depletes the lithium source, thus causing capacity decrease. Noteworthy Si does not react with either LSPSC or LGPS. This study challenges the traditional view that interfacial impedance is the main reason for battery failure in ASSBs, which provide important implications to design stable interfaces and boost the performance of ASSBs.

Methods

All-solid-state battery assembly

Micron-sized silicon (μ-Si) particles (Aladdin, 99.9%) were used as the negative electrode without electrolyte, NCM was used as the positive electrode with electrolyte powder in a weight ratio of 7:3 using an agate and grind for half an hour to ensure uniformly in a glove box, positive electrode with LGPS (99%, 5–10 μm diameter, MTI China) and LSPSC (99%, 5–10 μm diameter, GLESI China) as the two different electrolyte interlayers. During solid-state battery testing, we compressed 100 mg of electrolyte into a Polyether Ether Ketone (PEEK) tube and maintained it under a pressure of 120 MPa and maintained it for 2 min, and obtained a pellet with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 1 mm. The negative electrode consists of 2 mg of pure micrometer-sized Si compressed at 240 MPa pressure for 5 min, while the positive electrode, made up of a mixture of 20 mg NMC811 and LSPSC electrolyte, was compressed at 720 MPa pressure for 5 min (Fig. S63). The N/P ratio was 2.5 (calculated based on the theoretical capacity of 3500 mAh/g about μ-Si negative electrode) (Fig. S63). All the components were compressed together at 460 MPa for electrochemical testing. In Li symmetric cells and LiSi symmetric cells, 100 mg of electrolyte was compressed and molded at 720 MPa for 5 min. Subsequently, lithium metal (99.9%, MTI China) or LiSi was added to both sides of the electrolyte, and assembly was completed under 5 MPa and 25 MPa. During electrochemical testing, stack pressures of 5 MPa and 25 MPa were applied to the Li and LiSi symmetric cells, respectively. The LiSi alloy was formed by lithium metal and micron-sized Si particles in a muffle furnace at 800 °C for 2 h in glove box.

Electrochemical testing

The Si|SSE | NMC811 ASSB was operated within the voltage range from 2.6 V to 4.3 V on a battery testing system (CT3001AU, LANHE, China) in a constant temperature chamber at 25 °C. All the cells for electrochemical characterization were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box with the H2O and O2 contents below 0.01 ppm. The in-situ potentiostatic EIS measurements of the Si|SSE | NMC811 ASSBs were carried out by using the Princeton P4000 analyzer from 1 Hz to 1 MHz with an amplitude of 40 mV at 25 °C, and the ex-situ potentiostatic EIS measurements were also conducted at different discharge/charge states with an amplitude of 10 mV and frequencies ranging from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz at 25 °C. And the open-circuit voltage time applied before carrying out the EIS measurement is 30 min and each EIS spectrum consists of 61 data point. For the DRT analysis of interfacial impedance, we employed the open-source MATLAB code shared online by Professor Francesco Giucci38. Based on DRT analysis, the EIS spectra of the battery are decoupled into electrochemical/physical processes with different time constants, enabling the interpretation of various kinetic processes.

Electron microscopy

The lamella for electron microscopy study was prepared by Focused Ion Beam (FIB). All the ex-situ samples for Cryo-FIB and Cryo-TEM characterization were taken from the cell in the delithiated state of Si. After cycling, the all-solid-state cell was removed from the mold in an Ar-filled glove box (H2O and O2 contents both below 0.01 ppm), and transferred into the FIB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Helios G4 CX) instrument using a sealed specimen transfer box (Zeptool, Cryo-T) to prevent air exposure. From the all-solid-state cell, a lamella was prepared by the standard FIB lift-out procedure, and then thinned using the 30 kV ion beams with the currents of 2.5, 0.79, and 0.23 nA sequentially to electron transparency. Afterwards, an 8 kV/68 pA beam and subsequently a 5 kV/41 pA beam were applied to remove its surface amorphous layer. To avoid damage caused by ion bombardment, these procedures were conducted with the specimen cooled to −170 °C using a cryo-stage as have done previously. The specimen prepared this way was transferred to a transmission electron microscope operated at 300 kV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Titan ETEM G2) using a cryo-transfer holder (Fischione, MODEL 2550), which ensured the absence of air exposure and a temperature of −170 °C during specimen transfer. To suppress the electron-beam irradiation damage, the TEM and STEM observation was performed at −170 °C as well. The EELS measurements were conducted in the STEM mode using a Gatan Image Filter Quantum-965 spectrometer with a 5 mm aperture and energy dispersion of 0.1 eV per channel. All steps of sample transfer and storage after battery disassembly are carried out in an argon atmosphere and liquid nitrogen to prevent the sample from reacting with air.
