Impact of COVID-19 among women seeking abortion services in Nepal: results from a longitudinal study
Authors: Anupama Ale Magar, Mahesh C. Puri, Sunita Karki, Dev Chandra Maharjan, Rachel Murro, Diana Greene Foster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has significantly impacted health and well-being worldwide, but limited evidence exists on its effects on women seeking reproductive health services. This paper examined the reported prevalence of COVID-19 signs/symptoms among the women who sought abortion services and the impact pandemic had on women who either received or were denied abortion services over a 3.5-year period in Nepal.MethodsThis paper draws on data from a longitudinal study involving 1,832 women who sought abortions at 22 health facilities across Nepal between 2019 and 2020. Participants were interviewed at the time of enrollment, then again at 6 weeks, and every 6 months over a three- and a half-year period. For the analysis, data from 1,739 women were used to examine trends in the prevalence of COVID-19 symptoms and assess the broader impact of the pandemic. To evaluate the effects of COVID-19 pandemic over time, the study period was divided into 6-month intervals. Both bivariate analyses and multivariate regressions were employed to assess the pandemic’s impact on these women, considering overall trends as well as differences between those who received abortions and those who were denied.ResultsApproximately three-quarters of the respondents reported some impact of COVID-19 pandemic between March and August 2020. Following another peak in March-August 2021 (71% reporting impacts), experience of pandemic impacts decreased steadily to 26% by the end of the study period (Mar-Jun 2023). Economic disruptions related to work or business were the most common impact reported followed by impacts on children’s education and the inability to access or afford basic food or necessities After adjusting for baseline differences, women who gave birth after being denied an abortion were more affected by the pandemic when it came to affording daily necessities (OR = 1.78, p < .01) and accessing reproductive (OR = 2.59, p < .01) and non-reproductive health services (OR = 3.07, p < .01).ConclusionThe study highlights the increased vulnerability of pregnant women, particularly those seeking abortion care, during the COVID-19 pandemic. The findings emphasize the critical importance of maintaining access to essential reproductive health services, such as abortion, especially during disease outbreaks and humanitarian crises. Ensuring the continuity of these services should be a top priority, and health systems must be strengthened to deliver them without disruption in future emergencies.

Context

COVID-19 was first identified in Wuhan city, China, in late December 2019 and later became a worldwide pandemic affecting more than 200 countries with a total of 777,504,802 confirmed cases including 7,089,979 deaths until February, 2025 [1, 2]. To limit the spread of the virus and reduce the negative health outcomes of the disease, various policy measures were adopted at the global level that limited travel, imposed quarantines and lockdowns, and shuttered businesses and schools impacting access to health institutions, education and employment [3–7]. Globally, over 40% of countries, mainly low- and middle-income, faced major disruptions in quality health services. More than one quarter of countries reported interruptions in facility-based births, safe abortion, and post-abortion care services, as well as intimate partner and sexual violence prevention and response services [4].

Nepal experienced its first case in January 2020. A nationwide lockdown from March to July 2020 prohibited domestic and international travel, closed borders, and barred nonessential services [8]. The first wave peaked in late October 2020, with 5,743 cases per day and a case death rate less than 1%. From January 2021, vaccination against COVID-19 was rolled out, initially prioritizing frontline healthcare workers and then other populations [9]. The Government of Nepal (GoN) strengthened health infrastructures, including upgrading health facilities, training medical personnel and improving COVID-19 screening for case detection and management [9, 10]. The second wave in mid-2021, driven by delta variant had significant impact —overwhelming hospitals, causing shortages of oxygen and essential supplies. In response, the GoN imposed a second phase of lockdown between April and September 2021 [9, 11]. The onset of a third wave of the COVID-19 pandemic (omicron variant) in Nepal peaked in early 2022, spread rapidly but was less fatal [9]. Although the vaccine coverage has improved, by mid-2023 the cumulative toll included over than 1 million COVID-19 cases and 12,000 deaths across all three waves [9, 12].

Nepal’s fragile and under-resourced health care system may have contributed to the detrimental impact of the pandemic on the country’s health and economy [11]. The International Labor Organization estimated that as a result of the pandemic, there was disruption in 1.6 to 2.0 million jobs, predominantly among workers in the informal and service sectors [13]. Women were disproportionately affected, as they have high representation in such sectors [14]. Employment among women declined by 5% compared to 3.9% among men [3, 15]. However, the impact on women was not limited to the economy. There was an increase in the number of reported cases of maternal deaths due to restricted antenatal care services because of fear of infection and lockdown in Nepal [13]. The limited functioning of health facilities and outreach services, the unavailability of health staff, the unavailability of preferred choices of contraception as well as limited physical infrastructures and other health commodities, and the difficulty in traveling and visiting health facilities due to concerns about COVID-19 exposure were major barriers to maternal health services, including abortion care [16]. A multi-country study which included Nepal revealed that 27–44% of women surveyed in each country indicated that the pandemic influenced their decision to delay or avoid pregnancy [16, 17].

Several other factors may have exacerbated the impact on women’s access to health services, including inadequate knowledge about COVID-19, low prioritization of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, limited autonomy within families, financial constraints due to loss of jobs, and social stigma. Also, during lockdown, spending more time at home with family limited women’s privacy and autonomy. They often needed their partner’s permission to seek services, and their visits to health facilities were often questioned by other household members . These all factors collectively hindered women’s ability to seek and receive essential healthcare, particularly during the pandemic [16, 18]. One example is the Marie Stopes health facility in Nepal, where the average number of women accessing abortion care decreased by 37%, compared with pre-lockdown; and as a result, many were forced either to continue an unintended pregnancy or to seek medical abortion from outside the formal regulated system [19]. However, the government endorsed COVID-19 guidelines which allowed home-based medical abortion through an outreach model and telemedicine to increase access to abortion services during the pandemic period [20]. Moreover, there was a significant increase in mental health issues, such as depression, anxiety, stress, insomnia, and suicide, as well as domestic violence, sexual abuse, and rape, during the lockdown period, especially among women [8]. Although, in recent years, COVID-19 cases have been minimal and the lockdown situation has eased, its effects are still evident. Women are considered to be more vulnerable than men due to their lower social and economic status [21]. Studies have shown that COVID-19 has significantly impacted working women as well as vulnerable women, such as migrants, refugees, and pregnant women, indicating that they are at high risk of being victimized from violence, poverty/financial stress, increasing care burdens, food insecurity, school dropout and job loss [21–23]. Among these groups, pregnant women seeking abortion are particularly vulnerable, as they are more likely to belong to low-income groups, have a history of abuse, and have higher chances of seeking illegal or unsafe termination alternatives when they are denied abortion [24–26]. In the context of a pandemic, women who sought abortion and were denied for it, faced unique and intensified challenges. Carrying an unwanted pregnancy to term often results in significant health risks, medical cost, economic hardship, and long-term caregiving responsibilities, all of which may be amplified during public health emergencies like COVID-19 [27]. These women may require additional prenatal care, be more likely to experience violence and remain with an unsupportive or abusive partner, or experience job loss and food insecurity due to caregiving burdens [27, 28]. In contrast, those who successfully accessed abortion care may have had greater autonomy to manage their health and economic decisions during the crisis [29]. These divergent experiences are likely to manifest not only at the time of abortion denial, but also across time, especially during the postpartum period when physical, emotional, and economic demands increases [30]. In Nepal, despite legal provisions for abortion, barriers such as gestational limits and lack of awareness continue to impede access to services [20, 31].

There is a lack of empirical evidence concerning the impact of the pandemic on women who sought abortion care, especially in lower- and middle-income countries such as Nepal. Given that individuals who access or are denied abortion services are often shaped by distinct characteristics and face different consequences depending on the abortion outcome, their experiences with COVID-19 infection may also differ. Thus, the aim of this paper was to explore the impact of COVID-19 pandemic among women who were seeking abortion care in 22 health facilities across Nepal, comparing among those who have either received or denied abortion services over a 40-month follow-up period. Additionally, we further assessed and compared the reported prevalence of COVID-19 infection over time to capture potential differences by abortion group.

Data and methods

Data

The data for this paper came from a longitudinal study of 1,832 women (aged 15–45 years) who were enrolled in the study from 22 health facilities across Nepal at the time they were seeking abortion services between April 2019 and December 2020. In this study, abortion-seeking women are the individuals who presented at a participating facility explicitly seeking to terminate a pregnancy, regardless of whether they ultimately received or were denied abortion services.

All women and girls presenting for abortion care were screened for study eligibility by a point person at the facility. These point persons—doctors, nurses, counselors, or receptionists—completed an eligibility form for every woman seeking an abortion over the entire study period. The form recorded the woman’s age, estimated gestation, provider assessment of eligibility for abortion, and reason for ineligibility, if relevant. If a woman was eligible for the study and willing to participate in the study, the point person at the facility referred her to speak with trained female research staff who was stationed in a private room/corner at each clinic. The research staff confirmed study eligibility, obtained written informed consent (for women unable to sign, a thumbprint was obtained), conducted the baseline survey in the clinic via Qualtrics software (tablet-based), and uploaded it to a secure web-based storage platform.

The interviews were conducted to ensure the respondents’ confidentiality, and the data were anonymized to protect the identities of the participants. In the case of minors under 18 years of age, consent from one biological parent and assent from the girl were obtained, ensuring their understanding of the study’s purpose and potential risks. The follow-up interviews were conducted after six weeks and subsequently every six months for three and half years either at health facilities or other locations where the participants preferred. Interviewer conducted the surveys in the Nepali, Maithili, Tharu, or Bhojpuri languages. Each participant received financial compensation equivalent to approximately $4 USD for the baseline and each subsequent interview. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the University of California, San Francisco, and Nepal Health Research Council.

Although the main aim of the parent study was to identify predictors for the denial of abortion and its consequences for women’s health and their children, we collected data on the experience of COVID-19 symptoms and theimpact pandemic had on women and their families when it started in Nepal. We collected COVID-19-related information between March 2020 and June 2023, and all the participants interviewed during that time period were asked about (i) symptoms of COVID-19 infection in their household and (ii) the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on their lives. As only participants who were interviewed during this period were asked about COVID-19-related experiences, the analytic sample for this paper is therefore limited to those respondents.

Measures

In this paper, we measured reported signs/symptoms of COVID-19 infection and the broader impact of COVID-19 pandemic on women who sought abortion services and their families. Respondents were asked “Whether you or anyone in your family has symptoms of COVID-19?” and “How has the disease impacted your family?” The reported impacts were coded and categorized: impact on work/business, food affordability, availability of goods, access to health services, child health and education, travel difficulties, emotional problems, and relationship problems.

At each enrollment site, women who sought abortion care either received or were denied care and were classified accordingly. The “denied and gave birth” group includes all women who were unable to obtain an abortion and ultimately carried out the pregnancy to term for any reason—provider refusal, personal reasons, or facility constraints. The “received abortion” group includes those who received their abortion, including women who were initially denied an abortion but subsequently received an abortion elsewhere or at a later date. It also includes a very small number of women who were denied an abortion but later reported having a miscarriage.

The demographic variables include age in years (< 24,25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40+), marital status (married, never married, divorced/separated/widow), employment status, type of family (nuclear, joint), living arrangement (with or without husband), number of children (0, 1, 2, 3 or more), ethnicity (Brahmin/Chhetri/Thakuri, Hill Janajati, Terai Janajati, Dalit, Others) and year of schooling (No/some formal schooling, Primary, Secondary, Higher). Autonomy was assessed on the basis of mobility and decision-making capabilities. Mobility categorized as limited and standard mobility was determined by using the physical immobility index [32], which measures whether the respondent had independently visited any of the specific location in the past year, such as the market, hospital/clinic, or outside the village. Standard mobility was defined as having gone to at least one of these places on their own, whereas limited mobility was defined as not having visited any of these places on their own. Decision-making was assessed based on that focused on two types of decision: healthcare seeking, and major purchases [33]. Based on responses, decision-making was categorized into no involvement, involved in one type (buying or health), or involved in both types (buying and health). The wealth quintile was calculated on the basis of the ownership of various assets and housing characteristics and then categorized into five categories, i.e., ‘Lowest’, ‘Lower’, ‘Middle’, ‘Higher’, and ‘Highest’. Income sufficiency was self-reported and categorized as ‘not adequate’, ‘adequate/more than adequate’, and ‘Don’t know.’ Desire for additional children was also recorded with responses coded as ‘Yes’, ‘No’ and ‘Unsure’.

Analysis

Of the 1832 participants, 93 were lost to follow-up prior to March 2020, so they did not have any COVID-19 data and were excluded from this analysis. Hence, a total of 1739 women and 10,473 data points (average of 6 surveys per woman) were included in the analysis, with a retention rate of 94%. Sociodemographic variables were described for the full sample, and by the reported impact of COVID-19 from March 2020 to June 2023; differences in COVID-19 impacts across sociodemographic variables were tested via Pearson’s chi-square test. To analyze trends over time and account for temporal changes in the experience of COVID-19 symptoms and their impact, the full observation period was divided into six-month intervals. Self-reported experiences of COVID-19 signs/symptoms were assessed in each period along with the impact of pandemic on them and their families (overall and by abortion group). Calendar year dates were used as the time indicator, rather than visit number or time since baseline, to capture the effects of the COVID-19 waves in Nepal.

Finally, we ran a series of multivariate logistic regressions to assess the independent associations between abortion groups and self-reported COVID-19 pandemic impacts at any time during follow-up. All multivariate models included the following characteristics measured at baseline: age, marital status, education, employment status, caste, household income adequacy, household wealth quintile, living situation (with or without husband; nuclear vs. joint), fertility intentions, empowerment, and parity, and included random intercepts to reflect clustering by recruitment facility. These variables were selected as likely predictors of abortion denial on the basis of previous studies [24, 25, 34] and on the expert knowledge of the study team.

Results

Characteristics of the participants, overall and by Covid impacts

Between March 2020 and June 2023, a total of 1,739 abortion-seeking women were surveyed regarding their experiences with COVID-19 symptoms and the pandemic’s broader effects. Demographically, most women were under 30 (62%), married (97%), cohabitating with spouses (83%), and had secondary level of education (61%). The majority had children (87%), with over 60% not wanting more children. Economically, 54% earned a cash income, nearly three-quarters reported adequate household finances. 24% had limited mobility, and 53% were not involved in household decision-making (Table 1).

Statistical analysis revealed significant associations between the perceived impact of the pandemic and several demographic and socioeconomic factors. Women with children were more likely to report impacts than their nulliparous counterparts. Impacts were most commonly reported among those with higher education and least commonly reported among those with primary education. In terms of ethnicity, Brahmin/Chettri/Thakuri and Terai Janajati women more often reported impacts, compared to than Dalit and Hill Janajati participants.

Experience of symptoms of COVID-19

Throughout the study period, 78% of respondents did not report any personal or family COVID-19 symptoms during the study period, 25% experienced symptoms at least once. The experience of COVID-19 symptoms in participants or their families was recorded in 10,473 surveys (participants were asked about in each follow-up interview). COVID-19 symptoms were reported 800 times throughout the study period, with slightly greater reporting across most time points among women receiving abortion than those who were denied. During the first wave of COVID-19 from March to August 2020, 0.7% of the respondents reported the presence of COVID-19 symptoms, which increased and reached 13.5% in the time period of September 2021-February 2022 and then decreased to 8.25% in the time period of March-June 2023 (Fig. 1).

Impact of COVID-19 pandemic

Overall, 93% of the respondents reported having some type of impact of COVID-19 pandemic on them or their families over the course of the study. Reporting of any impact decreased from 76% between March and August 2020 to 26% between March and June 2023, with a notable additional, peak in March-August 2021 (71%). Throughout most of the study period, the most mentioned effect of the pandemic was its impact on work or business (ranging from 22 to 49% across time periods), followed by impacts on children’s education (ranging from 16 to 43%). These two impacts followed similar trends – peaking in March-August 2021 and then steadily decreasing throughout the rest of the study period (though there was also an early peak in March-August 2020 for work/business impacts. Two other commonly reported impacts – difficulty accessing goods and difficulty affording goods – also experienced a peak in reporting in March-August 2021 (32% and 27%, respectively) and then decreased towards the end of data collection. Report of travel difficulties within countries and from abroad increased steadily over most of the study period, from less than 10% reporting in March-August 2020 to over 30% in September 2022 to February 2023. Reports dipped by the last stage of data collection; the proportion reporting travel challenges declined to 20% between March and June 2023 (Table 2).

Difference in COVID-19 pandemic impact by receiving and denial of abortion

While at the start of the study period (March-August 2020) both abortion groups tended to report COVID-19 pandemic impacts at similar rates (75–76%), this prevalence decreased to 28% in the receiving group and 17% in the denial group after three years (March-June 2023). The most common pandemic impact, i.e., on work or business, was found to be similarly reported by abortion group until March-August 2022 when it became more commonly reported by those who received their abortion; this mirrored the trend observed for report of any impact. Similar trends were also observed in terms of the accessibility/availability of daily consumption although those who were denied their abortion more commonly reported these impacts during March-August 2021. This increased reporting of impacts by the abortion denial group compared to those who received their abortion in March-August 2021 was also observed for difficulty affording food and other necessities. Child education impacts were consistently more commonly reported by those who received their abortion. While child health impacts were consistently reported by both groups at all time points aside from March-August 2021, when there was a spike in reporting for those who were denied their abortion. Difficulties accessing reproductive and non-reproductive health services were found to be greater for the women who were denied abortion than for those who received such services throughout the study period. Difficulties in traveling within the country and from abroad were more commonly cited among women who underwent abortions throughout the second half of the study period. (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides a longitudinal assessment of the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, including health and socio-economic impacts, among women who sought abortion care in Nepal. We examined self-reported experience of COVID-19 symptoms to capture potential differences based on whether the woman received or was denied an abortion. The study results revealed that the reporting of COVID-19 symptoms was relatively greater among women who had received an abortion than among those who had denied and given birth. This difference may be because women who have received an abortion are more likely to be out of the home, continue with their job or school, etc. [27], which increases their exposure to COVID-19. Further, we found the impact of the pandemic varied by ethnicity, education level, and number of living children.

A study conducted in Nepal on the impact of COVID-19 on abortion care revealed that women with lower levels of education were disproportionately affected, aligning to some extent our finding that those with no formal schooling were more likely than those with primary or secondary education to report impacts [19]. In contrast, our results also showed that highly educated women more often reported pandemic impacts than their counterparts who were less educated possibly due to their higher likelihood of having greater mobility, work responsibilities, and public exposure. Also, women engaged in cash-earning employment more often perceived household-level impacts. However, no statistically significant associations were found between reported impacts and either employment status or mobility [23, 35–37]. Similarly, women with more children faced more impacts, likely due to added responsibility of childcare and burden of providing adequate resources aligning to the findings of previous studies [23, 36]. While earlier studies have shown a disproportionate impact of COVID-19 on Dalit groups, our findings indicated that more advantaged groups more commonly reported impacts [23, 38, 39]. Dalit and disadvantaged groups have historically faced similar hardships even without a pandemic, whereas more advantaged groups may have encountered a greater increase in challenges due to COVID-19, resulting in greater self-reported impacts. This may also be due to different interpretations of “hardship” by different ethnic groups. This underscores the need for targeted interventions that consider the unique challenges by these groups.

In addition to the direct threat to physical health, the COVID-19 pandemic has had large impacts on people’s daily and social lives worldwide [40]. Economic impact, specifically the inability to go to work or business, was the most common impact of COVID-19 reported in the initial and subsequent follow-up periods, which is consistent with other studies [41, 42]. National lockdown as a response to COVID-19 has been one of the major reasons behind such impacts [41], reflected in the early peaks of reported covid impacts we observed in March-August 2020 and March-August 2021. A study conducted in Atlanta, Georgia, reported that nearly half of the participants had trouble accessing health care during the COVID-19 pandemic, either for themselves or their children [43]. This type of impact was also frequently reported in the present study. Additionally, other impacts, such as travel restrictions, reduced access to abortion services, long delays in obtaining appointments, and overcrowding of hospitals, have also been reported in other similar studies [16, 44, 45].

Overall reporting of impacts of COVID-19 pandemic decreased over time, both for those who received their abortion and those who did not, but women denied abortion reported greater hardships than those who received one. However, compared to those who received their abortion, women who were denied and gave birth experienced greater peaks in several key pandemic impacts around March-August 2021: inability to afford food and other necessities, inaccessibility of basic necessities, and adult and child health. Struggles in affording food and other necessities may be due to an increase in household size following unwanted birth, resulting in increased poverty [27]. For women who were denied abortions, births would typically have occurred approximately 7–9 months after the time of recruitment, assuming they remained pregnant. Recruitment for this study occurred continuously between April 2019 and December 2020, meaning these births would have occurred between late 2019 through mid/late 2021, This timing aligns with the observed increase in reported hardship among these groups until 2022, suggesting that the addition of a new child to the household may have contributed to financial and emotional strain. Financial hardship is a key reasons for seeking abortion [27], and carrying unwanted pregnancies to term during the pandemic increased the economic burden on women and their families. Additionally, unwanted births result in more lost workdays and reduced labor productivity compared to those receiving an abortion, ultimately lowering the standards of living [46, 47]. Previous work has also shown that unwanted births can reduce parents ability to care for existing children, lead to worse socioeconomic outcomes and increase risk of living in poverty with insufficient resources to cover food, housing, and transportation [48].

Women who gave birth faced greater difficulties accessing reproductive and non-reproductive health services than those who received abortion and those who gave birth required a broader spectrum of care, including routine checkups, mental health support, and long-term maternal health care [49]. Meeting these needs is even more difficult during a pandemic. Similar to the findings of our study, multiple studies reported the impact of COVID-19 on reproductive health (RH), citing barriers in seeking, reaching and receiving RH services, including abortion [50, 51]. Studies find that women are anxious, scared, confused due to different myths, and misconception during the pandemic, leading to decreased health-seeking behavior, and lower utilization of health facilities for preconception, contraception and family planning counseling [16]. In addition, the cessation of local transportation, limited availability of ambulance services, and geographical remoteness had been additional barriers to reach health facilities [45]. Reduced office hours at health care facilities, restricted access to information related to RH, and interruption of access to abortion care provided by a large network of private clinics in Nepal were the major barriers to receiving RH services [16, 18, 52]. Apart from reproductive health care, difficulty in accessing non-reproductive care was also noted by abortion-seekers in our study. Some studies have shown that, during the pandemic, non-communicable disease patients, such as those with cancer, those with diabetes faced difficulties accessing long-term care and medicines [39]. Before the pandemic, access to care for such patients was suboptimal, and infrastructure barriers made this worse, disrupting regular services such as screening, investigative tests, etc [41, 53]. Additionally, regular immunization, vitamin A, and deworming tablets were also missed, as the government postponed the national level regular program during the pandemic period [8, 54, 55]. Infrastructure gap, digital illiteracy, and limited internet access further hinder the adoption of telehealth [41, 45]. Health care workers were redirected to the pandemic response, limiting the service availability of both reproductive and non-reproductive services [45]. Moreover, limited access to essential health care services, including abortion, during the pandemic had significant negative impacts of women’s health and wellbeing.

This is one of the first longitudinal studies in Nepal to follow women who sought abortion care, examining long term impacts of COVID-19 pandemic for those who received or were denied an abortion. These findings can be used by programs and policy makers to understand how the pandemic has affected women seeking abortion and prioritizing access to critical reproductive health services such as abortion during disease outbreaks and humanitarian crises. Uninterrupted access to abortion and related RH care should be ensured to not only for safeguarding reproductive rights and autonomy but also for mitigating the compounded impact of unwanted births and public health crises. Strengthening health system capacity as well as improving outreach and awareness about available services and addressing socio-economic barriers are crucial to reduce the disproportionate impact on economically vulnerable women.

This study has a few limitations. First, although analyses were stratified by six-month intervals, time was not adjusted for in the multivariate analysis, which may have influenced the observed associations. Second, we asked about reported symptoms of COVID-19 rather than confirmed diagnoses because testing was not widely available through all periods of the pandemic. We acknowledge that underreporting of both COVID-19 symptoms and pandemic impacts may be present and may have occurred differently across sociodemographic groups. Cultural, educational, and other social characteristics may influence whether individuals are able to identify COVID-19 symptoms, and whether they attribute negative experiences specifically to the pandemic or to other life circumstances. Lastly, the participants for this study were selected from public and NGO-run facilities, so the findings of this study cannot be generalized to women who sought abortion outside the health care system or at pharmacies or private clinics as well as women who were unable to seek abortion services at all.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant, varying impacts of COVID-19 pandemic on women who sought abortion care in Nepal. Ethnicity, education level, and number of living children were significantly associated with reporting of the impact of COVID-19 pandemic.

The pandemic has significantly impacted women who presented for abortion services, with different burdens placed on those who received abortion care versus those who were denied abortion care. Economic impacts, especially impacts on work or business, were consistently the most cited pandemic effects. Compared with those who received abortions, women who gave birth after the denial of abortion services were more likely to report certain COVID-19 related hardships, including difficulty accessing and affording daily necessities, reproductive, and non-reproductive health services. These study results reveal the heightened vulnerability of women who were denied abortions during the pandemic, stressing the need for accessible reproductive health services to mitigate disparities in such crisis situations. The continuation of critical reproductive health services such as abortion is even more important during disease outbreaks and humanitarian crises.
