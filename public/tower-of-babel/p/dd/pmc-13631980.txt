NR3C1 promotes group 4 medulloblastoma invasion via activating VCAN
Authors: Jing Wang, Zichang Zhang, Han Lin, Yantao Liu, Yifei Wang, Yu Peng, Ziwen Sun, Ruichao Chai, Tao Jiang, Qing Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately 30% of group 4 medulloblastomas (G4-MBs) present with metastasis at diagnosis, yet the molecular mechanisms remain unclear. To elucidate differences of genes’ expression between primary tumors of metastatic (M+) and non-metastatic (M0) G4-MB, we performed multi-omics profiling, including RNA sequencing, proteomics, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), and spatial transcriptomics on tumor samples. Integrative analyses identified VCAN, a chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, as the most significantly up-regulated gene in primary tumors of M+ G4-MB. High expression of this gene was correlated with poor patient prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that VCAN promotes proliferation and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis. In addition, NR3C1 was predicted as the key activator of VCAN by Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC). High-definition spatial transcriptomics revealed that NR3C1 and VCAN are highly co-expressed within the same spatial domains. Multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed the co-localization, providing spatial evidence of their regulatory interaction. ChIP-qPCR subsequently confirmed direct binding of NR3C1 to the VCAN promoter. Knockdown of NR3C1 or VCAN suppressed invasion and proliferation and induced apoptosis of the tumor cells, which were partially reversed by VCAN overexpression. Together, these findings revealed that the NR3C1–VCAN axis played a pivotal role in the metastatic progression of G4-MB, highlighting a potential therapeutic target for high-risk patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02374-z.Keywords: Group 4, Medulloblastoma, Metastasis, NR3C1, VCAN

Introduction

Medulloblastoma (MB) is the most common malignant pediatric brain tumor, which originates in the cerebellum [1–4]. A combination of surgery, craniospinal irradiation, and chemotherapy has significantly improved patient survival rates. However, the 5 - year survival rate for relapsed patients is only 36% [5–7]. Currently, MB is at least classified into four molecular subtypes: WNT, SHH, group 3 (G3), and group 4 (G4) [8–10]. G4-MB, the most prevalent subtype, constitutes approximately 40% of all MB cases [11, 12]. Around 30% of G4-MBs present with early metastasis at the time of diagnosis, which are classified as high-risk tumors [10, 13, 14]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the tumor metastasis remain poorly understood.

To uncover the mechanisms that drive G4-MB metastasis, we performed multi-omics analysis on primary tumor tissues from metastatic (M+) G4-MB versus non-metastatic (M0) G4-MB. Patient-derived cells (PDCs) from G4-MB patients were then applied for in vitro validation. In the present study, we identified VCAN, a chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, as one of the most significantly upregulated proteins and genes in metastatic tumors and its high expression was associated with poor prognosis of G4-MB patients. Functional assays confirmed that VCAN promotes tumor proliferation, invasion, and inhibiting apoptosis. Importantly, we discovered that the transcription factor NR3C1 directly binds to the VCAN promoter and drives its expression and functions. Furthermore, high-definition spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunofluorescence revealed NR3C1 and VCAN were highly co-expressed in the same spatial domains, which provided spatial evidence of their regulatory interaction. Functional assays revealed that the NR3C1–VCAN axis play a pivotal role in the metastatic progression of G4-MB.

Taken together, our work firstly uncovered a novel regulatory pathway underlying G4-MB metastasis. The identification of the NR3C1–VCAN axis provides new insights into the biology of G4-MB and highlights this axis as a promising therapeutic target for high-risk G4-MB patients.

Materials and methods

Tissue collection and patient data

Metastatic status was determined based on preoperative MRI evaluation. Cases with radiological evidence of metastatic lesions on preoperative imaging were classified as M+, whereas those without such evidence were defined as M0. To minimize misclassification, the M0 cohort excluded any patients who developed metastatic disease within 12 months after diagnosis; all included cases had a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Tumor molecular subgrouping was performed using the NanoString medulloblastoma subgrouping panel to identify Group 4 medulloblastoma (G4-MB) cases and exclude other molecular subgroups [15]. Paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary tumor tissues from ten metastatic (M+) and ten non-metastatic (M0) G4-MB cases were subjected to proteomic analysis. Fresh-frozen primary tumors from four M + and seven M0 G4-MB cases were used for RNA-seq; single nuclear RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) was run on frozen samples from four M + and four M0 G4-MB cases. Spatial transcriptomic profiling used FFPE primary tumor sections from three M + and three M0 G4-MB patients. The sample identifiers corresponding to each omics platform are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and detailed clinical information for all cases is summarized in Supplementary Table 2. All specimens were obtained from Beijing Tiantan Hospital (BTH) of Capital Medical University between March 2016 and January 2022. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (approval number KY2024-212-02) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptomics samples preparation, process and analysis

RNA sequencing was performed using the Illumina Hi-seq platform with a strand-specific mRNA sequencing approach. Total RNA was extracted from the samples and assessed for quality and integrity. Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads and cDNA was synthesized. The libraries were pooled in equimolar ratios and sequenced on the Illumina Hi-seq platform using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration according to the manufacturer’s standard protocols. The resulting raw reads were subjected to quality assessment using FastQC. The count matrix was generated by aligning the high-quality reads to the reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR aligner and quantifying the number of reads mapped to each gene feature with featureCounts. To identify significantly differentially expressed genes between primary tumors from M0 group and M+ group, the count data were analyzed using the DESeq2 package. Differential expression analysis identified significantly dysregulated genes (|Log2 fold change| > 0.585, adjusted p-value < 0.05). “clusterProfiler” R package was used to perform functional enrichment analysis, uncovering key biological processes, molecular functions, and pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes [16].

Proteomics samples preparation, process and analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary tumor tissues were collected from 20 patients with G4-MB (10 metastatic and 10 non-metastatic). Samples were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol, lysed and sonicated, incubated at 90 °C for 90 min, and digested with trypsin overnight at 37 °C; digestion was quenched with 1% TFA. Peptides were desalted using SDB desalting columns and lyophilized. For spectral library generation, pooled peptides were fractionated by high-pH reverse-phase fractionation and measured in DDA mode. LC–MS/MS was performed using mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile); 400 ng peptides were loaded onto a 25 cm C18 column (100 μm i.d., ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ 1.5 μm) and separated using a gradient from 3.5 to 32% B in 17 min, 32% to 95% B in 1 min, held at 95% B for 2 min, and re-equilibrated to 1% B in 2 min. MS data were acquired on a timsTOF_HT mass spectrometer (Bruker) with a Captive Spray ion source. Fractionated samples were acquired in DDA mode (m/z 100–1700; 10 PASEF cycles; total cycle time 1.1 s; accumulation time 100 ms), and single-shot samples were acquired in DIA mode (m/z 300–1500; total cycle time 1.23 s; accumulation time 50 ms). For data processing, DDA MS data from fractionated pools (6 fractions) and DIA MS data from single-shot samples were used to generate a DDA-library and a direct-DIA-library, respectively, which were merged into a hybrid spectral library in Spectronaut (Biognosys, v15.7.220308.50606); the hybrid library was then used for final protein identification and quantification of the single-shot DIA samples against the human UniProt reference proteome (canonical and isoform sequences). Searches used carbamidomethylation as a fixed modification and protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation as variable modifications, with trypsin/P allowing up to two missed cleavages (peptide length 7–52 aa); precursor and protein FDR were controlled at 1%, and protein intensities were normalized using Spectronaut “Local Normalization”. For downstream differential analysis, intensities were further median-normalized across common proteins, proteins with > 50% missing values were removed, and remaining missing values were imputed using the KNN algorithm. Differential abundance between M + and M0 groups was assessed using a two-sided t-test, and differentially expressed proteins were defined as those with fold change > 1.2 (or < 1/1.2 for downregulation) and a nominal p-value < 0.05.

Single-nuclear RNA sequencing preparation, process and analysis

A total of eight samples were included in the analysis, comprising four non-metastatic and four metastatic tumors. Nuclei were isolated from frozen tumor tissues by mechanical dissociation combined with hypotonic lysis buffer (e.g., NP-40 lysis buffer). Samples were centrifuged at 500×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting nuclear pellet was resuspended and washed twice with DPBS containing 0.04% BSA. Nuclear concentration was determined using a hemocytometer, and the suspension was adjusted to a final concentration of 700–1200 nuclei/µL in nuclear suspension buffer (DPBS supplemented with 1% BSA) to meet the optimal loading requirements of the 10x Chromium system, targeting the capture of 5000–10,000 nuclei per sample. Subsequently, nuclei were encapsulated together with gel beads and library reagents into nanoliter-scale droplets using the Chromium Controller microfluidic system, following the manufacturer’s instructions for the 10x Genomics Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1. Reverse transcription and cDNA amplification were performed, with 12–14 PCR cycles used for library amplification. Final libraries were quantified using Qubit fluorometry and assessed for quality using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, with acceptable libraries showing a dominant fragment size peak between 350 and 600 bp. High-throughput sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a 150-bp paired-end sequencing mode, with a target depth of at least 50,000 reads per nucleus. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (v3.1.0) for alignment and quantification against the GRCh38 reference genome. Summary statistics of snRNA-seq data quality, including retained nuclei, median UMIs, genes, and reads per nucleus, are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Nuclei with less than 1000 UMIs/nucleus or 500 genes/nucleus were removed from the analysis, according to pre-established best practices. Nuclei with > 5% of mitochondrial RNA were also excluded. Data normalization was performed using Seurat::NormalizeData() (Seurat v4.4.0) [17]. 3000 features with high variable expression across the nuclei were identified, and these were used in a subsequent Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using the Seurat::RunPCA() function. Prior to doublet removal, ambient RNA contamination was computationally corrected using DecontX, implemented within the celda R package, to minimize the confounding effects of background RNA on downstream analyses. DoubletFinder v3 was applied to detect and exclude putative doublets on a per-sample basis [18]. To remove biases arising from different samples, Harmony integration was performed on the PCA embedding [19]. For downstream analyses, we used the first 30 principal components to construct the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph and performed graph-based clustering with a resolution of 0.1 (Seurat::FindNeighbors and Seurat::FindClusters), followed by UMAP visualization (Seurat::RunUMAP). Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes/cluster markers was performed using clusterProfiler (v3.12.0).

Cell type annotation and single-nucleus regulatory network inference

Cell type annotation was conducted based on unsupervised clustering and the expression of canonical marker genes. Major cell populations were identified using established lineage-specific markers, including astrocytes (GFAP, AQP4, PLOD2), oligodendrocyte lineage cells (PLP1, MAG, MOG), neurons (SYT1, GRIK2, GABRB2), microglia cells (TMEM119, P2RY12, CX3CR1), macrophages (CD14, CD163, GPNMB), T cells (CD2, CD3D, CD3E), vascular cells (DCN, COL1A2, VWF). Tumor cells were identified after the annotation of major cell populations by using copy number variation (CNV) inference at the single-nucleus level using the inferCNVpy approach (v0.6.0). After classification of malignancy cells, Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) was performed to reconstruct gene regulatory networks (GRNs) and define stable cell states from snRNA-seq data. We employed pySCENIC (v0.10.3) with GRNBoost2 to infer co-expression modules between transcription factors (TFs) and candidate targets, RcisTarget to filter modules by cis-regulatory motif enrichment, and AUCell to score regulon activity in each cell [20].

Spatial transcriptomic profiling data preparation, process and analysis

Spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed using the 10x Genomics Visium HD platform to capture genome-wide gene expression data while preserving tissue spatial architecture. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from six samples of G4-MB were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol, mounted onto Visium HD slides, and processed within pre-defined capture areas containing billions of uniquely barcoded oligonucleotide probes.

Following tissue deparaffinization and optimized permeabilization, RNA molecules were released from the tissue and hybridized to spatially barcoded capture probes on the slide surface. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed in situ directly on the slide, incorporating spatial barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), after which cDNA was collected for downstream library construction following the standard Visium HD workflow. Sequencing libraries were generated and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using a paired-end configuration, with Read 1 capturing the spatial barcode and UMI information and Read 2 covering the cDNA insert corresponding to the transcript sequence. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Space Ranger pipeline (version 4.0; 10x Genomics) for read alignment to the reference genome, spatial barcode and UMI processing, and quantification of gene expression, resulting in spatially resolved feature–spot expression matrices. Summary statistics of spatial transcriptomic data quality, including spot-level gene and UMI counts, are provided in Supplementary Table 4. The spatial barcode and gene expression matrices generated by Space Ranger were subsequently imported and parsed for downstream computational analyses. For colocalization and spatial interaction analyses, expression data derived from 8 μm spatial resolution spots were used, enabling high-resolution interrogation of spatial gene expression patterns within the tissue microenvironment. To deconvolve the cellular composition of each spatial spot, Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) was applied using the “doublet” mode, which was based on our snRNA-seq data. This approach allowed for accurate assignment of cell type identities to individual spots while accounting for the mixed cellular content inherent to spatial transcriptomic data, thereby facilitating the precise characterization of cell type co-localization and intercellular spatial interactions within the tumor microenvironment.

Cell culture

Established human G4-MB cell line

The human G4-MB cell line CHLA-01R-MED was obtained was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). CHLA-01R-MED cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12; Gibco), plus 20 ng mL−1 EGF (#PHG0314; Gibco), 20 ng mL−1 bFGF (#HY-P7004; Gibco), 2% B-27 (#17504044; Invitrogen) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, 95% humidity. Cells were STR-authenticated and mycoplasma-free before use.

Patient-derived primary G4-MB cells

Primary tumor tissues were obtained from G4-MB patients at surgery (Beijing Tiantan Hospital, KY2024-212-02). Clinical information for patient-derived primary G4-MB cells is provided in Supplementary Table 5. Tissue was transported in ice-cold DMEM/F-12 and processed within 2 h. Briefly, 1–2 mm³ fragments were minced in ice-cold PBS, enzymatically dissociated with Accutase (15 min, 37 °C) and 0.2% collagenase IV (10 min, 37 °C), triturated through 100 μm and 40 μm strainers, and red blood cells were removed with red blood cell lysis buffer (3 min). Viable cells (trypan blue > 85%) were plated on laminin-coated (10 µg cm⁻²) dishes in DMEM/F-12 containing 2% B-27, 20 ng mL⁻¹ EGF/bFGF, 1% GlutaMAX and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cultures were grown at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, 95% humidity; half-medium changes were performed twice weekly. Cells were used at passages 2–4 and confirmed mycoplasma-negative by PCR. DNA from early-passage cells and their matched tumor tissue was analyzed using the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation850K BeadChip and classified with the Heidelberg medulloblastoma methylation classifier (v12.8), confirming Group 4 identity in both sample types. Detailed classifier outputs are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

HA and SH-SY5Y cell line

The human astrocyte cell line HA and the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For neuronal differentiation, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with 10 µM all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) for 7 days to induce neuron-like differentiation. The differentiation medium was refreshed every 2–3 days during the induction period.

SiRNA and plasmid transfection

We transfected G4-MB cells with three NR3C1 siRNAs, three VCAN siRNAs, a negative control (NC) siRNA, empty vector (pCMV6-Entry) or pCMV6-VCAN-FLAG plasmid (VCAN-OE) in six-well plates at a density of 8 × 10⁵ cells per well, using the jetPRIME reagent (Catalog #101000046; Polyplus Transfection). NR3C1 and VCAN siRNA sequences are provided in Table 1. At 48 h post-transfection, RNA and protein were collected for qPCR and western blot validation.

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Catalog #74104; Qiagen). The High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Catalog #4368814; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green (Catalog #A25742; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for cDNA synthesis and qPCR. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and the experiment was repeated three times. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

FFPE primary-tumor sections from three M + and three M0 G4-MB patients were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and heat-retrieved in EDTA. After 0.3% H₂O₂ (15 min) and blocking, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-VCAN (1:2000; ab270445, Abcam), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. DAB (ZLI-9017, ZSGB-BIO) was used for visualization. The IHC score was calculated by multiplying the intensity score (0 for no staining, 1 for light yellow, 2 for yellowish brown, 3 for brown) by the percentage of positive tumor cells (1 for 0–25%, 2 for 26–50%, 3 for 51–75%, 4 for > 75%), yielding a final score ranging from 0 to 12.

Western blotting

Proteins from cells or tissues were lysed with RIPA (high) + 1% PMSF (Solarbio R0010), quantified by Pierce BCA (#23227, Thermo), and 20 µg per lane was resolved on 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (120 V, 90 min). After wet transfer (200 mA, 240 min) onto methanol-activated PVDF membranes, blots were blocked with 5% skim milk (1 h), incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1000; NR3C1 24050-1-AP, VCAN ab270445, GAPDH 60004-1-Ig), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, 1 h). Signals were developed with ECL substrate and captured on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc system; band intensities were quantified with ImageJ.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed to demonstrate the binding of the NR3C1 transcription factor to the VCAN gene promoter, using the ChIP Assay Kit (Protein A + G Agarose) (Catalog No. K4625; APExBIO). Cells were cross-linked, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin. The lysate was immunoprecipitated with an NR3C1 antibody (24050-1-AP, Proteintech) or a control antibody. After washing, DNA was purified and eluted. PCR primers were designed based on two predicted NR3C1 binding sites on the VCAN promoter (5’ to 3’): Site 1 (forward primer: GCACTGAAAAGACCACTGGACTA; reverse primer: GCAGAATCAGACTTCAACCTTCA) and Site 2 (forward primer: GGTGGGCCTGACTGAAGAAA; reverse primer: CTGAGTAACAGGGACTTCTTGGA). qPCR amplification was performed to confirm NR3C1 binding to the VCAN promoter.

Invasion assay

After a 48-hour transfection with NC, NR3C1-si3, VCAN-si3, vector or VCAN-OE cells were detached and re-suspended in serum-free medium. The upper chamber of a 24-well transwell plate (Cat# 3422; Corning), precoated with Matrigel, was seeded with 1 × 10⁵ cells in 300 µL of cell suspension. The lower chamber contained 700 µL of medium with 10% fetal bovine serum. Following a 48-hour incubation at 37 ℃, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Cat# C8470; Solarbio) for 20 min. Cells that migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were counted under an inverted microscope in three random fields, and images were analyzed using ImageJ software. All migration assays were analyzed by normalizing the number of migrated cells to the total cell number assessed in parallel under identical conditions.

Cell proliferation assay

At 24 h post-transfection (NC, NR3C1-si3, VCAN-si3, vector or VCAN-OE), 5 × 10³ cells per well were reseeded in 96-well plates with 100 µL complete medium. CCK-8 reagent (10 µL; CK04, Dojindo) was added at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h, incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, and absorbance (450 nm) measured on a microplate reader. Each time-point had triplicate wells; the experiment was performed three times independently.

Apoptosis assay

Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI (Cat# 556547, BD Biosciences) in binding buffer for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6). The sum of early- (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) and late-stage (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) apoptotic percentages was calculated using FlowJo v10; data represent three independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8, with results presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (Mean ± SEM). All experiments were repeated three times. Student’s t-test and one - way ANOVA were used for intergroup comparisons, with P-values < 0.05 deemed statistically significant. Asterisks denote significance levels as follows: P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; ** indicates P < 0.01, and *** indicates P < 0.001.

Result

Significantly poor prognosis of metastatic group 4 medulloblastoma

We analyzed the ratios of four molecular subgroups in medulloblastoma (MB) from Beijing Tiantan Hospital (BTH) (n = 188) and GSE85217 database (n = 763). Group 4 medulloblastoma (G4-MB) was presented in 44.68% of the former cohort and 42.67% of the later, respectively, representing the highest incidence among all subgroups (Fig. S2A, B). In addition, G4-MB exhibited the higher metastasis rate than other subgroups, with the percentage of 33.33% in BTH cohort and 30.98% in GSE85217, respectively (Fig. 1A and B). Representative MRI image of G4-MB patients with metastasis was shown in Fig. S2C. Analysis of overall survival (OS) in two independent cohorts (BTH and Clifford’s) demonstrated that metastatic G4-MB patients had significantly shorter survival time compared to their non-metastatic counterparts (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C and D) [21]. To further characterize the metastatic mechanism of this subtype of MB, we compared primary tumors from metastatic (M+) patients with those from non-metastatic (M0) counterparts.

To identify the molecules closely related to tumor metastasis, we performed proteomics on paraffin tissues from M+ G4-MB (n = 10) and M0 G4-MB (n = 10) primary tumors. Totally, 82 proteins were identified with higher expression level in M+ compared to M0 samples (Fig. S2D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses of the proteomics data demonstrated that these proteins are significantly enriched in integrin-mediated signaling pathway, cell−substrate adhesion, and substrate adhesion-dependent cell spreading, which are closely associated with tumor migration (Fig. S2E) [22]. To further elucidate the molecular landscape associated with tumor metastasis, RNA sequencing was performed on frozen samples from M+ G4-MB (n = 7) and M0 G4-MB (n = 4) primary tumors. Totally 156 upregulated genes were identified in M+ compared with M0 samples (Fig. S2F), which were significantly enriched in regulation of neurotrophins TRK receptor signaling activity, sphingolipid metabolic process and sphingolipid biosynthetic process (Fig. S2G).

To further clarify the cellular composition and gene expression patterns associated with metastatic properties in G4-MB, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on frozen primary tumors from M+ G4-MB (n = 4) and M0 G4-MB (n = 4) primary tumors (Fig. 1E). The data from snRNA-seq resolved the tissue into eight distinct cell types: malignancy, neuroglial cell (astrocyte, oligodendrocyte and neuron), immune (microglia, macrophage and T cell), vascular cell (Fig. 1F and G). Infercnv was used to identify chromosomal copy number variation and distinguish malignancy from non-malignancy cells (Fig S3A-C). Among these, tumor cells were the predominant cell type, followed by neurons and immune cells, while vascular components were barely detectable (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, based on our snRNA-seq data of primary tumors from 4 M + and 4 M0 G4-MB cases (n = 8), we stratified the tumor cell population into four distinct subgroups: progenitor_like, glutamatergic differentiated_like, photoreceptor differentiated_like, and proliferation_like cells (Fig.S3D-F) [23]. Differential gene expression analysis found 807 genes in tumor cells of M+ group have higher expression level than M0 group, including SYCP1, CDH18, CUX2, NR3C1 and VCAN etc. (Fig. 1I). GO analyses demonstrated that these genes are significantly enriched in synapse organization, axonogenesis, and neuron projection guidance, which that are closely associated with neurodevelopment (Fig. 1J).

VCAN was identified as a marker of poor outcome in G4-MB and has positive correlation with tumor metastasis

To identify specific genes that may serve as biomarkers for tumor metastasis, we analyzed multi-omics datasets. These analyses screened out three upregulated genes in M+ G4-MB: VCAN, NID2, and CHN2, suggesting a potential link to tumor metastasis (Fig. 2A). When comparing with normal cerebellum tissue, VCAN is overexpressed in medulloblastoma tissue (Fig. 2B). Data of Clifford’s cohort demonstrated that higher mRNA expression of VCAN was closely correlated with poorer overall survival in G4-MB patients (n = 127) (Fig. 2C). However, expression levels of NID2 and CHN2 showed no significant association with patient prognosis (Fig. S4A-B). Using single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE119926) comprising nine G4-MB cases, we confirmed M+ G4-MB with significantly higher VCAN expression than M0 G4-MB (Fig. 2D-E). Spatial transcriptomics of three M + and three M0 tissue sections demonstrated increased VCAN expression in M+ tissues (Fig. 2F and G and Fig. S4C). In line with these results, qPCR and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses of patient specimens confirmed the upregulation of VCAN at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2H and J). Consistent with these findings, analysis based on the Sharma et al. classification, which divides G3/4-MB into eight subtypes, showed that VCAN was most highly expressed in subtype V, which exhibits the highest metastatic rate among G3/4-MB subtypes (Fig. S4D-E) [23]. We further found that VCAN expression was predominantly enriched in the progenitor_like subgroup of tumor cells. (Fig. 2K). Furthermore, spatial deconvolution analysis of the 10x Visium HD data demonstrated that VCAN expression was significantly higher in progenitor_like tumor cells of M+ G4-MB samples than in those of M0 samples (Fig. 2L and Fig. S5).

VCAN promoted metastasis of G4-MB cells in vitro

To evaluate the function of VCAN in tumor progression of G4-MB, VCAN knockdown assay was performed. To determine whether VCAN expression was tumor-associated, we first compared VCAN expression levels between the human astrocyte cell line HA1800 and G4-MB cells, including CHLA-01R-MED and MB09 (a patient-derived primary G4-MB cell line established from fresh tumor tissue). qPCR analysis demonstrated that VCAN expression was markedly higher in G4-MB cells than in HA1800 cells (Fig. S6A). In addition, the knockdown efficiency of VCAN siRNAs in HA1800 cells was validated by qPCR, showing that both siRNAs effectively reduced VCAN expression compared with the negative control (Fig. S6B). We then validated VCAN knockdown efficiency in our two G4-MB cell lines, CHLA-01R-MED and MB09. Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed that VCAN-si3 achieved the most robust and consistent effect on reducing VCAN mRNA and protein levels significantly in both cell lines (Fig. 3A, C, E and G). Transwell invasion assays further demonstrated that VCAN knockdown significantly reduced the invasive capacity of G4-MB cells (Fig. 3D and H), supporting a role for VCAN in metastatic behavior. To further delineate the phenotypic consequences of VCAN knockdown, we first examined its impact on tumor cell apoptosis. Apoptosis assays revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells upon VCAN knockdown compared to scramble siRNA controls (Fig. 3I and K). In contrast, VCAN knockdown did not significantly affect apoptosis in HA1800 cells (Fig. S6C-D). Similar findings were observed in differentiated SH-SY5Y neuron-like cells (Fig. S7). CCK8 assays, which evaluate cell viability, demonstrated that VCAN knockdown markedly impaired tumor cell growth (Fig. 3L and M). Altogether, these findings demonstrated that VCAN promotes proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and enhances invasion in G4-MB cells.

NR3C1 may act as a transcriptional activator of VCAN in G4-MB

To elucidate the transcriptional regulation on VCAN, we employed bioinformatic tools to predict its potential upstream transcription factors (TFs). Intersection analysis between predicted TFs and tumor-upregulated genes revealed eight overlapping candidates—including NR3C1, NR2F1, RUNX1, STAT5A, EBF1, GLIS3, PPARG, ZHX2—suggesting their potential involvement in VCAN dysregulation in G4-MB (Fig. 4A). Using Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC), we constructed a TF-target network and identified NR3C1 as a key regulator positioned upstream of VCAN (Fig. 4B). Notably, NR3C1 exhibited enhanced activation in M+ tumor cells, implicating its role in aggressive disease phenotypes (Fig. 4C and D). To validate whether NR3C1 directly regulates VCAN, we first performed in silico motif analysis using JASPAR2020, which predicted high-confidence binding sites for NR3C1 within the VCAN promoter region (Fig. 4E). Subsequent ChIP-qPCR assays confirmed significant enrichment of NR3C1 at site 1 and site 2, indicating a direct interaction (Fig. 4F). To verify the regulatory relationship, we knocked down NR3C1 in two independent cell lines and observed a consistent downregulation of VCAN at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4G and N). These findings suggested that NR3C1 may act as a transcriptional activator of VCAN in G4-MB.

NR3C1 is overexpressed in metastatic G4-MB and promotes malignant phenotypes partially through VCAN

Given the significant upregulation of NR3C1 activity and expression in M+ tumor cells, we next investigated its functional role in G4-MB progression (Fig. 6A-B). Using a clinical cohort of G4-MB specimens from Beijing Tiantan Hospital, we first validated that NR3C1 expression was significantly elevated in M+ cases at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6C-E). To further investigate the relationship between NR3C1 and VCAN in G4-MB, rescue experiments were performed by overexpressing VCAN in NR3C1-knockdown cells. Transwell invasion assays showed that NR3C1 knockdown significantly reduced the invasive capacity of tumor cells, while VCAN overexpression partially restored the invasive phenotype (Fig. 6F). Apoptosis assays demonstrated that NR3C1 knockdown significantly increased apoptotic cell populations compared with control cells, whereas VCAN overexpression partially reversed this effect (Fig. 6G-H). In addition, CCK8 assays demonstrated that NR3C1 knockdown significantly impaired tumor cell viability, which could also be partially rescued by VCAN overexpression (Fig. 6I). These findings suggest that VCAN mediates, at least in part, the malignant phenotypes associated with NR3C1 activity in G4-MB.

Discussion

Group 4 medulloblastoma (G4-MB), the most prevalent molecular subtype, frequently presents with metastatic disease at diagnosis and represents a major contributor to poor prognosis in pediatric patients. Exploring the metastatic mechanism of G4-MB is critical for identifying innovative therapeutic target [24, 25]. Our study combined multi-omics analyses from metastatic (M+) and non-metastatic (M0) primary tumors of G4-MB with experiments in vitro to investigate the metastatic mechanism of the tumor. We found for the first time that VCAN was upregulated directly by NR3C1, a transcription factor, and can promote tumor invasion and metastasis in G4-MB. In addition, VCAN expression was enriched in progenitor-like tumor cell populations of G4-MB.

Our proteomics analysis revealed enrichment of integrin-mediated signaling and cell adhesion pathways in M+ samples, while transcriptomic and single-nucleus RNA sequencing highlighted neurodevelopmental programs, including neurotrophin-TRK signaling, sphingolipid metabolism, and axonogenesis—all processes previously implicated in promoting tumor migration and invasion [26–30]. Accumulating evidence links VCAN to neurodevelopmental programs by demonstrating its role in sustaining neural stem cell maintenance and differentiation—fundamental processes for neural maturation [31–33]. These convergent findings suggest that VCAN, as a downstream effector of NR3C1, may integrate signals from cell adhesion and neurodevelopmental pathways to potentiate the metastatic trajectory of G4-MB. Consistent with this, our snRNA-seq data showed that VCAN expression was enriched in progenitor-like tumor cell populations of G4-MB (Fig. 2H), suggesting that it may preserve stem-like properties that endow these cells with heightened capacity for initiation, progression, and metastasis [34, 35]. These data collectively underscore the complex interplay between neurodevelopmental processes and metastatic progression, offering new insights into the pathogenesis of G4-MB and highlighting VCAN as a potential therapeutic target.

Building on this functional link to neurodevelopment, VCAN should also be recognized as a member of the proteoglycan superfamily and a critical component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [36, 37]. Beyond its developmental roles, VCAN contributes to tissue homeostasis and regulates key cellular behaviors, including adhesion, proliferation, migration, and embryonic development [38, 39]. Dysregulated VCAN expression alters ECM architecture and signaling, thereby reshaping the tumor microenvironment and influencing malignant progression. Indeed, VCAN is markedly overexpressed in a wide range of cancers—such as ovarian, pancreatic, breast, lung, and esophageal carcinomas—and its upregulation consistently correlates with enhanced metastatic potential and unfavorable prognosis [40, 41]. In gliomas, VCAN has also been shown to promote tumor cell proliferation and migration [42, 43], reinforcing its broad oncogenic relevance and strengthening its candidacy as a therapeutic target in G4-MB.

Previous study showed that VCAN can be upregulated by transcriptional factors (TFs) in various tumor [34, 44]. To elucidate the upstream transcriptional regulation of VCAN in G4-MBs, we screened active transcription factors in primary tumors from M + and M0 G4-MB. The Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD) was applied to predict potential transcription factors of VCAN. Further analysis revealed that compared to M0 G4-MB, NR3C1 exhibits enhanced transcription factor activity and increased expression in M+ G4-MB, as demonstrated by bioinformatics analysis, qPCR and Western blot analyses of clinical samples (Fig. 6A-E).

To further confirm direct transcriptional regulation, we performed ChIP-qPCR in G4-MB cell line and demonstrated that NR3C1 binds specifically to the VCAN promoter region. Knockdown of NR3C1 reduced VCAN expression level, suppressed invasion and proliferation, and induced apoptosis, whereas restoration of VCAN expression rescued these phenotypes. Spatially, 10× HD visium imaging and multiplex immunofluorescence revealed prominent co-localization of NR3C1 and VCAN within G4-MB-M+ tissue sections, further supporting their regulatory interaction (Fig. 5B-D). Collectively, NR3C1 is the transcription factor which potentially regulate VCAN expression and may play significant role in G4-MB tumor metastasis.

NR3C1, acting as a master transcription factor and a crucial regulator within the glucocorticoid receptor signaling pathway, exhibits varied functions across different types of cancer. In colorectal cancer, NR3C1 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting the transcription of CX40, thus suppressing cancer progression [45]. However, in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), NR3C1 can promote proliferation and migration of cancer cells by activating endoplasmic reticulum stress–mitophagy [46]. Although NR3C1/GR regulation of VCAN has been reported in non-malignant biological contexts, the present study is the first to identify and characterize the NR3C1–VCAN regulatory axis in G4-MB, and to link this axis to tumor invasion, metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes. NR3C1 knock-down in G4-MB cells increased apoptosis and reduced proliferation and invasion; VCAN re-expression partially reversed these effects, confirming VCAN as a critical downstream effector of the NR3C1 axis. The present study established VCAN as a critical downstream effector of NR3C1, confirming that the NR3C1–VCAN axis drives metastatic behavior in G4-MB. At present, there are no clinically approved therapies that directly target NR3C1 or VCAN in medulloblastoma. NR3C1 encodes the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), which can be pharmacologically modulated by glucocorticoids. However, these agents have pleiotropic and context-dependent effects and are primarily used for symptom management rather than tumor-directed therapy. Selective glucocorticoid receptor modulators (SGRMs) are under investigation to dissociate anti-inflammatory effects from potential pro-tumorigenic signaling, yet no SGRM has been approved for cancer therapy [47]. VCAN, a major extracellular matrix proteoglycan, has been implicated in tumor invasion and microenvironment remodeling across multiple malignancies, but direct targeting strategies are still largely experimental and limited to preclinical studies. Therefore, identifying the NR3C1–VCAN regulatory axis in G4-MB provides a rationale for future efforts aimed at indirect or combinatorial strategies that modulate tumor–microenvironment interactions [48–50]. For the first time, we revealed the possible role of the NR3C1-VCAN axis in metastatic G4-MB. Future studies should employ animal model to further explore the mechanism of NR3C1-VCAN axis during this tumor metastasis in vivo. In addition, our core cohort has several demographic and sampling limitations, including a male predominance and a relatively limited sample size. These factors may affect the representativeness and generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, we sought to improve robustness through multi-layer cross-validation across transcriptomic, proteomic, single-nucleus, spatial, and in vitro functional data. Future validation in larger, multicenter, independently annotated cohorts will still be required.

In conclusion, our study sheds light on the molecular mechanisms underlying metastasis in G4-MB, highlighting the NR3C1–VCAN axis as a potential therapeutic target. Further research is also needed to explore the potential clinical applicability of targeting this axis in combination with existing therapies.

Conclusions

This study shows that NR3C1 is activated in metastatic G4-MB and promotes tumor metastasis by directly binding to the VCAN promoter, enhancing its transcription. Knockdown of either NR3C1 or VCAN reduces the invasive capacity of G4-MB cells, highlighting the NR3C1-VCAN axis as a potential therapeutic target for metastatic G4-MB.
