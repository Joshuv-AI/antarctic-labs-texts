The impact of obesity on postoperative pulmonary complications in patients undergoing aortic surgery
Authors: Hui Wang, Chun Chen, Yun-Tai Yao, the Evidence in Cardiovascular Anesthesia (EICA) Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are common after surgery in patients with acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD). This study aimed to evaluate the incidence and risk factors for PPCs in ATAAD patients.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 203 patients with ATAAD who underwent urgent aortic surgery between January 2023 and August 2024 Patients were divided into PPCs and non-PPCs groups. The incidence and types of PPCs were recorded, and risk factors were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression. Postoperative outcomes were compared between two groups.ResultsPatients undergoing surgery for ATAAD, 36 (17.7%) developed PPCs. Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified obese as an independent risk factor for PPCs (adjusted OR 3.805, 95% CI 1.408–10.285, P = 0.008).ConclusionsObese was an independent risk factor for PPCs in patients undergoing surgery for ATAAD. These findings highlight the importance of preoperative BMI assessment in optimizing perioperative management.

Introduction

Aortic intimal rupture or intramural bleeding can cause separation of the aortic wall layer, leading to the formation of true and false lumens in the artery, resulting in aortic dissection. The anatomy involving the ascending aorta is called ATAAD. ATAAD is a life-threatening disease that develops rapidly and can affect important branches of the aorta, such as the brachiocephalic artery, subclavian artery, and coronary artery. Surgery is the preferred treatment method. Surgical repair for ATAAD comprises two principal components: root reconstruction and arch reconstruction. Root reconstruction techniques encompass the Bentall, David, and Wheat procedures. Arch reconstruction strategies range from hemiarch replacement to total arch replacement, with the latter often combined with the frozen elephant trunk technique, collectively known as Sun’s procedure. The International Aortic Arch Surgery Research Group emphasizes that early hospitalization related adverse events include in-hospital mortality, gastrointestinal bleeding, amputation related paralysis, acute renal failure, low cardiac output syndrome, and cerebrovascular disease [1]. Despite continuous advancements in surgical techniques, the perioperative mortality rate and incidence of pulmonary complications remain high. A study showed that the incidence of pulmonary complications was 29.2% [2]. Another study revealed that the incidence of hypoxemia was 27.4%, and the incidence of pulmonary infection was 17.7% [3]. Obesity increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, and obesity also increases in-hospital mortality in patients with dissection [4, 5]. Patients with obesity were found to be at increased risk for severe postoperative hypoxemia and higher BMI was significantly associated with the occurrence of severe hypoxemia [6]. High BMI, postoperative procalcitonin, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, and low albumin were independent risk factors for postoperative ARDS in ATAAD patients [7]. The obesity paradox refers to the observation that overweight or obese patients with certain diseases may have a better prognosis than their normal‑weight counterparts [8]. However, the role of obesity in the development of PPCs among patients with acute ATAAD remains poorly understood. This study investigates the impact of BMI on PPCs in ATAAD patients, aiming to improve perioperative strategies and outcomes.

Methods

Ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of a Hospital (2019 − 1301), with informed consent waived. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to STROBE guidelines.

Data collection

This retrospective study included 203 patients with ATAAD between January 2023 and August 2024. Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with confirmed ATAAD who underwent urgent surgery within 2 weeks of symptom onset at a Hospital. The surgical approach required cardiopulmonary bypass(CBP) with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA). Patients who received total aortic arch replacement and stent elephant trunk surgery for aortic arch pathologies were included. Patients who died intraoperatively or within 24 h postoperatively were excluded. Patients with a history of severe respiratory disease were excluded.

Data were collected from electronic medical records and laboratory reports, including age, sex, BMI, history of smoking, history of diabetes, history of hypertension, history of hyperlipidemia, history of renal dysfunction, history of cerebrovascular events, history of coronary heart disease, preoperative labs (the final labs obtained prior to surgery), time from symptom onset to surgery, preoperative heart rate (HR), preoperative SpO2, preoperative SBP, preoperative DBP, intraoperative variables (CPB time, DHCA duration, ACC duration, minimum nasopharyngeal temperature, operation duration), and PPCs. HR, SpO2, SBP and DBP were measured immediately upon the patient’s arrival in the operating room.

Postoperative data recorded included the duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) and the length of stay (LOS) in the intensive care unit (ICU). Postoperative complications assessed included in-hospital mortality, reoperation, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), cerebral complications, paraplegia, decreased muscle strength, thromboembolic events and PPCs. PPCs were defined according to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome (EPCO) definitions. They were diagnosed based on standardized criteria, including respiratory infection, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, and aspiration pneumonitis [1]. The perioperative care protocol entailed intraoperative lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volume and personalized PEEP, coupled with effective analgesia and proactive postoperative physical therapy.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 was used for statistical analyses. Continuous variables were analyzed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests. Categorical data were compared using chi-square tests. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), where appropriate. Significant variables from univariate analysis (P < 0.1), in addition to clinically relevant factors, were entered into logistic regression to identify independent risk factors.

We employed a multivariable logistic regression model to systematically evaluate the association between BMI and PPCs. Firstly, an unadjusted model (Model 1) was established to assess the crude effect of BMI. Secondly, considering that operation duration is a known important confounding factor and obesity may prolong operation duration, we adjusted for operation duration in Model 2 to evaluate the effect of BMI independent of surgical complexity. Finally, given that obesity is associated with a chronic inflammatory state, and inflammation may increase the risk of complications, we further adjusted for neutrophil count in Model 3 to explore the inflammation-mediated component of the BMI effect.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 226 patients who underwent surgery for ATAAD were identified from the medical record system. Among these patients, 20 were excluded because the time from surgery to onset of symptoms exceeded 2 weeks. 3 were excluded because they died intraoperatively or within 24 h postoperatively. Finally, 203 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

The preoperative and intraoperative variables of patients are reported in Table 1. There were 36 patients in the PPCs group and 167 in the non-PPCs group, with an incidence rate of 17.7%. BMI was higher in patients with PPCs than in those without (30.9 ± 6.7 vs. 26.8 ± 4.0; P = 0.001). Preoperative oxygen saturation in the PPCs group was lower than in the non-PPCs group (93.5 ± 4.8 vs. 96.2 ± 3.5, P = 0.002). Systolic blood pressure upon entering the room was higher in the PPCs group compared to the non-PPCs group (132.5 ± 22.3 vs. 124.4 ± 19.5, P = 0.03). The duration of surgery in the PPCs group was longer than in the non-PPCs group (7.3 ± 1.6 vs. 6.6 ± 1.9, P = 0.042). Additionally, the number of neutrophils in the PPCs group was greater than in the non-PPCs group (11.4 ± 4.3 vs. 9.9 ± 4.1, P = 0.048).

Incidence of PPCs

PPCs occurred in 17.7% of patients. Respiratory infection (11.3%) was the most frequent complication, followed by respiratory failure (6.9%), pleural effusion (1.0%), and pneumothorax (0.5%).

Risk factors

According to WHO standards, BMI was converted from a continuous to a categorical variable, categorizing patients into three groups: obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), overweight (30 > BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²), and normal weight (25 > BMI ≥ 18.5 kg/m²). These categories were coded as BMI2 (obese), BMI1 (overweight), and BMI0(normal weight), respectively. The results of the univariate logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 2. To systematically assess the correlation between BMI and PPCs, we constructed a series of multivariable logistic regression models (Table 3). In Model 1, BMI2 demonstrated a significant correlation with an elevated risk of PPCs (OR = 4.286, 95%CI: 1.616–11.369, P = 0.003). In Model 2, after adjusting for operation duration, the association between BMI2 and PPCs retained statistical significance(OR = 3.892, 95% CI: 1.452–10.433 P = 0.007). However, operation duration itself did not exhibit an independent association with PPCs in this model (OR = 1.181, 95%CI: 0.981–1.422, P = 0.079). In Model 3, following further adjustment for preoperative neutrophil count, BMI2 continued to be a significant independent risk factor of PPCs (OR = 3.805, 95%CI: 1.408–10.285, P = 0.008). Neither operation duration (OR = 1.160, 95% CI: 0.962–1.399, P = 0.121) nor neutrophil count (OR = 1.076, 95% CI: 0.984–1.176, P = 0.109) demonstrated a significant independent association with the outcome. Overweight (BMI1) was not significantly associated with the outcome in any model.

Postoperative outcomes

The duration of mechanical ventilation was expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). It was 28.5 (38.0) hours in the PPCS group and 12.0 (15.0) hours in the non-PPCS group. The difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The length of ICU stay was significantly longer in the PPC group than in the non-PPC group (6.7 ± 5.2 vs. 3.4 ± 2.9 days; P = 0.001)(Table 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that obese was an independent and significant risk factor for PPCs in patients undergoing surgery for ATAAD. Across sequentially adjusted multivariable logistic regression models, obesity was persistently identified as a potent independent risk factor for PPCs. Furthermore, patients who developed PPCs experienced significantly worse clinical outcomes, including prolonged mechanical ventilation duration and longer ICU stay.

PPCs are common postoperative complications in ATAAD patients. It has been reported that the frequency of respiratory events following ATAAD is 21.3% in previous research, which is similar to the 17.7% incidence observed in our research [9]. In this study of 203 patients, we found that obese was correlated with PPCs. The pathogenesis of PPCs following surgery for ATAAD remains poorly defined. A clearer understanding of the associated risk factors and underlying mechanisms is therefore essential for developing effective preventive strategies. Among the numerous factors influencing postoperative outcomes, obesity has emerged as one of the more prominent indicators in recent years. A previous study has demonstrated that high preoperative bradykinin levels and increased BMI are independent predictors of severe postoperative hypoxemia in patients with ATAAD [10]. BMI was an independent predictor of acute kidney injury after urgent aortic total arch replacement surgery with a frozen elephant trunk implantation [11]. Among surgical patients, obesity is associated with an increased incidence of perioperative complications and other adverse outcomes [12].

This study establishes obese as an independent risk factor for PPCs. After stepwise adjustment for potential confounders, including operation duration and neutrophil count, obese remained significantly associated with the outcome, demonstrating the robustness of this association. After adjustment for operation duration in Model 2, obesity remained significantly associated with PPCs, whereas operation duration itself was not independently associated with the outcome. This suggests that the increased PPC risk is attributable to pathophysiological changes related to excess adiposity, rather than prolonged surgical exposure. Furthermore, intraoperative blood loss was comparable between the two groups, indirectly mitigating the concern that bleeding-related complications were the primary driver. Some Chinese scholars found that obesity was an independent risk factors for postoperative severe hypoxemia in patients with ATAAD [13]. A study showed a higher BMI may be closely correlated with prolonged intubation for patients undergoing aortic dissection surgery [14]. Another study showed that Obesity is an independent risk factor for PPCs [15].

The relationship between obesity and increased PPCs after urgent aortic surgery is a complex, multifactorial process involving respiratory mechanics, lung function, inflammatory response, fluid management, and immune regulation. The increased weight of abdominal and thoracic adipose tissue exacerbates the compressive forces transmitted by the chest wall to the lung and exaggerates the cephalad displacement of the diaphragm. These produce a rightward shift of the respiratory system pressure–volume curve, higher pleural pressure, lower respiratory system compliance and lower functional residual capacity (FRC) before and after induction of general anesthesia. The combined effect of compression of the dorso-caudal lung and the gas absorption in lung units exposed to small airway closure results in greater risk of perioperative pulmonary atelectasis in obese than nonobese patients [16, 17]. In obese patients, a pre-existing chronic inflammatory state due to adipose-derived factors (IL-6, TNF-α) is amplified by surgical trauma [18–20]. Many of the factors involved in the CPB create multiple inflammatory responses [21]. The resultant inflammatory surge increases pulmonary capillary permeability, causing fluid leakage into the lung spaces. This process exacerbates pulmonary edema and hypoxemia, potentially progressing to acute respiratory distress syndrome(ARDS). The inflammatory factors secreted by the adipose tissue of obese patients, suppress immune function and increase the risk of postoperative infection [22].

Obesity may cause obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and obstructive sleep hypopnea (OSH). Increased fat deposition in the pharynx resulting in decreased patency of the pharynx increases the likelihood that relaxation of the upper airway muscles will cause collapse of the soft-walled oropharynx between the uvula and epiglottis [23]. Increased cervical adipose tissue leads to decreased upper respiratory tract patency.

Furthermore, obese patients are susceptible to PPCs after a prolonged surgical procedure due to the anesthetic drug accumulating in fat cells, which slows metabolism, resulting in delayed recovery of bodily functions.

The current study has some limitations. The study is single-center, retrospective of nature. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other centers that manage different patient populations, surgical techniques, anesthetic protocols, and postoperative care pathways. The relatively small sample size may further limit the multivariable analysis power and increase the risk of overfitting. Further multi-center studies and a high-quality meta-analysis should be carried out to provide more evidence. Information regarding preoperative oxygen supplementation, ventilatory support, or mechanical ventilation was not recorded. As a result, the relationship between preoperative respiratory status and PPCs could not be analyzed. Future studies are warranted to prospectively collect these data to further elucidate the potential impact of preoperative respiratory support on PPCs. PPCs of varying severities were pooled for analysis, which may mask the actual impact of obesity on major adverse events. Future studies should focus specifically on severe PPCs to yield findings with greater clinical implications.
