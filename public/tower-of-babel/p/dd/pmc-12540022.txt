Restaging of a urothelial tumour from a rare cardiac metastasis: a multidisciplinary challenge
Authors: Mariana Sousa Paiva, Bruno M Rocha, Francisco Albuquerque, João Abecasis, Jamal Nasir Khan, Alexandru Patrascu, Abdelsalam Bensaaud
Published: 2025

Case description

A 69-year-old male with a history of locally advanced urothelial carcinoma was referred for evaluation of an incidental cardiac mass on transthoracic echocardiogram. The patient had no significant cardiac history except left bundle branch block with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction.

Transoesophageal echocardiogram revealed a bulky, pedunculated, and highly mobile mass in the right atrium (RA), apparently attached to the tricuspid annulus, protruding into the right ventricle in diastole (Figure 1A and B; Supplementary material online, Video S1). Cardiac magnetic resonance (MRI) confirmed the presence of a heterogeneous multilobulated mass with malignant features at tissue characterization (Figure 1C–E; Supplementary material online, Videos S2 and S3). Several T2-hyperintense infracentrimetric nodules were simultaneously detected in the pulmonary parenchyma (Figure 1F), suspicious for metastasis, despite no metabolic activity on positron emission tomography (PET)—likely due to limitations in PET sensitivity.

Multimodality imaging panel of the mass. Transoesophageal echocardiography showing the mass (arrow) crossing the tricuspid valve plane (dashed line) and protruding into the right ventricle during diastole: (A) mid-oesophageal right ventricular inflow-outflow tract view; (B) transgastric right ventricular inflow view. At cardiac magnetic resonance, the mass was heterogeneous at SSFP-cine sequences (C—inflow-outflow tract of the right ventricle), with presumed areas of high signal intensity at T2-weighed images (D—STIR), and with areas of late gadolinium enhancement (E—PSIR at short-axis). Due to its high mobility, visualization and characterization were limited in some of the sequences. HASTE sequences also depicted high T2 signal nodules on both pulmonary fields (arrowhead) (F). (G and H) Cardiac computed tomography detailed intimate relation of the right atrium mass with the atrioventricular groove and right coronary artery path. (I) Macroscopic appearance of the mass after fixation. Histological analysis of the cardiac mass revealed a significant thrombotic component associated with giant cells exhibiting nuclear pleomorphism [haematoxylin and eosin stain, (J) ×200 magnification, (K) ×400 magnification], which were positive for cytokeratin AE1/AE3 (L—immunohistochemistry, ×400 magnification), consistent with an epithelial origin and morphologically identical to the primary tumour. RA, right atrium; RCA, right coronary artery; RV, right ventricle; SVC, superior vena cava.

After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent cardiac surgery for mass excision to obtain a definitive histopathological diagnosis and to mitigate the immediate risk of embolization. Cardiac computed tomography (CT) excluded coronary artery disease and better depicted RA mass topography (Figure 1G and H). Direct inspection revealed anterior RA insertion, between the tricuspid annulus and the RA appendage. Although the tumour was in close proximity to the right coronary artery at the atrioventricular groove, safety margins were obtained (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). The mass consisted of a thrombus containing scattered giant multinucleated cells of epithelial origin, morphologically identical to a urothelial tumour, thus confirming the diagnosis of a cardiac metastasis of urothelial origin (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1I–L). The patient then started cisplatin-based chemotherapy but died 6 months later of septic shock following bilateral ureteral stent implantation.

This case highlights the complementary roles of echocardiography for lesion detection, cardiac MRI for tissue characterization, CT for surgical planning, and PET-CT for systemic staging—demonstrating the necessity of a multimodal imaging approach in accurately diagnosing and managing rare cardiac metastases. In particular, when available, 3D echocardiography can add incremental value by providing enhanced spatial characterization of cardiac masses, which may guide both diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Although cardiac metastases from urothelial carcinoma are typically associated with a dismal prognosis—averaging 2 months of survival—our patient lived for 6 months following diagnosis, after undergoing surgical excision and systemic chemotherapy.1 While the survival benefit of surgical intervention remains uncertain due to the scarcity of reported cases, our experience suggests that, in carefully selected patients, surgical excision may contribute to improved clinical outcomes by reducing embolic risk, confirming diagnosis, and enabling timely systemic treatment.2, 3
