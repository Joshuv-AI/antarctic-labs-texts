With his clarion call for ‘A World Without Measles’ in 2024, epidemiologist and immunisation specialist Jon Kim Andrus presented the economic, practical and ethical-humanitarian rationales for worldwide efforts to eliminate the disease.1 The WHO defines elimination as the absence of endemic transmission within a given geographical area for at least 12 months as certified by national and regional verification commissions. Since the early 2000s, many countries and regions have gained then lost and sometimes regained elimination status while measles outbreaks have continued to occur regularly. Events such as the COVID-19 pandemic and the withdrawal of US funding from global health further expose the reversibility of gains towards measles elimination. In 2025, the Americas lost their elimination status and, as of 1 July 2026, 331 407 suspected measles cases were reported in 165 WHO Member States. The fragility of elimination calls for a careful reassessment of the logics and strategies of measles control.

Although modelling demonstrates the cost-effectiveness of elimination programmes,2 their reliance on supplementary immunisation activities (SIAs) and enhanced epidemiological surveillance to achieve or maintain elimination certification raises questions about the broader, unmeasured costs and impacts of elimination-based policies on local healthcare systems and on global public health. Revisiting the history and ramifications of elimination’s pursuit, this commentary argues for the urgent necessity of reframing the goal of measles control programmes to incorporate the social drivers of health and a primary healthcare (PHC) centred approach.

Five decades in pursuit of elimination

Measles elimination has been called for since shortly after the first measles vaccines were approved in 1963, becoming an ascendant global health goal by the 2010s. Aiming for what was called at the time ‘local eradication’, and then ‘elimination of indigenous measles’, the US launched vertical campaigns in 1967–1969 and again in 1977–1982 to demonstrate the effectiveness of mass measles vaccination. Biological data strongly argued in favour of the feasibility of eradication because humans are the only natural hosts for measles virus (the disease has no animal reservoir) and because only one serotype of the measles virus exists.3 Spurred by a drastic decline in cases, Centers for Disease Control (CDC) officials first proposed ‘global measles eradication’ in 1982. Its feasibility was, the CDC argued, further supported by the success of global smallpox eradication (1980) and the low cost and high efficacy of measles vaccines. Eradication would ‘result in the saving of large sums of money being spent on measles treatment, vaccination and surveillance’.4

This proposal relied on infrastructure developed by the WHO-UNICEF Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), launched in 1974 to promote vaccination for all the world’s children against diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, measles, poliomyelitis, tuberculosis and smallpox. Among EPI leaders, however, as well as in European countries, many opposed the vertical (specialised and hierarchical) tendencies of the disease control programmes based on US ideology and CDC methodologies. Instead, EPI’s priority was to expand community-level vaccination services and increase coverage rates. Critics’ opposition coincided with the adoption of the Alma Ata Declaration of 1978, which emphasised a PHC framing of vaccination as part of intersectoral and comprehensive community-based services to improve well-being and health equity.5

The beneficial impact of immunisation on public health was recognised to the point that it remained a core service within the more restrictive ‘selective’ PHC (SPHC) strategies that emerged by 1982. SPHC, a more technology-focused lives-per-dollar approach than PHC, framed vaccination as cheap and highly cost-effective.6 At the time, nations in the Americas proved particularly receptive to US calls for global measles eradication. Following Cuba’s achievement of measles elimination in 1986 through a ‘catch-up, maintain and follow-up’ immunisation strategy, Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) regularly pressed member states to support regional targets.7 In 1994, as the Americas were declared polio-free, PAHO announced an enhanced goal ‘of eradicating measles from the western hemisphere’, by 2000.7

Canada and the US declared national measles elimination in 1998 and 2000, respectively, raising the profile of a stepwise (national then regional then global) measles elimination plan. Multilateral and philanthropic investments in global health further buttressed the importance and achievability of elimination targets, emphasising the number of ‘lives saved’ per dollar spent—and the urgency of preparing for emerging biosecurity threats.8 Immunisation in the Global South, in general, and for measles in particular, also got a boost from the launch in 2000, of the public–private global health initiative GAVI, The Vaccine Alliance. GAVI, EPI and the WHO identified measles vaccine coverage and outbreak control as the best indicators of a country’s overall infectious disease prevention efforts.9

By 2010, all WHO regions had pledged to achieve measles elimination through participation in the Global Vaccine Action Plan,10 but the World Health Assembly still needed to pass a resolution on measles global elimination. Lack of political will, competing priorities, inadequate resources, especially in the Global South,11 immunity gap challenges and the COVID-19 pandemic12 have postponed any ruling from the World Health Assembly on the matter.

The burden of elimination

The measles vaccine has saved more lives than any other EPI vaccine with the highest cost efficacy ratio; it accounts for 60% of the estimated 154 million lives saved by EPI over 50 years, of which 95% are in children under the age of 5.13 Measles vaccines are safe and effective, with 95%–97% seroconversion following two appropriately-timed measles-containing vaccine doses in childhood. In the face of an extremely contagious disease (RO between 12 and 18) that remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality,14 we agree that it is vital to maintain the highest possible measles immunisation rates and to reduce regional and local disparities. In 2024, 84% of children got their first dose (compared with 86% in 2019) and only 76% their second one; ‘30 million remained under-protected often in fragile, conflict-affected or vulnerable settings’.15

Equating the need to close immunisation gaps with the pursuit of elimination, however, undercuts other central components of PHC such as understanding the social dynamics of infection and under-vaccination locally.16 Vaccine hesitancy was listed among the top 10 ‘threats to the improvement of world health’ in 201917 and, to officials’ dismay, the 2025–2026 measles outbreaks in the USA, Canada and Mexico have illustrated the complex social and political tensions that threaten herd immunity. Other concerns, both in the Global North and South, can also be overlooked by solely pursuing elimination, such as fair and equitable access to healthcare and the stigmatisation of non-vaccinated or under-vaccinated communities.

Moreover, national measles elimination certification hinges on the differentiation of ‘endemic’ cases from ‘imported’ ones, at a time of accelerating globalisation that blurs the distinction between the two categories.18 This practice is also misaligned with the conceptualisation of global public health as a shared, transnational responsibility. The quest for national elimination status can lead to competition between countries for resources and credibility from global funders; it can also stigmatise mobile ‘vectors’ such as migrants, who are then portrayed as invading and disrupting national spaces of immunity. Certifying elimination—and maintaining the certificate—is, moreover, a costly, lengthy process that requires sophisticated surveillance mechanisms, including early diagnostic and genotyping tests used not only to track cases and control outbreaks but also, sometimes exclusively, to determine if they are ‘imported’

Pursuing elimination may further shunt resources away from routine, multiantigen immunisation activities into targeted one-time SIAs. In Latin America, focusing on elimination has competed with the imperative to fund health personnel sustainably, to prepare health systems to cope with increased vaccine demand, to promote vaccination widely and to maintain adequate vaccine storage and distribution chains.19 In Lao PDR, in 2024, an SIA cost about 1 million USD and the genotyping of a single sample (sent to the WHO regional reference laboratory in Hong Kong) US$ 5000. By contrast, volunteer health workers conducted most of the immunisation work, which, as they told two of the authors, often detracted from their time and commitment to other community health work. SIAs have led to the cancellation of routine immunisation sessions,20 the decrease of routine immunisation activities21 and possibly negatively affected the utilisation of maternal and child services.22 The measles virus is a complex, fast-moving target. Six decades of mass vaccination have raised questions about the number of doses required for elimination. China has introduced a third dose in some of its biggest cities,23 as did Cambodia in 2024 during outbreaks, as mentioned during interviews conducted with UNICEF and WHO staff in early 2025. Long experience with measles vaccination has prompted questions about ideal vaccination rates (>95%), the most appropriate age for a first shot and, more recently, the need to develop newer (costly) high-tech vaccine delivery methods24 as well as more efficient vaccines.25 Increasing inequities worldwide reinforce the impact of poverty, malnutrition, forced migration and overcrowding on both the experience of measles—in terms of transmission and severity—and the efficacy of vaccines and vaccination programmes.12

Prescribing change

Given these costs and challenges, is elimination—as a series of steps expanding from national to regional then global levels—the best measles policy goal? We should consider instead the need to direct resources towards supporting PHC and to protect the well-being of individuals and communities more broadly.

What if, instead of imagining a far-off ‘end of measles’, we aspire to maintain and expand the regular rhythms of routine immunisation towards maximal coverage, alongside other essential health services? During the 1980s and 1990s, decades of acute underinvestment in African public health, many saw the expansion of vaccination infrastructure as a gateway to the provision of rural PHC.6 As our own and others’ observations have shown, immunisation practices and systems continue, especially in the rural Global South, to be tied to a range of health promotion activities, including ‘health talks’, mother and baby wellness checks, reproductive and non-communicable diseases health education and the provision of essential services to—and collection of basic health information from—underserved communities.26 Thinking of measles vaccination as woven into larger systems for supporting life and well-being can indeed produce larger and more meaningful ‘returns’. The Ministry of Public Health in Lebanon, alongside the country’s UNICEF office, recently demonstrated how immunisation platforms can serve as entry points to serve communities afflicted by social conflict and economic collapse, offering them maternal–child health, nutrition and social protection services, that, in turn, strengthen vaccination uptake.27 More broadly still, the WHO’s Immunization Agenda 2030 has taken into consideration multiple country experiences, showcased by the Lancet Commission on Investing in Health, of how a PHC agenda to achieve universal health coverage can facilitate and enable the immunisation activities of specific disease elimination programmes.28 29

We propose that if the goal were to achieve high sustainable coverage rates and limit measles-related fatalities, suffering and disability, the benefits of immunisation would be more equitably distributed within and across nations.30 Pivoting towards more and more effective care would also help understand and address the social drivers of measles outbreaks and likely restore some of the trust of communities in public health authorities by better addressing the challenge of vaccine hesitancy—which might be fuelled by the loss of elimination status.

We are proposing to shift the policy framework from an elimination/non-elimination binary to a set of cost–beneficial and equity-oriented goals: (1) redirect resources from repeated national SIAs and genotyping towards strengthening local ‘tailored’ routine immunisation16 and improving subnational vaccination coverage through mobile services in particular; (2) integrate immunisation (against measles and other EPI vaccines) within PHC services including screening of social vulnerabilities27; (3) invest in community-level vaccine confidence and health literacy initiatives and (4) identify and use new outcome measures that integrate equity.

History shows that pursuing disease eradication comes at a high economic and social cost—including the neglect of other health investments. History can also help us think of measles control differently. For some, resurgence calls for the intensification of elimination-driven vaccination. Instead, we suggest that, in conflating optimal vaccination with eliminationism (as well as in conflating the benefits of vaccination with those of elimination as a policy framework),2 31 we have split measles vaccination off from broader aspirations to support life and health at the heart of the PHC approach. ‘Zero endemic cases for 12 months’ is a suboptimal proxy for human flourishing. How can nations be more ambitious and imaginative in addressing measles as a global issue and a pathology of vulnerability? How can we address the risk that measles poses not only in terms of leaky borders and immunisation gaps, but also in terms of unequal access to basic health services and living conditions, and of identifying and caring for the most vulnerable? Considering these questions is a first step towards reframing global measles policies.
