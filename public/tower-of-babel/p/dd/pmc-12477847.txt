Heterologous expression and foldase-assisted refolding of LipGoM, a Pseudomonas lipase from family I
Authors: Elena Lizbeth Garcia-Villegas, Itzel Anahí Hidalgo-Manzano, Liliana Pardo-López, Enrique Rudiño-Piñera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipases from Pseudomonas species are valuable biocatalysts, but their heterologous expression is often complicated by the need for different protocols to ensure proper folding and activity. In this study, we successfully produced a soluble and active form of LipGoM, a lipase derived from Pseudomonas sp., that employs a refolding strategy after solubilization in urea. Our findings indicate that refolding LipGoM in the presence of its native foldase, LifGoM, is essential for restoring its enzymatic activity. Furthermore, we identified that optimizing the refolding conditions, specifically pH, ionic strength, protein concentration, and the addition of glycerol, reducing agents, and ions, was critical for success. The lipase-lipid GoM mixture, Lip-Lif GoM, exhibited maximal hydrolytic activity toward p-nitrophenyl-octanoate at 55 °C and pH 8.0, demonstrating notable stability in the presence of detergents and organic solvents. These results demonstrate the indispensable role of its cognate foldase, LifGoM, in activating LipGoM, as well as the significance of buffer composition, particularly glycerol, for the effective refolding of the enzyme and its subsequent activity.Keywords: Lipase, Foldase Lif, Refolding, Pseudomonas, Heterologous expression

Introduction

Triacylglycerol acyl hydrolases (EC 3.1.1.3), commonly known as lipases, form a group of enzymes catalyzing the hydrolysis of triacylglycerides and other substrates containing ester groups, resulting in the production of fatty acids and glycerol as final products [[1], [2], [3]]. Bacterial lipases have gained significant importance in biotechnology due to their catalytic versatility, ease of genetic manipulation, and high production yield. Bacterial lipases are classified into 19 families based on sequence homology, phylogenetic criteria, and conserved motifs [4]. Family I includes the named true lipases, classified into eight subfamilies. Lipases of subfamilies 1.1 and 1.2 comprise lipases of Gram-negative bacteria such as Burkholderia, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and Vibrio species [4,5].

Lipases from the genus Pseudomonas are extracellular enzymes that demonstrate exceptional stability in alkaline conditions and tolerate organic solvents, detergents, and temperature extremes [6,7]. This versatility makes them valuable biocatalysts for a broad range of biotechnological applications. However, their expression and soluble production pose significant challenges that must be resolved before commercialization can be pursued [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In nature, these lipases are usually part of the same operon as their respective foldase [6,11]. The foldase, Lif, acts as a dedicated chaperone, facilitating the correct folding of the lipase, Lip, and enabling it to reach its native, secretion-competent state [6,9,12]. Without the corresponding Lif, Lip expressed in heterologous systems often accumulates as inclusion bodies, hindering their functional and structural characterization [14,15]. Multiple strategies have been explored to obtain active and soluble lipases from Pseudomonas in quantities sufficient for biochemical characterization [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Commonly used techniques include the co-expression of lipase genes with their Lif in prokaryotic and eukaryotic expression systems, as well as in vitro refolding methods [13,18].

At the end of the 1990s, several studies evaluated the in vitro refolding of lipases belonging to subfamilies 1.1 and 1.2 in the absence of their cognate foldases, Lif, resulting in inactive enzymes or enzymes with low hydrolytic activity [8,[18], [19], [20]]. Subsequently, a different approach was adopted, performing refolding in the presence of their respective foldase, which proved effective, especially when complemented with optimized buffer conditions [15,20,21]. To date, several cases of lipases from Pseudomonas, Burkholderia, and Ralstonia have been reported to assist in refolding in vitro into active enzymes, thereby demonstrating the functional dependence of these lipases on Lif proteins [18,20,[22], [23], [24]].

In 2015, during a metagenomic campaign using seawater samples collected from the southeastern Gulf of Mexico, the gene encoding a lipase from Pseudomonas sp. GoM6 was isolated [25]. The enzyme, named LipGoM, shares 86 % sequence identity with Lipomax, a triacylglycerol lipase derived from Pseudomonas alcaligenes, which is known for its tolerance to anionic detergents [26]. Currently, Lipomax is employed at an industrial level; however, achieving its soluble and active form requires extensive optimization efforts [26]. Given the high sequence similarity between the two enzymes, the functional expression of LipGoM in E. coli is particularly noteworthy, as demonstrated in this work, as it exhibits properties similar to those of Lipomax.

In this work, we describe a method for obtaining LipGoM in a soluble form using a refolding procedure assisted by LifGoM, along with the dilution-refolding method, and its subsequent biochemical characterization. The results presented highlight the critical role of LifGoM in achieving a properly folded and fully active form of LipGoM, in agreement with observations reported for other Pseudomonas lipases. However, they also emphasize the importance of the buffer composition in obtaining a hydrolytically active form of LipGoM.

Methodology

Construction of plasmids

LipGoM and LifGoM genes were identified from the genomic library of Pseudomonas sp. GoM6 (Rodríguez-Mejía et al., 2024). Briefly, the genomic DNA was partially digested using the restriction enzyme Bsp143l (Thermo Fisher Scientific™; Waltham, MA, USA). The digested genomic DNA fragments, ranging from 3 to 6 kbp, were excised from the agarose gel and ligated into the pUC19 plasmid using the T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Scientific). The ligation reaction was transformed into electrocompetent E. coli DH5α cells, which were then plated on modified LB plates supplemented with 1 % tributyrin as a lipidic substrate. A colony surrounded by a clear halo was considered a potential clone with lipolytic activity, and its DNA was extracted using the ZR Plasmid Miniprep-Classic kit from ZYMO RESEARCH (Irvine, CA, USA) according to the protocol specifications. The LipGoM-LifGoM coding genes were amplified by PCR using the Supreme NZYProof DNA polymerase, and the oligonucleotides FWLipGoMNdeI (5′- ACTGCACTGC ATATGAAAAA GCTGCTG - 3′) and RVfoldXhoI (5′- TATAATCTCG AGTCAGAGTT CGGCATC - 3′) with 30 cycles of amplification (96 °C 30 s, 70 °C 30 s, and 72 °C 45 s). The PCR product of 1950 bp was digested with BamHI and SalI, gel-purified, and then inserted into the pET-28a (+) vector (Novagen), which had been previously digested with the same enzymes. The LifGoM gene was amplified using the oligonucleotides FwfoldNdeI (5′-ACTGATACTCATATGTCCAGAGCGAATCATC- 3′) and RvfoldXhoI (5′

TATAATCTCGAGTCAGAGTTCGGCATC- 3′), and the PCR product of 1036 bp was digested with XhoI and NdeI, gel-purified, and inserted into the pET-28a (+) vector (Novagen). Both plasmids contain a histidine tag at the N-terminus of each protein and confer resistance to kanamycin. The resulting plasmids (pET28a-LipGoM and pET28a-LifGoM) were verified by DNA sequencing and used to transform cells from the E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain. The lipolytic activity was detected on modified lysogeny broth (LB) plates (Fig. S1) (0.5 % peptone, 0.3 % yeast extract, 1 % tributyrin, 1 % Arabic gum, and 1.3 % Bacto agar).

Sequence analysis of LipGoM and LifGoM

Sequences of LipGoM from Pseudomonas sp. GOM6 and its foldase, LifGoM, were analyzed using different bioinformatic platforms. Sequence similarity was analyzed using the Blast program (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Multiple sequence alignment was performed with the ClustalW 2.0 program using the default parameters, and sequences were visualized using EsPript 3 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/). The signal peptide of LipGoM was predicted using the SignalP 4.1 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-4.1). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the MEGA 11.0 software and the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The sequences used to generate the phylogenetic tree of LipGoM and its homologs were obtained from Ref. [26]. The sequences used in the phylogenetic tree of LifGoM and its homologs were obtained from Ref. [9]. The LipGoM and LifGoM from the Pseudomonas sp. GoM6 three-dimensional models were obtained using AlphaFold2 via Google Colaboratory and visualized in UCSF ChimeraX (Fig. 1a and c) [27,28].

Expression of LipGoM gene and solubilization of inclusion bodies

A 2 mL pre-inoculum of E. coli BL21 (DE3) transformed with the pET28a-LipGoM plasmid was used to inoculate a 100 mL LB medium culture at 37 °C and 200 RPM. Upon reaching an OD600 nm of 0.6, cells were induced with 0.5 mM Isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). After IPTG addition, the temperature was reduced to 18 °C, and agitation at 200 RPM was maintained for 18 h. Subsequently, transformed E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8960g for 20 min at 10 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer, containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, and 150 mM sodium chloride, followed by cell lysis via sonication with pulses of 40 s on and 40 s off at an amplitude of 40 % on a device Ultrasonic Processor (Cole Parmer). The resulting cell extract was centrifuged at 8960 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the insoluble protein pellet was treated with a denaturation buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM sodium chloride, 8 M urea, and 10 % glycerol. A supernatant fraction containing the solubilized and denatured lipase was obtained after centrifugation at 8960g for 10 min at 10 °C.

Expression and purification of LifGoM

A 50 mL pre-inoculum of E. coli BL21 (DE3) transformed with the pET28a-LifGoM plasmid was used to inoculate 1 L of LB medium culture at 37 °C and 200 RPM. Once the culture reached an OD600 nm of 0.8, cells were induced with a final concentration of 0.5 mM IPTG. After the IPTG addition, the temperature was reduced to 30 °C, and agitation was maintained initially for 3 h post-induction at 200 RPM. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8960g for 20 min at 10 °C. LifGoM was purified using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid affinity chromatography on a 2 mL His-tag resin (ROCHE cOmplete™). The column was equilibrated with 20 mL of equilibration buffer consisting of 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 50 mM sodium chloride, and 5 mM imidazole. The sample was applied to the His-tag resin and incubated for 12 h at 4 °C. An additional 30 mL of the equilibration buffer was used to remove proteins that were bound nonspecifically. LifGoM was then eluted in two steps: first with the addition of 20 mM imidazole and then with 500 mM imidazole, both in the same buffer. Fractions of 2 mL were collected and analyzed using 12 % SDS-PAGE stained with Coomassie blue.

Screening for refolding conditions for LipGoM assisted by LifGoM

A qualitative refolding screen was designed, similar to those previously reported [[29], [30], [31], [32]], to identify optimal conditions for the in vitro refolding of LipGoM, assisted by its cognate foldase, LifGoM. A variety of components using a sparse matrix approach were analyzed, including pH, buffer composition (Tris and Hepes), protein solubulity stabilizer (such as glycerol), salt concentration (sodium chloride), presence or absence of calcium ions, and a reducing agent (BME).

The screening process, conducted in a 96-well format, began with 10 μL of LifGoM (approximately at 1 mg/mL), supplemented with 180 μL of the refolding buffer tested, and finally adding 10 μL of the unfolded LipGoM (at 1 mg/mL) (Fig. S2). This setup resulted in a final volume of 200 μL per well. After adding all the components, the plate was incubated with gentle shaking at either room temperature or at 4 °C for 24 h (Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3).

To determine the optimal LipGoM refolding condition, a qualitative measurement of activity against pNP octanoate (C8) was performed under all screened conditions. An enzyme was deemed as properly refolded when hydrolytic activity against pNP octanoate (C8) was detected in the well, indicated by a color change to yellow within the first 5 min of the assay. This color change occurs due to the hydrolysis of the substrate, which releases p-nitrophenol. Samples that precipitated during the refolding process or that showed no activity within the specified incubation time were discarded (Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3).

For a quantitative evaluation of hydrolytic activity, the following steps were taken: 180 μL of activity buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, with 50 mM sodium chloride), 10 μL of the Lip-LifGoM mixture, and 10 μL of pNP octanoate (C8) dissolved in isopropanol were added to each well of a 96-well plate. The plate was then incubated at 30 °C for 5 min. Before this assay, each refolded sample was dialyzed against 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8, with 50 mM sodium chloride to remove any additives used during the refolding process. This strategy generated 11 conditions (Fig. S2 and Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S3, and S4).

The condition that produces the highest hydrolytic activity against pNP octanoate (C8) was used to refold LipGoM in the presence of the purified LifGoM in a 1:2 M ratio in 200 mL. The refolding buffer used in this work contains 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 50 mM sodium chloride, 20 % glycerol, and 5 mM calcium chloride.

Once the condition described in the previous paragraph was obtained, it was used to incubate the Lip-LifGoM mixture at 3 RPM at 4 °C for 24 h. The sample obtained was concentrated with an Amicon with a 30 kDa cut-off and dialyzed against 1 L of 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8. The resulting mixture of LipGoM and LifGoM, Fig. 2c, was utilized to characterize the lipase activity against different substrates and in the presence of various additional additives such as detergents, organic solvents, and divalent metallic ions.

Hydrolytic activity assays

The specific activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was measured using a spectrophotometric method with pNP octanoate (C8) (Sigma). Absorbance was recorded at 410 nm using the Cary 60 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent). The reaction mixture consisted of 10 μl of the refolded Lip-LifGoM mixture (25 ng), 180 μl of the activity buffer (Tris-HCl 20 mM, pH 8, sodium chloride 50 mM), and 10 μl of five solutions containing different p-nitrophenyl derivatives (p-NPL) at 10 mM. All assays were conducted in triplicate, and a control without adding Lip-LifGoM mixture was included. The hydrolytic reaction was conducted at 55 °C for 2 min. One unit of lipase activity is defined as the amount of the Lip-LifGoM mixture that releases 1 μmol of p-nitrophenol per minute. It is important to note that although we worked with samples displaying at least 4 bands in an SDS-PAGE, we used densitometry to estimate the amount of the Lip-LifGoM mixture used in each experiment.

Biochemical characterization of the Lip-LifGoM mixture

Activity assays were performed using pNP with various chain lengths to evaluate the substrate specificity of the sample. The substrates tested included pNP butyrate (C4), pNP octanoate (C8), pNP decanoate (C10), pNP dodecanoate (C12), and pNP palmitate (C16). Short- and medium-chain substrates were dissolved in 100 % isopropanol, while long-chain substrates were prepared in a mixture of 100 % isopropanol and 100 % acetonitrile at a 4:1 ratio.

The folded mixture was incubated at various temperatures ranging from 30 to 60 °C to assess the effect of temperature on hydrolysis activity. To determine the optimal pH, the mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 5 min across different pH values ranging from 6 to 10. Different buffers were used for this purpose: MES for pH 6, Tris-HCl for pH 7 to 8, and glycine-NaOH for pH 9 to 10. pNP-octanoate was used as the substrate for these assays.

The effects of calcium, magnesium, solvents, and detergents on enzyme activity were examined. The enzyme was pre-incubated with either 1 or 5 mM of CaCl2, MgCl2, and EDTA; 0.001 %, 0.005 %, or 0.025 % (w/v) of SDS, Triton X-100, and Sarkosyl; and 10 % or 30 % (v/v) of various solvents, including isopropanol, acetone, tert-butanol, ethanol, acetonitrile, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). In all cases, the mixture was pre-incubated for 5 min at 4 °C, and the hydrolytic activity was determined using p-NP octanoate as the substrate.

Results and discussion

Protein sequence and phylogeny analysis

A BLAST search of the LipGoM amino acid sequence revealed significant similarity to lipases from species such as Pseudomonas sp. BMS12, P. sp. PDM15 and Pseudomonas alcaligenes (Supplementary Table S5). Sequence alignment showed that LipGoM exhibits key features of Pseudomonas lipases, including the α/β hydrolase domain, the G-H-S-Q-G pentapeptide, the catalytic triad (Ser106, Asp256, His278), a putative disulfide bond (Cys208–Cys262), and conserved calcium-binding residues (Asp239, Asp279), (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, using the SignalP 4.1 server, we identify that the first 27 amino acids form the signal peptide (Fig. 1a). LipGoM belongs to the lipase subfamily 1.1, clade 1.1a, as depicted in the phylogenetic tree presented in Fig. 1b. Structural data from the PDB indicate that LipGoM shares only 49.14 % identity with its closest homolog, the Pseudomonas aeruginosa lipase (PDB ID: 1EX9), highlighting the need to expand the three-dimensional information available for this protein family.

Upon analysis of the LifGoM sequence, using the Blast server, the alignment of sequences with the highest identity comprises the foldase from Pseudomonas sp. BMS12, Pseudomonas sp. ML96 and Pseudomonas alcaligenes (Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S6). In this alignment, we have a sequence identity below 40 % among homologous sequences, and only 19 out of an average of 340 residues are fully conserved among different foldases (Lifs), showing an apparent specificity regarding its lipase (Fig. S3), as was proposed in previous works [9]. Lif proteins have been reported to contain a highly conserved motif, RxxFDY(F/C)L(S/T), located near the N-terminal region (Fig. 1c), which has been proposed to play a key role in the interaction with the corresponding lipase [9,33,34]. Lif proteins are grouped into four families [9], with LifGoM classified within family 1, as shown in the phylogenetic tree produced in this work (Fig. 1d). Analysis of the Lif sequences used to construct the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1d), revealed the presence of additional motifs, such as “LxxF(D/E)Y(Y/F)(L/M)A(G/A)”, and “(R/K)xxL(N/E)Y(L/F)(V/L)S(G/D)” (Fig. 1d). These motifs are generally conserved within the same Lif family. However, notable variations still occur, even among closely related foldases. Our analysis reveals that the motif RxxFDY(F/C)L(S/T) is not fully conserved, as previously stated [33,35]. This variability suggests that the interaction between lipase and foldase may depend on family-specific sequence features, reinforcing the hypothesis that each Lip–Lif pair possesses distinct specificity within its genome [9,23,34]. A previous study described that the foldase from P. aeruginosa could not be substituted with foldases from B. glumae or Acinetobacter calcoaceticus. However, the P. aeruginosa foldase activated the lipase from Pseudomonas alcaligenes, which belongs to the same family [18]. There are only two complexes of lipases and their cognate foldases in the PDB: from Burkholderia plantarii (PDB ID: 2ES4) and from Acinetobacter baumannii (PDB ID: 9G8U). When analyzing their protein sequences, we observed a sequence identity of 32 % and a sequence similarity of 55.9 %. Notably, the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) among both complexes is relatively low, measuring 2.41 Å with the inclusion of 464 Cα atoms. This finding highlights a significant degree of structural conservation. However, due to the limited structural information available on lipase–foldase complexes, we cannot make definitive statements regarding the extent of this structural conservation.

Overexpression and solubilization of recombinant LipGoM

The LipGoM gene was cloned into the pET28a vector and expressed in the insoluble fraction of transformed cells of the E. coli BL21(DE3) strain. Upon evaluating the enzyme using a 12 % SDS-PAGE, a band with an approximate molecular mass of 35 kDa was detected, corresponding to LipGoM (Fig. 2a). From a 100 mL cell culture, the insoluble fraction was isolated and solubilized in 20 mL of denaturation buffer (see Materials and Methods) to obtain the soluble and unfolded enzyme (Fig. 2a).

Overexpression and purification of recombinant LifGoM

LifGoM was cloned into the pET28a vector and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. The enzyme was found in the soluble fraction and purified using nickel affinity chromatography. From 1 L of transformed cell culture, we obtained 15 mg of LifGoM. The purification process involved a two-step gradient in a His-Tag purification column, and the enzyme was eluted in the second step at a concentration of 500 mM imidazole. Analysis of the fractions using 12 % SDS-PAGE revealed a band of approximately 39 kDa corresponding to the expected molecular mass of LifGoM (Fig. 2b). The obtained foldase was subsequently utilized to assist in the refolding of LipGoM.

In vitro refolding of LipGoM assisted by LifGoM

Initially, we assessed the refolding of LipGoM and the hydrolytic activity of the refolded enzyme without the addition of LifGoM. We observed low enzymatic activity (see Fig. S5). This finding aligns with previous studies on lipases from Burkholderia cepacia, Pseudomonas sp., and Acinetobacter sp. XMZ-26, which are all members of lipase family I. When these lipases are expressed without their specific foldases, they tend to accumulate in the insoluble fraction and exhibit significantly reduced hydrolytic activity [9,18].

In this work, LipGoM was successfully obtained in its active form through a dilution refolding method that included LifGoM. We demonstrated that the addition of LifGoM significantly increased hydrolytic activity against p-NP esters with acyl chains ranging from C8 to C16, highlighting its essential role in the refolding and structural stabilization of LipGoM. These findings, along with previous works [8,15,20], suggest that specific foldases significantly enhance the solubility and activity of family 1 Pseudomonas lipases when they are expressed heterologously in E. coli BL21 (DE3).

The success of LipGoM refolding, achieved by mixing it with LifGoM, was influenced by the components and their concentrations in the refolding buffer. As previously reported, choosing the appropriate buffer is crucial for efficiently refolding lipases. We investigated the effects of various components in the refolding buffer to identify the optimal conditions. Our findings indicated that the 1:2 LipGoM:LifGoM molar ratio, combined with glycerol (up to 20 %), NaCl (200 mM), and CaCl2 (5 mM), resulted in the highest hydrolytic activity for the Lip-LifGoM mixture (Fig. S2 and Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S3, and S4). Other studies have shown that glycerol can enhance the yield of correctly folded lipases at concentrations ranging from 10 % to 45 % (v/v) [15]. Our results demonstrate that a 20 % (v/v) concentration of glycerol, in conjunction with LifGoM, promotes the highest hydrolytic activity of LipGoM. However, glycerol alone cannot replicate the effect of LifGoM on LipGoM activity, as both proteins precipitate in the absence of glycerol.

Previous studies have indicated that calcium chloride is an essential additive in the refolding buffer, as Ca2+ ions stabilize the active site of lipases from Family 1 [15]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that the absence of calcium in the refolding buffer led to a significant decrease in catalytic activity in the Lip-LifGoM mixture across all tested substrates. Our results align with earlier research on the lipase from Psychrobacter cryohalolentis K5T, which also requires calcium for an effective in vitro refolding process [15]. As a result of the assisted refolding process, we obtained a Lip-LifGoM mixture that exhibited a specific activity of 100 U/mg against p-NP-octanoate (Fig. 2, Fig. 3a, specific activity value used to calculate the relative activity for the remaining substrates used in this study (Fig. 3a). This activity is comparable to that of Lipomax, a commercially available lipase sourced from Pseudomonas alcaligenes, which is co-expressed in Pichia pastoris with its foldase and demonstrates a specific activity of 97.5 U/mg against pNP octanoate (C8) [8].

Biochemical characterization of LipGoM refolded in the presence of LifGoM

Substrate specificity for Lip-LifGoM mixture

The hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was evaluated using substrates of varying chain lengths, temperatures, and pH values to determine the optimal conditions for hydrolytic activity. Consistent with previous studies on Pseudomonas lipases, maximum activity was observed with pNP-octanoate (C8) [22,[36], [37], [38]]. The Lip-LifGoM mixture also showed the ability to hydrolyze other medium-length pNP substrates (C10-C12) and it could hydrolyze long-chain pNP substrates, which is uncommon in other Pseudomonas lipases (Fig. 3a) [22,26,36,38].

Effect of temperature and pH on Lip-LifGoM mixture activity

When analyzing the hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture at different pH values, the maximum activity was observed at pH 8 (Fig. 3b), using pNP octanoate (C8) as the substrate in a buffer composed of 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, and 50 mM NaCl. This behavior is characteristic of alkaline lipases, and is consistent with that of other homologous enzymes previously reported [22,26,36,39]. The hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was also evaluated over a temperature range of 30–60 °C, again using pNP-octanoate as the substrate. The maximum activity was recorded at 55 °C; however, at 60 °C, only 50 % of the maximum activity was maintained (Fig. 3c). Importantly, Lipomax shows an optimal activity at 40 °C [26].

Effect of calcium and magnesium, detergents, and organic solvents on Lip-LifGoM mixture activity

The hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was evaluated after incubation in the presence of each additive for 5 min at 4 °C. The activity of Lip-LifGoM mixture was increased by the presence of Mg2+ using two different concentrations, 1 mM and 5 mM. In contrast, in the case of Ca2+, the activity was positively affected only at 5 mM, but without reaching the relative activity obtained with 5 mM Ca2+. This observation indicates a prevalence of Mg2+ over Ca2+ for the Lip-LifGoM mixture hydrolytic activity, as was previously described in other lipases from family 1 [40,41]. In our study, the activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was significantly improved by adding Mg2+ and Ca2+. Additionally, the substantial decrease in activity observed when EDTA was added highlights the importance of the presence of specific metal ions in enhancing the hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture (Fig. 4a).

Effect of metal ions, detergents, and organic solvents on the hydrolytic activity of the LipGoM-LifGoM mixture. (a) Relative activity of the LipGoM-LifGoM mixture in the presence of various metal ions and EDTA. (b) Relative activity in the presence of detergents, including Triton X-100, sarkosyl, and SDS. (c) Relative activity in the presence of acetone, DMSO, acetonitrile, ethanol, isopropanol, and tert-butanol. In all cases, the hydrolytic activity assays were performed in 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) using pNP-octanoate as the substrate at 55 °C.

The effect of organic solvents on the hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4c, the enzyme demonstrated tolerance to 10 % and 30 % acetone, maintaining over 89 % of its original relative activity. However, isopropanol, DMSO, acetonitrile, ethanol, and tert-butanol at a 10 % v/v concentration inhibited the activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture by 46 %–68 %. When the concentration of DMSO, acetonitrile, and ethanol were increased to 30 % (v/v), the residual activities dropped to between 29 % and 43 % of the control activity in aqueous conditions. In contrast, when 30 % (v/v) acetone was added, the enzyme retained nearly 90 % of its original activity. Notably, isopropanol and tert-butanol significantly diminished the activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture (Fig. 4c). Considering the polarity index of the solvents tested under the mentioned conditions, a clear trend emerges as follows: the lower the polarity the lowest the hydrolytic activity measured.

Remarkably, the stability of the Lip-LifGoM mixture in acetone, a solvent commonly used in the hydrocarbon industry, must be considered for putative applications. However, it is essential to note the instability of the Lip-LifGoM mixture in solvents such as isopropanol, tert-butanol, and acetonitrile.

Lipases from Family 1 are well-regarded in the detergent industry, where variants with desirable properties are continuously explored [42,43].

[36,37]. Notable examples include lipases from Pseudomonas alcaligenes (Lipomax) and Pseudomonas mendocina (Lumafast) [42]. The hydrolytic activity of the Lip-LifGoM mixture was evaluated in the presence of three detergents, Triton X-100, sarkosyl, and SDS, at different concentrations (Fig. 4b). When the mixture was treated independently with the three detergents, the hydrolytic activity was negatively affected. Sarcosyl was the detergent that had the least effect on the mixture activity at the concentrations tested. Finally, we observed that SDS is the detergent that most strongly inhibits the Lip-LifGoM mixture activity at the highest concentration tested in the assay (Fig. 4b). These findings emphasize the Lip-LifGoM mixture's potential for industrial applications, particularly in the detergent industry.
