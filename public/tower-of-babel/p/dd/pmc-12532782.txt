Novice point-of-care ultrasound for the assessment of acute dyspnea in the emergency department
Authors: Joseph O’Brien, Kevin R. Caja, Matthew P. Tabbut, Jon W. Schrock
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDyspnea is one of the most common presentations to the ED. The goal of this study was to determine the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS examination completed by a medical student for several causes of dyspnea.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted in the ED of an academic center. Adult patients (≥ 18 yo) who presented to the emergency room with dyspnea were enrolled in the study. The study investigator was a medical student who was blinded to the clinical work up. Standardized POCUS examinations were performed following a modified BLUE protocol to identify the dyspnea etiology as one of the following diagnoses: asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, and non-diagnostic. POCUS diagnoses were compared to the clinical diagnosis made by the treating emergency physician.Results250 patients were enrolled in the study. 99 (39.6%) patients were male, and 136 (54.4%) patients identified as Black. POCUS demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, pneumonia, and pneumothorax. Concordance was optimal (0.8 < k < 1) for the diagnosis of asthma/COPD, acute decompensated heart failure; good (0.6 < k < 0.8) for pneumonia and non-diagnostic; and moderate (0.4 < k < 0.6) for pneumothorax and pulmonary embolism. Overall concordance was optimal (k = 0.84).ConclusionPOCUS examinations completed by a medical student demonstrated acceptable diagnostic accuracy for asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, and pneumonia. Medical student POCUS examinations could be used for risk stratification to identify patients that require additional diagnostic imaging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-025-00995-0.

Introduction

Dyspnea is one of the most common presenting symptoms in the emergency department. Numerous underlying diseases can present with a chief complaint of dyspnea, ranging from acute problems like pulmonary embolism to more chronic issues such as anemia [1, 2]. Due to the wide range of acuity, quickly and accurately determining the cause of the dyspnea is paramount. While physical examination and a thorough history can often elicit the cause of dyspnea, an estimated 30–50% of cases require further diagnostic evaluation [2]. Patients with dyspnea are often initially evaluated with a chest X-ray and may require subsequent chest CT scans, exposing the patient to radiation [3]. These diagnostic tools have limited use in pregnant women and children, as well as the critically injured patient. Thus, there is a need for an early diagnostic tool to guide intervention for the dyspneic patient who presents to the emergency room.

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) represents a diagnostic tool to determine the underlying etiology of a patient’s dyspnea and inform the appropriate intervention. Indeed, several studies have applied POCUS to evaluate dyspnea, with mixed results [4–6]. Several of these studies only examined cardiac etiologies for dyspnea [7, 8]whereas others were limited by small sample sizes [9, 10]. Moreover, most of these studies were conducted by expert sonographers with years of ultrasound experience [6]. While it is useful to know the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS from an experienced sonographer, there are relatively few fellowship trained emergency medicine physicians in ultrasound. Many institutions train emergency physicians in basic and advanced sonography techniques. However, emergency medicine physicians often face time constraints and a high caseload, requiring them to prioritize efficiently in their clinical decision-making process. In contrast, medical students typically have fewer responsibilities and more time to dedicate to tasks such as performing POCUS examinations. As a result, medical students can aid in the diagnosis and timely management of dyspnea patients in the emergency department by using POCUS. Thus, there is a need to determine the accuracy and prognostic value of POCUS completed by a novice sonographer in evaluating the dyspneic patient in the emergency room setting.

Methods

Study design

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Emergency Department of MetroHealth Medical Center between January 2024—April 2024. Patients eligible for inclusion included adults ≥ 18 years of age who presented to the emergency room with dyspnea listed as one of their complaints. Patients < 18 years of age, pregnant patients, non-English speaking patients, prisoners, and those with altered mental status were excluded from the study. The study investigator was blinded to the patient’s clinical workup and only saw the chief complaint recorded in the triage note before scanning the patient. Demographic data, medical history, clinical data, and ultrasound findings were collected. Based on ultrasound findings, a diagnosis was made by the study investigator. The following diagnoses were considered: asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, and non-diagnostic. At a later time point, medical interventions in the emergency department, clinical diagnosis, and outcomes were collected through chart review. The final composite clinical diagnosis as determined by the treating emergency medicine physician was obtained through chart review and used as the gold standard to compare to the ultrasound diagnosis. A secondary analysis was performed to examine the time it took to perform the POCUS examination compared to the time it took to obtain a chest X-ray (CXR). Time-to-CXR was chosen because CXR is a commonly ordered diagnostic test for the dyspnea work up and strongly influences the clinical diagnosis. In other words, time-to-CXR acts as proxy for the time it takes the clinician to make a clinical diagnosis. In total, 250 patients were enrolled in this study. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board, ensuring compliance with ethical standards and regulatory requirements.

Ultrasound protocol

The study investigator was a fourth-year medical student who completed a one-month ultrasound clerkship to obtain basic proficiency. Each POCUS examination was performed using a multiprobe machine (Phillips Sparq Diagnostic Ultrasound System, 2014) following a systematic, standardized protocol. A modified bedside lung ultrasound examination (BLUE) protocol [2] was developed through consultation with fellowship-trained emergency medicine physicians in ultrasound (Supplemental Fig. 1). In this modified BLUE protocol, lung ultrasound was performed with a 6- to 13-MHz linear probe using longitudinal scans on the anterior chest, midaxillary line, and posterior (PLAPS point) line. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed with 1- to 5-MHz curved array probe to assess the heart in three windows—the parasternal long axis, apical, and subxiphoid view. POCUS examination was performed with the patient in supine or near-to-supine position.

Study definitions

Ultrasound diagnoses were made following our standardized protocol (Supplemental Fig. 1). Pulmonary edema was diagnosed with the presence of lung sliding with a B-profile, or at least 3 B lines which emanate from the pleural line, move with lung sliding, and extend to the periphery of the field [1]. Pneumonia was diagnosed with the presence of shred sign, lung consolidation, hepatization of the lung, abolished lung sliding due to thickened pleura, dynamic air bronchograms, confluent B-lines, and/or subpleural consolidations [1]. Pulmonary embolism was diagnosed with the presence of D-sign indicating right ventricle strain [2]. Asthma or COPD were diagnosed as the presence of A-profile with lung sliding and/or atelectasis with static bronchograms in a patient with a suggestive medical history. Pneumothorax was diagnosed with the absence of lung sliding and identification of a lung point [1]. A non-diagnostic, or rule-out, diagnosis was made in the absence of any of the above findings.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics are described as frequency counts and percentages, mean ± standard deviation, or median and the interquartile range, as appropriate based on the univariable data distribution. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy, positive likelihood ratio, and negative likelihood ratio were determined for asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, and non-diagnostic finding for POCUS by comparing to the ultimate clinical diagnosis made in the emergency department. Statistical analysis was conducted in R (Linux version 4.1.0, http://www.r-project.org/) using the dyplyr package. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Study population

Two-hundred and fifty patients with dyspnea included in their chief complaint were evaluated using POCUS. Ninety-nine (39.6%) patients who participated were men (Table 1). The majority of patients self-identified as Black, with White and Hispanic as the second and third most common groups, respectively. Eighty-seven (34.8%) patients were tachypneic and 75 (30%) patients were tachycardic upon initial presentation to the emergency department. Forty-nine (19.6%) patients had low oxygen saturation measurements (SpO2 < 92%) upon initial presentation. Patients presented with a variety of past comorbidities, with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and prior pneumonia as the three most common comorbidities. During the ED evaluation, 219 (87.6%) patients underwent CXR, 49 (19.6%) underwent CT imaging, and 22 (8.8%) underwent formal ECHO imaging.

Ultrasound findings

Of the 250 patients who underwent POCUS examination, 152 (60.8%) were negative for any pathology, while 98 (39.2%) were positive for pathology. In our study, the most common final composite diagnosis was a non-diagnostic (n = 121), or rule-out diagnosis, in which they had normal sonography findings and had another diagnosis causing their shortness of breath outside of the etiologies included in this study. (Table 2). Other causes of shortness of breath not included in the study varied widely, including acute coronary syndrome, COVID-19, influenza, and anxiety. These etiologies were all categorized together as a “non-diagnostic” finding because they lack any abnormal pathology that can be reliably detected by sonography. The most common pathological finding was asthma/COPD exacerbation, occurring in 66 (26.4%) patients. Acute decompensated heart failure was the second most common pathology, occurring in 32 (12.8%) of patients. Pneumothorax and pulmonary embolism were relatively rare pathologies, occurring in 1 and 5 patients, respectively.

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, and accuracy of POCUS for the various dyspnea etiologies are reported in Table 2. POCUS demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy across various etiologies of dyspnea, with sensitivity ranging from 60.0 to 100.0% and specificity ranging from 93.0 to 99.2%. POCUS showed excellent performance in diagnosing asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, and non-diagnostic cases, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 80.00% and 90.0%, respectively. While sensitivity for pneumonia and pulmonary embolism was moderate (80% and 60%, respectively), specificity remained high (> 97%). Pneumothorax was identified with 100% sensitivity and 99.20% specificity.

Concordance between POCUS diagnosis and clinical diagnosis

The receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 1) demonstrates the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS examinations performed by a medical student for identifying various causes of dyspnea. The area under the curve (AUC) was highest for pneumothorax (0.996), indicating near-perfect discrimination, followed by asthma/COPD (0.921) and acute heart failure (0.902), reflecting excellent diagnostic accuracy. POCUS also showed strong performance for pneumonia (0.887) and non-diagnostic cases (0.882). The lowest AUC was observed for pulmonary embolism (0.796), though it still demonstrated acceptable discrimination. Overall, the ROC curves indicate that POCUS examinations conducted by a medical student achieved good-to-excellent diagnostic accuracy across most dyspnea etiologies.

In addition to providing accurate findings, POCUS examinations can be obtained quickly. The mean time it took to complete a POCUS scan in this study was 10.4 min, compared to the 102.1 min it took, on average, to obtain a CXR. The time to POCUS diagnosis was significantly shorter than the time it took to obtain a CXR (P < 0.0001, Welch’s Two Sample t-test).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that POCUS examinations completed by a novice sonographer offer reasonably accurate prognostic value. Of the 250 scans completed, 210 (84%) correctly identified the underlying diagnosis. This study demonstrated that ultrasound diagnosis was concordant with the clinical diagnosis, with a high concordance value (k = 0.84) validating POCUS examination completed by a novice sonographer as a reliable early diagnosis tool in the dyspneic patient. The significant reduction in POCUS examination time compared to the time it took to obtain a CXR further illustrates the potential role of medical student-led POCUS examination in expediting diagnosis and early treatment.

The most common pathology found in this study was asthma/COPD (26.4% of diagnoses). The sensitivity and specificity were similar to what have been reported in the published literature [6, 11]. Similarly, POCUS exhibited favorable sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV for acute heart failure and pneumonia. The specificity for acute heart failure was comparable to the pooled data (99.08% vs. 96%), but the sensitivity is lower compared to studies with POCUS examinations conducted by ultrasound experts (81.25% vs. 95%).5–8 Similarly, the sensitivity for diagnosing pneumonia was lower (80% vs. 92%) than published studies conducted by ultrasound experts [6, 12–15].

Medical student POCUS examinations were highly specific (99%) for pulmonary embolism but demonstrated poor sensitivity (60%). The low sensitivity may be due to our standardized ultrasound protocol not performing a compression ultrasound to look for DVT. This compression ultrasound was not performed because it is more invasive and would require a chaperone to be present in the room while the medical student scanned the great saphenous vein; this increase in emergency department resources and personnel was antithetical to the goals of this study.

In non-diagnostic cases, POCUS demonstrated moderate sensitivity (83%) and specificity (93%), with a low negative likelihood ratio (0.18), indicating its ability to effectively rule out certain conditions. Ruling out cases with POCUS could reduce the need for CXR, which would reduce cost, time spent in the emergency department, and unnecessary radiation exposure. Overall, these findings underscore the potential of POCUS as a valuable diagnostic tool in the evaluation of dyspnea, offering clinicians a rapid and accurate means of assessing various underlying pathologies.

There are several limitations with this study. For example, it can be questioned whether the study investigator would still be considered a novice sonographer after 250 scans. However, it should be noted that the study investigator only received 1-month of formal sonography training. Over the course of the 250 scans, it is possible that the investigator was repeating the same mistakes. Over the first 125 scans, the study investigator accurately identified the cause of the dyspnea in 102 (81.6%) instances. Over the subsequent 125 scans, the study investigator correctly identified the etiology in 113 (90.4%) instances, indicating a modest improvement in performance.

This study was conducted during influenza season, leading to many flu patients receiving non-diagnostic scans. This study may have also excluded the most acutely ill patients. Because informed consent is required to participate, acutely ill patients who were unconscious or had an altered mental status were ineligible for study inclusion. These patients would likely have had pathologic sonographic findings but were unable to be captured in the study population. Moreover, some patients who were acutely dyspneic declined to participate due to their condition, which may have influenced the results. The low number of pneumothorax and pulmonary embolism makes it difficult to draw conclusions on the diagnostic accuracy of medical student POCUS for these conditions. To overcome these limitations, we enrolled a large number of patients to try to capture a wide range of dyspnea etiologies. Out of the 49 published studies investigating the use of POCUS for determining the cause of dyspnea, this is the fourth largest study [6].

This study demonstrated the important role of novice-conducted POCUS in the emergency room setting for assessing the etiology of dyspnea. The study showed good concordance between the POCUS diagnosis and clinical diagnosis, with the POCUS examination occurring much faster than the standard clinical work-up of obtaining a CXR for the dyspneic patient. The significant reduction in time is highly valuable, as a rapid diagnosis and early intervention can be critical for a patient’s prognosis. Moreover, the reasonable prognostic values of medical student POCUS for asthma/COPD, acute heart failure, and pneumonia illustrate a potential role for medical students or other novice sonographers in early diagnosis and risk stratification to identify patients who should undergo further imaging. Future work should attempt to replicate this study with additional medical students acting as sonographers to determine the inter-user variability.
