Retina-specific long non-coding RNAs associated with inherited retinal disease genes
Authors: Emma Delanote, Karla Alejandra Ruiz-Ceja, Alfredo Dueñas Rey, Dalila Capasso, Manon Bouckaert, Martina Sofia, Martina Di Guida, Mario Failli, Victor López Soriano, Hanne Lenaerts, Kelly Lemeire, Birthe Dorgau, Jasper Verwilt, Sarah De Keulenaer, Filip Van Nieuwerburgh, Manon Huizing, Sarah Glorieux, Pieter-Jan Volders, Eva D’haene, Elfride De Baere, Majlinda Lako, Katrien Remaut, Nicolina Cristina Sorrentino, Pieter Mestdagh, Diego di Bernardo, Steve Lefever, Frauke Coppieters, Sandro Banfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long non‑coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are increasingly recognized as important regulatory molecules, yet their roles remain largely unexplored in many tissues. This study aimed to identify and characterize lncRNAs involved in human retinal biology and inherited retinal disease (IRD), a leading cause of blindness worldwide. By integrating retinal tissue-specificity, location in IRD gene topologically associating domains (TADs), and co-expression analyses, we identified a set of 126 lncRNAs that are potentially regulating IRD genes. In-depth characterization of eleven selected lncRNAs, one of which is novel (LINC03128), allowed us to dissect their transcriptional units and cell type-specific expression, and revealed significant associations with visual function. Furthermore, knockdown of LINC03127 in human retinal explants and retinal organoids resulted in marked downregulation of photoreceptor-related gene pathways and pointed to a potential regulatory interaction with ABCA4, a major IRD gene located in cis. Collectively, our study provides the first systematic assessment of lncRNAs in the human retina and establishes a comprehensive resource of candidate regulatory lncRNAs with potential relevance to retinal function and disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06259-1.

Introduction

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are key regulatory transcripts that do not encode for a protein [1]. Although 30,000–60,000 human lncRNA loci are known [2, 3], only a small fraction has been functionally characterized. LncRNAs function either in cis by regulating nearby gene expression or in trans by modulating distant genes, nuclear architecture, proteins or other RNAs. They participate in crucial cellular processes such as chromatin and splicing regulation, transcription, and translation [4–6], however, their roles in human health and disease remain largely unexplored.

This is particularly true for the human retina, a neuronal tissue lining the back of our eye that is responsible for processing incoming light. For only a few lncRNAs, a retinal function has been established in either physiological [7–10] or pathological conditions [11–13]. The best-characterized lncRNA in the retina is MALAT1 [11, 14], which was reported as a marker for healthy rod photoreceptors and a potential target for retinal preservation [15, 16]. Furthermore, little is known about the contribution of lncRNAs to inherited retinal disease (IRD), a significant cause of vision loss and blindness. IRDs are a genetically heterogeneous group of Mendelian conditions caused primarily by progressive degeneration of photoreceptor cells [17]. Although many pathways are known to contribute to IRD disease progression [18], a precise knowledge of the molecular events and genes that play a role in photoreceptor loss is still poorly defined. In that respect, a major knowledge gap lies in the role of lncRNAs. The typical low abundance, highly cell type-specific expression and rapid evolution across species of lncRNAs hamper the identification of biologically relevant lncRNAs and downstream functional characterization [19–22].

Recent advancements in experimental techniques on human post-mortem retinal tissue and computational analyses hold the potential to deepen our understanding of species- and tissue-specific lncRNAs. Here, we report the use of two independent and complementary strategies to unravel lncRNAs potentially regulating IRD genes. The first one aimed to identify lncRNAs with a retina-specific expression and location in the same topologically associating domains (TADs) as IRD genes. In the second strategy, we reconstructed the gene co-expression networks of lncRNAs that may modulate the expression of IRD genes.

This dual approach resulted in a set of 126 lncRNAs potentially regulating IRD genes, eleven of which were selected for further characterization. Finally, a loss-of-function evaluation for LINC03127, located in cis of the major IRD gene ABCA4, supported its role in photoreceptor functions and potential ABCA4 regulation. Overall, this study represents the first systematic assessment of lncRNAs in the human retina and offers a set of lncRNAs with a potential role in IRD gene regulation and retinal biology.

Materials and methods

RNA-seq datasets

Table S1 contains an overview of all RNA-seq datasets re-analyzed in this study. Raw sequence data derived from 53 tissues/cell lines of the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project [23] were downloaded as Short Read Archive (SRA) data from the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP accession number phs000424.v8.p2) using the SRA-Toolkit (v2.8.2) and converted into FASTQ format. For each tissue, a selection of samples with the highest number of sequenced bases was selected, and, where applicable, a balance between sexes was maintained (max 200 samples per tissue).

Next, we retrieved paired-end FASTQ files derived from post-mortem retina samples generated by Ratnapriya et al. [24, 25] (GSE115828). Of the 453 samples that passed quality control, we only considered donor retinas that showed no age-related macular degeneration progression in downstream analyses (n = 102; EyeGEx dataset) (Table S1). A second retinal dataset consisted of sequencing data from 50 human retinas from non-visually impaired post-mortem donors [26] (TIGEM dataset; Table S1).

Transcriptome assembly and quantification

RNA-seq reads were aligned by the Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) aligner (v.2.6.0) [27] against the GRCh38 Ensembl human genome reference. The obtained BAM files were then used as input for de novo transcript assembly by StringTie (v1.3.5) [28] using default parameters. For each tissue of the EyeGEx, TIGEM and GTEx datasets, the resulting GTF files were combined with GffCompare (v0.11.6) [29] using -C -A -X –K flags into a single GTF, which was then filtered for transcripts supported by at least two samples, creating a single set of assembled transcripts with a minimum length of 50 nucleotides. Next, we used GffCompare to merge all filtered GTF files of each tissue into a unified and non-redundant pan-tissue transcriptome (Ensembl Human Release v101 [30]). The index for the pan-tissue transcriptome was built by Kallisto (v0.44.0) [31].

Selection of lncRNAs located in IRD TADs and tissue-specificity analysis

Using genome-wide in situ Hi-C data of four human retinal donors, the TAD landscape of the retina was recently established [32]. For this, 25 kb boundaries were used to determine the retinal TADs across the human genome, defining a TAD as the region between two consecutive boundaries (including the boundaries).

Next, we selected a subset of 218 nuclear-encoded genes from RetNet (https://sph.uth.edu/retnet/; accessed February 2023) with an established causal role in monogenic forms of IRDs for further analyses (Table S2). For all 218 IRD genes, the corresponding TADs, containing one or more IRD genes, were selected (Table S3). Within these TADs, all transcripts corresponding to Ensembl Biotype ‘lncRNA’, as well as transcripts not corresponding to any annotated gene (GffCompare classification code ‘u’ unknown/intergenic) [29], were extracted to evaluate both annotated and potentially novel lncRNAs. To enable filtering of the annotated lncRNAs, transcripts with the GffCompare classification code ‘=’ (complete, exact match of intron chain), ‘c’ (contained in reference), or ‘k’ (containment of reference) were selected [29].

Using the pan-tissue transcriptome described above, all transcripts of each tissue were re-quantified using Kallisto (v0.44.0). Next, using the re-quantified data, a Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) score was calculated at the transcript level [33]. This JSD score is a measure of tissue-specific expression of the transcript, with values ranging from 0 to 1, from ubiquitous to tissue-specific expression, respectively. This analysis was performed for the EyeGEx and GTEx datasets as well as for the TIGEM and GTEx datasets. The resulting datasets contain for each transcript the JSD score and the accompanying tissue in which the transcript is enriched. For downstream filtering, the comparison between EyeGEx and GTEx was used.

In the downstream selection process, public available data on retinal transcription and regulation was interrogated, including CAGE data (FANTOM5 [34]), adult human retina single-cell Nuc-seq, adult human retina ATAC-seq [35], and ChIP-seq of three major retinal transcription factors (OTX2, CRX and NRL) [35]. Figure S1 contains an overview of the re-quantification and tissue-specificity strategy.

RNA-seq meta-analysis and co-expression analysis

To identify lncRNAs significantly co-expressed with IRD genes, we used RNA-seq samples from the TIGEM and the EyeGEx datasets with a coverage of at least ten million reads [24–26]. Gene counts were retrieved using featureCounts (v1.6.0) [36] with the default parameters. Counts per million (CPM) normalization was performed using edgeR (v.3.38.4) [37]. The EyeGEx and TIGEM datasets were evaluated independently for the co-expression analyses of IRD genes [38]. The weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) R package (v1.69) [39] was used for data clean-up. The Pearson correlation score was calculated to retrieve the top 1% of positively co-expressed genes (99th percentile threshold) with each of the 218 IRD genes and of the selected lncRNAs per analyzed dataset. The 99th percentile threshold corresponded to Pearson r values ranging from 0.61 to 0.81 across REIR-lncRNAs (mean r = 0.71) (Figure S1).

Functional prediction analysis

To infer the putative biological function of the selected lncRNAs co-expressed with IRD genes, clusterProfiler (v4.4.4) [40] was exploited using Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) and cellular component (CC) on the related co-expression gene lists. For the TIGEM and EyeGEx datasets, we used background lists of 19,976 and 18,151 genes, respectively. The above numbers correspond to the total number of genes detected in the two different datasets, namely those passing the expression filtering criteria. A False Discovery Rate (FDR) was used as the p.adjust method, with a chosen cut-off of 0.05.

Human retina sample collection

Residual material from human donors without a history or clinical evidence of retinal disease was collected from either the Ghent (Ghent, Belgium) or Antwerp (“Biobank Antwerpen”, Antwerp, Belgium; ID: BE 71030031000”) University Hospital in compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and under ethical approval of the Ethics Committee of the Ghent University Hospital (2018/1072, B670201837286). Table S4 describes all donors included. Eyes were transported in CO2 Independent Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) until dissection. After visual inspection to exclude any cross-contamination with RPE, the retinas were immediately processed (creation of 5 mm retinal explants for lncRNA knockdown) or snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. For single-molecule RNA in situ hybridization (smRNA-ISH), the back of the eye was dissected and fixated in buffered neutral formaldehyde for 16–24 h. In addition, human retinal explants were fixed-frozen by fixation in 4% PFA for two hours, overnight immersion in 30% sucrose, three hours immersion in 1/1 ratio 30% sucrose and Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) embedding media, followed by immersion in OCT and frozen on dry ice. Moreover, human retina sections embedded in paraffin (Catalog N.: T2234110) were also acquired from BioChain Institute Inc. (Newark, California, Lot N.: C707001 corresponding to a 74-year-old female donor).

RNA extraction

RNA samples were extracted using the RNeasy Mini kit or miRNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following manufacturer’s guidelines, followed by DNase treatment (ArcticZymes, Tromsø, Norway). The RNA samples were obtained from post-mortem adult human retina and RPE (Table S4), human fibroblasts, lung cells and podocytes (generous gifts from Drs. L. Staiano, L. Galietta and B. Franco) and human retinal explants (see below). In addition, blood samples were collected and stored in a Tempus™ Blood RNA Tube (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA from blood samples was extracted using the Tempus Spin RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The RNA samples were used for RT-(q)PCR and/or (long- or short-read) RNA-seq.

Long-read RNA-seq

Total RNA from post-mortem adult human retina (n = 3; Table S4) was subjected to polyA capture (NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA magnetic isolation module, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) followed by direct-cDNA library preparation according to the supplier's protocol (SQK-DCS109, ONT). Multiple reactions were performed for each sample, sequentially using the same elution solution, to obtain sufficient polyA RNA. Each library was then loaded on a flow cell (FLO-PRO002, ONT) for 72 h. MinKNOW (v5.1.0) was used to generate fast5 files, which were then basecalled with Guppy (v6.1.5) to yield FASTQ files (number of passed reads with minimal Q-score of 7: 10,206,706; 18,463,865; 11,388,116, respectively and reads N50: 962; 968; 1060 bases; respectively). Reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using minimap2 (v2.17-r941) [41] with the -ax splice flags to allow spliced alignments. Alignment files were converted to BAM format, sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.15) [42]. StringTie2 (v2.1.1) [43] was run for transcriptome assembly using the -L parameter, and a custom retinal transcriptome annotation, based on the short-read integration, was used as a guide. For the interrogation of the transcript structure of the selected lncRNAs, transcripts present in at least two of the three donors and a TPM (transcripts per million) > 1 were considered.

In silico assessment of lncRNA expression in human retina

For an initial in silico assessment of the cellular distribution of the lncRNAs identified in this study, we relied on Spectacle, a publicly available tool (https://singlecell-eye.org/app/spectacle) comprising several preprocessed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) datasets from different eye tissues and different organisms [44, 45].

The novel LINC03128 lncRNA was not included in the datasets in Spectacle, hence published scRNA-seq data of human adult retinal data [15] (n = 3) was re-analyzed for evaluating the expression of this lncRNA at the single-cell level. Raw FASTQ files were retrieved from ArrayExpress (E-MTAB‐7316). Kallisto bus (v0.46.1) was used to pseudo-align the reads to the reference transcriptome (GRCh38), to which the lncRNA transcripts were added. Bustools (v0.41.0) [46] were then run for correction of barcodes, collapsing of UMIs, and assignment of reads to genes to count matrices. Expression matrices were processed using SCANPY (v1.9.6) [47]. Preprocessing and quality control were conducted to remove outlier cells. Briefly, we only considered genes with counts in at least three cells and filtered out cells that had unique feature counts of < 200 or > 2,500 and/or that expressed > 10% mitochondrial counts. The data were then total-count normalized, logarithmized, filtered for highly variable features, and scaled to unit variance. After quality control preprocessing, merging, and batch correction, a total of 24,673 retinal cells were kept for dimensionality reduction and clustering. Markers associated with major neural retina cell populations (rods -RHO, PDE6A-, cones -ARR3, OPN1LW-, retinal ganglion cells -SNCG-, Müller glia -RLBP1-, amacrine cells -CHAT-, bipolar cells -VSX2-, horizontal cells -ONECUT1-, retinal astrocytes -GFAP-, microglia -HLA, DPA1-) were used for annotation and assessment of lncRNA cell-type specificity.

In silico evaluation of coding potential

Evaluation of coding potential was performed by visual inspection of the smoothed PhyloCSF [48] tracks in the UCSC genome browser and calculation of coding-potential scores by PLEK (v1.2) [49] and CPC2 [50] using default parameters. As input, a FASTA file was used containing the transcript sequences of the top three highest expressed transcripts in the short-read RNA-seq (EyeGEx) as well as the long-read RNA-seq data for each lncRNA.

RT-(q)PCR for validation and expression analysis of identified lncRNAs

RT-(q)PCR was applied to validate the lncRNAs, evaluate their expression across different human tissues (retina, RPE, blood cells, fibroblasts, lung cells and podocytes) and to validate novel isoforms and exons. Retina and RPE RNA samples were obtained from non-visually impaired post-mortem donors (Table S4) and RNA samples from human blood cells, fibroblasts, lung cells and podocytes were used as controls. cDNA was obtained using either the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) or the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Exon-exon junction-directed real-time PCR primers were designed using OligoCalc [51] or Primer3Plus [52] and synthesized by Eurofins Genomics (Ebersberg, Germany) or Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA). Primer sequences are listed in Table S5. RT-qPCR experiments were performed using the LightCycler 480 SYBR GREEN I Master Mix (Roche) or the Advanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and run on a LightCycler 480 System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Data were analyzed with qbase+ [53] and normalized to at least two housekeeping genes (YWHAZ, HPRT1, HMBS or SDHA). RT-qPCR data of retinal and non-retinal tissues were normalized using HPRT1. The qPCR amplicon lengths were assessed on a Fragment Analyser using the DNF-915 Reagent Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

To validate the extended 3’ UTR of NR2E3, primers spanning the extended 3’ UTR were designed (Table S5). RT-PCR was performed on retina cDNA samples using KAPA2G Robust HotStart ReadyMix (Sopachem Life Sciences, Ede, the Netherlands) followed by Sanger sequencing of the PCR amplicons using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Single-molecule RNA in situ hybridization

To determine the expression of the lncRNAs within the human retina, we carried out RNAscope or BASEscope approaches (Advanced Cell Diagnostic, Newark, CA, USA), depending on the size of the transcript, according to the manufacturer’s protocol [54]. smRNA-ISH was performed using the BaseScope Detection reagent kit (ACD, no. 323910), the RNAscope 2.5 HD Duplex Assay (ACD, no. 322430), or the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent v2 reagent kit (ACD, no. 323285). Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) and fixed-frozen sections were dehydrated, post fixated and pre-treated, including target retrieval at 95–100 °C or overnight at 60 °C according to customized protocols. After pre-treatment, if applicable, tissue sections were incubated with rabbit polyclonal antibody to human Cone Arrestin (1:50; #LS‑B9272, LifeSpan BioSciences, Lynnwood, WA, USA) or to human GNAT1 (1:50; #sc-389, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) in co-detection antibody diluent (#323160, Advanced Cell Diagnostic, Newark, CA, USA) and then incubated with RNAscope Protease III (ACD, no. 322380) at 40 °C. Tissue sections used for the co-staining of the lncRNA and IRD gene mRNA were bleached in a 1X citrate buffer (pH6) for 4 h using the MERSCOPE Photobleacher (Vizgen, Cambridge, MA, USA). Probes (information available upon request) were applied to sections; then the amplification cascade was performed as stated in the manufacturer’s protocol and detected by using TSA Vivid Fluorophor 520 (ACD.323271, for RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent v2), or HRP and Fast Red stains (for RNAscope 2.5 and BASEscope, included in kit). After smRNA-ISH protocols, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies, including Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; Molecular Probes; Eugene, OR, USA). Sections were counterstained with DAPI, mounted with GEL/MOUNT (Biomeda, Foster City, CA, USA), analysed with a ZEISS confocal microscope LSM800 or LSM880 with AiryScan and viewed by Zen blue software. For the quantification of the tissue sections co-stained with lncRNA and IRD gene mRNA, four to five regions per condition originating from one donor were analyzed using the ZEISS Arivis software.

Evaluation of transcription factor binding motifs and mining of chromatin profiling data for LINC03127

In order to compare the transcription factors (TFs) underlying differential regulation of ABCA4 and LINC03127, predicted sites for TF binding profiles in the JASPAR CORE collection (JASPAR 2022 TFBS) were retrieved for both ABCA4 and lncRNA promoters, as defined by 550 bp upstream of their transcription start sites and supported by the integration of multiple multi-omics datasets derived from the human retina [35, 55]. A custom R script was then used to compute the number of binding sites per TF in each orientation and for each promoter.

Chromatin profiling data derived from human and murine retina were retrieved from the studies of D’haene et al. [32] and Hughes et al. [56]. In particular, domainograms and contact intensity profiles around the viewpoints of interest (ABCA4 and LINC03127 promoters) were created from normalized UMI-4 C profiles using the umi4cpackage (v0.0.1; Accessed August 15, 2023) [57]. In addition, processed bigWig files containing normalized ATAC-seq reads, consensus peaks and normalized RNA-seq reads derived from different murine cell types (i.e., wild-type rods, wild-type green cones, and Nrl−/− blue cone photoreceptors) were downloaded from GEO (GSE83312), converted into bedGraph files using bigWigtoBedGraph UCSC utility [58], and filtered for the syntenic locus spanning the region between the Arhgap29 and Abca4 genes.

Human retinal explant and retinal organoid culture and antisense oligonucleotide-mediated LINC03127 knockdown

From each human retina sample, twelve explants (5 mm diameter) were created and cultured in 12-well plates with Neurobasal-A medium supplemented with 2% B-27, 2% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine, products supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). To keep a relatively uniform distribution of retinal cell types, explants were taken from peri-macular regions equidistant from the macula. Free-floating explants were treated (free uptake, 10 µM) with different locked nucleic acid (LNA)-modified antisense oligonucleotides (gapmer ASOs, Table S6), synthesized by Eurogentec (Liege, Belgium) or Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) or a nontargeting control ASO (NTC ASO) as negative control. Retinal explants were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2, prior to RNA isolation, RT-qPCR to validate lncRNA knockdown and RNA-seq. An NCBI BLAST was performed (allowing up to three mismatches, maximum identified complementarity of two mismatches) to identify potential off-target genes, resulting in a maximum complementarity of two mismatches (Table S6).

Retinal organoids were generated from induced pluripotent stem cells derived from a healthy control (female, age 69), following the protocol described by Hallam et al. [59], which favors cone photoreceptor development. LINC03127 knockdown was performed on two independent batches of retinal organoids. On day 188 (batch 1) and day 199 (batch 2), the organoids were treated (free uptake, 10µM) with one of two gapmer ASOs (ASO4 or ASO6) or the NTC ASO. Half of the medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. Three weeks after treatment, three pools of two (batch 1) or four pools of three (batch 2) organoids were collected in trizol and RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), followed by RT-qPCR to assess the extent of lncRNA knockdown. In addition, three weeks after treatment, five retinal organoids per condition were collected for immunohistochemistry. Retinal organoids were fixed, cryosectioned and stained for RHO (MAB5356, 1:200; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and OPN1MW/LW (AB5405, 1:200; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) following the protocol described by Hallam et al. [59]. Images were acquired using Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

The RNA samples underwent Poly(A) RNA-seq to evaluate the transcriptomic consequences of LINC03127 knockdown. Prior to library preparation, RNA integrity and purity were assessed on a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the HS RNA analysis DNF-472–0500 kit and concentrations were determined using the Qubit RNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit on a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Libraries were generated following the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation protocol (Illumina, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit, 2 × 100 cycles or NovaSeq 6000 S1 Reagent Kit, 2 × 100 cycles (Illumina, The Netherlands, Eindhoven)). The average number of read pairs per sample was 75.6 million (minimum: 6.9, maximum: 216.6).

Transcripts were quantified through pseudo-alignment by Kallisto (v.0.46.1) using default parameters to build the Kallisto index (derived from the Ensembl human GRCh38 including the lncRNA) and subsequent transcript quantification. Abundance estimates were then imported into R, and differential expression was inferred using DESeq2 (v1.36.0) [60]. Genes with a count below one were removed from the analysis. Significance for differential expression was accepted at an FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted) p < 0.05. To identify overrepresented GO terms and infer possible enriched pathways among the sets of differentially expressed genes, enrichment analyses were performed using ClusterProfiler (v4.4.4) [40]. Differentially expressed genes at the Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05 level was used as input, and only genes expressed in the analyzed samples were part of the reference background list. First, the enrichment analysis was performed for all differentially expressed genes, followed by separate analyses for the up- and downregulated genes. Differential transcript usage of ABCA4 was analyzed using DRIMSeq [61]. Lowly expressed transcripts were filtered by requiring a minimum expression of ten counts in at least two samples, and statistical significance was set at an FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted) p < 0.05.

Results

Analysis and selection of lncRNAs potentially regulating IRD genes

We used a dual approach − (1) colocalization within IRD gene TADs coupled to tissue-specificity and (2) co-expression analysis − to identify lncRNAs with a potential role in the regulation of IRD genes. Figure 1 summarizes both strategies and the subsequent characterization of eleven selected lncRNAs, which we termed Retina Enriched IRD gene-Related lncRNAs (REIR-lncRNAs).

Identification of retina-specific transcripts located in IRD gene TADs

To identify both known and novel lncRNAs that are exclusively or predominantly expressed in the human retina, we re-analyzed RNA-seq data of 152 healthy adult post-mortem retinal transcriptomes [24–26] and of 53 tissues from the GTEx project (5–200 samples/tissue, 7,294 transcriptomes in total) [23]. Upon pseudo-alignment of all transcriptomes, reassembly was performed per tissue, generating 54 tissue-specific transcriptomes, which were then merged into a single pan-tissue transcriptome.

Next, a Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) specificity score was calculated at the transcript level across all tissues [33], which represents a measure of tissue-specific expression. Each transcript included in the novel pan-tissue transcriptome was assigned a JSD score, which is linked to the tissue in which the transcript shows the highest level of enrichment. The highest JSD score was found for a transcript in the retina (max JSD: 0.97), followed by cells of a leukemia cell line (CML, max JSD: 0.93), testis (max JSD: 0.91) and liver (max JSD: 0.91; Figure S2, Table S7). The median JSD value of lncRNAs (0.27) is significantly higher than that of protein-coding transcripts (0.25) in the retina (EyeGEx) (Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test, p-value: 2.20E-16; Table S7). Figure 2 shows the expression profile and JSD scores of both retina-specific and ubiquitously expressed (non-coding) genes, supporting the strength of using the JSD score to identify retina-specific transcripts. Importantly, this analysis provided a tissue-specificity score across all human tissues analysed, allowing to identify novel, tissue-specific (lncRNA) transcripts outside human retina as well. The tissue-specificity analysis is publicly accessible through the following URL: https://target-ugent.be/retina-specificity (Figure S3).

As this study aimed to identify lncRNAs regulating IRD genes, we hypothesized that lncRNAs acting in cis on IRD genes should be located in the same TAD. Therefore, we extracted all transcripts from annotated lncRNAs as well as all novel transcripts located within the TADs of 218 IRD genes (Table S2), established using in situ Hi-C on adult human retina [32]. In total, 242 retinal TADs contain an IRD gene, with eighteen containing two IRD genes (Table S3). In addition, 40 IRD genes span two or three TADs.

For subsequent lncRNA prioritization, a distinction was made between annotated lncRNAs and novel transcripts. First, we evaluated 785 annotated lncRNAs located in IRD gene TADs. Of these, 107 displayed predominant retinal expression. To select biologically relevant, highly retina-enriched lncRNAs, transcripts with a top 25% JSD score (JSD > 0.35) and TPM above 1 were retained, resulting in 37 transcripts from 26 lncRNAs (Table S8). Of this set, four multi-exonic lncRNAs - OTX2-NT1, LINC03127, AC104938.1 and NEUROD1-DT, located in the TADs of IRD genes OTX2, ABCA4, NR2E3, and CERKL and NEUROD1, respectively - were selected for further analysis because of (1) the relevance of the IRD gene and (2) data supporting their transcription and retinal regulation (Figure S4). Furthermore, these four lncRNAs were annotated as “novel transcript/gene” in Ensembl, with, to our knowledge, no functional analyses performed thus far.

OTX2-NT1 (Fig. 3) is located in the TAD of OTX2, a retinal transcription factor with a major role during development and in adulthood [62]. Pathogenic variants in OTX2 are linked to Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) and pattern dystrophy (autosomal dominant macular degeneration) [63]. The OTX2-NT1 transcript ENST00000557467 has a TPM of 46.32 and has the highest JSD score (0.88) of all lncRNAs located in IRD TADs. Interestingly, this lncRNA overlaps with OTX2-AS1 (antisense orientation). Retinal transcription of OTX2-NT1 is supported by CAGE data [34], adult human retina single-cell Nuc-seq, and adult human retina ATAC-seq [35]. LINC03127 is located in the TAD of ABCA4. ABCA4-associated retinopathy is the most prevalent single-gene IRD [64]. We identified a novel, polyadenylated isoform of this lncRNA (TPM = 7.70, JSD = 0.84). Transcription of LINC03127 is supported by CAGE-seq, single-cell Nuc-seq and ATAC-seq, and the promoter region contains DNA binding sites for OTX2, CRX, and NRL (Figure S4).

The AC104938.1 lncRNA (JSD = 0.66) is located 154 bp downstream of, and on the same strand as NR2E3, a key transcription factor that activates rod and inhibits cone photoreceptor development [65–67]. Variants in NR2E3 cause autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa [68] and autosomal recessive enhanced S-cone syndrome [69]. Retinal transcription is supported by retinal single-cell Nuc-seq (Figure S4). Notably, neither CAGE peak nor retinal transcription factor binding sites were identified.

Finally, NEUROD1-DT (JSD = 0.47) is located in the TAD of CERKL and NEUROD1, two disease genes for autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa [70, 71]. Retinal transcription is supported by retinal single-cell Nuc-seq and ATAC-seq (Figure S4).

Second, the dataset was interrogated to identify novel retina-specific lncRNAs. In total, 5,795 novel loci, whose transcripts do not overlap with any annotated genes, were identified in the IRD gene TADs. To reduce this number, the region was limited to 30 kb up- and downstream of the IRD gene, resulting in 784 loci. Of note, with this approach, we did not identify novel lncRNAs overlapping with protein-coding genes. In total, 36 putative lncRNA loci displayed a JSD score associated with retina and a TPM above one (Table S8). The transcript with the highest JSD score (0.85), located in the TAD of GUCY2D, was selected for further analysis and named LINC03128. Variants in GUCY2D constitute a significant cause of autosomal recessive LCA and the most common cause of autosomal dominant cone-rod degeneration [72]. The novel transcript is single-exonic and located ~ 8 kb upstream and antisense of GUCY2D. Its transcription is supported by CAGE-seq, retinal single-cell Nuc-seq and retinal ATAC-seq. In addition, the promoter region contains OTX2, CRX, and NRL binding sites (Figure S4).

Identification of lncRNAs co-expressed with IRD genes

Co-expression analysis aims to identify genes that display similar expression patterns across different conditions, which may underlie their engagement in similar biological processes and modulation by the same regulatory mechanisms [73, 74]. We carried out a co-expression analysis to identify lncRNAs positively co-expressed with IRD genes. To achieve this goal, we relied on the TIGEM and the EyeGEx RNA-seq datasets [24–26] and calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between each of the 218 IRD genes vs. all genes detected in the human retina samples. We focused on the top 1% positively co-expressed genes with each IRD gene to ensure the strongest functional associations and to minimize spurious correlations.

We then extracted annotated lncRNAs from the above co-expression list, retrieving 1,666 and 983 lncRNA entries from the TIGEM and EyeGEx datasets, respectively. To prioritize those with a higher likelihood of common co-regulation, we selected for further study only lncRNAs that shared co-expression with at least ten IRD genes (n = 64; Fig. 2B; Figure S5; Table S9). We next applied an additional filter based on expression value, retaining lncRNAs that display a median TPM value of at least 100 [75], which led to a list of nineteen lncRNAs (Table S10). We applied this stringent expression filter to further increase the likelihood of identifying biologically relevant lncRNAs.

We proceeded to evaluate the putative expression of these lncRNAs by taking advantage of the public database Spectacle, which contains multiple single-cell RNA-seq datasets from human retina [44, 45]. Twelve out of nineteen lncRNAs were reported to be expressed in photoreceptors, with some examples of predominant/restricted expression in either rods (n = 5) or cones (n = 2) (Table S10; Figure S6). We decided to exclude the remaining seven lncRNAs from further analysis because of a more diffuse, non-photoreceptor-predominant expression in the retina.

Overall, the two strategies, i.e., tissue-specificity and location in IRD TADs on the one hand and co-expression analysis on the other hand, generated a global set of 126 lRD gene-associated lncRNAs, including 90 annotated and 36 novel candidate lncRNAs (Fig. 1, Tables S8, S9). From this dataset, the selection described above resulted in a subset of seventeen lncRNAs. These seventeen lncRNAs were then subjected to both in silico and experimental evaluations, as reported below and summarized in Fig. 1.

Characterization of selected lncRNAs

Elucidation of lncRNA genomic organization

The genomic organization of the seventeen selected lncRNAs was evaluated both to determine their transcript repertoire and to assess their independence with respect to adjacent, protein-coding genes. To this end, we performed a meta-analysis of the re-assembled short-read RNA-seq retinal transcriptomes and of in-house generated direct cDNA long-read RNA-seq (ONT) of three post-mortem human retinas (10.2, 18.5 and 11.4 million reads passing filter with minimal Q-score of 7, respectively). Novel, multi-exonic isoforms and novel single-exonic transcripts were identified for thirteen and eight lncRNAs, respectively. Of these, two transcripts containing a novel exon not previously described (LINC03127 and WWC2-AS1), detected in all three donors, were validated using RT-qPCR (Figure S7 Panel A).

This analysis was highly instrumental in determining the independence of the identified lncRNAs with respect to adjacent transcriptional units. Since OTX2-NT1 is located 7 kb apart from OTX2 but on the same strand, we evaluated whether OTX2-NT1 represents a potential alternative isoform of OTX2 by using RT-qPCR (Table S11) and inspection of long-read RNA-seq data (Figure S8). This revealed that OTX2-NT1 and OTX2 both occur as individual transcripts with limited readthrough transcription. Conversely, evaluation of both the short- and long-read RNA-seq data suggested that AC104938.1 is actually part of the NR2E3 gene locus (in 3/3 donors), probably representing an extended 3’ UTR and novel NR2E3 isoforms (Figure S4). This was confirmed by sequencing of three PCR amplicons spanning both the putative lncRNA and NR2E3 (data not shown). The same is true for FAM13A-AS1 and NRAV. Although they both show a CAGE peak and the upstream region of NRAV contains OTX2, CRX, and NRL binding sites, we observed a significant extent of readthrough transcripts with the neighboring - HERC3 and SRSF9 genes, respectively in all three donors (Figure S4). Finally, we found that AC010319.5 is a novel isoform of NXNL1 in all donors. Therefore, we decided to exclude AC104938.1, FAM13A-AS1, NRAV, and AC010319.5 from further analysis, thus reducing the list of lncRNAs to thirteen. Finally, 26 of the 36 retina-specific putative novel lncRNAs were confirmed by long-read RNA-seq. RT-qPCR and Sanger sequencing performed for four loci in three additional donors, validated expression at three loci (Table S8).

Determination of lncRNA tissue distribution

First, the lncRNA expression pattern was evaluated using RNA-seq data from 55 tissues from GTEx (n = 53), retina [24–26], and RPE [76–78]. All lncRNAs showed a highly predominant retinal expression, apart from DSP-AS1 (JSD = 0.32) and AC027307.2 (JSD = 0.22–0.40) (Figure S9). Next, we performed RT-qPCR on total RNA extracted from both human retina and RPE and other non-retinal tissues/cell types including blood, lung, fibroblasts, and podocytes. This analysis confirmed the findings from RNA-seq data, with eleven of the tested lncRNAs being detected only in the retina, while DSP-AS1 and AC027307.2 were also found in non-retinal tissues (Figure S7 Panel B and C). Therefore, we decided to exclude the latter two lncRNAs from downstream analyses.

In conclusion, analysis of the lncRNA genomic organization and expression pattern resulted in a final selection of eleven Retina Enriched IRD gene-Related lncRNAs (REIR-lncRNAs). Table 1 summarizes their location with respect to protein-coding genes (Figure S4).

Prediction of REIR-lncRNA protein-coding potential

LncRNAs have been reported to contain short open reading frames that encode micropeptides, some of which have a potential role in disease [79–81]. To gain knowledge on the REIR-lncRNA coding potential, in silico predictions were performed for the top three highest expressed lncRNA transcripts using three tools. For five lncRNAs (OTX2-NT1, LINC03128, LINC03012, SEMA6D-AS1 and CAMTA1-AS4), PLEK predicted one to three transcripts to be coding; however, all were predicted non-coding by CPC2 and PhyloCSF (Table S12). Overall, a low coding potential was predicted for all lncRNAs.

The majority of the shared enriched CC terms were related to photoreceptor structures (cilium and inner segment) (Fig. 5C; Table S15). Next, we compared the GO terms among each REIR-lncRNA and its closest protein-coding gene, often located in an antisense orientation (Figure S11). As expected, the REIR-lncRNAs identified by IRD TAD colocalization were enriched in “eye-related” terms similar to their corresponding IRD gene (e.g., Fig. 3D). Single-molecule RNA in situ hybridization reveals that REIRs are predominantly expressed in photoreceptors

To assess the cellular distribution of REIR-lncRNAs, we carried out smRNA-ISH assays on post-mortem human retina (n = 1). This analysis revealed an enriched expression in the photoreceptor cell layer for the majority of REIRs. The only exceptions were WWC2-AS1 and NEUROD1-DT, which were also found in other retinal layers such as the inner nuclear layer (Figure S12-S13).

To assess cell type-specific localization of REIR-lncRNAs identified by co-expression analysis, we combined smRNA-ISH with immunofluorescence for cone- (ARR3) and rod- (GNAT1) specific protein markers. A representative example is SEMA6D-AS1 that was predicted to have a cone-restricted expression by Spectacle (Fig. 4D). smRNA-ISH assays confirmed this by enriched RNA staining in ARR3-positive cells and almost no signal in GNAT1-positive cells (Fig. 4E). On the contrary, MEOX2-AS2 expression was mainly restricted to rods (Figure S12). For the remaining four REIRs evaluated (LINC03012, ATP2C1-AS1, CAMTA1-AS4, and PLA2G4C-AS1), smRNA-ISH analysis revealed lncRNA expression in both rod and cone photoreceptors (Figure S12). Interestingly, smRNA-ISH showed a predominant localization of LINC03012, CAMTA1-AS4, WWC2-AS1 and PLA2G4C-AS1 in photoreceptor nuclei, whereas MEOX2-AS2, SEMA6D-AS1 and ATP2C1-AS1 were also detected in extranuclear regions. These data are only partially concordant with single-cell RNA-seq data (Spectacle) and, therefore, highlight the added value of smRNA-ISH to precisely assess lncRNA cellular distribution in the retina, particularly for lncRNAs that display overall lower expression levels or are expressed in a single cell type.

For the lncRNAs identified by IRD TAD colocalization, a co-staining was performed for the mRNA of the IRD gene(s) followed by quantitative expression analysis applied to confocal images (four or five regions, Figure S13; Table S16). The highest lncRNA/IRD gene colocalization was found for LINC03127 and ABCA4, with 37% colocalization of lncRNA dots and ABCA4 mRNA dots. The lowest colocalization was observed for NEUROD-DT1 and CERKL, where only 6% of the lncRNA dots colocalized. OTX2-NT1 expression was predominantly restricted to photoreceptors, with 98.6% of lncRNA dots located within the photoreceptor layers (outer nuclear layer and photoreceptor inner segments). In contrast, the expression of OTX2 and OTX2-AS1 was observed not only in the photoreceptor layers (78.9% and 64.0%, respectively) but also in other neuronal cells (inner nuclear layer and inner plexiform layer). The latter is in agreement with the single-cell RNA-seq data (Fig. 3B and E). Interestingly, 27.2% of the OTX2-NT1 dots colocalize with the OTX2 mRNA dots.

Functional evaluation of LINC03127

The previous results support the role of REIR-lncRNAs in IRD gene regulation. As a proof of concept, we further characterized LINC03127 (Fig. 6), which was identified in the same TAD as ABCA4, one of the most clinically relevant IRD genes [64].

Comparison between LINC03127 and ABCA4 expression and regulation in photoreceptors

Given the different expression of LINC03127 (predominant in rods) and ABCA4 (rods and cones) (Fig. 6C, Figures S6 and S13 ), we evaluated which TFs are predicted to bind to either promoter region (JASPAR CORE Collection). A total of 535 different predicted TF motifs were identified, with 102 and 84 exclusively found within the promoters of LINC03127 and ABCA4, respectively (Table S17). Interestingly, although no major differences were observed in well-established TFs regulating photoreceptor cell fate specification (e.g., NR2E3, CRX, OTX2), binding sites for TFs associated with cone differentiation (THRB, HES6) were predicted exclusively within the promoter of ABCA4. On the other hand, the number of predicted binding sites for the master regulator gene of rod photoreceptor determination, NRL, was found to be higher within the lncRNA promoter (n = 4) compared to that of ABCA4 (n = 1).

LINC03127 is transcribed from a functionally conserved potential retinal enhancer of ABCA4

The putative promoter region of LINC03127 was found to exhibit chromatin signatures of active enhancers in the retina (Figure S4). To evaluate whether this locus could act as a proximal enhancer of ABCA4, UMI-4C data derived by D’haene et al. [32] from adult human retina was mined for this locus. The generated profiles confirmed a reciprocal interaction between the promoters of ABCA4 and LINC03127 (Figure S14A), altogether suggesting that LINC03127 is transcribed from an active retinal enhancer of ABCA4. Although sequence conservation was found to be low, positional conservation and retinal expression were observed in mice (Figure S14B). Additionally, assessment of chromatin profiling data derived from murine photoreceptors revealed that the syntenic locus is particularly accessible in rod photoreceptors as opposed to cones or Nrl-knockout (Nrl−/−) photoreceptors (Figure S14B), hence suggesting functional conservation of the locus.

Knockdown of LINC03127 in human retinal explants and retinal organoids

To gain insight into the functionality of LINC03127, seven gapmer ASOs (Table S6) were evaluated to trigger its degradation. Due to its photoreceptor-specificity and the lack of reliable human photoreceptor cell lines, a functional model based on human retinal explants was set up. An efficient lncRNA knockdown (60% decrease) was achieved using ASO4 (Figure S15A). Next, polyA RNA-seq was performed on a total of 25 explants derived from four donors to assess transcriptome-wide differential expression. These included explants treated with ASO4 (ten explants, four donors), untreated explants (ten explants, four donors), and explants treated with a nontargeting control ASO (NTC ASO, scrambled version of ASO4) (five explants, two donors). Differential gene expression analysis revealed 836 differentially expressed genes (DEG, adjusted p-value < 0.05) upon ASO4 treatment compared to untreated explants. Of these, 532 genes were downregulated, including LINC03127, and 304 were upregulated (Figure S16). GO enrichment analysis of all the DEGs combined revealed biological processes related to visual and sensory perception, detection of stimuli and antigen processing (Table S18). In addition, GO enrichment analysis of the downregulated DEGs only showed terms related to visual and sensory perception and detection of stimuli, whereas analysis of the upregulated DEGs resulted in terms related to antigen processing (Fig. 6E; Table S18). Interestingly, visual and sensory perception terms were also found in the GO enrichment analysis of the top 1% co-expressed genes of LINC03127 (Fig. 6B).

To exclude the possibility that these results represent an aspecific effect of ASO4, inherent to the ASO but independent of the lncRNA knockdown, LINC03127 knockdown was performed in one donor using a second ASO (ASO6, 67% decrease, Figure S15A). This resulted in 207 DEGs (144 down- and 63 upregulated) upon ASO6 treatment compared to untreated explants (adjusted p-value < 0.05). For the downregulated DEGs, similar GO biological process terms were found as for ASO4 (visual and sensory perception and detection of stimuli) (Fig. 6E; Figure S16; Table S18). The upregulated DEGs resulted in terms related to protein folding. Of note, LINC03127 itself was not significantly differentially expressed (log2 fold change: −1.35, adjusted p-value: 0.5016), probably because this analysis included only one donor. A total of 56 DEGs were shared between ASO4 and ASO6 (Table S20). In addition, potential toxicity of the ASOs was evaluated by treating ARPE-19 cells with the ASOs followed by evaluation of their growth. No difference in confluence across conditions was observed, pointing to no toxic effects (Figure S15B).

Comparison of both ASOs to NTC ASO-treated explants resulted in 147 and 190 DEGs, respectively, however, LINC03127 downregulation was again not significant (log2 fold change: −1.06 and − 1.40, adjusted p-value 0.1291 and 0.1325 respectively). Similar GO biological processes were enriched in the downregulated ASO6 DEGs vs. NTC ASO as compared to the untreated explants (no enrichment found in ASO4 downregulated DEGs; Table S18). Finally, 30 genes were differentially expressed between NTC ASO-treated and untreated explants, of which 10 and 2 (of 836 and 207) were also present in the DEGs of respectively ASO4- and ASO6-treated compared to untreated explants (Table S18). We hypothesize that these are associated with ASO delivery.

Subsequently, human retinal organoids (> day 180) were treated with ASO4 and ASO6 for three weeks. Retinal organoids from two different iPSC differentiations (one iPSC line) were treated with the ASOs. These experiments included four replicates for ASO4 and three replicates for ASO6, NTC ASO, and untreated from the first differentiation (two organoids pooled/replicate). From the second differentiation, four replicates per condition were analysed (three organoids pooled/replicate). The PCA plot indicates a separation in clustering between the two gapmer ASOs and control conditions (NTC and untreated) (Figure S17).

We identified 8,928 (4,415 down- and 4,513 upregulated) and 6,513 (3,078 down- and 3,435 upregulated) DEGs in retinal organoids treated with ASO4 and ASO6, respectively, compared to untreated organoids. Importantly, the top enriched biological processes in the downregulated DEGs are related to synapse and visual perception, as was observed in the retinal explants (Fig. 6E). The same biological processes were found in the down- and upregulated DEGs when comparing both ASOs to NTC ASO (Table S19, Figure S17), however in this case the LINC03127 decrease was not statistically significant. Furthermore, OPN1MW (ASO4vsNT: log2FC: −5.76, adjusted p-value: 3.37E-18) and OPN1LW (ASO4vsNT: log2FC: −4.84, adjusted p-value: 1.33E-11) were among the top ten downregulated DEGs following LINC03127 knockdown using both ASOs. Consistent with these transcriptomic findings, immunofluorescent staining of ASO-treated retinal organoids confirmed the loss of OPN1MW/LW protein expression (Fig. 6F, Figure S18).

Next, we evaluated the overlap between ASO4 and ASO6 treatment. 4,871 genes were differentially expressed in both ASO4- and ASO6-treated organoids compared to the untreated condition. Of these, 1,201 (incl. LINC03127) had an absolute log2 fold change above one. Moreover, a total of 31 genes were found to be differentially expressed in both retinal explants and retinal organoids. Interestingly, several cone-enriched genes (e.g. OPN1LW, GNAT2 and PDE6C) were present in this list (Table S20).

Furthermore, to exclude the possibility that the effect was caused by off-targets, genes that are partially complementary to ASO4 and ASO6 were checked among the differentially expressed genes. Only one gene, C2CD3, was downregulated (33% decrease of expression) in retinal organoids upon ASO6 treatment (Table S6). C2CD3 was not downregulated in retinal explants (low expression) nor upon treatment with ASO4. This further supports the inclusion of multiple independent ASOs as well as multiple models in ASO-knockdown experiments.

Finally, LINC03127 knockdown in retinal organoids (three weeks treatment) resulted in significant downregulation of ABCA4 (ASO4vsNT: log2FC: −1.95, adjusted p-value: 7.20E-20) (Table S19). This was not observed in the retinal explants (48 h treatment) (Table S18). To investigate this further, differential transcript usage (DTU) of ABCA4 was assessed upon LINC03127 knockdown in both retinal explants and organoids. Interestingly, LINC03127 knockdown by both ASOs in retinal explants resulted in a significant DTU event. Expression of the canonical transcript (ENST00000370225.4) decreased, while expression of ENST00000536513.5 increased significantly (adjusted p < 0.05, Figure S19, Table S21). In retinal organoids, no significant DTU events were observed. Cross-tool validation of the DTU using satuRn and DEXseq confirmed significant DTU events for ABCA4 (Table S21).

Overall, these results suggest that LINC03127 is involved in critical functions of photoreceptors such as visual and sensory perception, and point to a potential regulation of the ABCA4 gene.

Discussion

In the last decade, the vast amount of sequencing data generated by large-scale consortia has painted a richer and more sophisticated picture of the human genome with a myriad of new genes that do not encode proteins, including lncRNAs [4, 82]. Despite a substantial body of literature supporting lncRNA biological relevance in health and disease [80], their mode of action and overall function remain poorly understood, particularly in the retina. We leveraged a unique combination of omics datasets to obtain a novel catalogue of transcripts expressed in the adult human retina. Akin to recent studies conducted in other species [12, 83, 84], our dataset provided novel insights into the transcriptional landscape of human retinal lncRNAs.

A better understanding of lncRNA biology in the retina harbors a significant potential to elucidate transcriptional pathways that underlie both physiological and pathological retinal processes. To date, up to 30% of IRD patients remain molecularly unsolved even though over 500 IRD genes have been identified [85]. Part of the remaining undiagnosed cases could potentially be attributed to non-coding regulatory regions of known IRD genes. Current research primarily focuses on DNA elements such as promoters and (distal) enhancers [32, 35, 86–88], while the role of non-coding RNA remains largely unexplored. A potential role of non-coding RNA in IRD is supported by recent studies showing pathogenic variants in LINC00632 and CDR1as/ciRS-7, microRNA miR-204 and for the small nuclear (sn)RNAs RNU4-2 and RNU6 [89–92]. Furthermore, new insights in lncRNA function have the potential to contribute to novel IRD gene-agnostic therapeutic approaches tackling disease mechanisms instead of the primary disease gene [93–96]. These could circumvent the currently limited availability of gene-specific treatments for IRD due to its extremely high genetic heterogeneity. Here, two complementary strategies were developed to identify lncRNAs potentially regulating IRD genes. For the first strategy, we aimed to unravel tissue-specific lncRNAs located in the same TAD as known IRD genes. In the second strategy, lncRNAs co-expressed with IRD genes were identified.

First, we hypothesized that retina-specific lncRNAs located in the TADs of IRD genes could be involved in regulating IRD gene expression in the retina. Moreover, lncRNAs themselves may be involved in the formation of TADs and, as such, in regulating the expression landscape of genes in those TADs [97]. In addition to annotated lncRNAs, we extended our approach to novel transcripts, allowing the identification of a novel, single-exonic and retina-specific lncRNA, LINC03128, located antisense of the GUCY2D IRD gene. In the past, single-exonic lncRNAs were often removed from transcriptome assemblies as they were expected to be genomic contamination. However, when transcription is strand-specific, which is the case for LINC03128, it does not originate from random DNA fragments since they are expected to map to both strands. Furthermore, transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation by single-exonic lncRNAs has already been described [98].

The second approach focused on gene co-expression. Gene co-expression analyses are generally used to study and prioritize potential disease genes and to identify gene regulatory networks. A key advantage of co-expression analysis is its potential to identify genes that may be involved in the same biological processes [73]. The study of co-expression networks by RNA-seq data facilitates the identification of biologically relevant genes, including lncRNAs, and it provides more insight into their putative regulatory mechanisms in biological processes [73]. Only a few studies so far relied on the analysis of co-expression to predict the biological role of lncRNAs in the retina [8, 99], but none of them were applied to the human retina. Moreover, this is the first time that co-expression analysis is carried out in a systematic manner to identify lncRNAs endowed with a putatively relevant role in function and dysfunction. As a first step towards the exploitation of co-expression analysis to identify lncRNAs potentially contributing to IRD pathogenesis and progression, we focused on positive correlation with IRD genes. The latter choice was based on both technical and conceptual considerations. Concerning the technical aspects, the interpretation of positive gene co-expression data is less challenging than that related to negative gene co-expression data, which is made more complex by the low expression levels that may be detected. More importantly, our goals in this initial effort were to identify IRD-related lncRNAs (a) involved in the same functional pathways of IRD genes; (b) co-expressed in the same cell types; and (c) sharing co-regulation. To achieve this, positive co-expression analysis is definitely more effective compared to negative co-expression analysis, particularly when using bulk RNA-seq retinal datasets. Nevertheless, performing negative correlation studies to identify lncRNAs that may exert a negative influence on IRD gene expression, is of the utmost importance and may represent the subject of future studies if applied to a repertoire of transcriptome datasets also including retinal single-cell RNA-seq data. Moreover, it would be interesting to carry out co-expression analysis starting from genes that, despite not endowed with a primary pathogenic role in IRD, are known to contribute to photoreceptor degeneration progression, such as specific transcription factors and signaling molecules [18]. The above issues may be addressed in future studies that could shed further light on the possible architecture of gene networks involved in IRD.

Both approaches are complementary toward the goal of identifying candidate lncRNAs regulating retinal functions. In terms of expression levels, the first approach enables the detection of lncRNAs with low(er) expression levels and is centered explicitly on IRD gene(s) within the same TAD. In contrast, the second approach works more effectively on lncRNAs with relatively higher expression levels as it aims at the identification of lncRNAs that are co-expressed with at least ten IRD genes, which in general display high expression in the retina.

The final result of this dual approach is a set of 90 annotated and 36 novel candidate lncRNAs with a potential role in IRD gene expression and photoreceptor function. Notably, the novel candidate lncRNA loci represent a valuable dataset that requires further investigation to unravel their function. Subsequently, eleven lncRNAs, termed REIRs, were selected for further characterization. First, we discovered novel isoforms expressed exclusively in the retina for eight lncRNAs, thus highlighting the importance of including relevant tissue in lncRNA identification. Furthermore, the full scope of isoform diversity was further uncovered through direct cDNA long-read sequencing, a technology that allows full-length characterization and unbiased quantification of transcript isoforms [100, 101]. Differences were observed between the transcripts identified by short- and long-read RNA-seq, intrinsic to the technologies [102]. These methods are complementary and combining them provides a more comprehensive view. Readthrough transcription, which occurs in 34% of protein-coding genes in normal cells and across various tissues [103], was evaluated for OTX2-NT1 due to its proximity and orientation relative to OTX2. Although some readthrough transcription was detected, smRNA-ISH demonstrated distinct expression patterns for OTX2-NT1 and OTX2. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis of co-expressed genes revealed differences in biological processes and cellular components, indicating that OTX2-NT1 is not merely an extended 5’UTR of OTX2. In contrast, we discovered that AC104938.1, FAM13A-AS1 and NRAV are actually extended UTRs of NR2E3, HERC3 and SRSF9, respectively.

Most REIR-lncRNAs (six out of eleven) are located in antisense orientation with respect to protein-coding genes. While it is generally assumed that antisense transcripts regulate their cognate sense genes, it was also recently shown that this can be highly variable depending on the pattern and degrees of overlap between the sense and the antisense genes [104]. In the retina, it was previously shown that some antisense lncRNAs (e.g., Vax2os1 and Six3os) indeed contribute to the regulation of their cognate sense protein-coding gene [7, 9, 105]. Interestingly, most of the antisense REIRs we identified, particularly those identified by co-expression analysis, do not show apparent correlation with their cognate protein-coding gene, i.e., SEMA6D, PLA2G4C, ATP2C1. This assumption is based on two observations. Firstly, we did not detect any significant co-expression between the sense and the antisense members of these pairs and, secondly, there was little overlap in the enriched GO terms, with the lncRNAs showing enrichment in terms related to visual function contrary to the corresponding sense protein-coding genes (Figure S11). Overall, these data support the evidence that the presence of a functional correlation in gene expression regulation between members of sense-antisense gene pairs should not be considered the rule but should rather be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

Most of the REIR-lncRNAs are associated with photoreceptor-specialized functions such as phototransduction, visual perception, and cilium-related processes. In contrast, GO enrichment analysis on genes co-expressed with LINC03128 revealed terms related to a high metabolic demand, including glycolysis and mitochondrial-associated processes. Photoreceptors are known to be highly metabolically active due to the continuous renewal of outer segments, sustained ion fluxes to maintain dark current, and high rates of neurotransmitter release [106]. These processes impose a substantial energetic burden and require tight coupling between glycolytic and mitochondrial pathways. In this context, the enrichment of metabolic processes suggests that this lncRNA may be involved in supporting the metabolic demands of photoreceptors.

As previously mentioned, the evolutionary conservation of lncRNAs is difficult to assess. Indeed, it is known that conservation does not necessarily entail nucleotide conservation, as shown particularly for subsets of intergenic lncRNAs for which solely the act of transcription might be under selective constraint [19]. Interestingly, in addition to LINC03127, we detected the presence of three lncRNAs displaying retinal expression in the mouse genome, in regions syntenic to the loci of the human OTX2-NT1, NEUROD1-DT and ATP2C1-AS1 (data not shown). Further studies are needed to determine whether these represent the orthologs of the human lncRNAs and therefore represent candidates for in vivo functional evaluation of REIR lncRNAs.

Single-molecule RNA in situ hybridization revealed that most REIR-lncRNAs exhibit photoreceptor-specific expression (Figures S12 and S13). For seven REIR-lncRNAs, smRNA-ISH in combination with immunofluorescence with cone- and rod-specific protein markers revealed distinct expression patterns, with a selection of lncRNAs specifically expressed in either rods or cones. Notably, these findings only partially aligned with single-cell RNA-seq data, particularly for lncRNAs with lower abundance, highlighting the value of smRNA-ISH on post-mortem human retina. Moreover, smRNA-ISH allowed us to gain some initial insight into the subcellular localization (predominantly nuclear vs. both nuclear and extranuclear, Figs. 4, S12 and S13). Given the rapid evolutionary divergence of lncRNAs across species [19–21] and the structural differences in the retina between animal models [107], post-mortem human retinal is the most relevant tissue for these studies.

Additional characterization was conducted for LINC03127, a lncRNA located in the TAD of the most common recessive IRD gene ABCA4 [15, 64]. We identified a novel transcript of LINC03127, containing a new exon, and smRNA-ISH and single-cell RNA-seq showed predominant expression of LINC03127 in the photoreceptors. Furthermore, a relatively high percentage of LINC03127 colocalized with ABCA4 mRNA (smRNA-ISH). Notably, colocalization of LINC03127 with ABCA4 cannot distinguish co-transcription from direct regulation. There is limited nucleotide conservation in mouse for this region, however, signatures of open chromatin and active transcription in photoreceptors were observed, thereby indicating potential functional conservation of the locus. In line with these observations, the lncRNA promoter region has been nominated as a candidate enhancer for ABCA4 expression by D’haene et al., as supported by the presence of H3K27ac and H3K4me2 histone marks and increased frequency of chromatin interactions with the ABCA4 promoter [32]. The latter, coupled with the distinctive conservation of the locus, raised the hypothesis that this lncRNA exerts a cis-regulatory function on ABCA4 expression.

To investigate the role of LINC03127 in the retina, we performed a knockdown of the lncRNA in its native environment using post-mortem human retinal explants and retinal organoids. Based on the in situ hybridization results, ASO-mediated knockdown was chosen over other tools due to its higher efficiency in depleting nuclear-retained lncRNAs [108]. ASO knockdown supports involvement of LINC03127 in the proper functioning of photoreceptors, as GO biological processes related to specific photoreceptor functions are enriched among the downregulated differentially expressed genes upon knockdown. The fact that this is observed using two independent ASOs is critical, as it helps differentiate between specific and unintended off-target effects. The latter is apparent from different GO enrichment of the upregulated genes of the two ASOs in retinal explants.

Importantly, the heterogeneous nature of the human retinal explants, intrinsic to the human donor and the post-mortem time and procedure, complicates the interpretation of the results. Ideally, experiments should be performed with larger sample sizes to increase statistical power. Furthermore, retinal organoid culture is known to be variable [109]. This variability might (partially) underly the high number of DEGs found in retinal organoids compared to retinal explants. Of note, retinal organoids and retinal explants are two distinct model systems; organoids represent a developmental stage, mimicking the fetal retina, while explants reflect a mature, fully differentiated state.

Finally, ABCA4 expression was significantly downregulated only in retinal organoids, whereas no significant change in overall expression was observed in retinal explants. Nevertheless, in retinal explants a switch in transcript usage was observed upon treatment, which is suggestive for differential splicing of ABCA4 upon knockdown of LINC03127. This was not observed in organoids. One potential explanation for this difference is the treatment duration: explants were treated for 48 h, capturing acute regulatory effects, whereas organoids were treated for three weeks, potentially allowing compensatory mechanisms to emerge. In addition, the distinct developmental stages of explants and organoids may influence their sensitivity to splicing regulation. Together, these results suggest that LINC03127 potentially exerts a splicing-related regulatory effect on ABCA4 that is detectable in a mature retinal context. Further research is required to explain the difference between LINC03127 knockdown in retinal explants and retinal organoids, including long-term ASO treatment in retinal explants and additional assays to distinguish direct cis-regulation of ABCA4 from indirect transcriptional changes.

To conclude, the dissection of the functional role of lncRNAs in human still represents a formidable challenge due to the extreme heterogeneity and the high number of lncRNA transcripts. Here, we demonstrate that the combined use of two independent and complementary strategies was highly instrumental for a first global evaluation of lncRNAs in the human retina and for the identification of a subset of them (REIR) endowed with a potential role in the control of expression of IRD genes. These data contribute to a better understanding of the biological processes underlying retinal function and disease. Moreover, our integrated bioinformatic/experimental pipeline can be extended to the analysis of other human tissues, thus increasing our general knowledge on the contribution of lncRNAs to biological processes and to human disease in general.
