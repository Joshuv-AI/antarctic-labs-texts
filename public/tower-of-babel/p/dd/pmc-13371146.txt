Cumulative Childhood Trauma and Relationship Distress: The Intermediary Role of Emotion Regulation Among Couples Seeking Relationship Psychotherapy
Authors: Maria Belen Field Lira, Zoé Benoit, Laurie Raphaelle Beauvilliers, Mathilde Renaud, Caroline Dugal, Audrey Brassard, Natacha Godbout, Katherine Péloquin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCumulative childhood trauma (CCT) can lead to long‐lasting consequences, such as difficulties in emotion regulation, which, in turn, can affect romantic relationships. While problems with emotion regulation are associated with poorer relationship outcomes, few studies have examined these associations among couples seeking relationship psychotherapy, despite an estimated 80% of individuals in such psychotherapy having experienced CCT. The present study investigated the intermediary role of emotion regulation difficulties in the association between CCT and relationship distress in 199 couples seeking relationship psychotherapy. Path analysis revealed both a direct and an indirect association between one's CCT and one's relationship distress, through difficulties in emotion regulation. This study highlights the need for further investigation into the mechanisms underlying the consequences of CCT on romantic partnerships.Keywords: couples, cumulative childhood trauma, emotion regulation difficulties, relationship distress, relationship psychotherapy

Acts of psychological, physical, and sexual abuse, as well as instances of neglect and exposure to interparental violence occurring before the age of 18, constitute distinct forms of childhood interpersonal trauma. These experiences can cause actual or potential harm to a child's health, survival, development, or dignity within relationships characterized by responsibility, trust, or power (Bernstein et al. 2003; Fink et al. 1995; Finkelhor et al. 2007b; World Health Organization 2015). A meta‐analysis combining 24 studies (19,652 participants) indicated that approximately 40% of individuals living in occidental countries have experienced childhood interpersonal trauma (MacDonald et al. 2016). Moreover, epidemiological studies revealed that 22% of adults acknowledged experiencing four or more types of traumas (Finkelhor et al. 2007a, 2009). These results bring attention to a phenomenon known as cumulative childhood trauma (CCT; Cloitre et al. 2009; Hodges et al. 2013), which refers to the accumulation of various types of interpersonal trauma endured during childhood. The combination of various forms of childhood interpersonal trauma has been linked with profound consequences on a person's development, including interpersonal difficulties (Dvir et al. 2014; Ford et al. 2010). The distressful personal, relational, social, and economic outcomes associated with CCT establish it as a major public health issue (D'Andrea et al. 2012; Cloitre et al. 2009; Hodges et al. 2013; Li et al. 2016). Indeed, CCT is linked to a wide range of severe short‐term and long‐term consequences (Hodges et al. 2013), including physical, behavioral, or psychological disorders, distortions in representations of the self and others, and difficulties with emotion regulation (Bolduc et al. 2018; Dugal et al. 2021; Li et al. 2016). These consequences can then have adverse effects on the self and on interpersonal relationships, particularly romantic relationships.

Supporting the notion that CCT may result in relationship difficulties, the Secondary Trauma Theory (STT; Nelson and Wampler 2000) suggests that CCT may shape relationship outcomes for both partners. According to this theory, partners may manifest similar outcomes to those observed in individuals who have experienced CCT. Since CCT occurs within a relational context, its consequences often extend to other intimate interpersonal relationships. Indeed, recurrent patterns in which past trauma is re‐enacted within couple dynamics often result in instability and conflict in the relationship (MacIntosh 2017). Therefore, partners may also be affected by CCT. A meta‐analysis conducted by Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. (2024) supported significant associations between an individual's childhood interpersonal trauma and their partner's lower relationship satisfaction, higher instances of intimate partner violence, and increased psychological distress. A systematic synthesis of 15 quantitative studies also indicates that distress experienced by both primary and secondary trauma survivors is strongly associated with poorer couple functioning (Smedley and Nelson Goff 2024). Based on theoretical and empirical observations, the CCT survivor, their partner, and the couple's dynamics may all be impacted by CCT (Nelson and Wampler 2000). Therefore, using dyadic data analysis to examine the associations between CCT and relationship difficulties may provide a better understanding of couple dynamics. Unlike simple correlations that examine relationships between variables one at a time, dyadic analysis allows us to consider all these factors together in a single model. This is important because it considers the interdependence between partners, offering a more accurate view of how the variables interact within a couple. This deeper understanding makes the conclusions more nuanced and relevant for clinical practice.

Given that a romantic relationship is an experience that most adults share (Government of Canada, Statistics Canada 2022), and considering that childhood interpersonal trauma is highly prevalent, it becomes imperative to explore the potential impact of CCT on relationships in adulthood and to understand the pathways by which relationship difficulties ensue from CCT.

CCT and Relationships Distress

Relationship distress encompasses feelings of dissatisfaction with the relationship, the perception of partner inattentiveness to one's needs, and the belief that the relationship is encountering significant difficulties (Whisman et al. 2009). Relationship distress is prevalent among long‐term couples, with nationally representative research showing that 31% of married couples in the general population would report clinically significant levels of relationship distress (Whisman et al. 2008). CCT involves interpersonal traumas, which often include elements of betrayal and neglect, leading to feelings of insecurity about the reliability of others. Experiences of CCT can therefore shape the nature of intimate relationships and pave the way to relationship problems and distress (Dugal et al. 2016). The high proportion of individuals with a history of childhood interpersonal trauma who seek sexual and relationship psychotherapy (80%; Berthelot et al. 2014; Dugal et al. 2018) indeed suggests that the experiences of CCT could be linked to increased relationship distress.

Studies investigating the links between CCT and relationship distress are, however, very limited (Bigras et al. 2017; Priest 2015). Rather, most studies have assessed associations between CCT and relationships satisfaction among community samples, finding that individuals who reported experiencing CCT tended to report lower levels of relationship satisfaction (Bradbury and Shaffer 2012; Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2019). However, individuals in these samples were generally satisfied with their relationship functioning, suggesting a potential bias toward higher‐functioning couples. Although these findings suggest possible associations between CCT and lower relationship satisfaction, further examination is needed to explore these associations in clinical samples of couples with significant relationship difficulties, such as those seeking relationship therapy. Research suggests that clinically distressed or discordant couples differ not only quantitatively but also qualitatively from nondiscordant couples (Whisman et al. 2008), highlighting the importance of studying clinical samples to better understand how CCT can contribute to significant relationship distress.

Additionally, very few studies have investigated the mechanisms linking CCT to higher relationship distress or similar outcomes (e.g., Bradbury and Shaffer 2012; Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2019). For example, in a community sample of 365 couples, Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. (2019) found that individuals with higher levels of CCT perceived their partner as less likely to disclose intimate thoughts and feelings, which partially explained the association between individuals’ CCT and their own and their partner's lower relationship satisfaction (Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2019). Exploring these mechanisms is essential, as psychotherapy may not alter past events but can address the residual consequences of CCT that affect relational well‐being in adulthood. Considering the key contribution of romantic relationships to physical and psychological health (Robles et al. 2014), it is crucial to clarify the pathways through which CCT may be associated with greater relationship distress in both partners within the couple. In turn, this could allow the identification of relevant intervention targets for couples who seek help for relationship distress.

Emotion Regulation as an Explanatory Mechanism

Bradbury and Shaffer (2012) suggested that difficulties in emotion regulation may explain the association between emotional maltreatment during childhood and relationship distress. Emotion regulation refers to the capacity to manage and endure intense and adverse emotions without relying on avoidance strategies that divert or shift attention away from emotional distress (Briere 2002). The role of emotion regulation difficulties as a potential pathway through which CCT may lead to relationship difficulties in adulthood is also supported by the Self‐Trauma Model proposed by Briere (2002). This model suggests that early and severe childhood maltreatment disrupts typical child development, predisposes individuals to respond negatively to stimuli associated with abuse, and hinders the usual acquisition of self‐capacities, especially the acquisition of skills for regulating emotions.

Consistent with the Self‐Trauma Model, several studies indicated that individuals who have a history of childhood interpersonal trauma tend to report more problems with regulating their emotions (Bradbury and Shaffer 2012; Dugal et al. 2018; Krause‐Utz et al. 2019; O'Mahen et al. 2015). Indeed, childhood is recognized as a crucial phase in the development of emotion regulation abilities (Eisenberg et al. 2010). Abusive and neglectful environments, characterized by their uncontrollable, emotionally distressing, and unpredictable nature, create conditions that hinder a child's acquisition of adaptive behaviors (Briere 2002). Instead, such environments may foster the adoption of ineffective emotion regulation strategies, such as emotional nonacceptance (e.g., feeling weak when being upset; Bjureberg et al. 2016), which can have detrimental effects on emotional well‐being in the future (Briere and Jordan 2009; O'Mahen et al. 2015). In turn, difficulties in emotional expression, awareness, and regulation can result in hypersensitivity and hyperreactivity to negative stimuli (Briere 2002), such as the everyday challenges that couples encounter, thereby creating conditions for the development and maintenance of relationship distress.

Several studies have shown associations between problems with emotion regulation and lower relationship satisfaction and relationship distress (e.g., Rick et al. 2017; Riahi et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2023). For instance, in a sample of 275 couples who were starting relationship psychotherapy, Xu et al. (2023) found that emotion dysregulation was negatively associated with relationship satisfaction. In this study, the authors highlighted the importance of studying emotion regulation difficulties within couples. Indeed, effective emotion regulation, which involves accurately recognizing and responding to emotions, plays a central role in adapting to the demands of intimate relationships, where managing emotionally challenging situations is essential for thriving (Berzenski and Yates 2010). Therefore, they argued that emotion regulation should be viewed as both an intra‐ and interpersonal process (Ben‐Naim et al. 2013). Exploring emotion regulation within romantic contexts is highly valuable for therapists, as it enhances their understanding of the intra‐ and interpersonal dimensions of emotion regulation and clarifies the links between emotion regulation and relationship dynamics. However, in this study, the authors did not consider how previous experiences of CCT could explain the emotion dysregulation and relationship distress experienced by these couples.

To our knowledge, no studies have tested the intermediary role of emotion regulation difficulties in the association between CCT and relationship distress in couples seeking psychotherapy and reporting clinically significant relationship distress. Examining this pathway in a clinical population is particularly important, as identifying emotion regulation as mechanism linking CCT to relationship distress provides clinicians with a meaningful target for intervention. Specifically, such findings could lend empirical support to therapeutic approaches that conceptualize relationship distress through the lens of emotional difficulties, including Emotion‐Focused Therapy for Couples (EFT‐C; Goldman and Greenberg 2013; Greenberg and Goldman 2008) or Developmental Couple Therapy for Complex Trauma (MacIntosh 2019). Both approaches place emotional regulation at the core of their model, emphasizing skills such as self‐soothing, mentalization, and strategies that enable partners to recognize, understand, and manage their emotions—key processes in fostering healthy relationships and overall well‐being. Thus, demonstrating the role of emotion regulation difficulties in this association would not only advance knowledge but also validate and reinforce the clinical relevance of these therapeutic models for couples affected by CCT.

Objectives and Hypotheses

The present dyadic study aimed to examine the associations between CCT experiences and both partner's relationship distress among couples entering relationship psychotherapy. The role of emotion regulation difficulties in explaining these associations was also investigated. As illustrated in Figure 1, it was expected that (1) the experience of CCT reported by an individual would be associated with their own and their partner's higher relationship distress, and that (2) individuals with higher levels of CCT would report higher levels of emotion regulation difficulties, which, in turn, would be associated with higher levels of relationship distress, as reported by both partners.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

The sample included 199 couples entering relationship psychotherapy in a private practice in a large metropolitan area in the province of Québec, Canada. Couples were recruited between 2020 and 2024. The sociodemographic characteristics of the couples are presented in Table 1. The sample comprised 7 same‐gender/sex couples and 192 mixed‐gender/sex couples, with 195 participants identifying as men, 203 as women, and 2 as non‐binary. Most couples in this study had been in their relationship for over 10 years. Additionally, 80% of the couples had at least one child, and the majority (66%) were not married. These demographics closely mirror the typical relationship profiles in the province of Quebec, Canada (Binette Charbonneau 2019). Most participants identified as White (97%), with 3% of participants identifying as Black, 5% as Latino Hispanic, 4% as Asian, and 6% as Middle Easterners. Participants could identify with more than one ethnic group. Most participants’ first language was French (86.9%) and English (7%). Half of the participants had a personal annual income exceeding CAN$90,000, and most of the participants had a university degree (74.1%).

During the couples’ first psychotherapy session, their psychologist (n = 16 psychologists) explained the procedure, the overall objectives, the risks, and the benefits associated with participating in the study. To be eligible to participate, both partners (1) had to agree to participate in the study; (2) both partners had to be 18 years of age or older; (3) needed to have access to a smartphone, tablet, or computer to complete the online questionnaires; and (4) had to be able to read and understand French or English. Involvement in the study was entirely optional, and individuals had the freedom to withdraw from the study at any time, without affecting the quality of the services they received at the clinic. This project was approved by the Research Ethics Committee in education and psychology at Université de Montréal (CEREP‐19‐069‐D).

Couples who agreed to participate in the study signed a consent form, which informed them about the study objectives, confidentiality procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without prejudice. They also consented to being contacted by the research team for study‐related purposes. Subsequently, the therapists provided both partners’ email addresses to the research assistants, who sent out a baseline online questionnaire to each partner. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaires individually, without consulting their partner. The participants’ data were protected and kept confidential, but an overview of their responses was sent to their therapist to assist in case conceptualization and treatment planning.

Measures

Sociodemographic (e.g., age, sex, gender, annual income, education) and relationship information (e.g., relationship status, duration of the relationship) were collected using multiple‐choice questions.

A shortened version of the Childhood Cumulative Trauma Questionnaire (CCTQ; Godbout et al. 2017) was used to measure CCT. This self‐report measure consists of 24 items and assesses seven types of childhood interpersonal trauma that can be experienced before the age of 18 (i.e., psychological and physical neglect, childhood sexual abuse, physical and psychological abuse, and witnessed physical and psychological violence between parental figures). To compute a score of CCT, each type of childhood interpersonal trauma was dichotomously coded (0 for absence, 1 for presence). These values were then summed, resulting in a continuous score of CCT, which ranged from 1 to 7, indicating the number of different forms of childhood interpersonal trauma experienced by the individual. The CCTQ has shown satisfactory psychometric properties (α = 0.86; Dugal et al. 2018), and the use of the shortened version does not alter the validity of the CCT score. Internal consistency was adequate (α = 0.68) in the current sample.

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale‐16 (DERS‐16; Bjureberg et al. 2016) was used to measure difficulties in emotion regulation. This self‐report measure contains 16 items (e.g., “I have difficulty making sense out of my feelings” and “When I am upset, I have difficulty focusing on other things”) which are rated on a 5‐anchor scale ranging from (1) Almost Never to (5) Almost Always. The DERS‐16 provides an overall score of emotion regulation difficulties, which is obtained by summing each item. The overall score ranges from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher levels of emotion dysregulation. The DERS‐16 showed excellent internal consistency (α = 0.92) and good test‐retest reliability with the second administration occurring between 7 and 14 days (ρ = 0.85, p < 0.001; Bjureberg et al. 2016). Moreover, convergent validity was supported by strong associations with alternative measures of emotion regulation and related constructs (Bjureberg et al. 2016). Excellent internal consistency was found in this sample (α = 0.92).

The Marital Taxon Self‐Report Measure (MTAXON; Whisman et al. 2009) designed to detect the relationship discord taxon for clinical and research purposes was used to assess relationship distress. Participants were presented with 10 items and were asked to indicate whether each statement was true or false for them (e.g., “I get pretty discouraged about our relationship sometimes”). Answers were given a score of 1 (true) or 0 (false); total scores ranged from 0 to 10. A score equal to or higher than 4 indicates significant relationship distress (Whisman et al. 2009). The MTAXON showed good internal consistency (α = 0.82 for women; α = 0.81 for men; Whisman et al. 2009) and presents an excellent ability to distinguish between individuals with clinical relationship distress and those without, indicating strong criterion validity (Whisman et al. 2009). Internal consistency was adequate (α = 0.65) in the current sample.

Analytic Strategy

Preliminary analyses were conducted using SPSS 29 to assess factors such as normality and the presence of extreme data. Main analyses were conducted with Mplus 8.4 using the maximum likelihood estimator. Missing data were addressed using the full information maximum likelihood (FIML) function. To investigate actor and partner effects while accounting for the interdependence of dyadic data, path analyses were conducted using the Actor‐Partner Interdependence Model (Kenny et al. 2006) with maximum likelihood estimation (ML). Compared to linear regression or separate mediation analyses, APIM path analysis is better suited when working with couples because it accounts for the interdependence between partners’ answers and allows the study of both direct and indirect associations within a single analytic model. In the model, actor and partner effects were estimated simultaneously. The maximum number of iterations was set to 10,000 to facilitate model convergence. To estimate the direct and indirect effects, a one‐step bootstrapping approach (Hayes 2009) was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) on 5000 samples. The model was specified according to the hypothesized directional associations among variables, including both partners’ childhood cumulative trauma (CCT) as predictors, both partners’ emotion regulation difficulties as intermediate variables, and both partners’ relationship distress as outcomes. We treated both partners as indistinguishable dyads to include same‐gender couples in the analyses. Participants’ gender was randomized in the data set, and we constrained all model parameters to be equal between partners (Kenny et al. 2006). The model fit was assessed as per four criteria: a nonsignificant chi‐square value (χ 2), a comparative fit index (CFI) greater than 0.95, and a root means square error of approximation (RMSEA), and an SRMR lower than 0.08 (Kline 2016; McDonald and Ho 2002). Residual correlation matrices were also inspected to evaluate local model fit, with residual correlations smaller than |.10| considered indicative of adequate fit.

Discussion

Using a sample of distressed couples undergoing relationship psychotherapy, we investigated the associations between both partners’ CCT experiences and relationship distress. Additionally, we explored whether difficulties in emotion regulation played a role in these associations. The findings suggest that difficulties in emotion regulation explained the association between individuals’ CCT and their own relationship distress.

CCT and Relationship Distress

Supporting our initial hypothesis, we found an association between an individual's CCT and their own relationship distress. This result is consistent with previous studies that have identified similar associations (Bigras et al. 2017; Priest 2015; Smedley and Nelson Goff 2024). Indeed, as a form of relational trauma, CCT can influence the dynamics of intimate relationships in adulthood. Interpersonal traumas often involve betrayal and neglect, fostering insecurity about the trustworthiness of others, which can impact interpersonal relationships later in life, including romantic relationships (Dugal et al. 2016). However, our study provides novel insights by specifically assessing relationship distress, as opposed to other studies that have focused on relationship satisfaction (Bradbury and Shaffer 2012; Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2019, 2021). This finding indicates that CCT is not only associated with lower satisfaction, as previous research has demonstrated, but also with higher clinically significant relationship distress. This is particularly important, as it contributes to current knowledge suggesting that experiences of CCT may generate more negative outcomes in couples, such as relationship distress. This is especially relevant in contexts where couples are dissatisfied, such as those seeking relationship psychotherapy. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to explore the associations between CCT and relationship distress in couples undergoing relationship psychotherapy. Most prior research has examined these links in the general population, focusing on couples who were satisfied with their relationships (Bradbury and Shaffer 2012; Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2019; Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2023). Therefore, our findings broaden the generalizability of these results to couples experiencing greater relational difficulties.

The Intermediary Role of Emotion Regulation Difficulties

As predicted, emotion regulation difficulties explained the association between an individual's CCT and their own relationship distress. Specifically, individuals who experienced more types of traumas reported higher emotional regulation difficulties. This finding aligns with the Self‐Trauma Model and previous research that has established associations between CCT and challenges in emotional regulation (Briere 2002; Dugal et al. 2018, 2021; Krause‐Utz et al. 2019). These associations may be explained by the fact that individuals who have experienced CCT tend to use less adaptive emotion regulation strategies (e.g., emotional nonacceptance; Briere 2002). In turn, such emotional difficulties were associated with increased relationship distress, which is also consistent with prior studies (Riahi et al. 2020; Rick et al. 2017; Xu et al. 2022).

Romantic relationships are among the most emotionally demanding dynamics (Riahi et al. 2020). Indeed, they require partners to engage in sensitive behaviors when offering support, adjust their tone during potentially stressful interactions, and manage their own experience and expression of negative emotions while monitoring and responding to partners’ reactions and behaviors. These tasks can be particularly challenging for individuals with reduced or impaired emotion regulation capacities (Riahi et al. 2020; Rick et al. 2017). Therefore, if individuals feel they lack the tools to manage their negative emotions, and this is often the case for those with greater difficulties in emotion regulation, they may experience heightened distress that can contribute to greater relationship distress (Rick et al. 2017). Thus, CCT appears to play a role in shaping adult emotional regulation skills and should be considered by clinicians, as it could contribute to increased relationship distress.

Partner Effects

Contrary to our hypothesis, individuals’ CCT and emotion regulation difficulties were not linked to their partner's higher relationship distress. This lack of association may be explained by the fact that, although the study was conducted with a clinical population and the prevalence of childhood trauma was high in our sample, relatively few individuals reported elevated levels of CCT. Indeed, individuals who have experienced a high level of trauma may encounter barriers to seeking help. For example, some may avoid psychotherapy to prevent discussing their traumatic experiences, while others may face financial difficulties (Kantor et al. 2017), which can make accessing services in private practice more challenging and may have contributed to the underrepresentation of individuals with very high levels of CCT in our sample. Moreover, emotion regulation difficulties were not particularly high in our sample. Therefore, it is possible that when overall individuals’ difficulties in emotion regulation are not severe, the distress experienced by partners may not be substantially impacted. Higher levels of CCT and difficulties with emotion regulation might reveal clearer partner effects, but further research is needed to confirm this.

Another factor that might explain our results is that, among couples seeking psychotherapy for severe relationship difficulties, more proximal factors may better account for partner distress. Indeed, long‐term couples seeking relationship psychotherapy may have been facing significant challenges that impact their relationship, such as the transition to parenthood, relationship conflict, disagreement regarding money, trust issues, sexual problems, and so forth. Moreover, couples experiencing more relationship distress may have less adaptive conflict resolution strategies compared to couples who are not experiencing such distress (Fincham and Beach 1999; Wagner et al. 2019). In the context of long‐term relationships, where couples may report higher levels of relationship distress and seek professional help for their difficulties, more proximal factors, such as those listed above, might exert a greater influence on partners’ relationship distress than their partners’ developmental history, including exposure to CCT.

Clinical Implications

These findings highlight the importance of incorporating emotion regulation strategies into therapeutic interventions for couples. One promising avenue is Emotionally Focused Therapy for Couples (EFT‐C; Goldman and Greenberg 2013; Greenberg and Goldman 2008), which guides partners in recognizing and expressing primary emotions while fostering openness, empathy, and responsiveness to each other's needs. Yet, for individuals with a history of CCT, these therapeutic tasks may present unique challenges. For example, some may avoid sharing vulnerable emotions due to fears of rejection, anger, or retaliation from their partner. While protective in the short term, such avoidance can reinforce relational difficulties and reduce opportunities for authentic connection. Adapting interventions to address these trauma‐related patterns could improve therapy effectiveness and empower couples to understand how past experiences continue to shape their present relationship dynamics. Nonetheless, the modest effect size observed suggests that additional mechanisms beyond emotion regulation contribute to the link between childhood trauma and relationship distress. Clinicians should therefore view emotion regulation work as a valuable, but partial, element within a comprehensive therapeutic framework.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

This study presents methodological strengths that bolster its contributions to the literature, including the use of a dyadic design, which allows for the consideration of both partners’ experiences within the couple. Furthermore, to our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the associations between experiences of CCT, emotion regulation difficulties, and relationship distress in couples seeking relationship psychotherapy. Indeed, most studies that have explored associations between CCT experiences and relational factors have focused on young adults in relatively satisfying relationships. Finally, the utilization of a clinical sample allowed us to investigate CCT, emotion regulation difficulties, and relationship distress within couples where these characteristics are more commonly observed.

This study nonetheless has some limitations. First, this study had a cross‐sectional correlational design. Consequently, it is not possible to infer causality nor to be sure about the direction of the associations between CCT, emotion regulation difficulties, and relationship distress. Examining these associations within a longitudinal design could help mitigate this limitation. Second, participants mostly had a high socioeconomic status and were in a mixed gender/sex relationship, which limits the generalization of the results. Future studies could include participants with more diverse ethnicities, socioeconomic statuses, and relationship statuses to see if the present results can be generalized to these different samples. Third, we utilized self‐report questionnaires to assess CCT, emotion regulation, and relationship distress, which could introduce potential biases related to desirability, recall, and self‐awareness. Finally, while we accounted for the multiple experiences of childhood trauma, we did not account for the frequency and severity of CCT experiences, limiting our ability to conclude specific to trauma severity.

Future studies could also benefit from focusing on each type of trauma within CCT to explore its differential associations with emotion regulation difficulties and relationship distress in both partners. They could also seek to identify additional, more proximal pathways through which CCT may be linked to relationship distress, for example, communication dynamics, intimacy, or sexual difficulties.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of further examining the relational repercussions of CCT to shed light on its underlying mechanisms and to develop interventions tailored for couples seeking relationship psychotherapy. Indeed, in line with previous research (Bradbury and Shaffer 2012; Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2019), individuals in our sample who have experienced CCT reported more emotion regulation difficulties and more relationship distress. Thus, working on emotion regulation strategies could be a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. Future studies could aim to conduct research into contexts where psychotherapy is more accessible and recruit individuals and couples who are more diverse in sexuality and gender.
