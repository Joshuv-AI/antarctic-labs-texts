Combined lifestyle habits, subjective well-being, and all-cause mortality among older adults in China
Authors: Chunsu Zhu, Zhiwei Lian, Jing Sun, Yongying Huang, Shengmei Li, Heng Zhang, Jianmin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough studies have demonstrated that both lifestyles and subjective well-being (SWB) are associated with mortality risk, the joint effects of a healthy lifestyle and SWB on the risk of mortality are unclear. This study aimed to explore the combined impact of lifestyles and SWB on total mortality among the older Chinese population.MethodsData were obtained from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey conducted from 2002 to 2018. A total of 13,282 participants aged ≥ 65 years were included. SWB was measured by an eight-item tool covering life satisfaction, optimism, conscientiousness, anxiety, loneliness, personal control, feeling useless, and happiness. A total healthy lifestyle score was calculated by summing across five lifestyle factors (smoking, drinking, exercise, dietary diversity and body mass index), ranging from 0 to 5, and was categorized into the following groups: healthy (4–5 points), intermediate (2–3 points) and unhealthy (0–1 point). Cox proportional regression analyses were carried out adjusting for demographic characteristics and other potential confounders.ResultsIn total, 8549 (64.4%) deaths were reported during the follow-up period. Compared with the higher healthy lifestyle score, the hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality for the intermediate and unhealthy lifestyle scores were 1.11 (1.04–1.17) and 1.17 (1.09–1.26), respectively, independent of SWB. A similar relationship between SWB and total mortality was observed, independent of lifestyle factors. The participants who had worse SWB experienced a 1.19 (1.14–1.25) higher risk of total mortality in comparison with those who had better SWB after adjusting for lifestyles. Combinations of unhealthy lifestyle scores and poor SWB were associated with an increased risk of total mortality, and the highest risk was found among participants with a healthy lifestyle score of 0–1 and worse SWB (HR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.28–1.55).ConclusionsA stronger risk of total mortality was found when worse SWB and unhealthy lifestyle occurred together, suggesting that a comprehensive approach to healthy lifestyle and SWB might be a potential strategy to improve longevity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06345-2.

Introduction

Population aging has emerged as a key public health issue both worldwide and in China [1, 2] presenting governments and caregivers with growing healthcare and socioeconomic challenges [3]. As one of the major developing countries in the world, China currently houses approximately 18% of the world’s population, and is experiencing rapid population aging [1]. In 2019, it was estimated that there were 164.5 million citizens aged 65 years and above in China, and the number is projected to be 365 million by 2050, accounting for 26.1% of China’s total population [1]. Population aging is a primary contributor to a series of chronic disorders that are all associated with lower quality of life and shorter survival in the elderly [2, 4, 5]. Considering the substantial aging population, formidable healthcare burden, and limited medical resources, it is necessary to identify cost-effective modifiable strategies that individuals can easily adopt to promote healthier, happier and longer lives in elderly individuals, especially for developing countries, such as China.

Lifestyle factors, such as smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, body mass index (BMI) and physical activity, are the most common modifiable lifestyle factors and have been reported to be individually and jointly associated with a reduced risk of mortality [6, 7]. A recent meta-analysis of 142 cohort studies reported that participants with the healthiest lifestyles had a lower risk of total mortality than those with the least-healthy lifestyles [8]. In addition, a previous study also examined the individual and combined effects of lifestyle factors on the risk of all-cause mortality among the Chinese population aged ≥ 65 years and found that a healthier lifestyle was inversely associated with total mortality [9]providing new evidence to support previous recommendations that adopting a healthy lifestyle is a “best buy” strategy for promoting longevity in the elderly [10].

Subjective well-being (SWB) is another important modifiable factor that could influence longevity [11]. It encompasses one’s cognitive and affective evaluations of their life. The cognitive aspects are related to one’s overall judgement regarding life satisfaction. While the affective components pertinent to the presence of positive emotions and moods and the absence of negative emotions and moods [12, 13]. There are growing investigations exploring the association between SWB and mortality, yet the results are still inconclusive [14–16]. Most previous studies tend to use single variables (e.g., life satisfaction, happiness, feeling of uselessness) to represent individual-level SWB, which only reflects part of the overall SWB level; thus, it is necessary to construct a comprehensive SWB variable comprising different aspects of SWB. In our previous study, we found that healthier SWB was independently associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality, suggesting that adopting a healthier SWB is a potential approach to improve longevity among older individuals [11].

Previous studies have suggested a bidirectional relationship between a healthy lifestyle and SWB [17, 18] and these factors may influence and reinforce each other. On the one hand, healthy lifestyle factors can have an impact on SWB. For instance, a study found that health behaviors, including physical activity, dietary habits, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption, are associated with SWB levels [18]. Another study also reported that a higher Mediterranean diet adherence is positively associated with a high SWB profile [19]. On the other hand, the level of SWB can influence one’s healthy behaviors. It was found that life satisfaction was significantly associated with not smoking, exercise participation, eating fruits and limited fat intake [17]. In addition, people who were generally very happy and optimistic tended to increase their physical activity more than others. However, it remains unclear whether these factors interact to influence the risk of mortality. Studying healthy lifestyles and SWB simultaneously may have important implications at both the individual and public levels. However, no study, to the best of our knowledge, has explored the combined effect of healthy lifestyles and SWB on the risk of mortality.

Therefore, building on our previous study [11] which examined the independent association between SWB and all-cause mortality, the current study aims to assess the interaction and joint associations of combined healthy lifestyles and SWB with total mortality among a nationally representative Chinese sample of the older population. We hypothesized that participants with an unhealthy lifestyle pattern or poor SWB status had an increased risk of all-cause mortality, independent of each other. We also hypothesized that a higher risk of mortality would be found among participants with unhealthy lifestyles and poor SWB compared to their counterparts.

Methods

Study population

The Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) is a prospective, nationally representative cohort study of older adults in mainland China, as described elsewhere in detail [20]. In brief, beginning in 1998, subsequent examinations of CLHLS were conducted every 2 to 3 years. To ensure representativeness, a multilevel cluster sampling method was used. In total, 631 cities or counties from 22 provinces were randomly selected, covering 85% of the Chinese population. Interviews were administered by qualified interviewers with a standardized questionnaire at the interviewees’ homes. For participants who were unable to answer questions, interviews were conducted with proxy respondents (e.g., spouses or close family members). However, questions regarding SWB were answered by the participants themselves. The CLHLS is a voluntary study, and participants were not provided with financial compensation or material rewards for their involvement. It was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and it was approved by the Duke University Healthy System’s Institutional Review Board, the National Bureau of Statistics of China, and the Ethical Committee of the Social Science Division of Peking University. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

The current study was based on six waves of the CLHLS from 2002 to 2018. The first wave (in 1998) of the CLHLS was excluded because participants aged 65–79 years were not surveyed in this wave. In total, 16,064 individuals were interviewed in 2002. Of these, 13,781 responded to the SWB items and were considered eligible for analysis. We excluded participants who had missing values for lifestyle factors (n = 382), demographic characteristics (n = 105) and physical comorbidities (n = 12). As the proportion of missing data among eligible participants was less than 5% of data, thus we did not perform imputation. Finally, 13,282 participants were included in further analyses. The baseline characteristics of participants included and excluded in our study were compared in supplementary Table S1. The participants excluded in our study tend to be older, be female, be widowed/separated/others, be illiteracy, and are less likely to have hypertension, diabetes, cancer and heart disease.

Subjective well-being

SWB was measured with a previously validated tool [12] as described in our earlier methodology [11]. The tool included eight items1) life satisfaction, 2) optimism, 3) conscientiousness, 4) anxiety, 5) loneliness, 6) personal control, 7) feeling of uselessness, and 8) happiness. The questions used to assess these items are provided in supplementary Table S2. Each item was coded as 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5, and negative items were reverse coded. The total SWB score was calculated by summing scores across all items, varying from 5 to 40, with a higher score indicating better SWB. Participants whose SWB scores were above the third quartile (≥ 31) were classified as having better SWB, while the others were regarded as having worse SWB, following previous studies [12, 21]. The Cronbach’s α for the internal consistency of the SWB scale was 0.68 and the confirmatory factor analysis by a previous study suggested this SWB scale has good validity among the older population [12].

Healthy lifestyles

Lifestyles of interest in the current study included dietary diversity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, exercise, and BMI. Dietary diversity data were collected at baseline based on a food frequency questionnaire including seven major food groups (fruits, vegetables, fish, eggs, meat, beans, and tea). Responses for each food group were recoded as “often or almost every day”, “occasionally”, or “rarely or never”. Participants who answered “often or almost every day” received one point, the others were scored as zero point. The total dietary diversity score was calculated via summing the points across all food groups, ranging from 0 to 7, with higher score indicating better dietary diversity. Individuals with a total dietary diversity score at or above the mean value were classified as having good food dietary diversity, following previous studies [9, 22, 23].

Body weight was recorded to the nearest kilogram with participants wearing light clothing. Height was estimated using knee height, measured as the vertical distance from the sole to the upper surface of the knee with both the knee and ankle flexed at a 90° angle. A validated equation was applied: height = 67.78 + 2.01 × knee height for men and height = 74.08 + 1.81 × knee height for women [24]. BMI was calculated by weight in kilograms divided by the squared height (m [2]), and was classified into four groups: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2), overweight (24.0 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28.0 kg/m2) and obese (BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2) [25]. The question “do you exercise regularly at present?” was used to assess the exercise, and the response was recorded as yes or no. Smoking status (never, current and former) and alcohol consumption (never, current and former) were self-reported at baseline. Individuals who had good dietary diversity, never smoked, never drank, exercised regularly, and had normal weight were categorized as healthy. For each lifestyle, participants received a score of 1 if the individuals met the criteria for health, or 0 if they did not. The total lifestyle score was calculated by summing the points across all lifestyle factors for a maximum score of 5, with higher scores indicating a healthier lifestyle [8, 26]. We classified the participants into the following three lifestyle groups: unhealthy (0–1), intermediate (2–3) and healthy (4–5), following previous studies [9].

Mortality

To ensure the completeness and accuracy of mortality data, interviews with close family members or village doctors were conducted in each follow-up survey in 2005, 2008, 2011, 2014, and 2018. The individuals who survived in 2018 wave or who were lost-to follow-up were defined as censored cases. Participants who could not be reached or contacted after more than three reasonable efforts were defined as ‘loss to follow-up’. Previous studies have suggested that data on mortality in CLHLS are of high quality and have been widely used [27, 28].

Covariates

Covariates were collected using a standardized questionnaire at baseline by qualified interviewers. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, educational level (illiteracy [no education], literacy [≥ 1 year education]), marital status (married, widowed/separated/singled/others), and place of residence (urban/city, rural). The urban/city and rural area were classified based on the definitions provided by the National Bureau of Statistic China. Physical comorbidities were defined based on a self-report of doctor’s diagnosis, including hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, cerebrovascular diseases and cancer.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented across different levels of healthy lifestyles, and differences among groups were compared by analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazard regression models were fitted to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of all-cause mortality associated with SWB and healthy lifestyle scores. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed graphically, and no evidence of obvious departure from this assumption was detected. The person-years were calculated from baseline until the date of death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever came first. Three models were fitted: model 1 was unadjusted to calculate the crude HR and 95% CI for the SWB and lifestyle score with total mortality; model 2 was adjusted by age and sex; model 3 was further adjusted for marital status, place of residence, educational level, and physical comorbidities (including hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, and stroke); and model 4 was mutually adjusted for SWB and healthy lifestyle score. In these analyses, the healthy lifestyle (with a lifestyle score of 4–5) and the better SWB group (SWB score ≥ 31) were regarded as the reference group.

Stratified analyses were further carried out to investigate the associations of SWB with all-cause mortality among elderly Chinese individuals in different lifestyle groups, and vice versa. To examine the joint associations, we classified the participants into six groups according to SWB (better, worse) and lifestyle score (healthy, intermediate, unhealthy) and estimated the HRs of all-cause mortality in different groups, in comparison with those with better SWB and healthy lifestyles.

Several sensitivity analyses were further conducted to test the robustness. We repeated the analyses for the combination of healthy lifestyles and SWB under the following scenarios. First, to reduce reverse causation, we excluded those who died within the first year of follow-up because those who died quickly may have pre-existing conditions influencing their lifestyles, SWB and mortality risk. Second, we excluded those aged 85 years and above because they are likely to have a higher risk of death than their younger counterparts. Finally, we excluded those with potentially life-threatening diseases, including heart disease, cancer and cerebrovascular disease because both SWB and lifestyles could be influenced by major chronic diseases. All analyses were carried out using SPSS, version 26.0 and R software version 3.6.1, and two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered to be significant.

During the follow-up period between 2002 and 2018, 8549 (64.4%) deaths occurred. The crude mortality rate was higher among participants with lower healthy lifestyle scores and lower level of SWB (Table 2). After adjusting for a wide range of potential confounders (model 3), participants with worse SWB at baseline were associated with a 21% increased risk of total mortality (HR = 1.21, 95% CI = 1.15–1.26), compared with participants with better SWB. After further adding the healthy lifestyle score into the model, the HR between SWB and total mortality was reduced slightly, but the significance remained similar. Compared with participants who had a healthy lifestyle score of 4–5 (healthy), the adjusted HR of participants with a healthy lifestyle score of 0–1 (unhealthy) was 1.22 (95% CI = 1.13–1.31), after adjusting for potential confounding factors. The associations between healthy lifestyles and total mortality were mildly diminished after additionally adjusting for SWB (Table 2). The results of the stratification analyses are shown in Figure 1. We found associations of SWB with all-cause mortality among older individuals with healthier lifestyles (4-5) and intermediate healthy lifestyles (2-3), while no such association was detected among those with unhealthy lifestyles (0-1) (Figure 1A). Moreover, the associations of healthy lifestyle with total mortality remained consistent among older participants with better SWB, but not among those with poor SWB. The interaction between SWB and healthy lifestyle score on mortality risk was statistically significant (p for interaction < 0.001), indicating that the protective effect of a healthy lifestyle may depend on the levels of SWB.

The HRs for the joint association are shown in Figure 2. As expected, the combination of better SWB and higher healthy lifestyle scores was associated with a reduced risk of total mortality, and the highest mortality risk was observed in participants with a healthy lifestyle score of 0-1 and concurrently with a worse SWB status (HR=1.41, 95% CI=1.28-1.55) in the fully adjusted model. Compared with participants with a healthy lifestyle score of 4-5 and better SWB, unhealthy lifestyle (0-1) was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality in the presence of poor SWB: the adjusted HR increased from 1.31 (unhealthy lifestyle and better SWB) to 1.41 (unhealthy lifestyle and worse SWB). Compared with the reference group, the presence of poor SWB also increased the risk of total mortality for the intermediate healthy lifestyle (2-3): the HR increased from 1.16 (intermediate lifestyle and better SWB) to 1.37 (intermediate lifestyle and worse SWB) in the fully adjusted model.

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort study of older adults aged ≥ 65 years in mainland China, we examined the association between combined healthy lifestyle, SWB and all-cause mortality, and found that participants with unhealthy lifestyle patterns or poor SWB experienced a higher risk of total mortality than their counterparts. During an average follow-up period of 16.5 years, individuals with an unhealthy lifestyle had a 17% higher risk of total mortality than those with a healthy lifestyle, independent of SWB. Similar associations were detected between SWB and all-cause mortality, independent of healthy lifestyle. The results of stratified and joint analyses suggested that participants with a healthier lifestyle may benefit more from maintaining a positive SWB status, and vice versa. Combinations of a lower healthy lifestyle score and poor SWB were associated with a significantly increased risk of total mortality, and the highest mortality risk was observed among participants with a healthy lifestyle score of 0-1 and concurrently with a worse SWB status. Significant multiplicative interactions of healthy lifestyle score with SWB on total mortality exist.

Our findings that the healthy lifestyle score was negatively associated with the risk of all-cause mortality were in line with previous studies [7, 10, 29]. Specifically, compared with participants who led a healthy lifestyle, those who led an intermediate or unhealthy lifestyle had an 11% and 17% increased risk of total mortality, respectively. The HRs were mildly reduced after the adjustment for SWB. Growing evidence has reported that a healthier lifestyle pattern is associated with reductions in all-cause and cause-specific mortality, further supporting our findings [29–32]. The Seguimiento Universidad de Navarra cohort in Spain analyzed 20,094 participants with a median follow-up of 10.8 years, and found that the highest adherence to a healthy lifestyle showed a 60% lower risk of total mortality than the lowest adherence category [33]. The Singapore Chinese Health Study (SCHS) of 44,052 Chinese adults showed that adopting five healthy lifestyle factors was associated with a lower risk of total mortality (HR=0.38, 95% CI=0.27-0.51), compared to the reference group (no factor) [31]. The Healthy and Lifestyle Survey (HLS) among 8123 UK adults also reported that a 1-point increase in the unhealthy lifestyle score was associated with a 9% increased risk of mortality [32]. In addition, associations between healthy lifestyle patterns and the risk of total mortality have been found in several meta-analyses and systematic reviews [6, 8].

While several studies have examined the association between SWB and mortality [14, 15, 34, 35], most did not take the role of lifestyle factors or their potential interaction with SWB into consideration. In our previous study, we found that higher overall SWB, particularly items such as feelings of uselessness and happiness, are independently associated with mortality risk [11].The current study extends that work by being, to the best of our knowledge, the first to prospectively measure the combined and interactive impact of healthy lifestyles and SWB on total mortality in a nationally representative sample of older adults. Consistent with our hypotheses, we found that individuals with poor SWB at baseline had a 21% higher risk of total mortality than those with better SWB. Additional adjustment for healthy lifestyles slightly reduced these HRs, but the significance was similar. In addition, the protective effect of a healthy lifestyle was evident only among those with higher SWB, and the inverse association between SWB and mortality was significant only among those with intermediate or healthy lifestyle scores. The greatest mortality risk was observed among participants with both poor SWB and the lowest lifestyle scores. These findings suggest that promoting healthy lifestyles or SWB alone may be insufficient; integrated interventions targeting both may be more effective in improving the longevity of older adults.

Several potential pathways may explain the relationship of healthy lifestyles and SWB with mortality risk. First, participants with a healthier lifestyle are more likely to experience enhanced physical health, and when coupled with better SWB—characterized by life satisfaction and positive emotions—this synergy may amplify the protective effects and reduced mortality risk [17]. Second, positive SWB can motivate individuals to engage in- and maintain health-promoting behaviors. Participants with better SWB are more likely to have beneficial lifestyles, such as regular exercise and balanced diet, which contributes to longevity [36, 37]. Third, poor SWB and unhealthy lifestyles may increase the mortality risk through several potential biological mechanisms. For example, cigarette smoking is associated with increased inflammation, thrombosis and oxidation stress, all of which could significantly influence the cardiovascular risk [38]. In contrast, regular exercise training or physical activity could lower blood pressure, regulate glucose metabolism, and reduce oxidative stress—factors known to prevent major diseases and premature deaths [39, 40]. Additionally, high SWB was reported to be associated with lower cortisol levels [41], lower inflammation [41, 42], and alteration of metabolic parameters (e.g., plasma cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin) [41], which may impact the risk of mortality [43]. Despite these insights, the joint effect of healthy lifestyle and SWB on the risk of all-cause mortality among older people remains less understood. More evidence from diverse populations is needed.

This study has several strengths. First, we assessed the combined impact of healthy lifestyle and SWB on total mortality among older Chinese people—a relationship that has been largely overlooked in previous research. Second, the prospective study design, nationally representative sample, and large number of mortality events allowed us to produce robust and reliable results, which improved the generalizability of our findings. In addition, our results have practical implications: they may motivate individuals to change their lifestyle patterns and maintain positive SWB, while also provide confidence to primary care providers to encourage their patients to address these modifiable risk factors to promote longevity. This is particularly relevant given the rapidly growing aging population and the increasing emphasis on healthy aging. Finally, many sensitivity analyses were performed to supplement the main results and show the robustness of our findings.

However, several limitations should also be noted. First, healthy lifestyles and SWB were only assessed once at baseline, and cause-specific mortality data were unavailable in the CLHLS. This limited our ability to examine long-term trajectory and to differentiate deaths unrelated to lifestyle and SWB. Future studies with repeated measures and cause-specific mortality data are needed. Second, approximately 14.2% of older participants were unable to answer questions for the SWB items, which may be attributable to their poor health status (e.g., cognitive dysfunction, dementia) [44]. The excluded participants are generally older than those included, potentially resulting in a younger and healthier sample, which may underestimate the effect of healthy lifestyle and SWB on mortality risk. However, significant associations between healthy lifestyle, SWB and total mortality were still observed, highlighting the necessity of promoting a healthier lifestyle and better SWB among older people. Moreover, participants with a healthier lifestyle (scores 4–5) were more likely to report chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, suggesting potential reverse causation. Individuals may have adopted healthier behaviors following diagnosis or due to increased medical contact and health education. To deal with the potential reverse causation, we conducted several sensitivity analyses by excluding participants with advancing age (≥85 years), those who died within the first year of follow-up, and those with potentially fatal diseases (e.g., cancer, heart disease, cerebrovascular diseases) at baseline; the results remained stable. Third, the healthy lifestyle score was created by summing the number of each healthy lifestyle factor, assuming that each factor was weighted equally on mortality, which may not be true in the real world. For instance, individuals who had a balanced diet and/or participated in regular exercise are more likely to have healthy weight and normal BMI [45, 46]. Nevertheless, the simple calculation of the healthy lifestyle score is much easier for patients to understand and is more applicable for healthcare providers to communicate with patients in medical practice. Additionally, several lifestyle factors were assessed through self-report and with limited details. Physical activity was measured using a simple yes/no question, lacking data on frequency, duration, or intensity. Similarly, dietary assessment focused solely on diversity without quantity, and smoking and alcohol use were not quantified. These measurement limitations may affect the prevision and generalizability of our findings and have been acknowledged in previous studies using CLHLS data [47, 48]. Finally, although a wide range of confounding factors were controlled in our analyses, residual bias may still exist. Notably, the absence of data on important factors, such as depression, dementia, and socioeconomic status could lead to residual confounding. For instance, if higher SWB is associated with lower rates of depression or higher socioeconomic status, and these factors independently reduce mortality risk, our findings may overestimate the effect of SWB on mortality. Future studies should include these factors to better clarify the complex interplay between SWB, lifestyle, and health in older adults.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this nationally representative cohort study of older Chinese adults, we found that participants with unhealthy lifestyles had a significantly increased risk of total mortality, independent of SWB. Similar associations were observed between SWB and total mortality, independent of healthy lifestyles. Combinations of a lower healthy lifestyle score and poor SWB were associated with an increased risk of total mortality. In light of the rapid population aging and restricted health resources in China, developing an integrated approach to address both healthy lifestyle factors and SWB, such as changing unhealthy lifestyles and/or maintaining a healthy SWB status, might be an affordable and feasible strategy to improve the survival and longevity of older people. Future investigations are needed to explore this issue in different populations.
