Monitoring terrestrial water storage changes using GNSS vertical coordinate time series in Amazon River basin
Authors: Yifu Liu, Keke Xu, Zengchang Guo, Sen Li, Yongzhen Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAiming at the Terrestrial Water Storage(TWS) changes in the Amazon River basin, this article uses the coordinate time series data of the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), adopts the Variational Mode Decomposition and Bidirectional Long and Short Term Memory(VMD-BiLSTM) method to extract the vertical crustal deformation series, and then adopts the Principal Component Analysis(PCA) method to invert the changes of terrestrial water storage in the Amazon Basin from July 15, 2012 to July 25, 2018. Then, the GNSS inversion results were compared with the equivalent water height retrieved from Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) data. The results show that (1) the extraction method proposed in this article has better denoising effect than the traditional method; (2) the surface hydrological load deformation can be well calculated using GNSS coordinate vertical time series, and then the regional TWS changes can be inverted, which has a good consistency with the result of GRACE inversion of water storage, and has almost the same seasonal variation characteristics; (3) There is a strong correlation between TWS changes retrieved by GNSS based on surface deformation characteristics and water mass changes calculated by GRACE based on gravitational field changes, but GNSS satellite’s all-weather measurement results in a finer time scale compared with GRACE inversion results. In summary, GNSS can be used as a supplementary technology for monitoring terrestrial water storage changes, and can complement the advantages of GRACE technology.Keywords: GNSS, GRACE, VMD-BiLSTM, PCA, TWSSubject terms: Hydrology, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Terrestrial Water Storage (TWS) encompasses the total volume of water resources present on the Earth’s land surface and subsurface, encompassing rivers, lakes, groundwater, glaciers, snow, wetlands, and other water forms1. Presently, TWS and the water cycle face numerous challenges and transformations. Climate change, human activities, urbanization, and industrialization have contributed to freshwater resources accessible to humans accounting for only 0.26% of total water, with the majority being groundwater, and the fraction available from lakes and rivers comprising a mere 0.014% of the total. This scarcity highlights the critical need for careful exploitation and utilization of these limited freshwater resources2. Despite their scarcity, terrestrial water resources play a vital role in sustaining human society’s industry, agriculture, and daily life3. The Amazon Basin, situated in South America, stands as the world’s largest drainage system and river system, covering approximately 7,000,000 square kilometers. Abundant precipitation, rivers, lakes, wetlands, and ice and snow contribute to its rich water resources4. The effective management and preservation of these water resources are essential for their sustainable use and the overall health of ecosystems5. Consequently, accurate monitoring of regional hydrological load deformation and the temporal and spatial terrestrial water storage changes holds great scientific significance. It aids in understanding the dynamic process of vertical crustal deformation, establishing a high-precision crustal reference frame, and unveiling the patterns of land water cycle6.

With the rapid development of the GRACE, it has become increasingly integral in research fields such as TWS inversion7. Launched in March 2002 as a collaborative mission between NASA and the German Aerospace Center (Deutsches Zentrum für Luft- und Raumfahrt, DLR), the GRACE satellites provides valuable earth science data by accurately measuring the Earth’s gravity field8,9. This allows for effective monitoring of large-scale mass migration on the ground, including significant water mass changes10,11. However, the inherent characteristics of GRACE gravity satellites, such as low spatiotemporal resolution (1°×1° spatial and monthly temporal), limit their application in detailed time and mass change inversion in small areas12.

Currently, GNSS data combined with GRACE gravity satellite data have been employed to analyze both crustal tectonics and non-tectonic deformation. In regions with dense GNSS stations, GNSS vertical deformation time series can accurately invert terrestrial water storage changes13–15. Despite its global coverage and all-weather, high-precision capabilities, the uneven distribution of GNSS stations limits its applicability in terrestrial water storage changes studies16,17. Recent years have witnessed considerable research on TWS in the Amazon River Basin, primarily based on traditional processing of GNSS or GRACE data. The rapid advancement of machine learning introduces new possibilities for terrestrial water storage changes research in this region, as seen in its application to GNSS time series feature extraction, deformation monitoring, and GRACE equivalent water change prediction18–20. For instance, in reference21, a method combining the removal-recovery technique with autoregressive prediction proves effective in capturing variations in TWS for regional analysis. However, it is imperative that the climatology used for removal aligns appropriately with the selected region. Reference22 employs a non-linear autoregressive external input neural network technique to predict anomalies in land water storage. While this method yields the best correlation coefficients in the Amazon basin, its performance is less satisfactory in the Yangtze basin. Another scholar utilizes Singular Spectrum Analysis for predicting TWS change rates23, achieving favorable outcomes.

Reference24 introduces a novel and fully non-recursive model known as Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD). VMD is a data decomposition method that breaks down a signal into a set of base functions and their corresponding amplitude modulation functions25. In comparison to Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), VMD exhibits greater stability. EMD often faces the challenge of mode mixing during signal decomposition, where in certain signal components may be distributed across multiple Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMF), resulting in an unstable decomposition26. However, VMD offers the advantage of controlling the number of generated modes during the decomposition process. By adjusting regularization parameters, it becomes possible to govern the quantity of generated base functions and amplitude modulation functions, providing flexibility to adapt to the distinct decomposition requirements of various signals. This capability helps mitigate issues of instability or excessive mode quantities, thereby offering improved control over the results27. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models exhibit excellent capabilities in handling non-stationary signals, effectively capturing long-term dependencies between sequences28. Currently, most re-search on GRACE remains focused on the application of GRACE products29–31, with limited studies on prediction. Among the existing prediction studies, applications of LSTM methods are often unidirectional, combining them with traditional methods or enhancing network structures20,32. There is relatively less research on bidirectional predictions, specifically using Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) models. This model can extract features from both forward and backward directions, considering information from future time steps, thereby enhancing sensitivity to pat-terns and dependencies within the sequences33.

This paper, utilizing GNSS vertical time series data from the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory (NGL) and the International Center for Global Earth Models, builds upon the latest GRACE/GRACE-FO data (RL06 version) obtained from the International Center for Global Earth Models (ICGEM). The study focuses on feature extraction of GNSS deformation time series and the joint inversion of terrestrial water storage changes based on GNSS and GRACE data. The findings indicate that GNSS and GRACE complement each other in monitoring daily terrestrial water storage changes, thereby further confirming the application of surface monitoring technology and gravity technology in hydrological monitoring.

Materials and methods

Study area

Situated in South America, the Amazon River Basin stands as one of the largest river basins globally, acclaimed as the largest freshwater river system on earth34. Encompassing nine South American countries—Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Venezuela, Bolivia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, and Suriname—the Amazon River basin spans over 7 million square kilometers, constituting approximately one-fifth of the world’s freshwater resources. This vast basin is characterized by a network of numerous tributaries and rivers, with the Amazon River itself being the principal river, boasting a length of about 6,400 km and ranking among the world’s largest rivers35.

The Amazon River traverses through the rainforest, creating a vital habitat for a myriad of wildlife species36. Consequently, the study of terrestrial water storage changes in the Amazon River Basin holds immense significance for the preservation of species diversity within the basin and the sustainability of the environment and development in this region. Fortunately, the Amazon River basin benefits from a well-established network of densely distributed GNSS reference stations, providing a substantial advantage for TWS research37. Research data and processing

GNSS measurement

The Amazon River Basin is equipped with a network of dense GNSS observation stations38, providing relatively abundant data for studying crustal deformation. This paper utilizes time series data from 34 GNSS stations, sourced from the NGL. The dataset includes observed values for each station, spanning year, month, day, three directions (E, N, U), latitude, longitude, among other parameters.

In GNSS time series, the E and N directions are primarily influenced by geological structure changes and are less susceptible to environmental loads39. Consequently, this study focuses on GNSS vertical time series, as the horizontal component is less sensitive to non-geological structures. The vertical time series data for the 34 GNSS stations in the Amazon River Basin are predominantly affected by non-geological structures such as non-tidal atmosphere and ocean40. This results in the vertical time series being impacted by seasonal terms and vertical noise but is minimally affected by trend terms.

Outliers, defined as values exceeding three times the median error of each site sequence, are considered in this study. Sequences with a missing degree of more than one year are excluded, and linear interpolation is applied to complete missing values. Additionally, a linear fitting method is used to extract and remove trend components.

Non-tidal atmospheric and oceanic load corrections for each station are obtained from the Earth System Modeling Group at Deutsches GeoForschungsZentrum (ESMGFZ). We use the sliding window method to calculate the daily correction, in other words, to calculate the average value at fixed intervals. Since the data acquisition interval is 3 h, the average value of every 8 data is calculated as the single-day correction, that is, the window size is 8. The results calculated by the above method are daily Non-Tidal Atmospheric Load (NTAL) and Non-Tidal Ocean Load (NTOL) for each site13,41–43. Subsequently, the NTAL and NTOL terms are subtracted based on the GNSS coordinate vertical time series after linear interpolation.

Following the deduction of mass load factors, including non-tidal atmospheric and ocean loads, from the vertical time series, this paper employs the rapidly evolving machine learning method. VMD, in conjunction with the Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) algorithm, conducts modal decomposition of GNSS vertical time series data and synthesizes effective signals that meet the Sample Entropy (SE) decision condition44,45. The synthesized eigenmode function components are then utilized to extract the changes in GNSS time series, allowing for the extraction of the GNSS hydrological load signal characteristics in the Amazon River Basin.

GRACE spherical harmonic coefficient (SHC) products

This paper relies on the most recent RL06 version of GRACE/GRACE-FO data ac-quired from the International Center for Global Earth Models (ICGEM), spanning from April 2002 to December 2022. The dataset comprises a total of 216 months, with the highest order encompassing a set of lunar harmonic coefficients up to order 60. Figure 2 illustrates a total of 35 months of missing data, including some non-consecutive missing months and an 11-month gap during the vacuum period between GRACE de-commissioning and GRACE-FO liftoff.

Absence of GRACE Spherical Harmonic Coefficient File.

Figure 2 illustrates that there are sporadic missing data points since the launch of the GRACE satellite in 2002 and a more extensive period of missing data from 2011 to 2016. The entire dataset is unavailable from the second half of 2017 to the first half of 2018. In the case of missing GRACE data, the monthly spherical harmonic coefficient files in GRACE RL06 data result in the absence of entire monthly data files for the periods with missing data. To address this, we opted for singular spectrum analysis interpolation, as referenced in46, to ensure the reliability and consistency of the interpolated data.

Given that GRACE satellites lack sensitivity to low-order terms of the gravity field and cannot provide first-order terms47, pre-processing of GRACE data necessitates an increase in low-order terms. Due to the suboptimal measurement of C20 items by GRACE, Satellite Laser Ranging (SLR) ranging data is utilized to solve for C20 items, serving as a replacement for C20 items in the GRACE coefficient48. Additionally, Gaussian filtering with a radius of 300 km is applied to denoise high-order noise, and the Swenson de-striping method is employed to eliminate the correlation of spherical harmonic coefficients49.

Nontectonic deformation of the Earth’s crust typically transpires gradually, unfolding over extended time scales, often spanning years or more. Crustal subsidence and uplift stand out as primary contributors to changes in crustal surface elevation57. These alterations primarily stem from factors such as atmospheric load, land water storage, ocean load disturbances, and human activities58. Among these, the impact of changes in land water storage assumes a predominant role in nontectonic deformation of the crust. In the context of utilizing GNSS vertical deformation to infer TWS changes, it becomes imperative to subtract the effects of geological nonstructural deformation16,59. Figure 16 reveals that the EWH changes from GNSS and GRACE inversion exhibit comparable seasonal variations62, and both showed the same temporal variation. In view of this, there is a strong correlation between TWS changes retrieved by GNSS based on surface deformation characteristics and water mass changes calculated by GRACE based on gravitational field changes63,64. The results show that the variation of TWS in the Amazon River basin increases in spring and summer, and decreases in autumn and winter, and the variation is large. In terms of years, TWS changes in this region showed a steady upward trend from 2013 to 2015, but there was a sharp decrease in 2016, and then returned to the characteristics of increasing year by year. The calculated results further show that the maximum and minimum values of GNSS inversion results can reach 255.8 mm and − 288.3 mm, and the inversion results of GRACE inversion results can reach 248.5 mm and − 247.4 mm. The inversion results of the two are comparable, and their Pearson correlation coefficient can reach 0.8638, which has a strong consistency. However, GNSS technology can provide coordinate information of all-weather single-day solutions, while GRACE technology can only provide monthly gravity data at present. Therefore, GNSS results as shown in Fig. 16 have a relatively fine time scale, while GRACE results only have monthly values.

Conclusion

In this study, the innovative application of the VMD-BiLSTM method for extracting GNSS hydrological load deformation, combined with GNSS coordinate time series and GRACE time-varying gravity spherical harmonic coefficients, contributes to the examination of terrestrial water storage changes in the Amazon River Basin. A comprehensive comparative analysis and interpretation of the research results lead to the following findings:

The VMD-BiLSTM method used in this paper has a good forecasting ability for time series45. Combined with the PCA method, the vertical crustal displacement caused by hydrological changes can be effectively extracted, then the EWH time series of Amazon River basin can be calculated by Green’s function method, which can be used for GNSS inversion of terrestrial water storage changes.

The VMD-BiLSTM method used in this paper has a good forecasting ability for time series45. Combined with the PCA method, the vertical crustal displacement caused by hydrological changes can be effectively extracted, then the EWH time series of Amazon River basin can be calculated by Green’s function method, which can be used for GNSS inversion of terrestrial water storage changes.

The combined inversion of GNSS and GRACE data reveals similar seasonal variation characteristics between the two technologies16. There is a strong correlation between TWS changes retrieved by GNSS based on surface deformation characteristics and water mass changes calculated by GRACE based on gravitational field changes. In summary, GNSS proves valuable as an auxiliary technology for monitoring terrestrial water storage changes, complementing the advantages of GRACE technology.
