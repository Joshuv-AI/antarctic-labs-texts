Comparison of in‐Hospital Complication Rates After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Patients With Bicuspid Versus Tricuspid Aortic Valves: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Tingxi Zhu, Jiawei Luo, Xuan Huang, Lulu Liu, Kehan Li, Wei He, Xiaobo Zhou, Yingqiang Guo, Xiaoyan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become a popular alternative to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) for patients with valvular heart disease (VHD), particularly for those with aortic anomalies.AimsThe study aimed to compare the risks of post‐TAVR complications between patients with bicuspid and tricuspid aortic valves and to identify associated risk factors.MethodsThe association between complications and valve type (bicuspid or tricuspid) was assessed. The study also explored various combinations of factors to understand their impact on complications. Separate analyses were conducted to identify specific risk factors for each complication.ResultsOut of the 1154 eligible patients, 508 had bicuspid aortic valves (BAV) and 646 had tricuspid aortic valves (TAV). The study identified 40 cases of acute kidney injury, 134 instances of new‐onset permanent pacemaker implantations, 129 occurrences of paravalvular leak, and 30 device successes. The comprehensive logistic regression revealed no statistically significant association between complications and valve type (ORs: 0.52 (95% CI, 0.23−1.09) for acute kidney injury, 1.29 (95% CI, 0.87−1.92) for permanent pacemaker implantation, 1.29 (95% CI, 0.82−2.02) for paravalvular leak, and 0.54 (95% CI, 0.22−1.23) for device success). Total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin (ALB), age, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) scores, among other factors, were associated with specific post‐TAVR complications.ConclusionsThe incidence of acute kidney injury, new permanent pacemaker implantations, paravalvular leaks, and device success did not differ significantly between patients with BAV and TAV following TAVR. Specific risk factors for these complications were identified, highlighting the importance of careful clinical monitoring in post‐TAVR management.

Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) and aortic regurgitation (AR) are the most common causes of valvular heart disease (VHD) in the Western world. These conditions have affected more than 41 million people since 2000, leading to significant premature mortality and reducing quality of life [1, 2, 3]. Historically, the primary treatment for VHD was the high‐risk open‐heart procedure known as surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) [4, 5]. However, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has recently emerged as a viable interventional treatment modality. It is now a well‐established therapy, particularly for inoperable and high‐risk patients with clinical VHD [5, 6, 7, 8].

In the general population, the aortic valve typically comprises three similar, semilunar‐shaped aortic cusps, termed the tricuspid aortic valve (TAV). In contrast, some individuals possess only two leaflets of unequal size, which is referred to as a bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) [9]. BAV is the most prevalent congenital heart valve anomaly, present in approximately 0.5%−2% of the global population [10, 11]. Notably, in our data, the ratio of BAV to TAV is approximately 0.79, indicating a significant proportion of BAV cases. The distinct valve anatomy in BAV patients can lead to an “annulus‐valve” mismatch, complicating the selection of an appropriate TAVR system size for physicians. Additionally, certain BAV patients may exhibit associated aortic abnormalities, such as a dilated ascending aorta, potentially resulting in macrovascular complications [9]. Although some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have investigated the post‐TAVR complications in BAV patients, the findings have been inconsistent [12, 13, 14]. Over recent years, many researchers have called for TAVR to be adopted more widely in BAV patients. However, due to the novelty of TAVR and its associated high costs, many relevant studies have been constrained by small sample sizes. Based on an early‐stage literature review conducted by our team, while the number of studies on TAVR in BAV patients has increased from 2014 to 2022, over 60% of these investigations included fewer than 100 BAV patients, and 85% had fewer than 500 BAV participants (Table S1).

West China Hospital, the leading general hospital in southwestern China, was one of the first in the country to introduce TAVR. Its comprehensive electronic medical record (EMR) system, encompassing both the Hospital Information System (HIS) and the Laboratory Information System (LIS), has chronicled medical histories of over 7 million patients annually since 2000. This wealth of data, which includes detailed valve information, sociodemographic profiles, socioeconomic statuses, and a substantial record of TAVR cases, offers a unique research opportunity.

In this study, our objective is to evaluate the post‐TAVR complication risks in VHD patients with BAV in comparison to those with TAV. We also examine the specific risk factors associated with each complication.

Materials and Methods

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study Population

The HIS and LIS recorded data on 2393 middle‐aged patients who underwent TAVR at West China Hospital between 2010 and 2022. The hospital's EMR system automatically logged patient information during their stay, including sociodemographic details such as age and sex, health‐related metrics like body mass index (BMI) and New York Heart Association (NYHA) score, laboratory indices such as white blood cell count (WBC) and direct bilirubin (DBIL), as well as pre‐existing conditions like hypertension and diabetes. Patients who lacked a definitive aortic valve type or were missing more than 75% of their laboratory tests were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 1154 patients were included in the research. The detailed patient selection process is shown in Figure 1.

The flowchart of including and dividing patients.

Valve Types

Valve data were extracted from medical records, discharge summaries, in‐hospital examination results, and surgical reports. Cases with discrepancies between different records underwent further verification for study eligibility.

Complications

This study monitored four complications: acute kidney injury (AKI), new‐onset permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI), paravalvular leak, and device success. TAVR complications were defined according to the Valvular Academic Research Consortium‐3 (VARC‐3) guidelines. AKI was defined as an increase in serum creatinine ≥ 150%–200% within 7 days compared with baseline or an increase of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (≥ 26.4 μmol/L) within 48 h of the index procedure. PPI was defined as a new permanent pacemaker implanted during hospitalization after TAVR; paravalvular leak was defined as any degree of paravalvular leak that occurs during hospitalization; device success was defined as patients undertook a successfully TAVR with technical success and freedom from mortality, surgery or intervention related to the device or to a major vascular or access‐related issues, or cardiac structural issues, as well as achieving the intended performance of the valve (mean gradient < 20 mmHg, peak velocity < 3 m/s, Doppler velocity index ≥ 0.25, and less than moderate AR).

Covariates

Data on sociodemographic characteristics such as age and sex, health‐related factors like BMI and NYHA score, laboratory indices (including WBC, DBIL, among others), and underlying diseases (specifically hypertension and diabetes) were extracted from the EMR. BMI was calculated using height (in cm) and weight (in kg) recorded at admission. NYHA scores of III or IV were categorized as high‐level.

In our main analysis, all factors that might influence the incidence of complications were treated as potential confounders, with the exception of valve type (BAV vs. TAV), as suggested by prior literature. The covariates were classified into three categories: (1) Sociodemographic features, encompassing age, sex, and BMI. (2) Cardiac health indicators, such as hypertension, NYHA score, troponin, and myoglobin. (3) General health metrics of other organs, which included measures like hemoglobin, platelet count (PC), red blood cell (RBC) count, WBC count, total bilirubin (TBIL), DBIL, indirect bilirubin (IBIL), aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase ratio (ASL/ALT ratio), albumin (ALB), glomerular filtration rate (GFR), creatine kinase‐myoglobin binding (CK‐MB), cholesterol, triglyceride, international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), and medical histories relating to hypertension and diabetes.

In addition, based on the results of our main analysis, we grouped all the cases together, regardless of their valve type, to test the risk factors associated with the four complications mentioned above.

Missing Data

Covariates with a missing rate exceeding 25% were considered inappropriate for imputation and were therefore excluded from the study. We used the MICE package in R for multiple imputation, a popular method for addressing data gaps due to its ability to provide unbiased and consistent estimates of missing values [15]. Following imputation process, no significant changes were observed in the distributional characteristics (Refer to Table S3).

Statistical Analysis

The incidence rate of complications was determined using a 95% confidence interval (CI). A multivariable logistic model was deployed to evaluate the influence of various risk factors on the four observed complications.

For our main analysis, we investigated the association between valve type and the aforementioned complications. We started with a basic model, centered solely around the valve type. Subsequently, the model was systematically expanded by introducing different groups of covariates: sociodemographic attributes, heart health metrics, and health indicators of other organs. Utilizing hierarchical multiple regression, we thoroughly assessed the effects of integrating these covariate groups into diverse sequences. Every possible sequence of introducing these covariate groups was meticulously explored. With each new group included, we conducted analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests to discern statistical variations before and after their addition. Through this methodical approach, we derived six unique nested models, encompassing our foundational statistical model.

In the additional analysis, building on the outcomes from the main analysis, we constructed separate multivariable logistic regression models for each complication. This allowed us to identify the specific risk factors for each complication.

Statistical significance was set at a two‐tailed p‐value of less than 0.05. All computations and analyses were performed using R software, version 3.6.1.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1239 people were excluded according to the exclusion criteria, leaving 1154 eligible patients for further analysis. Of these, 508 had been diagnosed with BAV and 646 with TAV. The male‐to‐female ratio was approximately 1.51, and the median age was 71.90 years. Compared with TAV patients, we found that the proportion or means were statistically lower in BAV patients: Age (70.26 vs. 72.12), NYHA high level (81.7% vs. 92.6%), and higher in BAV patients: GFR (73.58 vs. 69.86), female proportion (43.5% vs. 36.8%), ALB (42.13 vs. 40.98), triglyceride (1.37 vs. 1.27), CK‐MB (2.47 vs. 2.19). BAV patients were also found to have a higher proportion of diabetes (20.9% vs. 15.3%) and a lower proportion of hypertension (41.9% vs. 58.7%) (Table 1).

Incidence of Complications

Of all patients, 40 cases of AKI (11 in BAV and 29 in TAV, OR 0.52 [0.23, 1.09]), 134 cases of new‐onset PPI (67 in BAV and 67 in TAV, OR 1.29 [0.87, 1.92]), 129 cases of paravalvular leak (43 in BAV and 86 in TAV, OR 1.29 [0.82, 2.02]), 1105 cases of device success (492 in BAV and 613 in TAV, OR 1.65 [0.86, 3.29]) were recorded (Table 2). Of these cute kidney injury cases, 24 were stage 1 (8 in BAV and 16 in TAV), 10 were stage 2 (2 in BAV and 8 in TAV), and 6 were stage 3 (1 in BAV and 5 in TAV).

Main Analysis

Valve Type and Complication Incidence

In the crude model, the odds ratio (OR) for AKI was 51% lower in BAV patients than in TAV patients (OR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.23−0.96; p = 0.045). The other three complications showed no significant association with valve type. Specifically, the OR was 1.31 (95% CI, 0.91−1.88; p = 0.139) for PPI, 1.13 (95% CI, 0.74−1.70; p = 0.564) for paravalvular leak, and 1.66 (95% CI, 0.91−3.12; p = 0.105) for device success. After progressively adding sociodemographic characteristics, health status of heart characteristics, and health status of other organs as potential confounders, the full multiple logistic regression showed that none of the complications, including AKI, new‐onset PPI, paravalvular leak, and device success, were statistically significantly associated with valve type. Specifically, the OR was 0.52 (95% CI, 0.23−1.09) for AKI, 1.29 (95% CI, 0.87−1.92) for PPI, 1.29 (95% CI, 0.82−2.02) for paravalvular leak, and 1.65 (95% CI, 0.86−3.29) for device success (Figure 2).

Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and their confidence intervals for different complications based on valve types.

Hierarchical Multiple Regression Models

Analysis of the hierarchical multiple regression models suggests a potentially significant correlation between the incidence of AKI and the health status of organs other than the heart, as significance was demonstrated in half of the examined scenarios (three out of six). Notably, the requirement for a PPI was statistically associated with both sociodemographic characteristics and the health status of other organs. Furthermore, the health condition of the heart appeared to influence the occurrence of paravalvular leak, with significance shown in three out of six scenarios. Importantly, only the sociodemographic variables displayed a clear correlation with device success (Table S2).

Additional Analysis: Risk Factors for Complications

As the valve type was not found to be statistically significantly related to complications, it was omitted from the full model to further investigate specific risk factors for each complication in more detail. The subsequent results are detailed below:

AKI: TBIL and ALB were found to have a significant influence on the incidence of AKI. An increase of one unit in TBIL was associated with a 2% increase in the odds of AKI (OR: 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00−1.04), whereas an increase of one unit in ALB was linked to a 12% decrease in its odds (OR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.80−0.95).

PPI: Significant associations were observed with age, NYHA scores, TBIL, and ALB. For each unit increase in age and ALB, the odds of requiring a permanent pacemaker rose by 5% (age—OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.02−1.08; ALB—OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.00−1.11). Conversely, every unit increase in TBIL led to a 5% decrease in these odds (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.92−0.98). Additionally, patients with high NYHA scores had 54% lower odds of requiring a permanent pacemaker compared to those with low scores (OR: 0.46; 95% CI: 0.28−0.77).

Paravalvular leak: A significant correlation was found with PC. Specifically, for each unit increase in PC, the odds of a paravalvular leak decreased by almost 1% (OR: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.99−1.00).

Device success: WBC count emerged as a pivotal factor. Each unit increase in WBC count led to a 16% decrease in the odds of device success (OR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.91−0.98) (Table 3).

Discussion

BAV is a low‐prevalence congenital disease with a prevalence of approximately 1% of the population, of which 1/3−1/4 are female [16]. Due to the high medical costs (approximately 300,000 RMB), TAVR is a relatively uncommon option for VHD patients in China compared to SAVR. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time a study has been conducted in China on a large TAVR population cohort, specifically those with BAV. The results of this study are of great clinical importance in helping physicians to understand the prognosis of TAVR in patients with BAV, as well as the risk factors influencing the TAVR complications.

In this large‐scale TAVR‐undergone population, we found that patients diagnosed with BAV were not at increased risk of complications. Based on the anatomical structure, unlike TAV, the BAV has an asymmetrical leaflet shape and an oval sinus orifice, which may obstruct blood flow through the valve opening, resulting in increased flow velocity and eddy flow, and ultimately, valve thickening and asymmetric calcification [17]. Previous research has found that BAV is prone to accelerated aortic valve calcification due to both genetic mechanisms and anatomical factors; therefore, patients with BAV should undergo surgery earlier than those with TAV [18, 19]. Other studies have pointed out that the outcome of TAVR surgery in BAV patients is affected by the overall calcium burden and the presence of calcified raphe, which can prevent optimal device expansion [20]. For instance, Mentias et al. found that BAV patients were more likely to require a pacemaker than TAV patients (12.2% vs. 7.6%) [21]. In fact, BAV patients have long been considered unsuitable for TAVR and have often been excluded from randomized clinical trials [22]. In this study, the ORs for valve type (BAV or TAV) were not significant in the adjusted models. It is worth noting that, in the crude model of AKI, we observed a trend toward reduced AKI incidence in BAV patients. However, this trend gradually levelled off in the subsequent regression model, suggesting that the model was confounded by risk factors. This also indicates that the results of our adjusted model are reasonable. Our study recommends that TAVR should be performed on BAV patients when the surgeon judges the patient to be suitable for.

AKI is a common postoperative complication of TAVR. In our study, we found that patients with high TBIL and low ALB are more likely to experience AKI than others. High TBIL indicates abnormal liver function, which is believed to increase the risk of kidney damage [23]. ALB reflects the nutritional status of the body. Patients with low ALB may have a reduction in plasma osmotic pressure, which leads to an imbalance of fluid in the body between blood vessels and tissues, increasing on the kidney and resulting in AKI.

Permanent pacemakers are used more often in elderly patients and those with a poorer cardiac function score. This may be related to the poorer cardiac function, which requires more attention for TAVR in these patients. TBIL and ALB have also been shown to be associated with PPI, and further studies are needed to clarify their association and interpretation.

Paravalvular leak is another common complication after TAVR. Our findings suggested that patients with lower PC were at higher risk for moderate or severe paravalvular leaks. However, their relationship is just on the border between significant and insignificant, so more research is needed to explore further whether there is a relationship between paravalvular leak and PC or not.

Device success was defined as a successful TAVR with technical success and freedom from mortality, surgery, or intervention related to the device or to major vascular or access‐related issues, or cardiac structural issues, as well as achieving the intended performance of the valve. 49 patients (4.25%) didn't achieve device success, including two deaths (0.17%) in this study. The model showed that patients with higher WBC count were under higher risk of device success, which is consistence to general knowledge. This result may be related to whether the patient has a bacterial infection and further study is also needed.

It is obvious that BAV patients in this study may have been influenced by their better baseline characteristics, including lower age (70.26 vs. 72.12, p < 0.001), lower proportions of high‐NYHA scores (81.7% vs. 92.6, p < 0.001), and lower proportions of hypertension (41.9% vs. 58.7%, p < 0.001). This may lead to a lesser degree of valve calcification and aortic wall lesions in this group of patients, ultimately resulting in a comparable risk of complications in BAV and TAV patients. This indicates that the surgical decisions made by physicians are influenced by valve type: more stringent screening criteria are implemented for BAV patients. This tendency to treat BAV patients differently from TAV patients may finally affect the occurrence of complications.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the absence of ultrasound imaging data from our patients restricted our ability to examine information relating to the movement of the valve and blood flow. Second, we did not explore long‐term complications and their risk factors, an area that clearly warrants further research. Besides, retrospective studies are limited by data collection circumstances and are often subject to selection bias, which is unavoidable. While propensity score matching was employed to minimize selection bias through covariate balancing, residual confounding may persist given the retrospective design—particularly for unmeasured variables. This inherent limitation warrants cautious interpretation of comparative outcomes. Lastly, although our study sample was relatively large, it was limited to a single‐center study.

Conclusion

It is suggested that, based on present evidence, TAVR is an appropriate treatment for patients with BAV. Patients with a BAV diagnosis are not at an increased risk of complications. Furthermore, we recommend that physicians should consider the possibility of AKI in patients with abnormal liver function. Patients with cardiac insufficiency are at risk of PPI, and those with anemia are prone to paravalvular leaks and require more intraoperative attention.
