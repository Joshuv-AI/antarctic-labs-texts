Minimally invasive mitral surgery in patients with high pulmonary artery pressure: Results of a multicenter observational study
Authors: Silvia Mariani, Cristina Barbero, Valentina Gislao, Cecilia Capozza, Pierluigi Stefano, Paolo Centofanti, Vittoria Lodo, Francesco Onorati, Giorgia Cibin, Germano Di Credico, Andrea Audo, Ruggero De Paulis, Mauro Rinaldi, Giovanni Marchetto, Minimally Invasive Mitral Valve Surgery Registry Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary hypertension is diagnosed in 40% to 75% of patients affected by mitral valve diseases. Little is known regarding outcomes of patients with pulmonary hypertension undergoing mitral valve oeprations through minimally invasive approaches. This study compares the characteristics and in-hospital outcomes of patients undergoing minimally invasive mitral valve surgery, stratified by systolic pulmonary artery pressure.MethodsThis multicenter, retrospective, prospective, observational study included adults undergoing mitral valve operations through a right minithoracotomy with or without tricuspid valve surgery and atrial fibrillation ablation between 2020 and 2024. Comparisons were performed between patients with systolic pulmonary artery pressure less than 31 mm Hg, systolic pulmonary artery pressure 31 to 55 mm Hg, and systolic pulmonary artery pressure more than 55 mm Hg based on European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II cutoffs for systolic pulmonary artery pressure categories.ResultsThis analysis included 1121 patients (female, 47.5%; median age, 69 years, interquartile range, 59-77). Median systolic pulmonary artery pressure was 38 mm Hg (interquartile range, 30-50; systolic pulmonary artery pressure <31 mm Hg: n = 307, 27.4%; systolic pulmonary artery pressure 31-55 mm Hg: n = 152, 55.3%; systolic pulmonary artery pressure >55 mm Hg: n = 194, 17.3%). Patients with higher systolic pulmonary artery pressure were older, mainly female, and characterized by more comorbidities including atrial fibrillation and renal disease. A critical perioperative state was diagnosed in 12% of patients with systolic pulmonary artery pressure more than 55 mm Hg (P < .001). Patients with higher systolic pulmonary artery pressure more frequently underwent mitral valve replacement and tricuspid annuloplasty and experienced more postoperative dialysis and pacemaker implantation but comparable rates of conversions to sternotomy, respiratory and bleeding complications, and in-hospital mortality (systolic pulmonary artery pressure <31 mm Hg: n = 3, 1%; systolic pulmonary artery pressure 31-55 mm Hg: n = 6, 1%; systolic pulmonary artery pressure >55 mm Hg: n = 5, 2.6%; P = .222) compared with patients with lower systolic pulmonary artery pressure.ConclusionsDespite a higher preoperative complexity, patients with high systolic pulmonary artery pressure could be considered for minimally invasive mitral valve surgery with acceptable rates of major intraoperative or postoperative complications regardless of their systolic pulmonary artery pressure values.Key Words: heart failure, minimally invasive, mitral valve surgery, pulmonary hypertension

MIMVS stratified by PAPs.

Central Message

Patients with higher PAPs undergoing MIMVS exhibit higher preoperative risk profile but acceptable rates of complications and in-hospital mortality.

Perspective

Patients undergoing MIMVS with higher PAPs have increasing preoperative complexity but acceptable rates of complications and in-hospital mortality. This study supports the use of MIMVS regardless of high PAPs also in case of critical perioperative states or multiple procedures.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) occurs in 40% to 75% of patients diagnosed with mitral valve disease.1,2 It is more common among elderly and female patients, often secondary to elevated left-sided filling pressures and observed in advanced stages of mitral valve disease3 as an indicator of disease severity, influencing both timing and indication for surgical intervention.4,5 PH represents a risk factor for negative postoperative outcomes in cardiac surgery, with a 7-fold increased risk of in-hospital mortality.2,6 Nevertheless, mitral valve surgery, regardless of the surgical technique, has been associated with improved outcomes, a reduced rate of progressive heart failure,7 and a reduced mortality risk among patients with PH.7

Over the past decade, minimally invasive mitral valve surgery (MIMVS) has become the preferred approach for mitral valve diseases in experienced centers.4 Compared with conventional full sternotomy, MIMVS is associated with reduced surgical trauma, less postoperative complications such as wound infection and renal failure, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and improved cosmetic results. MIMVS has demonstrated favorable outcomes in frail or high-risk patients,8 whereas benefits in patients with PH remain uncertain.9 Indeed, longer cardiopulmonary bypass and clamping time observed in MIMVS, particularly at the beginning of the learning curve, might lead to an increased incidence of right ventricular failure,10,11 already more frequent in patients with PH.12,13 For this reason, clinicians may be reluctant to use MIMVS in patients with PH, especially in emergency cases or unstable patients. Nevertheless, studies specifically addressing this topic are lacking, leaving unclear indications in such high-risk patients.

The present study compares characteristics and in-hospital outcomes of patients undergoing MIMVS, stratified by systolic pulmonary artery pressure (PAPs) values. We hypothesize that patients with higher PAPs have higher in-hospital morbidity and mortality.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study is an analysis of the Italian Multicenter Registry for Minimally Invasive Mitral Valve Surgery: a nationwide, multicenter, retrospective, prospective, observational study collecting data from 8 centers experienced in MIMVS (minimum 25 MIMVS operations/year and 3-year experience). Institutional Review Board was obtained at the coordinating center (University of Turin, protocol code: 0001455; date of approval: October 19, 2022). Informed consent was received from participants. The current study is reported according to the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement.14

Patient Population

The study included adults (age ≥18 years) requiring mitral surgery with or without combined procedures (ie, tricuspid valve surgery, atrial fibrillation ablation, and atrial septal defect closure) through a right minithoracotomy between January 2020 and December 2024. Salvage procedures were excluded. For the current analysis, patients were stratified by PAPs values according to European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) II15 categories.

Data Collection and Outcomes

Data were collected in an electronic case report form (s4trials-europe.com), according to the predefined protocol and variable definitions (Appendix E1). The primary outcome was all-cause in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital events such as conversion to sternotomy, intubation time, intensive care unit and hospital stays, and postoperative complications (ie, stroke, low output cardiac syndrome, pacemaker implantation, dialysis).

Statistical Analysis

Data were merged and analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM), and R 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The full cohort was categorized into 3 study groups: PAPs less than 31 mm Hg, PAPs 31 to 55 mm Hg, and PAPs more than 55 mm Hg.15

Variables are expressed as numbers (valid percent on available data) for categorical variables and median (first and third quartile, interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables. The percentage of missing values was calculated (Table E1). Descriptive statistics were conducted on available data only. Normality was investigated with Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk, and inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Homogeneity of variance was tested with Levene's test. For each 2 × 2 table, the chi-square test was used if all expected cell counts were 5 or greater; otherwise, the Fisher exact test was used. The Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test was used for tables with more than 2 × 2 cells. If equal variances could be assumed, continuous variables were tested by the independent Student t test; otherwise, the Welch's test was used. If they did not comply with normality, they were tested with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Post hoc comparisons were performed and adjusted by the Bonferroni correction for multiple tests. Univariable logistic regressions were fitted to assess the association between PAPs class and in-hospital outcomes. Results were reported as odds ratios with 95% CIs. Based on the possible differences related to the disease mechanism, a post hoc subgroup analysis including only patients with Carpentier type II lesion was performed.

Results

Preoperative and Surgical Characteristics

The cohort included 1121 patients: 307 with PAPs less than 31 mm Hg (27.4%), 620 with PAPs 31 to 55 mm Hg (55.3%), and 194 with PAPs more than 55 mm Hg (17.3%, Figure 1). Patients’ overall median age was 69 years (IQR, 59-77) with older patients in higher PAPs values group (P < .001; Table 1 and Table E2). Female sex was reported in 47.5% (n = 532) of the population with lower rates in the PAPs less than 31 group (P < .001). Patients in the PAPs 31 to 55 and PAPs more than 55 group were characterized by a more severe risk profile including higher rates of chronic renal disease (P = .030) and atrial fibrillation (P < .001). A critical perioperative state was diagnosed in 11.9% of patients with PAPs more than 55 mm Hg (n = 23), a higher rate compared with the PAPs less than 31 (n = 5, 1.6%) and PAPs 31 to 55 (n = 28, 4.5%; P < .001) groups.

In-Hospital Outcomes

Conversion to sternotomy was necessary in 8 patients in the PAPs less than 31 group (2.6%), 20 patients in the PAPs 31 to 55 group (3.3%), and 8 (4.1%) patients in the PAPs more than 55 group (P = .652; Figure 2, A and Table E3). Median mechanical ventilation time was slightly higher in the PAPs more than 55 group (P = .035; Table 4). Patients in the PAPs more than 55 group required reintubation in 5.7% (n = 11; P = .077) of cases and required a more frequent use of hemodialysis (P < .001; Table E4). Additionally, 8.3% of patients in the PAPs more than 55 group required a pacemaker implantation (P = .004). In-hospital death occurred in 1% of patients in the PAPs less than 31 (n = 3) and PAPs 31 to 55 (n = 6) group and 2.6% of patients in the PAPs more than 55 group (n = 5; P = .222; Figure 2, B).

Subgroup Analysis: Carpentier Type II Lesion

The subgroup analysis including patients with Carpentier type II lesion showed homogeneous preoperative characteristics (Table E5, Table E6, Table E7, Table E8, Table E9). It confirmed the more advanced age (P < .001) in the PAPs more than 55 group that included less patients with Barlow disease (P = .008), more patients with fibroelastic deficiency (P = .017), and 42% (n = 34) of patients with associated tricuspid valve disease (P < .001). Patients with PAPs more than 55 required reintubation and hemodialysis in 8.5% (n = 7; P = .006) and 4.9% (n = 4; P = .041) of cases. In-hospital death occurred in 3.7% of patients with PAPs more than 55 (n = 53; P = .076).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective prospective observational study demonstrates that despite a higher preoperative complexity, patients with high PAPs could be considered for MIMVS with no increased risks of major intraoperative or postoperative complications regardless of their PAPs values. This study has 4 main findings. First, patients with higher PAPs requiring MV surgery are older and mainly female, with more comorbidities such as renal disease and atrial fibrillation. Second, 12% of patients with PAPs more than 55 mm Hg are in a critical perioperative state. Third, despite the minimally invasive approach, patients with PAPs more than 55 mm Hg undergo more combined procedures, mitral valve replacement, and tricuspid annuloplasty with no observed increase in intraoperative times. Fourth, patients with PAPs more than 55 mm Hg might experience 4.1% conversions to sternotomy and more postoperative dialysis and pacemaker implantation but comparable rates of respiratory and bleeding complications. Finally, we could not identify major differences regarding in-hospital mortality.

Current indications for management of patients with PH and mitral valve disease undergoing surgery are still debated. A less invasive approach may provide benefits in frail patients,8 but surgeons might hesitate to apply it in high-risk and technically demanding cases. This study demonstrates that high PAPs characterizes mitral valve disease16 in older patients with higher incidence of renal impairment and atrial fibrillation, and a more complex cardiovascular profile. Indeed, PH is often accompanied by comorbidities that may complicate surgery.17,18 Considering this high-risk profile, our findings emphasize the importance of patient optimization through improvement of renal function19 and discussion of concomitant surgical procedures.20 Moreover, the higher prevalence of elevated PAPs in older and female patients underlines the need for an early screening and adequate follow-up of specific populations.21

Severe mitral regurgitation was prevalent across all groups, although slightly less frequent in patients with higher PAPs, likely reflecting the increased proportion of patients undergoing reoperation due to prosthetic valve dysfunction in this group.22 Indeed, 12.4% of patients with higher PAPs had a history of previous cardiac surgery. In patients undergoing redo surgery, MIMVS might reduce risks of injuries during re-sternotomy, dissection of adhesions, and bleeding.23 Such an aspect might be of particular importance in patients with increased PAPs and right heart disease who might have a certain degree of liver and renal impairment.24,25 The increased frequency of concomitant mitral and tricuspid valve surgery in patients with higher PAPs (16.5%) reflects the involvement of the right heart structures. Although this might explain the higher incidence of PM implantation in this population, it also emphasizes the importance of a tailored surgical planning and the feasibility of MIMVS approach in multiple valve cases.8,9,26

Cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic crossclamp times were comparable among groups, suggesting that elevated PAPs did not translate into longer operative times,27 a variable potentially impacting clinical outcome.10,11 Conversely, the comparable operative times suggest that MIMVS is applicable even when double valve surgery is required, considering chances of beating heart tricuspid repair. However, comparable or shorter clamping times could be due to a higher percentage of mitral valve replacement instead of complex repair in the higher PAPs group.

A 4.1% rate of conversion to sternotomy was observed in the high PAPs group: 2.1% conversions for adhesions and 2.1% conversions for other reasons. Such evidence might reflect these patients’ complexity and support adequate preoperative planning and patient information.

Although PH is recognized in the literature as independently associated with higher risk of postoperative stroke,28 the incidence of this event was similar between our study groups. The higher incidence of multiorgan failure in the group with higher PAPs could partly reflect the preoperative higher risk profile encompassing a higher incidence of kidney damage, heart failure, and critical perioperative state.

Likewise, the higher rate of postoperative pacemaker implantation in the PAPs more than 55 group could be explained by the greater number of tricuspid valve surgeries observed in the group. In-hospital death was 2.6% in patients with PAPs more than 55 mm Hg despite a more critical perioperative state and 1% in patients with PAPs less than 55 mm Hg. The observed in-hospital mortality rates reflected the predicted 30-day EuroSCORE mortality and were in line with previously observed data. Berretta and colleagues9 investigated the outcomes of MIMVS and observed a 14.3% in-hospital mortality in extreme high-risk patients (defined by EuroSCORE II >12%), 3.7% in-hospital mortality in high-risk patients (EuroSCORE II 8%-12%), and 3.4% in low-intermediate risk patients (EuroSCORE II < 8%). Furthermore, Helmers and colleagues1 demonstrated a 1.9% 30-day mortality in MIMVS patients with PH of all stages. The same study emphasized a 4.9% 30-day mortality in patients with severe PH who underwent full sternotomy. Gosain and colleagues29 analyzed the outcomes of patients with PH who underwent mitral or aortic minimally invasive surgery and observed a 3% in-hospital mortality for patients with severe PH and 4% in patients with mild to moderate PH.

All these data support the belief that in centers with adequate expertise, PH should not be considered an absolute contraindication to minimally invasive surgery. Nevertheless, it remains mandatory to choose a tailored preoperative care and surgical approach for every patient. Our results support the idea that MIMVS could benefit patients with high PAPs, with acceptable rates of complications and in-hospital survival when adequately planned.1,29 Finally, although approximately half of our patients were discharged to rehabilitation facilities due to this common practice within the Italian healthcare system, adequate home care and follow-up programs should be considered to further improve outcomes.

Study Limitations

The structured data collection and large sample size enhance data robustness and statistical power. However, our study is observational by nature, preventing causal inferences and suffering from confounding by indication. Despite this, we adopted a descriptive statistical approach to remain as close as possible to the observed reality.

Risk stratification was based solely on the EuroSCORE II because variables to calculate other risk scores (ie, Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality) were not available. For the current analysis, case classification was based on the EuroSCORE II PAPs echocardiographic definition rather than right heart catheterization data, introducing a possible information bias. Given the observational nature of the study, detailed data on right ventricular function and the onset timing of the mitral valve disease were not available. Nevertheless, we used proxy variables to estimate the acute onset of the disease based on the presence of a critical perioperative state and surgical urgency. The local policies and MIMVS techniques might have differed among participating centers. We tried to control for such heterogeneity by including experienced centers with a minimum of 25 minimally invasive mitral operations per year and 3-year experience that perform 90% or more of all mitral valve surgeries through thoracotomy. Nevertheless, this might have introduced a referring bias limiting the generalizability of our results. Despite the large sample size, the current study might have been underpowered to identify differences in main outcomes. Furthermore, findings should be interpreted considering the potential for residual confounding, because multivariable logistic regression analysis was not performed because of the low number of events and consequent model nonconvergence. An in-depth analysis of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative hemodynamic parameters, and preoperative and postoperative echocardiographic data regarding mitral valve, tricuspid valve, and ventricular function, postdischarge survival, quality of life, functional status, and rehospitalization after discharge was not possible.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that MIMVS can be performed in patients across a broad spectrum of PAPs, including those with the highest values. This expands the potential indications for less invasive surgical approaches and may contribute to improved perioperative management and outcomes in this challenging patient population. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate the impact of MIMVS on long-term survival and quality of life in patients with PH.
