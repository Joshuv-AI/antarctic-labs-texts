Determinants of ECG misdiagnosis of atrial fibrillation or flutter in the emergency department and their clinical implications: results from a single-center observational study
Authors: Yuval Avidan, Baruch Weizman, Sameha Zahra, Oded Mizrahi, Hani Sliman, Haim Katalan, Nili Stein, Razi Khoury, Asaf Danon, Sameer Kassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAtrial fibrillation (AF) and atrial flutter (AFL) are common arrhythmias frequently misdiagnosed. The emergency department (ED), a fast-paced environment, presents challenges for accurate ECG interpretation. This study examined whether timing of ED presentation affects diagnostic accuracy.MethodsOver five years (2019–2024), consecutive ECGs from patients discharged with AF or AFL were independently reviewed by two cardiologists. Diagnoses were classified as correct, partially correct (AF misread as AFL or vice versa), or overinterpreted (non-AF/AFL rhythms misread as AF/AFL). Arrival times were grouped into morning (07:00–15:00), afternoon (15:00–23:00), and night (23:00–07:00) shifts. Multivariable logistic regression analyzed predictors of overinterpretation.ResultsAmong 2,003 patients (1,794 AF; 209 AFL), interpretations were correct in 1,780 (88.9%), partially correct in 166 (8.3%), and overinterpreted in 57 (2.8%). Overinterpretation rates were highest in the afternoon (4.3%) versus morning (2.8%) and night (0.7%) (p = 0.012). Independent predictors included afternoon arrival (OR 1.87; 95% CI 1.07–3.25; p = 0.027) and premature beats (OR 2.22; 95% CI 1.30–3.81; p < 0.001). Overinterpretation was independent of shift workload and physician experience, and resulted in inappropriate new prescriptions of rate-control agents (38.5%), antiarrhythmics (28%), and anticoagulants (56%), with minor and major bleeding occurring in 16% and 3.5% of cases, respectively. Diagnosis was revised during outpatient follow-up in only 5% of cases.ConclusionOverinterpretation of AF remains a concern in the ED. Error rates did not rise during nighttime shifts, implying that factors other than clinician fatigue may underlie these mistakes.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Emergency department, Nighttime

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and atrial flutter (AFL) are the most prevalent arrhythmias globally [1], accounting for approximately 2% of all emergency department (ED) presentations and remains the leading arrhythmic cause of hospitalization [2], though a considerable proportion are discharged directly from the ED [3]. Proficiency in electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation is essential for medical practitioners [4]; yet, concerns remain about its consistency across different healthcare providers [5–8]. Erroneous interpretation remains a persistent challenge [9, 10], potentially resulting in medical errors, including the unwarranted use of anticoagulants and antiarrhythmic medications [11, 12]. While physicians’ diagnostic errors are multifactorial, the ED environment, with its high patient volumes, prolonged shifts, multitasking demands, and constant interruptions, creates a setting highly susceptible to medical errors [13, 14]. Nighttime work, may amplify these risks, further compromising clinical decision-making and diagnostic accuracy [15, 16]. However, the impact of nighttime shifts on the misdiagnosis rates of AF and AFL remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate whether the timing of patient presentation affects the accuracy of ECG interpretation for these arrhythmias.

Material and methods

Study design

This retrospective, single-center study analyzed medical records of all consecutive patients aged ≥ 18 years who were discharged from the ED of Carmel Medical Center [17], a regional healthcare facility, with a final rhythm diagnosis of AF or AFL over a five-year period (December 1, 2019 – December 1, 2024), based on ICD-9 codes for AF or AFL. The study was approved by the Lady Davis Carmel Medical Center Ethics Committee (CMC-006–25) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Collected data included demographic characteristics, and clinical information, which were extracted from electronic medical records and hospital databases. Follow-up data were obtained from the Clalit Health Care database, the largest healthcare provider in Israel, covering approximately 4 million members. To assess the influence of presentation timing on diagnostic performance, patient arrival times were categorized into three distinct shifts: morning (07:00–15:00), afternoon (15:00–23:00), and night (23:00–07:00). These time periods correspond to the scheduled shifts of the medical and nursing staff.

All 12-lead ECGs analyzed were recorded using a standard ECG device and printed in a 6 × 2 format, with a paper speed of 25 mm/s, an amplitude of 10 mm/mV, and a filter range of 35–50 Hz. In our ED, ECG machines are configured not to generate an automated computerized interpretation. Medical records, including printed ECG tracings, were retrieved from institutional archives and independently reviewed by two consultant cardiologists, who were blinded to the study’s purpose, patient demographics, and clinical history. The evaluation encompassed all available ECG records, including those obtained from emergency medical services and single- or three-channel ECG strips recorded during the ED stay, such as tracings acquired during vagal maneuvers or post-adenosine administration. Each cardiologist independently analyzed the ECGs, classifying rhythms and evaluating key parameters such as heart rate, QRS duration, and the presence of premature beats. In cases of diagnostic disagreement, a third consultant—an electrophysiologist with over 20 years of experience—served as an arbitrator, establishing the gold standard diagnosis. This methodology is consistent with prior research in the field [8, 18]. Patients with atrial or ventricular paced rhythms, as well as those with an indeterminate rhythm classification by the majority consensus, were excluded. The finalized ECG interpretations were subsequently compared with the original diagnoses recorded by ED physicians. Diagnoses were subsequently categorized as correct (true AF or AFL), partially correct (AF misdiagnosed as AFL or vice versa), or overinterpreted (non-AF/AFL rhythms erroneously identified as such). In cases of multiple rhythms, if any part of the ECG showed AF, the correct diagnosis was considered AF. This classification was adopted, as overinterpretation of rhythms that do not carry thromboembolic risks—and therefore do not require anticoagulant therapy—presents far greater clinical implications than the misclassification of AF and AFL as one another. As for AF and AFL, while both carry similar thromboembolic risk, their rate and rhythm management somewhat differ [19, 20], thus deserving the physician’s attention. Within the ED, ECGs are routinely reviewed and signed by a physician within minutes, after which the patient encounter continues. As this process is not computerized, the average completion time cannot be determined. In uncomplicated cases of AF—such as those included in this study, all of whom were discharged from the ED—cardiology consultation is typically not involved.

Reference ECG analysis

AF was defined based on electrocardiographic criteria [21], as an irregularly irregular supraventricular rhythm, characterized by the absence of distinct P waves, which are replaced by rapid oscillations or irregular fibrillatory waves that vary in amplitude, shape, and timing. AFL was defined as a supraventricular arrhythmia characterized by organized, regular flutter waves with an atrial rate of 240 to 350 beats per minute [20].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as counts and proportions. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared across three categories of diagnostic performance (correct, overinterpretation, and partially correct) using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables. Workload (patients per physician per shift) was defined as the total number of new patients admitted to the ED divided by the number of physicians scheduled for that shift. Physicians signing the ECGs were classified into three groups: residents, senior staff with less than 5 years of clinical experience, and senior staff with 5 or more years of clinical experience. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction. To account for repeated visits per patient, a generalized estimating equation model with a logit link function was used to assess the association between work shifts and overinterpretation, adjusting for clinical and demographic characteristics. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between ED shift timing and the risk of AF overinterpretation, adjusting for variables such as age, heart rate, arrival timing, clinician experience, premature beats, and QRS width. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The ED recorded 255,338 new patient visits during the study follow-up period as illustrated in Fig. 1. During the study period, 2,034 ECG records were reviewed, with 31 excluded due to paced rhythm or indeterminate rhythm classification. Of the remaining cases, 1,794 (89.6%) had a discharge diagnosis of AF and 209 (10.4%) of AFL. In total, 1,780 (88.9%) ECGs were correctly diagnosed, 166 (8.3%) were partially correct, and 57 (2.8%) were overinterpreted (Fig. 2). Among the 57 overinterprated rhythms were: various supraventricular arrhythmias (n = 37), sinus rhythm with Mobitz I, artifacts, or premature contractions (n = 19), and multifocal atrial tachycardia (n = 1). As shown in Table 1, the clinical and electrocardiographic characteristics were largely comparable between correctly and incorrectly diagnosed patients, with the exception of a significantly higher mean age, prevalence of coronary artey disease, and a greater prevalence of premature beats in the overinterpreted group (both p < 0.001). The clinical experience of the physicians signing the ECGs did not differ statistically between groups, although, notably, physicians with more than 5 years of experience accounted for 16% of all interpretations included. Shift workload did not differ significantly between groups, with patients per physician per shift averaging 15.1 ± 4.0 in the morning, 15.8 ± 4.0 in the afternoon, and 15.7 ± 4.4 at night.

Figure 3 presents the distribution of ECG diagnoses across different working shifts. A total of 942 cases occurred during the morning shift, 651 during the afternoon, and 410 during the night (p = 0.012). When comparing the distribution of overinterpreted versus correctly interpreted ECGs, 5.3% of overinterpretations occurred during the night shift, 45.6% during the morning, and 49.1% during the afternoon (p = 0.007). In contrast, the distribution of partially correct versus correct interpretations did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference (p = 0.181). Overinterpretation accounted for only 0.7% of all ECGs during the night shift, compared to approximately 4.3% and 2.8% during the afternoon and morning shifts, respectively. As shown in Table 2, multivariable logistic regression analysis identified arrival during the afternoon shift (OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.07–3.25; p = 0.027) and the presence of premature beats on the ECG (OR 2.22, 95% CI 1.30–3.81; p < 0.001) as independent predictors of ECG overinterpretation.

Table 3 summarizes the clinical consequences of overinterpretation. A substantial proportion of patients initiated inappropriate treatment based on the ED diagnosis, including rate-control agents (n = 22, 38.5%), antiarrhythmics (n = 16, 28%), and anticoagulants (n = 32, 56%). Among those newly prescribed anticoagulation, 11 patients experienced bleeding complications during a mean follow-up of 19 months, with 2 classified as major. Discussion

Our study yields several key take-home messages. First, while ECG misinterpretation in the ED is relatively uncommon, it carries clinically meaningful consequences, including inappropriate initiation of anticoagulants, antiarrhythmics, and rate-control therapy. Second, and contrary to conventional assumptions, errors were not more frequent during night shifts; in fact, misinterpretation occurred less often at night than during day or evening shifts. Finally, these results highlight the need for targeted educational interventions and the strategic implementation of safeguards in high-risk scenarios to enhance diagnostic accuracy.

Medical professionals, routinely work hours that surpass the standards of acceptable labor practices in other sectors [22]. The demands of extended work hours and circadian rhythm disruptions have been persistent challenges in the medical profession [23, 24]. Often referred to as the Achilles’ heel of the profession [25], sleep deprivation remains a critical concern affecting physician well-being and patient safety. Despite the widely recognized impact of fatigue on clinical performance, our study found no association between the time of presentation and the likelihood of incorrect ECG interpretation. Notably, overinterpretation of AF and AFL remains a persistent issue in the ED, affecting 2.8% of all patients discharged with these diagnoses—an error that, as illustrated, can have significant clinical consequences. Consistent with prior studies, premature beats were associated with an increased risk of diagnostic error [9].

Despite extensive research, establishing a definitive link between sleep deprivation and impaired clinical performance remains challenging. While numerous studies indicate that fatigue negatively affects performance in simulated clinical tasks [23, 26–28], the evidence remains inconclusive and highly context-dependent. For instance, sleep deprivation impairs hand–eye coordination in laparoscopic surgeons [29], yet it does not affect surgical residents’ performance on written board exams [30]. Fatigue induces cognitive bias, a systematic distortion in judgment arising from the brain’s reliance on heuristics to optimize information processing. While sleep deprivation and fatigue have the potential to impair clinical judgment and contribute to patient harm [13, 23, 31, 32], our findings indicate that ECG misinterpretation occurs less frequently during night shifts compared with day or evening shifts. This suggests that factors beyond fatigue play a significant role, with insufficient ECG competency presumably being a primary contributor, as depicted in Fig. 4. Physicians have long struggled with ECG interpretation, particularly in accurately diagnosing AF [7, 9, 10, 33]. Given the significant consequences of these diagnostic errors, implementing protective measures is imperative. This raises a fundamental question: is it possible to establish a systematic and sustainable improvement in the diagnosis of AF within the ED? To address this shortcoming, several promising avenues warrant further exploration. An apparent clear priority is enhancing ECG education with a specific focus on AF and AFL mimickers and addressing ECG confounding factors. As our data indicate, the causes of ECG misinterpretation as AF are well-defined and frequently recurring. Therefore, training ED physicians to systematically screen and scrutinize ECGs for these errors is likely both practical and attainable within a relatively short timeframe. Additionally, increasing the availability of experienced ECG interpreters for ambiguous cases, including through digital consultations, can serve as a critical safeguard against potentially hazardous diagnostic errors. Moreover, adhering to the principle that “two are better than one,” as previously suggested in the context of ED errors [14], implementing a “two-reviewer” approach—where two physicians evaluate ECGs in cases most susceptible to errors—could help minimize diagnostic mistakes while maintaining efficiency. In this context, artificial intelligence (AI)–augmented ECG interpretation may serve as a complementary decision-support tool rather than a replacement for clinician judgment. Pairing clinician interpretation with a high-performing AI algorithm has the potential to reduce missed AF diagnoses and improve diagnostic consistency, as deep learning ECG models have demonstrated sensitivity and specificity comparable to expert readers [34–36]. However, risks such as automation bias, over-reliance on algorithmic outputs, and concerns regarding generalizability and workflow integration underscore the need for continued human oversight and real-world validation [36]. Finally, assessment at a designated AF clinic with documented ECG tracings prior to commencing long-term anticoagulation, may improve diagnostic precision [11]; however, accessibility concerns may render this approach impractical.

Diagnostic accuracy varied by time of day, with significantly higher error rates during the afternoon shift. Although new case numbers were similar across shifts, afternoon workload may be effectively higher, as many patients admitted in the morning still require evaluation, disrupting workflow and increasing interruptions—factors linked to medical errors [37, 38]. In addition, although systematic data on referral patterns are lacking and this remains speculative, our clinical experience suggests that the timing and source of patient presentation may influence this trend. Many patients are initially assessed in primary care during morning hours, often including outpatient ECGs. When AF is suspected, these patients are frequently referred to the ED, typically arriving in the afternoon. This referral pattern may introduce diagnostic bias, as ED physicians often interpret ECGs already labeled or suspected as AF. In contrast, patients presenting at night or early morning—usually via emergency services or self-referral—arrive without pre-labeled rhythms, enabling a more unbiased diagnostic assessment.

This study has several limitations. As a single-center analysis, its generalizability may be limited. We could not assess referral patterns, time to ECG, or time to physician evaluation—factors that could influence diagnostic accuracy. As noted, workload per shift was calculated from new arrivals rather than the total number of patients present in the ED at a given time. We acknowledge that additional factors—such as patient acuity, total patients seen, patients requiring re-evaluation, and ED congestion—also affect workload and clinical performance; unfortunately, these data were not available in our dataset. Additionally, due to the retrospective design and reliance on diagnostic codes for patient inclusion, the study cannot assess the rate of ECG arrhythmia misinterpretation. Furthermore, some patients may have had prior erroneous diagnoses, introducing potential bias. Given the retrospective design, no a priori power calculation was performed; therefore, non-significant p-values should be interpreted cautiously, as they do not preclude clinically meaningful effects. Finally, because the study focused on discharged, generally stable patients, these findings may not be generalizable to admitted patients. Nonetheless, the study reflects real-world ED practice over five years, encompassing a diverse patient population and rotating medical staff.
