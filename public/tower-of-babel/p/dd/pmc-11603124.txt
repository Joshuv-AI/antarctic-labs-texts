Bilateral scrofuloderma: An incessant entity
Authors: Parakriti Gupta, Ivneet Kour, Varsha Gupta, Varinder Saini, Lipika Singhal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Scrofuloderma is most common presentation of cutaneous tuberculosis in India. A 15-years-old immunocompetent male presented with bilateral cervical necrotising lesions and was treated with ATTx9 months, without improvement. Patient was diagnosed with scrofuloderma and ZN staining revealed 2+acid-fast bacilli. Cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test showed rifampicin resistance. Patient was initiated on shorter regimen, but deferred treatment owing to technical portal problems. After 3 months, neck swelling had enlarged and he was started on multi-drug regimen x24 months. This underlines requisite to consider scrofuloderma in differentials of bilateral cervical lesions and to periodically monitor compliance to prevent subsequent emergence of resistance.

Introduction

Scrofuloderma, also recognized as tuberculosis colliquativa cutis, is one of the most common presentations of cutaneous tuberculosis (CTB) in India [1], [2], [3], which contributes to ∼1–2 % of extrapulmonary TB [4], [5]. Scrofuloderma is consequence of contiguous spread of TB from underlying lymph nodes, bones or joints and usually presents as unilateral disease in ∼75–80 % cases [6,7]. It is common in children and patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), in whom this is the most common presentation of extra-pulmonary TB. Here, we present an interesting case that posed diagnostic dilemma in an immunocompetent male.

Case

A 15-years-old male, presented to outpatient clinic with necrotising bilateral cervical lesions, for past 3 months. He had past history of anti-tubercular therapy (ATT) intake (oral drugsx6 months, details not available) eight years back for abdominal tuberculosis. After an interval of six years, he presented with bilateral cervical swellings and treated with ATTx 9 months for lymphadenitis; but without any clinical improvement. Fine-needle aspiration cytology revealed necrosis and patient was diagnosed with scrofuloderma. Microbiological analysis revealed 2 +acid-fast bacilli in ZN smear (Fig. 1) and Xpert MTB/RIF assay confirmed presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, with rifampicin resistance. Patient was started on shorter multi-drug regimen (moxifloxacin 600 mg, ethionamide 800 mg, clofazamine 160 mg, pyrazinamide 2000 mg, high-dose isoniazid 800 mg, ethambutol 800 mg, kanamycin 800 mg), via DOTS, however, was deferred after 5 days, owing to technical glitches in Nikshay ID.Fig. 1Ziehl-Neelsen staining showing 2+ acid-fast bacilli.Fig. 1

Consequently, the lesions worsened to bilateral necrotising lesion, associated with itching and foul-smelling discharge, enlarged neck swelling, after 3 months. (Fig. 2a and b) On examination, the nodes were non-tender, matted, with crusting of overlying skin, and multiple pus points. Chest X-ray and computerized tomography of neck revealed lymph node enlargement. ZN-smear and CBNAAT divulged similar findings as earlier. Other laboratory parameters were within normal limits. Patient was started on multi-drug regimen for 24 months, which included kanamycin (750 mg I/M OD), levofloxacin (750 mg OD), ethionamide (500 mg OD), cycloserine (500 mg OD), pyrazinamide (1250 mg OD), ethambutol (800 mg OD) with pyridoxine (100 mg HS). Discussion

The present case poses a diagnostic dilemma, owing to absence of immunocompromised status. Scrofuloderma (tuberculosis colliquativa cutis) is the most common presentation of cutaneous tuberculosis (CTB) in India, commonly reported in children and patients with HIV/AIDS [1]; while CTB itself is rare presentations (∼1–2 % of extrapulmonary TB) [2], [3]. In this case, patient became non-compliant, owing to technical problems in nikshay ID. Recurrence could be attributed to the high endemicity of infection in the region, with 17.5–23.5 % rifampicin resistance [6]. This case further illustrates gaps in the health care system viz., failure to perform FNAC at initial visit, technical glitches and variation in individualized regimen. Scrofuloderma should be considered in differential diagnosis of bilateral cervical lesions, which might be misdiagnosed as atypical mycobacterium, actinomycosis, sporotriciosis or nocardiosis. Follow-up and counseling is mandatory to achieve the goal of TB eradication [5].
