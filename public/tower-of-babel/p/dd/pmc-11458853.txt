Novel gain-of-function mutants identify a critical region within CFTR membrane-spanning domain 2 controlling cAMP-dependent and ATP-independent channel activation
Authors: Solène Castanier, Ahmad Elbahnsi, Benoit Chevalier, Nesrine Baatallah, Iwona Pranke, Lynda Berri, Aleksander Edelman, Isabelle Sermet-Gaudelus, Jean-Paul Mornon, Isabelle Callebaut, Alexandre Hinzpeter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCFTR is an anion channel that has evolved from the mold of an ABC transporter. It possesses specific structural features, including a lateral portal between the cytoplasmic extensions of its transmembrane helices TM4 and TM6. This TM4-TM6 portal is lined by basic residues attracting anions from the cytosol towards the intracellular vestibule. Even though a symmetric, open portal is not observed at the level of the TM10/TM12 interface, basic amino acids are also present at this level, exposed to solvent in the vicinity of the regulatory R region, whose phosphorylation enables channel activation. Here, using all-atom molecular dynamics simulations in combination with functional and biochemical assays, we investigate the importance of these basic amino acids (R1158 and R1030), and of a neighboring aromatic amino acid (W846) in the regulation of CFTR activity. Results indicate that mutation of these amino acids globally increased channel activity and enabled channel opening by potentiators without the need to elevate cAMP levels. These effects (i) were observed even when the binding site of the potentiator VX-770 was mutated, revealing a probable independent mechanism, and (ii) were additive to one gain-of-function mutant within the selectivity filter. Taken together, our results indicate that the region of the membrane-spanning domain 2 (MSD2), symmetric to the lateral portal located between MSD1 TM4 and TM6, is a novel critical actor of CFTR regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-024-05431-9.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR, ABCC-7) is the unique anion channel within the large ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter superfamily [1]. Mainly expressed in epithelial cells, CFTR is a key factor in fluid secretion and absorption [2]. Mutations within the CFTR gene (NM_000492.3; MIM# 602421) cause Cystic fibrosis (CF; MIM# 219700), a severe genetic disease mainly affecting secretory epithelia [3]. The most deleterious mutations affect protein synthesis (e.g. premature termination codons), folding (e.g. F508del, the most frequent mutation) or channel gating (e.g. G551D), some of which also leading to combinatorial defects [4].

CFTR has evolved from the general frame of an ABC transporter to exhibit PKA-activated, ATP-gated anion channel activity (reviewed in [5, 6]). Like other ABC transporters, CFTR is formed by two Membrane-Spanning Domains (MSDs) and two Nucleotide Binding Domains (NBDs) [7]. CFTR has two asymmetric ATP-binding sites formed at the interface of NBD1 and NBD2, one of which (site 2) being hydrolysis competent, while the other (site 1) is degenerate or non-canonical, with longer ATP resident time (for review see [6]). CFTR has also a unique regulatory (R) region, which separates the two halves of the protein and behaves as an intrinsically disordered sequence. Activation of the channel requires phosphorylation of the R region by PKA, which relieves inhibitory interactions with the rest of the protein and allows NBD dimerization, fostered by ATP binding at the NBD interface. The conformational wave initiated by tightening of the NBD dimer is propagated to the MSD through interfaces including short coupling helices in the MSD intracellular loops (ICLs), which in turn results in pore opening [8]. This allosteric pathway leading to the gate of the pore includes the discontinuous segment of transmembrane helix TM8, a specific feature of CFTR in the ABC transporter superfamily [9].

The clinically approved potentiator VX-770 (named ivacaftor) [10], which increases the open probability (Po) of CFTR, represents a breakthrough in the treatment of CF patients carrying mutations affecting channel gating [11]. It was shown to enhance CFTR activity by (i) increasing both ATP-dependent and ATP-independent gating [12, 13], (ii) slowing down non-hydrolytic closing of the channel [13, 14] and (iii) potentiating mutants with defective Nucleotide-Binding Domains (NBDs) dimerization such as CFTR-G551D [10, 15] or CFTR-W1282X [16, 17]. However, its mechanism of action remains largely elusive at the molecular level, despite the description of binding sites [18–21]. One binding site was identified at the level of the discontinuous segment of TM8 by two independent studies [20, 21], based on cryo-electron microscopy [20] and molecular docking [21]. Potentiators are all the more important in CF treatments as they enhance treatment efficacy when associated with correctors, i.e. molecules enabling the proper folding of CFTR-F508del [22].

Evolution of the ABC CFTR protein towards a channel activity is associated with the degradation of “gates” typical of the alternating access mechanism governing the ABC transporter cycle, as well as with the stabilization of an open conformation [5]. Several studies, conducted at both the experimental and theoretical levels, have shed light into the structural features of CFTR associated with this specific evolution (reviewed in [5, 23]). In particular, a lateral portal has been identified between MSD1 transmembrane (TM) helices TM4 and TM6 in the ATP-bound 3D structure of CFTR, in which the NBDs are tightly associated (Fig. 1A and B) [24–29]. This lateral portal, lined with basic amino acids (K190, R248, R303, K370), allows hydrated anions to freely diffuse between the cytosol and the inner vestibule at the base of the channel pore. As reviewed in [5], this evolution of CFTR is linked to specific features of the amino acid sequence of TM6 [30], which is kinked outward relatively to the canonical architecture of ABC transporters [28]. In the cryo-EM 3D structures of the ATP-bound, but still closed channel [28, 29], the region between the MSD2 TM10 and TM12, symmetrical to that of the TM4-TM6 portal, has also a number of basic amino acids (R1030, K1041, R1048, R1158), but does not have a similar opening. Modelling work [24] and MD simulations on the zebrafish CFTR experimental 3D structure [31] have however indicated the possibility of a secondary lateral portal between these two TM10 and TM12 helices. Recent MD simulations performed with the human CFTR experimental 3D structure argue against this secondary portal and favor the presence of an off-permeation pathway binding site for chloride ions within the intracellular vestibule [32] (Fig. 1B). This ion-binding site, formed by basic amino acids (K1041, R1048) from TM10, is located next to the first main ion-binding site of the permeation pathway, including K190, R248 and R303 [32]. Other basic amino acids from TM10 (R1030) and TM12 (R1158) are located within a groove at the surface of the protein (Fig. 1A and B), in close proximity to the elbow helix of MSD2, which marks the N-terminal end of this transmembrane domain. This region is likely to interact with the C-terminal end of the R region. A model of this part of the R region was proposed [33], forming a linker connecting an helical segment, which was observed in the cryo-EM 3D structure, to the MSD2 elbow helix. The helical segment, located 20 Å from the elbow helix, is in contact with the N-terminal part of the lasso, but had an inadequate density to be modeled beyond backbone atoms [28] (Fig. 1A). A noticeable feature is that the asymmetry of the two TM4-TM6 and TM10-TM12 sites described above persists in the cryo-EM 3D structure of the non-phosphorylated, ATP-free conformation, in which the two NBDs are separated [34]. TM4 and TM6 are indeed fully dissociated, while at the same level, TM10 and TM12 are still in contact, moreover in close proximity of a helical, unassigned segment of the R region (Fig. 1C and D).

Here, we explored the impact of the mutation of TM12 R1158 and TM10 R1030, by considering the effect in both the WT protein and gating mutants (e.g. G551D). We also evaluated the influence of W846, an aromatic residue located on the MSD2 elbow helix at the edge of the TM10-TM12 groove, as well as amino acids E838 and T844 of the C-terminal end of the R region, which were predicted by the model described above [33] to interact with amino acids of this groove.

Experimental results showed that mutations of the basic and aromatic residues globally increased channel activity in both WT and gating mutation background, even enabling channel opening by potentiators without the need to phosphorylate the channel. These effects were observed when the VX-770 binding site was mutated and were additive to gain-of-function mutants within the selectivity filter. Collectively, these results indicate that this region of MSD2 is a novel critical actor of CFTR regulation.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and mutagenesis

The cDNA of CFTR WT (M470) was subcloned in pTracer as previously published [35]. Mutagenesis of all the indicated mutants was performed using the QuickChange XL II mutagenesis kit (Agilent, Les Ulis, France) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Obtained mutants were fully sequenced (Eurofins Genomic, Les Ulis, France), amplified and purified (Macherey-Nagel, Hœrdt, France). Plasmid concentrations were measured using a Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France).

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293 cells were cultivated in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France) and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2. For western blot analysis, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with 1.5 µg CFTR plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France). For halide sensitive YFP assay, cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France) with a total of 1.5 µg of plasmid, either 1.3 µg of YFP plasmid and 0.2 µg of CFTR plasmids in WT background or 0.75 µg of YFP plasmid and 0.75 µg of gating mutant CFTR plasmids.

Western blot analysis

The day after transfection, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (Roche Life Science, Basel, Switzerland) and protein concentration measured using RcDc assay (BioRad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Western blot analysis was performed using 60 µg of protein from each sample separated on 7% SDS-PAGE gels. After transfer onto nitrocellulose membranes, CFTR was probed using antibody 660 or 596 (J.R. Riordan, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, and Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Therapeutics, Chapel Hill, NC, USA). and alpha-tubulin probed with antibody DM1A (SantaCruz, Dallas, TX, USA). Quantification of band intensities was performed using ImageJ software.

Halide sensitive functional assay

CFTR activity was measured in transiently transfected HEK293 cells using the halide-sensitive yellow fluorescent protein YFP-H148Q/I152L [36]. The day after transfection, cells were transferred to poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France) coated 96 well black/clear bottom microplates (Greiner-BioOne, Les Ulis, France). After 24 h, plates were washed with PBS and each well incubated 30 min with 100 µL of PBS containing 8-(4-chlorophenylthio)-adenosine 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate (cpt-cAMP, 100 µM) and 3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX, 100 µM) (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France) with and without VX-770 (10 µM) or Glpg1837 (from 10 − 6 to 30 µM) (both from Selleckchem, Planegg, Germany). Plates were then transferred to a ClarioStar plate-reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany) and fluorescence measured (YFP filters) during injection. After 3 s, 200 µl of PBS-NaI (PBS solution where NaCl is replaced with NaI) are injected (final NaI concentration of 91 mM). Cell fluorescence recordings were normalized to the initial average value measured and signal decay was fitted to an exponential function to derive the maxi-mal slope. Maximal slopes were converted to rates of change in intracellular I- con-centration (in mM/s).

CFTR cell surface quantification

CFTR cell surface was measured using hIBIT complementation (Promega, Charbonnières-les-Bains, France) as already performed [37]. This assay is based on the reconstitution of the nanoluciferase activity which is split in two parts: an eleven amino acid peptide (smBIT) inserted between CFTR amino acids S898 and R899 and a large subunit (lgBIT). Transfected HEK-293 cells were plated in white 96-well plates and 24 h after transfection cells were washed with PBS. First cells were incubated with a recombinant lgBIT and furimazin substrate to measure cell surface CFTR. Second, detection of luminescence was performed after cell permeabilization using Triton x100 (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France) to enable the diffusion of the LgBIT inside the cells and the quantification of the total amount of CFTR. Quantifications were performed by calculating the Surface-CFTR/Total-CFTR ratio. Detection of the produced luminescent was performed on a Mitras plate reader (Berthold, Thoiry, France).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were described as mean (± SEM). Comparisons to WT conditions and between treated and untreated conditions were made using a one-way ANOVA for multiple comparison and post-test Tukey’s for p evaluation. Dose response curves were fitted using Hill equation (log[agonist]vs response) with a slope factor of n = 1.0. Calculated EC50 is given with a 95% confidence interval.

3D structure analysis and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

3D structures were extracted from the Protein Data Bank (cryo-EM 3D structures of CFTR, pdb 5UAK, 6MSM and 6O2P) and from the article of Wong and colleagues [33] (Model of the C-terminal part of the R region). 3D structures were visualized with the UCSF Chimera package [38].

For MD simulations, the 3D structure of phosphorylated, ATP-bound human CFTR (pdb:6MSM) was first embedded in a 1-palmytoyl-2-oleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (POPC) bilayer and solvated in a 150 mM NaCl solution. The CHARMM36 force field [39] was used for the protein, lipids and ions, and the TIP3P model for water. Minimization, equilibration and production steps were performed on the occigen/cines supercomputer using Gromacs 2019.1 [40]. The standard CHARMM-GUI inputs [41] were used for the minimization and equilibration of the systems. During these steps, harmonic restraints applied to the protein heavy atoms and the lipid heads and were gradually released during 1.2 ns. The production dynamics were then performed in the NPT ensemble without any restraints. Nose-Hoover thermostat [42] and Parrinello-Rahman barostat [43] were used to keep the temperature and the pressure constant at 310 K and 1 bar. Periodic boundary conditions were used and the particle mesh ewald algorithm was applied to treat long range electrostatic interactions [44]. A switching function was applied between 10 and 12 Å for the non-bonded interactions. LINCS [45] was applied to constrain the bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms. The integration timestep was set to 2 fs and the overall length of the trajectories was 1 µs.

In order to more closely resemble a plasma membrane, made of different types of lipids that are distributed asymmetrically across the two leaflets of the bilayer [46], a second set of MD simulations was performed using the phosphorylated, ATP-bound 3D structure of human CFTR (pdb 6O2P), from which the bound-VX-770 was removed. The lipid bilayer consisted here in a mixture of POPC (30%), POPE (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine, 30%), POPS (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine, 10%), DSM (sphingomyelin, 10%) and CHOL (cholesterol, 20%). The asymmetry related solely on POPS (only in the inner leaflet) and DSM (only in the outer leaflet), according to other studies [47]. The overall length of the trajectories was here 500 ns (2 replica).

Distances between amino acids of interest were calculated and plotted using an in-house python script using the MDanalysis library [48, 49]. Electrostatic contacts between amino acids and lipid molecules, involving N, O, S, and P atoms, were calculated along the MD simulations with the Voronoi Laguerre Delaunay for Macromolecules (VLDM) program [50, 51], which is based on a tessellation method. A contact occurs whenever two atoms share a common face in the tessellation.

Results

Mutations within the TM10-TM12 groove induce gain-of-function in the WT context

We first considered amino acid R1158, lying at the center of the groove at the TM10-TM12 interface. We evaluated the position of this amino acid after having embedded the experimental 3D structure of wild-type, ATP-bound CFTR in a simplified membrane bilayer, made of phosphatidylcholine (POPC), and performed 1µs of molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. This MD is important to consider for assessing the dynamics of CFTR residues over time in presence of lipids, particularly at the membrane-cytosol interface. R1158 is located in the cytoplasmic extension of the TM12 helix, in close contact with TM10 (Fig. 1A and black curve in Figure S1A). This last helix corresponds to the ICL4 internal helix of a four-helix bundle formed by the four ICLs in the ATP-bound conformation of CFTR. In the model built after having assigned the amino acids of the unknown helical segment of the R region [33], R1158 was moreover predicted to interact with the C-terminal end of the R region, which likely runs perpendicular to the membrane plane (Fig. 1A, in green). The R1030 position was also investigated, as it is also located in the TM10-TM12 groove, but at the edge of the membrane inner leaflet. In our MD simulation, the side chain of R1030 interacts stably with both D1154 (salt-bridge, black curve in Figure S1B) and the polar head of a lipid, in the vicinity of W846 at the border of the R region and the MSD2 elbow helix (Fig. 2A, left). We also considered this tryptophane as it makes the transition between the TM10-TM12 shallow groove and the deeper groove formed by the elbow helix at the surface of MSD2. We performed a second set of MD simulations (2 replica of 500 ns) with a more complex, asymmetric membrane bilayer, including different types of lipids in order to be closer to the composition of a plasma membrane (see Material and Methods for details). These MD simulations supported the involvement of amino acids of this region in the interaction with polar heads of lipids (in particular POPS). POPS made stable bonds with R1030, but also with W846, as well as more occasionally with R1158, drastically shifting its side chain from its exposed position to the solvent towards the membrane-cytosol interface (Fig. 2A, right and Table S1).

Mutagenesis was first performed in the wild-type (WT) background to generate mutants R1030A, R1158A, R1158T. Western blot analysis revealed that these mutants presented an unmodified maturation profile (Figure S2A) while functional analysis using the YFP-halide sensitive assay showed altered behavior. WT was found, as expected, to be inactive in absence of cAMP elevating cocktail (cpt-cAMP/IBMX, referred to as cAMP hereafter) or in the VX-770 alone condition while active with both cAMP and cAMP/VX-770 (Fig. 2B and D). None of the R1158A/T and R1030A mutant channels showed activity in control DMSO condition but addition of VX-770 alone was sufficient to activate them, contrary to WT channels (Fig. 2 C and D). In the presence of cAMP, CFTR-R1158T showed enhanced activity as compared to WT, R1158A or R1030A channels. This was not linked to increased channel abundance at the cell-surface as measured using nano-luciferase complementation assay (Figure S3). Addition of cAMP/VX-770 had no effect on either R1158A, R1158T or R1030A, contrary to the WT condition (Fig. 2 C and D). Mutagenesis was also performed to generate mutants W846A, W846Q, W846Y and W846E which did not modify protein maturation or increase cell surface expression (Figures S2B and S3). Contrary to WT and similarly to R1158A/T and R1030A, W846Y/E mutants were found to be active after addition of VX-770 alone or upon cAMP stimulation while responding poorly to potentiation when the channel was phosphorylated (Fig. 2E).

These results indicate that mutating amino acids of MSD2 at the TM10-TM12 interface enable VX-770 to activate the channel independently of cAMP induced PKA activation and that potentiation was ineffective when the channel was activated by phosphorylation.

Mutations within the TM10-TM12 groove induce gain-of-function in the context of gating mutant G551D

To evaluate the requirement of NBD dimerization in this channel opening, R1158A/T and R1030A mutations were introduced in plasmids encoding gating mutation G551D, which is located in ATP-binding site 2 and alters NBD dimerization and/or ATP binding [52, 53]. As expected for a gating mutant, CFTR-G551D activity could only be measured in the cAMP/VX-770 condition (Fig. 3A and C). Introduction of R1158T within the G551D background did not alter protein maturation (Figure S2C). Contrary to CFTR-G551D, double mutants CFTR-G551D-R1030A, G551D-R1158A and G551D-R1158T showed cAMP induced activity even in the absence of potentiator (Fig. 3B and C). Treatment with potentiator VX-770 alone did not induce channel activity while the cAMP/VX-770 combination enhanced channel activity of double mutants, with double mutants presenting enhanced activity as compared to CFTR-G551D (Fig. 3B and C). A similar response could be observed with CFTR-G551D-W846Y/E mutant while CFTR-G551D-W846A/Q had no effect (Fig. 3D).

Dose response curves were obtained with Glpg1837, a potentiator that binds CFTR in the same pocket than VX-770 [20]. Analysis showed similar EC50 for G551D (EC50 = 0.81 [0.44–1.48] µM) and G551D-R1158T (EC50 = 0.32 [0.18–0.57] µM) and a clear leftward shift for G551D-R1158A (EC50 = 0.005 [0.002–0.011] µM, p < 0.05) and G551D-R1030A (EC50 = 0.054 [0.019 − 0.015] µM, p < 0.05) without increasing the maximal response (Fig. 3E). While no such shift was observed with CFTR-G551D-W846A (EC50 = 0.64 [0.46–0.54]), both CFTR-G551D-W846E (EC50 = 0.011 [0.006–0.019], p < 0.05) and CFTR-G551D-W846Y (EC50 = 0.015 [0.007–0.030], p < 0.05) also induced a marked leftward shift (Fig. 3F). Altogether, these results indicate that enhanced channel activity induced by R1158A/T, R1030A and W846Y/E mutations also occurred in the presence of gating mutant G551D and that some mutants altered the sensitivity (R1158A, R1030A, W846Y/E) to potentiation and one (R1158T) the amplitude of response.

Mutations within the TM10-TM12 groove induce gain-of-function of other gating mutants

The effect of R1158A/T mutants was also evaluated in the context of the gating mutation G1349D located in NBD2, within the ATP-binding site 1. Maturation of CFTR-G1349D was not affected by R1158T (Figure S2C). CFTR-R1158A-G1349D and CFTR-R1158T-G1349D were active in both cAMP and cAMP/VX-770 conditions, but contrary to CFTR-G551D-R1158A/T, application of VX-770 alone also elicited channel activity (Fig. 4A and B C). R1158T rescued the activity of other gating mutants located in different CFTR domains, such as G178R (ICL1), G551S (NBD1), S1244E (NBD2) and G1255P (NBD2), with here again activity measured upon application of VX-770 alone, cAMP and cAMP/VX-770 (Figure S4C). These results suggest that G551D is less responsive to R1158T gain-of-function as compared to other gating mutants, especially in response to the VX-770 alone condition.

Finally, we also considered the gating mutation G551D in association with the non-sense mutation W1282X, a frequent mutation associated with severe disease [54] and leading to premature termination of the protein, which lacks one half of NBD2 and the C-terminal end. Maturation of CFTR-G551D-W1282X was not affected by R1158T (Figure S2CD). Contrary to double mutant CFTR-G551D-W1282X, triple mutant CFTR-G551D-R1158T-W1282X was active in the cAMP elevating cocktail condition, confirming that R1158T induced gain-of-function was independent of NBD dimerization (Fig. 4D, E, F). Interestingly, CFTR-G551D-R1158T-W1282X was also found to be active after application of VX-770 alone, contrary to CFTR-G551D-R1158T (Fig. 3B C). This effect was not reproduced by the removal of the C-terminal PDZ domain as determined with CFTR-G551D-R1158T-Q1476X. These results indicate that a complete NBD2 or the C-terminus of the channel inhibited CFTR-G551D activity in this condition (Fig. 4G).

Exploring the C-terminal end of the R region

A modeling work [33] suggested that the C-terminus of the R region preceding the MSD2 elbow helix (aa 831–846) might contact the TM10-TM12 groove (Fig. 5A). This intracellular segment was first removed, with the crude aim of examining the effect of this region on potentiation. Western blot analysis showed that the C/(B + C) maturation ratio of CFTR-del(831–846) (60 ± 3%, n = 4) slightly reduced as compared to CFTR-WT (77 ± 5%, n = 5) (illustrated in Figure S2E). Similar to CFTR-WT, CFTR-del(831–846) was found to be active upon application of cAMP or cAMP/VX-770 combination, albeit at a lower level (Fig. 5B and C), consistent with a reduced maturation of the protein. On the other hand, CFTR-del(831–846) was clearly active upon application of VX-770 alone, contrary to CFTR-WT (Fig. 5B and C). These results indicate that CFTR-del(831–846) can be activated independently of cAMP induced PKA activation similarly to R1030A, R1158A/T and W846Y/E mutants.

To evaluate the requirement of NBD dimerization in this channel opening, the del(831–846) mutation was introduced in plasmids encoding mutant G551D or G551D-W1282X. CFTR-G551D-del(831–846) showed a similar response profile to CFTR-G551D (Fig. 5D), the lower amplitude in the response to cAMP/VX-770 being again consistent with the maturation defect (Figure S2E). Western blot analysis of CFTR-G551D-del(831–846)-W1282X showed lower size of the protein as expected for a truncated protein and reduced maturation as observed with CFTR-G551D-del(831–846) (Figure S2E). Contrary to double mutant CFTR-G551D-del(831–846), triple mutant CFTR-G551D-del(831–846)-W1282X was found to be active in the VX-770 alone conditions (Fig. 5D and E).

According to the model of the R region C-terminus in the cryo-EM 3D structure of phosphorylated, ATP-bound human CFTR [33], two amino acids of this segment (E838, T844) were predicted to establish direct contacts with key amino acid R1158 (Fig. 5A). Indeed, R1158 could make a salt-bridge and an H-bond with the side chains atoms of E838 and T844, respectively. Moreover, E838 also interacts with the side chain N atom of Q1042. These contacts should not be sensitive to lipids, since they are distant from the inner leaflet of the membrane bilayer. However, our MD simulations run with CFTR embedded in an asymmetric bilayer, including different types of lipids, indicated that this network could be influenced by bonds that POPS might establish with R1158, repositioning its side chain towards the membrane-cytosol interface (Fig. 2A).

Introduction of mutations E838A or E838A-T844A did not modify protein maturation or cell surface expression (Figures S2B and S3). Functional results indicated that both mutant E838A and double mutant E838A-T844A responded as WT channels in all tested conditions (Figure S5A). In the presence of gating mutation G551D, mutants of the C-terminal end did not respond in the cAMP alone condition (Figure S5B). Dose response curves performed with Glpg1837 on G551D-E838A (n = 5) and G551D-E838A-T844A (n = 5), showed an unmodified EC50 (EC50 = 0.81 [0.36–1.81] and EC50 = 0.45 [0.22–0.94]) (Figure S5C). These results reveal that altering contacts between the TM10-TM12 groove and the C-terminus of the R region, via disruption of the predicted R1158-E838-T844 network did not modify CFTR activity.

This series of results show that channels lacking amino acids 831–846 behaved similarly to point mutants located within the TM10-TM12 shallow groove but that this was probably not linked to direct contacts between the two regions. Of note, interpretation of these results is challenging because the 831–846 deletion may also abolish a critical interaction involving an upstream region, in particular in contact with the lasso (see discussion).

Effect of R1158 mutants in the context of mutations within the VX-770 binding site and the selectivity filter

We then wished to evaluate the interplay between the TM10-TM12 interface and sites known to be involved in the modulation of anion flux. A binding site for VX-770 and Glpg1837 was mapped on the cryo-EM 3D structure of human CFTR, at the level of the unwound part of helix TM8 [20]. As with the TM10-12 interface, this binding site is not in direct contact with the channel pore or with the TM4-TM6 portal (Fig. 6A).

As already reported [20], introduction of the Y304A mutation within the VX-770 binding site did not affect channel maturation (Figure S2F) but prevented response to potentiator VX-770 (Fig. 6B) and shifted to higher concentrations the dose-response curve to Glpg1837 (EC50 = 7.45 [3.06–18.17] µM, p < 0.05) (Fig. 6C). The effect of R1158A which shifts the dose response curve to lower values was tested on CFTR-G551D-Y304A. Triple mutant CFTR-G551D-Y304A-R1158A showed strong basal activity upon cAMP stimulation (Fig. 6B), associated with both a leftward shift of the dose response curve (EC50 = 1.5 [0.39–4.95] µM) and enhanced response to potentiation (Fig. 6C). These results show that R1158A shifted the dose response curve even in the presence of Y304A.

Finally, we evaluated the effect of R1158T which increased the response amplitude without affecting the dose response curve on a mutant presenting a reduced conductance. Located within the selectivity filter (Fig. 6A), F337S was shown to substantially reduce the unitary currents while inducing gain-of-function by increasing channel sensitivity to PKA activation [55]. The F337S mutation did not affect CFTR maturation or increase cell surface expression (Figures S2F and S3). Introduction of F337S within the G551D background did not modify the response to DMSO or VX-770 alone but elicited a small response to the cAMP elevating cocktail and reduced by half the response to cAMP and VX-770 (Fig. 6D). Addition of R1158T in the F337S-G551D context enabled channel activity in the VX-770 alone condition contrary to either CFTR-F337S-G551D or CFTR-G551D-R1158T (Figs. 3D and 6D), indicating that the two gain-of-function mutations were additive. In the cAMP condition, CFTR-F337S-G551D-R1158T was found to be less active than CFTR-G551D-R1158T (Figs. 3D and 6D), consistent with R1158T not modifying the reduced conductance associated with F337S. Dose response curves showed that CFTR-G551D-F337S had a reduced EC50 (EC50 = 0.02 [0.009–0.053] µM) while CFTR-F337S-G551D-R1158T was found to be non-responsive to Glpg1837 at all tested concentrations (Fig. 6E). These results show that R1158T did not correct the reduced conductivity of F337S but enabled channel opening in the VX-770 alone condition while preventing the response to potentiators when the channel is phosphorylated.

Discussion

CFTR channel opening requires different events, dominated by the phosphorylation of the R region via the protein kinase A (PKA), ATP binding to the NBDs that induces their dimerization and the onset of a conformational wave towards the MSDs (reviewed in [1, 6, 56]). Several studies have highlighted long-range allosteric communications connecting the NBDs to the channel pore (e.g [8, 9, 57]). and allosteric hotspots within the MSD TMs [55] and ICLs [58, 59]. ICLs play a central role in the allosteric communication through their coupling helices, which establish contacts with the NBDs, but also by forming through their internal helices (TM3, TM4, TM9, TM10) a four-helix bundle, which represents a critical structural feature of the open form of the channel [60, 61]. Of note is that simple binding of PKA to CFTR prior to phosphorylation allows channel opening by ATP [62], probably not by direct binding to the R region but by lowering the free energy of the NBD-dimerized state [63]. On another hand, PKA phosphorylation is still required for ATP-independent channel opening [12], indicating that multiple mechanisms are probably involved in the allosteric modulation associated with the phosphorylation of the R region [59]. Allostery is also central to the effect of the potentiator VX-770 [20] and the dual potentiator-corrector VX-445 (elexacaftor) [64], even though their mechanisms of action are not yet fully understood. Here, we show that channel opening in response to phosphorylation is affected by modifications of a region located within MSD2, at the edge of the membrane inner leaflet. It includes two basic amino acids from TM10 (R1030) and TM12 (R1158), the MSD2 counterpart of the MSD1 TM4-TM6 which forms a lateral portal through which ion flux occurs from the cytoplasm towards the inner vestibule of the pore. According to the cryo-EM 3D structure of the ATP-bound, phosphorylated CFTR [20], this region does not form a symmetrical portal but instead, closes the end of a groove in which a critical tryptophan (W846) lies, at the N-terminal end of the MSD2 elbow helix, at the precise junction point with the C-terminal end of the R region.

In the WT context, mutations of these three amino acids (R1158A/T, R1030A and W846Y/E) enabled channel opening with potentiator VX-770 even in the absence of cAMP (data summarized in Table S2). These results indicated that modifications in this region lead to the activation of the channel, in a similar way than PKA phosphorylation does. Intriguingly, mutated channels showed a different sensitivity to potentiation depending on their phosphorylation state. When channels were not phosphorylated, potentiators induced channel opening while when channels were PKA phosphorylated, potentiators had little or no effect. This result is consistent with potentiators and mutations promoting channel opening via a similar mechanism.

Several hypotheses can be put forward to understand the critical role of the highlighted MSD2 region. The proximity of R1158 to the C-terminus of the R region, according to Wong’s model [33], suggests a direct effect on it. In turn, changes of the R region induced by PKA phosphorylation could be transmitted directly to MSD2 leading to channel opening. This hypothesis is however not supported by this study as mutations of amino acids from the R region predicted to interact with R1158 had no effect. Nonetheless, this hypothesis cannot be completely ruled out, as the model for this end of the R region may be incorrect or need to be refined. The deletion of the fragment encompassing amino acids 831 to 846 also does not allow us to conclude that there is a direct interaction between MSD2 and the R region, insofar as the effect of this deletion must be understood in the general context of the perturbations it induces at medium distance, in particular at the point where the R region comes into contact with the N-terminal interfacial lasso motif (Fig. 5A). This lasso motif wraps around MSD1 TM2 and TM6 and MSD2 TM10 and TM11, with these two last helices forming the domain-swapped ICL4 interacting with NBD1. Several studies have otherwise highlighted the importance of this lasso-R region interface for allosteric communications. First, Wong et al. [33] demonstrated the critical role of the I37R mutation located in the lasso motif and predicted to interact with the R region for CFTR channel gating activity. Second, Fiedorczuk and Chen [64] have highlighted in this same region (including the lasso motif, TM10, TM11 and TM2) a binding pocket for the dual modulator elexacaftor. Finally, computational studies have recently highlighted an allosteric hotspot proximal to the docking site of the R region on the MSDs [57].

Our MD simulations performed using the cryo-EM 3D structure of phosphorylated, ATP-bound human CFTR (Fig. 7A) suggest that, while R1158 stably interacts with TM10 Q1038 in the wild type condition (Fig. 7B and C and Figure S1A), the R1158T mutation locally brings the TM10 and TM12 closer together, thereby enhancing their interaction at level of their crossing point (Fig. 7D). We should also point out that R1158 is one residue before S1159, for which two rare CF variants (S1159F and S1159P) were reported to severely inhibit ATP-dependent channel gating [65]. In contrast to R1158, S1159 side chain is oriented towards the pore, forming an H-bond with TM9 D979 (Fig. 7B and C and Fig. S1C). This interaction is likely to contribute to the stabilization of the open form of the channel, as already reported for other non-covalent bonds (salt-bridges) within the TMDs (TM6 R347 - TM8 D924; TM6 R352 TM9 D993 [29, 66–68]) and the MSDs [60, 61]). The direct link on the polypetidic chain of R1158 and S1159 suggests an effect of the R1158 mutation on the network of non-covalent bonds within the pore, a hypothesis which was supported by our MDs, where salt bridges between TM9 D979 and TM10 R1048 appeared to be more stable over time in the R1158T CFTR than in the WT condition (Fig. 7D and Figure S1D). Therefore, these salt-bridges may act in favor of an increased activity of the channel, by stabilizing the open state. This mechanism, similar to that suggested for the VX-770 one [69], may explain the poor response to potentiators observed with mutants R1158A/T, R1030A or W846Y/E.

Finally, we cannot rule out the possibility that R1158A/T, R1030A or W846Y/E mutations could lead to the opening of an additional portal to the TM4-TM6 portal, as suggested by MD performed on the zebrafish CFTR 3D structures [31], or other as yet undescribed portal(s), even if this hypothesis is speculative at this stage. Whether the effect of the mutations studied here at the TM10-TM12 interface may rely on a perturbation in the modulation exerted by bilayer lipids, as suggested here for R1158 by the preliminary analysis of MD simulations, is also a question that deserves further exploration.

The mutations described here also induced gain-of-function in combination with gating mutations or mutations impacting NBD dimerization. In combination with R1158T, all the gating mutations tested (summarized in Table S3) displayed increased sensitivity to PKA activation, with channel activity being measured in response to cAMP elevation. Increased PKA sensitivity is observed with other gain-of-functions mutations such as K978C [59], located at the same level as R1158 but oriented towards the pore (Fig. 6), or D1341R and D173R [58]. The two last mutations were suggested to affect channel activity by enhancing the interaction between the NBD2 X-loop and ICL1. A similar effect on the transmission interfaces with NBDs could thus be considered for the R1158T mutation, which may act on the ICL4:NBD1 interface through the MSD2 swapped TM10-TM11 pair or on the ICL3:NBD2 interface, in direct continuation of TM12 (see before). Gating mutants associated with R1158T also responded to potentiation even in the absence of cAMP, with the exception of G551D. CFTR-G551D-R1158T mutation responded to cAMP only when the C-terminus of the protein (aa W1282 to L1481) was removed, uncovering an inhibitory effect of this region in this condition.

Mutations R1030A, R1158A and W846Y/E also affected the sensitivity to potentiators, as observed with shifts in the dose response curves towards lower concentrations. As mutations probably do not modify the potentiator binding site and the effect still occurs in combination with Y304A, a mutation hindering potentiator binding [20], these results would indicate a higher facility of the channel to opening. In this context, mutation R1158T behaved differently as it increased transport rates without changing the sensitivity to potentiators. When R1158T was associated with F337S, a mutant that reduces channel conductance but enhances channel sensitivity to PKA activation [55], channel activation by potentiators was observed even in the absence of PKA activation. This result revealed that F337S and R1158T induced gains of function were additive. On the other hand, as maximal transport rates were not modified, F337S reduced conductivity was probably not modified.

These latter results would need to be further investigated using single channel analysis to assess channel opening and closing behavior, single channel conductance or sensitivity to varying ATP or PKA concentrations. Indeed, these parameters are not accessible when measuring global responses in intact cells such as performed here using the halide sensitive YFP assay. Of note regarding the recent cryo-EM data on CFTR-PKA complexes [63] is that the CFTR TM10:TM12 interface studied here is not involved in the interaction with PKA, which is a critical actor of CFTR regulation.

In conclusion, we demonstrate here the importance of a region within MSD2 symmetric to the TM4-TM6 portal, which plays a critical role in the regulation of the CFTR channel activity, affecting both sensitivity to PKA and potentiators. Point mutations within this region increased both channel activity and sensitivity to potentiation and also enabled channel opening by potentiators without the need to elevate cAMP levels. These effects were observed when NBD dimerization was compromised or when the binding site of the VX-770 potentiator was mutated and were additive to gain-of-function mutants within the selectivity filter. Taken together, these results identify that this region of MSD2 is a novel critical actor of CFTR regulation.
