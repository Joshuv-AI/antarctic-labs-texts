Continuity of care and hospitalization frequency for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions after hearing-disability onset: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhaoyan Piao, Heekyung Choi, Boyoung Jeon, Euna Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe assessed the effect of continuity of care (COC) on the frequency of hospitalization for Ambulatory care-sensitive conditions (ACSCs) to estimate the impact of COC and hearing disability relative to the controls. This retrospective cohort study used claim data of Korean National Health Insurance Service – National Sample Cohort 2.0 DB. We used propensity score matching to determine a control group for the hearing disability group by age, sex, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index. The hearing-impaired group included 720 participants, and the non-disabled control group, consisting of individuals without any form of disability, had 1,423 individuals. We used the frequency of hospitalization for ACSCs during one-year follow-up as the dependent variable for Poisson regression. We measured COC with the Bice–Boxerman Continuity of Care Index (COCI); higher COCI values represent better continuity of care, with COCI values ranging from 0 to 1. Poisson regression showed that disability status modifies the effect of COCI on the incidence of hospitalization. COCI = 1 reduced hospitalizations in people with hearing disabilities (adjusted Incidence Rate Ratio [aIRR]: 0.30, 95% CI: 0.20–0.44) but was not statistically significant for controls. In the COCI = 1 group, the effect of disability was not significant(aIRR: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.83–1.44). Introduction

Ambulatory care-sensitive conditions (ACSCs) allow high-quality outpatient care to avoid hospitalizations1–3. The United States Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) initially defined ACSCs as diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, heart failure, pneumonia, urinary tract infection (UTI), and asthma3. The evidence shows that on-time appointments and high-quality care can lower hospitalization rates for ACSCs4. In South Korea, 180.8 per 10,000 people were hospitalized because of ACSCs in 2015, although average hospitalization rates for ASCSs dropped from 2008 to 20195,6.

A hearing disability is a multifaceted condition with often unmet needs for healthcare utilization. For example, people with hearing disability experience communication barriers when seeking medication and care, which can affect subsequent medication adherence and care7. Additionally, hearing loss has been associated with an increased risk of hospitalization8. And sensory or sensorineural hearing loss is considered a chronic condition that requires ongoing management, similar to other chronic diseases such as diabetes. Therefore, it should be evaluated with continuity of care9. It is likely to affect older people more since more than 60% of people with hearing impairment globally are over 5010. In South Korea, the 2017 Survey on Persons with Disabilities found that 79.1% of people with hearing disabilities have been living with chronic disease such as hypertension and diabetes for more than three months11. Additionally, because of their particularities, people with hearing disabilities may have suffered from other health conditions before their hearing was impaired12,13. According to the World Health Organization, the main causes of hearing loss or deafness in adults (including older adults) are chronic disease, smoking, otosclerosis, age-related sensorineural degeneration, and sudden sensorineural impairment14.

Continuity of care (COC)15is an important concept for doctors and patients, as it can influence disease management. For patients, COC means that the medical provider is aware of previous health conditions and that the same provider will continue to care for them16. For medical providers, COC entails helping patients believe that their providers understand their conditions enough to practice their skills optimally and to have confidence that other medical providers will recognize their contributions16. Higher levels of COC are connected to fewer hospital and emergency visits, lower mortality rates, and higher use of preventive care17,18. COC was also associated with greater patient satisfaction with treatment results19,20.

Most COC studies have surveyed the general population regarding chronic conditions such as ACSCs4,5. However, only a few COC studies have assessed people with disabilities21,22. These studies included relatively few participants (less than 500) and focused on patients with Type 2 diabetes; therefore, they do not adequately represent the hearing disability population21. Most previous studies on the association between COC and ACSC hospitalization have not always shown a positive association between COC and ACSC hospitalization23. Additionally, there is no data regarding people with hearing disabilities hospitalized for ACSCs. The current study fills the gap in the previous literature by assessing whether COC’s impact on hospitalization for ACSCs differs depending on the presence of a hearing disability. In addition, we explored the age effect and hearing disability level effect.

Methods

Data

We analyzed data from the National Health Insurance Service – National Sample Cohort (NHIS-NSC) 2.0 DB, a nationally representative retrospective cohort database based on insurance claims data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), South Korea’s obligatory universal public health insurance system24,25. South Korea created the NHIS-NSC 2.0 DB by taking approximately 2% of the NHIS population as of January 1, 2002 (1,137,861 people) and following them for 18 years until December 31, 2019. No survey or sample weighting is required for the NHIS-NSC 2.0 dataset, as it uses a stratified sampling method that ensures the sample is representative of the entire Korean population across key demographic variables. We randomly extracted information on the eligible population in proportion after stratifying according to age, sex, region, type of health insurance, and insurance premium (a proxy for household income)26. The NHIS-NSC 2.0 DB includes data on eligibility and insurance premiums, death records, medical treatment, health checkups, and healthcare provider information25. We restricted the data to outpatient care visits in this study, given the reduced levels of concern regarding continuity of inpatient care.

Study sample

Our sample included people with hearing disability and a control without any disability. We used propensity score matching (PSM) to reduce selection bias in observational studies27, particularly a nearest neighbor matching without a specified caliper distance since our sample size was sufficiently large. We thus selected appropriate controls for the hearing-disability group using sex, age, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)28, with a 1:2 ratio for the hearing-disability group to the control group.

Figure 1 shows 1,137,861 participants registered in the NHIS-NSC 2.0 DB between 2002 and 2019. From these, we excluded 63,038 people with other disabilities and 448 people whose disability type changed, leaving 9,761 for the disability group and 1,064,614 for the control group. Among 9,761 people registered as having hearing disability, we excluded persons with the following conditions: registered with a hearing disability before 2006 (N = 2,557), missing values on disability occurrence year, aged under 20 on disability occurrence year, less than two outpatient visits for ACSCs in any of the six years after the onset of hearing disability, hospitalized during the COC measurement period, i.e., one year after the hearing disability occurrence, and having more than ten hospitalizations in any year after disability onset.

Selected participants.

For the control group of 1,064,614 people without any disability throughout the entire study period (2002–2019), we used data between 2006 and 2019. We included data between 2006 and 2019 to ensure that individuals in the control group did not have hearing disabilities or other disabilities prior to 2006, aligning the control group with the hearing-disabled group who developed disabilities after 2006. We excluded people with the following criteria: missing values in age and income level, aged less than 20, having less than two outpatient visits for ACSCs, or having more than 10 hospitalizations. We then conducted PSM and excluded 21 persons hospitalized during the COC measurement period and 29 persons hospitalized in the year before the COC measurement. Thus, we had 720 participants in the hearing disability group and 1,423 in the control group.

Variables

Dependent variable: inpatient hospitalization for ACSCs

We counted the frequency of hospitalizations for ACSCs one year after disability onset (using the date for matching a person in the disability group to the control group) as the dependent variable. We used the definition of ACSCs from the U.S. AHRQ3 to determine whether patients visited outpatients clinics or were hospitalized for each respective ACSC condition using the primary ICD-10 code for each sample person (Supplemental Table 1).

Hearing disability

Once physicians diagnose people with disabilities, they become beneficiaries of the National Disability Registration System in South Korea and registered in the qualification data administered by the Ministry of Health and Welfare11,29. The NHIS-NSC database extracts the registration data from the National Disability Registration System and compiles disability information, including type and severity, updated annually29. Although we used the registration date as the proxy for disability onset in this study, it may well represent the actual onset given the financial incentives to register, as people with disabilities in Korea are entitled to targeted assistance from the social security system and adjusted premiums for health insurance30. To register as having a hearing disability, the person’s hearing must meet the threshold of ≥ 60 dB in both ears or ≥ 80 dB in one ear and ≥ 40 dB in another31. We categorized hearing disability into two levels: mild and severe.

Individuals with multiple disabilities must register with only their primary disability32; hence, we could not identify those with multiple disabilities in our dataset. We also eliminated data from participants whose disability type changed over the research period to maintain consistency. As mentioned, the onset of hearing disability ranged from 2006 to 2014.

Continuity of care

The continuity of care (COC) in the year of hearing disability onset is the key independent variable in this study. We measured the COC for ACSCs using the Bice–Boxerman Continuity of Care Index (COCI)33during the year of disability onset, which could be any year between 2006 and 2014. The COCI indicates the dispersion of visits to each provider, given the total number of outpatient visits for ACSCs and the total number of providers visited. Scores on the measure range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating higher COC34. A score of 0 indicates that all of a patient’s visits were to different medical providers, whereas a score of 1 indicates that all were to the same provider33,35. There is no definitive criterion for quantifying COC based on COCI scores; previous studies have used dichotomous36, tertiles4,37,38, quartiles34, quintiles39, median40, and continuous measures41. In the current study, we divided the continuous measure for the COCI into two levels representing low (COCI < 1.0) and high (COCI = 1.0) continuity, given that more than 60% of people had a COCI of 1. Because South Korea does not have a formal system of primary care physicians, the COCI is not restricted to the primary care physicians system and was applicable in our study42.

Covariates

Covariates controlled in the estimation included sex, age, CCI, residence area, health insurance type, income level, and the calendar year of hearing disability onset. We categorized age as 20–60 or 60 + years old. Income level was the household insurance premium level divided into deciles, with higher deciles indicating higher premiums. We classified participants’ residences as metropolitan, urban, or rural. Health insurance types included local heads of households (non-employees), local household members, work subscribers (employees), dependents of work subscribers, medical aid beneficiaries, and dependents of medical aid beneficiaries.

Statistical analysis

We conducted chi-squared tests to compare all the variable distributions of the disability group with that of the control group. We used Poisson regressions to determine the independent impact of COC and hearing disability on the hospitalization frequency of ACSCs and any interactive effect of COC and hearing disability. We also compared severe hearing disability to mild hearing disability, which served as the reference group. This model assesses the effect of COCI on hospitalization within each disability severity level. We adjusted all multivariable regressions for sex, age, CCI, residence area, health insurance type, income level, and year of disability onset (for controls, we assigned the value for the matched disability person, e.g., 2010 for a person matched with someone who developed a disability in 2010). We conducted all analyses in SAS 9.4, with the significance level set at 5%.

Yonsei University’s Institutional Review Board granted review exemptions for this project (IRB No. 7001988-202206-HR-1593-01E). The study is retrospective and consent waiver from the Yonsei University’s Institutional Review Board. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

General characteristics of study participants

Table 1 shows the participants’ characteristics in the year of hearing disability occurrence. In the hearing-disability and control groups, most individuals were 60 + years old (84.37%), most were male (51.89%), more than 65% had a CCI of 0 or 1, more than half had health insurance as work-related dependents, and more than 80% lived in metropolitan or urban areas. The hearing-disability group had a slightly lower proportion of high COC (65.83%) than the control group (69.36%). In addition, significant differences were also observed in insurance type across groups (p = 0.02).

Poisson regression: the impact of COC on the frequency of hospitalization

Table 2 shows the relationship between COCI, hearing disability, age, and frequency of hospitalization for ACSCs during the one-year follow-up. When COCI = 1, there was a 47% reduction in the incidence of hospitalization during the one-year follow-up period compared with those with COCI < 1 (adjusted incidence rate ratio (aIRR): 0.53, 95% CI: 0.42–0.67). The hospitalization rate was 112% higher in the hearing-disability group compared with the control group (aIRR: 2.12, 95% CI: 1.70–2.65).

Among the hearing-disabled population, those in the COCI = 1 group had a 70% reduction in the incidence of hospitalization (aIRR: 0.30, 95% CI: 0.20–0.44). In contrast, among the non-disabled, the impact of COCI was not statistically significant (aIRR: 1.13, 95% CI: 0.81–1.56). In addition, we analyzed the effect of the high COCI and disability by disability severity for subgroups of people with hearing disabilities (Table 3). The results suggest that COCI = 1 (aIRR: 0.32, 95% CI: 0.22–0.48) is associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of hospitalization, while severe hearing disability (aIRR: 2.89, 95% CI: 1.94–4.31) is associated with a significant increase in hospitalization rates. In the COCI = 1 (aIRR: 4.04, 95% CI: 2.30–7.08) and COCI < 1 (aIRR: 3.58, 95% CI: 1.88–6.82) groups, hospitalization occurred more frequently in people with severe hearing disability than in people with mild hearing disability. However, the effect of COCI was only meaningful in those with a mild hearing disability (aIRR: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.23–0.53).

Discussion

According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimations, 698 million individuals worldwide will have moderate to severe hearing loss by 205010. We anticipate a continued rise in the number of people with hearing disabilities due to a rapidly aging population10. Among Koreans over 20 years old, the prevalence of chronic diseases is higher among people with hearing disabilities compared to those without disabilities; examples include hypertension (49.9% vs. 25.7%) and diabetes (22.2% vs. 10.7%)43, similar to findings from the United States44. People with hearing disabilities reported more depressive symptoms and had a higher prevalence of chronic diseases such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and stroke compared to people without disabilities45,46.

People with hearing disabilities who seek medical care may face difficulties, such as communication problems with doctors47,48. Therefore, COC and the consistency of the relationship between a patient and a medical service provider49 are particularly important for people with hearing disabilities.

Our study reports the beneficial impact of COC on healthcare outcomes among persons with hearing impairment. We demonstrated the effect of COC on the frequency of hospitalization for ACSCs, with the protective effect of the high COCI (of 1) being greater in the hearing-disability group. In a previous study, high-COC patients were more likely to be hospitalized for ACSC than low-COC patients, with the estimated OR ranging between 1.34 and 8.6923. A previous study linked higher levels of COC to lower risks of emergency department visits and complications41. A study by Jang et al21. reported that patients with any disabilities in the “high continuity” COC range (COCI ≥ 0.75) had a reduced risk of end-stage renal disease compared with those in the “low continuity” COC range (COCI < 0.75). However, their sample included relatively few individuals with any disabilities (less than 500), and they all had Type 2 diabetes.

Measurement of COC being precedent to outcome measures is crucial to validly estimating the effect of COC on health outcomes23. We accounted for the order of the COC and ACSC hospitalization by only including individuals who had ACSCs but no hospitalizations at the time of the COC measurement. We also measured COC levels after hearing disability onset for those who were newly registered with a hearing disability to effectively control for the potential reverse effect of hearing disability on the COC.

This research has a few limitations. First, our data only reflected hearing disability and was not representative of the whole population of people with disabilities. Second, this data can not capture whether patients have hearing loss alongside other comorbidities, such as cognitive impairment. This constraint may overlook a subgroup of the population. Third, when we observed the ACSCs hospitalization frequency, we considered hospitalization for any ACSCs without specifying hospitalization for a specific disease. However, Raddish et al. concluded that a patient with a high COC for one condition may also have a high COC for another condition50. Therefore, despite the lack of disease-specific analysis in this study, our results are still meaningful because of the association between COC for one disease and COC for other diseases. However, we could not estimate the changes in the type of disability, severity, and comorbidities after each hospitalization for ACSCs because of the study’s data constraints.

This study applies chronic disease models to hearing loss, offering some insights into a relatively underexplored area. Our results support the hypothesis that improving COC can reduce the frequency of ACSC hospitalizations for people with hearing disability. This outcome improvement indirectly supports the importance of an effective disability-specific healthcare delivery system to promote COC. Further studies need to investigate specifics of the impact of COC on hospitalization among patients with hearing disabilities, such as length of stay, adverse events, or more direct health outcomes in the long term. And we recommend that future studies consider hearing aid use to better understand its impact on patient outcomes. In conclusion, our findings imply that the COC has a greater impact on hospitalization among people with disabilities. Therefore, addressing the barriers to continued use of healthcare for patients with hearing disabilities is essential.
