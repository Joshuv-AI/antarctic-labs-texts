Confirmatory Molecular Diagnosis of RFC1 Expansion in a Korean CANVAS Case Using Nanopore Cas9-Targeted Sequencing
Authors: Jee Min Kim, Narae Kim, Seungbok Lee, Jangsup Moon
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent report by Yun et al.1 describing the first genetically confirmed case of cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy, and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) in Korea. The authors identified biallelic intronic AAGGG repeat expansions in RFC1 using short-read whole-genome sequencing and ExpansionHunter analysis, and reported that the smaller alleles carried “around 20 or more” repeats. While the case is clinically well characterized and phenotypically consistent with CANVAS, it is important to recognize that short-read-based methods such as ExpansionHunter may not accurately resolve repeat size or motif composition, particularly in structurally complex repetitive loci such as RFC1.2

The intronic repeat locus of RFC1 is highly polymorphic. In addition to the canonical pathogenic (AAGGG)n expansion, alternative pathogenic repeat motifs, including ACAGG and rarer motifs such as AGGGC, AAGGC, and AGAGG, have been reported.2345 By contrast, motifs such as AAAAG or AAAGG are generally considered non-pathogenic polymorphic alleles.234 Determining motif composition is therefore critical for accurate molecular diagnosis. Because the RFC1 repeat is embedded within a highly repetitive Alu element, short-read-based approaches may face mapping ambiguity and limited ability to accurately determine repeat size and motif composition.

Although ExpansionHunter is valuable as a screening tool, its size estimation for large repeat expansions is indirect from short-reads and carries limitations.2 Pathogenic AAGGG expansions in RFC1 are typically in the hundreds to thousands of repeat units, well beyond the read length of short-read sequencing, thus cannot span the full repeat tract. Consequently, size estimation must rely on anchored or in-repeat reads, which may lack the precision of direct long-read sequencing.

To confirm the genetic diagnosis, we conducted Nanopore long-read sequencing and subsequent motif analysis for the reported case for clarification. This amplification-free approach enables direct long-read sequencing across the entire repeat region and allows precise characterization of repeat size and motif composition.6 Because nCATS selectively enriches regions of interest defined by Cas9 guide RNA design, it provides targeted long-read analysis without the need for whole-genome sequencing, thereby reducing sequencing burden and cost and improving clinical feasibility for confirmatory testing of repeat expansion disorders. We further analyzed the dataset using STRiker (https://github.com/BaeLab/STRiker), a dedicated algorithm developed to generate read-level repeat quantification and intuitive visualization outputs.6 The repeat size was estimated based on aligned read length, and the final repeat number was determined using the most frequent repeat size (modal value) among at least five fully spanning reads. Partially spanning reads were excluded to ensure accuracy.

Long-read analysis confirmed that the reported patient carried a biallelic pathogenic-range expansion composed exclusively of the canonical AAGGG motif, with both alleles exceeding 800 repeats (Fig. 1), which is well above the established pathogenic threshold of 400 or more repeats. No alternative or rare motifs were detected. To further validate these findings and evaluate potential co-segregation, we performed identical sequencing and analysis on DNA from the patient’s son, who presented with a chronic cough, to investigate his affected status and the accuracy of the expansion call. This demonstrated a heterozygous state, consisting of one expanded AAGGG allele (>800 repeats) and one non-pathogenic allele containing the reference AAAAG motif (approximately 100 repeats), in line with expected inheritance from the affected parent.

These findings substantiate the genetic diagnosis of the first Korean CANVAS case and clarify the underlying repeat architecture. This highlights the limitations of relying solely on short-read analysis in the diagnosis of repeat expansion disorders, and emphasizes that long-read sequencing can provide definitive structural characterization, including accurate repeat sizing, motif composition, and zygosity for RFC1 expansions. Lee et al.7 reported another Korean case of CANVAS in which an RFC1 expansion was suspected via whole-genome sequencing and subsequently validated using repeat-primed polymerase chain reaction (PCR). While repeat-primed PCR serves as an effective tool for screening predefined motifs, its ability to provide precise repeat size estimates and to determine zygosity is often limited. Therefore, long-read sequencing is recommended when available for more accurate and comprehensive characterization.

Given the expanding phenotypic and motif spectrum of RFC1-related disease,2348 we insist that confirmatory methods, such as long-read sequencing, should be incorporated when RFC1 expansions are suspected or reported from short-read analysis or repeat-primed PCR. The use of Cas9-enrichment nanopore sequencing coupled with motif-aware visualization (e.g., STRiker) can offer a feasible and scalable framework for robust molecular confirmation in both clinical and research settings.
