Development and characterization of nucleosomal DNA-based reference materials for the epidermal growth factor receptor gene liquid biopsy
Authors: Sae Rom Hong, Jaehyeong Park, Sang-Soo Lee, Do-Yei Kim, Yong-Joon Cho, Young-Kyung Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiquid biopsy has emerged as a valuable tool in cancer research and diagnosis; however, its clinical utility relies heavily on the accurate detection of low variant allele frequencies (VAFs). Evaluating such sensitivity demands reference materials (RMs) that biologically resemble cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in addition to confirmed basic attributes. Here, we report the development and characterization of a pilot batch of nucleosomal DNA (nDNA)-based EGFR cfDNA RMs. These RMs harbor clinically relevant mutations (T790M, L858R, G719S, and exon 19 deletion (E746_A750del; COSM6223)) at four nominal VAF levels (0%, 0.2%, 1%, and 5%). Analytical validation using digital PCR (dPCR) confirmed assay specificity with limits of detection determined to be below 0.2% VAF. The pilot batch exhibited acceptable homogeneity across bottles and maintained stability under simulated transport conditions and after long-term storage for three years at −70 °C. Orthogonal validation via amplicon sequencing confirmed the reference values with high concordance to dPCR. These findings provide evidence that nDNA-based RMs can serve as a biologically representative and reliable standard for validating assays at low VAFs, expanding to large-scale RM production and interlaboratory evaluation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00216-026-06475-5.Keywords: EGFR, Liquid biopsy, Digital PCR, Reference material, Nucleosomal DNA

Introduction

Liquid biopsy has emerged as a valuable tool in precision medicine, enabling non-invasive detection and monitoring of cancer through analysis of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in plasma [1–3]. Precise quantification of variant allele frequency (VAF), which represents the proportion of ctDNA within total cfDNA, is fundamental for liquid biopsy applications. In particular, low VAF (< 1%) is essential for early cancer detection, minimal residual disease monitoring, and identification of emerging drug-resistant mutations, as VAF levels are correlated with tumor burden and treatment response [4–6]. Therefore, the clinical implementation of liquid biopsy can be expanded by resolving analytical challenges regarding the validation and standardization of detection methods, thereby requiring well-characterized reference materials (RMs) to address these issues [7–9].

The substantial advantages of liquid biopsy have driven the development of diverse analytical methodologies targeting ctDNA within cfDNA, which can be broadly categorized into targeted and comprehensive profiling strategies. Targeted approaches, including quantitative PCR (qPCR) [10], digital PCR (dPCR) [11–13], amplicon sequencing [14, 15], and hybrid-capture sequencing [16, 17], focus on well-studied pathogenic variants and are widely adopted in clinical laboratories due to their cost-effectiveness and analytical performance. Among those approaches, dPCR offers higher sensitivity compared to qPCR and sequencing methods through partitioning PCR reactions into 20,000 compartments, enabling the detection of low abundance targets [18]. On the other hand, comprehensive approaches utilizing whole-genome sequencing [19] and whole-exome sequencing [20] provide broader coverage, but require well-established bioinformatic pipelines and time. The use of diverse technologies underscores the urgent need for standardized RMs to ensure analytical accuracy and comparability across different methodologies.

Among various cancer biomarkers in genes, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations play a crucial role in non-small cell lung cancer management since EGFR variants serve as both predictive biomarkers for targeted therapy and prognostic indicators [6, 9]. For example, the EGFR T790M (COSM6240) variant causes resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) [21], unlike the L858R (COSM6224) variant and E746_A750del (COSM6223; Ex19del) which are common sensitizing mutations [11]. The EGFR G719S (COSM6252) variant is another oncogenic driver, accounting for 2–3% of EGFR mutations, and is targeted by FDA-approved companion diagnostics (CDx) for specific TKI therapies [22]. Thus, highly sensitive analytical methods and well-characterized RMs are essential to validate CDx assays and ensure reliable detection of EGFR variants [23–25].

RMs for liquid biopsy applications must fulfill several analytical characteristics including homogeneity and stability. Patient plasma samples may best represent ideal RM forms; however, these present inherent challenges such as limited availability, instability, and genetic variability. To address these limitations, various approaches such as shearing genomic DNA (gDNA) [26, 27], spiking in mutant DNA [21, 28], and including enzyme-treated DNA [27, 29] have been developed and validated with multiple methodologies [25]. However, these artificial RMs often cannot recapitulate the fragmentation patterns of cfDNA derived from apoptotic cells. This discrepancy highlights the necessity of developing improved RMs based on nucleosomal DNA (nDNA), which preserves the histone-protected structure and size distribution characteristic of cfDNA. Moreover, a recent study using micrococcal nuclease (MNase) to digest chromatin successfully demonstrated the feasibility of the nDNA-based RM approach [30].

In this study, we developed and characterized a pilot batch of multiplex EGFR cfDNA RMs targeting four major EGFR variants (T790M, L858R, G719S, and Ex19del) using nDNA derived from A549, NCI-H1975, SW48, and PC9 cancer cell lines. The pilot batch comprised four vials with distinct VAF levels nominally ranging from 0 to 5% to cover clinically relevant values. We validated our dPCR methods by confirming the specificity, limit of blank (LoB), limit of detection (LoD), and repeatability. Furthermore, the homogeneity and stability of the pilot batch were assessed. Both dPCR and amplicon sequencing demonstrated concordant VAF measurements. The successful development of this pilot batch could provide fundamental evidence for the potential of nDNA-based RMs to enhance the standardization of liquid biopsy testing, ultimately contributing to more accurate cancer diagnosis and monitoring.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and nDNA preparation

NCI-H1975 (T790M and L858R), SW48 (G719S), PC9 (Ex19del), and A549 (wild-type control) human cancer cell lines were used to prepare EGFR cfDNA RMs. All cell lines were obtained from the Korea Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells cultured in 90-mm cell dishes were harvested by removing the medium, treating with 0.25% trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and washing with PBS. Approximately 1 × 106 cultured cells were collected as pellets for DNA extraction.

nDNA was extracted from each cell line using the EZ Nucleosomal DNA Prep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, which incorporates MNase digestion. The nDNA was eluted with 30 μL of elution buffer included in the kit and stored at −20 °C until use. The concentration and size distribution of the extracted nDNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Each nDNA sample was diluted with Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) to obtain a concentration of 5 ng/μL. For comparison, gDNA samples were also extracted from each cell line using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

T790M and L858R assays were obtained from our previous study [31], while G719S and Ex19del assays were developed in this study. The dPCR assays, using a QX200 Droplet Digital PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), were performed in 20 μL reaction volumes containing 10 μL of Supermix for Probes (Bio-Rad), 3 μL of the assay mixture (10 pmol of forward primer, 10 pmol of reverse primer, 5 pmol of FAM-labeled probe, and 5 pmol of HEX-labeled probe per reaction), and 4–5 μL of template or no template control (NTC). Assay validation

Assay performance, including specificity, sensitivity, and repeatability, was validated. Specificity was assessed by analyzing both nDNA and gDNA from mutation-positive (NCI-H1975, SW48, and PC9) and wild-type (A549) cell lines. To determine the LoB and LoD, we analyzed over 85 wells of A549 nDNA (approximately 600–1500 copy/μL) for each assay. A VAF-based approach was adopted to account for the background noise from potential false-positive droplets. The LoB was estimated using a non-parametric approach (95th percentile method) as per the CLSI EP17-A2 guideline [32], as the blank signals did not follow a normal distribution. Due to limitations, the estimated LoD was calculated based on the mean and the standard deviation of A549 nDNA (blank sample), following the Eurachem guidelines (2025) [33]. Precision was evaluated in terms of repeatability and intermediate precision. Repeatability (within-run precision) was assessed by measuring 10 technical replicates of each variant in a single run. Intermediate precision (day-to-day) was determined by analyzing the variability across three independent runs performed on different days.

Production of reference materials

To prepare a pilot batch of EGFR multiplex cfDNA RMs, we utilized nDNA extracted from four human cancer cell lines (A549, NCI-H1975, SW48, and PC9). The copy number concentration of each EGFR variant in the extracted nDNA was quantified by dPCR. Based on these values, calculated amounts of mutant nDNA were mixed with wild-type nDNA to generate mixtures containing all four EGFR variants at targeted VAFs. Four nominal VAF levels were prepared for this pilot batch: level 1 (0%, wild-type only), level 2 (0.2%), level 3 (1%), and level 4 (5%). After mixing, each nDNA solution was filtered using a 0.22 μm pore-size MF-Millipore Membrane Filter (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to remove impurities. Each filtered nDNA mixture was aliquoted into 200 vials (approximately 100 μL each), thereby producing 200 complete pilot RM sets, each consisting of four vials corresponding to the VAF levels. All vials were stored at −70 °C until use.

Amplicon sequencing

To characterize the four EGFR variants (T790M, L858R, G719S, and Ex19del), amplicon sequencing libraries were prepared using a two-round PCR method [35]. In the first round, target-specific primers compatible with Illumina’s TruSeq DNA library preparation (Supplementary Table 1) were used to amplify EGFR RMs in 20 μL monoplex reactions containing 4 μL of EGFR RMs, 10 μL of 2X Platinum SuperFi II Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10 pmol of each primer. PCR was conducted on a SimpliAmp 96-well Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following conditions: 98 °C for 30 s; 30 cycles at 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 20 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The first-round PCR products were quantified with the Quantus ONE dsDNA system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and sized using the D1000 ScreenTape analysis on a 4150 TapeStation System (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Based on these measurements, 10 ng of PCR products per sample were pooled and diluted to 400 μL with nuclease-free water.

The second round of PCR was performed in 20 μL reactions containing 1 μL of the diluted 1 st PCR product, 10 μL of 2X Platinum SuperFi II Master Mix, and 4 μL of Unique Dual Index Kit (1–24) (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). A SimpliAmp 96-well Thermal Cycler was used for PCR under the following thermal conditions: 98 °C for 30 s; 14 cycles at 98 °C for 10 s, 61 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 20 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The quantity and size of the constructed library were measured using the Quantus ONE dsDNA system and D1000 ScreenTape analysis using a 4150 TapeStation, respectively. Then, libraries were pooled into a single vial to 10 ng/μL and purified with 1.4 × AMPure XP Reagent (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The purified libraries were quantified using the NEBNext Library Quant Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The generated libraries were sequenced using a NovaSeqX (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 150 bp paired-end sequencing reagent (Illumina). Raw reads processed by Cutadapt [36] and PEAR [37] were analyzed using STRait Razor v3 [38] with a custom configuration file (Supplementary Table 2) filtering out less than 400. Using a mapping-free and anchor-based approach, unique alleles were retrieved and quantified directly from exact matches to flanking sequences. VAF were calculated based on the read depths of mutant and wild-type alleles.

Results

Production of RMs

The overall workflow for developing the EGFR multiplex cfDNA RMs is illustrated in Fig. 1. The nDNA was extracted from four human cell lines: A549, NCI-H1975, SW48, and PC9. The extracted nDNA exhibited a fragment size distribution of approximately 150–170 bp (Supplementary Figs. 1–2A) with insubstantial contamination of gDNA (Supplementary Fig. 2B), consistent with the typical size distribution of cfDNA in plasma. The copy number concentration of each nDNA extract was quantified by dPCR to calculate the proportion of each extract required to produce four mixtures representing the targeted nominal VAF levels (0%, 0.2%, 1%, and 5%).

Assay specificity

We first validated the specificity of the in-house dPCR assays using four nDNA extracts: A549 (wild-type), NCI-H1975 (T790M and L858R), SW48 (G719S), and PC9 (Ex19del). Each assay exclusively detected its target variant in the corresponding cell line nDNA, with no cross-reactivity (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the wild-type control (A549 nDNA) presented no variant signals across all four assays. These data confirm that our dPCR methods are highly specific for the target variants.

To verify the absence of bias in VAF between gDNA and nDNA, we compared VAF measurements obtained from paired gDNA and nDNA samples extracted from the same cell lines (Fig. 2B). No statistically significant differences in VAF were observed between the two DNA types for any of the tested variants (p > 0.05, Mann-Whitney U test). This concordance demonstrates that our dPCR assays are robust to variations in DNA fragment size, supporting the applicability of nDNA as a reliable material for measuring VAF.

Analytical performance: LoB, LoD, and precision

The analytical performance of the dPCR assays was characterized in terms of LoB, LoD (Supplementary Table 3), and precision (Supplementary Table 4). The LoB was determined by analyzing over 85 reactions of wild-type A549 nDNA. The frequency distributions of both VAF (Fig. 3A) and mutant copy numbers (Fig. 3B) exhibited a non-normal distribution; therefore, the LoBs with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the non-parametric percentile method. T790M and G719S exhibited broader tail distributions compared to L858R and Ex19del, reflecting the presence of false-positive droplets in these two assays. This is consistent with the determined LoB for VAF values: 0.10% for T790M and 0.11% for G719S, versus 0.04% for both L858R and Ex19del (Supplementary Table 3).

Percentage distribution of wild-type nucleosomal DNA (nDNA). A Variant allele frequency (VAF) and B mutant copy number per reaction obtained from over 85 reactions of wild-type A549 nDNA. The histograms depict the highly skewed, non-normal distribution of each EGFR variant. Notably, T790M and G719S exhibit broader tail distributions indicating higher limit of blanks, whereas L858R and Ex19del show distributions sharply near zero

The LoD was calculated based on the mean and SD of A549 nDNA (blank) samples following Eurachem guide [33]. The estimated LoDs for VAF values of four variants remained below 0.15% (Supplementary Table 3), suggesting their potential for liquid biopsy applications. Precision was evaluated by calculating the relative standard deviation (RSD) for repeatability (within-run) and intermediate precision (day-to-day) (Supplementary Table 4). At higher VAF levels (levels 3 and 4), the RSDs for all variants ranged from 2.5% to 12.0%, indicating high reproducibility. At level 2 (nominal 0.2%), the RSDs were observed to be higher, ranging from 10.2% to 20.5%. However, the mean mutant copy number concentrations at level 2 were approximately 1.9 to 2.6 copy/μL. These increased RSDs are probably attributable to the quantification of low-abundance mutant copy numbers, rather than material heterogeneity.

Stability test

The stability of the EGFR cfDNA RM pilot batch was evaluated under both short- and long-term storage conditions (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 4–5). For the short-term stability test, three randomly selected sets were stored at 4 °C and −20 °C for 7 days. dPCR analysis showed no significant differences in VAFs compared with the assigned reference values, indicating that the pilot batch remained stable under simulated shipment conditions. Long-term stability was assessed by storing the RMs at −70 °C for 7 months and 3 years. After 3 years of storage, the measured VAFs showed no significant changes relative to the assigned reference values. The copy number concentrations of both mutant and wild-type alleles were stable as well. To address potential handling variability, freeze-thaw stability was assessed using a pooled Level 3 vial for up to 5 cycles between −70 °C and 4 °C. We confirmed that up to five repeats of freeze-thaw did not affect both VAF (Fig. 5) and wild-type copy number concentrations. These results demonstrate that the pilot batch of EGFR cfDNA RMs maintains stability under transport, long-term storage, and freeze-thaw conditions supporting its feasibility as an RM.

Uncertainty evaluation

To evaluate uncertainties for our reference values, uncertainties from characterization (uchar), heterogeneity (uhom; the same as sbb), and manual thresholding (uTypeB) were combined. For this pilot batch, uncertainties from stability tests were excluded according to the stability test results. A coverage factor k ensuring a 95% confidence level was determined to calculate the expanded uncertainty (URM).

Comparison to amplicon sequencing results

To provide orthogonal validation of the pilot batch, amplicon sequencing was performed in triplicate for all four variants, and the VAFs for T790M, L858R, G719S, and Ex19del were quantified (Supplementary Table 5). Each variant achieved high average read depth: 593,840 × for T790M; 525,300 × for L858R; 468,312 × for G719S; and 369,457 × for Ex19del. These high read depths enabled reliable VAF estimation even at level 2, where the mean VAF was determined to be 0.24% by dPCR. This confirms that the developed RM pilot batch can be characterized not only by dPCR but also by sequencing-based methods at clinically relevant low VAF levels. Notably, the VAFs obtained from amplicon sequencing showed strong correlations with those measured by dPCR (overall R2 = 0.9945; each variant R2 ≥ 0.9982) (Fig. 6). This strong overall correlation between the two methodologies supports the orthogonal validation of the pilot batch.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and characterized a pilot batch of KRISS Reference Material EGFR Multiplex cfDNA derived from nDNA samples of four human cancer cell lines. The reference values are expressed in VAF for each EGFR variant at the clinically relevant levels (up to 5%). To mimic cfDNA derived from clinical samples, we used an nDNA-based approach for the development of the pilot batch, rather than using sheared gDNA or plasmid-derived amplicons. The existing RMs based on sheared gDNA or plasmid-derived amplicons provide accurate VAF values, and the nDNA-based approach also demonstrates the same VAF levels to gDNA (Fig. 2). However, sheared gDNA shows a broader range of fragments compared to cfDNA specimens [26] and amplicons may lack biological traits, including epigenetic patterns. This nDNA-based approach aligns with recent findings using MNase digestion to develop RMs exhibiting a distinct mononucleosomal DNA size [30]. Our pilot batch successfully mimics clinical cfDNA in the size patterns of mononucleosomal DNA size (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Additionally, our materials are applicable to methylation patterns analyses.

Analytical validation demonstrated that the dPCR assays were specific, reliable, and highly sensitive (Fig. 2). All assays achieved LoD values below 0.15% VAF, highlighting their ability to detect variants at low VAFs. Notably, differences in LoDs were observed between EGFR variants; the L858R and Ex19del assays showed significantly lower LoDs than the T790M and G719S assays (Fig. 3). These trends were consistent with previous studies using dPCR methods [8, 22, 39]. Specifically, a LoB of 0.18% for the T790M assay was reported under similar low DNA input conditions (~ 20 ng) [39], indicating that our assay could exhibit similar performance in clinical settings. The slightly higher LoDs for T790M and G719S may stem from the sequence composition (high GC contents and transition mutations) of the regions targeted by these assays [8]. Contamination of the A549 extract is unlikely, as high-depth amplicon sequencing confirmed that it only contained wild-type sequences for the four EGFR variants.

The average copy number per partition, specified as λ, is a critical parameter influencing dPCR precision. In this study, the λ values for wild-type alleles were between 0.12 and 0.17, while those for mutant alleles were significantly lower (Supplementary Table 4), reflecting the low VAF levels. Although higher λ values theoretically improve precision [18, 40], we intentionally targeted lower λ values in this pilot batch. This design closely mimics low-input scenarios in clinical liquid biopsy samples, where the total yield of extractable cfDNA is often limited. For example, the reference gene copy number concentration of lung cancer patients’ cfDNA plasma is 3646 copy/mL [41]. Based on the scenario, extracting cfDNA from 5 mL of plasma with the minimum elution volume of 20 μL, 910 copy/μL elute can be obtained assuming no loss. This assumed example shows a similar total copy number concentration to our pilot batch, highlighting the relevance of our pilot batch to the clinical samples. Thus, this pilot batch can provide realistic analytical conditions. However, recognizing that higher measurement precision is desirable for rigorous quality control, future large-scale production batches can be designed to have higher total copy number concentrations.

Orthogonal validation using amplicon sequencing provided additional support for the validity of the pilot batch. The sequencing results showed strong concordance with dPCR (overall R2 > 0.99), confirming the accuracy of the assigned reference values (Fig. 6). Although the RSDs observed in amplicon sequencing were higher than those in dPCR, this increased variability is likely attributable to the smaller sample size and variability from PCR bias and cumulative polymerase errors during two-step PCR library preparation. This strong cross-platform concordance supports their utility as reported in previous comparison studies [42].

Beyond the targeted EGFR variants, the use of well-characterized cell lines (A549, NCI-H1975, SW48, and PC9) potentially offers broader variant coverage. Public databases such as DepMap and COSMIC indicate that these cell lines harbor other pathogenic variants, including KRAS G12S and TP53 R273H. Consequently, this pilot batch could serve as a broader mutation measurement standard. Furthermore, since these materials retain DNA methylation patterns unlike PCR amplicons, the pilot batch could expand its applications to epigenetic marker measurement.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed and characterized a pilot batch of KRISS Reference Material EGFR Multiplex cfDNA containing EGFR T790M, L858R, G719S, and Ex19del variants in an nDNA format. This pilot RM batch better mimics liquid biopsy samples compared to conventional controls. Comprehensive validation confirmed the RM’s homogeneity, stability, and cross-methodology consistency. We also performed comprehensive method validation using dPCR to LoB, LoD, and precision. These results provide sufficient evidence that nDNA-based RMs can serve as reliable measurement standards for improving the accuracy and reproducibility of variant detection.
