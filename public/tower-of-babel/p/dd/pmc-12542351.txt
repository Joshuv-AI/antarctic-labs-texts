A case of pregnancy complicated by caroli disease
Authors: Weiwei Wang, Xiaoteng Qiang, Xiufen Cai, Qing Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports a rare case of Caroli disease (CD) combined with pregnancy, and analyses and discusses the etiology, differential diagnosis, clinical treatment of CD, and its special characteristics during pregnancy; this study suggests that medical and surgical comorbidities occurring during pregnancy should be given due attention, and the possibility of congenital intrahepatic bile duct structural abnormality should be identified, with a view to providing more insights into the diagnosis of CD and improving the level of obstetricians and gynaecologists’ diagnosis and treatment of this rare disease, in order to timely diagnose and aggressively treat it, thereby improving maternal and infant outcomes. This study suggests that the possibility of congenital intrahepatic bile duct structural abnormalities should be identified in order to provide more insights into the diagnosis of CD and to improve the diagnosis of this rare disease by obstetricians and gynaecologists, so that timely diagnosis and proactive treatment can be made to improve the outcome of mother and child.Keywords: Pregnancy, Caroli disease, Diagnosis, Fever, Pathogenesis, Polycysti Kidney and Hepatic Disease 1 (PKHD1)

Background

Caroli disease (CD) [1], an autosomal recessive disorder, is also known as congenital cystic dilatation of intrahepatic bile ducts and refers to the rare congenital developmental abnormality of the biliary tract. CD can present sporadically or as Caroli syndrome caused by the mutation of polycysti kidney and hepatic disease 1 (PKHD1) gene encoding fibrocystic protein on chromosome 6p12. It may be associated clinically by congenital hepatic fibrosis and or polycystic kidney disease [2].The main feature of the disease is congenital cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts, and clinically there are mostly no symptoms, when the congenital bile ducts structural abnormalities lead to cholestasis and recurrent cholangitis may appear fever, abdominal pain and jaundice and other symptoms. Early diagnosis and intervention of CD can increase patient survival and improve neonatal prognosis to some extent. CD is exceptionally rare, with an incidence rate of only 1/1,000,000 [3], and has been exceedingly rarely reported in pregnant women. This article reports a case of pregnancy complicated by CD, with the aim of elevating the level of diagnosis and treatment of this disease by clinicians and providing early interventions, thus improving maternal and infant outcomes.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old gravida 4 (para 3) woman was admitted to the hospital with a complaint of epigastric pain for 3 days at 35 weeks + 5 days of gestation. The patient did not undergo regular prenatal examinations. She developed chills, fever, abdominal distension, nausea, icteric skin, and persistent epigastric pain without identifiable causes 3 days prior to admission, leading to her admission due to the aggravation of these symptoms. The patient had no prior history of chronic hemolytic anemia or symptoms associated with cholestasis (e.g. pruritus, epigastric pain, or jaundice). She had a history of hepatitis B for more than 20 years without any standardized treatment. During the physical examination, the following results were noted: body temperature at 36.2 °C, pulse at 100 beats per minute, respiration at 20 breaths per minute, and blood pressure at 127/76 mmHg. Her physical examination revealed icteric skin and sclera, abdominal distension, right upper abdominal tenderness, marked percussion pain in the hepatic region, a positive Murphy sign (+), no rebound tenderness, negative mobile turbidities, and no evidence of liver palms or spider angioma. Laboratory results indicated the following: stool was yellowish-brown; no abnormalities were observed. Virological findings showed positivity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), and hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb). The patient tested negative for tertiary peritonitis (TP) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Antibodies related to autoimmune liver disease were negative (anti-SP100-, anti-GP210-, anti-ANA2-). Thyroid function was normal, and additional details can be found in Table 1. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) indicated diffuse dilatation of intrahepatic bile ducts, based on which CD was considered; severe stenosis was observed in the portal area and the proximal portion of the common hepatic duct, accompanied by obstructive dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts, cholangitis, and multiple stones in the intrahepatic bile ducts (Fig. 1).

Upon admission, the patient underwent a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment. Due to critical condition, multiple complications and severe infection, the patient underwent an emergency caesarean section to terminate the pregnancy in the presence of a paediatrician. The surgery was successful and the patient delivered a healthy newborn, the neoborn received hyper immune globulin in one arm and the vaccine in another arm once delivered. After the caesarean section, the patient was transferred to the Department of Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery and underwent ultrasound-guided percutaneous transhepatic cholangiopancreatography and drainage (PTCD) on the same day for palliation, followed by symptomatic treatment such as anti-infections, fluids and antiviral therapy. After treatment, the patient’s condition improved. Considering that the patient was a woman of advanced maternal age and had just had a cesarean section, the risk of surgery at this time was extremely high. After comprehensive consideration, it was recommended that the patient receive surgical treatment in the hospital two months later. Two months after delivery, the patient underwent left hemihepatectomy in our hospital, and the autopsy showed cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts with a large number of stones (Fig. 2). The patient’s pathology was described as: bile duct dilatation with intact epithelium, periportal fibrous tissue hyperplasia, and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration; bile duct hyperplasia in the collecting area, fibrous tissue hyperplasia, and lymphocyte infiltration (Figs. 3 and 4). Postoperative patients were standardized on hepatitis B virus treatment and underwent long-term, comprehensive follow-up, and the skin jaundice completely subsided 10 months after the operation, and there is no other discomfort so far.

Discussion

CD, first described in 1958 by the French gastroenterologist Jacques Caroli [4], is an autosomal recessive disorder with an incidence rate of 1 per 1,000,000. CD is more common in children and adolescents. Reportedly, approximately 7% of CD patients can eventually develop malignant tumors, and the risk is as high as 100-fold higher in CD patients than in the normal hepatobiliary population [5].

CD is currently believed [6] that CD is inherited in an autosomal recessive fashion and is characterized by extensive phenotypic variability. For CD, PKHD1 is the only pathogenic gene currently known, which is located on human chromosome 6 and has a high degree of interfamilial and intrafamilial phenotypic variability. This gene encodes fibrocystin/polyductin (FPC) protein (a single transmembrane protein) which is abundantly expressed in the epithelial cells of renal collecting ducts and bile ducts and regulates the differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis of epithelial cells. Mutations in hepatic PKHD1 cause FPC deficiency, which results in defects in primitive cilia of bile duct epithelial cells and then induces DPM, thereby compromising the normal development of the bile ducts and leading to cystic lesions of the biliary system. Accordingly, mutations in PKHD1 account for the development of CD [7–9].

According to histopathology, CD is classified into two types: type I: simple CD involving multiple cystic dilatations of the intrahepatic bile ducts, which does not affect the portal venous system but causes a high incidence of bile duct stones and cholangitis and often manifests as fever, jaundice, and abdominal pain; type II: mixed CD involving perivenous fibrosis, which is often accompanied by cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts, splenomegaly, portal hypertension, and upper gastrointestinal bleeding [10].According to the extent of the lesion, it is subdivided into limited and diffuse. Based on the patient’s MRI and intraoperative situation, the symptoms of this patient are consistent with the manifestations and features of type I diffuse CD.

Due to the lack of specific blood biochemical markers, CD is primarily diagnosed and staged through imaging and pathology [4]. Ultrasound, CT, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography can increase the sensitivity of diagnosis [6]. In particular, cholangiopancreatography technology has been widely used in the clinic and is of great value for the diagnosis of CD and the selection of surgical procedures because of its development in recent years and its advantages of non-invasiveness and high specificity. For our patient, preoperative CT and MRI were suggestive of cystic dilatation of intrahepatic bile ducts. Based on a comprehensive analysis of the patient’s imaging and pathology, the case was not associated with congenital hepatic fibrosis or polycystic kidney disease. Imaging and pathological findings support the diagnosis of type I CD.

Our patient was a middle-aged woman who was first diagnosed with CD during pregnancy, combined with HBV infections, and presented with epigastric pain, jaundice, and fever. Since these symptoms are not specific, they are often mistaken to be caused by hepatitis or pregnancy-related diseases. This patient suffered from CD and congenital structural abnormalities in bile ducts, as well as hepatitis B virus infections complicated with gallstones. Meanwhile, the patient was in the third trimester, which increased the levels of estrogen and progesterone and led to the mechanical compression of the enlarged uterus on the liver and bile ducts. These factors jointly contributed to increased bile secretion and impaired bile excretion, thereby inducing cholestasis. Accordingly, the differential diagnosis of CD is difficult, particularly from diseases such as intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) and hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome. ICP is a liver complication specific to the middle and late stages of pregnancy, which is characterized by jaundice, skin itching, elevated bile acids and BIL, and liver function abnormalities [11]. HELLP syndrome mainly manifests as epigastric pain, accompanied by symptoms including nausea and vomiting, and shows hemolysis, increased liver enzymes, and decreased platelets in the routine blood test, which is often insidious and progresses rapidly, endangering the life of the mother and child [12]. These diseases can be excluded on the basis of the symptoms and auxiliary examinations of the patient.In addition, the diagnosis of CD necessitates the exclusion of other hepatobiliary diseases, particularly primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC). PSC is a chronic cholestatic liver disease attributed to bile duct stenosis induced by chronic inflammation and progressive fibrosis of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts. The clinical symptoms of PSC are atypical, including jaundice, itching, and epigastric pain, which are very similar to the symptoms of our patient [13, 14]. Consequently, the two diseases are easily confused. Importantly, there are some differences in the imaging and histopathological manifestations of CD and PSC. Regarding imaging manifestations, CD is characterized by the diffuse cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts which mostly presents clustered distribution along the hepatic bile ducts in the periphery; PSC is featured by irregular and segmental stenosis and dilatation of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, among which dilatation is milder than that of CD and is not cystic [15, 16]. In terms of hepatic histopathology, CD possesses pathological characteristics of limited tissue fibrosis surrounding dilated bile ducts and relatively intact biliary epithelial cells; the characteristic histological change of PSC is “onion-skin” and diffuse fibrosis surrounding the bile ducts with atrophy or complete loss of biliary epithelial cells. Hence, the combination of imaging, intraoperative anatomical exploration, and postoperative pathology supported the diagnosis of CD in this case [17, 18]. Of note, the combination of imaging, intraoperative anatomical exploration, and postoperative pathology supported the diagnosis of CD in this case.

In recent years, considerable progress has been achieved in the treatment of CD. The current treatment of CD includes medication and surgery. Specifically, medication mainly consists of symptomatic treatment, such as oral ursodeoxycholic acid and antibiotics, which can quickly relieve jaundice and treat cholangitis [19]. Our patient was not treated with ursodeoxycholic acid, which is mainly used to promote the flow of bile and alleviate the symptoms of cholestasis, since he had biliary obstruction, with disturbed bile flow. Specifically, biliary obstruction would be aggravated due to increased bile excretion, if ursodeoxycholic acid was used [20, 21]. Currently, surgery is often recommended for patients with ineffective conservative treatment or severe symptoms: resection of the corresponding hepatic segment or lobe for type I CD [22]and liver transplantation for type II CD [23]. Our patient was diagnosed with Type I diffuse CD and was at 35 + 5 weeks of gestation. Considering the condition of the patient and the intrauterine condition of the fetus, the cesarean section was first performed to terminate the pregnancy, followed by PTCD to resolve the obstruction. As the patient was in the puerperium, of advanced maternal age, and immunocompromised, she underwent left hemihepatectomy in our hospital after 2 months. The patient recovered well after the surgery, with greatly improved quality of life.

Several clinical and in vitro studies have revealed a strong association between hepatitis B virus infections and the development of gallstones. In an animal study, hepatitis B virus infections were observed to increase the risk of gallstones [24]. A retrospective study exhibited a significantly higher risk of developing gallstones in patients with hepatitis B virus infections than in normal individuals [25]. Additionally, accumulating studies [26, 27] have unveiled that hepatitis B virus infections also lead to dysregulation of the immune system. Hepatitis B is an immune-mediated disease, in which the immune system is activated in the body when infections occur and persistent infections provoke immune dysregulation manifested by immune cell function abnormalities, immune tolerance, and immune escape. There is no clear evidence that hepatitis B virus infections elevate the risk of developing PSC. Therefore, standardized treatment is important for patients with hepatitis B virus infections.

CD is inherited in autosomal recessive way, the patient was asked in detail about his family history and denied any other family members developing CD. The auxiliary tests of our case were inadequate, that is, the patient failed to promptly undergo genetic tests due to his poor economic conditions and severe condition, coupled with limited local medical and technical conditions, which resulted in the need for genetic testing to be sent out with a long duration. Additionally, since CD is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, it is also essential to screen family members for early diagnosis and management. Consequently, it is necessary to perform genetic testing for patients and family members we encounter in our future work.

Conclusion

This article reports a rare case of pregnancy complicated by CD. In this case, CD occurred late in pregnancy in a pregnant woman with advanced maternal age. On imaging, the patient was highly suspected of having CD. This case was adequately differentiated from similar diseases in the light of previous clinical experience and was managed promptly with favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. Imaging is important for the diagnosis of CD and the selection of surgical modalities, and individualized treatment is the key to maximizing the benefit of patients with pregnancy complicated by CD. Obstetricians should comprehensively assess the condition of patients and the intrauterine condition of their fetuses and terminate pregnancy at the appropriate time. In addition, it is necessary to increase the ability of obstetricians to recognize, diagnose, and distinguish rare diseases, so as to detect the disease in time, formulate a safe and effective treatment plan, and provide clinicians with some useful experiences and suggestions to broaden their clinical thinking.
