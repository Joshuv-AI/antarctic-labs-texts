PFAS exposure induces hearing loss by targeting cochlear hair cells and spiral ganglion neurons
Authors: Pankaj Bhatia, Katherine Roth, Zhao Yang, Michael Petriello, Samson Jamesdaniel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are persistent environmental pollutants linked to adverse health effects. Recent epidemiological data suggest an association between PFAS exposure and hearing loss, but underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study examined PFAS-induced auditory dysfunction in mice exposed to a mixture of five PFAS compounds (2 mg/L each) in drinking water for seven weeks. Ldlr−/− mice were used due to their susceptibility to metabolic dysfunction, a risk factor for hearing loss. Auditory brainstem responses (ABR) indicated that PFAS exposure significantly elevated hearing thresholds by 18–33 dB across multiple frequencies. Distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs) revealed impaired outer hair cell (OHC) function, and immunohistochemical analysis indicated a 20% OHC loss in the basal turn of the cochlea. In addition, PFAS exposure reduced ABR wave-1 amplitudes, and caused a 50% reduction in spiral ganglion cell density, indicating impaired auditory nerve function. Overall, this study provides the first evidence for PFAS-induced high-frequency hearing loss in mice. The findings further indicated that cochlear OHCs and spiral ganglion neurons are potential targets in PFAS-induced hearing loss. Together, these data suggest that PFAS exposure elicits a multifaceted ototoxic response, affecting both sensory and neural elements of the cochlea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51693-7.Keywords: PFAS, hearing loss, outer hair cell loss, spiral ganglion neuronsSubject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Environmental exposure to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) poses serious risks to human health, particularly for neonates, children, and older adults1–4. PFAS comprise a vast category of man-made chemicals, totaling thousands, that have been extensively used in industrial and consumer products since the 1950s, including nonstick cookware, waterproof textiles, food packaging materials, and firefighting foams5–9. Among these, perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), and perfluorohexane sulfonic acid (PFHxS) are well-known long-chain PFAS that strongly bind to proteins, persist in the body, and exhibit high bioaccumulation and toxicity10–12. In contrast, ammonium perfluoro(2-methyl-3-oxahexanoate) (GenX) is a short-chain PFAS that is more water-soluble and less bioaccumulative but is environmentally persistent13,14. Due to their resistance to degradation, PFAS accumulate in the environment and the human body15–17. PFAS are found in almost 4% of water supplies in the United States, impacting over 110 million people. Additionally, about 98% of American adults have detectable blood levels of PFAS2,18.

Exposure to PFAS is linked to a broad range of adverse health effects, including increased risks of various cancers, such as kidney, testicular, and prostate cancers19–23, cardiometabolic disorders, including hypercholesterolemia and type 2 diabetes24,25, developmental impacts such as low birth weight, altered puberty timing, and behavioral changes1,26. PFAS exposure also impairs immune function, notably reducing vaccine response27,28. Hormonal disruption29, reproductive toxicity30, hepatic injury31, thyroid dysfunction32–34, and neurotoxicity35,36 have also been documented, with neurotoxic effects involving dopaminergic37 and glutamatergic pathways3,4,36. The nervous system is highly susceptible to PFAS toxicity38. Recent research shows a link between PFAS and neurodegenerative diseases, including Parkinson’s disease37 and Alzheimer’s disease39,40. PFAS have been found to cross the blood-brain barrier and accumulate in brain tissue, potentially contributing to the development and progression of these disorders36,41. PFAS exposure has been shown to disrupt synaptic neurotransmission36, potentially through mechanisms involving dopamine-glutamate imbalance42,43 and calcium dysregulation44–47. Further, PFAS induces oxidative stress via excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation48 and depletion of antioxidant defenses49, while also promoting neuroinflammation through microglial activation and disruption of the blood-brain barrier50, which increases the risk of neurodegeneration.

In addition to the well-known toxic effects of PFAS on multiple organ systems, recent studies have indicated a possible association between PFAS exposure and hearing loss51,52. Analysis of data from 5,560 American adults revealed that 8.3% of the participants with moderate hearing trouble had higher urinary levels of hydrocarbons and polyfluorinated compounds51. Another study indicated that adults with very high serum concentrations (top 10%) of PFNA or PFDA exhibited approximately 70–75% higher odds of high-frequency hearing loss53. Similarly, elevated serum PFOS levels were associated with a 9.75% increase in thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), indicative of thyroid disruption54. Prenatal exposure to PFAS, especially PFOA and PFOS, has been linked to altered neonatal hormone levels55. Evidence from in vitro, animal, and human studies demonstrates that PFAS can interfere with thyroid function, with increased risks of hypothyroidism observed in pregnant women and children34. Importantly, congenital hypothyroidism, characterized by deficient thyroid hormone production, is a significant risk factor for both central and peripheral hearing disorders56 and has been strongly associated with auditory impairments57. Disruption of thyroid hormone signaling, such as reduced sensitivity of thyroid hormone receptor α (TRα1), can lead to early-onset high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, outer hair cell (OHC) loss, and accelerated age-related hearing decline58. Since PFAS are known endocrine disruptors that interfere with thyroid hormone homeostasis, exposure during critical developmental windows may contribute to OHC damage and hearing loss34. Several studies also indicate that PFAS can modulate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), nuclear hormone receptors involved in regulating cellular proliferation, differentiation, and metabolism59–62. As PPARs such as PPARα and PPARγ are expressed in OHCs and inner hair cells (IHCs) in the cochlea59, such modulation could plausibly influence auditory function. Despite this supportive evidence, the PFAS-induced auditory dysfunction is yet to be fully characterized. The present study elucidates the effect of PFAS exposure on cochlear structure and function in low-density lipoprotein receptor knockout (Ldlr−/−) mice, an established model of hyperlipidemia and metabolic syndrome, to assess PFAS-induced ototoxicity under metabolically stressed conditions (Fig. 1).

Hearing loss was assessed by measuring the shift in ABR thresholds, and OHC activity was evaluated by measuring DPOAE amplitudes. Results

PFAS exposure did not alter body weight

Ldlr−/− mice on a C57BL/6J genetic background were exposed to a mixture of PFAS following the protocol used in a previous study63. Accordingly, mice consumed drinking water containing five PFAS compounds (PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS and GenX) at 2 mg/L each for seven weeks. This exposure regimen produces biologically relevant plasma PFAS levels in exposed animals. In previous studies, LC-MS/MS analysis indicated that females exhibited plasma concentrations of 20.1, 21.6, 23.5, 31.2, and 1.5 µg/mL, and males showed 9.7, 12.9, 14.3, 23, and 1.7 µg/mL for PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS, and GenX, respectively, after this exposure63. Measurement of body weight in the current study indicated that there were no significant differences between PFAS-exposed and control mice throughout the exposure period (Fig. 2A), and there were no signs of dehydration in PFAS-exposed mice.

PFAS exposure induces a shift in hearing thresholds

Auditory brainstem response (ABR) was recorded to evaluate the effect of PFAS exposure on hearing. ABR thresholds, a measure of auditory sensitivity, showed significant elevations in PFAS-exposed mice (18–33 dB shift), with 4 kHz (p = 0.007, n = 6), 8 kHz (p = 0.03, n = 6), 16 kHz (p = 0.001, n = 6), 24 kHz (p = 0.000008, n = 6), 32 kHz (p = 0.0005, n = 6), and click stimuli (p = 0.001), compared to controls (Fig. 2B). These threshold shifts were observed across multiple frequency regions, indicating that PFAS exposure causes substantial hearing loss in mice. As animals from both sexes were included together in the study, the sex specific variabilities, if any, could not be discerned from this data.

PFAS exposure induces outer hair cell loss

Distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs), a sensitive indicator of OHC function, showed a significant decrease in DPOAE amplitudes with 16 kHz (p = 0.01, n = 4), 24 kHz (p = 0.006, n = 4), and 32 kHz (p = 0.02, n = 4) stimuli in PFAS-exposed animals (Fig. 2C). These deficits were frequency-specific and were more pronounced in the high-frequency cochlear regions. The full input-output function for 8, 16, and 24 kHz is provided as supplementary material (Supplementary Figure S1). Immunolabeling of cochlear surface preparations from three different regions (Fig. 3A) using anti-myosin VIIa, along with DAPI nuclear staining, revealed a loss of OHCs localized to the basal turn of the cochlea (Fig. 3B). Though some DAPI labeling resembles the cuticular plate, suggesting potential cross-talk between the channels, it is not clear whether the variable size of nuclei in the basal region is due to the effects of PFAS on the hair cells. Furthermore, quantification of hair cells confirmed a 20% loss of OHCs (p = 0.02, n = 6) in this region, while IHCs remained largely unaffected (Fig. 3C–D).

PFAS exposure alters ABR wave-1 amplitudes

To assess functional consequences, ABR wave-1 amplitudes were analyzed as a measure of auditory nerve fiber synchrony. PFAS-exposed mice showed a significant reduction with 24 kHz (p = 0.004, n = 6–7), 32 kHz (p = 0.03, n = 6–7), and click stimuli (p = 0.004, n = 6–7), indicating altered auditory nerve function (Fig. 4A). Although the greatest reductions occurred at high frequencies, a downtrend in the amplitude was observed across multiple tested frequencies (Fig. 4A). Representative ABR waveforms recorded at 16 kHz and 90 dB SPL further show that control mice consistently exhibited clear, well-defined wave-1 peaks, whereas PFAS-exposed mice had smaller, less synchronized waveforms (Fig. 4B).

PFAS exposure induces loss of spiral ganglion neurons

The neurodegenerative effects of PFAS exposure on the cochlea were assessed by immunolabeling spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) using β3-tubulin, a neuron-specific cytoskeletal protein, enabling clear visualization and quantification of SGNs by confocal microscopy. Mice exposed to PFAS exhibited a loss of SGNs in the basal region of the cochlea, compared to control animals (Fig. 5A). Quantitative assessment showed a 50% decrease in spiral ganglion density (p = 0.00003, n = 6), suggesting that PFAS-induced neurotoxicity likely contributes to the observed hearing loss (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

This study provides the first experimental evidence that chronic exposure to a mixture of environmentally relevant PFAS induces both structural and functional damage in the auditory system. Mice exposed to PFAS-contaminated drinking water exhibited significantly elevated ABR thresholds across a wide frequency range (4–32 kHz), indicating sensorineural hearing loss. Functional deficits in OHCs, evidenced by reduced DPOAEs, were accompanied by region-specific OHC loss in the basal turn of the cochlea. In addition, immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated a marked decline in spiral ganglion density. These morphological alterations were accompanied by significant reductions in ABR wave-1 amplitudes. Our findings identify OHCs, and SGNs as key cochlear targets in PFAS-induced auditory dysfunction. Both male and female mice were included, and data were analyzed collectively to reduce potential sex-related bias. Epidemiological studies, including data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), have reported positive correlations between serum levels of PFAS compounds such as PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, and PFHxS and hearing loss52,53. However, most previous studies have examined the effects of individual PFAS compounds52,53, with limited investigation into PFAS mixtures, which more accurately reflect real-world exposure. The present study evaluates the ototoxic potential of a PFAS mixture in a mouse model, revealing that combined exposure can significantly impact cochlear structure and function. As prior studies have investigated the systemic effects of PFAS mixtures, including PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS, and GenX, in Ldlr−/− mice63, we used the same exposure paradigm to assess auditory outcomes in this study. The mixture of these long-chain PFAS (PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS)64 and emerging short-chain PFAS (GenX)65 was selected as they represent both legacy and emerging PFAS that are commonly detected in contaminated water and ecosystems2,53,66,67.

After seven weeks of PFAS exposure, the average circulating concentrations ranged from 12 to 31 µg/mL in exposed Ldlr−/− mice63. These concentrations are markedly higher than those observed in the general human population (typically a few ng/mL) and even in highly exposed human cohorts, where serum PFAS levels usually range from ng/mL to low µg/mL68–73. Such human cohorts include industrial workers, residents living near PFAS-contaminated areas (e.g., sites impacted by aqueous film-forming foam [AFFF] spills), and communities with contaminated drinking water74,75. Furthermore, the exposure paradigm used in this study resulted in PFAS accumulation in circulation, indicative of a substantial body burden63, thereby providing a relevant model for high-exposure human scenarios. Importantly, structural and functional auditory deficits observed in PFAS-exposed mice strongly align with previous epidemiological reports, reinforcing the hypothesis that PFAS compounds may impair auditory function52,53. Although water intake was not directly measured in the present study, a previous investigation by Roth et al. (2024) reported significantly increased water consumption in PFAS-exposed mice63. Consistent with this, we observed no signs of dehydration and no significant differences in body weight between experimental groups.

Ldlr−/− mice were used in this study because they represent a well-established model of hyperlipidemia and associated metabolic disturbances76–79 that are common conditions in human populations. These metabolic abnormalities are known risk factors for cochlear dysfunction and hearing loss, primarily through their effects on cochlear blood flow, inflammation, and oxidative stress80. Because PFAS exposure is also associated with cardiometabolic disturbances in humans24,25, this strain provides a relevant model to study how PFAS may worsen auditory system vulnerability, particularly in metabolically at-risk individuals. Moreover, LDL receptor (LDLR) is expressed in key cochlear cell types involved in hearing function, including hair cells, supporting cells, and the stria vascularis80, underscoring the biological relevance of this model for auditory research. As Ldlr−/− mice lack LDLR, the resulting elevation in LDL/VLDL cholesterol and metabolic stress76 may exacerbate their susceptibility to environmental toxins such as PFAS. The cochlear targets identified such as OHCs and SGNs, are conserved across mammalian species, and the pattern of high-frequency hearing loss observed in this model is consistent with epidemiological associations between PFAS burden and hearing loss in humans53. Thus, the Ldlr−/− model remains informative for identifying PFAS-sensitive cochlear structures and generating mechanistic hypotheses for future studies in wild-type strains and human populations.

OHCs are crucial for amplifying sound signals, and dysfunction of these cells can result in significant hearing loss. Notably, the hearing threshold shifts and the OHC loss indicate that the damage was more pronounced in the basal turn of the cochlea, suggesting that this region is more susceptible to PFAS-induced ototoxicity. Although a higher OHC loss than that observed in this study did not affect cochlear amplification81, potential damage to other regions, such as stria and spiral ligament, could lead to a loss of endocochlear potential, which in turn could affect DPOAEs even with lower levels of OHC loss. Extensive evidence indicates that the high-frequency region of the cochlea is well known to be more vulnerable to diverse auditory insults. Studies in animals, humans, and large populations consistently show that noise exposure causes greater injury in this region, including faster loss of auditory nerve fibers and OHCs82, and increased high-frequency hearing thresholds, even in people with normal hearing on standard audiograms83. Other ototoxic agents, including aminoglycosides84, cisplatin85, and solvents such as toluene86, similarly cause more damage in this region and produce pronounced high-frequency hearing loss. Our finding that PFAS exposure mainly impairs high-frequency hearing is consistent with the known vulnerability of the cochlear base, supporting the conclusion that this region is a primary target of PFAS-induced ototoxicity. PFAS may contribute to this process via disruption of thyroid hormone homeostasis, which plays an essential role in the development and maintenance of the auditory system32,33,87,88. PFAS are also known to activate peroxisome proliferator–activated receptors (PPARs), which regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and lipid and glucose homeostasis60–62,89. Since PPARs are expressed in both OHCs and IHCs59, PFAS exposure may impair the function and survival of these sensory cells through PPAR-mediated mechanisms. The higher resting metabolic rate of the OHCs makes them more susceptible than the IHCs to ototoxicants. Moreover, PFAS exposure has been linked to increased oxidative and nitrative stress90,91. Because cochlear oxidative/nitrative stress can trigger apoptosis of auditory hair cells92 and damage other cochlear structures, leading to hearing loss93, the significant OHC loss observed in our study may be attributed, at least in part, to PFAS-induced oxidative/nitrative stress.

ABR wave-1 amplitude is widely recognized as a key indicator of auditory nerve response strength. In the present study, PFAS-exposed animals exhibited a significant reduction in wave-1 amplitude and less defined waveform morphology compared to controls. Previous studies with other ototoxicants, such as lead, have shown that auditory neural dysfunction can occur independently of OHC loss94, underscoring the particular vulnerability of auditory neural pathways to environmental toxicants. In line with these findings, our results suggest that PFAS exposure can alter auditory nerve responses, providing evidence that PFAS may negatively impact the neural components of the cochlear pathway. Exposure to PFAS such as PFOS and PFOA disrupts neurotransmission by modulating GABAA95, which are expressed in type I spiral ganglion afferent neurons96,97, and glutamate receptor activity98, dysregulating potassium and calcium homeostasis99. PFAS-induced oxidative stress and dysregulated lipid metabolism can damage synaptic structures and impair neurotransmission100–103. Further, SGN degeneration has been linked to threshold elevations across multiple frequencies104. In this study, SGN loss was most pronounced in the basal turn, though a downward trend was also observed in apical and middle regions, which may explain threshold elevations across all frequencies despite OHC loss being confined to the basal turn. In addition, PFAS exposure led to a marked reduction in spiral ganglion cell density, providing evidence of PFAS-induced neuronal damage in the cochlea. These findings highlight neural degeneration as a major contributor to PFAS-related auditory dysfunction.

Collectively, these findings provide the first experimental evidence identifying critical cochlear targets in PFAS-induced auditory dysfunction. Exposure to PFAS mixture induced significant damage to OHCs and SGNs in the base of the cochlea, underscoring the importance of real-world exposures, which often involve combinations of multiple PFAS compounds. Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying PFAS-induced cochlear damage and discover effective interventions to mitigate PFAS-induced auditory dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The effect of PFAS exposure on cochlear structure and function was evaluated in mice (Fig. 1). All experiments included both male and female mice, and the data were combined for analysis. Electrophysiological analyses were done in 4–7 biological replicates (mice), while immunohistochemistry analyses were done in at least six biological replicates.

Animals

B6.129S7-Ldlrtm1Her/J mice (strain # 002207), on a C57BL/6J genetic background, were bred in-house at the Wayne State University animal facility. Baseline auditory testing was conducted at 13 weeks of age (body weight: 18–24 g). This strain carries a targeted mutation in the low-density lipoprotein receptor (Ldlr) gene, resulting in loss of functional LDLR activity and increased susceptibility to hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis78. Because this is a good model for studying metabolic alterations and their impact on auditory function80, and PFAS is known to affect metabolic functions, we used this model to study PFAS-induced ototoxicity. Following baseline testing, mice were exposed to a mixture of five PFAS compounds in their drinking water for seven weeks. The mixture contained equal concentrations (2 mg/L each) of both long-chain PFAS such as PFOS (Sigma-Aldrich # 2795-39-3, ≥ 98.0%), PFOA (Sigma-Aldrich # 335-67-1, 95%), PFNA (Sigma-Aldrich # 375-95-1, 97%), PFHxS (J&K Scientific # 3871-99-6, ≥ 98.0%), and emerging short-chain PFAS such as GenX (Synquest Laboratories, CAS No. 62037-80-3, 95%). Control mice received normal drinking water, which was replaced weekly. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Wayne State University. All experiments were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported as per the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation.

Auditory brainstem responses and distortion product otoacoustic emissions

ABRs and DPOAEs were recorded at baseline and after PFAS exposure. Sound stimulus delivery and data acquisition were performed using the Tucker-Davis Technologies (TDT) System III hardware with BioSigRZ software (v5.7.6). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% for induction, 1.5% maintenance, oxygen flow 1 L/min) and placed on a temperature-controlled heating pad (38 °C) inside a sound-attenuated chamber (Model ECKEL-AB-4230, Morrisburg, Ontario, Canada). Subdermal needle electrodes were positioned at the left mastoid (reference), skull vertex (active), and right ear (ground). Acoustic stimuli, consisting of clicks and tone bursts at frequencies of 4, 8, 16, 24, and 32 kHz, were delivered to the external auditory canal. For each stimulus type, waveforms were obtained by averaging responses from 512 presentations delivered at a rate of 21 stimuli per second. Stimulus intensity was decreased in 5 dB increments starting from 90 dB SPL. The ABR threshold was determined manually by a trained rater who was blinded to the exposure, defined as the lowest intensity that evoked a wave-1 response. The wave-1 amplitude (peak-to-trough) was analyzed at 90 dB SPL.

The DPOAE test used two primary tones (f1 and f2) presented at a fixed frequency ratio (f2/f1 = 1.2), and the emitted distortion product at 2f1–f2 was measured. The sound level difference between the tones (L2–L1) was + 10 dB, with L1 decreasing in 10 dB steps from 80 to 20 dB SPL. The f2 frequencies ranged from 4 kHz to 32 kHz. DPOAE signals were recorded using a sensitive probe microphone (ER10B+, Etymotic Research, Elk Grove Village, IL, USA), and data were collected every 20.971 ms and averaged over 512 repetitions to enhance signal reliability.

Immunohistochemistry of cochlear surface preparations

Immediately after euthanasia, the temporal bones were extracted from mice and placed in ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4). Cochleae were perfused via the oval window with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and post-fixed overnight at 4 °C. The tissue was decalcified using 100 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) in PBS at 4 °C for 48–72 h. Using a stereomicroscope (Leica M165 C), the cochleae were dissected into apical, middle, and basal turns, corresponding approximately to low-, mid-, and high-frequency regions. The dissected tissues were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific # 50062Z) in PBS containing Triton X-100 and bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibodies: mouse anti-myosin VIIa (IgG2a, 1:400, Santa Cruz Biotechnology # 74516), and rabbit anti-tubulin β-3 (TUBB3; IgG, 1:100, BioLegend # 802001). After thorough washing, tissues were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit AF 647 (IgG, 1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific # A-21244), and goat anti-mouse AF 488 (IgG2a, 1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific # A-21131). Samples were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific # P36935) and sealed with coverslip adhesive (Thermo Fisher # NC0154994).

Confocal microscopy and image processing

Confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope. A 40x oil-immersion objective (NA 1.3) was used to visualize hair cells and spiral ganglions. For hair cells and spiral ganglions, Z-stacks consisting of 5–10 optical sections were collected. All stacks were processed using maximum intensity projections, and images were analyzed using Zeiss Zen Blue 3.7 software. Tonotopic regions were defined using the ImageJ cochlear frequency mapping plugin, and hair cells and SGNs were consistently sampled across the apical (6–10 kHz), middle (16–20 kHz), and basal (36–40 kHz) turns in all mice to ensure region-matched comparisons. One image from the apical, middle, and basal cochlear turns of each animal was quantified for hair cells and SGNs, and group comparisons were based on the mean across animals. Immunolabeled hair cells were quantified along 100 μm lengths of the cochlear epithelium, and spiral ganglions labeled with β-3 tubulin were counted within standardized 10,000 μm² areas.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (v10.2.3, La Jolla, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365, v2404, Redmond, WA, USA). As the study was not powered to evaluate the sex-specific effects, data from both sexes were pooled for analysis. All datasets were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05) and analyzed with two-tailed unpaired t-tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.
