First report of widespread kdr-L995F pyrethroid-resistant An. arabiensis and Temporal trends of pyrethroid resistance in urban Ilorin, Kwara State, Nigeria
Authors: Abiodun Obembe, Tolulope Oyeniyi, Adedayo Olatunbosun Oduola, Folashayo Asekun, Adedapo Adeogun, Samson Awolola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffectiveness of currently deployed insecticide-based malaria vector control measures depends on continuous insecticide resistance monitoring and management. Urban malaria is becoming a serious health problem in Africa partly due to vector resistance in urban areas. To provide insecticide resistance trends information for effective resistance management, this study assessed insecticide susceptibility status and occurrence of knock down resistance mutation in Anopheles mosquitoes from urban Ilorin, Nigeria in two different years.MethodsMalaria vectors in urban Ilorin were assayed for susceptibility to WHO-recommended insecticides in 2017 and 2020 using WHO test kits. Sub-sets of exposed An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes were PCR-assayed for sibling species and knock down resistance (kdr) mutation detection.ResultsFull mosquito susceptibility (98–100% mortality; Niger and Baruba) and suspected resistance (95–97% mortality; Shao garage and Edun) to alphacypermethrin were recorded in 2017. Significantly (p ≤ 0.010) reduced mosquito mortalities indicating increased alphacypermethrin resistance were recorded in 2020. Permethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin and DDT resistance increased significantly (p ≤ 0.03) from 2017 (42–89%) to 2020 (5.8–58%) but not deltamethrin (p ≥ 0.086). Mosquitoes tested were mostly susceptible to bendiocarb and pirimiphos-methyl. The kdr-L995F detected increased from 15 to 20% for An. arabiensis, 24–36% for An. gambiae and 0–18% for An. coluzzii from 2017 to 2020. For both 2017 and 2020, most of the pyrethroid resistant (≥ 80%) and susceptible (≥ 75%) mosquitoes from each site were identified as An. arabiensis.ConclusionsThis study presents a first report on widespread occurrence of kdr-L995F mutation in An. arabiensis mosquito populations in urban Ilorin, Kwara State, Nigeria. The report of pyrethroid-resistant An. arabiensis prevalence in urban Ilorin triggered the switch from pyrethroid only to dual active ingredient ITN distribution in the city since 2020. Extension of dry season periods due to climate change may drive more preponderance of An. arabiensis in the study area. Therefore, the implementation of supplementary vector control measures targeted at An. arabiensis is required in this locality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11789-3.

Background

Significant reductions in global malaria burden have relied mainly on insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) of insecticides for vector control in endemic communities [1]. The ease of delivering ready-to-use ITNs especially in remote communities informs the ongoing periodic ITN campaign efforts implemented towards achieving the WHO recommended universal coverage of all populations at risk of malaria [2]. Consequently, the extensive use of the pyrethroid insecticides (in all ITNs and most IRS) continues to increase the insecticide selection pressure on the malaria vectors [3]. Indeed, increasing pyrethroid resistance have been reported in at least one malaria vector in 87% of 88 countries and 68% of sites across the globe [4]. Reports on pyrethroid insecticide resistance in malaria vectors from urban areas in Nigeria [5–8] and Africa [9–12] are also increasing. The main target site mutation conferring resistance to pyrethroids and cross-resistance to dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) in the major malaria vectors is the knock-down resistance mutation (kdr) [13]. Two amino acid changes in the sodium channel gene at codon 995; a leucine-to-phenylalanine substitution (L995F) and a leucine-to-serine substitution (L995S) are involved in conferring the knock down resistance [14, 15]. In Nigeria, where 31% of the global malaria deaths occurs [1], pyrethroid resistance have been reported in the major malaria vector groups collected from different parts of the country [3, 16–19]. However, data showing temporal trends in insecticide resistance status of prevailing malaria vectors within specific geographical settings are lacking. Such aggregated data should form critical components of in-country insecticide resistance profile required to inform local decision-making, and enhance understanding of the implications for malaria vector control [15]. Urban human population increases have been noted from 31% in 2000 to 41% in 2019, and is projected to increase to 47% by 2030 [20]. Malaria vectors are fast acclimatizing to the poorly planned urban settings in sub-Saharan Africa [21]. Unrestrained urbanization and urban mosquito vector prevalence have elevated urban malaria to a major health problem [21].

Initial mass ITN distribution and replacement campaigns have been implemented in urban Ilorin, Kwara State, North Central Nigeria over the years [22] without routine resistance monitoring data to detect, mitigate and manage insecticide resistance. This study was conducted in 2017 and 2020 to assess temporal trends in insecticide susceptibility status of the major malaria vectors in Ilorin. The involvement of the kdr-L995F mechanism in the insecticide resistance was also investigated. Data elucidated on the dynamics of insecticide resistance provided information for effective resistance management and informed vector control decision-making in the study area since 2020.

Methods

Study area

Ilorin (80 30’ 0.0000’’ N, 40 32’ 60.0000’’ E [23]) is the capital city of Kwara State located in North central Nigeria. The climate is tropical with mean annual temperature, relative humidity and rainfall of 25–28.9 °C, 65–80% and 1150 mm, respectively [24]. Asa River and its tributaries are the major water bodies running northwards through residential and commercial areas of Ilorin city [25]. Industrial effluents and agricultural waste run offs are released into the river depending on the point of contact [25]. The river tributaries running through the low-cost residential areas of the city also receive domestic wastes forming pockets of heavily productive mosquito larval breeding sites. Anopheles mosquito larvae were collected from such breeding sites in Shao-garage (N08°50.67ˈ E004°50.52ˈ), Edun (N08°48.98ˈ E004°56.07ˈ), Baruba (N08°50.41ˈ E004° 54.75ˈ) and Niger (N08°48.93ˈ E004°55.41ˈ) areas within the Ilorin metropolis (Fig. 1).

Mosquito collection and rearing

Mosquito collections were done using standard dippers according to the method of WHO [26] within the same months of June to September in 2017 and 2020, hereafter referred to as first and second year respectively. The mosquitoes were collected from the same breeding site within each area. For both years, the collected larvae were reared to adulthood in the insectary (26o−28 °C, 78–82% RH) at the Department of Zoology, Kwara State University following standard protocol [27]. Newly emerged adult Anopheles mosquitoes were fed with 10% sugar solution.

Insecticide susceptibility tests

Insecticide susceptibility tests were performed using WHO standard procedures [28] and test kits for adult mosquitoes ordered from the Vector Control Research Unit, University Sains Malaysia. For both first and second-year bioassays, at least five replicates of 20–25 non-blood-fed 3–5-day old female adult Anopheles mosquitoes were exposed for 1 h to WHO test papers containing 0.05% Deltamethrin, 0.05% lambda-cyhalothrin, 0.75% permethrin, 0.75% Alphacypermethrin, 4% DDT, 0.1% propoxur, 0.1% bendiocarb and 0.1% primiphos-methyl in the laboratory section of the insectary (26o−28 °C, 78–81% RH). Two replicates of 25 mosquitoes each were also exposed to control papers during the same 1-hour period to serve as controls. Numbers of knocked down mosquitoes were recorded every ten minutes during the 1-hour exposure period. Exposed mosquitoes were then transferred into holding tubes, supplied with 10% sugar solution and monitored for mortality 24 h after exposure. Each dead or alive mosquito was preserved separately over desiccated silica gel in Eppendorf tube for further analysis.

Mosquito identification and kdr-L995F detection

The mosquitoes were identified as An. gambiae s.l using standard morphological keys [29]. Genomic DNA extracted from a sub-set of the preserved dead and alive mosquitoes [30] was used for standard species-specific PCR [31] and PCR-RFLP [32] sibling species identification at the Molecular Entomology and Vector Control Research Laboratory of The Nigerian Institute of Medical Research. The species-specific PCR primers used were (Primer sequence 5’ to 3’): 0.52ng primer Melas (TGA CCA ACC CAC TCC CTT GA), 0.53ng primer An. gambiae s.s (CTG GTT TGG TCG GCA CGT TT), 0.57ng primer An. arabiensis (AAG TGT CCT TCT CCA TCC TA), 0.49ng primer An. quadriannulatus (CAG ACC AAG ATG GTT AGT AT) and 0.47ng primer Universal (GTG TGC CCC TTC CTC GAT GT) [31]. Master mix contained 0.25 µl of An. quadriannulatus primer, 0.5 µl each of the Universal, An. gambiae s.s., An. arabiensis and An. melas primers, 4 µl of Firepol® Solis Biodyne premix, 1 µl of extracted DNA and 5.25 µl of distilled water in a 12.5 µl final reaction volume. The PCR conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min, 35 cycles each consisting of 30 s denaturation at 95 °C, 30 s annealing at 55 °C, 40 s elongation at 72 °C and 5 min final elongation at 72 °C. Amplified fragments of the PCR products, electrophoresed in 1.5% agarose gel, were captured under a gel documentation machine (Additional file 1: Figure S1). PCR products of An. gambiae s.s were then digested in the thermal cycler at 37 °C for 6 h after the addition of 0.2 µl Heamophilus haemolyticus restriction enzyme (Hha1), 0.6 µl cut buffer and 1.0 µl distilled water [32]. The resulting An. coluzzii and An. gambiae PCR products were electrophoresed in 1.5% agarose gel and captured under the gel documentation machine (Additional file 2: Figure S2). Allele-specific PCR for the detection of the West African kdr-L995F mutation were conducted on genomic DNA from a sub-set of the pyrethroid resistant mosquitoes using the standard allele-specific primers: Agd1 (ATAGATTCCCCGACCAfTG), Agd2 (AGACAAGGATGATGAACC), Agd3 (AATTTGCATTACTTACGACA), and Agd4 (CTGTAGTGATAGGAAATTTA) and protocol [14]. Master mix for kdr were prepared with 4 µl Firepol® Solis Biodyne premix, 1.5 µl extracted DNA, 5 µl distilled water and 0.5 µl each of the Agd1-Agd4 kdr primers in a 12.5 µl final reaction volume. The PCR amplification for kdr followed the conditions of 94 °C for 5 min, 40 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 48 °C for 2 min, 72 °C for 2 min, and a final extension of 72 °C for 10 min [14]. The products were run on 2% agarose gel and photographed in a gel documentation machine (Additional file 3: Figure S3). At least 20% of the alive/resistant An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes from each site was randomly selected for sibling species identification. The few alive/resistant An. gambiae and An. coluzzii mosquitoes identified were all analyzed for kdr. Twenty samples representing a minimum of 30% of the abundant resistant An. arabiensis from each site were randomly selected for kdr analysis.

Data analysis

Mosquito populations with less than 90% mortality were considered as resistant to the particular insecticide, 90–97% mortality indicated suspected resistance while 98 to 100% implied susceptibility of the mosquito population [28]. Correction of percentage mortalities with Abbott’s formula was not required since mortalities in all the control experiments was less than 5%. Time to 50% and 95% knockdown of each mosquito population were estimated by the log-time probit model [33]. First and second year percentages of mosquito knockdown and mortalities per insecticide were compared for each site using Pearson’s chi-squared tests. Allelic frequencies [F(R)] of kdr-L995F mutation in each Anopheles sibling species was calculated as F(R) = (2 × RR + RS)/2 N [34] and expressed as percentages [F(R) % = F(R) x 100], where RR = number of homozygote resistant mosquitoes; RS = number of heterozygote resistant mosquitoes; N = total number of mosquitoes analysed.

The 24 h post-exposure mortalities of An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes were higher in the first compared to second year for pyrethroids and DDT but not bendiocarb and pirimiphos-methyl (Fig. 2). Spatial heterogeneities involving full susceptibility (98–100% mortality; Niger and Baruba) and suspected resistance (95–97% mortality; Shao garage and Edun) to alphacypermethrin were recorded in the different sites. Mosquito mortalities indicating alphacypermethrin susceptibility (98–100%) in Baruba and Niger sites in the first year reduced (50–52%) significantly (p ≤ 0.010) indicating confirmed resistance in the second year. Confirmed alphacypermethrin resistance with significantly (p < 0.001) reduced mortality (42%) was recorded in mosquitoes from Edun and Shao garage in the second year compared to the first year (95–97%).

Resistance to permethrin, deltamethrin and lambda-cyhalothrin were recorded in the mosquito populations from all the sites both in the first (42–88% mortality) and second (5.8–82%) years. An. gambiae s.l. resistance to permethrin (p ≤ 0.024) and lambda-cyhalothrin (p ≤ 0.007) increased significantly in all the sites from the first to the second year except in Shao garage (p = 0.123). Increase in deltamethrin resistance from first (73–87%) to second (40–75%) year was not significant (p ≥ 0.086) except in Edun (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

The DDT resistance recorded in the mosquitoes from all the sites increased significantly (p ≤ 0.03) from first (63–89%) to second year (41–51%) except for Shao garage (p = 0.059). All mosquito populations were susceptible (98–100%) to bendiocarb and pirimiphos-methyl during the study years except for Baruba, for which resistance to this insecticide was suspected in the first year (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study revealed temporal trends in pyrethroids resistance of An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes in urban Ilorin, Nigeria. Increased kdt50, reduced percentage knockdown and reduced percentage mortalities of mosquitoes from first to second year indicated increased pyrethroids resistance in the mosquito populations. Similar temporal increases in pyrethroid resistance have been reported in urban Anopheles mosquito populations in North-western Jigawa State, Nigeria between 2009 and 2017 [35].

Initial widespread resistance to permethrin, deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, but not alphacypermethrin in the sites suggested differential pyrethroid response of the mosquito populations. This provided earlier evidence to suggest the use of alphacypermethrin impregnated bed-nets in these areas. However, the subsequent confirmed alphacypermethrin resistance recorded together with all other pyrethroid resistance in the mosquito populations from all sites in the second year became a threat to the continued deployment of pyrethroid only treated bed nets in the city. The observation of temporal changes in vector resistance supports the need for regular insecticide susceptibility testing to unravel the dynamics and trends required for timely vector control decisions. The detection and increased percentage occurrence of kdr mutations accompanying reduced percentage mosquito mortalities indicated the involvement of this target site mechanism in the rising insecticide resistance observed.

Knock down resistance mutations were not found in earlier works on An. arabiensis in Nigeria [5, 36, 37]. Indeed, earlier assertions on the rarity of pyrethroids resistance in An. arabiensis in Nigeria [38] have been supported by the presence of permethrin and deltamethrin susceptible An. arabiensis species in Lagos and Niger States [37]. However, the incidence of deltamethrin resistant An. arabiensis in Aiyede Kwara State [37] and current preponderance of pyrethroids resistant An. arabiensis in our study indicates the possibility of unreported spread of pyrethroids resistance in the An. arabiensis species in Nigeria. A previous study by Ibrahim et al. [39] identified kdr-L995F only in four An. arabiensis mosquitoes in Jigawa Northwest Nigeria. To our knowledge, this study reports for the first time, widespread occurrence of kdr-L995F in An. arabiensis populations across four different areas in urban Ilorin, Nigeria. The kdr-L995F allele have also been identified in several An. arabiensis mosquito populations in other parts of Africa [11, 40–42].

Low kdr frequencies (10–25%) in the predominant An. arabiensis populations in our study suggests the possibility of other resistance mechanisms contributing to the resistance detected in this species. The kdr-L995F mutations (13–50%) detected in the less abundant An. gambiae species also suggest ongoing pyrethroid resistance selection in the vector population. It is still uncertain whether insecticide resistance in the mosquitoes leads to vector control failure [43–46] or not [47–50]. Therefore, efforts to preserve local malaria vector susceptibility to recommended insecticides must continue.

Pyrethroid-piperonyl butoxide (PBO) as well as dual active ingredient (AI) treated bed-nets containing both pyrethroid and an insect growth regulator/pyrrole insecticide designed to kill mosquitoes carrying pyrethroid resistance genes have been developed for enhanced vector control. Such insecticide combinations have the dual potential to improve malaria vector control and contribute to insecticide resistance management especially in areas where resistance to both active ingredients is not yet established [1]. As such, confirmation of kdr associated alphacypermethrin resistance in our study may have implications both for the use of alphacypermethrin-only impregnated bed-nets or a switch to the emerging alphacypermethrin-based dual AI nets. Further studies investigating pyrethroid resistance intensity and the involvement of metabolic resistance mechanisms in the mosquitoes from these study sites are vital towards other evidence-based decisions such as a switch to pyrethroid-PBO bed nets. Susceptibility status of the mosquitoes in most of the sites in the study area to bendiocarb and pirimiphos-methyl signifies these insecticides as viable options for the control of the vectors. High level pyrethroid resistance together with full bendiocarb and pirimiphos-methyl susceptibility have also been reported in Anopheles arabiensis populations elsewhere in Africa [51, 52].

An. arabiensis thrives in dry seasons and arid regions [29]. Its predominance (≥ 80%) in this study in urban Ilorin may not have been due to the rainy season when the mosquitoes were collected. Much lower An. arabiensis incidence (0–10.5%) have been identified during the dry season in rural areas within the same Kwara State [53–55] where our study was conducted. This corroborates earlier reports of An. arabiensis larvae predominance in urban areas compared to rural areas [38]. Non-cattle rearing rural areas such as the ones with the low An. arabiensis incidence above may not harbor much of temporary sun lit pools required for An. arabiensis larval breeding. Availability of such suitable temporary breeding sites in urban Ilorin, the Kwara state capital, could have birthed the An. arabiensis prevalence in the present study site compared to the low incidence of the species in the rural communities within the state. Prevalence of An. arabiensis species (77–100%) have also been reported in urban areas in Oyo and Ogun States compared to much lower incidence of the species (0–13%) in rural areas [38, 56].

The knock down pyrethroid resistance detected in this study may be due to long-term exposure of the mosquitoes to pyrethroid insecticides in ITN [1] and those used for personal protection/domestic purposes. Equally, mosquito metabolism alterations, induced by larva-xenobiotics interactions in the breeding sites, may have triggered the development of mechanisms cross-selecting resistance to pyrethroids [57]. Adult An. arabiensis avoids prolonged contact with ITN and IRS insecticides [58, 59] thereby thriving under high indoor ITN coverage through persistent but cautious short-lived anthropophagic indoor foraging behaviour [57]. As such, studies linking long-term ITN utilization with An. arabiensis prevalence have been reported in Africa [60]. An. arabiensis persistent short-lived indoor foraging behaviour is sustained by subsistence on outdoor human and animal blood feeding in the event of unsuccessful indoor feeding attempts [58]. The 54% population of urban Ilorin residents using aerosol insecticides and 92% of the residents utilizing ITN all attested to spending the first five hours of the night outdoors [22, 61] thereby increasing malaria exposure risks through potential An. arabiensis outdoor feeding.

Urban insecticide resistance may represent a threat to universal ITN coverage and indoor residual spraying against mosquitoes in African urban areas [21]. The report of pyrethroid-resistant An. arabiensis prevalence in urban Ilorin has led to the switch from pyrethroid to dual active ingredient ITN distribution in the city since 2020. Implementation of complementary control methods targeted at the unique traits of An. arabiensis species in the study area is also recommended.

Conclusions

This study presents a first report on widespread kdr-L995F mutation occurrence in An. arabiensis populations in urban Ilorin, Kwara State, Nigeria. The results of this study led to a change from pyrethroid only to dual active ingredient ITN procurement and distribution in the study area since 2020. Predominance of pyrethroid-resistant An. arabiensis in urban Ilorin may still require complementary control methods targeted at the unique traits of the mosquito species.
