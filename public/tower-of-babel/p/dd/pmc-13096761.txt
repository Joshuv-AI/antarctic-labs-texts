Extending the Median Odds Ratio (MOR), the Interval Odds Ratio (IOR), and the Proportion of Opposed Odds Ratios (POOR) for Use With 3‐Level Multilevel Logistic Regression Models
Authors: Peter C Austin, George Leckie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultilevel data occur frequently in health services, population and public health, and epidemiologic research (e.g., patients nested within hospitals). Multilevel logistic regression models allow one to account for the clustering of individuals within higher‐level units when estimating the association of individual‐level and cluster‐level characteristics with individual‐level binary outcomes. Odds ratios for cluster‐level variables generated from a multilevel logistic regression model have an interpretation that is conditional on the random effects, compared to the marginal interpretation of odds ratios from logistic regression models that do not incorporate cluster‐specific random effects. The interval odds ratio (IOR) and the proportion of opposed odds ratios (POOR) are two summary measures that have been proposed for quantifying the magnitude and heterogeneity of the association of cluster‐level variables with individual‐level binary outcomes. The median odds ratio (MOR) is a measure of the between‐cluster heterogeneity of a binary outcome. The IOR, the POOR, and the MOR were developed for use with two‐level multilevel logistic regression models (e.g., patients nested within hospitals). In applied research, data with a three‐level multilevel structure are common (e.g., hospitalized patients cared for by the attending physician who in turn practice within hospitals). We mathematically derived extensions of the MOR, the IOR, and the POOR for use with three‐level multilevel logistic regression models. We then illustrated the application and interpretation of the MOR, the IOR, and the POOR by analyzing mortality in patients hospitalized with acute myocardial infarction, where patients are nested within physicians who in turn are nested within hospitals.Keywords: interval odds ratio, logistic regression, median odds ratio, multilevel models, odds ratio, proportion of opposed odds ratios

Introduction

Data with a multilevel structure occur frequently in health services, population and public health, and epidemiology. Examples include patients hospitalized with acute myocardial infarction (AMI or a heart attack) who are nested or clustered within the hospitals to which they were admitted, residents nested within the neighborhoods in which they live, and individuals nested in primary care practices or general medical practices. Multilevel regression models (also referred to as hierarchical regression models, mixed effects models, or random effects models) are regression models for the analysis of multilevel data [1, 2, 3]. Multilevel models allow one to both quantify the magnitude of between‐cluster variation in the outcome and the magnitude of the association between the outcome variable and variables measured at different levels of the hierarchy.

Multilevel regression models can incorporate explanatory variables measured at different levels of the hierarchy. Consider data with a 3‐level multilevel structure: patients hospitalized with an AMI (Level‐1 units), nested within the attending or most responsible physician (Level‐2 units), nested within the hospitals in which they practice (Level‐3 units). Level‐1 explanatory variables could consist of patient demographic characteristics (e.g., age and sex) and comorbid conditions (e.g., diabetes, acute renal failure, congestive heart failure). Level‐2 explanatory variables could consist of characteristics of the physician who treated the patient (e.g., physician specialty or training, the number of AMI patients cared for during that year). Level‐3 explanatory variables could consist of characteristics of the hospital to which the patient was admitted (e.g., academic status of the hospital, number of AMI patients treated during that year, presence of facilities for coronary revascularization). Outcomes are measured on the Level‐1 units (e.g., mortality within 30 days of admission).

Multilevel regression models incorporate cluster‐specific random effects to account for unobserved cluster‐level variables which induce within‐cluster homogeneity in outcomes. A failure to incorporate cluster‐specific random effects leads to the odds ratios being estimated with spurious precision, especially for the cluster‐level covariates. A consequence of incorporating cluster‐specific random effects is that the interpretation of measures of association between covariates and individual‐level outcomes is conditional on the random effects [4]. Therefore, regression coefficients are said to have a conditional or within‐cluster interpretation.

To aid in the interpretation of the odds ratio as a measure of association in multilevel logistic regression models, Larsen and colleagues introduced the concept of the interval odds ratio (IOR), which was subsequently popularized in a series of articles [5]. For a given variable, the IOR provides an interval such that 80% of the odds ratios for that variable comparing outcomes between two different clusters whose value of that variable differs by a specified amount (and consequently with two different values of random effects) lie within the given interval. Merlo and colleagues subsequently developed a complementary measure of association, the “sorting out index,” which was later relabeled as the “proportion of opposed odds ratios” (POOR) [6, 7]. The POOR is the proportion of the previously defined odds ratios that are in the opposite direction to the overall odds ratio. The IOR and POOR allow investigators to quantify whether the effect of a cluster‐level variable is large in comparison with the unexplained or residual between‐cluster variation. The IOR and the POOR aid the analyst to quantify the magnitude of the odds ratio by summarizing the distribution of odds ratios for pairwise comparisons of clusters. These two measures can be used to quantify how much variation there is in the pairwise comparison of clusters. They provide essential context for assessing the importance of the original model‐based odds ratio for the fixed effect of that cluster‐level variable.

The IOR and the POOR provide information on the magnitude of the association between a cluster‐level covariate and the odds of the outcome and the extent to which the odds ratios vary across clusters. Larsen and colleagues also proposed the median odds ratio (MOR) to quantify the between‐cluster variation in the outcome using the odds ratio scale [4, 5, 6]. As such, there is a unique MOR for a given model, and not one MOR for each variable in the model. The MOR is the median of the distribution of all possible odds ratios comparing the odds of the outcome between two individuals from two distinct clusters when the two individuals have the same value of all model covariates and when the individual with a greater likelihood of the outcome (due to having a higher value on the cluster random effect) is compared with the individual with the lower likelihood of the outcome (i.e., so that the pairwise odds ratio will always be greater than or equal to one).

A limitation of the IOR, POOR, and the MOR is that they were developed for use with a 2‐level data structure (e.g., patients nested within hospitals). However, 3‐level data structures are common in health and epidemiological research. The need to use these measures in three‐level studies is no less compelling than in two‐level studies. Namely, without them, researchers are left to report cluster‐ and supercluster‐level variance components on the log‐odds scale, which lack a clear and intuitive substantive interpretation. As a result, the magnitude of residual clustering and superclustering is inadequately conveyed, as is the relative importance of cluster‐ and supercluster‐level covariates compared with these two sources of unexplained heterogeneity. To the best of our knowledge, none of the IOR, POOR, and MOR have been extended for use with three‐level multilevel logistic regression models.

For those less familiar with the IOR, the POOR, and MOR, we provide a brief example to highlight their interpretation. We used the case study data described in Section 3 and treated it as having a 2‐level hierarchy: patients nested within hospitals. We fit a simplistic multilevel logistic regression model in which death within 30 days was regressed on one patient‐level variable (age) and one hospital‐level variable (a binary variable denoting whether the hospital was an academic teaching hospital). The MOR was 1.12. Thus, when comparing two individuals of the same age and admitted to different hospitals, but such that both hospitals had the same teaching status, the median odds ratio for 30‐day mortality across all possible pairwise comparisons between hospitals was 1.12 (when comparing mortality at the hospital with the higher odds of death with the hospital with the lower odds of death). The IOR for the hospital‐level variable denoting teaching status was (0.887, 1.379). Thus, when comparing the odds of death for a patient of a given age admitted to a teaching hospital to the odds of death for a patient of the same age admitted to a non‐teaching hospital, across all possible pairwise comparisons of hospitals, 80% of the odds ratios would lie between 0.887 and 1.379. The overall odds ratio for hospital teaching status was 1.106. The POOR for the hospital‐level variable denoting teaching status was 0.28. Thus, when comparing the odds of death for a patient of a given age admitted to a teaching hospital to the odds of death for a patient of the same age admitted to a nonteaching hospital, across all possible pairwise comparisons of hospitals, 28% of the odds ratios would be in the opposite direction from 1.106. In other words, in 28% of the possible comparisons, the odds of death would be lower at the teaching hospital compared to at the nonteaching hospital.

The objective of the current study was to extend the IOR, the POOR, and the MOR to 3‐level multilevel data structures. The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we formally define Larsen's IOR and MOR and Merlo's POOR for use with 2‐level multilevel data and then extend them to settings with a 3‐level multilevel data structure. In Section 3, we provide a case study illustrating the application of the IOR, the POOR, and the MOR to data with a 3‐level multilevel structure. Finally, in Section 4, we provide a brief discussion and summary of our findings. A large language model (ChatGPT) was used in conducting the literature review, conducting background research and calculations, and in code development.

Derivation of the IOR

Consider two individuals in clusters j and j′ (with j ≠ j′). Assume that the values of the pth cluster‐level variable for these two clusters are zpj and zpj′, respectively. Furthermore, assume that, apart from this, the values of the k individual‐level variables are the same for these two individuals and that the values of the m − 1 other cluster‐level variables are the same for the two clusters to which these two individuals belong. Based on Model (1), the odds ratio comparing the odds of the outcome for these two individuals is expβp×zpj′−zpj+α0j′−α0j. This is the odds ratio comparing clusters j and j′. We now determine the distribution of this odds ratio across all possible pairs of clusters satisfying the given conditions. Note that the log‐odds ratio, given by the argument within the exponential function is a normal random variable with mean βp×zpj−zpj′ and variance 2τ2 (since the two cluster‐specific random effects are independent of one another). Thus, the odds ratio follows a log‐normal distribution. A normal random variable whose distribution is Nμ,σ2 has as its α‐percentile μ+σΦ−1(α), where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Thus, a normal random variable with mean βp×zpj′−zpj and variance 2τ2 has as its α‐percentile βp×zpj′−zpj+2τ2Φ−1(α). Since the exponential function is a monotonic function and quantiles are preserved under monotonic transformations, we have that the α‐percentile of the odds ratio is expβp×zpj′−zpj+2τ2Φ−1(α).

Larsen suggested that the IOR be defined as the interval that contains the middle 80% of all possible odds ratios comparing individuals from two clusters (i.e., exclude the lower and upper 10% of odds ratios). The interval odds ratio is thus defined as: IOR=expβp×zpj′−zpj+2τ2Φ−1(0.1),expβp×zpj′−zpj+2τ2Φ−1(0.9). Note that the IOR integrates information on both the strength of the association (βp) and the between‐cluster variation in outcomes (τ2).

The above formula for the IOR is for a difference in the pth covariate equal to zpj′−zpj. Frequently, we want to compare the effect of increasing the value of the covariate by one unit. This would be the case when considering a categorical variable and comparing a non‐reference level to the reference level (when using dummy variable coding) or when considering a continuous variable and estimating the effect of increasing the value of that covariate by one unit. If zpj′−zpj=1 then IOR=expβp+2τ2Φ−1(0.1),expβp+2τ2Φ−1(0.9).

Derivation of the POOR

Merlo and colleagues defined the POOR as the proportion of the previously defined distribution of odds ratios (i.e., odds ratios comparing clusters j and j′) that are in the opposite direction to the overall odds ratio [7]. Without loss of generality, we assume that zpj′−zpj=1, which would be the case when the cluster‐level variable is categorical and the analyst is comparing a non‐reference level to the reference level (using dummy variable coding) or when the variable is a continuous variable and the analyst is computing the odds ratio for a one‐unit increase in the continuous variable. Thus, the distribution of the pairwise log‐odds ratio (derived above) is normal with mean βp and variance 2τ2: X˜Nβp,2τ2, where X denotes the pairwise log‐odds ratios.

We consider two cases: βp≥0 and βp<0. First, if βp≥0, we want to determine Pr(X < 0). We have that Pr(X<0)=PrX−βp2τ2<−βp2τ2=Φ−βp2τ2=Φ−|βp|2τ2, where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Second, if βp<0, we want to determine Pr(X > 0). We have that Pr(X>0)=PrX−βp2τ2>−βp2τ2=1−Φ−βp2τ2=Φβp2τ2=Φ−|βp|2τ2. Thus, in either case the proportion of pairwise log‐odds ratios having the opposite sign to βp is Φ−βp2τ2.

Derivation of the MOR

The log‐odds ratio comparing two individuals from different clusters where all the individual‐level and cluster‐level variables take on the same values for these two individuals is α0j′−α0j. The distribution of this log‐odds ratio has mean 0 and variance 2τ2: α0j′−α0j˜N0,2τ2. We require that the two individuals be ordered such that we are comparing the individual with the greater likelihood of the outcome with the individual with the lower likelihood of the outcome. This is equivalent to examining the distribution of ∣α0j′−α0j∣. This random variable follows a half‐normal distribution with variance 2τ2 [8]. This half‐normal distribution has cumulative distribution function F(x)=2Φx2τ2−1 [8]. The median of this distribution is determined by solving F(x) = 0.5 for x: 2Φx2τ2−1=0.5. This results in x=2τ2Φ−1(0.75). Since this is the median of the absolute value of the log‐odds ratio comparing two clusters, the median of the odds ratio is exp2τ2Φ−1(0.75).

As is typical, we assume that the Level‐2 random effects and the Level‐3 random effects are independent of one another.

Larsen's IOR and Merlo's POOR compared the odds of the outcome between two randomly selected clusters such that the values of the given cluster‐level covariate differ by a fixed amount (typically a difference of 1). Larsen's MOR compares the odds of the outcome between two individuals in two different clusters, where the individual in the cluster with the higher random effect is being compared to the individual in the cluster with the lower cluster random effect (so that the odds ratio will be greater than or equal to one) and such that the values of all covariates are the same for these two individuals. With a 3‐level data hierarchy, there are three comparisons that can be made: (i) between two randomly chosen Level‐2 units that are nested in the same Level‐3 unit; (ii) between two randomly chosen Level‐2 units that are nested in different Level‐3 units; (iii) between two randomly chosen Level‐3 units. We consider each of these comparisons separately and derive the IOR, the POOR, and the MOR for each of these comparisons.

Case Study

We provide a case study to illustrate the application of the IOR, the POOR, and the MOR to a setting with a three‐level multilevel structure. The data consisted of patients hospitalized with AMI (Level‐1 units) who were treated by most responsible physicians (Level‐2 units) within hospitals (Level‐3 units).

Data Sources

We used data from the Ontario Myocardial Infarction Database, which contains data on patients hospitalized with an AMI at Ontario hospitals between 1992 and 2025 [9]. For the current case study, we used hospitalizations that occurred in the 12‐month period between April 1, 2024 and March 31, 2025. Furthermore, we restricted the sample to patients cared for by physicians who were general practitioners/family practice, general internists, or cardiologists. Due to the study inclusion and exclusion criteria, no patient had more than one hospitalization during the one‐year time frame of the study. The study sample consisted of 16 375 patients treated by 2698 physicians at 155 hospitals. The number of patients treated per physician ranged from 1 to 118, with a median of 2 (25th–75th percentiles: 1–6), while the number of patients per hospital ranged from 1 to 953, with a median of 43 (25th–75th percentiles: 15–114).

Eleven patient‐level variables, consisting of the variables in the Ontario AMI Mortality Prediction Model (age, sex, congestive heart failure, cardiogenic shock, arrhythmia, pulmonary edema, diabetes mellitus with complications, stroke, acute renal disease, chronic renal disease, and malignancy), were measured on each patient [10]. The one continuous explanatory variable (age) was centered around the sample average (this was achieved by subtracting the average value of age in the entire cohort from each subject's age).

Two physician‐level variables were included: physician training (family practice/general practice vs. general internal medicine vs. cardiology, with the latter serving as the reference level) and physician AMI volume (number of AMI patients treated during that year). Physician volume of AMI patients was centered around the average physician‐specific AMI volume across the population of physicians.

Three hospital‐level variables were included: hospital academic status (teaching hospital vs. non‐teaching hospital), hospital capacity for coronary revascularization (the presence of facilities for conducting coronary artery bypass graft surgery or percutaneous coronary intervention), and hospital volume of AMI patients (number of AMI patients treated during the year). The first two are binary, while the third is continuous. Hospital volume of AMI patients was centered around the average hospital‐specific AMI volume across the population of hospitals.

The binary outcome for the case study was death due to any cause within 30 days of hospital admission, which occurred for 961 (5.9%) patients in the sample.

Statistical Methods

We fit a 3‐level multilevel logistic regression model in which the binary variable denoting death within 30 days of hospital admission was regressed on the patient, physician, and hospital variables listed above. We divided patient age by 10 years, so that the odds ratio would denote the association between mortality and a 10‐year increase in patient age, all else being equal. We divided physician AMI volume by 10, so that the odds ratio would be for an increase of 10 in the number of AMI patients treated. We divided hospital AMI volume by 50, so that the odds ratio would be for an increase of 50 in the number of AMI patients treated. The model incorporated random effects for physicians and for hospitals. We computed the IOR and the POOR for all physician and hospital‐level variables. We computed the MOR for the fitted model. We used the parametric multilevel bootstrap procedure to estimate 95% confidence intervals for the MOR and the POOR [11, 12]. We used 2000 bootstrap replicates and computed the 95% confidence intervals using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the distribution of the measure of heterogeneity across the 2000 bootstrap replicates (note that the bootstrap procedure was computationally intensive, requiring approximately 479 h of CPU time, due to the fitting of 2000 3‐level logistic regression models). This procedure has previously been found to work well for interval estimation for the MOR for a 2‐level logistic regression model [13].

The analyses were conducted using the R statistical software language (version 3.6.3). The multilevel logistic regression model was fit using the glmer function from the lme4 package (version 1.1–27). Statistical software code for conducting the case study is provided in the supplemental online material.

The MOR represents the median odds ratio from pairwise comparisons of identical patients treated by randomly selected clusters with identical characteristics. The MOR comparing patients treated by two physicians within the same hospital was 1.83 (95% confidence interval: 1.62–2.26). Thus, when comparing two individuals with identical characteristics, treated by two physicians with the same characteristics, at the same hospital, the median odds ratio was 1.83. The MOR comparing patients in two different hospitals and therefore treated by two different physicians was 1.91 (95% confidence interval: 1.68–2.28). Thus, when comparing two individuals with identical characteristics, treated by two physicians with identical characteristics at different hospitals with identical characteristics, the median odds ratio was 1.91. These MORs had approximately the same magnitude as the effect of a 10‐year increase in patient age (odds ratio = 1.892). Thus, the magnitude of the effect of residual clustering on mortality, at either the physician‐level or hospital‐level, was comparable to the effect of a 10‐year increase in patient age on mortality. The use of the MOR is attractive because it allows one to quantify the magnitude of residual between‐cluster variation in the outcome on the odds ratio scale and this can be compared with the odds ratios for individual‐level variables.

The IORs and POORs along with their associated 95% confidence intervals are reported in Table 2 (these can be contrasted with the odds ratios for the fixed effects in Table 1). For the physician‐level variables, we present two IORs and two POORs: comparing two different physicians in the same hospital and comparing two different physicians in two different hospitals. The IORs were all wide and included 1. For example, the IOR comparing general practitioners/family physicians (GP/FPs) with cardiologists practicing within the same hospital was (0.512, 5.064), while the IOR comparing GP/FPs with cardiologists practicing at different hospitals was (0.472, 5.487). Larsen and Merlo provide the following suggestions for interpreting the IOR: “The interval is narrow if the residual between‐cluster variation is small, and it is wide if the between‐cluster variation is large. If the interval contains 1, the residual cluster variability is large in comparison with the effect of the cluster‐level variable. If the interval does not contain 1, the effect of the cluster‐level variable is large in comparison with the unexplained between‐cluster variation” (page 83) [4]. Elsewhere they suggested that “if the interval contains the value one, this indicates that the effect of the area characteristic under scrutiny is not that strong when compared with the remaining residual area level heterogeneity” (page 296) [6]. All our intervals were wide and contained one, suggesting that residual variation between physicians and variation between hospitals is large compared to the effect of physician‐level or hospital‐level characteristics. Furthermore, the width of the intervals suggests that between‐cluster variation is large.

The POORs ranged from 0.294 (general internists vs. cardiologists compared within the same hospital) to 0.486 (hospital AMI volume). From a theoretical perspective, the POOR can range from 0 to 0.5 (0%–50%). The odds ratio for the fixed effect for general internist was 1.625 (95% CI: 1.245–2.119), indicating that, after adjusting for patient, physician, and hospital characteristics and conditioning on the random effect, patients treated by general internists had an odds of 30‐day mortality that was 62.5% higher than the odds of death for patients treated by cardiologists. However, in 29.4% of the possible pairwise comparisons between patients treated by general internists and cardiologists at the same hospital, the patient treated by the general internist had lower mortality than the patient cared for by the cardiologist. The range of the POORs suggests that the effect of the physician‐level and hospital‐level variables is small compared with the unexplained between‐cluster variation, particularly for the hospital‐level variables, for which the POORs all exceed 0.4.

Discussion

Multilevel regression analysis allows one to quantify the magnitude of the association between the individual‐level outcome and both individual‐ and cluster‐level variables. It also allows investigators to quantify the magnitude of the between‐cluster variation in outcomes. Regression coefficients in a multilevel regression model have a conditional or within‐cluster interpretation. Larsen and colleagues proposed the interval odds ratio (IOR) to quantify the magnitude and the variation of the effect of cluster‐level variables [4, 5]. Merlo and colleagues proposed the proportion of opposed odds ratios (POOR) to quantify heterogeneity in the effect of cluster‐level variables [6, 7]. Finally, Larsen and colleagues proposed the median odds ratio (MOR) as a measure of between‐cluster variation in the outcome [4, 5]. These measures were developed in the context of a 2‐level multilevel logistic regression model (i.e., patients nested within hospitals). In health services research, population and public health research, and in epidemiologic research, there are often more than two levels to the data hierarchy (e.g., patients nested within physicians who in turn are nested within hospitals). In the current paper we have extended the IOR, POOR, and MOR for use with 3‐level multilevel logistic regression models. Our mathematical derivations provide a template for extending these metrics for use with 3‐level Poisson or negative binomial regression for count data, for 3‐level frailty models for the analysis of survival outcomes, or for multilevel data that have more than three levels [14, 15, 16].

We conducted a PubMed search of journal article titles, abstracts, and keywords on October 29, 2025 using the two search terms “median odds ratio” and “interval odds ratio” and identified articles published between January 1, 2000 and December 31, 2024. The annual number of occurrences of these two terms is reported in Figure 1 (in a similar search, no occurrences of the term “proportion of opposed odds ratios” were identified). The annual number of occurrences of “median odds ratio” has grown rapidly since 2010, with a growth rate that is approximately exponential. In contrast to this, the annual number of occurrences of “interval odds ratio” has increased more slowly. We hypothesize that the MOR is used more frequently as investigators are aware of the need to quantify between‐cluster variation in the outcome, while the IOR may be used less frequently as many investigators are unaware of its utility for quantifying the magnitude and the variation of the effect of cluster‐level variables.

In our derivations and in the case study we have focused on settings in which the Level‐1 units are individuals and with one outcome measurement per individual. However, multilevel models can also be used to analyze repeated measurement data. In such a setting, the Level‐1 units are the measurement occasions and the individuals are Level‐2 units. The Level‐2 units could then be nested within Level‐3 units. For example, Level‐1 units could be clinic visits by patients and the binary outcome was whether or not the patient's blood pressure was measured at a given clinic visit. The Level‐2 units are the patients who may then be nested within family practices, which are the Level‐3 units. The methods that we have described can be used when fitting repeated measures logistic regression models.

In the current study we have extended the IOR, POOR, and MOR for use with 3‐level multilevel logistic regression models. There are a several directions for subsequent extensions of our work. First, the IOR, POOR, and MOOR can be extended for use with multilevel logistic regression models in setting with four or more levels to the data hierarchy. Our impression is that, in applied analyses, most multilevel data structures are restricted to either two or three levels. However, in rare instances, investigators may be faced with multilevel structures with four or more levels (e.g., patients nested within physicians nested within hospitals nested within counties). The methods that we have described can be used to extend the definitions of these measures for multilevel structures with four or more levels. While the mathematical calculations would be relatively straightforward, the number of POORs and IORs increases rapidly as the number of levels increases. For instance, consider a four‐level data structure consisting of patients nested within physicians nested within hospitals nested within counties. For a given physician‐level variable (e.g., physician specialty), there would be three IORs and POORs: (i) comparing two physicians within the same hospital (and the same county); (ii) comparing two physicians in different hospitals, but in the same county; (iii) comparing two physicians in different hospitals and in different counties. For a given hospital‐level variable (e.g., hospital volume), there would be two IORs and POORs: (i) comparing two hospitals in the same county; (ii) comparing two hospitals in different counties. For a given county‐level variable (e.g., urban/rural), there would be one IOR and POOR. A second direction for extension is to multilevel logistic regression models that incorporate random slopes in addition to random intercepts. In the context of 2‐level multilevel data, Yarnell and colleagues extended the MOR for use with multilevel logistic regression models with random slopes in addition to random slopes [17]. It would be relatively straightforward to use derivations based on theirs to extend the IOR, POOR, and MOR for use with 3‐level multilevel logistic regression models that incorporated random slopes. A third direction for extension would be to consider multiple membership multilevel data (Level‐1 units can be contained in multiple Level‐2 units or Level‐2 units can be contained in multiple Level‐3 units) or cross‐classified multilevel data (e.g., individuals are nested within neighborhoods and individuals are nested within hospitals, but neither hospitals nor neighborhoods are nested in the other).

In summary, we have extended the definition of the MOR, the IOR, and the POOR for use with 3‐level multilevel logistic regression models. The use of these metrics allows investigators to quantify between‐cluster variation in the outcome and to quantify both the magnitude and heterogeneity of the association between cluster‐level variables and the individual‐level outcome.
