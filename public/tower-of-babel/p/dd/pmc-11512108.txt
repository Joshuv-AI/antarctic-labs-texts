Health literacy of rheumatology patients in three clinics in Australia and its association with sociodemographic and clinical characteristics
Authors: Ted Yi-Eng Tsai, Alexander Podgorski, Anmar Al-Heilfi, Geraldine Hassett, Alexandra Gorelik, Rachelle Buchbinder, Kathryn Gibson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe and compare health literacy (HL) using contrasting instruments of Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine (REALM) and the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ) in patients attending three Australian rheumatology clinics and their associations with sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of rheumatology patients at Liverpool (Sydney) and Canberra public hospital clinics and a private Liverpool clinic using the REALM test and HLQ sociodemographic and clinical questionnaires. One-way analysis of variance and multivariate ordinal and binary logistic regressions were used to examine the association between HL and sociodemographic and clinical factors.ResultsA total of 278 people participated (111 Liverpool public, 112 private, 55 Canberra public clinic). Compared with Canberra, Liverpool patients were older, non-native English speakers, had less than a tertiary education and were more likely to have comorbidities. A total of 40% of Liverpool public, 29% of private and 13% of Canberra patients had suboptimal REALM HL scores (P < 0.05). Lower REALM scores were associated with male gender, <10 years of education, being born outside Australia/New Zealand and English as a secondary language, but was not associated with clinical characteristics. All nine HLQ domain scores did not differ across sites despite intergroup comparisons as stratified by REALM bands. Completing 10 years of schooling was associated with better scores in three HLQ scales: ‘Navigating the healthcare system’ (P = 0.009), ‘Ability to find information’ (P < 0.001) and ‘Reading and understanding information’ (P < 0.001).ConclusionPatient HL as measured by the REALM differed across sites, explained by differences in gender distribution, country of birth, English as the primary language and years of education. HL as measured by the HLQ did not differ across sites and was more informative for understanding differing patient HL needs.

Introduction

Since inception, the notion of health literacy (HL) has continued to broaden [1]. Initially HL was thought to be an extension of literacy and closely related to education levels [2], but more recent conceptions of HL are more complex [3]. HL skills such as the ability to find and interpret health information involves interactions between an individual’s cognitive and social skills, and influences from their education, social and cultural factors.

HL is now increasingly accepted as a key determinant of health and represents a target to improve healthcare inequities and outcomes [3]. Lower levels of HL are implicated in detrimental health behaviours, such as decreased vaccination rates and cancer screening and poorer medication adherence [4, 5]. Lower levels of HL have also been found to disproportionately affect vulnerable and disadvantaged populations, resulting in poorer health outcomes [3] that further widen health inequity.

Various studies have investigated the HL of patients with RA and other rheumatic diseases [6–8]. For example, RA patients with better HL have been found to have lower disease activity and are more likely to be prescribed biologic therapies, while patients with lower HL may be more likely to be prescribed conventional DMARDs and are more likely to be prescribed prednisolone [7]. Several studies across different locations (USA [9], Australia [10, 11], Scotland [12]) have shown worrying low HL prevalence in up to 24% of rheumatology patients, which can impact correct usage of cytotoxic and expensive biologic medications [7]. This emphasizes the need for rheumatologists to address HL as a barrier to effective treatment.

Tools to evaluate HL and its determinants and impacts on wider healthcare outcomes have lagged behind concepts of HL [8]. Early tools to measure HL, such as the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine (REALM), test participants’ reading and pronunciation of health-related words and uses general literacy levels stratified by grade-school equivalents as a proxy for HL [13]. They therefore do not capture broader constructs of HL. In contrast, the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ) was developed using a grounded psychometric approach and includes assessment of both cognitive and social domains of HL [14]. Scale scores across nine domains provide insights into both the strengths and limitations of the respondent’s HL as well as his/her HL needs, with the scores most useful when viewed as an aggregate ‘health literacy profile’ of an individual respondent [14].

The primary aims of this study were to determine whether HL and HL needs differ across three different rheumatology populations in Australia and to examine the association of HL with sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. If HL and HL needs differ by site, then this imply different strategies may be required to address HL needs in order to optimize care and outcomes. A second aim was to determine the correlation between the REALM and the nine scales of the HLQ.

Methods

Ethics

This multicentre cross-sectional study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Australian South Western Sydney Local Health District HREC (EC00136) as a low and negligible risk research project (AU/6/8320111 LPOOL/462, local project number 12/272). Participants signed written informed consent after researchers explained the study protocol and consent form.

Participants and recruitment

We invited consecutive patients attending outpatient general rheumatology clinics in two Australian states: Liverpool Hospital and a private Liverpool practice (both in Sydney) and the Canberra Hospital (Canberra). Liverpool is in an area of Sydney with lower socio-economic status compared with Canberra. Patients attending private practice in Liverpool are more likely to have higher socio-economic status compared with patients attending Liverpool Hospital. In all settings, patients in the waiting room were approached individually by either a Sydney- or Canberra-based clinician-researcher at designated weekly clinics over 3 months in 2015. There were no exclusion criteria.

Data collection

Consenting participants self-completed sociodemographic and medical history questionnaires and the HLQ, with assistance from accompanying family, an interpreter and/or the researcher if required. To reduce participation bias, e.g. due to limited English or literacy, all patients were invited to participate as much or as little as they wished. Individualized support was provided to maximize participation, including the use of a private research room, help in reading and completing questionnaires and the use of oral and/or written translation tools. Patient companions could provide assistance in completing the questionnaires and we used interpreters as needed.

Completed questionnaires were checked for data quality and completeness by the researcher at the time of data collection. Participants were asked to provide any missing data or clarify unclear responses. Diagnoses and comorbidities were corroborated from the participant’s medical records. If patients consented, the REALM was then administered by the researcher.

Health literacy assessment

REALM

The REALM has been widely used and is known for its ease of use [15]. It scores participants’ ability to read aloud 66 health-related words with correct pronunciation. Test words increase in complexity. Each word is scored as either a 0 if incorrectly pronounced or the respondent is unable to pronounce it, or as a 1 for correct pronunciation (total score 0–66 points).

Scores are categorized into four REALM bands based on standardized school language literacy skills, with REALM scores <30 classified as ‘≤3rd grade’, 31–44 as ‘4th–6th grade’, 45–60 as ‘7th–8th grade’ and 61–66 as ‘≥high school’. Scores <61 are considered suboptimal HL.

HLQ

The HLQ consists of 44 items across nine independent conceptually distinct HL domains (Table 1). Scores for each scale are the average of item scores within the scale. A higher score reflects an individual’s higher HL with respect to that domain and an aggregated score is not used [14].

HLQ domains and scores

The HLQ has broad applicability and has been used in various disease populations and multiple nationalities [16, 17], with demonstrated robust consistency across 15 languages [18]. In the rheumatology literature, the HLQ has been limited to studies of RA [3, 6, 7, 10], gout [3], SpA [3, 19], low back pain [20] and osteoporosis [21]. They include cross-sectional studies showing a high prevalence of low HL [10]; correlations between HL, disease activity and treatment differences [7]; and discordance between clinician estimates and patient’s self-assessment of HL [19], but no significant HL differences between rheumatic diagnoses [3].

Statistical analysis

Demographic and health-related characteristics of the study cohort were summarized using n (%) for categorical data, mean (s.d.) for age and HLQ subdomain scores and median interquartile range (IQR)] for follow-up duration and REALM scores. The examined demographic and clinical factors are listed in Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online.

Differences in patient sociodemographic and HL data across the three sites, as well as between the Liverpool (both sites combined) and Canberra sites, were assessed using either chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for age and HLQ subdomain scores and the Kruskal–Wallis test for follow-up duration and REALM scores. For data analysis, REALM scores were stratified in bands representative of school-grade literacy, as per convention and specified in the REALM scoring. The top quartile for each HLQ scale was calculated to identify those with better HL.

Since not all participants completed the REALM questionnaire, the differences between those who completed the REALM and those who did not were assessed to examine systematic differences between groups. This was done using chi-squared for categorical data, two-sample t-test for age and Mann–Whitney U-test for disease duration and frequency of medical review.

To identify independent socio-economic and clinical predictors of better HL as reflected by HLQ thresholds, multivariate binary logistic regression was used. Similarly, socio-economic and clinical predictors of better HL based on REALM bands were determined using multivariate ordinal logistic regression.

To compare differences between HL as described by REALM vs HLQ, correlation between REALM and HLQ domain scores were assessed using Spearman’s correlation with Bonferroni correction for multiplicity. As education was the most significant independent factor for HL identified in both the REALM and HLQ, the association between education level and HL was assessed using Fisher’s exact test for REALM bands and one-way ANOVA for HLQ scales. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 21 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Stata 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The overall level of significance for the study was set at P < 0.05. Missing data were excluded from analysis.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 374 patients were invited to participate in the study. Ninety-six declined participation, 66 (70%) due to poor English and the remainder (n = 30) for other reasons such as time constraints. The final sample comprised 278 participants (response rate 74%): 112 Liverpool private clinic, 111 Liverpool public clinic, 55 Canberra public clinic. Mean age was 52.8 years (S.D. 15), 70% female. More females attended the Liverpool private compared with Liverpool public clinics (79% vs 63%; P = 0.011). Compared with the private clinic, Liverpool public clinic patients had a higher prevalence of self-reported disability (26% vs 13%; P = 0.017) and Australian concession healthcare cards (61% vs 45%; P = 0.013), which is granted to those with government certified disability or low-income deemed eligible for discounted medicines and healthcare access due to poor socio-economic status.

Compared with Canberra participants, Liverpool participants were on average older [mean 53.8 years (s.d. 15.5) vs 49.0 (s.d. 14.9)], more likely to be born overseas (57% vs 16%), less likely to speak English at home (81% vs 96%) and less likely to have finished tertiary education (48% vs 70%). Liverpool participants were less likely to have private health insurance (27% vs 42%) and more likely to have healthcare concession cards (53% vs 29%). There were no significant differences among internet access at home, disease duration or frequency of medical review.

Liverpool participants had fewer inflammatory diseases (RA, psoriatic arthritis, SpA, CTDs and vasculitides) (Liverpool private 54%, Liverpool public 68%, Canberra public 91%), and an increased prevalence of comorbidities (Liverpool private 26%, Liverpool public 25%, Canberra public 11%) and self-reported disability (Liverpool private 14%, Liverpool public 27%, Canberra public 9%).

Health literacy

The REALM was completed by 238 participants [86% of 278 participants: 91 (82%) Liverpool public, 94 (84%) Liverpool private, 53 (96%) Canberra public]. Forty (14%) participants, all from non-English-speaking backgrounds, declined due to inadequate basic English reading literacy. Those who completed the REALM were younger [mean age 51.7 years (s.d. 15.0) vs 59.5 (16.9)] and a greater proportion had a university degree (57% vs 26%) and internet access (78% vs 53%). A greater proportion also reported English was their primary language (90% vs 48%) and they were born in Australia or New Zealand (59% vs 5%) and a lower proportion had a healthcare concession card (43% vs 79%) (Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online).

The median REALM score was 62 (IQR 58–65) for Liverpool patients and 66 (IQR 64–66) for Canberra patients (P = 0.001) (Table 3). Compared with Canberra participants, a greater proportion of Liverpool participants had suboptimal HL [36 (40%) Liverpool public, 27 (29%) Liverpool private, 7 (13%) Canberra public; P = 0.004].

Actual education levels and REALM

Completing 10 years of schooling was associated with better scores in three HLQ scales: ‘Navigating the healthcare system’ (P = 0.009), ‘Ability to find information’ (P < 0.001) and ‘Reading and understanding information’ (P < 0.001). Completing higher education was marginally associated with higher ‘Reading and understanding health information’ HLQ scores [OR 1.8 (95% CI 1.0–3.6)], Supplementary Fig. S2 (available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online).

Discussion

Our multicentre cross-sectional study found significant HL differences between three rheumatology populations in Australia, related to sociodemographic but not rheumatic disease or healthcare differences. Geographically, there was greater socio-economic disadvantage and more immigrant patients in Liverpool and greater inflammatory disease prevalence in Canberra. The prevalence of suboptimal HL, as measured by the REALM, was significantly higher among Liverpool participants, while there were no important differences in HL as measured by the nine HLQ scales across the three sites.

Intercity REALM differences were independently explained by tertiary education, being born in Australia/New Zealand and having English as the primary language, all of which were more prevalent in Canberra participants. These intercity sociodemographic differences are reflective of the relative socio-economic advantage and disadvantage for these two cities as determined by socio-economic indices for areas [22].

Rheumatic disease and other healthcare factors were not significant determinants of HL.

Our results highlight the complex interplay between socio-economic status, education level and HL. While education level was a significant independent variable for better REALM scores—with completion of 10 years of schooling a significant threshold—we found that using education level as a surrogate for HL would misclassify 20–50% of rheumatology patients, both over- and underestimating HL.

In an integrative review exploring these relationships, education level was identified as the most important socio-economic factor contributing to HL, but it also acknowledged that the link between socio-economic status and health, mediated by HL, was not fully understood [23]. This is highlighted in other studies that found that using education as a proxy for a patient’s HL can be misleading [10]. This is postulated to be due to differences in cognitive abilities and factors outside of schooling years also being important in HL [24]. Compared with education, HL may also be a better predictor of disease outcomes, such as measured by disease activity scores (DAS-ESR) in RA, which were consistently poorer in patients with worse HL, but independent of patients’ education level [7].

The validity of the REALM as a surrogate measure of HL has also been questioned [25]. Our study demonstrated the novel finding that REALM score was moderately correlated with the ‘Reading and understanding health information’ HLQ scale, but only minimally correlated with other domains of HLQ. Based on the ability to read and recognize health-related words, REALM is a surrogate measure for language-based HL. In Nutbeam’s classification of HL [26], this only reflects a patient’s ‘functional health literacy’, which comprises the foundational basic reading, writing and numeracy skills required to understand and use health information in everyday situations. REALM is not informative about a patient’s ‘interactive health literacy’—their ability to effectively communicate with healthcare providers—nor assess ‘critical health literacy’—their ability to apply critical thinking skills in analysing quality health information from various sources or make informed decisions about their health.

In contrast, the HLQ was developed using systematic grounded methods and a validity-driven approach. It comprises nine scales that independently reflect important domains of HL, based on a wide range of lived experiences of people attempting to understand, access and use health information and health services [27]. It also provides an important reflection about the quality of health and social service provision. This likely explains the poor overall correlation between the two instruments. Moreover, the HLQ appears not to be affected by education and socio-economic factors, as demonstrated by the very similar results across the Liverpool and Canberra cohorts, despite their obvious sociodemographic differences.

Study strengths and limitations

Our study described the socio-economic characteristics and HL of patients attending two contrasting public hospital clinics in Liverpool and Canberra and a private clinic in Liverpool. All sites are metropolitan, so our results may not apply to patients attending regional and rural rheumatology clinics.

We attempted to address sample bias by approaching all patients in person in the waiting rooms and captured reasons for non-participation. A quarter of patients declined participation, most notably due to poor English. Our results may not be generalizable to those with poor basic English-language skills.

Participant numbers also differed between Liverpool and Canberra. This may have potentially skewed data where subpopulation numbers were small (e.g. lowest REALM and HLQ quartiles stratified by site), and we were unable to meaningfully analyse these correlations.

Implications for practice

Due to great population and individual HL differences, and its recognized effects on disease and treatment outcomes in rheumatology, HL should be measured in all patients as a fundamental component of patient assessment and management [28, 29]. Formal HL assessment is needed, as clinician estimations are often wrong and education level is a poor proxy. Our study demonstrated the utility of the HLQ in providing information across nine independent scales about patient HL skills as well as their needs. This information can be used to individualize treatment and optimize care and outcomes.

Future directions

HLQ results are more meaningful when looking at domain scores as cluster patterns, akin to an HLQ ‘fingerprint’ of distinct domain profiles, rather than sample aggregates of individual domain scores [14]. We intend to identify unique HLQ profiles and patterns in this study using cluster analysis and examine their associated socio-economic factors and impacts on disease activity using data collected from the Routine Assessment of Patient Index Data 3, a multidimensional health assessment questionnaire instrument.

Conclusion

There are significant sociodemographic and clinical differences between patients attending for rheumatology care in different settings. Patients with lower socio-economic status tended to have a lower ability to correctly pronounce health-related words, explained by being born overseas, having a primary language other than English and having less education. Measuring HL using the HLQ provides useful information about a person’s HL skills, including the ability to find, understand and use health information and navigate the healthcare system, independent of socio-economic factors. These skills are important for health and managing chronic illness. Identifying difficulties in these HL abilities provides useful information to clinicians that can help them to optimize care and meet the HL needs of their patients.
