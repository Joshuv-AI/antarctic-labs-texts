Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging in neuroblastoma chemotherapy: a narrative review
Authors: Xiao Lu, Zhao Liu, Yuewen Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroblastoma (NB) is a very heterogeneous childhood cancer with its own problems in both the treatment and the assessment of therapeutical responses. The current study has identified the recent application of multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in measuring efficacy of chemotherapy in NB. The typical conventional MRI is based on the alteration in tumor size and signal density, however, it is affected by tissue changes caused by post-chemotherapy processes like necrosis and fibrosis, which degrades its accuracy. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) represent advanced functional MRI techniques that provide quantitative information regarding cellular density, water diffusion, and microcirculation perfusion and, therefore, can be used to early measure tumor responses. The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) of diffusion mode is linked to tumor differentiation and tumor therapy outcomes, where increasing values of the ADC following chemotherapy are an indicator of positive response. Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) further adds value to testing by measuring the changes in hemodynamics. Although these benefits exist, issues still exist such as the necessity to implement uniform imaging guidelines, ensure parameter repeatability and sample size limitations. This narrative review is intended to provide clinicians and researchers with a comprehensive overview of the current evidence and to propose future directions for prospective multicenter studies. Multimodal MRI, as a non-invasive tool, plays a crucial role in assessing the response, stratifying the risk, and individualizing treatment plans for neuroblastoma.Combination of structural, functional, and metabolic MRI information promises to improve the diagnostic accuracy and prognostic accuracy in pediatric cancer.Keywords: Neuroblastoma, Multimodal MRI, Chemotherapy response evaluation, Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), Tumor microenvironment, Non-invasive imaging, Biomarkers, Pediatric oncology

Background and clinical challenges

Neuroblastoma (NB) is a childhood cancer originating from immature nerve cells, primarily in infants and young children, originating in the sympathetic nervous system and can also occur in the adrenal gland [1, 2]. NB, which is four times more prevalent than brain tumors in children, is also among the commonest of tumors of the peripheral nervous system [3]. Investigations and management of neuroblastoma has great difficulties because of its heterogeneity in the etiology and clinical manifestation [2, 4]. NB makes about 7–10% of the ratio of all cancer in childhood with different age groups showing varying incidence. It reaches its peaks in children aged 4–5 years of age and 90% of the cases are diagnosed when one is under the age of 2 [5].

Neuroblastoma is a condition that is usually treated with multiple disciplinary approach to enhance patient survival by the use of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy among other therapeutic interventions [6–10]. Chemotherapy is the primary treatment modality for high-risk patients, but accurately evaluating its therapeutic efficacy has remained a clinical challenge. Traditional assessment relies on tumor size changes; however, chemotherapy-induced necrosis, fibrosis, and inflammatory responses often interfere with judgment, leading to inaccurate evaluation [11, 12]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for an imaging method that can early, non-invasively, and quantitatively assess changes in the tumor microenvironment, such as cell density, perfusion, and metabolism—this is precisely the value of multi-modal MRI.

Neuroblastoma is a disease with clinical manifestations that depend on the age of the patient, location of the tumor, type of histologic sediment, and stage of the disease [13–15]. The abdomen is the most common site, manifesting as abdominal pain or a mass. Other parts, such as the chest and spine, may also exhibit systemic manifestations including pain, fever, fatigue, and weight loss [16]. Histologically, it is classified into neuroblastoma (immature), undifferentiated type, and differentiated (mature) type [17]. In terms of molecular mechanisms, MYCN amplification (observed in 20–25% of patients and associated with poor prognosis), ALK gene mutations, epigenetic alterations, and other factors play critical roles [18–25].

Neuroblastoma is a heterogeneous and multifaceted pediatric tumor, which is responsible for an estimated 12% of cancer associated mortality in children below the age of 15. Although recent advances in combination therapies based on the MAID regimen, as well as immunotherapeutic autologous stem cell transplantation, have demonstrated promising efficacy in treating neuroblastoma [26–30], and significant progress has also been made in gene therapy, targeted therapy, and CAR-T cell therapy for its management [31–40], the prognosis for high-risk patients remains poor [41–44]. In 2017, the International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria (INRC) underwent a revision to provide a standardized way to assess neuroblastoma (NB) [45]. This revision incorporates the input of the primary tumor, soft tissue, bone metastases, bone marrow, which obviates tumor assessment modalities that are not as sensitive and/or specific to neuroblastoma (e.g., bone scintigraphy and catecholamine levels) and replaces them with nuclear imaging modalities (such as I123-MIBG and FDG-PET imaging). The changes enhance the identification of viable neuroblastoma or ganglionic neuroblastoma in soft tissue and bone metastatic and include histopathological assessment to measure bone marrow involvement [46–48]. The chemotherapy evaluation criteria for primary lesions were defined as follows. A complete response (CR) is characterized by a residual primary lesion of 10 mm or less with no uptake of 123I-MIBG or 18FDG. A partial response (PR) is indicated when the sum of the longest diameters of the primary lesion decreases by at least 30%, regardless of whether uptake remains stable, increases, or decreases. Progressive disease (PD) is identified when the sum of the longest diameters increases by 20% or more, with an absolute increase of at least 5 mm compared to the minimum sum recorded during treatment; if the minimum was recorded at baseline, the baseline sum serves as the reference. Stable disease (SD) refers to cases in which the primary lesion shrinks but does not meet the criteria for partial response, nor enlarges enough to qualify as progressive disease. In cases of bilateral adrenal neuroblastoma, the sum of the longest diameters of both lesions is recorded unless biopsy confirms that one lesion is glomerulonephritis (GN) [49–51]. For multifocal non-adrenal neuroblastoma, the largest lesion is considered the primary lesion, while the remaining lesions are classified as metastatic unless biopsy confirms GN. Evaluation of treatment efficacy also encompasses soft tissue and bone metastases, bone marrow involvement, and overall systemic response [52].

In recent years, with the diversification of treatment modalities, the five-year survival rate for patients with neuroblastoma (NB) has improved significantly. Nevertheless, in the high-risks NB patients, despite the use of multimodal therapy which is surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, stem cell transplantation, and immunotherapy, tall term survivability is lower than 50%. Hence, to inform clinical diagnosis and treatment, it is important to identify high-risk NB patients as early as possible [53]. Also, reduction of the negative effects of the treatment and the quality of life improvement are significant goals during the treatment of non-high-risk NB patients. The most widely accepted risk stratification that is accepted by most countries is the COG (Child Oncology Group) risk stratification, introduced by the American Children Oncology Group [54], and these groups consist of low-risk, intermediate-risk and high-risk NB. In this system, several aspects are involved such as the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) stage, assessment of age, amplification of the Myc gene (MYCN), the international NB pathology classifications, and the DNA ploidy [55]. Out of these, only one of them, namely age, is not very invasive, and the majority of the outcomes can be received only after surgery. Therefore, non-invasively obtaining risk stratification for NB after imaging examinations and before acquiring pathological specimens will play a crucial role in further diagnosis and treatment [56]. Multimodal magnetic resonance, combined with various quantitative parameters of structural, functional, and metabolic MRI information, as a non-invasive tool, plays a crucial role in evaluating the response, risk stratification, and personalized treatment planning of neuroblastoma, providing new directions for future clinical research.

Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging

Conventional MRI sequences have extremely high soft tissue contrast and resolution, and can clearly distinguish structures such as blood vessels, nerves, and muscles, especially in complex regions such as the chest wall and mediastinum [57]. It has no ionizing radiation and is suitable for children, pregnant women, and patients who need long-term follow-up [58, 59]. It can perform multi-parameter and multi-sequence imaging, and can provide anatomical morphology and functional status information simultaneously.Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging refers to the comprehensive evaluation of the pathological and physiological states of tissues or organs by integrating multiple MRI techniques such as structural imaging, functional imaging, and metabolic analysis across different dimensions. The core of this approach lies in combining various sequences or modalities, including T1- and T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced Magnetic Resonance Imaging (DCE-MRI), magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), and perfusion imaging techniques such as arterial spin labeling (ASL), and so on, to provide complementary diagnostic information [60].

MRI manifestations of neuroblastoma include the following: T1-weighted images demonstrate low to moderate signal intensity, with areas of hemorrhage potentially appearing as high signal intensity. T2-weighted images show high signal intensity, with necrotic regions exhibiting even higher signals. Contrast-enhanced scans reveal marked marginal enhancement, while the central necrotic zone shows no enhancement. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI): Due to the high cellular density of neuroblastoma, DWI shows restricted diffusion, particularly in the central and necrotic areas. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS): Neuroblastomas may exhibit alterations in specific metabolites, such as elevated lactate, reflecting higher malignancy [61]0.3.Nuclear medicine imaging: Neuroblastoma can take up iodine-131–labeled MIBG [62]. MIBG imaging is useful for detecting metastatic lesions throughout the body. It allows for more accurate assessment of tumor metastasis, particularly in the skeleton and bone marrow [63–66].

It possesses numerous core advantages.Multimodal parameters for comprehensive assessment combine anatomical and functional imaging to simultaneously display tissue morphology(such as tumor boundaries)and functional status (such as blood flow perfusion and metabolic activity). Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced Magnetic Resonance Imaging (DCE-MRI) can evaluate the permeability of vasculature in neuroblastoma, revealing heterogeneity within the tumor (Fig. 1). The multimodal parameters (Ktrans+R2*+T1) validated in this animal model can be utilized to discriminate between different genotypes of neuroblastoma [67].magnetic resonance angiography(MRA) for assessing arterial stenosis or thrombosis [68, 69]. T1/T2-weighted imaging reveals lesions, whereas diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) analyses damage to white matter tracts [70, 71], Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) can detect metabolic abnormalities (such as elevated choline, indicating tumor proliferation) [72]. Certain functional MRI techniques can generate quantitative parameters to evaluate tissue status. For instance, the apparent diffusion coefficient(ADC) derived from DWI assesses the degree of water diffusion restriction, serving as an indirect indicator of tissue cellularity [73]. IVIM perfusion parameters could distinguish between malignant childhood tumor types, providing potential imaging biomarkers for their diagnosis [74]. Studies have shown that multimodal magnetic resonance imaging can also support treatment decisions and follow-up, such as assessing myocardial viability [75] and postoperative tumor evaluation [76].

Multimodal MRI markedly improves the sensitivity and specificity of disease diagnosis by integrating structural, functional, and metabolic information, particularly in complex lesions—including tumors, inflammatory processes, and metabolic disorders—where it offers irreplaceable advantages. Multiple advantages make it a crucial tool in the era of precision medicine [77].

Oncology chemotherapy tends to impair the validity of the efficacy of treatment measurement with tumor necrosis, interstitial fibrosis and inflammatory responses. MRI has a high rate of soft tissue resolution thus making it a useful technique in the diagnosis, staging and evaluation of therapeutic response in neuroblastoma. Over the past years, following the introduction of technology, more functional MRI methods have been used to measure the effectiveness of treatment. These functional strategies can identify any alterations in the tumor microenvironment prior and after therapy to give understanding on the tumor biological behavior, and assist clinical decision-making which can help change therapy and enhance patient prognosis. Some of the currently popular techniques encompass diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), incoherent motion intravoxel (IVIM), dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) [78], T1 mapping, T2 mapping, and PET/MRI [79–83]. The purpose of the article is to revise the current state of both conventional and functional MRI methods in the treatment efficacy of neuroblastoma treatment to provide the future research direction.

Application of magnetic resonance technology in the efficacy assessment of neuroblastoma chemotherapy

Conventional MRI: Conventional MRI remains the most widely used technique for assessing tumor treatment response, primarily through quantitative and qualitative evaluations of changes in tumor size and signal intensity within and around the lesion before and after therapy [84]. In clinical practice, based on solid tumor efficacy evaluation standards, measuring changes in tumor size via CT or MRI before and after chemotherapy remains the primary method for assessing treatment efficacy. Although many guidelines emphasize the importance of tumor size changes for efficacy evaluation, pathological alterations induced by neuroblastoma chemotherapy such as tumor necrosis, interstitial fibrosis, edema, and inflammatory responses often interfere with accurate assessment of tumor volume, morphology, and involvement of adjacent vasculature. Relying solely on tumor size changes as a measure of solid tumor response may even lead to overestimation of inoperability after chemotherapy, potentially causing patients who could have undergone curative resection to miss the optimal surgical window. Consequently, the sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy of conventional MRI for detecting treatment-related responses are relatively limited, constraining its clinical utility [11, 12].With technological advancements, an increasing number of functional MRI techniques now enable multiparametric quantitative analyses of tissue cellular structures, tumor stroma, degree of fibrosis, and functional metabolism [85]. These approaches allow for early assessment of neuroblastoma response to chemotherapy, addressing many of the limitations inherent to conventional MRI.

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI): DWI is an example of noninvasive functional imaging, which quantitatively measures the diffusion of water molecules within tissues by the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC). Some factors that affect the ADC value include cellular density of the tissue, cell membrane integrity, extent of extra cellular matrix fibrosis and the viscosity of the fluid. The high nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio, as well as the high-density of the tumor-cells in neuroblastoma, limits the water molecule movement [86–88]. This gives higher signal intensity of DWI with high b values, which is the low value of ADC compared with tissues where water can freely diffuse [89]. The values of ADC are different with the various histopathological subtypes of neuroblastoma which have an upward trend starting with neuroblastoma up to ganglioneuroblastoma and ganglioneuroma [90, 91]. Wen [92] compared the use of DWI to determine the presence of tumor cell differentiation in patient with nodular and mixed-subtype ganglioneuroblastoma. The authors discovered that values of ADC were lower in nodular ganglioneuroblastoma compared to mixed subtype due to severity and mitoticnuclear index of nodular type being high. Moreover, values of the ADC of undifferentiated or poorly differentiated neuroblastomas were lower than the values of differentiated tumors. These results reveal that DWI is a good, noninvasive diagnostic tool of assessment and initial evaluation of pediatric neuroblastoma [93]. In an article that assesses the effectiveness of chemotherapy in the treatment of neuroblastoma patients using diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), researchers have evaluated the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value of the abdominal and pelvic neuroblastoma patients before and after chemotherapy [94]. They indicated that post-treatment the values of ADC went remarkably high. Neubauer et al. [91] also compared the ratios of signal intensity of DWI with that of contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging before and after chemotherapy. In spite of the fact that the signal intensity ratios of both modalities declined, DWI showed higher signal intensity ratios than contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging, which means that DWI not merely measures the effectiveness of neuroblastoma chemotherapy but has a better ability to reflect the characteristics of tumor (Fig. 2). Privitera et al. [87] in another study also compared the correlation between ADC values and MIBG uptake in patients with high-risk neuroblastoma before and after chemotherapy stating that the 25th percentile of ADC values after chemotherapy was accurate in identifying regions of nonviable tumor cells. These results will imply that the determination of the ADC values following chemotherapy can contribute to the active surgical resection [95]. Regarding prognostic prediction, Peschmann et al. [96] compared initial presentations and tumor ADC values at a 3-month follow-up and found that lower baseline ADC values were associated with malignant progression and neuroblastoma recurrence. Furthermore, during treatment, ADC values correlated with patient event-free survival, with decreasing ADC values indicating poorer prognosis. Collectively, these results suggest that ADC values may have potential as predictors of patient outcomes [97, 98].

IVIM Technique: Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) is a dual-exponential model of diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) based on multiple b values, which can separate diffusion from perfusion [74, 99] (Fig. 3). It quantifies the true diffusion coefficient (D), pseudodiffusion coefficient (D*), and perfusion fraction (f), providing a more accurate reflection of water molecule diffusion and perfusion status in pathological tissues than conventional DWI. By doing this, the method increases sensitivity and accuracy in the evaluation of some pathway characteristics of neuroblastoma including the extent of fibrosis [100]. The D value depicts the factual diffusion of water molecules excluding the effect of blood flow perfusion. D* is indicative of microcirculation perfusion and it inverts proportionally with the mean velocity of blood flow within the microvessel [101]. The f value is the ratio between microcirculation perfusion and the total diffusion which can be measured as the relative contribution of the D and D*. This research has shown that reduced pretreatment D values predict reduced overall and disease-free survival and increased chances of local recurrence. This could be due to the fact that low values of D translate to high cell density of the tumor and interstitial fibrosis, which enhance the local invasiveness, malignancy and resistance to chemotherapy. The treatment of the tumor cells by chemotherapy brings about necrosis and apoptosis accompanied by different levels of interstitial fibrosis, edema and inflammatory reactions and therefore producing extra and associate intercellular spaces and elevated D values. But then the selection of b values, noise, and the unsteadiness of tumor vascular systems are more prone to affect the D* and f values, thus, creating weakness to their stability and reproducibility. The applicability of such parameters in measuring the efficacy of treatment is therefore a debatable issue that needs subsequent investigation and correctification. Traditional DWI uses the assumption that the distance between molecules in water is diffused under a Gaussian distribution hence producing mono exponential decay of DWI patterns as the b increases. However, due to the substantial heterogeneity of tumor tissues, water diffusion does not always conform to a simple Gaussian distribution [102]. Consequently, diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI), which accounts for non-Gaussian water diffusion, has been proposed. Key indices measured in DKI typically include kurtosis and diffusion coefficients. Although the application of DKI in pediatric thoracic and abdominal neuroblastoma has not yet been reported, it has been shown to correlate significantly with the histopathological features of olfactory neuroblastoma. Xiao et al. [103] reported that kurtosis values based on DKI were significantly higher in patients with olfactory neuroblastoma compared with those with nasal squamous cell carcinoma, with areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.87 and a kurtosis-based diagnostic accuracy of 80% for differentiating the two tumor types. Further studies are warranted to investigate whether IVIM-DWI and DKI can provide additional clinical value for the diagnosis and risk stratification of neuroblastoma compared with conventional DWI [104].

T1 mapping and T2 mapping Techniques: T1 mapping and T2 mapping are quantitative MRI techniques that directly measure tissue T1 and T2 relaxation times, providing more objective information on tissue characteristics compared with conventional qualitative assessments. These techniques show potential value in the evaluation of neuroblastoma. T1 mapping quantifies tissue T1 relaxation times, reflecting changes in tissue composition. Pathological alterations following chemotherapy in neuroblastoma, such as necrosis and fibrosis, can affect T1 values. Studies suggest that effective treatment may be manifested as changes on T1 mapping, associated with decreased cellularity and expanded extracellular space [82]. Moreover, native T1 mapping (without contrast agent) has demonstrated potential in monitoring responses to MYCN-targeted therapies, offering a novel perspective for treatment evaluation. T2 mapping assesses alterations in the water molecular environment within tissues by measuring T2 relaxation times [105]. The necrosis and the edema which are prevalent in neuroblastoma at the end of chemotherapy have a great effect on T2 values. Long T2 values can be indicative of tissue edema or liquid foci necrosis, whereas shorter T2 values can be indicative of fibrosis or deposition of hemosiderin. This numerical data helps to identify the treatment-related alterations and the presence of the tumor tissue left over. In spite of the fact that T1 and T2 mapping are still in the experimental stages of their application in neuroblastoma, the quantitative parameters that they give a prospective of standardized assessment and therapy monitoring. Subsequent researches are necessary to confirm the clinical worth of the particular techniques and set up diagnostic limits of the techniques to determine the treatment response in neuroblastoma.

Multimodal MRI is a composite technique of the conventional MRI, functional MRI and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI), resulting in a composite imaging technique that provides structural, functional and metabolic data of tumors. The resulting expanded analysis enables a better assessment of the tumors, enhancing the detection and diagnostic accuracy of the tumors, as well as helping the clinicians to develop more specific treatment plans. Individualized treatment may be embraced based on the imaging data to optimize the effectiveness of treatments depending on the specificity of a particular tumor. Nonetheless, multimodal MRI has some drawbacks such as the fact that it is expensive as well as complex regarding the operation of imaging modalities and that the combination of several imaging processes can be challenging in cases of neuroblastoma in children. In addition, multimodal data integration and analysis demands a large amount of computing resources and specialized skills [94, 106]. Prechemodal MRI: Multimodal MRI provides a comprehensive assessment of the tumor before starting chemotherapy. It allows for accurate measurement of the tumor’s size and precise determination of its location, which are essential for designing an effective chemotherapy plan. Additionally, it evaluates local invasiveness by assessing whether the tumor has spread to surrounding tissues or organs, helping to determine the feasibility and safety of surgery. The examination also includes an assessment of lymph node involvement, which is important for predicting the patient’s prognosis.

Response to chemotherapy is detected: frequently, following the chemotherapy, regular multimodal magnetic resonance scans (MR) can be conducted to monitor the tumor response. Key Monitoring Indicators: Monitoring tumor response involves several important factors. Changes in tumor volume, assessed by comparing pre- and post-chemotherapy measurements, provide a direct indicator of treatment efficacy. ADC values derived from DWI serve as valuable markers of tumor cell response, with increases generally reflecting cell apoptosis and positive treatment response, while decreases may signal the development of treatment resistance. Additionally, DCE-MRI can evaluate changes in tumor blood flow and vascular permeability following chemotherapy, offering insights into tumor activity as well as drug delivery and treatment effectiveness.

Long-Term Surveillance and Recurrence: Long-term follow-up is a critical component of neuroblastoma management. Multimodal MRI serves as an invaluable tool for detecting early signs of recurrence and monitoring tumor status over time. By comparing new MR images with baseline scans, recurrence can be identified accurately. In cases of recurrence, analyzing changes in tumor biological characteristics—including significant shifts in ADC values can provide important information regarding chemotherapy resistance.

Challenges

The benefits of MRI include multiparametric imaging at free angles, lack of ionization radiation, and the high number of soft tissue resolution that allow repeatability, noninvasiveness, and early quantitative evaluation of the tumor cell structures, tissue fibrosis, and tumor microenvironment changes in neuroblastoma. MRI has proven to be better in measuring solid tumor efficacy than the conventional ones, however, some limitations are possible. To start with, currently, the samples of the available studies are small and rely largely on the retrospective analysis of single sequences. This should be overcome by increasing sample size and undertaking prospective, multimodal combined measurements in future studies. Second, the protocol does not increase the norms of the method applied to the selection of b values, which could undermine the reproducibility and stability of DWI and IVIM parameters. Additional guidelines ought to be standardized on the protocols of scanning of neuroblastoma. Third, ROIs definition may affect parameter indices stability, which may bias the overall evaluation of treatment efficacy because of the local histological heterogeneity. This should be done in the future in order to optimise measurement and analysis in order to have better diagnostic accuracy. Fourth, variability in the time of the posttreatment imaging, especially in terms of standard uptake values (SUVs) in PET/MRI, leads one to not only stress the importance of using early efficacy evaluations, but also to consider the possible false increase. The need to conduct further research to explain the best evaluation time points is apparent. Fifth, imaging assessment results may be affected by the absence of objective and standardized criteria of pathological assessment after chemotherapy. More standardization of the assessment criteria should be embraced in clinical practice because it would allow making more reliable correlations between the imaging findings and the histopathology.

Overall, the strengths and weaknesses of all the assessment parameters at present allow the multimodal MRI techniques to give the more detailed data about tumor metabolism, blood perfusion, and peritumoral effects, which enhances the accuracy of diagnostics in case of multimodal MRI in comparison to other single modalities. Finally, in the future, with the further evolution of functional MRI sequences and the further researches in multiparametric MRI, a complete neuroblastoma assessment system based on MRIs might be developed, which will provide more valuable and objective evidence of the efficacy of the chemotherapy in patients with neuroblastoma in the early noninvasive stages.
