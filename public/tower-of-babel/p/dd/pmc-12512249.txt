Tracking implementation determinants over time using the IFASIS: multi-site analysis of opioid treatment programs implementing a digital contingency management platform
Authors: Allyson L. Dir, Briana M. Patrick, Sarah Salino, Kira DiClemente-Bosco, Sara J. Becker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContingency management (CM) is the most effective treatment for stimulant use, but is underutilized due to implementation challenges. Digital CM platforms have shown promise in addressing barriers to implementation, but there is limited understanding of factors impacting delivery of a digital CM platform over time. The goal of this longitudinal study was to measure change in implementation determinants affecting uptake of a digital CM platform in opioid treatment programs (OTPs) for individuals who are stimulant users. The study used a novel assessment tool called the Inventory of Factors Affecting Successful Implementation and Sustainment (IFASIS) to compare implementation determinants across OTPs and measure changes in determinants over a 6-month implementation period.MethodsFive OTPs in Rhode Island completed the IFASIS prior to and 6 months into implementation of a digital CM platform. OTP staff completed the IFASIS as a team. Twenty-seven items measuring external context, internal context, intervention factors, and recipient factors were each rated on the impact of the factor on implementation (scores 1–2 = barrier, 3 = neutral, 4–5 = facilitator) and the importance of the factor (1 = not important to 3 = very important). Teams also verbalized the rationale for their ratings. Interviews were recorded and transcribed for rapid qualitative analysis. Quantitative ratings were recorded and median scores for each item were calculated.ResultsInternal organizational factors such as leadership commitment to implementation and organizational policies were perceived as becoming greater barriers to implementation over time. Staff capacity was perceived as a stable facilitator across sites despite sites experiencing turnover and new hires from baseline to follow-up. Despite initial perceptions of recipient and intervention factors as facilitators, these factors were generally viewed as neutral or as barriers at follow-up. Staff comments revealed unanticipated challenges in referring and engaging patients that contributed to the shift in their ratings.DiscussionFindings underscore the complex and dynamic nature of implementation determinants and highlight the importance of monitoring recipient and intervention factors when implementing digital CM platforms. Results of this study suggest the need to help OTP staff set realistic expectations about the implementation process, particularly with regard to the ease of identifying and engaging patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-025-00783-0.

Background

In order to combat the “fourth wave” of the opioid crisis and the general increase in stimulant use [1], there has been a push to increase access to and use of contingency management (CM), a behavioral treatment in which patients earn incentives for meeting treatment goals. CM is the gold standard treatment for stimulant use disorder and an effective adjunctive treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD) [2, 3]. Unfortunately, CM remains underutilized due to multiple implementation barriers at the system (e.g., restrictive policies preventing CM use), organization (e.g., lack of adequate funding), provider (e.g., stigmatizing views of CM), and intervention (e.g., complexity of the intervention) levels [4–6]. Digital CM platforms have been developed to combat many of these implementation barriers by reducing time- and resource-intensive demands on the clinic workforce, while increasing accessibility for patients through remote drug testing and electronic receipt of incentives. Such platforms have demonstrated effectiveness in both increasing rates of abstinence and treatment engagement [7–9]. While use of digital CM platforms is expanding, the field’s understanding of the factors impacting implementation of digital CM platforms remain understudied. The current study seeks to further explore implementation determinants impacting implementation of digital CM platforms in opioid treatment programs (OTPs) given the common comorbidity in opioid and stimulant use [2, 3].

Implementation determinants are factors that can either hinder or support the uptake of interventions. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) is a widely used determinant framework categorizing determinants across four domains [10]: external factors outside the organization (e.g., system-level policies and community support), factors within the organization implementintg the intervention (e.g., intervention integration into existing workflows), intervention-related factors, and factors related to the recipient (e.g., whether the intervention meets recipient needs). Understanding implementation determinants is critical to addressing barriers to intervention uptake and identifying appropriate implementation strategies to target determinants. However, despite the surge of studies assessing implementation determinants more generally [11], the measurement of this construct has been subject to two key limitations that have reduced impact to the field. First, determinants are often measured in a context-specific, highly localized manner that limits the potential to identify commonalities across studies. Studies often use the CFIR Qualitative Interview Guide [10] to elicit rich qualitative data, yet the absence of quantitative data renders aggregation and comparison across studies challenging. Second, determinants are typically assessed at a specific point in time, such as before the start or at the end of the implementation process. Evaluating determinants at a single time point assumes that determinants are static and limits the ability to capture dynamic changes throughout the implementation process. The Inventory of Factors Affecting Implementation and Sustainment (IFASIS), a quantitative measure developed by the Center for Dissemination and Implementation at Stanford (C-DIAS), was explicitly developed to address these limitations [12]. The IFASIS takes a mixed methods approach to comprehensively assessing implementation determinants. Following the CFIR domains, the IFASIS gathers both qualitative data on determinants for deeper understanding of context as well as quantitative ratings of determinant “scores” to allow for more objective and systematic comparison across OTPs over time.

The current study addressed these critical gaps in implementation determinants by using the IFASIS to examine factors impacting implementation of CM both longitudinally and cross-sectionally. The primary objectives of this study were to longitudinally track implementation determinants over a 6-month implementation period and identify patterns in determinants across a set of OTPs implementing a digital CM platform. By pursuing these complementary objectives, this study was designed to elicit generalizable knowledge regarding determinants of implementation of a digital CM platform in OTPs.

Methods

Study background

The present study was titled Maximizing the Implementation of Motivational Incentives in Clinics 2, or MIMIC2. Funded by the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), MIMIC2 was embedded within The Center for Dissemination and Implementation at Stanford (C-DIAS), a P50 Center of Excellence designed to advance equitable access to evidence-based addiction treatments. Building off the results of Project MIMIC, a 28-site hybrid type 3 cluster-randomized trial testing two multi-level CM implementation strategies [13], MIMIC2 assessed whether an optimized multi-level implementation strategy could help OTPs implement CM targeting stimulant abstinence. Here, we focus on data from OTPs in New England who received opioid settlement funds from the state Department of Behavioral Health to implement an evidence-based digital CM platform. The MIMIC2 team led both the delivery of the multi-level implementation strategy and the multi-level evaluation of the state-wide initiative.

Participating OTPs and IFASIS participants

All 23 OTPs in the state were invited to participate and six (26%) expressed interest. All six were invited to participate. Most of the participating OTPs were for-profit (60%), and patient census levels ranged from 500–850. The percentage of patients with stimulant use ranged from 20 to 85%. All programs predominantly dispensed methadone (85–100% of patients) with a smaller subset of patients receiving buprenorphine (0–15%).

OTPs nominated between 5–18 providers to receive implementation support. All nominated providers at each OTP were invited to complete the IFASIS as a group at baseline and again the end of the 6-month active implementation period. Five of the six OTPs completed a 6-month IFASIS: the five OTPs with both a baseline and follow-up IFASIS are included in the current analysis. The remaining OTP decided not to implement the digital CM platform and withdrew from the implementation initiative, thus, is not included in the manuscript results. Across the five included OTPs, 69 providers participated in at least one aspect of the implementation strategy (described further in the Implementation Support section).

Implementation support

The five OTPs each received a multi-level implementation strategy containing three key elements over the 6-month implementation period: didactic training, performance feedback, and external consultation. Didactic training consisted of a self-paced 1-h module describing the fundamentals of CM delivery, followed by a synchronous 1-h session on how to utilize the digital CM platform. Performance feedback consisted of weekly reports to each OTP summarizing the program’s cumulative referral and engagement rates. Finally, the consultation component involved monthly support calls delivered by a doctoral-level researcher with expertise in CM implementation. The consultation calls aimed to support the implementation of the digital CM platform by identifying and troubleshooting emergent barriers to implementation. The baseline IFASIS was administered in the first monthly support call and the 6-month IFASIS was administered in a separate session after the sixth and final support call.

Measures

The IFASIS is a novel, team-based assessment consisting of 27 items across four major domains: External Factors, Internal Factors, Factors about the Intervention, and Factors about Recipients [12]. External Factors consists of 5 items assessing factors outside the organization: two items relate to external policies and three items relate to community support. Internal Factors consists of 12 items examining factors within the organization: two leadership items, three organizational readiness items, three items related to organizational resources, one item related to feasibility of implementation and expansion, and three items related to person-focused care. Intervention Factors include three individual items related to perceived fit of the intervention with the organization, usability/complexity of the intervention, and relative advantage of the intervention to the current approach. Recipient Factors include 7 items related to the recipient’s needs and opinions: one item about whether the intervention is equitably beneficial to recipients and six items about recipient needs and values and their fit with the intervention. Each item is rated on a scale from 1 to 5 with anchor statements for each value; valence scores of 1–2 indicate barriers (negative valence), 3 indicates a neutral factor, and 4–5 indicates a facilitator (positive valence). Each item is also scored for its level of importance to implementation, from 1 (not important) to 3 (very important). The IFASIS can be self-administered as a team or by a trained facilitator.

For the current study, the IFASIS was administered by a trained facilitator at baseline and follow-up. The MIMIC2 team requested that 3–5 team members from each organization participate in the team-based assessment. Team members could be either leaders who were overseeing the implementation of CM, or providers who were directly referring their patients to the digital CM platform. Team members at each OTP were sent a copy of the inventory via email to preview prior to conducting the assessment. During the interviews, team members were asked to rate each IFASIS item as a team and disucss rationale for ratings (i.e., one team score was recorded for each item rather than individual team member scores). The inventory was completed and recorded using Zoom. The trained facilitator was supported by 1–2 BA level research staff who recorded numerical responses in real time using Qualtrics. At baseline, one OTP opted to complete the IFASIS independently and sent the research team their numerical responses; this OTP’s quantitative ratings, but not their qualitative comments, were included in the analyses (site 105). At follow-up, one OTP discussed and completed the inventory in advance during an internal team meeting, and their site champion summarized the team discussion to the research team; this OTP’s quantitative ratings and the leader’s qualitative feedback were included in the analyses (site 104). The remaining three sites completed the IFASIS and interivew as a team with audio recordings at both baseline and follow-up (sites 102, 103, 106). All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed for analysis.

Quantitative analysis

Impact and importance scores for each determinant were recorded at baseline and follow-up for each site; median scores across OTPs were calculated at both timepoints.

Qualitative analysis

We conducted rapid qualitative analysis on IFASIS transcripts collected during the follow-up interviews. The IFASIS baseline transcripts had been analyzed in a prior study [14]. A summary template and matrix approach was used given the structured nature of the IFASIS measure [15], and we utilized a matrix that our team had developed previously for the IFASIS [14]. Two coders, AD and BP, initially dual coded one transcript utilizing the matrix to ensure interrater reliability. Following this, AD and BP each individually coded the remaining transcripts (2 each). Raters then met to summarize qualitative results from each of the IFASIS domains.

Following separate quantitative and qualitative analysis, data were integrated or merged to guide interpretation [16]. Quantitative scores allowed for objective comparison across time and sites, and qualitative data served a supplementary role, providing further context for scores [16].

External factors

Across OTPs, external factors had the lowest ratings at both baseline and follow-up, such that OTPs rated these factors as barriers to implementing a digital CM platform. Over time, there was a trend for these factors to be perceived as more of a barrier, but as less important (see Table 2 for overview of scores across external factor subdomains).

Community support

Most OTPs rated support, consultation, and partnerships with other community organizations as barriers to implementation at baseline and follow-up. At baseline, most OTPs rated community support and partnerships as important to implementing a digital CM platform; by contrast, at follow-up, these factors were largely seen as “not important in what we do” (106).

External policies

OTPs had mixed perceptions of the extent to which system-level leadership support, defined as support from the Department of Behavioral Health, helped or hindered implementation. At follow-up, two OTPs rated system-level leadership as a facilitator, with one OTP asserting that “without their part on this, we wouldn’t have the program at all” (105). Conversely, two OTPs rated system-level leadership as a barrier, describing the support as insignificant or “non-existent” (103). While all OTPs rated system-level leadership as important early in implementation (median importance = 3), at follow-up, all but one OTP showed a reduction in perceived importance of leadership in implementation (median importance = 2).

Perceptions of system-level policies were far more consistent. All five OTPs viewed system-level policies as a barrier at baseline (median valence = 2) and even more so at follow-up (median valence = 1). In general, OTPs described a desire for more explicit policies, noting “we’re just doing it ourselves” (104). Two OTPs acknowledged that the Department of Behavioral Health had policies that were relevant to service delivery, but indicated they were not specific to digital CM platforms and thus did not have a major impact in this context. Perceived importance decreased between baseline (median importance = 3) and follow-up (median importance = 2).

Internal factors

Internal factors showed the most variability both across OTPs and within OTPs over time. Most OTPs initially rated internal factors as either important facilitators or neutral factors, but there was more variability at follow-up (see Table 2).

Leadership

Perceptions of leadership’s support for training and commitment to implementing a digital CM platform worsened over time. Three OTPs’ perceptions of leadership support shifted from the facilitator to the barrier zone over the 6-month period (median valence at baseline = 4, median valence at follow-up = 2). One OTP explained the shift: “They enrolled us in this program for a reason, but there's also no sustainability policies in place. So, I don't think it's something they don't want us to do, but it's not something that's…high on their end” (102). The importance of leadership support varied across OTPs: two OTPs (103, 104) rated leadership involvement as important, while others described functioning without their support, as one site noted: “It would be nice if they [leadership] had more of an idea of what was going on, but I don't think it impacts our ability and the work that we're doing with it” (105).

Resources

All OTPs rated staff shortage and turnover as a very important facilitator that was mostly stable across time (median valence at baseline and follow-up = 4, median importance at baseline and follow-up = 3). One OTP described recent staff hires as supportive of CM implementation, while other OTPs asserted that they felt well-equipped given they had “qualified” staff.

OTPs’ perception of funding for implementation shifted over time from initially being perceived as a barrier (median valence at baseline = 1) to a neutral factor (median valence at follow-up = 3). OTPs consistently and vocally recognized the importance of having financial capacity for sustainability (median importance at baseline and follow-up = 3) noting “if there's no funding, the program's going to fall flat” (103). Most OTPs recognized that while their organization did not have the financial means to implement the digital CM platform on their own, costs were currently being covered by the state. The cost–benefit of the digital CM platform was rated as a neutral factor with little variability over time (median valence at baseline and follow-up = 3). Two OTPs reported perceived cost–benefit as “to be determined” (103, 105) while two others acknowledged the clear benefit to patients: one noted it as a “great tool if patients use it” (106) and another shared “the patients are thriving on having some kind of reward” (102). At baseline, the cost–benefit was seen as very important (median importance = 3), but shifted to somewhat important at follow-up (median importance = 2).

Organizational readiness

There was significant variability both within OTPs over time and across OTPs with respect to organizational readiness factors. Specific organizational readiness items that consistently demonstrated changes over time across OTPs included implementation supported by policies, integration into existing workflows, staff flexibility/adaptability, and feasibility of implementation. All OTPs indicated that organizational policies to facilitate implementation of the digital CM platform (implementation supported by policies) were lacking or “non-existent”), rating implementation supported by policies as a barrier at baseline (median valence = 2) and even more of a barrier at follow-up (median valence = 1). One OTP noted that the lack of policies was very important, because it made it more likely that they would forget to use the digital CM platform: “it's not something that is…We're not told to implement continuously with every single patient. Unfortunately, it's not at the forefront of our mind when we see our patients…it's kind of put on the back burner” (103). Another OTP similarly viewed the lack of policies as a barrier but doubted its importance, noting “it doesn’t really need a policy” (105).

By contrast, integration into existing workflows and staff flexibility/adaptability were both viewed as facilitators by most OTPs at baseline, and shifted to neutral determinants at follow-up (median valence at baseline = 4, median valence at follow-up = 3). A key driver of the shift in ratings for the integration into existing workflows item was OTP staff’s realization that referring patients to the platform required the creation of separate workflows for patients that had a recent history of stimulant use. For instance, one OTP noted that the digital platform was “not part of our daily routine with our patients.” Similarly, the shift in ratings for staff flexibility/adaptability was driven by OTP staff awareness that some members of their team were quicker to adjust than others. As an example, one OTP acknowledged “we do have some staff that are definitely more understanding of it and kind of grasp onto the idea more quickly than other staff.” OTPs consistently viewed both of these factors as somewhat or very important (median importance of both items at baseline and follow-up = 3).

The exact same dynamic was observed for the perception of feasibility of implementation item, which also shifted from a facilitator to a neutral determinant over the implementation period (median valence at baseline = 4, median valence at follow-up = 3). Of the organizational readiness items, this item generated the most discussion among OTP staff. One OTP explicitly linked the shift in feasibility to the lack of universal organizational policies noting that “if it was something that we already had policies in place, where it was a set program that we were using with every patient…I think it would be more feasible for it to be done” (103). Other OTPs noting feasibility of implementation as a barrier described challenges engaging patients in the digital CM platform (103, 104, 105) both before and after referrals were made. One OTP shared:

I thought it was going to be something that everybody was going to want to participate in. And it doesn't feel like that. It's the people that have actually even participated in it, it was pulling teeth with them to get them to participate. It was frustrating. (104)

I thought it was going to be something that everybody was going to want to participate in. And it doesn't feel like that. It's the people that have actually even participated in it, it was pulling teeth with them to get them to participate. It was frustrating. (104)

Two OTPs attributed the "slower rate” than expected of implementation to the need for more resources to help promote the program, such as more support from the state or designated outreach positions within the organization to engage patients, while another OTP suggested that expanding the program eligibility criteria to patients beyond those who use stimulants might help the program grow.

Digital CM platform factors

At baseline, factors specific to the digital CM platform had the highest ratings across OTPs, such that they were seen as facilitators to implementation of the digital CM platform. Notably, across all OTPs there was a downward trend in OTPs’ ratings at follow-up, with ratings of intervention factors shifting from the facilitator to the neutral or barrier zone.

With respect to ease of use, all OTPs showed a reduction in their rating of ease of the digital CM platform as a facilitator from baseline (median valence = 5) to follow-up (median valence = 3). In general, OTPs described heterogeneity across their patient population in the ability to use the digital CM platform. One OTP noted that some of their patients were comfortable with technology while others were not “technologically savvy” (105). Another OTP described: “It’s mixed. Some patients complain, some patients had no problems” (106). One common challenge related to ease of use pertained to patients’ ability to complete the self-administered drug screens. For instance, one OTP reflected, “it’s [toxicology screens] probably been the most difficult part for the patients. I’m constantly getting emails that my patient missed their drug screen and stuff like that” (102). Meanwhile, other OTPs shared challenges navigating the provider website and portal. Median importance ratings indicated that ease of use was perceived as very important at both baseline and follow-up (median importance = 3), though two OTPs shifted from very important to only somewhat important at follow-up.

More encouragingly, OTPs’ ratings of fit of the digital CM platform with the organization and relative advantage of the digital CM platform were largely stable across OTPs from baseline (median valence = 4) to follow-up (median valence = 4). Both of these items were consistently rated as important to the implementation of the digital CM platform (median importance of both items at baseline and follow-up = 3).

Patient-level factors

While there was variability across OTPs, in general, most OTPs rated patient factors initially as facilitators or neutral factors impacting implementation but showed a decrease in ratings across time.

Perceptions that the digital CM platform is equitably beneficial to recipients and meets patients’ needs both showed a shift in median ratings from baseline (median valence = 5) to follow-up (median valence = 3). The main reason for the downward shift in ratings of equitable benefit was OTP staff discovering that some patients could not access the digital CM platform due to lack of adequate phone technology or Wi-Fi. One OTP observed that only “about half of our patients can actually afford the means to have the equipment to participate in the [digital CM platform]” (103), while another OTP lamented “there’s some patients who just don’t have the technical skills” (102). Similarly, the primary reason for the downward shift in ratings of meets patient needs was OTP staff’s discovery that fewer patients met criteria for recent stimulant use than they had expected. One OTP explained, “we have patients that may benefit from this program that aren't eligible for it because they're not testing positive for a stimulant” (103). Median ratings of both items were very important (median importance = 3) at both time points, though for both items 1–2 OTPs shifted their ratings from very important at baseline to somewhat important at follow-up.

By follow-up, four of the five sites perceived patient interest in digital CM as at least a somewhat important barrier. One site consistently rated this factor as a facilitator, however the importance decreased over time from very important (median importance at baseline = 3) to somewhat important (median importance at follow-up = 2). Another site consistently rated patient interest in digital CM as a very important barrier. The remaining three sites all rated this factor as a facilitator at baseline (either very or somewhat important) to a barrier at follow-up (either very or somewhat important). While many OTPs shared that they thought participants would be motivated and eager to engage at the start, many recognized patients’ lack of awareness of the digital CM platform and sometimes lack of interest despite being referred. As one participant described, “you need [patients] for the program to exist” (103). One participant described the need for more promotion of the program: “they don't necessarily know it's an option, so they're not asking about it. But when we've told them, a lot of them are interested” (102). OTPs described the need for resources in order to put more effort into promoting and marketing the digital CM platform to increase patient awareness.

Discussion

This longitudinal investigation of implementation determinants extends our understanding of factors influencing the implementation of digital CM platforms and advances the field of implementation science, by allowing quantitative identification of common determinants across OTPs and examining shifts in both the valence and importance of determinants over time. While most studies of contextual determinants assess factors qualitatively at a single time point, this study demonstrates the utility of longitudinally tracking and quantifying implementation determinants from beginning to end of a 6-month implementation phase. Notably, results revealed common patterns across OTPs both at each timepoint and over time, indicating that even though determinants at each OTP changed dynamically, there were generalizable trends that could inform the selection and sequencing of implementation strategies. Moreover, while we do discuss some potential connections between strategies and determinants, we do not suggest that multi-level implementation strategy as a causal mechanism of changes in determinants.

The domain that demonstrated the most variability in implementation determinants across OTPs was internal factors, although there were still some common patterns across OTPs. Most OTPs reported a general decrease in valence ratings over time, indicating that factors were increasingly viewed as barriers, in leadership commitment to implementation, feasibility of implementation, and implementation supported by policies. Several OTPs noted that because not all clients were eligible for the digital CM platform (i.e., not all patients used stimulants), it was more challenging than expected to gain leadership buy in and develop organization-wide policies. These data highlight the need to set leadership expectations about implementation upfront and to monitor leadership commitment and other internal factors throughout the implementation process. Organizational readiness and other internal factors, such as culture and climate, are typically only assessed and targeted in early or pre-implementation [17], despite implementation frameworks highlighting the influence of internal factors throughout implementation stages [18]. Our results suggest that internal factors such as organizational climate and culture should be monitored throughout implementation to adjust implementation strategies and increase the likelihood of sustainment. Of note, consultation calls and performance feedbakc strategies that occurred during the active implementaiton period targeted front-line staff rather than leadership. While we cannot infer causal mechanisms of implementation strategies, more ongoing engagement of leadership during the active implementation period could be beneficial. Leadership-level implementation strategies have been shown to be effective in aligning leadership priorities and values with implementation efforts and could be tailored to address emergent barriers and better support implementation efforts [19].

More consistent shifts in ratings across implementation were observed in the intervention and recipient factor domains; in particular, there were consistent shifts related to perceptions of the usability of the digital CM platform and the platform’s ability to equitably benefit patients and meet their needs. While there was initial enthusiasm and promise for the digital CM platform given its accessibility (i.e., ability for patients to engage with the platform from anywhere), the OTPs reported that patients had greater difficulty than expected accessing the appropriate resources (Wi-Fi, smartphones) and that some patients lacked adequate technical skills to utilize the digital CM platform. Challenges related to technology access and literacy have been well-documented as barriers to a range of digital health interventions [20]. These data are consistent with assertions in a recent thought piece by Khazanov and colleagues (2024) that digital CM platforms are likely to offer unique benefits in terms of increased flexibility and reduced burden [7], while introducing unique barriers related to technology [21]. It is possible that patient-focused strategies, such as the provision of resources (i.e., devices that are compatible with the digital software) or support in using the digital CM platform, could help to mitigate these barriers.

Within the recipient factor domains, OTP feedback revealed consistent challenges across OTPs and over time in referring patients to and getting them to engage in the digital CM platform. OTPs described a lack of patient interest and awareness of the digital CM platform, despite their expectations at baseline that patients would be interested in the financial incentives and the flexibility of the digital CM platform. Engaging patients in digital CM platforms is a known challenge [9], and OTPs noted the need for additional resources towards promoting, marketing, and referring individuals to the digital CM platform. The challenges in this domain were clearly dependent on the challenges observed in other domains, with OTPs noting that it was hard to consistently identify and refer patients when they did not have clear organizational policies or leadership commitment. Our results are highly consistent with the work of Graham and colleagues, which identified the need for digital health researchers to prioritize the selection and specification of implementation strategies designed to promote the consistent identification and referral of patients to the digital health intervention [22]. It is possible that making adaptations to the performance feedback implementation strategy, such as more problem solving strategies to address low recrutiment, could potentially help address these barriers.

Overall, this study was the first to use the IFASIS longitudinally and highlights the potential utility of using a quantitative measure of implementation determinants across multiple timepoints. Use of the IFASIS advances implementation science by simultaneously facilitating both an in-depth understanding of determinants with qualitative data as well as comparison across sites or time with quantitative ratings of determinants. Contextual inquiry typically relies on qualitative data, and while quantitative measures of determinants exist [23], most measures only assess determinants at a single level (e.g., provider attitudes scale; organizational climate scale). The IFASIS is a low burden measure that captures both qualitative and quantitative data on determinants spanning multiple levels.

The study is not without limitations. First, the contextual determinants and patterns identified in this study were based on a small number of OTPs and all from a similar, homogenous geographic setting in New England; thus, results may not generalize to other settings or programs. Nonetheless, even within a small sample of OTPs within a small catchment area there was variability in ratings of determinants across OTPs. This concern is somewhat mitigated by the fact that the direction of changes over time was remarkably consistent across OTPs and consistent with commonly reported determinants in other studies implementing digital CM platforms [8]. Second, the IFASIS is a relatively new instrument, and thus, has not been evaluated for concurrent validity or compared with other comprehensive measures of determinants, such as the CFIR [11]. Nor has the IFASIS been compared to other quantitative measures of individual determinants, such as those addressing provider attitudes or organizational climate [17, 24]. Additionally, further research is needed to understand whether there may be differences in ratings of determinants across team members in different roles (i.e., leadership vs. those delivering the intervention); while the IFASIS was developed as a team-based instrument, understanding the potenial for variability in team members’ scores is warranted. Moreover, there was some variability in how IFASIS scores were collected, with two sites not engaging in team interviews at one timepoint. While there were no differences in results, research is needed to understand whether variations in IFASIS administration impact the measure’s reliability and validity. Finally, it is important to note that while we examined change in perceived determinants over time after receiving a multi-level implementation strategy, we cannot conclude that the changes were due to the reciept of the implementation strategy. It is possible that the changes in perceived determinants would have occurred without the implementation strategy. Further studies are needed to test for causal effects of multi-level implementation strategies on change in determinants over time, in order to elucidate the direct impact of the implementation strategy on its intended targets, rather than the indirect impact of the strategy on implementation outcomes [25]. Such studies would address the recent push to better understand and measure mechanisms of action in implementation strategies [26].

Conclusion

This manuscript adds to the field of implementation science by demonstrating use of the IFASIS, a tool that generates both qualitative and quantitative data on contextual determinants. The IFASIS can be used to measure the context for implementation not only across different sites but also before and after implementation. These findings point to the utility of such a measure not only for informing appropriate implementation strategies, but also measuring the underlying mechanisms and impact of implementation strategies. Results also advance addiction science by highlighting the contextual determinants that influence the uptake of digital CM platforms in OTPs. Importantly, our results suggest that digital CM implementors should closely monitor recipient and innovation level factors, and should take great care to set realistic expectations at the start of the implementation process.
