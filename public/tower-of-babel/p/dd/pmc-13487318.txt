Health-related social needs and referrals for genetic evaluation among young children
Authors: Hadley Stevens Smith, Kristin N. Ray, Lina Ghaloul Gonzalez, Janel Hanmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Health-related social needs (HRSNs) may impact the receipt of genetic services, but the mechanisms are not well established. We aimed to evaluate the relationship between pediatric patients’ HRSNs and referrals for genetic evaluation. We analyzed HRSN survey data collected during clinic visits across 47 primary care offices for children <3 years of age. Using hazard ratios adjusted (aHRs) for child characteristics, we evaluated the relative likelihood of referral for genetic evaluation based on categories of HRSN. The sample included 42,390 children, 470 of whom received a referral for genetic evaluation. Children who screened positive for any HRSN (n = 235/15,019) were more likely to be referred than children who did not screen positive (n = 235/27,371; aHR = 1.29, P = .009). Children were more likely to receive referrals if they were insured by Medicaid rather than commercial insurance (aHR = 2.32, P < .0001), and if they received care in academic primary care clinics rather than community ones (aHR = 2.52, P < .0001). Holding child characteristics and other HRSNs constant, parent-reported financial stress was associated with a higher likelihood of referral (aHR = 1.38, P = .004). Child race and ethnicity were not significant predictors of referral in adjusted analyses. Children in our sample with any HRSN were more likely to receive a referral for genetic evaluation, which is a key step toward genetic diagnosis. Our findings highlight the need to better understand the relationship between HRSNs and genetic referrals and services.

Introduction

Access to genetics specialists and testing is critical for children with suspected genetic conditions, as it facilitates accurate diagnoses and the development of genetically informed care plans. Clinical guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend a genetic evaluation and genetic testing for infants and children with clinical presentations such as congenital anomalies and neurodevelopmental disorders.1,2 However, many patients face barriers to receipt of guideline-recommended genetic testing,3, 4, 5, 6 which remains underutilized.3,5 Evidence on real-world pediatric genetics referral patterns is limited.7

Within the United States, access to genetics specialists, testing, and recommended follow-up care varies substantially.8,9 Neighborhood resources and other social factors have been shown to influence the rate of pediatric genetics clinic visits and the length of diagnostic delays for patients with rare diseases.9,10 Referral to a genetics clinic is one key step in accessing genetic evaluation and testing and has implications for receipt of downstream care.3,9,11,12 For example, among pediatric neurology patients at a single tertiary care institution, genetics clinic referral rates were associated with patient age, rurality, diagnosis, visit frequency, and provider type; among those referred, White patients were twice as likely as Black patients to complete a genetics clinic visit.7

Most research examining the relationship between health-related social needs (HRSNs) and access to care relies on social characteristics documented in electronic medical records (EMRs) rather than self-reported HRSN data collected through routine surveys. Routine HRSN screening in primary care can help to identify families in need of connection to resources, and families generally find these surveys acceptable.13 The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends screening for needs such as food, housing, and utilities,14 and payers and regulators are increasingly requiring HRSN screening and response.15 However, gaps remain in understanding how parent-reported HRSNs relate to children’s receipt of genetic services. The goal of this study was to evaluate the relationship between parent-reported HRSNs and pediatric genetics referrals at a large academic medical center in the United States.

Materials and Methods

Study population

Our study sample included children born between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2024 who attended a pediatric primary care well-child visit between January 2022 and December 2024 within an academic medical center’s Children’s Community Pediatrics or General Academic Pediatrics practices that had HRSN data in the EMR. Together, these practices include 47 community pediatric primary care offices and 2 academic pediatric primary care practices across 6 urban and 10 rural counties. The academic medical center’s Genetic and Genomic Medicine division has 11 medical geneticists, 4 metabolic nurses, 24 clinical genetic counselors, 2 research genetic counselors, and 4 genetic counselor assistants.

HRSNs data

Routine screening for HRSNs for all children <11 years of age presenting for well-child visits at included practices was instituted in August 2021. Parents or guardians of pediatric patients were asked to complete a HRSN screening questionnaire when their child was scheduled for a well-child visit. The questionnaire can be completed in English by a parent or guardian on the patient portal, by tablet in the waiting room, or verbally with staff. Parents who preferred to communicate with clinicians in a language other than English were asked questions verbally with the use of an interpreter or were presented with a paper version of the screener in 1 of 6 languages, and then the responses were entered into the EMR.

The 8-question screener included validated items to assess financial stress,16 housing insecurity,17 food insecurity,18 and transportation needs.19 The items were derived from a 2014 National Academy of Medicine report on social and behavioral domains and measures in the EMR.20 The questionnaire was available for completion every 6 months to reduce questionnaire fatigue in infants’ parents who have multiple well-child visits within the first year of life. We analyzed data from the questionnaire that was first completed for each child.

Demographic information

Demographic information was extracted from the EMR, including the child’s age, sex, race, ethnicity, and insurance provider. Race and ethnicity data were extracted from the EMR and thus reflect EMR categories. These fields were collected by clinical and administrative personnel from the parents or guardians at the time of the patient’s first encounter and updated at the parent or guardian’s request. Race and ethnicity, both social constructs, were included because of the potential for experiences of racism to affect both the risk of experiencing HRSNs and the acceptability of disclosing social needs to the health care system.21,22 We categorized insurance providers as commercial, Medicaid or other governmental, or self-pay or missing.

Genetics service referrals

We focused on referrals for genetic evaluation from January 1, 2022, through December 31, 2024. Genetic services at the academic medical center were provided in 4 different settings: (1) the genetics clinic, with a physician certified in clinical genetics and genomics or clinical biochemical genetics; (2) Genetic Counselor Initiative for Access, a service that facilitates “next-step” genetic testing for existing genetics patients who have already been evaluated by a medical geneticist; (3) Genetic Counseling Service, which is designed to support nongenetics providers (eg, neurologists, cardiologists, and hematologists) in ordering genetic tests; and (4) the Child Development Unit (CDU), which includes pediatricians with specialized training in developmental-behavioral pediatrics, neurology, or neurology with neurodevelopmental disabilities. The Genetic Counseling Service facilitates targeted testing based on the patient’s presentation, for example, a cardiomyopathy gene panel for patients with cardiomyopathy or a seizure gene panel for patients with epilepsy. Once test results were available, the genetic counselor reviewed and released them to both the patient and the primary clinical team. If further evaluation is warranted, then the patient would be referred for a formal evaluation with a genetics specialist. The CDU does not include physician geneticists, and genetic counselors are not involved in this clinic. Physicians in the CDU performed basic genetic evaluations, such as metabolic screening (eg, plasma amino acids, acylcarnitine profile, and urine organic acids), Fragile X testing, karyotype analysis, and single-nucleotide variation microarray. Patients with an identified diagnosis are referred for formal genetic evaluation and follow-up, whereas those without a diagnosis are also referred to the genetics clinic for further assessment.

Only the genetics clinic with a physician geneticist had the authority to request exome sequencing, while other clinics, such as the CDU, could order limited genetic testing, such as karyotype or Fragile X testing. More comprehensive testing, such as gene panel testing, could be done through the genetics clinic with a physician geneticist or through the Genetic Counseling Service clinic. For our analysis, we considered a referral for genetic evaluation to include a referral to the genetics clinic or to the CDU. We analyzed the first referral for each patient in this study.

Analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics (ie, means, counts, and percentages) for demographic characteristics and HRSNs. Because the data are censored (ie, the amount of time that children are observed ranges from 1 day to 3 years), we used survival analyses to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for referral using PROC PHREG in SAS 9.4 based on the Cox proportional hazards model. Using genetic referral (yes/no) as the outcome, we constructed models in which an indicator for any HRSN, each HRSN type together, and each HRSN type individually were included as independent variables. We report unadjusted HRs and HRs adjusted (aHRs) for child race, ethnicity, insurance type, and primary care practice setting (academic vs nonacademic). When models that accounted for correlated errors at the clinic level were used, no change in conclusion was observed. Visual inspection of the Schoenfeld residuals plotted against time suggested that the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied.

A total of 15,019 patients screened positive for any HRSN. In unadjusted analyses, children were more likely to be referred for genetic evaluation if they were insured by Medicaid rather than commercial insurance (HR = 2.94, P < .0001) and if they were Black rather than White (HR = 2.07, P < .0001).

In adjusted analyses, children who screened positive for any HRSN were more likely to be referred (n = 235/15,019) for genetic evaluation than children who did not screen positive for any HRSNs (n = 235/27,371; aHR 1.29, P = .009) (Supplemental Table 1). Children insured by Medicaid were more likely to be referred for genetic evaluation than children insured by commercial insurance (aHR = 2.32, P < .0001). Children who were seen in academic primary care clinics were more likely to be referred for genetic evaluation than children who were seen in community primary care clinics (aHR = 2.52, P < .0001). Child race and ethnicity were not significant predictors of referral in adjusted analyses.

Looking at individual HRSN categories while keeping child characteristics and other needs constant (Table 2), children were more likely to be referred for genetic evaluation if their parents experienced financial stress rather than children with no financial stress (aHR 1.38, P = .004). Food insecurity (aHR = 1.01, P = .957), transportation need (aHR = 1.24, P = .210), and housing insecurity (aHR 0.78, P = .126) were not significantly associated with referral likelihood, controlling for other factors. Discussion

Genetic testing is an important part of the diagnostic workup for children with suspected genetic conditions, yet the relationship among HRSNs, perceived need for genetic services, and the ability to access those services is poorly understood. Using routinely collected HRSN data from pediatric clinics in a large academic medical center, we found that children with at least 1 HRSN were more likely to receive a referral for genetic evaluation. Child race and ethnicity were not associated with the likelihood of referral, consistent with analysis of genetics referral rates in pediatric neurology by Cole et al.7 Children with public insurance rather than commercial insurance were more likely to be referred for genetic evaluation, similar to prior studies showing greater genetic testing access among Medicaid-insured children.10,23

Differences in referral rates may reflect how clinicians prioritize patient needs. Although primary care physicians may perceive unmet social needs as a barrier to genetics referral,24 it is possible that physicians may be more likely to refer children for genetic evaluation to help the family obtain resources such as early intervention or specialized schooling plans if a diagnosis is confirmed.8,25 Families with more financial resources may be able to obtain comparable supports independently, which may have contributed to our observed differences in referrals.

A strength of this study is the use of routinely collected family-level, self-reported HRSN data, which may more accurately reflect individual family circumstances than the neighborhood-level proxies commonly used in health services research. However, our analysis is limited to outpatient pediatric practices at a single academic medical center, and thus we do not capture inpatient referrals that may have occurred at birth. However, the system is very large, with a large catchment area of 47 outpatient practices, a variety of built and economic environments, and rates of HRSNs commensurate with overall United States rates. Because our data contain visit-related diagnoses rather than all diagnoses, there is no straightforward way to identify children with a known genetic diagnosis at the time of establishing care at the primary care clinic. However, for children who received a genetic diagnosis prenatally or in inpatient care, outpatient genetics follow-up would generally be arranged directly by the genetics team, which would typically have made the diagnosis. We did not assess whether referred patients ultimately completed the genetics clinic visits or underwent testing, as EMR review was beyond the scope of the present study.

Genetics referrals stem from a heterogeneous set of clinical presentations, ranging from specific congenital anomalies to nonspecific concerns such as developmental delay. Future research should examine whether referral patterns differ among children with similar indications, the proportion of referrals that lead to completed visits and molecular diagnoses, and the temporal relationship between HRSNs and referrals. As an alternative to the HR approach, logistic regression may be used to calculate the odds of referral. Overall, our findings underscore the need to better understand how HRSNs affect access to genetic services and suggest that routinely collected HRSN data may be useful for similar studies at other institutions.
