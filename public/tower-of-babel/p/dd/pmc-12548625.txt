Association of role clarity on ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ping Zhang, Ying Zhang, Yana He, Tingyao Nie, Bing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between role clarity and ethical behaviour and to identify the primary factors influencing nurses’ ethical conduct.DesignA cross-sectional survey was conducted.Setting, participants and methodsA questionnaire survey was conducted from March to April 2024, involving 315 orthopaedic nurses from six tertiary hospitals in Hunan Province, China. The survey employed a general information questionnaire, the Role Clarity Scale and the Ethical Behaviour Scale for Nurses-Revise. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS V.26.0. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between role clarity and ethical behaviour, followed by multiple stepwise linear regression analysis to determine the factors influencing ethical behaviour.Primary outcomeThe scores of ethical behaviour and role clarity among orthopaedic nurses were analysed, along with the key factors affecting their ethical behaviour.ResultsThe average item scores for role clarity and ethical behaviour among the 315 nurses were 3.85±0.77 and 3.68±0.44, respectively. A positive correlation was observed between role clarity and ethical behaviour (r=0.444, p<0.001). Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis indicated that role clarity (95% CI 0.243 to 0.357, β=0.496, p<0.001) and the degree of affection for the nursing profession (95% CI 0.018 to 0.127, β=0.125, p<0.05) were significant factors influencing ethical behaviour. Together, these factors accounted for 28.0% of the variance in nurses’ ethical behaviour (R²=0.280, F=31.581, p<0.001).ConclusionsThe findings indicated that role clarity and ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses were at a moderate level. Nursing managers can enhance nurses’ ethical conduct by fostering accurate role perceptions and cultivating a supportive ethical environment within hospitals.

Introduction

Ethical behaviour refers to actions guided by ethical cognition that conform to social moral norms.1 For nurses, standardised ethical behaviour is reflected in strict adherence to professional codes of conduct and moral standards in clinical practice, as well as in the appropriate management of nurse-patient relationships.2 Nurses’ ethical conduct directly affects the fairness of their decision-making, the protection of patient privacy and their ability to establish trusting relationships with patients.3

Orthopaedics, as a specialised field of nursing, presents unique clinical characteristics and challenges. Orthopaedic patients often experience prolonged hospitalisation, slow functional recovery and substantial financial burdens—factors that can trigger psychological distress such as anxiety and depression4—placing higher ethical demands on nursing care. In this complex environment, orthopaedic nurses must address multiple issues, including patients’ emotional needs, family conflicts and financial pressures, while delivering technical care, making ethical decision-making increasingly complex. Exemplary ethical behaviour enables nurses to respond effectively to these challenges, enhancing patient satisfaction and treatment adherence, reducing nurse–patient disputes and ensuring nurses’ professional security.5 Conversely, lapses in ethical behaviour may result in decreased nursing quality, compromised patient rights and heightened professional burnout and turnover intentions among nurses.6 Therefore, a thorough understanding of the current state of ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses and its influencing factors is of considerable practical importance for improving specialised nursing quality and maintaining workforce stability.

In addition to ethical behaviour, role clarity is a key determinant influencing nursing professional efficiency and job satisfaction.7 Role clarity refers to an individual’s precise understanding of work standards, responsibilities and organisational expectations.8 Most studies consistently indicate that clear role definition positively impacts nurses’ autonomy, decision-making abilities and overall work performance.9

However, current research on factors affecting nurses’ ethical behaviour primarily emphasises moral resilience10 and hospital ethical climate,11 with relatively few studies examining the relationship between role clarity and ethical behaviour. From a theoretical perspective, a strong intrinsic connection may exist between role clarity and ethical conduct. Clear role cognition can enhance nurses’ professional confidence and sense of responsibility,12 motivating them to actively fulfil ethical obligations in clinical practice and to safeguard patient rights. Moreover, role clarity can reduce workplace uncertainty and stress,13 providing nurses with additional cognitive resources to focus on ethical considerations, thereby fostering ethical behaviour.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between role clarity and ethical behaviour and to identify the main factors influencing nurses’ ethical conduct, offering valuable insights for enhancing ethical practice among orthopaedic nurses and improving patient care quality.

Methods

Patient or public contribution

No patient or public contribution was involved in this study. The research focused on the current status of orthopaedic nurses’ perceptions of ethical behaviour and its relationship with role clarity; therefore, the participation of patients or the public was not considered necessary to achieve the specific research objectives.

Study design and participants

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in China using online survey methods. Based on the Kendal sample size calculation principle,14 the sample size was set to be 5–10 times the number of variables. As this study included 10 variables and accounted for a 20% attrition rate, the sample size ranged from 60 to 120. Convenience sampling was employed to recruit orthopaedic nurses from six different hospitals in China between March and April 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) orthopaedic nurses holding an active Chinese Nurse Practice Certificate and (2) volunteers who agreed to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses pursuing further education or in training and (2) nurses absent from work for more than 1 month during their employment.

Data collection

With approval from the administrators of the participating hospitals, one to two investigators were assigned to each hospital. Notifications were disseminated through social media platforms, such as WeChat, to inform potential participants about the study’s purpose, as well as the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This information was also provided in detail in the instructions section of the questionnaire. After obtaining informed consent, data were collected using the online survey platform ‘Questionnaire Star’. The procedural steps are illustrated in figure 1.

Measurements

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was self-designed by the researchers and included eight variables, such as age, gender, educational background, length of professional service and professional title, among others.

Role clarity scale

Developed by Rizzo et al15 and translated by Zhang and Liao,16 this scale comprises six items: (1) I am very clear about the rights I have in my work; (2) I have clear and specific goals and requirements for my work; (3) I know my job responsibilities; (4) I clearly understand the expectations for my work; (5) I am confident that I have allocated my work time appropriately and (6) I am unsure whether my work will be accepted by my superior. Responses were recorded on a 7-point Likert scale, where ‘strongly disagree’ was scored as 1 and ‘strongly agree’ as 7. Total scores range from 6 to 42, with higher scores indicating greater clarity regarding nurses’ work roles. The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.842. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.860.

The Ethical Behaviour Scale for Nurses-Revise

Developed by Ode17 and localised into Chinese by Chen and Chen,18 this scale comprises three dimensions: quality care (5 items), equitable care (5 items) and risk prevention (5 items), totalling 15 items. Responses are recorded on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 6 (‘strongly agree’). Total scores range from 15 to 90, with higher scores indicating better ethical behaviour practices among nurses in clinical settings. The scale demonstrated strong reliability, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.892, while in this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.767.

Data analyses

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS V.26.0 (IBM). Continuous data were expressed as mean±SD. Independent t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for multiple-group comparisons. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between role clarity and ethical behaviour, while multiple stepwise linear regression analysis was used to identify factors influencing ethical behaviour.

Results

A total of 320 questionnaires were distributed in this study. Questionnaires with multiple consecutive identical responses, incomplete answers or completion times of less than 5 min were considered invalid and excluded. This resulted in 315 valid questionnaires, yielding an effective response rate of 98.43% (figure 1). The majority of participants were female (90.5%), and more than half (75.9%) were aged between 20 and 35 years. Married participants accounted for 63.8%, while 59.7% had over 5 years of work experience. Additionally, 87.9% held a nurse or higher professional title, and 83.2% had a bachelor’s degree. Among participants, 71.4% held no administrative positions, and 70.5% reported liking nursing at a level of ‘relatively like’ or higher. Independent t-tests and ANOVA revealed significant differences in ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses with respect to years of work experience, professional title, degree of liking nursing and role clarity (p<0.05) (tables1 2).

Descriptive analysis of role clarity and nurses’ ethical behaviour

The total scores and dimension-specific scores for the two variables were expressed as mean±SD. The total score for nurses’ role clarity was 19.25±3.85, with a range of 6–42; higher scores indicated a clearer recognition of work roles among nurses. The total score for ethical behaviour was 55.20±6.60, with a range of 15–90; higher scores reflected better ethical behaviour practices in clinical settings. The dimension-specific scores were as follows: quality care, 14.90±3.35; risk prevention, 15.60±3.30 and fair care, 24.60±3.75 (table 3).

Correlation between role clarity and nurses’ ethical behaviour

Ethical behaviour and its three dimensions were all significantly correlated with role clarity (p<0.01), with most correlation coefficients ranging from 0.2 to 0.6, indicating a moderate relationship. Specifically, nurses with a clearer understanding of their role responsibilities demonstrated higher overall levels of ethical behaviour. This positive correlation was particularly evident in the dimensions of ‘quality care’ and ‘risk prevention’. Interestingly, role clarity exhibited a negative correlation with the ‘fair care’ dimension, suggesting that nurses with higher role clarity scored lower on this dimension (table 1).

Predictive factors of nurses’ ethical behaviour: multiple regression analysis

To identify the key factors predicting ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses, a multiple stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted. The results indicated that role clarity (95% CI 0.243 to 0.357, β=0.496, p<0.001) and the degree of affection for the nursing profession (95% CI 0.018 to 0.127, β=0.125, p<0.05) were significant independent predictors of nurses’ ethical behaviour. After controlling for other variables, nurses with higher role clarity continued to demonstrate significantly higher ethical behaviour scores (p<0.001). Together, these two factors accounted for 28.0% of the variance in ethical behaviour, emphasising their combined importance in understanding and predicting ethical conduct in nursing practice (tables4 5).

Discussion

This study describes the current status of ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses and analyses various factors influencing it. The findings indicate that ethical behaviour in orthopaedic nurses is associated with both the degree of affection for the nursing profession and role clarity.

Assessment of role clarity in orthopaedic nursing practice

Our study found that role clarity among orthopaedic nurses was at a moderately low level. This finding aligns with research on oncology nurses19 and may be attributed to shared characteristics between these two specialties. Both oncology and orthopaedic nursing involve complex disease management and high patient dependency, which can result in blurred role boundaries or task conflicts during interdisciplinary collaboration. Several factors may contribute to the suboptimal role clarity observed in this study. First, the complexity of orthopaedic nursing practice, encompassing diverse patient populations ranging from trauma cases to elective procedures, may create ambiguity in role expectations. Unlike general medical nursing, orthopaedic nurses must master specialised skills in mobility assistance, pain management and rehabilitation guidance, potentially leading to role confusion when responsibilities overlap with other healthcare professionals.20 Second, the dynamic nature of healthcare delivery systems may contribute to unclear role boundaries. As healthcare becomes increasingly collaborative and interdisciplinary, orthopaedic nurses may experience uncertainty about their specific responsibilities within the care team, particularly when working alongside physiotherapists, occupational therapists and orthopaedic technicians.21 Third, organisational factors within tertiary hospitals may influence role clarity. High patient turnover, staff shortages and frequent policy changes can create an environment in which role expectations are constantly evolving, making it difficult for nurses to maintain a clear understanding of their professional boundaries and responsibilities.22 23

Evaluation of ethical behaviour and its dimensional components

In this study, orthopaedic nurses demonstrated moderate levels of ethical behaviour, indicating substantial room for improvement. Their scores were lower than those reported by Li et al24 for Chinese oncology nurses, which may reflect fundamental differences in ethical challenges and coping strategies across nursing specialties. Oncology nursing often involves high-risk ethical situations, such as end-of-life care and life-and-death decisions25 26, requiring nurses to maintain heightened ethical vigilance and resulting in higher overall ethical behaviour levels. In contrast, ethical issues in orthopaedic nursing are typically long-term and routine, lacking continuous high-risk alerts, which may contribute to comparatively lower ethical vigilance. These differences in work characteristics are likely to account for the lower overall ethical behaviour levels among orthopaedic nurses compared with their oncology counterparts.

Furthermore, the results showed that among all dimensions, ‘fair care’ received the highest score (mean item score 4.92±0.75), whereas the ‘high-quality care’ dimension scored the lowest (mean item score 2.98±0.67). This pattern may be attributed to the high-intensity, high-workload environment in orthopaedic wards, which often lacks deeper-level ethical contexts. Under these circumstances, nurses may focus on upholding fundamental ethical principles while finding it challenging to engage in higher-level, more resource-intensive ethical behaviours.

Therefore, ethical behaviour is not uniform across all nursing specialties; its manifestations and challenges are strongly shaped by the specific context of each specialty. Nursing managers should acknowledge these specialty-specific differences and implement targeted ethical training and management strategies tailored to the orthopaedic nursing environment, enabling nurses to uphold core ethical standards while striving to deliver higher-quality care.

Correlation analysis between role clarity and ethical behaviour

The study results revealed a positive correlation between role clarity and ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses (p<0.05), indicating that higher role clarity is associated with stronger capacity to implement ethical behaviour. Additionally, role clarity showed positive correlations with the fairness and risk prevention dimensions of ethical behaviour, while exhibiting a negative correlation with the high-quality care dimension. This finding may be explained by two main factors:

First, role clarity fosters task orientation. Nurses with high role clarity tend to focus their efforts more explicitly on completing designated responsibilities.27 In orthopaedic settings characterised by heavy workloads and demanding tasks, this may lead nurses to allocate time and energy based on task urgency and complexity rather than adhering strictly to the principle of ‘equal treatment’ for all patients. While this approach may optimise resource utilisation, it can appear to contradict the principle of ‘fair care’ from a broader perspective, resulting in the observed negative correlation.

Second, role clarity influences the prioritisation of ethical principles. ‘Fair care’ represents a fundamental ethical principle encompassing all patients, whereas dimensions such as ‘high-quality care’ and ‘risk prevention’ are more closely tied to specific nursing operations and measurable skills.28 When nurses’ roles are clearly defined, they may be more inclined to focus on performance indicators that can be quantified and evaluated, while attention to ‘fair care’,” a more abstract and difficult-to-measure principle, may be comparatively reduced.

In summary, the negative correlation between role clarity and fair care does not indicate a lack of ethical intent, but rather reflects a tendency in ethical resource allocation driven by focused work priorities in high-pressure environments. This finding highlights a critical challenge in nursing management: how to ensure that nurses can comprehensively uphold all ethical principles while maintaining work efficiency and role clarity.

Determinants of ethical behaviour: a multifactorial perspective

The stepwise multiple linear regression analysis indicated that role clarity and the degree of affection for the nursing profession are significant factors influencing nurses’ ethical behaviour, accounting for 28% of the total variance. Nurses who expressed a strong liking for the profession were more likely to invest substantial time and effort into their work, attend carefully to details and derive satisfaction and a sense of value from their role.29 In contrast, nurses with lower affinity for the profession scored lowest in ethical behaviour. This may be because they perceive nursing primarily as a means of earning a living, dedicating minimal time and effort to their duties. For these nurses, ensuring medical safety and adhering to basic healthcare standards may constitute their primary professional goals, with limited motivation to exceed these minimum requirements in terms of ethical nursing conduct.

Strengths and limitations of this study

The strengths of this study are reflected in several aspects. First, it investigated the association between role clarity and ethical behaviour, a topic that has received limited attention. Second, the research team employed the Ethical Behaviour Scale for Nurses-Revise (EBSN-R), a validated instrument, to assess ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses, ensuring the reliability and validity of the findings. Third, examining ethical behaviour is crucial for nursing training, as it supports high-quality care and enhances patient outcomes. Our findings provide an evidence-based foundation for developing future intervention strategies and models aimed at improving nurses’ ethical behaviour.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling represents a primary limitation, as this non-probability sampling method may introduce selection bias, potentially limiting the representativeness of the sample. Consequently, the generalisability of our findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, participants were recruited from multiple hospitals, creating a clustered data structure. However, our statistical analysis did not adjust for potential hospital-level effects. Nurses within the same institution may share policies, training and work culture, making them more similar to each other than to nurses from other hospitals. Failing to account for this clustering can underestimate standard errors, thereby inflating the statistical significance of results. Third, the assessment of ethical behaviour relied on self-report questionnaires, which are susceptible to response biases, including social desirability bias. Participants’ responses may, therefore, reflect ideals rather than actual practices. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between role clarity and ethical behaviour and does not allow for examination of changes in these variables over time.

To advance research in this area, future studies should adopt more robust methodological designs. For example, longitudinal studies using probability sampling would provide stronger evidence. Additionally, employing multilevel statistical models to account for the nested data structure within hospitals is essential for obtaining more accurate estimates of the relationships between variables.

Implications for nurses’ self-growth and nursing management

With the continuous development of the healthcare industry, hospitalised patients increasingly demand nursing care that is more humanised, holistic and professional.30 This places higher expectations on nursing professionals, particularly regarding ethical behaviour in practice. Enhancing nurses’ ethical behaviour can be approached from two main perspectives.

First, to adapt to dynamic clinical environments and manage complex and unexpected situations, nurses should proactively engage in continuous learning.31 This includes participating in diverse courses, improving nursing skills and attending training programmes to acquire new knowledge. Such efforts can enhance role clarity and support professional self-development.32 Additionally, nurses should treat patients equally, protect their privacy, attend to their physical and mental well-being, and consistently adhere to ethical behaviour standards in professional practice.

Second, nursing managers should adopt a tailored approach that considers the unique developmental needs of nurses at different career stages and aligns these with the hospital’s growth trajectory.33 Managers should guide nurses towards diversified development, provide career planning support and assist them in formulating clear and achievable career goals to facilitate personal growth and advance the nursing profession.34 Regarding ethical behaviour education, managers should conduct regular ethical training and supervision, ensuring the implementation of holistic and humanistic care practices. Moreover, integrating ethical knowledge into patient education can improve patients’ understanding and recognition of nursing ethics. These efforts can reduce nurse–patient conflicts, enhance patient satisfaction and ultimately improve the quality of care.35

Conclusions

This study reflects the current status of ethical behaviour among orthopaedic nurses and identifies factors associated with ethical conduct. Intrinsic factors include the level of affection for the nursing profession and role clarity, while extrinsic factors involve length of professional service and professional titles. Special attention should be given to nurses with longer work experience and higher professional titles but lower levels of role clarity or affection for the profession. Nursing managers can implement measures to address extrinsic factors that enhance ethical behaviour, such as conducting targeted ethical training programmes, deepening nurses’ understanding and recognition of ethical practice, and reinforcing the implementation of ethical standards. These efforts aim to stimulate nurses’ intrinsic motivation, foster a positive professional attitude, encourage continuous knowledge acquisition and skill development, and promote proactive ethical care for patients. Such an approach can better meet patients’ care needs and improve the overall quality of nursing services.
