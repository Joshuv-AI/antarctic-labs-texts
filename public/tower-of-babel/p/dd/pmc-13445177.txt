An integrated biomass-to-algae pathway for producing emergency food components from lignocellulosic waste
Authors: Niroshan Siva, Tara Mather, Charles T. Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global food systems are highly vulnerable to catastrophic disruptions, highlighting the need for alternative food sources that do not rely on conventional crop production. Lignocellulosic biomass is an abundant non-food resource with substantial stored energy, but it is not directly edible by humans. We present an integrated bioconversion strategy that converts lignocellulosic biomass into three essential macronutrients. Lime [Ca(OH)2] pretreatment solubilizes lignin and deacetylates hemicellulose to liberate soluble acetate, facilitating subsequent enzymatic cellulose hydrolysis. This acetate-rich liquid supports the growth of an edible alga, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, under low light, producing 835–971 mg/L cell mass with 30–31% lipid content, approximately twice that of standard medium. The algal biomass contained 5-10% protein, while the remaining solid lignocellulosic residue yielded up to 22% glucose upon enzymatic hydrolysis, twice that from untreated biomass. These results show that lime-treated lignocellulosic biomass can be fractionated into glucose-rich hydrolysates and acetate-derived algal biomass enriched in lipids, although protein production remains limited. Life-cycle and techno-economic analyses indicate that this biomass-based algal system is environmentally and economically viable, offering a resilient food source under extreme conditions, although further optimization is needed to improve protein yield and practical scalability.

Introduction

Throughout history, natural disasters such as massive volcanic eruptions have released clouds of dust, diminishing solar irradiation of Earth's surface over prolonged periods (Helama et al., 2018; Larsen et al., 2008). For example, these phenomena likely contributed to near-simultaneous outbreaks of famine and the Justinianic plague in the eastern Roman Empire, decimating crop production and the synthesis of vitamin D in human skin, consequently diminishing overall human health (Helama et al., 2018; Büntgen et al., 2016). Similarly, human-caused catastrophes, such as a global nuclear conflict, might generate 150 Tg of soot, capable of blocking sunlight and inducing sub-freezing temperatures across many parts of the world for over a decade (Robock et al., 2007; Coupe et al., 2019). These environmental disruptions, which would be much more widespread and severe than local or regional disasters, pose a serious threat to crop cultivation by causing widespread plant mortality and preventing the growth of new crops.

The scale of food output loss directly correlates with the magnitude of the catastrophe (Xia et al., 2022). While a regional nuclear conflict might cause a 10% reduction in food output, an all-out global nuclear war could slash food production by at least 30%, encompassing declines in crop yields, marine resources, and livestock populations (Xia et al., 2022). Such large drop-offs in caloric availability would lead to mass starvation for at least a decade, risking billions of lives (Xia et al., 2022; Robock, 2010). These scenarios necessitate a reevaluation of conventional approaches to food production, compelling exploration into alternative sources of nutrition (Garcia Martinez et al., 2024).

Single-celled organisms, such as yeast and microalgae, have emerged as promising superfoods owing to their rich nutrient profiles (Masi et al., 2023; Onyeaka et al., 2022). For instance, 100 g of the green algae, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, can provide approximately 15-25 g of carbohydrate, 40-55 g of protein, and 10-30 g of fat in addition to several essential minerals and vitamins (Xu et al., 2018; Darwish et al., 2020). Furthermore, algae exhibit remarkable adaptability, thriving under diverse light and temperature conditions and utilizing nutrients from waste streams including nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals (Christenson and Sims, 2011; Rawat et al., 2011). Notably, C. reinhardtii is a facultative heterotroph that can grow photoautotrophically using sunlight and carbon dioxide, heterotrophically using acetate in the dark, or mixotrophically using both light/carbon dioxide and acetate (Heifetz et al., 2000; Boyle et al., 2017).

Cellulose, an abundant component of plant biomass, is composed of glucose monomers polymerized into recalcitrant microfibrils. Globally, plants contain an estimated 225 Gt of cellulose, equivalent to 248 Gt of glucose, representing a large but nutritionally incomplete pool of chemically stored energy (Bar-On et al., 2018; Peciulyte et al., 2015; Siva and Anderson, 2023). However, this energy cannot be directly accessed by humans and should not be interpreted as a complete dietary resource, since glucose alone cannot provide a balanced diet. Even in catastrophic scenarios where living vegetation suffers catastrophic mortality, vast quantities of non-food lignocellulosic biomass would remain physically intact and available as a dead carbon reserve (Denkenberger and Pearce, 2014). Perennial species like willow and switchgrass serve as highly representative models of this abundant, unutilized residual feedstock. However, lignocellulosic biomass is low in essential macronutrients such as protein and fat (Siva and Anderson, 2023). Furthermore, humans cannot digest cellulose due to a lack of endogenous cellulase enzymes (Chassard et al., 2010; Fujimori, 2021). Therefore, cellulose must be converted into glucose as a recoverable caloric substrate or other intermediate products such as acetate to incorporate into potential food systems such as microalgae cultivation. Compared with glucose, acetate might also provide a degree of selectivity when used as the dominant organic carbon source for cultivating acetate-assimilating microbes, because glucose is broadly metabolized by many microorganisms, whereas acetate utilization requires specific assimilation pathways and can exert week-acid inhibitory effects on some contaminating bacteria depending on pH and concentration (Hirshfield et al., 2003; Pinhal et al., 2019)

Converting cellulose from lignocellulosic biomass into sugars requires extensive pretreatment to remove the surrounding lignin and hemicellulose, which hinder the access of cellulase enzymes (Brodeur et al., 2011). Chemical pretreatments are often favored for their simplicity, speed, and efficiency compared to thermal or biological alternatives alone. For example, sulfuric, hydrochloric, or nitric acid at concentrations of 0.2–2.5% are applied at 130–210°C for 10–60 min to hydrolyze hemicellulose and partially solubilize lignin (Brodeur et al., 2011). Alternatively, alkaline pretreatments using hydroxide or ammonia at 1–5% are conducted at milder temperatures (80–120°C) for longer durations (1–24 h) to solubilize lignin and partially remove hemicellulose (Chen et al., 2013; Shukla et al., 2023). However, in resource-constrained environments, the scarcity of chemical reagents and reliable heating infrastructure might limit the feasibility of these conventional chemical pretreatments (Taylor et al., 2019).

Previous studies have shown that lignocellulosic hydrolysates and biomass-derived acetate can support microalgal cultivation. For example, rice straw hydrolysates produced after pretreatment have been used to cultivate Chlorella pyrenoidosa and C. protothecoides for biomass and lipid production (Li et al., 2011; Joe et al., 2015). Separately, C. reinhardtii is well known to use acetate under heterotrophic or mixotrophic conditions, and acetate-rich pyrolytic bio-oil from lignocellulosic biomass has supported C. reinhardtii growth after detoxification (Heifetz et al., 2000; Liang et al., 2013). More recently, alkaline-pretreated wheat straw was shown to release acetate that could be used by C. reinhardtii, including engineered strains secreting acetylxylan esterase (Ramos-Martinez et al., 2019). However, the direct integration of low-cost lime treatment with low-light for C. reinhardtii cultivation has not been previously evaluated as a strategy for dietary protein and fat production.

In post-catastrophe scenarios, direct photoautotrophic algal cultivation using only carbon dioxide and light may be constrained by reduced solar irradiation and low temperatures (Xia et al., 2022). Inedible lignocellulosic biomass, however, may remain available as a stored or residual resource, and lime pretreatment offers a relatively low-cost method for converting it into algal substrates. Because C. reinhardtii can grow on acetate under low-light or heterotrophic conditions, lignocellulose-derived acetate might provide a complementary route for producing lipid and protein containing food when conventional photosynthesis is limited. Here, we developed and tested an integrated approach that would be suitable for use in post-catastrophic environments to convert lignocellulosic biomass into nutritionally valuable food components. Lime [Ca(OH)2] treatment at 100°C was used to extract acetate from biomass, and the resulting acetate-rich solution, supplemented with minimal nutrients, supported the growth of C. reinhardtii under low-light conditions. After acetate extraction, the remaining solid biomass residue was subjected to enzymatic saccharification to recover glucose, providing a carbohydrate-rich fraction from the same feedstock. Algae cultivated on acetate derived from willow and switchgrass accumulated 30-31% fat and 5-10% protein (w/w, dry basis). These results indicate that algal biomass, with its potential for accumulating fat and protein, can complement carbohydrate-rich hydrolysates derived from pretreated biomass, together providing a more nutritionally balanced diet if conventional agriculture is no longer viable.

Material and methods

Willow and switchgrass samples

Shrub willow (Salix spp.) and switchgrass (Panicum virgatum) were obtained from The Pennsylvania State University's bioenergy crop fields (40°51′36.92″, −77°47′50.37″, elevation 370 m) in July 2021. The fresh willow was processed into woodchips (<0.5 cm x < 1 cm x < 5 cm), switchgrass was cut into 5 cm stems, and both types of biomasses were dried for 1 week at room temperature (∼23°C). The biomass was then dried in an oven at 60°C for 3 h (to constant weight). Biomass samples were ground in a Wiley mill (Wiley Model 4, Thomas Scientific, NJ) with a 1 mm mesh screen. Ten-gram aliquots of ground samples were further processed in a ball mill (TissueLyser, Qiagen 85210, Retsch, Germany) for 3 min at 5 Hz, followed by 5 min at 30 Hz, to generate a fine powder (Mather et al., 2024).

Acetate extraction from biomass

One-gram samples of Wiley-milled or ball-milled biomass were transferred to 250 mL glass beakers, and 0.2 g of lime [Ca(OH)2] was added. Initially, 30 mL of Milli-Q water was added, and the beakers were kept on a hot plate to boil for 1 h (Sierra et al., 2009). During heating, additional water was added periodically with stirring to compensate for water loss. After 1 h, the slurries were transferred to 50 mL conical tubes. The beakers were rinsed with 5 mL of Milli-Q water and transferred to the same 50 mL conical tubes to reach a final volume of 35 mL. The tubes were then centrifuged at 4500 x g rpm for 10 min. Supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C until further analysis. The biomass was washed with Milli-Q water 3 times with centrifugation at 4500 x g for 10 min after each wash, then freeze dried (FreeZone 12L, Labconco, MO, USA) for 3 days at −50°C under a vacuum of 0.004 PSI. Freeze-dried samples were stored at −70°C until later enzymatically treated to yield glucose (see section 2.8).

Acetate measurement

Acetic acid concentration in biomass-derived liquid samples was quantified based on an enzymatic colorimetric assay using UniFlex Acetic Acid Kit (AA-F060, Unitech Scientific, CA, USA) on a ChemWell-T automated spectrophotometric analyzer (Unitech Scientific, model 18347, CA, USA). The assay employs acetyl-CoA synthetase (ACS), citrate synthase (CS), and malate dehydrogenase (MDH) enzymes to catalyze a two-step reaction, wherein acetic acid is enzymatically converted to citrate with concurrent oxidation of NADH. The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm, corresponding to NADH consumption, was measured and correlated to acetic acid concentration using a standard curve generated from a known acetic acid standard included in the kit. Samples were analyzed in triplicate, with appropriate dilution to ensure readings within the linear range (0.05–1.0 g/L).

Algal growth conditions

Media for algal growth were prepared following the protocols provided by the Chlamydomonas Resource Center (https://www.chlamycollection.org/methods/media-recipes/tap-and-tris-minimal/). Tris-Acetate-Phosphate (TAP) medium consisted of 1 M Tris base, phosphate buffer II (K2HPO4 and KH2PO4), solution A (NH4Cl, MgSO4·7H2O, and CaCl2·2H2O), and Hutner's trace elements. The trace elements included EDTA disodium salt, ZnSO4·7H2O, H3BO3, MnCl2·4H2O, CoCl2·6H2O, CuSO4·5H2O, (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, and FeSO4·7H2O. Commercial acetic acid (1 g/L) was added as a carbon source. The biomass-derived media contained the same components as the TAP medium, with the exception that the water used to make the media was replaced by an acetate-rich solution derived from biomass. Tris-minimal medium (min TAP) included all the components of TAP except for acetate.

C. reinhardtii (strain 21gr+, CC-1690 from the Chlamydomonas Resource Center) was cultivated in 30 mL of TAP medium, biomass-derived media, or min TAP medium in 50 mL conical tubes under a light intensity of 33 μmol/m2/s, simulating the reduced sunlight conditions of a nuclear war scenario producing 150 Tg of soot (Coupe et al., 2019). Cultures were incubated at 22°C under three light regimes: 12-h light/12-h dark, continuous light (24 h light), or complete darkness (0 h light). The tubes were manually shaken once daily to maintain uniform suspension. Every other day, a 200 μL aliquot was collected under a laminar flow hood in a darkened room, and optical density (OD) at 800 nm was measured to monitor growth until cultures reached a stationary phase (Dao et al., 2025, Shin et al., 2026).

Algae mass for nutrient analysis

At the end of each experiment, algal biomass was harvested and washed three times with Milli-Q water, interspersed by centrifugation at 4500 x g for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of Milli-Q water, vortexed thoroughly, and frozen at −70°C overnight. Samples were then freeze-dried (FreeZone 12L, Labconco, MO, USA) for 3 days. The dried biomass was stored at −20°C until further analysis.

Protein measurements

Protein content was determined using the Bradford assay (Chia et al., 2015). Ten milligrams of freeze-dried algal biomass were weighed into a 15 mL conical tube, and 5 mL of Milli-Q water was added. The cells were disrupted using a probe sonicator (Sonic Dismembrator, model 100, Fisher Scientific, PA, USA) with six cycles of 10 s at 100W power followed by 20 s rest with the tubes submerged in crushed ice. One hundred microliters of the sonicated extract were briefly mixed with 1 mL of Bradford reagent (M172, VWR International, PA, USA) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm (Victor Nivo, HH3500, PerkinElmer, CT, USA). Bovine serum albumin (BSA, A9418, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) was used to generate a standard curve for quantification.

Lipid measurements

Lipid content was determined using the vanillin-phosphoric acid assay (Byreddy et al., 2016). Ten milligrams of freeze-dried algal biomass were weighed into a 10 mL glass tube. The samples were suspended in 2 mL of 98% sulfuric acid and heated at 100°C for 10 min. After cooling, 5 mL of vanillin-phosphoric acid reagent (0.2% vanillin in 17% (v/v) aqueous phosphoric acid) were added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance was measured on a plate reader (Victor Nivo, HH3500, PerkinElmer, CT, USA) at 530 nm, and lipid concentration was calculated using a standard curve prepared with known concentrations of triolein.

Enzymatic hydrolysis of lime-pretreated biomass and glucose quantification

One hundred mg of lime-treated and untreated willow or switchgrass biomass or ground filter paper (as a control) was transferred into 15 mL conical tubes. Ten milliliters of 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.8) and 25 μL of Cellic CTec2 enzyme (Sigma-Aldrich, SAE0020) were added, and the samples were incubated at 50°C for 72 h with 200 rpm horizontal shaking. Samples were then centrifuged at 3000 x g for 10 min, diluted 10-fold with Milli-Q water and filtered through a 13 mm-wide 0.45 μm syringe filter with a PTFE membrane (VWR International, Bridgeport, NJ, USA). Sugars were analyzed using HPAEC-PAD using previously published methods (Mather et al., 2024). All samples were processed in triplicate.

Estimation of macronutrient contribution

The potential dietary energy contribution of the integrated biomass-algae system was estimated on a per-kilogram basis of initial plant biomass feedstock. Glucose recovered after enzymatic hydrolysis was used to estimate the carbohydrate-derived energy output, using 4 kcal/g glucose. Algal protein and lipid outputs were calculated from algal dry biomass yield and measured protein and lipid contents, using 4 kcal/g protein and 9 kcal/g lipid, respectively. Daily macronutrient targets were based on a 2000 kcal adult diet and the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025, which recommend 45-65% of energy from carbohydrates, 10-35% from protein, and 20-35% from fat (USDA, 2020). Therefore, daily energy ranges used for comparison were 900-1300 kcal for carbohydrates, 200-700 kcal for protein, and 400-700 kcal for fat. The carbohydrate fraction produced in this study consisted primarily of free glucose. Therefore, these values were interpreted only as theoretical recoverable caloric contribution, not as evidence that glucose can replace the full dietary carbohydrate requirement, which normally includes complex carbohydrates and fiber-containing foods.

Life-cycle analysis

Two algal cultivation systems (a standard medium system with conventional algal nutrient inputs and a biomass-based medium system utilizing biomass hydrolysate as a carbon source) were compared for environmental performance. Both systems were modeled to produce equivalent functional outputs of lipid (1 kg) and glucose (25 g), with glucose treated as a co-product in the analysis. The system boundary followed a cradle-to-gate approach, comprising feedstock preparation, pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, algal cultivation, and product recovery, but excluding downstream distribution, use, and disposal (further information is provided in the Supplementary Material). Inventory data were obtained from actual laboratory-scale experiments and literature databases. Material inputs included switchgrass biomass, lime, water, enzymes, and algal growth media components (1 M Tris base, phosphate buffer II, solution A, and Hutner's trace elements). For the hydrolysate system, biomass hydrolysate replaced the water and acetate in the synthetic medium, and wood-derived heat substituted fossil-based energy. Electricity and heat requirements were modeled using average North American grid mixes. Background data for all materials and energy inputs were obtained from the ecoinvent v3.1 database. Life-cycle impact assessment was conducted in SimaPro 10.2 using the ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H) and Endpoint (H) methods (Huijbregts et al., 2017). Results were calculated for both midpoint and endpoint categories and normalized to the World 2010 baseline (PRé Sustainability, 2024). Midpoint indicators included global warming potential, ozone formation, particulate matter formation, acidification, eutrophication, ecotoxicity, human toxicity, and resource scarcity. Endpoint categories covered human health, ecosystems, and resource depletion. Single-score results were generated by aggregating endpoint damage into total environmental points (further information is provided in the Supplementary Material).

Techno-economic analysis

The techno-economic analysis (TEA) evaluated the production of C. reinhardtii biomass cultivated in a switchgrass-derived hydrolysate medium under three post-catastrophic scenarios. The system boundary covers the feedstock pretreatment, media preparation, algal cultivation in an open raceway pond, harvesting, and drying to obtain 1 kg of algal biomass per day. A functional unit of 1 kg dry algae was set and expressed as an annualized value for a small-scale facility operating at The Pennsylvania State University, PA, USA. Process description, energy and heat supply details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Results and discussion

Acetate recovery from lime-treated plant biomass for algal cultivation

Tris-Acetate-Phosphate (TAP) medium, commonly used to support algal growth under optimal conditions, typically contains 1 g/L of acetate (Bogaert et al., 2019). We found that extracts from lime-treated lignocellulosic biomass contained acetate concentrations up to 1.3 g/L (Fig. 1A). No significant differences in acetate concentration were observed between willow and switchgrass or between plant biomass ground using a Wiley mill alone versus Wiley milling followed by ball milling (Fig. 1A). We previously reported that the particle size distribution produced by a Wiley mill can be replicated using a household blender, an important consideration for resource-limited scenarios (Siva and Anderson, 2024). Growth responses of C. reinhardtii to plant biomass-derived acetate under varying light conditions

C. reinhardtii is a mixotrophic organism that can use light for photosynthesis and organic compounds such as acetate as an additional energy source. In the presence of light, photosynthesis provides ATP and reducing power and supports carbon dioxide fixation through the Calvin cycle, whereas acetate provides a reduced organic carbon source that can be used for respiration and biosynthesis. C. reinhardtii takes up acetate and converts it via the glyoxylate cycle into metabolic intermediates (triose phosphates) for growth (Heifetz et al., 2000; Chapman et al., 2015). Therefore, light and acetate can contribute complementary metabolic inputs with light primarily supporting photosynthetic energy generation and acetate supporting heterotrophic carbon assimilation and energy metabolism. Acetate uptake typically increases under reduced light or complete darkness, likely to compensate for the energy normally derived from photosynthesis (Singh et al., 2014). Under 12 h low-light conditions, algae cultivated in extracts from lime-treated willow or switchgrass showed slightly faster, but not statistically significant, growth during the first four days compared to algae grown in standard TAP medium, with growth rates of 1.20/day and 1.35/day, respectively, compared with 1.08/day in TAP medium (Fig. 1B). However, by the end of the tenth day, all three treatments resulted in similar cell densities. This similarity in final cell density might reflect the comparable organic carbon load among acetate containing media, since acetate concentrations recovered from lime-treated biomass extracts were within a a range similar to the acetate concentration in standard TAP medium (Fig. 1A). Under these conditions, acetate likely served as a major available organic carbon source for mixotrophic growth. Therefore, cultures might have reached a similar stationary phase once the available carbon supply became limiting, despite slight differences in early growth kinetics. Extract from untreated switchgrass supported limited algal growth, comparable to that observed in minimal TAP medium that lacks acetate, whereas extract from untreated willow failed to support any measurable algal growth. Under continuous light (24 h), algae grown in lime-treated biomass extracts showed a comparable growth rate to TAP-grown cultures until day 4 (growth rate ranged from 0.77 to 1.17/day; Fig. 1C). After that, algal growth in treated biomass-derived extract halted, whereas cultures in TAP medium continued to grow until the eighth day. Under continuous light, the additional photosynthetic energy available to TAP-grown cultures, together with the absence of potential extract-derived inhibitory or stress-associated compounds, might have allowed continued growth beyond day 4. Algal density in the lime-treated samples under 24 h light was similar to that under 12 h light conditions. In complete darkness, cultures grown in both lime-treated biomass extract and TAP showed limited algal growth with the growth rates ranging from 0.8 to 1.6/day (Fig. 1D) whereas cultures grown in untreated biomass extracts showed no measurable growth. This response is consistent with the ability of C. reinhardtii to grow heterotrophically in the absence of light when acetate is available as an organic carbon and energy source (Boyle et al., 2017). Both TAP medium and lime-treated biomass extracts contained comparable acetate concentrations (1.0-1.2 g/L), whereas untreated biomass extracts contained little or no recoverable acetate (Fig. 1A). Therefore, the improved growth observed in TAP and lime-treated biomass extracts under dark conditions was likely supported by acetate availability, while the limited growth across all treatments reflects the absence of photosynthetic energy input. Together, these results suggest that acetate recovery from lime-treated lignocellulosic biomass might offer a potential advantage in catastrophic or supply-disrupted settings by converting locally available plant biomass into an organic carbon source that can partially support algal growth when light availability and conventional agricultural inputs are limited.

Lime pretreatment enhances enzymatic glucose yield from lignocellulosic biomass

To assess the impact of lime pretreatment on enzymatic saccharification of the remaining lignocellulosic residue, we measured glucose release from willow and switchgrass biomass subjected to the same milling and lime treatments described above upon incubation with Cellic CTec2®️. Lime-treated residues yielded significantly more glucose (11–22% w/w, dry basis (db)) than untreated controls (0.2–4.8% w/w, db), except in the case of Wiley-milled willow (Fig. 2A). These improvements could be attributable to the alkaline treatment, which partially removes lignin, disrupts ester bonds between lignin and hemicellulose, and increases biomass porosity, thereby enhancing enzyme accessibility to cellulose (Kim and Holtzapple, 2005; Sun and Cheng, 2002). Wiley-milled, lime-treated switchgrass yielded more glucose (22% w/w, db) than ball milled, lime-treated switchgrass. In contrast, lime-treated, ball-milled willow yielded more glucose (20% w/w, db) than its Wiley-milled counterpart, suggesting that optimal combinations of biomass type and milling method maximize saccharification efficiency. Algal protein and lipid content from growth in plant biomass-derived media

Protein content in C. reinhardtii grown in plant-derived media ranged from 4.2% to 9.8% (w/w, db) with no significant statistical differences between willow and switchgrass extracts (Fig. 2B). However, protein content was significantly higher in algae cultured in standard TAP medium (29.7% w/w, db), whereas minimal TAP (lacking acetate) supported the lowest protein accumulation. Although the protein content of TAP-grown algae was lower than values commonly reported for Chlamydomonas grown under optimal, non-stress conditions, this difference may be partly explained by the low-light cultivation conditions used in the present study. The cultures were grown under a low-light regime, which might have limited photosynthetic energy generation and overall protein accumulation compared with standard laboratory cultivation conditions. In contrast, lipid content varied substantially among treatments (Fig. 2C). Algae grown in lime-treated switchgrass extract exhibited the highest lipid content (31.1% w/w, db), followed closely by lime-treated willow (30.3% w/w, db), both of which were significantly higher than algae grown in TAP medium (15.3% w/w, db). Even untreated biomass extracts supported lipid accumulation levels between 20.4% and 27.1% w/w, substantially exceeding those from TAP or minimal TAP controls. Because the present analysis quantified only protein and lipid fractions, the remaining dry biomass fraction was not separately measured. This fraction likely consisted mainly of carbohydrates, including starch and cell wall polysaccharides, along with pigments such as chlorophyll, nucleic acids, ash/minerals, and other minor cellular metabolites. This shift from protein to fat production is likely due to compounds in the biomass hydrolysate such as phenolics or furan derivatives, which might interfere with algal metabolism and protein synthesis. When microalgae are exposed to these byproducts, the resulting abiotic stress triggers a redirection of metabolic pathways similar to the response observed during nutrient limitation (Dodangodage et al., 2026; Rawat et al., 2021). Under such conditions, cellular resources can be shifted away from energy-intensive processes like protein biosynthesis and cell proliferation in favor of synthesizing energy-storage metabolites, particularly neutral lipids like triacylglycerols (Miazek et al., 2014; Rawat et al., 2021; Palanisamy et al., 2023). These results suggest that while plant-derived media may not match the protein-generating capacity of standard laboratory medium, they can support substantial production of lipids, which are essential for meeting dietary fat requirements.

Potential energy yield of a food system comprising lignocellulosic biomass and green algae

According to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020–2025, a healthy daily energy intake should consist of 45-65% carbohydrates (predominantly complex carbohydrates and fiber, limiting simple sugars to less than 10%), 10-35% protein, and 20-35% fat (USDA, 2020). In the integrated system under study here, we posit that carbohydrates are supplied by saccharified plant biomass (e.g., willow or switchgrass), whereas protein and fat are derived from algal biomass (Fig. 2D–F). On a per-kilogram basis of plant biomass feedstock, lime-treated switchgrass yields enough glucose to theoretically provide 67-97% of the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for carbohydrates, whereas other treatments contribute 5–40% (Fig. 2D). However, because this carbohydrate output consists only of free glucose rather than complex carbohydrates, relying on it as a main food source does not meet standard dietary guidelines. In an extreme agricultural collapse scenario, meeting long-term metabolic ideals is secondary to preventing immediate mass starvation. Therefore, these sugars must be viewed strictly as a source of acute, short-term survival energy. Furthermore, protein production was a major limitation of the system. Algae grown on biomass-derived media contained substantially less protein than algae grown in standard TAP medium, resulting in the potential for only a modest contribution to daily protein requirements. Lime-treated switchgrass could provide the highest contribution, meeting approximately 5.1% of the RDA, whereas the other treatments ranged from 1 to 3% (Fig. 2E). In terms of fat, lime-treated willow and switchgrass supplied 12.6–14.2% of the RDA, while untreated biomass- supported algal growth that yielded only 0–2% of the fat RDA (Fig. 2F). Ultimately, while this system might provide emergency caloric support through simple carbohydrates and fat, its limited protein yield indicates that it should be supplemented with additional food streams as soon as logistically feasible.

Life-cycle impacts of biomass-derived versus standard algal media

We compared the environmental impact of lipid production from algae using two cultivation systems. The first system used standard algal medium, while the second system used plant-derived hydrolysate as the carbon source and wood as the heat source. Glucose was considered to be a coproduct (Supplementary Fig. S1). Across most midpoint and endpoint categories, the biomass hydrolysate system showed lower environmental impacts than the standard medium system (Fig. 3A and B). These improvements stem primarily from replacing fossil-based energy and commercial nutrients with renewable biomass feedstock and wood derived thermal energy, which together minimize greenhouse gas emissions and dependency on industrial chemical production. Similar findings have been reported for other integrated systems incorporating algal biomass where nutrient recycling and bio-based energy sources lower cradle-to-gate impacts by 40-70% (Bello et al., 2017; Yuan et al., 2015). However, the hydrolysate system in the present study showed larger detrimental impacts on land use and ecosystems (Fig. 3A–C), reflecting the inherent trade-off between resource circularity and land occupation intrinsic to the ReCiPe 2016 model (Huijbregts et al., 2017). Because switchgrass in this analysis represents pre-existing or marginal biomass rather than dedicated, cultivated feedstock, such land-use penalties likely overestimate true biodiversity impacts, a limitation noted in similar LCAs of lignocellulosic bioenergy systems (Cherubini and Strømman, 2011). At the single score level (Fig. 3C), which integrates human health, ecosystems, and resources, the biomass derived system achieves ∼50% lower total environmental burden than the standard system. Although acid and strong alkali-based pretreatments are widely used for lignocellulosic biomass conversion, they were not included as direct LCA comparators in the present analysis because the functional unit of this study was algal lipid production from biomass-derived media rather than sugar production alone. Qualitatively, lime treatment may offer practical advantages in resource-constrained settings because lime is inexpensive, less corrosive than mineral acids, and potentially recoverable, whereas acid pretreatments often require corrosion-resistant equipment, neutralization, and wastewater handling, and strong alkali-based pretreatments require more complex recovery and more specialized infrastructure (Brodeur et al., 2011; Baruah et al., 2018). However, because these alternatives were not modeled here, the present LCA should be interpreted as a comparison between biomass-derived algal media and standard algal medium, not as evidence that lime pretreatment is environmentally superior to acid or strong alkali pretreatment.

We also performed a mass balance analysis of a theoretical integrated biomass-algae bioconversion (Supplementary Fig. S2). Pretreating 34.4 kg of switchgrass with 6.9 kg Ca(OH)2 and 1204 kg water yielded 10 kg of fresh (∼1 kg dry) algal biomass from a 1244 L pond along with 7.5 kg of glucose obtained from remaining plant biomass after enzymatic saccharification. Overall, the system achieved near-complete water recovery, demonstrating a closed-loop, nutrient-producing process.

Strengths and limitations of the proposed biomass-algae food system

The system's low complexity, modular design enhances its practical deployment potential in resource-constrained environments. In designing the experimental system, we prioritized minimal and readily available inputs, particularly lignocellulosic biomass and lime, to reflect conditions where access to refined chemicals and centralized supply chains may be limited. Lime is inexpensive, widely available, and can be safely handled in bulk. It effectively cleaves acetyl esters, liberating acetic acid into the liquid fraction, enhancing enzymatic digestibility of cellulose in the plant biomass at moderate temperatures. Furthermore, it can be recovered and re-calcined to regenerate lime (Baruah et al., 2018; Grimaldi et al., 2015). Thermal processing steps such as boiling could, in principle, be achieved using locally available fuels, including wood, coal, or other combustible materials, although fuel availability and emissions would need to be considered in any practical deployment scenario. Although enzymatic hydrolysis does require enzyme input, these enzymes could be produced locally via microbial fermentation or even be replaced by microbial pretreatment/cotreatment methods (Baruah et al., 2018). However, on-site enzyme production would require additional resources, including suitable microbial strains, nutrients, fermentation capacity, process control, and energy inputs (Ellilä et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Barta et al., 2010). Algal cultivation can be carried out in open ponds, and when acetate is available, even minimal lighting, such as low-intensity or periodic lighting from LEDs powered by portable generators, could support productive growth (Fig. 1B–D). Nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, other micronutrients) could be recycled from waste streams or other local resources. By situating the system near biomass sources such as forests or agricultural residues, food production could remain largely self-contained, making it well-suited for post-disaster or low-resource settings.

Despite these advantages, several challenges must be addressed before deployment. This system is not intended for deployment in blast zones or regions with severe radioactive contamination. Rather, it is aimed at areas indirectly affected by reduced sunlight, cooling, disrupted agriculture, and limited infrastructure. In this study, we experimentally simulated only low-light cultivation, and did not evaluate the effects of radiation, outdoor temperature fluctuation, precipitation changes, contamination, or microbial competition, which would need to be tested before deployment under real post-catastrophic conditions. Moreover, the elevated algal lipid content observed in the biomass-derived media might reflect a stress response rather than improved overall nutritional productivity. Such stress could redirect metabolism toward lipid storage while reducing protein accumulation and biomass yield. Therefore, harsher post-catastrophic stressors than those tested here could further compromise algal growth and macronutrient production, and this system would require validation under controlled cultivation conditions before practical use. Acetate yield is inherently limited by biomass acetyl content, so large quantities of feedstock are required to produce substantial algal biomass (Pawar et al., 2013). The nutrient profile of biomass hydrolysate might lack adequate nitrogen and micronutrients (vitamins, trace metals) (Ahmad et al., 2022), forcing reliance on nutritional supplements for the algae. These factors could restrict the scalability of the system outside controlled environments. Alternative edible algae such as Chlorella, Scenedesmus, or even genetically modified Chlamydomonas strains might be more robust and better suited to growth in biomass-derived media (Babu et al., 2022). Feedstock variability also poses risks due to toxic compounds, heavy metals, or anti-nutrients in biomass which could carry through to final products, necessitating rigorous feedstock screening (Kundu et al., 2024; Satpati and Pal, 2018). Plant biomass saccharification is an enzyme-intensive process and requires controlled conditions. Exploring on-site enzyme production or microbial hydrolysis could improve resilience and reduce external inputs (Isikgor and Becer, 2015).

Energy and logistical considerations are equally important. Each step (i.e., pretreatment, hydrolysis, extraction, algal cultivation, and downstream processing) demands energy inputs and infrastructure. Detailed engineering analyses (pond design, scale-up trials, and cost modeling) will be critical to determine overall feasibility and sustainability. Future efforts include optimization of pretreatment methods, implementation of nutrient recycling from algal residues and extracts, and evaluation of high-yield algal strains or co-culture systems to enhance overall conversion efficiency and nutrient profiles of resulting foods. Pilot-scale trials under simulated emergency conditions are also essential to assess safety, reliability, and nutritional adequacy.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates the conceptual feasibility of an integrated biomass-to-food system that combines lime pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, and algal cultivation to produce food-relevant carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids under conditions simulating severe agricultural collapse. Lime-treated lignocellulosic biomass yielded an acetate-rich liquid fraction that supported low-light algal cultivation. The resulting algal biomass was enriched in lipids, but protein production remained limited compared with growth in standard TAP medium. The post-lime solid residue also yielded glucose after enzymatic hydrolysis at levels that exceeded glucose yields from untreated biomass. Therefore, the system should be viewed primarily as a complementary source of lipids and carbohydrates, rather than a complete food system or a major protein source in its current form. Translating this concept into a field-ready setup will demand solutions for limited acetate production, low protein yield, variable biomass quality, nutrient availability, enzyme provisioning, infrastructure, and the energy and logistical requirements of the entire process. With targeted strain improvements, process optimizations, nutrient supplementation, and pilot-scale validation, such a system could offer a resilient, decentralized food components if conventional agriculture fails.
