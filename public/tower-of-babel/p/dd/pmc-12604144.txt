Capsule endoscopy findings following negative second look endoscopy in patients with suspected small bowel bleeding at a tertiary care center in Sri Lanka
Authors: Tilan Aponso, W. M. D. A. S. Wanninayake, N. M. M. Nawarathne, Ranjith Peiris, Nilesh Fernandopulle, I. P. Wijesinghe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsSmall bowel bleeding poses a significant diagnostic challenge for clinicians, and capsule endoscopy is currently considered the investigation of choice for evaluating suspected cases. However, evidence regarding the role of second-look endoscopy before capsule endoscopy is limited. This study aimed to evaluate capsule endoscopy findings after a negative second-look endoscopy in patients with suspected small bowel bleeding at a tertiary care center in Sri Lanka.MethodThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed data from patients who underwent capsule endoscopy for suspected small bowel bleeding at the National Hospital of Sri Lanka from June 1, 2018, to March 31, 2023. All patients had negative results on second-look endoscopies and abdominal imaging (CT, MRI, Enterography). The principal investigator reassessed all the capsule recordings at 3–6 frames per second in single-image viewing mode. The study primarily evaluated demographic data, capsule endoscopy findings, and mucosal visualization. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median. Comparisons for continuous variables were performed using independent-sample t-test and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe sample size was 71, with a mean age of 54.41 ± 18.44 years. 60.6% of patients were males. Overt bleeding occurred in 52.1% of patients, while occult bleeding occurred in 47.9%. The mean small intestine transit time was 5:16:30 ± 1:48:01 h. Hemoglobin was significantly lower (p < 0.001) in patients with overt bleeding, with a mean level of 9.27 ± 1.27 g/dL. Capsule endoscopy identified 30 patients (42.3%) with a definite cause for small bowel bleeding and 22 patients (31.0%) with a probable cause, resulting in an overall diagnostic yield of 73.2%. The proximal small intestine was the most commonly affected segment of the small bowel in cases of small bowel bleeding, accounting for 33 patients (46.5%). None of the patients had lesions in the endoscopically accessible sites of the upper or lower gastrointestinal tract. We noted vascular findings in 18 patients (25.4%), with inflammatory findings accounting for the majority (n = 30/42.3%). The predominant treatment option was medical management (71.8%), followed by endoscopic interventions (26.8%) and interventional radiological interventions (2.8%). The number of polyethylene glycol sachets used for bowel preparation did not affect the diagnostic yield or mucosal visualization quality.ConclusionCapsule endoscopy demonstrated a high diagnostic yield in Sri Lankan patients with suspected small bowel bleeding who had normal initial and second-look endoscopies. The proximal small intestine was the commonest involved segment, and, contrary to previous studies, inflammatory lesions were the predominant finding. We recommend low-volume bowel preparation as the number of polyethylene glycol sachets did not affect the diagnostic yield or quality of mucosal visualization. This study supports integrating capsule endoscopy into routine clinical practice for evaluating gastrointestinal bleeding and recommends performing a second-look endoscopy before proceeding to capsule endoscopy.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal bleeding is the leading cause of gastrointestinal-related admissions and emergencies. Approximately 50% of cases result from upper gastrointestinal tract bleeding, 40% from lower gastrointestinal tract bleeding and 5% to 10% from small bowel bleeding [1]. Small bowel bleeding originates between the ligament of Treitz and the ileocecal valve, and the etiology is often not detected on routine endoscopy. Small bowel bleeding presents a significant diagnostic challenge and results in a delay in the specific management and consequent increase in morbidity and mortality [1].

Before the introduction of capsule endoscopy in 2001, small bowel bleeding was primarily evaluated using abdominal imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and small bowel enteroclysis [2]. Although CT and MRI provide accurate assessment of solid organs, lymph nodes, and vessels, they can only offer limited information about the small intestinal mucosal abnormalities. Small bowel enteroclysis specifically targets the small intestine, but demonstrates low sensitivity and specificity in recognizing small and flat small bowel wall lesions [2] with diagnostic yields reported between 10% and 21% [3]. Push enteroscopy has a diagnostic yield of 26% for clinically significant findings, markedly lower than that of capsule endoscopy [4]. Capsule endoscopy outperforms push enteroscopy, small bowel enteroclysis, and computed tomography in identifying the cause of small bowel bleeding. In recent times, capsule endoscopy has established its position in the management algorithm for small bowel bleeding, which has had a significant impact on the outcome [5, 6].

Over the years, the utility of second-look endoscopies prior to capsule endoscopies has been debated. Furthermore, data on capsule endoscopy for small bowel bleeding in Asian populations remain limited, and the etiology and clinical associations of small bowel bleeding may differ from those observed in Western populations. This study aims to evaluate the capsule endoscopy findings and associated factors in Sri Lankan patients with negative second-look endoscopies, highlighting the unique contribution of this research.

Methods

Study collective and capsule system

We enrolled 80 patients with suspected small bowel bleeding between June 1, 2018, and March 31, 2023, at the National Hospital of Sri Lanka. All patients who underwent capsule endoscopy had negative initial gastroscopy and colonoscopy findings and a negative second-look gastroscopy and colonoscopy. Prior to capsule endoscopy, cross-sectional abdominal imaging and small bowel enteroclysis were performed in patients with obstructive abdominal symptoms, elevated inflammatory markers, and positive stool calprotectin. Capsule endoscopy was not performed in patients with small intestine strictures or malignancies on imaging; these patients were referred for alternative investigations. Nine capsule studies were excluded due to poor bowel preparation, slow transit, or technical issues.

All procedures were performed using MiroCam® Capsule Endoscope System (MC1600). This single- use system consists of a wireless capsule, a data recorder, and a workstation. The capsule is 1.0 × 2.4 cm in size with a 170° field of view, captures three frames per second, and has a battery life of approximately 11 h. It generates high-resolution (320 × 320-pixel) images allowing detailed inspection of the gastrointestinal mucosa. Data are transmitted wirelessly to a wearable receiver via ultra-high-frequency band radio telemetry and subsequently reviewed and interpreted using specialized capsule endoscopy software.

Indications

Capsule endoscopy was performed in patients with suspected small bowel bleeding. In patients presenting with gastrointestinal bleeding but negative findings on initial upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy, small bowel bleeding was suspected. Some patients in the study had overt gastrointestinal bleeding, while others had occult gastrointestinal bleeding. All patients suspected of small bowel bleeding in this study underwent negative second-look endoscopies and abdominal imaging prior to capsule endoscopy.

Mucosal visualization

Mucosal visualization was assessed according to a 10-point grading scale proposed by Brotz et al., which evaluate five parameters; percentage of mucosa visualized, presence of fluid and debris, presence of bubbles, bile and chime staining, and brightness (4). Adequate mucosal visualization is defined as a total score of 7 or more, while inadequate mucosal visualization is defined as a total score below 7. Capsule studies with a mucosal visualization score below 4 were excluded from this study.

CE evaluation

All capsule endoscopies were evaluated by a senior clinical fellow in gastroenterology using 3–6 frames per second in single-image viewing mode. Clinical records of all patients were retrospectively reviewed and results from the gastroscopy, colonoscopy, and the capsule endoscopy database at the National Hospital of Sri Lanka were examined to obtain relevant information for this study. Small intestinal transit time was defined as the time from the first duodenal image to the first caecal image. The proximal small intestine was defined as the portion examined in the first half of the small intestinal transit time, and the distal small intestine as the portion examined in the second half of the small intestinal transit time.

Capsule endoscopy findings were classified according to the Saurin classification, which includes no lesions (P0), slightly significant lesions (erosions, xanthomas, lymphoid nodular hyperplasia) as P1, and highly significant lesions (angiodysplasia, tumors, ulcers, hookworms, and other parasites) as P2. The diagnostic yield was calculated by counting the number of patients who had positive test results (P1 plus P2) among all patients who underwent testing [7].Vascular lesions detected during capsule endoscopy were further categorized according to Yano Yamamoto’s classification as type 1a (< 1 mm punctate erythema), type 1b (>1 mm patchy erythema), type 2a (punctate lesion with pulsatile bleeding), type 2b (pulsatile red protrusion without surrounding venous dilatation), type 3 (pulsatile red protrusion with surrounding venous dilatation), and type 4 (other lesions) [7].The capsule endoscopy Crohn’s disease activity index (CECDAI) was used to classify ulcers identified during the procedure with scoring based on the inflammation, stricture, and the severity of the disease [7]. This study assessed the scores associated with histologically proven Crohn’s disease and intestinal tuberculosis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 for Windows. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as percentages.

Comparisons between two independent groups (overt vs. occult bleeding) for continuous variables such as hemoglobin level were performed using the independent samples t-test. This test was selected because outcome variables were continuous and met the assumption of normality. Association between diagnostic yield (positive: P1/P2 lesions vs. negative: P0 lesions) and mucosal visualization quality (adequate : ≥7 and inadequate :<7) was assessed using the chi-squared test. When any expected frequency was < 5, the Fisher’s exact test was applied to ensure the validity of the results.

To identify the predictors of diagnostic yield, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. Logistic regression was chosen because it allows modeling of a binary dependent variable (diagnostic yield positive vs. negative) while simultaneously adjusting for multiple continuous and categorical predictors such as age, hemoglobin level and small intestine transit time. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported to quantify the strength of association. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Prior to analysis, all relavant statistical assumptions were evaluated to ensure the appropriateness of the selected tests.

Results

The total sample size was 71 patients. The analysis focused on the demographic characteristics, indications for capsule endoscopy, the relationship between transit times and diagnostic yield, mucosal visualization quality, and the categorization of findings using established scoring systems. Capsule endoscopy findings and the associations were also assessed. The study included 43 male participants (60.6%) and 28 female participants (39.4%) with suspected small bowel bleeding. The mean age was 54.41 ± 18.44. Among these patients, 52.1% had overt bleeding, while 47.9% had occult gastrointestinal bleeding, warranting further evaluation. The mean hemoglobin level was significantly lower in patients with overt bleeding (8.47 ± 0.97) compared to those with occult bleeding (10.11 ± 0.97, p-value < 0.001). Logistic regression was performed to evaluate the factors associated with the diagnostic yield of capsule endoscopy. Age (OR 0.957, 95% CI: 0.921–0.993, p = 0.021) and hemoglobin (OR 0.578, 95% CI: 0.347–0.961, p = 0.035) were identified as predictors of diagnostic yield, while small intestine transit time was not (OR 1.000, 95% CI 0.998–1.002, p = 0.269). These findings suggest that lower hemoglobin levels and increasing age are independently associated with higher diagnostic yield in capsule endoscopy.

Known medical conditions

Among patients with suspected small bowel bleeding, the majority (38.0%) had no comorbidities. The most common comorbidity was diabetes mellitus, present in 16 (22.5%) patients, followed by ischemic heart disease in 13 (18.3%). Ten patients (14.1%) were receiving antiplatelet therapy and a similar proportion (14.1%) was reported using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within the preceding month. Mucosal visualization

A total of 47 participants (66.2%) used 2 sachets of polyethylene glycol (59 g x 2), while 24 participants (33.8%) used 4 sachets (59 g x 4). According to Brotz et al. grading system, 27 (38%) patients had inadequate mucosal visualization (< 7), while 44 (62%) patients had adequate mucosal visualization (≥ 7). Capsule endoscopy findings revealed that 19 patients (26.8%) had P0 lesions (no bleeding potential), 22 patients (31%) had P1 lesions (uncertain bleeding potential), and 30 patients (42.2%) had P2 lesions (high bleeding potential).The overall diagnostic yield was 73.2%. There was no significant association between the quality of the mucosal visualization and the diagnostic yield of capsule endoscopy (χ² = 0.046, df = 1, p = 0.830).

A stratified analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between diagnostic yield and mucosal visualization quality according to the number of polyethylene glycol (PEG) sachets used for bowel preparation. In the 2 × 59 g PEG group, no significant association was observed (χ²=0.3, p = 0.581). Similarly, in the 4 × 59 g PEG group, no significant association was noted (χ²=0.29, p = 0.589). Tests of homogeneity indicated no significant difference between the strata specific effect estimates (p = 0.801). The pooled effect across strata was also not statistically significant (χ² = 0.54, p = 0.464). These results suggest that the number of PEG sachets used for bowel preparation did not modify the association between diagnostic yield and quality of mucosal visualization.

Discussion

Capsule endoscopy has revolutionized the evaluation of suspected small bowel bleeding, providing a reliable method to visualize the small intestinal wall while avoiding sedation, surgery, or radiation exposure. A recent meta-analysis reported that the diagnostic yield of capsule endoscopy in patients with obscure gastrointestinal bleeding ranged from 38% to 87% [8, 9]. In this study, the overall diagnostic yield was 73.2%. Specifically, the diagnostic yield was 80.9% for overt bleeding, and 65.7% for occult bleeding. The extended evaluation time at 3–6 frames per second, single-image viewing by a senior clinical fellow in gastroenterology, a 170-degree field of view of the capsule, and meticulous reporting of lesions according to validated definitions and classifications may have contributed to the relatively higher detection rate in our study.

This study observed a mean patient age of 54.41 years, with a predominance of older adults, particularly those over 60 years (47.9%). These findings align with existing literature, which reports that the incidence of small bowel bleeding increases with age, often due to the accumulation of comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, and the effects of long-term medication use [10]. The gender distribution showed a male predominance (60.6%), in contrast to some previous studies that reported a higher prevalence among females [11, 12]. However, prior research suggests that the overall incidence of small bowel bleeding does not significantly differ between sexes; the underlying etiologies may vary more with age than gender [10–12]. Recognizing these demographic trends can help healthcare providers identify at-risk populations and optimize diagnostic and management strategies.

Bowel preparation prior to capsule endoscopy remains a topic of debate. The first manufacturer recommended a specialized diet and 12 h of fasting before the procedure but did not recommend bowel preparation with laxatives. In contrast, the 2018 European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guideline recommends a modified diet the day before the procedure, along with bowel preparation using 2 L of polyethylene glycol and antifoaming agents like simethicone for improved visualization [13]. In our study, the amount of polyethylene glycol used (59 g x 2 vs. 59 g x 4) for bowel preparation did not affect the association between diagnostic yield and mucosal visualization. This may be due to our evaluation method (3–6 frames per second, single image view), which could mitigate the impact of suboptimal mucosal visualization. Nevertheless, excellent mucosal visualization remains essential for thorough evaluation, even though it did not appear to influence the diagnostic yield in this study.

Our findings on distribution of the lesions within the small intestine showed that most were located within the proximal segment (46.5%), followed by the distal segment (18.3%). Some participants had lesions in both proximal and distal regions (12.7%), while 22.5% had normal findings. These results highlight the importance of targeted evaluation of specific small intestinal segments when investigating the potential bleeding sources. Using a low frame rate is particularly important when reviewing the proximal small intestine, due to the higher likelihood of lesions in this area and faster capsule transit compared to the distal small intestine.

The distribution of findings in this study revealed that inflammatory lesions were the most prevalent (42.3%), followed by vascular lesions (25.4%). In contrast, studies from western countries and some Asian studies have reported vascular lesions as the leading cause of small bowel bleeding, with prevalence ranging from 51% to 79% [14–18]. However, several Asian studies found inflammatory lesions to be the most common (41%–53%), followed by vascular lesions (23%–24%), consistent with the findings of our study [16–18]. We propose that genetic factors, diet, and lifestyle in Asian populations may contribute to these differences, an area that warrants further research.

Among the vascular lesions detected during the capsule endoscopy, 10 patients (14.1%) had involvement in the proximal small intestine, 6 patients (8.5%) in the distal small intestine, and 2 patients (2.8%) in both proximal and distal segments. Previous studies have reported similar findings to our study, indicating that the proximal small bowel (jejunum and duodenum) is the most common site for vascular lesions which are reachable by push enteroscopy [19–21].

During the evaluation of comorbidities in patients with vascular lesions, we found that 6 patients (33.3% of all vascular lesions) had diabetes, 5 patients (27.8% of all vascular lesions) were on antiplatelet therapy, 2 patients (11.1%) had chronic kidney disease, 3 patients (16.7%) had chronic liver disease, and one patient (5.6%) was taking a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID). These findings reinforce the notion that small bowel bleeding often occurs in the context of complex comorbidities. The interaction between these comorbidities and small bowel pathology is crucial for developing effective treatment plans. Understanding the broader health context of these patients can facilitate more tailored and effective management strategies.

Inflammatory lesions, including ulcers (29.6%) and erosions (12.7%), were the most common cause of small bowel bleeding (42.3%) in this study, consistent with an Indian study where 66 (42.3%) out of 156 patients had ulcers or erosions [22]. Among the erosions detected during the capsule endoscopy, all the patients had proximal small intestine involvement. One patient with ulcers was histologically confirmed to have intestinal tuberculosis (CECDAI-15), and five patients with ulcers were confirmed to have Crohn’s disease (23.8% of all the ulcers). Their CECDAI scores were 12, 15, 20, 32, and 32. and both proximal and distal small intestines were involved in all these cases. In our study Crohn’s disease was diagnosed in only 16.7% of patients with inflammatory lesions, in contrast to an Indian study reported a prevalence of 63.6% in similar patients [22]. Notably, no malignancies were detected in our cohort. The use of second-look endoscopy, cross-sectional abdominal imaging, and small bowel enteroclysis prior to capsule endoscopy may have contributed to these findings in our study.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study, despite its valuable insights into the characteristics and findings associated with small bowel bleeding. First, the retrospective design inherently limits the ability to establish a causal relationship. Second, the relatively small sample size may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Prospective studies with larger cohorts are necessary to validate these findings and provide a comprehensive understanding of capsule endoscopy outcomes. Future research should also focus on long-term patient outcomes, including the effectiveness of various therapeutic interventions, to optimize management strategies. Moreover, investigating associations between risk factors and specific lesion types may further refine clinical decision-making. Finally, the incorporation of advanced imaging modalities and emerging technologies could enhance diagnostic accuracy and expand the role of capsule endoscopy in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Capsule endoscopy demonstrates a high diagnostic yield in Sri Lankans suspecting small bowel bleeding who had normal initial and second-look endoscopies. The proximal small intestine was the most commonly involved, and examiners should focus on this area to maximize the diagnostic yield. We recommend using low-volume bowel preparation because the number of polyethylene glycol sachets used for it did not affect either the diagnostic yield or mucosal visualization quality.
