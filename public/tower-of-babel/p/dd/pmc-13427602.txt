Prevalence and correlates of co-use of alcohol and cigarettes among Chinese adolescents: a cross-sectional national survey in 2021
Authors: Qingqing Xu, Ning Ji, Fulin Huang, Xinying Zeng, Ying Liu, Shanshan Liu, Shiwei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlcohol and cigarette use are prevalent among Chinese adolescents. This study examines the prevalence and correlates of co-use.MethodsData were drawn from the 2021 China Adolescent Alcohol Use Survey targeting middle and high school students, conducted in seven provinces from China's seven geographic regions. We examined the past-month prevalence of alcohol and cigarettes co-use across sociodemographic and behavioral subgroups. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with co-use.ResultsPast-month co-use prevalence was 1.7% (95% CI: 1.4%, 2.1%), and varied by peer substance use, with higher rates among those whose close friends smoked or drank. Co-use rates were higher among adolescents whose parents held favourable attitudes toward drinking (either parent: 4.0%, 95% CI: 3.1%, 4.9%; both parents: 3.7%, 95% CI: 2.9%, 4.5%) than among those whose parents did not (0.9%, 95% CI: 0.7%, 1.1%). Past-month e-cigarette users had a significantly higher co-use rate than non-users. Peer substance use, e-cigarette use, and parental attitudes were significantly associated with co-use.ConclusionsAlthough the prevalence of co-use among Chinese adolescents is low, the absolute number of affected youth is substantial given China's population size. Prevention should target peer influence and e-cigarette use.

Introduction

Tobacco and alcohol use are major threats to public health. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 reported that tobacco use caused 7.3 million deaths (12.8% of global deaths), while high alcohol use led to 1.3 million deaths (2.2% of global deaths) (GBD 2023 Causes of Death Collaborators, 2025; GBD 2023 Disease and Injury and Risk Factor Collaborators, 2025). Tobacco and alcohol use often begin in adolescence and persist into adulthood, contributing to a range of later-life health problems (Parada et al., 2025). Adolescent substance use therefore warrants particular attention. Smoking rate among Chinese middle school students declined from 5.9% in 2014 to 3.9% in 2019 and further decreased to 3.3% in 2021, while rates among high school students increased from 5.6% in 2019 to 6.5% in 2021 (Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2015; Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022; Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). The 2021 China Adolescent Alcohol Use Survey found that 44.1%, 32.7%, and 11.2% of adolescents had consumed alcohol in their lifetime, in the past year and in the past month, respectively (Huang et al., 2026).

Studies consistently demonstrate that students who drank were more likely to smoke and vice versa (Garnett et al., 2022; Weitzman and Chen, 2005; Chen et al., 2018), indicating that substance use behaviors co-occur, a phenomenon known as behavioral clustering (Whitaker et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024). The co-use of tobacco and alcohol amplifies health risks beyond those of either substance alone. Light alcohol use and moderate smoking significantly increased head and neck cancer risk (relative risk [RR] 4.26), with heavy use of both substances further elevating risk (RR 35.24) (Jun et al., 2024). In Denmark, dual use of cigarettes and alcohol fell from 18.1% to 8.6% among adolescents (Kjeld et al., 2022a). In the United States, dual use among high school students dropped from 11.8% to 1.7% (Dai, 2019). However, nationally representative estimates of co-use among Chinese adolescents are not available. This study aimed to provide relevant evidence for the development of effective strategies to control the co-use of tobacco and alcohol in China.

Methods

Study design and population

The data originate from the 2021 China Youth Alcohol Use Survey, conducted in seven provinces across China. This survey is the first to integrate alcohol use into an existing cigarette monitoring framework, designed to monitor alcohol and cigarette consumption and related factors among Chinese adolescents. Informed consent was obtained from both school administrators and student respondents. Under the supervision of teachers and trained investigators, participants anonymously completed paper questionnaires in a classroom setting. The survey was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 202115).

This survey employed a multistage stratified cluster probability sampling method to obtain a nationally representative sample of middle and high school students in the Mainland of China. First, one province was selected from each of China's seven geographic regions. Within each province, 10 counties/districts (5 urban districts and 5 rural counties) were chosen using probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) sampling. In each selected county/district, three middle schools (Grades 7–9, aged 13–15), two academic high schools (Grades 10–12, aged 16–18), and one vocational high school (Grades 10–12, aged 16–18) were randomly selected. One class per grade was randomly selected in each school, and all students in those classes were invited to participate in the survey. Detailed methods were published elsewhere (Huang et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2024).

Measures

The primary outcome variables were exclusive alcohol use, exclusive cigarette use, and co-use. Alcohol use was measured by asking ‘Have you had at least one drink in the past 12 months?’; the possible responses were 1 (No), 2 (Yes, before the previous 30 days), or 3 (Yes, in the previous 30 days). Those reporting past-30-day use were classified as current drinkers. Cigarette use was defined by asking the question: ‘How many days have you smoked cigarettes during the past 30 days?’ (0, 1–9, 10–19, 20–29, or 30 days). If students chose more than 0 days, they were defined as current smokers. Co-use was defined as using both alcohol and cigarettes in the past 30 days. Substance use was defined as using either cigarettes or alcohol. Exclusive cigarette use was defined as smoking cigarettes without concurrent alcohol use, and exclusive alcohol use was defined as drinking alcohol without concurrent cigarette use.

The covariates included a range of sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics. Pocket money was measured by asking: ‘How much money do you have at your disposal (no matter how you spend) in a week on average?’ (≤10, 11–30, 31–50, or >50 RMB). Parental tobacco/alcohol use was evaluated by asking whether father or mother frequently consumed tobacco/alcohol?’ (neither, mother/father, or both). Parental attitudes favourable toward drinking were evaluated by asking whether your parents allowed the respondent to drink alcohol?’ (neither allows, mother/father allows, or both allow). Peer tobacco/alcohol use was assessed by asking whether close friends consume tobacco/alcohol?’ (none/some, or most/all). School health education was assessed by asking whether respondents had learned in class that drinking/ smoking is harmful to health in the past 12 months (no or yes). Pro-alcohol advertising exposure was assessed by asking how often respondents saw any advertisements or signs promoting alcohol products in the past 30 days across five venues (TV, billboards, newspapers/magazines, internet, and events). Frequency was coded as no access, never, sometimes, or often. Those reporting ‘sometimes’ or ‘often’ in any venue were classified as exposed. Pro-tobacco advertising exposure was defined by asking two questions: ‘In the past 30 days, have you seen any tobacco advertisements or promotions at retail outlets or on the Internet?’ (no access, no, or yes). Respondents answering ‘yes’ to either question were classified as exposed. Anti-tobacco/alcohol advertisement exposure was measured by asking: ‘In the past 30 days, have you seen or heard any information on television, radio, Internet, etc., indicating that drinking alcohol/smoking is harmful to health?’ (no or yes). E-cigarette use was defined by asking the question: ‘How many days have you vaped during the past 30 days?’ (0, 1–9, 10–19, 20–29, or 30 days). Those reporting any vaping days were classified as current e-cigarette users.

Statistics

Considering the complexity of the sampling design, weighted approaches were applied to all estimations in this study. We determined the total weight of each respondent by 3 weights: the base weight, the non-response adjustment weight, and the post-stratification adjustment weight. We calculated the base weight by multiplying the primary sampling unit weight (reciprocal of sampling probability by population), the school weight (reciprocal of sampling probability by the number of students), and the class weight (total number of classes for this grade). We obtained the non-response adjustment weight by multiplying the reciprocals of the response rates of primary sampling unit, school, class, and student. We computed the post-stratification adjustment weight based on the composition of urban-rural, school type, gender, and grade (Huang et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2024). The point values and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The Rao-Scott χ2test was used to compare co-use prevalence across sub-groups.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with co-use, comparing co-users with exclusive alcohol users and exclusive cigarette users, with adjustment for sociodemographic and behavioral covariates. The covariates consist of gender, urban-rural residence, grade, school type, region, pocket money, parental and peer substance use, parental attitudes, school health education, exposure to pro-anti tobacco/alcohol advertising and e-cigarette use. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Inc. Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Characteristics of participants

Of the 59,748 eligible participants, 57,336 students aged 13–18 in grades 7–12 completed the survey, yielding a 96.0% response rate. Participants were recruited from 379 schools across seven provinces. The sample comprised 51.8% middle school (n = 29,694), 35.9% academic high school (n = 20,586), and 12.3% vocational high school students (n = 7056). Of these, 59.4% were urban students and 51.7% were male. (Appendix Table A).

Discussion

This study provides the first nationally representative descriptive estimates of past-30-day co-use of alcohol and cigarettes among Chinese adolescents. In 2021, the co-use prevalence was 1.7%, which was higher than Japan's 0.9% (Fujii et al., 2021), but lower than Denmark's 8.6% (Kjeld et al., 2022b). The absolute number of co-users is considerable given China's large adolescent population. Among substance users, co-use accounted for 13.1%, indicating that approximately one in eight substance users engages in co-use. A male-predominant pattern of co-use was observed, with the rate among boys nearly four times that among girls. This pattern was consistent with trends in most countries (Hong and Peltzer, 2019; Johnson et al., 2009) and aligned with the gender disparities in China (Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; Huang et al., 2026). Co-use and exclusive cigarette use were more common in vocational schools. These variations highlight the need for tailored, context-specific prevention strategies.

Parental contexts showed distinct patterns of association with co-use. Co-use rates were higher among adolescents whose parents held favourable attitudes toward drinking than among those whose parents did not. However, parental drinking and smoking behaviors were not significantly associated with co-use. Instead, parental attitudes favourable toward adolescent drinking emerged as the significant correlate. This suggests that perceived parental permissiveness may matter more than behavioral modelling, implying that interventions targeting parental norms may be more impactful than those focused solely on reducing parental substance use.

Peer substance use showed a strong association with co-use. Among substance users, the co-use proportion was substantially higher among those whose close friends smoked or drank. In adjusted analysis, having close friends who smoked was associated with five-fold higher odds of co-use versus exclusive alcohol use, and having friends who drank was associated with higher odds versus exclusive cigarette use. The magnitude of peer effects exceeded those observed for family factors, suggesting that peer contexts may be the dominant social influence on adolescent co-use. These findings align with social learning theory (Fujii et al., 2021) and the common liability model (Mayet et al., 2016), which posit that substance use clusters within social networks through peer selection and socialisation. The strong peer effects underscore the potential of school-based peer-led interventions and social norms campaigns (Rose-Clarke et al., 2019; McBride et al., 2007).

While originally developed as smoking cessation aids, e-cigarettes are often used by adolescents out of novelty and curiosity (Lin et al., 2022). This study revealed that past-month e-cigarette users had markedly higher co-use rates than non-users. This pattern persisted after adjustment. E-cigarette use was strongly associated with co-use versus exclusive alcohol use and modestly associated versus exclusive cigarette use. Among substance users, the proportion engaging in co-use was 40.8% in e-cigarette users versus 8.8% in non-users. This clustering suggests that e-cigarette use does not replace traditional substances but co-occurs with them (Noble et al., 2015), and may serve as a marker for broader substance use risk.

A key strength of this study is the use of a large, nationally representative survey among Chinese adolescents, which enabled the first descriptive analysis of co-use patterns, and identification of associated factors, providing baseline data for future interventions. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and differing metrics (alcohol: yes/no, cigarette: number of days) prevented assessment of co-use intensity or frequency. Nonetheless, the large nationally representative sample with complex survey weighting ensures robust prevalence estimates. Second, the school-based self-report may underestimate prevalence due to social desirability and recall biases. However, the use of anonymous questionnaires and a 30-day recall period may have mitigate these biases.

Conclusions

The first nationally representative study examined co-use patterns among Chinese adolescents. The overall prevalence was 1.7%, and approximately one in eight substance users engaged in co-use. Co-use was higher among boys, vocational school students, and those with whose close friends drank or smoked. Parental attitudes favourable toward adolescent drinking, rather than parental substance use itself, were associated with co-use. Past-month e-cigarette users had markedly higher co-use rates than non-users, with a nearly eight-fold adjusted odds of co-use versus exclusive alcohol use. These findings highlight peer influence and e-cigarette use as key intervention targets and support parental norm interventions to prevent adolescent substance use.
