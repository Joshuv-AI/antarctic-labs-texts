Influence of build orientation on the misfit and fracture load of three-unit 3D-printed permanent composite fixed dental prostheses
Authors: Nazire Esra Ozer, Zeynep Sahin, Arda Buyuksungur, Mehmet Ali Kilicarslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdditive manufacturing has expanded the clinical application of resin-based materials for definitive fixed dental prostheses (FDPs). This study aimed to evaluate the effect of different printing orientations on marginal and internal misfit, overall discrepancy, and fracture load of three-unit FDPs fabricated from a reinforced resin-based material (VarseoSmile TriniQ).MethodsForty-eight three-unit FDPs were fabricated and divided into four groups (n = 12): a lithium disilicate control group (IPS, IPS e.max CAD) and three experimental groups of a permanent composite resin (VST, VarseoSmile TriniQ) printed at 0°, 45°, and 90° build orientations (VST-0, VST-45, and VST-90). Marginal and internal misfit values were evaluated using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) before and after chewing simulation. Dynamic aging was followed by fracture load testing using a single-load-to-failure protocol. Failure modes were classified under stereomicroscopic examination. Data were analyzed using nonparametric statistical methods. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc analysis, while pre- and post-aging comparisons were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlations between misfit parameters and fracture load were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis (α = 0.05).ResultsPrinting orientation significantly affected the marginal and internal misfit of TriniQ restorations. The 45° build orientation demonstrated the most consistent fit values both before and after the chewing simulation. Chewing simulation resulted in statistically significant increases in marginal, internal misfit as well as overall discrepancy values across all groups (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences in fracture load were observed among the groups (p = 0.094). However, the IPS control group exhibited the highest Weibull modulus (m = 8.41). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed no statistically significant correlations between fracture load and overall discrepancy in any group (p > 0.05). IPS restorations predominantly exhibited catastrophic brittle fractures, whereas TriniQ restorations mainly exhibited connector-related and deformation type failure patterns.ConclusionsBuild orientation influenced the adaptation accuracy of additively manufactured three unit FDPs, whereas no statistically significant differences in fracture load were observed among the groups. Minor variations in overall discrepancy did not appear to influence fracture load under static loading conditions. All tested groups exhibited fracture resistance values exceeding reported physiological masticatory forces. IPS e.max CAD restorations demonstrated higher structural reliability and more consistent, yet comparatively brittle, fracture behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09183-9.Keywords: 3D printing, Three-uinit fixed dental prostheses, Build orientation, Fracture Load, Marginal and Internal misfit

Introduction

Recent progress in printing technologies and material development has broadened the role of three-dimensional (3D) printing within modern dentistry. Initially applied to the fabrication of impression trays, surgical guides, denture bases, and provisional restorations, additive manufacturing is now increasingly used for the production of more complex structures, including definitive fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) [1].

Although a wide range of material types can be processed through using additive manufacturing technologies [2, 3], resin-based systems have attracted particular interest in recent years [3]. Advances in material science have enabled the incorporation of commercially available ceramic fillers into resin matrices, thereby enhancing their mechanical, biological, and physical properties to a level suitable for definitive restorative applications [4]. Consequently, several resin formulations intended for permanent restorations have been introduced [1, 5]. However, many of these materials were initially developed for single-unit restorations, which may limit their clinical applicability in more complex situations [6]. To address this limitation, a reinforced resin-based material (VarseoSmile TriniQ; BEGO, Bremen, Germany) has been specifically developed for the additive manufacturing of definitive tooth-supported FDPs, including single crowns and three-unit FDPs for both anterior and posterior regions [4, 6–8].

The successful outcome of dental restorations largely depends on three critical factors: Esthetic perception, adaptation of the restoration, and fracture load performance [9]. While esthetic success is inherently subjective and influenced by multiple variables [10], the dimensional accuracy of a restoration, encompassing both marginal and internal misfit, plays a crucial role in its longevity and clinical performance [4, 11]. The marginal gap is defined as the space between the restoration margin and the prepared tooth surface, whereas the internal gap refers to the space between the internal surface of the prosthesis and the underlying tooth structure [12]. Inadequate marginal adaptation may result in cement degradation, microleakage, bacterial infiltration, hypersensitivity, secondary caries, gingival irritation, and periodontal inflammation [13, 14]. Similarly, poor internal fit can adversely affect marginal adaptation, increase cement thickness, reduce retention, alter occlusal contacts, and ultimately compromise the fracture load of the restoration [4, 15]. Achieving a clinically acceptable adaptation, commonly defined by a marginal misfit threshold of approximately 120 μm [16], requires optimal congruence between the digital design and the fabricated prosthesis [7].

Resin-based restorations produced through additive manufacturing can be influenced by several factors, including resin composition [5, 15], the type of 3D printer used [1, 17], printing parameters [7, 15, 18], and post-curing procedures [19–21]. These factors not only affect the dimensional accuracy of the restorations [7, 17, 22, 23] but may also influence their mechanical performance, including fracture load [15, 24]. Among these variables, printing orientation remains a subject of debate in the literature [6, 7, 15] as it may exacerbate the stair-step effect and layer-related surface irregularities, potentially compromising surface integrity, manufacturing efficiency, and dimensional accuracy [25]. In addition, layer orientation may affect polymerization shrinkage between successive layers, further influencing marginal and internal misfit values [7, 25]. Previous studies have reported that adjustable printing parameters significantly impact both production quality and misfit characteristics of single-unit and multi-unit additively-manufactured dental prostheses [7, 17, 22, 23]. Therefore, careful consideration of these parameters is essential to achieve restorations that exhibit precise dimensional adaptation while maintaining reliable mechanical performance.

Although 3D-printed resins demonstrate increasing potential in prosthetic dentistry, the clinical application of this technology remains relatively recent, and data regarding the long-term performance of newly developed materials intended for permanent restorations are still limited. Moreover, several available studies evaluated materials that are no longer commercially accessible, which complicates the assessment of their current clinical relevance [26]. This lack of data on clinically applicable and commercially available materials represents a significant gap in the literature. In particular, limited research has addressed the adaptation and mechanical performance of three-unit permanent FDPs produced with 3D-printed resins [1, 4, 6, 7, 15, 27–29]. Most of these studies have focused on single-bar designs [1, 15, 27] or other single-unit restorations, such as inlays [4] and endocrowns [28, 29], whereas only a limited number have investigated three-unit bridge restorations [6, 7]. To the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the effect of different printing orientations on the marginal and internal misfit of three-unit permanent FDPs using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), a non-destructive technique that allows detailed cross-sectional assessment, nor has any study correlated these misfit parameters with fracture load. This represents a significant knowledge gap in the current literature.

In this context, the present study aims of the present study is to evaluate the effect of different printing orientations on marginal and internal misfit, and fracture load of three-unit permanent FDPs fabricated from a newly developed reinforced resin-based material (VarseoSmile TriniQ). To determine the optimal printing orientation, the study compares these 3D-printed restorations with lithium disilicate-based IPS e.max CAD restorations, which are widely used among all-ceramic systems due to their high mechanical strength, reliable adaptation, and ability to meet aesthetic expectations. The null hypotheses of the study are as follows: (i) printing orientation has no statistically significant effect on marginal and internal misfit, and overall discrepancy; (ii) printing orientation has no statistically significant effect on fracture load; (iii) no statistically significant correlation exists between overall discrepancy and fracture load.

Material and method

The sample size for the study was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2; Heinrich-Heine-University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Marginal misfit was defined as the primary outcome variable for the power analysis because adaptation accuracy represented the main focus of the present investigation. The effect size was set at 0.50, based on the mean and standard deviation values reported in a previous study [7]. With a power of 90%, and a significance level of α = 0.05, a minimum of 12 samples per group was required, resulting in a total of 48 samples. Fracture load and correlation analyses were considered secondary outcome measures.

To fabricate three-unit FDPs, a mandibular right canine to second premolar span was selected, with the right first premolar designated as a modified ridge-lap pontic. Before tooth preparation, the typodont model (Frasaco Dental Model; Frasaco, Greenville, NC, USA) was scanned using a Trios 5 intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) to obtain the initial digital dataset. Subsequently, the right canine and second premolar were prepared according to established guidelines for all ceramic full coverage restorations, including approximately 2 mm occlusal reduction, 1.5 mm axial reduction, a convergence angle of about 6°, a 1 mm chamfer finish line at the gingival level, and rounded internal line angles [7, 10, 30]. The accuracy of the tooth preparation was confirmed using a silicone index, and the prepared surfaces were examined with a periodontal probe.

To standardize the master model on which all subsequent bridge restorations were fabricated, the typodont model was first prepared and then re-scanned using a laboratory scanner (Smart Optics Sensorem; Smart Optics, Bochum, Germany). The mandibular right segment corresponding to the planned three-unit design was then produced using a DLP 3D printer (BEGO Varseo XS; BEGO, Bremen, Germany). A light curable resin (VarseoWax Model Gray; BEGO, Bremen, Germany) was used and polymerized on the build platform with the occlusal plane oriented horizontally (0°) and a layer thickness of 50 μm, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended printing parameters. This model resin, designed for producing dimensionally stable working models, exhibits a modulus of elasticity of approximately 2.5 GPa and a Shore D hardness above 80, providing sufficient rigidity to serve as a stable substrate during cementation, fit evaluation, and subsequent fracture load testing without undergoing deformation under applied loads [31, 32]. After printing, the objects were removed from the build platform using the supplied spatula and cleaned following the resin manufacturer’s instructions: an unheated ultrasonic bath with 96% ethanol was used, and total cleaning time did not exceed 5 min. The prints were then air-dried under an extraction unit. Final post-curing was carried out using a xenon flash curing unit (BEGO Otoflash G171; NK Optik, Baierbrunn, Germany), following the validated protocol: two cycles of 2000 flashes (flash frequency 10 Hz), with the objects flipped between exposure cycles to ensure uniform polymerization [32].

A total of 48 3D-printed resin master models containing prepared abutment teeth were embedded in cylindrical molds using autopolymerizing acrylic resin. The specimens were randomly assigned to the experimental groups (n = 12) prior to restoration fabrication. Following the completion of all preparations, the model was re-scanned using a Trios 5 intraoral scanner to obtain the post-preparation digital records. All scan data were imported into dental CAD software (Exocad DentalCAD; Exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany), where the pre- and post-preparation datasets were superimposed to generate a design that preserved the appropriate anatomical contour. During the design phase, the cement space was set to 100 μm, ensuring standardized material thickness and CAD parameters across all groups [33, 34]. The finalized master design file was subsequently exported to different manufacturing workflows (CAD/CAM milling and 3D printing) for the production of three-unit FDPs, as outlined in Table 1.

For the milling group, the restorations were fabricated using a CAD/CAM unit (Redon Technology, Istanbul, Turkey) from lithium disilicate–based ceramic blocks (IPS e.max CAD; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). After milling, all restorations underwent crystallization firing according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

For the 3D-printed group, composite-based VarseoSmile TriniQ resin (BEGO, Bremen, Germany) was used to fabricate the restorations with a DLP-based 3D printer (BEGO Varseo XS; BEGO, Bremen, Germany) in three different build orientations (0°, 45°, and 90°). In the 0° orientation, the support structures were positioned perpendicular to the occlusal surface of the prosthesis. In the 45° orientation, the supports were attached to the occlusal surface at a 45° angle, whereas in the 90° orientation, the support structures were aligned perpendicular to the distal surface of the second premolar. Support structures were generated automatically using the printer specific CAM software (Varseo CAM; BEGO, Bremen, Germany) according to the manufacturer recommended default settings. The layer thickness was set to 50 μm, and the restorations were printed from the occlusal surface toward the margin. Post-processing of the printed samples was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Table 1).

All fabricated prostheses were examined under a 4× magnification loupe to confirm the absence of surface defects, and no internal adjustments were performed on the intaglio surfaces. To ensure standardization, to allow the control group material (IPS e.max CAD) to reach its final mechanical properties, and to provide the 3D-printed samples with their final surface characteristics, all samples were glazed in accordance with the respective manufacturers’ instructions (Table 1). After completion of these steps, the restorations produced through the different manufacturing workflows were cemented onto the master resin models using a dual-cure resin cement (Bifix QM; VOCO GmbH, Cuxhaven, Germany). The cement was applied uniformly to the intaglio surface of each restoration according to the manufacturer’s instructions prior to seating. The restorations were initially seated passively onto the master models using finger pressure, and excess cement was tack cured for 2 s to facilitate removal of cement remnants. Subsequently, a standardized vertical load of 5 kg was applied centrally to the occlusal surface of the fixed dental prosthesis using a cementation apparatus to ensure consistent seating during cementation [35]. Final polymerization was then performed for 20 s from each surface and abutment using a light curing unit (Elipar FreeLight 2; 3 M ESPE, St Paul, MN, USA). Following cementation, all samples were stored in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h under ambient humidity.

Measurement of discrepancies

Micro-CT was used to evaluate the marginal and internal discrepancies of the three-unit FDPs. Scans were obtained both before and after the chewing simulation. The distances between the internal surface of the restorations and the prepared abutments on the master resin model were measured in micrometers (µm) at predefined reference points. Based on the these measurements, marginal and internal misfit values, as well as overall discrepancy, were calculated for each restoration. All measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner (B.A.) with 15 years of experience in micro-CT analysis using a standardized measurement protocol. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was used for statistical evaluation. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis, which demonstrated an excellent level of measurement reproducibility (ICC = 0.94).

High resolution micro-CT scanning was performed using a SkyScan 1275 system (Bruker micro-CT, Kontich, Belgium). The scans were acquired at 80 kV and 125 µA using a 1 mm aluminum filter, an isotropic voxel size of 18 μm, a rotation step of 0.2°, frame averaging of 3, and flat field correction. Following image acquisition, the datasets were reconstructed in NRecon software (version 1.7.4.2; Bruker SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) using standardized reconstruction parameters, including smoothing level 3, ring artifact correction level 7, and beam hardening correction of 38%. The same scanning and reconstruction settings were applied to all specimens to minimize measurement variability. Once reconstruction was completed, the cross-sectional images were imported into CTAn software (version 1.23.0.2; Bruker SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) for quantitative analyses. DataViewer (version 1.7.0.1; Bruker SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) was used to visualize the reconstructed images and generate sagittal, coronal, and transverse sections. Linear measurements between the restoration surfaces and the corresponding abutment areas on the master resin model were obtained from predefined points on each slice. All measurements were obtained from anatomically corresponding reference locations identified using standardized sagittal, coronal, and transverse slice orientations to ensure consistent point selection across all specimens and repeated scans. In the coronal buccolingual sections, discrepancy measurements were obtained at 8 locations for the canine tooth and 9 locations for the premolar abutment. In the sagittal mesiodistal sections, 9 measurement points were recorded for both abutments. In total, each sample yielded 35 measurements (17 for the canine + 18 for the premolar) and all values were documented using a spreadsheet program (Microsoft Excel 2019, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). For evaluation, each section of each abutment was divided into four regions: the marginal area (MA), the midaxial wall area (AX), the axio occlusal transition area (AOT), and the occlusal area (OC) [36, 37].

Marginal misfit was assessed using two discrepancy parameters: marginal gap (MG) and absolute marginal discrepancy (AMD), as defined in previous studies [38]. The MG represented the vertical distance between the restoration margin and the outer boundary of the tooth preparation, whereas the AMD was defined as the horizontal discrepancy between the most external contour of the restoration and the preparation finish line [38]. The mean MG and AMD values were used to quantify marginal misfit. Internal misfit was analyzed based on the mean discrepancy values obtained from the AX, AOT, and OC regions. Measurements in the AX region were taken at the midpoint of the axial wall. Measurements in the AOT region were recorded at the axio-occlusal angle, defined as the junction between the axial wall and the occlusal surface. Measurements in the OC region were obtained at the center of the occlusal surface [18]. The overall discrepancy for each sample was calculated as the mean of the marginal and internal misfit values [4]. The measurement locations and definitions are presented in Table 2.

Following completion of the initial micro-CT scanning and analyses, the samples were subjected to chewing simulation. Using a chewing simulator (Esetron, Smart Robotechnologies, Ankara, Türkiye), cyclic mechanical loading was applied in combination with simultaneous thermocycling performed between 5 and 55 °C with a dwell time of 30 s. A total of 600,000 loading cycles were delivered at a frequency of 1.6 Hz and a load magnitude of 50 N, corresponding to approximately two years of clinical function [39]. After every 50,000 aging cycles, the integrity of the samples was assessed, and all surviving samples were carefully examined under a stereomicroscope (Leica MZ 12, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). In the event of a catastrophic failure, the affected sample was removed from the chamber and the corresponding cycle interval and failure location were documented. Following completion of the chewing simulation, all remaining samples underwent a second micro-CT scan and analysis to evaluate changes induced by dynamic loading.

Fracture load

Following dynamic loading, each sample underwent a single load-to-failure test using a universal testing machine (Lloyd LRX, Lloyd Instruments, Fareham, UK). The load was applied vertically at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min using a metal sphere with a diameter of 3 mm. The sphere was positioned on the wear facet formed at the center of the distal fossa of the pontic during the chewing simulation [40]. To prevent concentrated contact damage and to increase the contact surface area, a thin tin foil was placed between the specimen and the stainless steel indenter. All specimens were loaded until failure, and the fracture load was recorded in N at the point of initial crack occurrence.

After the fracture test, the failure modes of the restorations were examined under a stereomicroscope (Leica MZ12, Leica Microsystems Ltd, Germany) and classified as follows [40]:

Statistical analysis

The normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For the misfit parameters which exhibited a non-normal distribution, differences among the IPS, VST-0, VST-45, and VST-90 groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc procedure with Bonferroni adjustment. For within-group comparisons of before and after chewing simulator measurements, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Conversely, for the fracture load data which exhibited a normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine the differences among the groups. Structural reliability and predictability of the failure behavior were calculated using Weibull analysis. Associations between fracture load and the misfit parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The present study aimed to assess the influence of different printing orientations on the adaptation and mechanical behavior of a resin-based three-unit FDPs material. The first null hypothesis, which stated that printing orientation would not influence on marginal misfit, internal misfit or overall discrepancy, was rejected as statistically significant differences were detected among the test groups for both the canine and premolar abutments. In contrast, the second null hypothesis, suggesting that printing orientation would not affect fracture load, was accepted because no statistically significant differences were observed despite numerical variations among the groups. The third null hypothesis, which proposed that no statistically significant correlation exists between overall discrepancy and fracture load, was accepted, as the analyses did not reveal statistically significant.

IPS e.max CAD is a lithium disilicate glass-ceramic material designed for the fabrication of restorations using CAD/CAM milling technology. It is widely used in clinical practice for a broad range of indications, including crowns, inlay/onlay restorations, veneers, and three-unit FDPs supported distally by a second premolar [41]. Its extensive evidence base, favorable long-term clinical performance, and predictable fit characteristics [42] make it a relevant benchmark for assessing the adaptation and mechanical behavior of emerging restorative materials under comparable design constraints. Therefore, IPS e.max CAD was selected the control material in the present study, enabling direct comparison with VarseoSmile TriniQ and supporting the evaluation of whether this newly developed resin-based material meets the clinical expectations.

Indirect restorations have been evaluated for marginal and internal adaptation using several nondestructive techniques, including the silicone replica technique, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), and micro-CT [4, 43]. Although the replica technique is widely used, its accuracy may be compromised, particularly in complex restorations, due to material distortion and the limited definition of thin marginal areas [36]. CBCT is clinically accessible and involves lower radiation exposure; however, its spatial resolution is insufficient for the precise assessment of marginal discrepancies [4]. In contrast, micro-CT enables high-resolution 3D visualization of both marginal and internal gaps without damaging the specimen and is increasingly accepted as a reference method for in-vitro fit analysis [30, 37]. Therefore, micro-CT was selected in the present study to allow a comprehensive and nondestructive evaluation of marginal and internal discrepancies, as well as overall discrepancy under standardized conditions.

None of the mean misfit values obtained in the present study fell within the cement space of 100 μm defined in the digital design file or within the clinically accepted limit of 120 μm for FDPs [16]. In the literature, reported marginal and internal misfit values for lithium disilicate restorations have been reported over a wide range, depending on the fabrication technique and restoration design (marginal misfit: 45–190 μm; internal misfit: 58–475 μm) [44]. This finding suggests that, although lithium disilicate restorations are dimensionally stable and well documented in clinical use, increased misfit may occur in multi unit posterior configurations, potentially due to material hardness and limitations inherent to subtractive manufacturing workflows. Therefore, the relatively higher misfit values observed in the IPS group should be interpreted with caution and considered within the context of broader manufacturing- and geometry-ic related constraints. In this regard, Azarbal et al. reported that hybrid ceramic Vita Enamic crowns produced using the CEREC milling system demonstrated better marginal adaptation than lithium disilicate crowns [44]. In line with the present findings, this observation indicates that hybrid or more flexible materials may offer advantages in adaptation performance compared with ceramics. Notably, particularly in the VST-45 group, both canine and premolar regions exhibited the lowest misfit values before the chewing simulation, with deviations approaching the clinical threshold. These differences among groups may be attributed to variations in material composition, polymerization behavior, and manufacturing dynamics.

Scientific evidence supporting the manufacturer recommended printing parameters for permanent resin based additively manufactured FDPs [8], particularly the combination of a 45° build orientation and 50 μm layer thickness, remains limited. In the present study, the VST-45 group generally demonstrated favorable adaptation behavior across the evaluated experimental conditions and measurement parameters. The relatively stable performance observed in the VST-45 group suggests that an intermediate build orientation may promote a balanced interaction among geometric accuracy, support structure distribution, and polymerization behavior. Consequently, this orientation may represent a practical strategy for optimizing the fit of permanent resin-based 3D-printed FDPs. From a clinical perspective, optimization of printing orientation may contribute to improved adaptation behavior in additively manufactured posterior fixed dental prostheses. However, the findings of the present study should be interpreted within the context of the selected manufacturing conditions, since only a single DLP-based printer system and a single layer thickness of 50 μm were evaluated. Because printer architecture, printing resolution, and layer thickness may influence both dimensional accuracy and mechanical behavior, the present outcomes may not be directly generalizable to other additive manufacturing systems or printing protocols. Therefore, further in-vitro and long term clinical studies are required to confirm the biomechanical and clinical implications of these findings.

The margins of FDPs are structurally thin regions and are therefore inherently more susceptible to deformation during both fabrication and post-processing procedures [17]. In the 90° printing configuration, these marginal regions are oriented vertically. This orientation may increase susceptibility to inaccuracies related to gravitational effects, polymerization shrinkage, and cumulative layer misalignment during the digital light processing workflow [7] which may explain the consistently higher marginal deviation values observed in the VST-90 group in the present study.

The statistically significant increases observed in the misfit parameters after the chewing simulation suggest that dynamic loading may influence the marginal and internal adaptation behavior of posterior FDPs. When the absolute values were considered, the IPS and VST-90 groups generally exhibited comparatively higher discrepancy values before and after simulation, whereas the VST-45 group tended to demonstrate relatively lower discrepancy values across several evaluated parameters. These findings may indicate that an intermediate build orientation could contribute to a more balanced distribution of manufacturing related inaccuracies associated with layer stacking and polymerization related distortion. Within the limitations of the present study, printing orientation appears to be a potentially influential manufacturing parameter affecting both the initial adaptation and the post simulation discrepancy behavior of additively manufactured FDPs.

In line with the aim of the present study, the misfit outcomes observed in three-unit posterior FDPs fabricated from TriniQ resin should be interpreted together with the limited but growing body of literature evaluating this material. Previous studies investigating TriniQ resin in single-unit restorations, including laminate veneers, inlays, and endocrowns, generally reported favorable marginal and internal adaptation when fabricated using DLP-based systems with a 50 μm layer thickness [4, 28, 29, 45]. In particular, Daghrery et al. demonstrated superior adaptation of TriniQ laminate veneers compared with milled ceramics and alternative additively manufactured materials [45], while CBCT-based evaluations of TriniQ inlays and endocrowns also reported clinically acceptable dimensional accuracy [4]. These findings indicate that the TriniQ material and its associated printing protocol may provide comparatively favorable adaptation behavior in restorations with relatively simplified geometries; however, the clinical interpretation of absolute misfit thresholds in additively manufactured restorations remains controversial. Moreover, although resin cement may partially compensate for minor discrepancies by improving stress distribution and adaptation at the tooth restoration interface, such compensation should not be interpreted as eliminating the potential biomechanical implications of increased marginal or internal misfit values.

However, the present study extends these observations to a more complex restorative scenario by evaluating three-unit posterior FDPs, in which connector geometry, span length, and nonuniform polymerization stresses become increasingly influential. In this context, the significantly higher marginal and internal misfit values observed for specific TriniQ build orientations, particularly VST-90 and VST-0, indicate that the favorable adaptation reported for single-unit restorations may not be directly transferable to multi-unit configurations. Similar observations were reported by Donmez et al., who identified printing orientation as a critical determinant of adaptation accuracy in additively manufactured three-unit FDPs and demonstrated that vertically oriented build configurations were associated with increased marginal discrepancies [7]. Complementary findings were presented by Acicbe et al., who reported that variations in printing technology and processing-related parameters significantly affected the marginal and internal adaptation of definitive resin-based FDPs, even when the same resin material was used [6]. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that the adaptation behavior of TriniQ resin in multi-unit restorations is highly sensitive to manufacturing-related variables.

The use of natural teeth in in-vitro fracture load tests is inherently limited by substantial variations in tooth anatomy, patient age, hydration status, and post extraction storage conditions, all of which compromise experimental standardization [46, 47]. Moreover, such biological variability may obscure material-related effects and reduce the reliability of intergroup comparisons [46]. Consequently, many previous studies have employed artificial supporting dies fabricated from metal or resin-based materials with controlled and reproducible mechanical properties to minimize substrate-related variability and enhance comparability among restorative systems [4, 10, 37]. In the present study, a dimensionally stable model resin [31] was selected as the supporting substrate to ensure consistent boundary conditions during cementation, adaptation assessment, and subsequent static fracture testing. The use of identical 3D-printed resin dies for all experimental groups further minimized potential confounding factors related to operator technique and fabrication variability. This standardized experimental approach allowed fracture load outcomes to be interpreted primarily in relation to restorative material properties and manufacturing parameters rather than inconsistencies in the supporting structure.

According to biomechanical data reported in the literature, the maximum physiological bite force in the premolar region ranges approximately between 300 N and 600 N [48]. When these limits are considered, although the IPS group exhibited the lowest mean fracture load among the tested groups (1128 ± 162.1 N), all three-unit FDPs evaluated in this study demonstrated fracture load values well above normal masticatory forces. Despite its high flexural strength, lithium disilicate is a glass ceramic material characterized by a predominantly brittle microstructure and a susceptibility to crack propagation [42]. Although IPS e.max CAD exhibited the lowest mean fracture load among the tested groups, all restorations demonstrated fracture load values exceeding physiological posterior bite forces. The brittle behavior of lithium disilicate ceramics and the comparatively more compliant stress response of resin based materials may partly explain the differences observed in fracture behavior among the groups [39, 47].

In addition to mean fracture resistance values, Weibull analysis provided supplementary information regarding the structural reliability and variability of the tested restorative systems. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in fracture resistance among the groups, variations in Weibull modulus values suggested differences in the consistency of fracture behavior. From a clinical perspective, a higher Weibull modulus indicates greater structural reliability and a more predictable fracture behavior, whereas lower values suggest increased variability in mechanical performance [39].

In particular, the comparatively higher Weibull modulus observed in the IPS group may indicate a more homogeneous fracture response, whereas the wider variability observed in the additively manufactured groups could be associated with process dependent factors such as layer orientation and interlayer polymerization characteristics. This finding suggests that, although the additively manufactured restorations demonstrated fracture resistance comparable to the conventional ceramic control, their mechanical response may be less predictable because of greater specimen-to-specimen variability. Nevertheless, these static fracture resistance values should not be interpreted as direct indicators of long term clinical reliability, since fatigue related degradation, cyclic loading behavior, and cumulative damage accumulation may substantially influence the failure behavior of multi unit FDPs over time, particularly at connector regions.

When the mechanical behavior of resin-based restorations fabricated by 3D printing is considered, the configuration of the printed layers relative to the direction of loading is identified as one of the key process variables determining the final mechanical strength of the material [49]. Recent review studies emphasize that layer orientation plays a decisive role in elastic deformation, crack propagation pathways, and fracture patterns in 3D-printed dental materials, including ceramic reinforced hybrid resins [26]. In particular, layer alignment perpendicular to the loading direction as observed in 0° built orientations may increase susceptibility to interlayer separation, whereas inclined layer arrangements, such as 45° orientations promote a more homogeneous stress distribution and are commonly associated with higher fracture loads [26, 49].

In additively manufactured resin based materials, mechanical behavior is also influenced by anisotropic structural characteristics that arise from the layer by layer fabrication process [3]. In DLP-based systems, differences between interlayer and intralayer bonding may affect stress distribution and crack propagation behavior, since polymer cohesion within a single layer is generally stronger than adhesion between successive layers [50–52]. In addition, variations in photopolymerization behavior and curing conditions during the manufacturing process may contribute to localized differences in polymer network formation throughout the printed structure, potentially influencing dimensional stability and mechanical performance [20, 21]. These process dependent characteristics may partly explain the orientation related differences observed in the present study and highlight the complex interaction between printing parameters and the biomechanical behavior of additively manufactured FDPs.

Although fracture load data for three-unit FDPs fabricated with TriniQ are not yet available in the literature, several experimental studies have investigated the mechanical behavior of this reinforced resin using simplified bar-shaped specimens [1, 15]. Casucci et al. reported that resin bar specimens fabricated at different printing angles exhibited statistically higher flexural strength at a 45° orientation compared with a 0° orientation [15]. In agreement with these findings, Hoffmann and colleagues demonstrated that, in 3D-printed restorative materials developed for permanent use, mechanical properties are not only dependent on resin composition but are also significantly influenced by printer systems and process-related parameters [1].

In light of all these considerations, the absence of a statistically significant difference in fracture load among the groups in the present study suggests that printing orientation did not have a significant impact on mechanical fracture load. This finding indicates that orientation-dependent mechanical behaviors reported for simplified bar-shaped specimens may be reduced in restorations with more complex geometries with structural continuity, such as three-unit FDPs. In particular, the increasing influence of connector regions, span length, and load transfer pathways in multi-unit configurations may mask effects related to build orientation. Within this context, the present results suggest that restoration geometry and clinical configuration may influence mechanical behavior to an extent comparable to, or greater than, material type and manufacturing parameters. Therefore, further studies are necessary to evaluate the effects of orientation more comprehensively under different restorative designs and clinical loading conditions.

The absence of statistically significant correlations between fracture load and overall discrepancy, together with the generally low correlation coefficients observed, suggests that within the range of discrepancies evaluated in the present study, minor variations in marginal and internal adaptation were not associated with measurable differences in fracture load under static loading conditions. This result is consistent with the findings of Mohammed and Ibraheem, who reported that different cement space thicknesses ranging from 30 to 100 μm did not have a significant effect on the fracture strength of IPS e.max crowns [53]. Similarly, Wolfart et al. stated that adhesive cementation fills microgaps between the restoration and tooth preparation, homogenizes stress distribution, and compensates for the negative mechanical effects of minor discrepancies [54]. Nevertheless, the literature also indicates that excessive marginal discrepancies may facilitate crack initiation by promoting stress concentration [55], and that internal gaps leading to thick and heterogeneous cement layers may increase tensile stresses within the cement under loading, thereby facilitating crack initiation [56]. Owing to these conflicting findings, the effect of discrepancy on mechanical failure should not be regarded as an absolute relationship but rather as a condition dependent factor that must be evaluated in conjunction with the magnitude and distribution of the misfit and the structural design of the restoration. The graphical correlation analysis further supported this observation, as no consistent linear association pattern was identified between overall discrepancy and fracture resistance across the tested groups. Accordingly, the low correlation coefficients obtained in the present study suggest that restoration success should be assessed not solely on micron-level adaptation, but through the combined consideration of structural and manufacturing-related factors such as the elastic modulus of the material, printing orientation, restoration geometry, and applied loading conditions. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the subgroup sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect modest correlations, particularly in analyses performed separately for each material group. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant correlations in the present study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that no association exists between restoration discrepancy and fracture resistance. Future studies including larger sample sizes and broader ranges of discrepancy values are warranted to further clarify this relationship.

The fracture patterns observed in this study revealed distinct differences in the structural response of three-unit FDPs fabricated using additive and subtractive manufacturing techniques. The high incidence of catastrophic multiple fractures observed in the IPS group (75%) can be attributed to the material’s high elastic modulus and inherent brittleness. In contrast, the predominance of fractures localized at the connector and abutment regions in the VST groups indicates that load transfer was preferentially concentrated at structurally the weaker areas of the restoration. Notably, DC classified as Type 1 were observed exclusively in the VST groups and were not detected in the IPS group. This finding may be related to the greater flexural behavior of hybrid composite materials under stress, which can promote stress accumulation in narrow regions such as connectors. PA classified as Type 2 occurred in all groups except VST 45 and were particularly frequent in the VST 0 group (50%). This fracture pattern likely reflects the biomechanical lever effect generated by masticatory forces in the premolar region. The reduced incidence of abutment failures in the VST-45° group suggests that inclined layer alignment may facilitate more favorable stress distribution and reduce the risk of localized failure.

The extent to which fracture patterns observed under in-vitro conditions reflect the clinical setting is critical for the accurate interpretation of material behavior, particularly in multi-unit posterior FDPs. In this context, a prospective clinical study conducted by Hobbi et al. evaluated the one-year clinical performance of additively manufactured resin composite three-unit posterior FDPs (ELS Even Stronger, Saremco, Switzerland). Although clinically acceptable success rates were reported, the authors emphasized that mechanical stress in posterior regions was predominantly concentrated at the connector and abutment areas. This was considered critical for the durability of the restoration [57]. These findings are consistent with the failure patterns observed in the present study, in which fractures in the TriniQ groups were primarily localized to the connector and abutment regions, suggesting that in-vitro fracture modes may be indicative of clinically relevant mechanical behavior.

The literature reports that interlayer bond strength is lower than intralayer cohesion, and that printed structures become more vulnerable to separation or delamination along layer interfaces when the applied load is aligned with the printing direction [50, 51]. This mechanism is supported by the observations of Keßler et al., who reported that vertically oriented prints may promote crack propagation parallel to the structural layers [52]. Although layer-related fractures might therefore be expected to occur more frequently in certain build orientations, the overall distribution of failure types in the present study remained relatively comparable among the TriniQ groups. This apparent discrepancy may be attributed to differences in specimen geometry, loading mode, and aging protocols, as the bar-shaped specimens used in previous studies may not adequately replicate the complex stress distribution and connector effects present in multi-unit FDPs.

This study has several limitations. First, all specimens were fabricated using a single DLP-based printer system, and only build orientation (0°, 45°, and 90°) was the only manufacturing variable evaluated. Other printing and post-processing parameters, including layer thickness, printing resolution, and post-curing time and temperature, were kept constant, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other printer systems or manufacturing protocols. In addition, fracture load testing was performed using a rigid model resin die with a fixed elastic modulus, whereas the elastic modulus of natural dentin is known to vary whitin a wide range [58]. Consequently, the mechanical behavior of the supporting substrate may not fully reflect clinical conditions and may have influenced the load-bearing response of the restorations. In addition, periodontal ligament simulation and artificial saliva exposure were not incorporated into the experimental setup, and fracture load was evaluated using static vertical loading rather than multidirectional or fatigue to failure protocols, which may further limit direct clinical extrapolation of the findings. Furthermore, the present study did not evaluate the potential influence of different cementation variables, occlusal surface designs, or alternative substrate conditions, all of which may affect the biomechanical performance of fixed dental prostheses under functional loading. Long term properties of hybrid resin based materials, including wear resistance, aging behavior, and color stability, were also beyond the scope of this investigation. Finally, although in-vitro testing enables standardized comparisons, long term in-vivo investigations are still required to validate the clinical performance of these restorations under complex intraoral conditions.

While multiple fractures were predominant in both materials, TriniQ restorations showed connector-focused and deformation-related fracture patterns, whereas IPS e.max CAD restorations exhibited more extensive and brittle fracture behavior.

Clinical implication

While the fracture load of 3D-printed TriniQ restorations exceeds average posterior occlusal forces, meticulous connector design and adequate cross-sectional dimensions remain essential to minimize stress concentration and deformation under functional loading. Clinicians should carefully evaluate individual occlusal loading conditions when planning multi-unit posterior restorations with these resin-based materials. Although their favorable adaptation and initial strength indicate promising applicability, long-term clinical monitoring is necessary to validate their performance against complex intraoral stress distributions over time.
