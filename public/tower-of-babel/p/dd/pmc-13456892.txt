Giant retroperitoneal leiomyosarcoma of massive proportions: a case report
Authors: Rodolfo Chávez-Magallón, Liliana Daniela Suárez-Velázquez, Karen Fabiola Miramontes-Rodríguez, Fernanda Dessired Parra-Gómez, Iris Jocelyn Parrao-Alcántara, Kenny Omar Contreras-Becerra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Retroperitoneal leiomyosarcoma is a rare malignant neoplasm with insidious growth and delayed clinical presentation. Giant tumors pose major surgical challenges because of their anatomical relationships and the frequent need for multivisceral resection to achieve oncological control.Case presentation:We report the case of a 47-year-old male who presented with progressive abdominal distension and nonspecific symptoms. Imaging revealed a massive retroperitoneal tumor occupying nearly the entire abdominal cavity, with marked displacement of adjacent structures. The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy, and a complete macroscopic resection was achieved without multivisceral resection. The tumor encased a long segment of the right ureter, requiring segmental ureteral resection and urinary diversion with nephrostomy. The specimen measured 55 × 31.6 × 18 cm and weighed 19.5 kg. Histopathology confirmed a grade II retroperitoneal leiomyosarcoma with tumor at the inked peripheral surgical margins, consistent with R1 resection. Early postoperative imaging showed no macroscopic residual disease. During follow-up, recurrent tumor burden was identified approximately 3 months after surgery. The patient continued ifosfamide-based chemotherapy and subsequently showed an estimated 60% reduction in tumor size, with preserved renal function.Discussion:This case illustrates that complete macroscopic resection with organ preservation may be feasible in selected patients with giant retroperitoneal leiomyosarcoma. However, this approach is patient-specific and does not replace multivisceral resection when required for oncological clearance.Conclusion:Tumor size alone should not preclude surgical intervention. Surgical strategy should be individualized according to tumor biology, anatomical relationships, resectability, and expected functional consequences.

Introduction

Retroperitoneal leiomyosarcoma is an uncommon malignant neoplasm arising from smooth muscle cells and represents a subset of soft tissue sarcomas. In the retroperitoneum, sarcomas constitute most primary tumors; liposarcoma is the most prevalent subtype, followed by leiomyosarcoma, which is generally associated with more aggressive behavior and poorer prognosis[1]. Retroperitoneal leiomyosarcomas are exceedingly rare, with an estimated incidence below 1 per 100 000 individuals and representing approximately 27–28% of retroperitoneal sarcomas[2,3]. Retroperitoneal sarcomas comprise only 1–2% of all solid malignancies and have reported overall survival rates of 36–58%[2]. Prognosis is mainly determined by tumor grade, completeness of resection, and multifocality; large size and vascular invasion, particularly inferior vena cava involvement, are associated with worse outcomes[4]. Complete surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment, as systemic therapies provide limited and inconsistent benefit[2,4].

Their insidious growth reflects the expansive retroperitoneal space, which permits substantial enlargement before symptoms develop. Patients may remain asymptomatic until tumors cause a mass effect on adjacent structures[5,6]. At diagnosis, lesions often exceed 10–20 cm and may be considered giant when surpassing 20–30 cm, increasing surgical complexity and affecting resectability[6,7]. Clinical presentation is nonspecific, including abdominal or back pain, palpable mass, and gastrointestinal or urinary compression symptoms[1]. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography is the cornerstone of evaluation, defining tumor size, anatomical relationships, vascular involvement, and distant spread[1,6].

Complete surgical resection is the cornerstone of treatment and the main modifiable prognostic factor, although R0 resection is often difficult because of tumor size and proximity to critical structures[6]. Multivisceral resection is frequently required to achieve oncological control, but local recurrence remains common and is a major determinant of long-term outcomes[1,7]. Adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy have limited, largely adjunctive roles with inconsistent survival benefits [1].

In summary, giant retroperitoneal leiomyosarcomas represent a rare but highly challenging clinical entity, characterized by delayed diagnosis, significant tumor burden at presentation, and complex surgical management. These features highlight the importance of early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach to optimize oncological outcomes.

We report a 55 × 31.6 × 18 cm, 19.5 kg retroperitoneal leiomyosarcoma managed by complete macroscopic resection without multivisceral resection, despite right ureteral involvement, emphasizing individualized intraoperative decision-making.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[8].

Case presentation

A 47-year-old man presented with progressive abdominal enlargement over several months, nonspecific discomfort, and early satiety, without weight loss, gastrointestinal bleeding, urinary symptoms, prior abdominal surgery, or known malignancy. Examination revealed a markedly distended abdomen with a large, firm, non-tender mass occupying the abdominal cavity, poorly mobile and fixed to deep planes, without peritoneal irritation or peripheral edema.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a massive, heterogeneous retroperitoneal mass occupying nearly the entire abdominal cavity, with bowel displacement, necrotic areas, significant mass effect, and close relationships to major vascular structures, including the inferior vena cava and abdominal aorta (Fig. 1A and B). On 26 August 2025, an exploratory laparotomy confirmed a giant retroperitoneal tumor occupying nearly the entire abdominal cavity. Complete macroscopic resection was achieved without multivisceral resection. Operative time was 12 hours and 30 minutes, with 1600 mL blood loss; four packed red blood cell units and two fresh frozen plasma units were transfused. The tumor encased a long right ureteral segment, requiring segmental resection. After tumor removal, the urology team was consulted; primary repair was not feasible because of extensive involvement and lack of a safe reconstructive segment, so a right nephrostomy was performed.

The resected specimen measured approximately 55 × 31.6 × 18 cm and weighed 19.5 kg (Fig. 2A). The tumor surface on serial sectioning showed myxoid-appearing areas alternating with whitish fibrous regions and other yellow areas consistent with necrosis (Fig. 2B and C). Histopathology confirmed leiomyosarcoma (Fig. 3), with atypical spindle cells in fascicles, moderate-to-severe pleomorphism, 12 mitoses per 10 high-power fields, less than 50% necrosis, and no lymphovascular invasion. The tumor was FNCLCC grade II and pT4 pNx pMx according to the eighth AJCC edition. Despite no gross residual disease intraoperatively, tumor was present at the inked peripheral margins, consistent with R1 resection. Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse smooth muscle actin and focal desmin positivity, with negative S100 and CD99. The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit and had an uneventful course. One-week postoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed expected changes without macroscopic residual disease or early complications (Fig. 1C and D). He was discharged ten days after surgery. Renal function remained preserved, with creatinine within normal ranges, from 0.62 mg/dL at 24–48 hours to 0.50 mg/dL at discharge, and urea ranging from 34.9 to 40.2 mg/dL during the first 72 hours, with a value of 17.5 mg/dL at discharge.

The patient was referred to medical oncology and started on ifosfamide-based adjuvant chemotherapy with mesna for hemorrhagic cystitis prevention, along with supportive antiemetic and gastric protection according to institutional protocols. The regimen was delivered over several days and was initially well-tolerated.

During follow-up, 3-month CT, although without a formal report, was preliminarily reviewed by the treating team and showed recurrent tumor burden involving nearly the entire right hemiabdomen and at least three left-sided satellite lesions. At 5 months, increased abdominal circumference supported recurrence. The patient continued ifosfamide-based chemotherapy. The 8-month CT, reviewed by oncology, showed an estimated 60% tumor reduction versus the 3-month study, consistent with a partial response, with right pyelocaliceal dilatation. During the fifth cycle, renal function remained preserved, with a urea level of 26 mg/dL and creatinine level of 0.86 mg/dL. He remained alive under follow-up, with urological reassessment, surgical oncology evaluation for possible reintervention, and a sixth cycle scheduled.

The clinical timeline included progressive abdominal distension, contrast-enhanced CT diagnosis, complete macroscopic resection, early postoperative imaging without macroscopic residual disease, and continued oncological management after recurrent tumor burden was identified.

Discussion

Retroperitoneal leiomyosarcomas are characterized by insidious growth within a large anatomical space, allowing considerable enlargement before diagnosis. Symptoms usually reflect mass effect rather than early local invasion, and massive presentations, although reported, remain uncommon[2,5]. Several reports of giant retroperitoneal tumors, including leiomyosarcomas and liposarcomas, illustrate the potential for extreme growth in this compartment[1,3,7]. The present case further expands the spectrum of reported tumor burden.

Reported giant retroperitoneal leiomyosarcomas often require extensive resections because of close relationships with adjacent organs and major retroperitoneal structures. Mateo Vallejo et al described multiorgan block removal, and other reports emphasize the technical complexity of massive tumors and oncological clearance. Our case is notable for extreme size and weight, complete macroscopic resection without multivisceral resection, and preserved renal function despite ureteral involvement. However, the R1 margin and recurrence illustrate aggressive tumor biology and the limitations of macroscopic clearance alone.

Complete surgical resection with negative margins remains the cornerstone of treatment and the most important prognostic factor. Due to the close relationship of these tumors with adjacent organs and major vascular structures, multivisceral resection is frequently required to achieve adequate oncological control[1,4]. However, such approaches are associated with increased surgical complexity, perioperative morbidity, and potential impact on postoperative quality of life.

In contrast, the present case demonstrates that even in the setting of an extremely large tumor, complete macroscopic resection may be achieved without multivisceral sacrifice in selected patients. Right ureteral involvement was managed with segmental resection and nephrostomy rather than nephrectomy, preserving renal function. Apparent involvement may reflect displacement or compression rather than true parenchymal invasion, particularly in massive tumors. However, organ preservation should not be interpreted as a recommendation against multivisceral resection, which remains necessary when true invasion is present or when adequate oncological clearance requires a more aggressive approach.

This case does not suggest avoiding multivisceral resection, but rather individualizing the surgical extent according to tumor biology, anatomy, intraoperative findings, patient condition, and functional consequences. Although organ preservation was feasible, tumor at the inked margins underscores the difficulty of achieving negative microscopic margins in giant retroperitoneal sarcomas. Tumor size alone should not contraindicate resection; the feasibility of complete macroscopic removal and relationships with critical structures should guide the strategy. Further studies are needed to define optimal approaches in extreme presentations.

Conclusion

Retroperitoneal leiomyosarcomas can reach extreme sizes because of their insidious growth and delayed presentation. This case demonstrates that complete macroscopic resection without multivisceral resection may be feasible in selected patients when adjacent organ involvement reflects displacement or limited invasion rather than extensive infiltration. However, organ preservation must not compromise oncological principles, and multivisceral resection remains necessary when required for adequate clearance. Positive microscopic margins and recurrence highlight aggressive biology and the need for close follow-up. Surgical strategy should be individualized according to anatomy, tumor biology, resectability, and expected functional consequences.
