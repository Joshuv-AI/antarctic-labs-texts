Diagnostic ultrasound irradiation combined with microbubbles to modulate carotid body activity for the treatment of obesity-related hypertension in rabbits
Authors: Xiujuan Jiang, Wei Yu, Zijun Chen, Xingling Tan, Bo Xiong, Shunkang Rong, Yiheng Liu, Han Liu, Yiwen Zheng, Jing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarotid body (CB) ablation can reduce sympathetic activity and blood pressure but impair the body's ability to regulate hypoxia. This study explores the efficacy and safety of using microbubble contrast agents combined with high mechanical index diagnostic ultrasound irradiation (HMIUI) to modulate CB activity in treatment of hypertension in rabbits.MethodsObese hypertensive rabbits were randomly divided into three groups: unilateral intervention group (UIG, n = 6), bilateral intervention group (BIG, n = 10), and control group (CG, n = 7). Rabbits received intravenous injection of sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles for 15 min, and irradiation at the carotid bifurcation by continuous diagnostic ultrasound FLASH mode simultaneously. Blood pressure (BP), hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR), peripheral chemoreceptor sensitivity (PCS), and baroreceptor sensitivity (BRS) were measured, and values were compared with before the intervention and 1 month after. In addition, pathology and electron microscopy were used to observe the histological and ultrastructural changes of CB.ResultsIn both UIG and BIG groups, systolic and diastolic blood pressure significantly decreased compared to pre-intervention (p < 0.05). Compared to the control group, the BIG group showed a decrease of 10 mmHg exceeding in systolic and diastolic blood pressures. HVR and PCS decreased by nearly 50% from pre-intervention. Changes in CB injury and fibrous tissue proliferation were found by Histological. TUNEL assay showed varying degrees of apoptosis in the treated CB, and immunofluorescence confirmed the reducing expression of type I and II cells.ConclusionsUltrasound microbubbles combined with HMIUI effectively modulate CB function and reduce blood pressure in an obese hypertensive rabbit model in the short term.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-025-01451-z.Keywords: Diagnostic ultrasound, Microbubbles, Carotid body activity, Hypertension

Introduction

The prevalence of obesity-related hypertension continues to increase over the years worldwide [1]. In Canada, 22% of the population experiences obesity-related hypertension [2]. Similarly, among Chinese adults aged 45 and older, this prevalence has reached 22.7% [3]. Obesity-related hypertension is often accompanied by metabolic disorders, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation. Changes induced by obesity, such as airway narrowing, usually lead to obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome which promotes sympathetic excitation and exacerbates hypertension, resulting in refractory hypertension adversely affecting clinical outcomes [4].

Carotid body(CB), as an important peripheric chemoreceptor, which senses pathological states, such as hypoxia, hypoperfusion, and inflammation, and influences autonomic nervous system balance, can mediate the onset and progression of sympathetic hyperactivity diseases like hypertension. Its function is primarily carried out by two types of cells [5]. Under conditions of hypoxia, hypercapnia, or acidosis, type I cells are activated and release neurotransmitters to transmit signals to the carotid sinus nerve, thereby stimulating the central nervous system. Type II cells provide physical and nutritional support to type I cells, maintaining the structural integrity and microenvironment stability of the carotid body. Studies show it can produce a stronger antihypertensive effect by CB intervention than renal denervation surgery [6]. Animal experiments and clinical studies showed that CB ablation or minimally invasive interventions could provide therapeutic effects on hypertension and heart failure [7–10]. However, CB ablation severely impairs the hypoxia-regulating ability. It was found that CB removal could lead to nocturnal hypoxia, and even sudden fatal outcomes [11]. Therefore, CB intervention techniques need to be improved in safer and more effective ways.

Ultrasound irradiation combined with microbubble contrast agents can produce non-invasive target tissue damage, and it has become a research hotspot nowadays [12]. Xue et al. used the lipid microbubbles to carry the plasmids, combined with the focused ultrasound irradiation, reduced the CB activity and lowered the blood pressure in a hypertensive canine model [13]. It has been found that, using a high mechanical index diagnostic ultrasound irradiation (HMIUI), with the aid of the microbubble enhancement effect, the targeted function could be modulated, and diseased tissues could be destroyed [14–16]. Diagnostic ultrasound provides imaging support while targeting tissues and accurately delivering acoustic energy. This study explores the use of medical ultrasound microbubble contrast agents combined with HMIUI to achieve CB damage control, aiming to treat hypertension while preserving CB hypoxia regulation capabilities, providing an experimental basis for reducing CB activity to treat hypertension with more safety and convenience.

Results

General conditions before and after intervention

All experimental animals survived until the end of the observation period. The model rabbits showed significant increases in values in body weight, blood pressure, and heart rate compared to baseline (Table S1). There were no skin injuries at the ultrasound irradiation site in the intervention group. Throughout the study, the food intake and activity levels of the experimental animals remained stable. Biochemical tests of various markers showed no significant statistical difference in values before and after the CB intervention (Figure S1).

Effects of CB intervention on blood pressure, heart rate, and body weight

We used an t test to assess the differences before and after the intervention. One month after the CB intervention, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP) of UIG and BIG were significantly decreased in values from the pre-intervention phase (all P < 0.05, Fig. 1A–F).

After CB intervention, the HVR decreased by 26.41% in UIG from the pre-intervention phase (4.118 vs. 5.597 ml/min·%SpO2,P = 0.0004,η2 = 0.73), and 44.85% in BIG (3.198 vs. 5.799 ml/min·%SpO2, P < 0.0001,η2 = 0.87) (Fig. 2A, B). HVR showed a statistically significant difference among the three groups after intervention (F(2, 14)= 19.35, P < 0.0001, η2 = 0.73).HVR in UIG and BIG decreased by 25.56% and 42.19% compared with CG (P = 0.0056 vs. P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C). There was no statistically significant difference in SpO2 before and after the intervention in both UIG and BIG (Figure S3).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the safety and effectiveness of using HMIUI combined with the contrast agent in damaging CB in obese hypertensive rabbits, which could downregulate the CB activity and reduce blood pressure. The results (all η2 values indicated large effect sizes) indicate that the intervention effectively damages CB and promotes CB cell apoptosis, achieving non-invasive ultrasound-mediated attenuation of rabbit CB activity for the treatment of obesity-related hypertension. In addition, the respiratory regulation function of CB was preserved, and no significant damage to surrounding tissues and vessels, and no adverse systemic events were observed during the experiment. These findings suggest that this method provides an animal experimental basis for safely treating hypertension by moderately reducing CB activity with more safety.

P2X3 receptor antagonists have been approved by the EMA for the treatment of chronic cough. Some studies explored their use in targeting the carotid body to reduce sympathetic tone in treating heart failure and other high-sympathetic activity conditions [21, 22]. However, due to its lack of specificity for the carotid body, side effects such as taste disturbances and gastrointestinal discomfort were observed. Therefore, targeted ablation to reduce the carotid body’s activity emerges as an important alternative.

Compared to conventional ultrasound imaging modes, the FLASH mode of diagnostic ultrasound generates power pulses with an instantaneous release of ultrasound energy exceeding a mechanical index of 0.8. Microbubble rupture induces cavitation effects, leading to localized high temperatures and pressures to directly disrupt the tissue structure of the target organ. Free radicals and other reactive chemical substances produced by the effects can cause chemical damage, further damaging cells and tissues [23–25]. These effects and mechanisms have been explored in treatments, such as opening the blood–brain barrier, thrombolysis, and tumor therapy in humans [26–29]. However, for larger animals and humans [30], concentrations of microbubble contrast agents in target tissues through intravenous injection were limited, which required higher acoustic intensities and longer durations to achieve tissue-damaging effects. In this study, CB serves as the target organ for intervention, which is characterized by the highest blood flow per unit weight organ in the body [31, 32], and has a rich capillary network and dense connective tissue outer membranes [33]. Microbubbles entered CB through the carotid artery, and cavitation took place within the relatively enclosed cavity, effectively damaging tissue cells. Precisely targeted by imaging ultrasound, microbubbles stayed in the capillary network of CB, getting irradiation for up to 15 min to cause CB damage with its decreased activity achieved.

While reducing CB activity to lower blood pressure, preserving moderate chemoreceptor regulatory capacity is a key research objective. This study evaluates the regulation capabilities of the CB in response to hypoxia and high CO2 by measuring the HVR and PCS. The study systematically evaluated the impact of CB intervention on respiratory regulatory capability. Results showed that bilateral CB intervention reduced regulatory responsiveness by nearly 50%, yet SPO2 remained stable throughout, suggesting that moderate reduction of CB activity is reasonably safe.

The carotid sinus is rich in baroreceptors and adjacent to CB [34], posing a potential risk of impairing baroreceptor function with this method. However, results indicate that after the intervention, there were no significant abnormalities observed in BRS or carotid artery structure [35–39] in experimental animals. This may be attributed to the extremely fast blood flow velocity (20–30 cm/s) in the carotid artery [40], the wide vascular bed, the lower acoustic intensity and microbubble concentration overall, which did not lead to significant damage to the carotid artery and surrounding tissues. During HMIUI combined with microbubble, we observed a transient increase in blood pressure of approximately 20 mmHg, lasting about 5–10 s, followed by a return to baseline gradually. This reaction may be related to sympathetic excitation caused by ultrasound-induced mechanical stimulation. Given the long intervention duration and gradual response, safety is manageable and controllable.

Differing from our previous method of using custom-made cationic microbubbles to carry P2X3–gRNA plasmids to lower blood pressure, this study employed ultrasound contrast agents to enhance CB damage. With similar effects achieved, this new method in this study avoided challenges, such as low gene transfection rates or off-target safety concerns. Commerce available lipid-shelled sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles (SonoVue) were utilized in this study with its safety extensively validated [41–44]. Despite using relatively high doses of contrast agent per body weight in rabbits, no short-term safety issues were observed due to the technique of slow injection and continuous microbubble destruction.

The diagnostic ultrasound equipment used in this study has been widely employed in clinical diagnostics and is a standard device in modern hospitals. Clinicians are highly familiar with its operation and maintenance, which minimizes the technical challenges for widespread adoption. Compared to advanced surgical devices, ultrasound equipment is cost-effective and readily accessible, with energy parameters considered tissue-friendly and safe. In addition, existing clinically approved devices and contrast agents simplify the regulatory approval process, allowing faster and more straightforward translation into clinical practice compared to new drugs or gene therapies.

Unlike invasive treatments such as surgery or radiofrequency ablation, diagnostic ultrasound combined with microbubble therapy is completely non-invasive, causing minimal discomfort and is likely to achieve higher psychological acceptability among patients. With real-time ultrasound imaging, the therapy can precisely target specific tissues, minimizing damage to the surrounding normal structures. The localized accumulation of microbubbles at the target site further enhances the precision of the treatment. Due to the non-destructive nature of ultrasound and the rapid metabolic clearance of microbubbles in vivo, this approach can be repeatedly applied, making it suitable for the long-term management of chronic diseases, such as hypertension and heart failure. Therefore, the technology presented in this study holds broad prospects for translation to human applications, owing to its clinical availability of both devices and materials, non-invasiveness, repeatability, effectiveness, and safety.

While the present approach achieves blood pressure reduction while preserving the body's fundamental hypoxic regulatory capacity—aligning with the therapeutic concept of "moderate regulation"—several limitations regarding its long-term translational implications for humans remain: 1. Model limitations: This study employed a rabbit model of obesity-related hypertension. Whether these findings are generalizable to other hypertension models (e.g., salt-sensitive or age-related hypertension) remains unclear. Furthermore, differences in carotid body (CB) anatomy, hemodynamics, and metabolic characteristics between rabbits and humans necessitate further validation in large animal models, such as pigs or non-human primates. 2. Parameter extrapolation: Significant differences in body weight, carotid artery depth, and blood volume between rabbits and humans mean the acoustic parameters used in this study (MI = 1.2, exposure time = 15 min) will likely require adjustment for human applications. These parameters need to be re-optimized through modeling and experimental studies. 3. Unclear molecular mechanisms: This study focused on functional and morphological outcomes. The molecular signaling pathways (e.g., inflammatory response, oxidative stress, apoptosis pathways) potentially activated by ultrasound-mediated cavitation were not thoroughly investigated.4. Unknown long-term safety and durability: The effects were only monitored for 1 month post-intervention. The lack of long-term follow-up data leaves questions about potential compensatory CB hyperplasia, functional recovery, or the possibility of long-term metabolic or respiratory side effects unanswered. 5. Lack of targeting precision: Although the probe of the MyLab90 ultrasound system can locate the human CB, the FLASH irradiation field encompasses not only the CB but also adjacent vessels and tissues. For human translation, future research should focus on developing ultrasound probes capable of more precise targeting and control.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrated that using clinical HMIUI combined with intravenous injection of medical microbubbles could effectively damage CB, and reduce CB activity and blood pressure in a rabbit model of obesity-related hypertension, which needs further exploration and clinical translation for non-invasively treatment of obese hypertensive in humans.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and protocol

This study was conducted in strict accordance with ethical guidelines for animal research and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval Number: 2021–347).New Zealand rabbits (2.5–3.5 kg, 6 months; 13 males, 15 females) were acquired from Chongqing Medical University's Experimental Animal Center. All procedures adhered to NIH animal care guidelines.

Model induction and blood pressure monitoring

Twenty-eight rabbits were given a high-fat diet (MD12032, 45% fat, Medicine Diets, Jiangsu, China) and received L-NAME intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 15 mg/kg per day. The cuff was selected according to the manufacturer’s guidelines, positioned on the elbow distally aligned with the horizontal line of the heart, and then connected to the VM12 animal monitor. 5–10 min after the rabbits were calm, the cuff automatically inflated and recorded arterial blood pressure five times, and the average value was calculated. After 25 weeks, rabbits with both weight and blood pressure 25% above the baseline were included in the next study phase.

CB intervention methods

Using an online random group generator (randomlist.com), 23 successfully modeled rabbits (10 males, 13 females) were randomized into three groups, Bilateral Intervention Group (BIG, n = 10), Unilateral Intervention Group (UIG, n = 6), and Control Group (CG, n = 7). A diagnostic ultrasound device (MyLab90, ESAOTE, Italy) with a LA523 linear array probe was used to clearly visualize the carotid artery and its bifurcation in B-mode (Fig. 8A), and the probe position was fixed. A 5.9 mg/ml sulfur hexafluoride microbubble solution (SonoVue) was infused into the marginal ear vein at a rate of 40 ml/h using an infusion pump. Simultaneously, the ultrasound device was switched to the contrast mode to film carotid bifurcation (Fig. 8B), and the FLASH button was pressed continuously at a rate of twice per second to irradiate carotid bifurcation (Fig. 8C). The ultrasound irradiation parameters were set as follows: mechanical index at 1.2, sound pressure at 40 kPa, and power at 100%. Continuous ultrasound irradiation of 15 min on each CB of both sides was performed with animals in random order. Blood samples were collected pre- and post-intervention for biochemical tests, and arterial blood pressure was measured after the 1-month post-intervention.

A Carotid bifurcation image in diagnostic ultrasound B-mode. B Carotid bifurcation image in diagnostic ultrasound contrast mode. C Image of microbubble destruction upon clicking the FLASH button. CCA, common carotid artery. ECA, external carotid artery. ICA, internal carotid artery

CB tissue analysis Three hours after the intervention, four rabbits from the BIG and two rabbits from the CG were randomly selected for euthanasia, and bilateral carotid bodies were evaluated for ultrastructure and apoptosis detection. CBs were fixed with glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy (Hitachi HT7700, Japan). Apoptosis in the carotid bodies was detected using a TUNEL assay kit (11,684,817,910; Roche). Three regions were randomly selected on each slide, and the number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells was calculated using ImageJ software. CBs and surrounding tissues of the remaining animals were subjected to the HE, Masson, EVG, Sirius red staining and immunofluorescence examination at the end of the observation period.

Immunofluorescence detection

One month after CB intervention, dual immunofluorescence staining was performed using tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, 1:100; MA5-32,984, ThermoFisher) as a marker for type I cells, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, 1:100, 14–9892-82, ThermoFisher) as a marker for type II cells. Images were observed and captured using a fluorescence microscope (CX40, SOPTOP, China), and the average fluorescence intensity of TH and GFAP was calculated using ImageJ software.

CB and carotid sinus function testing

Hypoxia and CO₂ sensitivity were assessed based on Shin and Duffin’s methods [45, 46]. A #3 small animal anesthesia mask (Figure S6B) was fit to the experimental animal in a conscious state and then connected to an infant ventilator(SLE5000, SLE, UK), a flow sensor, and a multi-channel electrophysiological recorder(RM6240E; Chengdu Instrument Factory, Chengdu, China) (Figure S6A). For hypoxia response, tidal volume (TV), respiratory rate (RR), and SpO₂ values were recorded under normoxic and hypoxic conditions, calculating hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR) by formula HVR = (VE (10%O2)–VE (21%O2))/|(SpO2(10%O2)–SpO2(21%O2))|. For CO₂ sensitivity, the experimental animals inhaled a high CO2 gas mixture (7% CO2, 21% O2, 72% N2) through #3 mask, and the tidal volume change (Vi.0) values were recorded. Then we turned off the gas mixture and provided the animals 10–20 s of pure oxygen, then switched back to the high CO2 gas mixture and recorded the TV values again (Vi10–20 s) (Figure S6C) and calculated Peripheral chemoreceptor sensitivity (PCS) by formula ΔVi% = ΔVi/Vi.0 × 100% (ΔVi = Vi.0–Vi10-20 s). Using Geza's method [47], phenylephrine (PE, 0.1 mg/kg) was injected via the marginal ear vein. A 20G indwelling needle was inserted into the central ear artery and then connected to a multi-channel electrophysiological recorder to record the changes in blood pressure and heart rate (HR). The formula BRS = (HRbaseline–HRmin)/(SBPmax–SBPmin) was used to calculate baroreceptor sensitivity (BRS).

Statistical methods

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Simple linear regression was used to describe continuous variables. Paired or unpaired t tests were used to analyze differences before and after interventions in the same group, or between two groups. One-way ANOVA was used for data with homogeneity of variance in more than two groups and for the Mann–Whitney non-parametric test for non-normally distributed data. Data analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Translational impact statement

This study investigated the use of diagnostic ultrasound irradiation combined with microbubbles to regulate carotid body activity in rabbits, providing a novel, non-invasive strategy to treat obesity-related hypertension by targeting a key mechanism of sympathetic overactivation.
