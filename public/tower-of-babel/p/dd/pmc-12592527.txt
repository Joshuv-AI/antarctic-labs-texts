Remodeling of calcium signaling and store operated calcium entry as a consequence of human neural progenitor cell differentiation
Authors: Silke B Chalmers, Mélanie Robitaille, Francisco Sadras, Sarah J Roberts-Thomson, Gregory R Monteith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe differentiation of neural progenitor cells into glial and neuronal lineages is a fundamental process in neurodevelopment. This complex process is tightly regulated by several signaling pathways, including calcium signaling. Calcium signaling not only regulates neural progenitor proliferation and survival but also regulates gene expression changes that drive the differentiation process and cell fate. One calcium signaling pathway that has been associated with neural progenitor cells proliferation and differentiation is store-operated calcium entry (SOCE), mediated by STIM and ORAI proteins. Using human neural progenitor ReNcell VM model expressing a genetically encoded calcium indicator and high content imaging methods, calcium signaling changes associated with neuronal differentiation were assessed. Differentiated cells exhibited increased spontaneous Ca2+ oscillation and ligand-activated oscillations. Neural progenitor cell differentiation was associated with a shift away from canonical SOCE, with increased expression in ORAI3, which was also identified as a potential regulator of differentiation in this model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22675-y.Keywords: Calcium, ORAI3, Neurogenesis, SOCE, ReNcell VMSubject terms: Calcium signalling, Ion channel signalling

Introduction

Changes in cytosolic free Ca2+ levels ([Ca2+]CYT) are integral to a variety of processes that occur in cells of the brain. These processes include synaptic transmission1, neurotransmitter release2, gene expression3,4, neurite outgrowth5, synaptic plasticity6, proliferation4 and differentiation7. Intracellular Ca2+ levels are tightly regulated by a “toolkit” of Ca2+ channels, pumps and exchangers present on the plasma membrane and intracellular organelles8. Calcium influx in neural cells can occur through a suite of calcium permeable ion channels including various voltage gated Ca2+ channel subtypes9 and different members of the transient receptor potential (TRP) channel family10. In addition to controlling specific cellular events in neurons and glia, these channel types are also linked to disease pathways. For example, in a mouse Parkinson’s disease model, silencing the voltage gated Ca2+ channel Cav2.3 has a neuroprotective effect in substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons11. Similarly in human glial cells, TRPM2 is associated with inflammation-induced oxidative stress in models of Alzheimer’s disease12. Differentiation of murine neural progenitor cells is associated with a remodelling of voltage gated Ca2+ channel and TRP channel expression13,14, however potential changes in other calcium influx pathways have not yet been fully evaluated.

One calcium influx pathway that has been extensively characterized in cells of the immune system and non-excitable cells such as epithelial cells is store-operated calcium entry (SOCE). Several organelles participate in the storing and buffering of intracellular Ca2+ ions, with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) representing the largest store of releasable Ca2+15. The efflux of Ca2+ ions after cell activation produces a net loss of cellular Ca2+. Ca2+ released from the ER must therefore eventually be replenished. SOCE is the Ca2+ influx pathway by which the refilling of the ER Ca2+ store is often achieved16. The canonical pathway for SOCE involves the detection of reduced ER Ca2+ levels by interaction between stromal interacting molecule 1 (STIM1), which rapidly translocates to endoplasmic reticulum-plasma membrane junctions where it directly activates ORAI1 Ca2+ channels, promoting the entry of Ca2+ into the cytoplasm17. These Ca2+ ions are then pumped into the ER by sarcoendoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) pumps18. SOCE is not just involved in restoration of ER Ca2+ stores but can also regulate gene expression particularly through activation of the Ca2+ dependent transcription factor - nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT)19,20. Indeed, loss of functional ORAI1 is associated with immunosuppression, myopathy and ectodermal dysplasia21. Although not as extensively investigated in neurons and glia, SOCE is observed in neural cells where it regulates pathways as diverse as neuroinflammation22 neuromodulation and spontaneous transmitter release23,24.

Although ORAI1 and STIM1 are the canonical components of SOCE, the human genome encodes for three ORAI paralogs (ORAI1, ORAI2, ORAI3) and two STIM paralogs (STIM1 and STIM2). Each paralog has distinct tissue and cell type expression patterns and have unique functional features, leading to differences in both Ca2+ signaling and downstream signaling events25. For example, although ORAI1 activation causes nuclear translocation of NFAT, ORAI2 and ORAI3 do not support NFAT nuclear translocation26. Instead ORAI3 is linked to resistance to oxidative stress in T-helper lymphocyte cells27. Similarly, compared to STIM1, STIM2 has a lower Ca2+ affinity and is more sensitive to minor changes in Ca2+ levels in the ER and is a key regulator of basal [Ca2+]CYT levels28.

The study of SOCE in human neural cells is challenging, but neural progenitor cells offer an opportunity for the detailed study of pathways in human neurons and glia using higher throughput methods (e.g. high content imaging29 and during changes associated with neurogenesis30,31. Neural progenitor cells are multipotent neural cells capable of self-renewing and differentiating into neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. The development of the nervous system necessitates neural progenitor cell proliferation, migration and differentiation, steps in which Ca2+ signals play critical roles4,7,32. STIM1 and ORAI1 mediated SOCE is observed in mouse embryonic, neonatal and adult neural progenitor cells4, indicating that SOCE mediated Ca2+ entry is a conserved mechanisms throughout neural cell development and is present during adulthood4. Moreover, ORAI1 tissue-specific deletion in the brain achieved through a nestin-Cre mice is associated with impaired neural progenitor cell proliferation, both in vitro and in vivo4. In murine and human nervous systems, all STIM and ORAI homologs are expressed33. SOCE is implicated in physiological processes in mouse neural stem cells and neurogenesis4,34. However, such roles in human neurogenesis have not been demonstrated and the potential remodelling of SOCE associated with human neural progenitor cell differentiation has also not been fully assessed.

ReNcell VM is a human neural progenitor cell line derived from the ventral mesencephalic region of the developing brain that have been immortalized by retroviral transduction with the myc oncogene35. This model has been used in the studies of neurodevelopmental disorders36 and neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease37 and Alzheimer’s disease38. Mitogen withdrawal induces differentiation in this model39 and removal of growth factors from ReNcell VM culture induces differentiation into three lineages (neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes) and associated phenotypic changes, such as expression of β3-tubulin in neurons and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GPAP) in glia31,35. Neurons differentiated from ReNcell VM human neural progenitor cells are electro-physiologically active35 but detailed assessment of calcium signaling changes including SOCE has not yet occurred. However, undifferentiated ReNcell VM express SOCE channels and low levels of voltage-gated calcium channels40. Given their reliable neuronal differentiation, immortality, ease of handling and short differentiation time, ReNcell VM cells were chosen as a practical system for investigating calcium signaling dynamics during neural differentiation.

In these studies, we combined the ReNcell VM human neural progenitor cell line model with a genetically encoded calcium indicator and high content imaging methods, to define possible calcium signaling changes and SOCE remodelling associated with neurogenesis. We also assessed a possible role for ORAI3 upregulation associated with ReNcell VM human neural progenitor cell differentiation.

Results

Differentiation of ReNcell VM human neural progenitor cells

Under routine maintenance, proliferative ReNcell VM exhibited a cobblestone morphology typical of undifferentiated cells (Fig. 1a). Consistent with previous reports35, spontaneous sporadic cells with modest GFAP (astrocytic) or β3-tubulin (neuronal) expression were observed in the proliferative population (Fig. 1a, arrow). Removal of growth factors resulted in the formation of interconnected cellular networks structures and elongated cells with spindle-like morphology characteristic of differentiated neural cells (Fig. 1a). GFAP positive cells showed filamentous branching morphologies characteristic of glia while β3-tubulin positive cells showed elongated neurite outgrowths typical of a neuronal phenotype (Fig. 1a). Differentiated monolayers consisted of between 7 and 11% of β3-tubulin positive cells, and 70–74% GFAP positive cells, as assessed via immunofluorescence (Fig. 1b). Levels of mRNA expression of the astrocytic marker GFAP and neuronal marker MAP2 were assessed in proliferative and differentiated ReNcell VM culture at various time points. Both GFAP and MAP2 mRNA were significantly increased after 4 days of differentiation with elevated levels maintained at 14 days after growth factors removal (Fig. 1c). These results indicate that human ReNcell VM neural progenitor cells differentiated under the conditions used in this study.

Basal calcium signaling in proliferative and differentiated rencell VM

In human pluripotent stem cells, neuronal differentiation is associated with enhanced spontaneous electrical activity41 and differentiated human ReNcell VM grown as neurospheres are associated with changes in spontaneous calcium oscillations42. In these studies, we used the genetically encoded calcium indicator jRCaMP1b and single cell high content imaging to comprehensively compare spontaneous and stimulated Ca2+ signals in proliferative and differentiated ReNcell VM neural progenitor cells, respectively. Time-lapse imaging of live ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b was conducted on both proliferative and day 7 differentiated cells (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Video 1). Proliferative ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b had mostly a quiescent basal state (inactive) while differentiated cells exhibited a significant increase in spontaneous calcium transient activity (active) (Fig. 1e).

Heterogeneity of the basal calcium activity was further characterized using an automated unbiased approach inspired by Salles et al.43. Cells were classified into four classes in accordance with their calculated response fraction (RF), which represented time spent above baseline [Ca2+]CYT. These four groups were: (1) inactive cells (RF = 0); (2) transitory cells (0.2 RF 0); (3) regular (0.8 RF 0.2; and (4) maintained (RF 0.8) (Fig. 2a). Approximately 70% of proliferative cells were classified as inactive compared to only 43% of differentiated cells (Fig. 2b). The highest difference between proliferative and differentiated cells in the active state was observed in the transitory class (Fig. 2b). Within the two most prominent active groups; transitory and regular cells; differentiated cells had significantly higher Ca2+ oscillations in comparison to proliferative cells (Fig. 2c). The high-throughput approach used in these studies has demonstrated that in addition to increased spontaneous [Ca2+]CYT transient activity in differentiated neural progenitor cells, it is also the nature of spontaneous activity that is remodeled.

Differential Ca2+ signaling responses in activated proliferative and differentiated ReNcell VM cells

To define possible remodelling of activated Ca2+ signaling with differentiation we first assessed changes induced by the purinergic receptor activator ATP. Purinergic signaling can increase [Ca2+]CYT levels through both ionotropic P2X Ca2+ permeable ion channels and the release of Ca2+ from internal stores via P2Y G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) and the generation of inositol trisphosphate (IP3)44. Addition of ATP to proliferative ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b resulted in an initial rapid increased in [Ca2+]CYT often followed by a period of sustained [Ca2+]CYT (Fig. 3a). ATP activated differentiated ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b also exhibited an initial increase in [Ca2+]CYT followed by a sustained often oscillatory period of elevated [Ca2+]CYT (Fig. 3b). While both proliferative and differentiated cells had significantly higher [Ca2+]CYT after ATP stimulation in comparison to vehicle control, ATP induced relative peak [Ca2+]CYT was significantly higher in differentiated cells compared to proliferative cells (Fig. 3c). Similarly, although ATP treatment increased the average number of oscillations per cell in both proliferative and differentiated ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b, ATP induced oscillations were significantly higher in differentiated cells when compared to proliferative cells (Fig. 3d). Carbachol, a cholinergic agonist that induces Ca2+ influx directly by activating ionotropic nicotinic receptors and can increase [Ca2+]CYT via IP3 production following activation of muscarinic GPCRs45, was also assessed. Similarly to ATP, carbachol also induced [Ca2+]CYT oscillations and these were significantly higher in differentiated cells (Supplementary Fig. 1).

SERCA pumps actively transport Ca2+ from the cytosol into the ER and represent the main mechanism to refill the ER Ca2+ store after calcium store depletion46. Thapsigargin is an irreversible SERCA inhibitor, and we used it to compare the consequences of SERCA inhibition in proliferative and differentiated ReNcell VM cells. Proliferative ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b cells responded to the addition of thapsigargin with a modest and sustained increase in [Ca2+]CYT (Fig. 3e&g). This result could be consistent with some basal ER Ca2+ leak and activation of SOCE. In comparison, differentiated ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b cells did not respond to thapsigargin and no sustained increases in [Ca2+]CYT were observed (Fig. 3f,g). This could be reflective of negligible basal ER Ca2+ leak in differentiated ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b cells and hence an inability for thapsigargin to induce an increase in [Ca2+]CYT and deplete ER Ca2+ stores sufficiently to activate SOCE. Cyclopiazonic acid (CPA), a reversible SERCA inhibitor was also assessed (Supplementary Fig. 1). Similar to thapsigargin, CPA also induced a sustained increase in [Ca2+]CYT in proliferative but not differentiated ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b cells. Collectively, these studies suggest that a significant change in ER Ca2+ homeostasis is a feature of ReNcell VM differentiation. We further explored a specific potential consequence of this remodelling through the assessment of the expression of the components of SOCE and their related isoforms.

Assessment of SOCE components and their related isoforms during differentiation of ReNcell VM cells

Given our observation of altered [Ca2+]CYT responses to pharmacological SERCA inhibition with neural progenitor cell differentiation, high levels of the ORAI2 and ORAI3 isoforms in neural tissue (The human Protein Atlas, https://www.proteinatlas.org/)47 and the lack of previous studies assessing the expression of canonical SOCE components and their related isoforms in this ReNcell VM model, we compared the levels of ORAI1-3 and STIM1-2 during differentiation.

ORAI1 mRNA expression was biphasic during ReNcell VM differentiation, with a significant decrease in ORAI1 mRNA at 2, 4, and 7 days of differentiation and a return to levels similar to proliferative cells by day 14 (Fig. 4a). In contrast, expression of ORAI2 and ORAI3 increased significantly during differentiation (Fig. 4a). Similarly, STIM1 and STIM2 had differential patterns of expression during differentiation. STIM2 mRNA levels did not significantly change at any stage of differentiation (Fig. 4a). In contrast, expression of STIM1 significantly increased at 7 and 14 days (Fig. 4a). ORAI1 and STIM1 protein expression in proliferative and 14 days differentiated ReNcell VM was assessed by immunoblotting (Fig. 4b). For ORAI1, a predominant band at approximately 25 kDa and a weaker band at approximately 35 kDa were detected, consistent with the expression of ORAI1β and ORAI1α variants respectively48. When compared to proliferative ReNcell VM, despite a return of ORAI1 mRNA levels at day 14, ORAI1 protein was still significantly downregulated at 14 days of differentiation (Fig. 4c). Consistent with STIM1 mRNA changes, STIM1 protein expression was significantly upregulated with differentiation (Fig. 4c). At the time these studies were conducted, assessing ORAI2, ORAI3 and STIM2 protein levels was not possible due to unavailability of selective antibodies. However, given the differences between ORAI1 mRNA and ORAI1 protein at day 14, future studies should assess levels of these proteins in this model. These results are consistent with a shift away from canonical SOCE with neural progenitor cell differentiation. Given the ORAI channel isoform switch was most pronounced for ORAI3, we explored the possible association between ORAI3 and ReNcell VM differentiation.

ORAI3 silencing reduces expression of MAP2 and GFAP during ReNcell VM differentiation

To assess the role of ORAI3 in neural progenitor cell differentiation, ORAI3 expression was reduced using shRNA in proliferating ReNcell VM cultures. Following differentiation by mitogen removal, mRNA levels of GFAP and MAP2 differentiation markers were assessed in scrambled control cells and ORAI3 knockdown (KD) cells (Fig. 5). ORAI3 expression levels decreased by approximately 50% in proliferative cells and downregulation persisted during differentiation (Fig. 5a). ORAI3 downregulation significantly reduced neuronal marker MAP2 and astrocytic marker GFAP expression when compared to scrambled control cells expressing a non-targeting shRNA (Fig. 5b,c). A second shRNA targeting a different ORAI3 sequence was used to exclude an off-target effect. While knockdown efficiency following differentiation was lower with this shRNA (Supplementary Fig. 3a), a significant decrease in neuronal marker MAP2 expression was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The astrocytic marker GFAP was also lower, but this did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 3c). These findings suggest that ORAI3 contributes to ReNcell VM differentiation, with its downregulation partially decreasing differentiation marker expression.

Discussion

Calcium signaling plays a central role in neural development, regulating processes like proliferation, migration, survival and differentiation7. ReNcell VM neural progenitor cells provide a valuable model for studying human neural differentiation35,49. Compared to other models such as iPSC, ReNcell VM cells offer the advantage of continuous expansion in monolayer culture and rapid differentiation. However, they do not capture the full range of developmental stages or the diversity of neuron types that iPSCs can generate. Here, we examined how calcium signaling changes after differentiation of ReNcell VM cells, comparing progenitor and differentiated states. Morphological and molecular changes observed following differentiation by growth factor removal (Fig. 1a–c) were consistent with established phenotypic characteristics of differentiation35.

Analysis of basal calcium activity revealed that proliferative ReNcell VM had mostly a quiescent basal state while differentiated cells exhibited a significant increase in spontaneous calcium transient activity (Fig. 1d,e), which is similar to studies in human dentate gyrus granule cells, where differentiated neural progenitor cells exhibited a significant increase in spontaneous [Ca2+]CYT transient activity50. Single cell analysis of basal [Ca2+]CYT activity identified distinct remodelling of spontaneous [Ca2+]CYT activity in proliferative and differentiated cells (Fig. 2). Further experiments combining high-throughput [Ca2+]CYT imaging with cell type identification methods, such as immunofluorescent staining, could extend these observations further and provide insights into whether observed Ca2+ activity types correspond to different cell types in neural progenitor cell monolayers.

When the differentiated ReNcell VM cells were exposed to thapsigargin or CPA, they did not exhibit an increase in [Ca2+]CYT levels (Fig. 3a–d, Supplementary Fig. 1). Due to differences in basal Ca2+ leak from ER Ca2+ stores via mechanisms such as enhanced Ca2+ release from IP3 receptors and ER Ca2+ channels, different cell types can differ dramatically in response to SERCA inhibitors. Cell types with a high basal leak of Ca2+ from the ER will exhibit robust increases in [Ca2+]CYT due to a lack of SERCA mediated Ca2+ re-sequestration into the ER and activation of SOCE due to reduced ER Ca2+ levels. Cells with very little basal activity of ER Ca2+ release channels will not lose ER Ca2+ with SERCA inhibition. For example, in cardiac cells and neurons, thapsigargin often produces no detectable increase in [Ca2+]CYT51,52, whereas in human colonic epithelial cells and MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells SERCA inhibitors cause robust and sustained increases in [Ca2+]CYT levels53,54. The differentiation of ReNcell VM hence produced a thapsigargin response phenotype consistent with neurons and suggest less ER Ca2+ leak than cell types such as epithelial cells. However, further experiments, such as direct measurement of ER Ca2+ leak using ER-targeted calcium indicators, are required to confirm this hypothesis.

The differentiation of neural progenitor cells is associated with increased expression of voltage-gated Ca2+ permeable ion channels, which has been linked to the enhancement of membrane excitability13,55. For example, in mouse neural progenitor cells, differentiation is associated with an increase in T-type calcium channel Cav3.256. Stanslowsky et al. reported a significant increase in mRNA levels of several T-type and L-type Ca2+ channels in human neural progenitor cells upon differentiation57. During the differentiation of SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells, ORAI1 protein is downregulated following 9cRA-induced differentiation58. In the same study, STIM1 protein expression remains unchanged but subcellular redistribution of STIM1 is observed following differentiation58.

Following our observations of altered response to pharmacological SERCA inhibition, expression levels of SOCE contributors were assessed in proliferative and differentiated ReNcell VM cells (Fig. 4). Our observed decrease in ORAI1 expression accompanied by increased ORAI2 and ORAI3 expression was consistent with a shift away from canonical SOCE with neural progenitor cell differentiation. This is consistent with our thapsigargin Ca2+ assays, which suggested that differentiated cells had less ER Ca2+ leak and thus likely to have a reduced requirement for maintained SOCE. It is also consistent with reports that the ORAI1 homologs ORAI2 and ORAI3 are often found at high levels in neural cells (The human Protein Atlas, https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Although STIM1 is the classic activator of ORAI1 and SOCE, STIM1 also interacts with ORAI3 where it drives voltage-independent Ca2+ entry in adult rat hypertrophied cardiomyocytes59. Moreover, in several models, ORAI2 negatively modulates SOCE60–62. For example, in the human neuroglioma cell line H4, ORAI2 overexpression decreases SOCE and reduced ORAI2 expression increases SOCE, implicating ORAI2 as both an ion channel and in some cases an inhibitory regulator of SOCE62. Hence, reduced SOCE associated with ReNcell VM cell differentiation could be the result of not only a down regulation of ORAI1 expression but also an increase in ORAI2. ORAI channels assemble into heteromeric complexes, where the relative abundance of ORAI2 and ORAI3 modulates SOCE and downstream signaling63. In HEK293 cells, increasing the proportion of ORAI2 and ORAI3 within these complexes dampens ORAI1-mediated NFAT nuclear translocation, indicating that ORAI channel composition finely tunes gene regulation26. Moreover, ORAI paralogs play distinct roles in oscillatory Ca2+ signaling at low agonist concentrations26, raising the possibility that they might drive activation of differential transcription factors. Future studies could therefore assess the differential impact of ORAI isoform silencing on Ca2+ oscillations and signalling in this ReNcell VM progenitor cells differentiation model.

Given the marked increase in ORAI3 associated with differentiation, we reduced ORAI3 expression via shRNA and identified ORAI3 as a potential regulator of differentiation of ReNcell VM progenitor cells (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3). ORAI3 downregulation led to reduced induction of both MAP2 and GFAP, suggesting impaired differentiation. As a next step, it would be important to determine whether ORAI3 knockdown also diminishes spontaneous calcium activity and oscillations following mitogen withdrawal as this activity was characteristic of differentiated ReNcell VM cells. Future studies could also assess effects of ORAI1 and ORAI2 silencing which had mRNA expression changes that were more modest compared to ORAI3 in this model. Moreover, given the similar increase in STIM1, we also attempted to silence STIM1 in ReNcell VM progenitor cells but failed to achieve knockdown using two independent STIM1 shRNAs (data not shown). It is unknown if the difficulties in silencing STIM1 is a result of a stronger driver for increased STIM1 expression that could not be reversed by shRNA, or if STIM1 is essential to cell survival. Future studies, using an inducible shRNA/CRISPR/Cas9 system could be used to address this question. Such studies are warranted given STIM1 is a regulator of the early differentiation of mouse embryonic stem cells into neural progenitor cells64.

Finally, ReNcell VM cell line has been proposed as a model for several conditions and diseases including neuroinflammation65, neurodevelopmental disease36 Parkinson’s disease37 and Alzheimer’s disease38. We have developed a high throughput methodology that allows assessment of intracellular calcium levels in neural progenitor cells and during their differentiation into neurones and astrocytes. This methodology could be applied to drug screening, assessing intracellular calcium changes typical of proliferative versus differentiated cells and this could be useful in the assessment of therapeutic targets for various neurological diseases.

Methods

Cell culture

ReNcell VM cells were purchased from Merck (RRID: CVCL_E921). Cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling at QIMR Berghofer, Brisbane, Australia and mycoplasma tested at the Translational Research Institute, Brisbane, Australia. Proliferative cells were cultured on pre-coated laminin (12 ug/mL, 23017015, ThermoFisher Scientific) plasticware in ReNcell Maintenance Medium (SCM005, Merck) supplemented with 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, GF003, Merck) and 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF, GF001, Merck). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 up to passage nine. Differentiation was achieved by cultivating the cells in growth factor free ReNcell Maintenance Medium. Cells were differentiated for 2–14 days as indicated in the figure legends. Media was refreshed at least three time per week.

Generation of rencell VM-jRCaMP1b cell line

Genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator jRCaMP1b (pGP-CMV-NES-jRCaMP1b RRID: Addgene_63136) was cloned into pCDH-EF1-FHC (RRID: Addgene_64874) using BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites. Lentiviruses were generated in HEK293T cells (generous gift from Prof Sunil Lakhani at The University of Queensland Centre for Clinical Research) using second generation lentivirus (pCMV-dR8.2, RRID: Addgene_8455 and pCMV-VSV-G, RRID: Addgene_8454) and lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001, ThermoFisher Scientific). The day after transfection, HEK293T media was removed and replaced with ReNcell maintenance medium. After 24 h, the supernatant was harvested, cleared by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C for at least 1 h. Proliferative ReNcell VM cells were plated at 2.5 × 105 cells/flask on a laminin coated T75 flask (day 1) and transduced with jRCaMP1b virus preparation (4.5 mL of viral supernatant, 5.5 mL of ReNcell maintenance medium with final concentration of 20 ng/mL bFGF, 20 ng/mL EGF, and 4 µg/mL of polybrene (TR1003G, Millipore) (day 2)). On day 3, media was replaced with fresh ReNCell VM maintenance medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL bFGF and 20 ng/mL EGF and 0.5 ug/mL puromycin was added on day five.

Generation of rencell VM-shRNA knockdown

pLKO.1 TRC-scrambled shRNA (RRID: Addgene_1864) and pLKO.1 TRC-stuffer (RRID: Addgene_10878) were purchased from Addgene. ORAI3 targeting sequences were cloned using AgeI and EcoRI restriction sites. Forward oligo sequences are as follow: shORAI3:CCGGCATCCACAACCTCAACTCTGTCTCGAGACAGAGTTGAGGTTGTGG ATGTTTTTTG and shORAI3#2: CCGGGCAGGAACTAGAGGAACTGAACTCGAGTTC AGTTCCTCTAGTTCCTGCTTTTTTG. Lentiviruses were generated in HEK293T cells (generous gift from Prof Sunil Lakhani at The University of Queensland Centre for Clinical Research) using second generation lentivirus (pCMV-dR8.2, RRID: Addgene_8455 and pCMV-VSV-G, RRID: Addgene_8454) and lipofectamine 2000 (11668027, ThermoFisher Scientific). The day after transfection, HEK293T media was removed and replaced with ReNcell maintenance media. After 24 h, supernatant was harvested, cleared by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C for at least 1 h. Proliferative ReNcell VM were plated at 4000 cells/well on laminin coated 96 well plate (Phenoplate, PerkinElmer) (day 1) and transduced with shRNA virus preparation (35 μL of viral supernatant, 65 μL of ReNcell maintenance media with 40 ng/mL bFGF, 40 ng/mL EGF, and 4 µg/mL of polybrene (day 2)). On day 3, media was replaced with ReNcell VM maintenance media supplemented with 20 ng/mL bFGF and 20 ng/mL EGF and 0.5 μg/mL puromycin was added to the next media change on day 5. Proliferative samples were harvested on day 8 post transduction and growth factors were removed to start the differentiation process, while maintaining puromycin selection pressure.

Immunofluorescence

ReNcells VM were plated on pre-coated laminin black-walled 96 well plates (FAL353219, Falcon) and differentiated by growth factor removal as described above. Cells were washed 3× with PBS, fixed in 4% formaldehyde (28908, ThermoFisher Scientific) for 20 min, washed 3× with PBS and permeabilised with 0.3% Triton-X 100 (RT8787, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA, B4287, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 5 min at room temperature. After 3x PBS washes, blocking was performed with 5% BSA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C in the dark with primary conjugated antibodies in blocking buffer (GFAP Cy3 primary conjugate at 1:200, RRID: AB_11213580, Merck; anti-tubulin βIII clone 2610-TB3 at 1:50, RRID: AB_2573607 ThermoFisher Scientific). The next day, wells were washed 3× with PBS then incubated in the dark with the nuclear counterstain 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:1000 in PBS, D1306, Molecular Probes) for 10 min, followed by 3× PBS washes. Cells were maintained in PBS for imaging via automated epi-fluorescent microscopy in an ImageXpressMICRO(Molecular Devices). Proportion of neural types in differentiated monolayers were assessed with MetaXpress software (Version 7.0, Molecular Devices) using the Multi Wavelength Cell Scoring tool.

RT-qPCR

ReNcell VM were plated in pre-coated laminin 96 well plates and differentiated by growth factor removal as described above. RNA from pooled wells were harvested with RNeasy Mini kit (74106, Qiagen) prior to differentiation (day 0) and at 2, 4, 7 and 14 days of differentiation as per manufacturer instructions. Isolated RNA was reverse transcribed using the Omniscript RT kit (Qiagen) as per the manufacturer instructions. Synthesized cDNA was amplified using TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (4352042, Applied Biosystems), and real-time PCR reactions were performed using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under universal cycling conditions. Relative gene expression was quantitated using the comparative CT method (ΔΔCT), normalized to GAPDH (Hs99999905_m1). TaqMan gene expression assays used for this study were GFAP: Hs00909233_m1, MAP2: Hs00258900_m1, ORAI1: Hs03046013_m1, ORAI2: Hs00259863_m1, ORAI3: Hs00743683_s1, STIM1: Hs00162394_m1, STIM2: Hs00372712_m1 (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in protein lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitors (11836153001 and 04906845001, Roche Applied Science). Gel electrophoresis was performed (10 µg protein) using Mini-PROTEAN® TGX Pre-cast Gels, and proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (4568084 and 1704157, Bio-Rad Laboratories). Membranes were blocked for 1 h in 5% skim milk in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (P9416, Sigma-Aldrich) (PBST) before incubating overnight at 4 °C with anti-ORAI1 (RRID: AB_1078883, 1:4000, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-STIM1 (RRID: AB_398267, 1:1000, BD transduction Laboratories) or anti-β actin (RRID: AB_476744, 1:10000, Sigma-Aldrich). Goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase conjugate secondary antibodies RRID: AB_11125142, RRID: AB_2921252, Bio-Rad Laboratories) were diluted 1:10,000 in 5% skim milk in PBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were imaged using SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Chemiluminescent Substrate (34076, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Intracellular calcium measurement in rencell VM-jRCaMP1b

ReNcell VM-jRCaMP1b were seeded on pre-coated laminin black walled 96-well plates (FAL353219, Falcon) and proliferated for 48 h or differentiated for 7 days as described above. Prior to testing, ReNcell maintenance media was removed and replaced with FluoroBrite™ DMEM (extracellular Ca2+ concentration of 1.8 mM, A1896701, ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 4 mM L-glutamine and 25 mM HEPES (15630106, ThermoFisher Scientific). Cells were imaged at 37 °C using an epi-fluorescent automated ImageXpressMICRO system (Molecular Devices) and a 10× objective (excitation/emission 561/594 nm, 100 ms exposure and 3× binning). Images were taken at 1 image every second for a total of 180 s, with automated addition of reagent after 30 s of imaging. Agents added were, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (A1852, Sigma-Aldrich), Carbachol (212385, Merck), Thapsigargin (T9033, Sigma-Aldrich), Cyclopiazonic acid (CPA) (C1530, Sigma-Aldrich) UltraPure water (10977015, ThemoFisher Scientific) and DMSO (D8418, Sigma-Aldrich).

Image analysis

Image quantitation and single cell segmentation was conducted via MetaXpress software (version 7.0, Molecular Devices, USA) as previously described method66. Recorded fluorescence values were analysed with MATLAB (R2021b, MathWorks, USA). Fluorescence values for each individual cell were normalised against its own minimum fluorescence value (F/Fmin). Active cells were defined as those with a F/Fmin value above 1.2 during image acquisition period. Further classification of activity types (inactive, transitory, regular or maintained) was determined as time spent above F/Fmin of 1.2; referred to as response fraction (RF)43,67. Activity classes were defined as follows: inactive (RF < 0), transitory (RF = 0–0.2), regular (RF = 0.2–0.8) and maintained (RF > 0.8). Ca2+ oscillations were detected using the MATLAB find peaks function, with minimum peak prominence defined as 0.3 F/Fmin. AUC was calculated using the MATLAB function for trapezoidal numerical integration (trapz), on fluorescence data which had been normalised against its own minimum fluorescence value (ΔF/Fmin). Single cell responses were analysed across Ca2+ imaging experiments, data is shown as mean of cell populations (average number of cells analysed per experimental condition was 290).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10 Version 10.2.2 using statistical tests stated in figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted on mean population values.
