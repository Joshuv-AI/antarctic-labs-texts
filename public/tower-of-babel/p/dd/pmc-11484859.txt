Exploring public attitudes toward implementing green infrastructure for sponge city stormwater management
Authors: Chunyan Shi, Yueqiu Xia, Hong Qiu, Xiaoyang Wang, Yue Zhou, You Li, Gen Liu, Siwen Li, Weijun Gao, Tongyu Xu, Jianing Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSponge city stormwater management (SCSM) strategy in China aims for sustainable stormwater handling. While many studies have examined the technical aspects of sponge city green infrastructure (SCGI), few have explored public perspectives. This study sought to understand public perceptions, the perceived value of SCGI’s ecosystem service benefits, and the potential for diverse financial compensation methods in sponge city construction. A survey conducted in five Northeastern Chinese cities, involving 1,534 participants, was analyzed using descriptive statistics and logistic regression. The findings reveal no significant correlation between socio-demographic factors and understanding of stormwater management, indicating the concept’s broad accessibility. Public valuation of ecosystem services showed clear priorities, and factors like homeownership and flood experiences significantly impacted the valuation of specific services. Moreover, the study identified a generally positive public attitude towards investing in SCSM, particularly through stormwater fees, underscoring the viability of diverse funding mechanisms. These insights are pivotal for policymakers and urban planners in formulating sustainable and resilient urban water management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74343-2.Keywords: Sponge city stormwater management, Sponge city green infrastructure, Ecosystem service benefits, Willingness to invest, Perception surveySubject terms: Environmental economics, Environmental impact, Psychology and behaviour, Sustainability, Ecosystem services, Freshwater ecology

Introduction

Flooding and waterlogging disasters, extensively studied worldwide, pose significant threats to urban water safety, economic stability, and human life1,2. The increasing frequency of these issues, exacerbated by rapid urbanization and more frequent extreme stormwater events, is particularly problematic in cities with inadequate water management. Internationally, various urban resilience design approaches have been developed to bolster cities’ abilities to manage urban flooding risks. These include best management practices and low impact development in the US and Canada, water sensitive urban design in Australia, and sustainable drainage systems in the UK. These strategies, focusing on enhancing urban stormwater management resilience, are at the forefront of city design and have garnered substantial global attention.

This issue, exacerbated by climate change-induced extreme weather and ineffective urban drainage systems, is prevalent across China, notably in the water-scarce north3. Urban flooding not only disrupts daily life but also impacts the economy4. Addressing urban flooding and enhancing urban resilience against extreme weather has thus become a pressing concern in China. Facing significant sustainability challenges, particularly urban flooding due to rapid urbanization and climate change, China has initiated the sponge city stormwater management (SCSM) strategy5–7. This innovative approach to urban stormwater management aims to enhance a city’s resilience in adapting to environmental changes and managing rainwater-related disasters. A sponge city operates akin to a sponge, absorbing, storing, and purifying rainwater, subsequently filtering it through the soil to replenish urban aquifers and releasing it for reuse when necessary. The SCSM approach primarily utilizes various green infrastructures such as permeable pavements, green roofs, detention basins, rain gardens, and rain barrels, etc. These methods are effective in reducing runoff, peak flows, and water velocity, thereby mitigating issues like waterlogging, flood risk, and water scarcity8–10.

Much research on sponge city green infrastructure (SCGI) has centered on its engineering and technical aspects, aiming to engage stakeholders in its widespread adoption. However, the human aspect of this initiative is still not adequately explored11,12. Typically, SCGI solutions often lead to win-win outcomes, effectively improving ecosystem services that enhance net societal value and ecosystem resilience13. Beyond just mitigating floods, SCGI offers an array of broader ecosystem benefits14,15, such as improving air and water quality, enhancing biodiversity, augmenting scenic beauty, restoring groundwater infiltration, and mitigating urban heat island effects, among others16–22. These benefits, closely related to public life, present tangible and valuable effects. High level of recognition on SCGI and its benefits usually positively influences residents’ behavior23.

A formidable challenge in SCSM is ensuring adequate fiscal resources to scale SCGI from pilot programs to widespread projects, sustaining long-term development and operation funding. Research suggests that public financial support can be crucial, with policies from developed countries like the USA, Australia, and Brazil, which collect rainwater fees for the construction, maintenance, and operation of storm sewers, ditches, streams, retention ponds, and roads, serving as models24–26. Therefore, understanding public perception and preferences is vital for integrating SCGI into stormwater management programs effectively. Specifically, comprehending people’s existing knowledge about flood risk, SCGI’s benefit preferences, and their acceptance and support for sponge city construction, and how this knowledge influences their attitudes and motivations towards adopting new solutions, is key to enhancing SCGI designs and reducing implementation barriers27.

Previous studies have explored residents’ views on green infrastructure. Some researchers have analyzed how citizens perceive green roofs and their willingness to pay for the installation and maintenance of these nature-based solutions28. Others have investigated citizens’ viewpoints on a set of stormwater beneficial management practices in Brazil29. The focus of these studies has primarily been on which types of infrastructure the public deems effective, rather than their preferences for the ecosystem service benefits these infrastructures provide. For the public, benefits are more tangible and understandable, relating more to the human dimension than the technical one. Several studies have examined the statistical relationship between various socio-economic, demographic, and environmental factors that might influence residents’ perceptions of green infrastructures and their decisions to implement them30,31. Assessing residents’ willingness to pay to support or participate in SCGI programs has also been a popular topic in the literature32,33. However, there has been limited research exploring the perceived value of SCGI benefits from the residents’ perspective.

To fill these knowledge gaps and enhance our understanding of the perceived value of SCGI benefits from residents’ perspectives, this study aimed to examine the influence of regional socioeconomic factors and other elements on residents’ perceptions and preferences. It also sought to explore public willingness for different investment and payment approaches, delving into multidimensional financial compensation strategies for SCSM strategy. The findings of this research can provide key insights and information for policy makers and urban planners in creating sustainable and resilient cities. Additionally, this study contributes to the relatively sparse literature on the public’s perception and attitudes towards the ecosystem service benefits of SCGI practices. This information could be crucial in planning resilient cities by promoting the adoption of sustainable stormwater management practices.

Materials and methods

Study site

The survey was conducted in five northeastern Chinese cities: Shenyang, Dalian, Changchun, Siping, and Baicheng. These cities are representative pilot sponge cities selected for their unique characteristics and strategic importance in the implementation of Sponge City initiatives (shown in Fig. 1). Located in the temperate monsoon climate zone, these cities experience an average annual rainfall of approximately 800 mm, predominantly concentrated between June and October. This results in significant spatial and temporal variability in water resources. Northern China, particularly these cities, is characterized by rapid urbanization, severe freshwater scarcity, and frequent urban flooding, making them ideal locations for studying sponge city construction. These cities also differ in population size, economic development, and urban infrastructure, providing a diverse context for analyzing the effectiveness of sponge city initiatives. For instance, Shenyang and Dalian are major urban centers with significant GDP contributions, while cities like Siping and Baicheng represent smaller, less economically developed areas. This diversity ensures a comprehensive understanding of how sponge city strategies can be adapted to different urban contexts.

Study area location map34,35. (The map was created by the author using source data from https://www.nbcharts.com and further enhanced with Microsoft PowerPoint).

The implementation of sponge city projects in these cities began in 2013, supported by both national and local governments. Several major projects have been rapidly executed, yielding promising results. By the time of our survey, residents in newly developed or renovated areas had already experienced the impacts of these projects for approximately two years.

Additionally, the cities selected vary in the scale of sponge city construction, the extent of urban flooding issues, and the number of designated flood-prone areas, providing a broad spectrum of experiences with urban water management. This variation allows for a more accurate and less biased assessment of the public’s perceptions and the effectiveness of sponge city construction across different urban settings.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire comprised four main sections (as illustrated in Fig. 2). The first section gathered socio-economic data from respondents, including gender, age, education level, employment status, household income, home ownership in the area, and experiences of property damage due to flooding or waterlogging. The second section focused on awareness and preference surveys, asking respondents about their familiarity with the SCSM initiative and their valuation of the ecosystem service benefits provided by SCGI. The third section, an informational segment, did not require responses but presented relevant concepts about SCSM initiative and explanations of SCGI. This was intended to equip respondents with the necessary context for more informed answers in the subsequent section. In the fourth section, the survey explored public investment willingness in sponge city construction, focusing on three different types of investments: public investment, purchase of green bonds, and rainwater fees. Public investment questions aimed to gauge support for government-funded sponge city construction. Green bonds, defined as special bond tools for raising funds for environmental protection and sustainable development with longer return periods, are specifically used for eligible green projects. Stormwater fees, paid by each user, are allocated for the construction, maintenance, and operation of storm sewers, ditches, streams, retention ponds, roads, etc. This section sought to explore multidimensional compensation methods for funding sponge city construction from three distinct perspectives and approaches. The full questionnaire used in the survey is provided in the supplementary material.

Data collection

In April and June 2023, our research team conducted intercept surveys in various cities to gather data on public attitudes towards sponge city projects. Survey sites were strategically chosen to include residential neighborhoods, urban plazas, and parks that feature sponge city infrastructure, aiming to capture a broad spectrum of public opinions. Additionally, to maximize the reach and diversity of respondents, the questionnaire was also disseminated online via social media platforms. Participation in the survey was voluntary, with all participants being informed adults. Before participating, individuals were introduced to the purpose of the survey and informed about the incentive of receiving a reusable grocery bag upon completion as a token of our appreciation for their time. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants before their participation. This consent process was approved as part of our study protocol by the Institutional Review Board.

Before commencing the survey, we ensured that all methods were carried out in strict accordance with the guidelines and regulations set forth by the ethical standards of the University of Kitakyushu. The experimental protocols, including the survey method and data collection process, received prior approval from the University of Kitakyushu’s Institutional Review Board, ensuring adherence to ethical research practices.

Data analysis

Data analysis encompassed both descriptive analysis and statistical evaluation, utilizing chi-square tests, Pearson’s correlation, and a binary logistic regression model. Our survey garnered 1,534 responses, which were meticulously compiled, organized, and analyzed. We selected the chi-square test to identify any significant associations between socio-economic factors (such as gender, education, and income) and perceived knowledge of stormwater management because it is a non-parametric test well-suited for categorical data. The chi-square test was appropriate for our analysis as it allows us to evaluate whether there is a statistically significant association between two categorical variables—in this case, socio-economic factors and respondents’ knowledge of stormwater management—using a P-value threshold set at p< 0.05 for significance36,37. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation was employed to explore relationships between continuous or ordinal socio-economic variables and preferences for ecosystem service benefits. This test is particularly useful for identifying the strength and direction of relationships between variables that are measured on an interval or ratio scale.

Regarding the respondents’ investment decisions, these were treated as dichotomous variables, addressable through a cumulative probability distribution function. Falling under utility maximization models, the binary logistic regression model integrates microeconomic theories of consumer behavior and psychological choice behavior38. Consequently, a binary logistic regression model was adopted for analyzing the willingness to invest issue. Respondents’ willingness was recorded in a binary format (YES = 1, NO = 0). Results

Sample demographics

A total of 1,534 individuals from five distinct cities participated in the survey. The socio-demographic data gathered from these respondents has been analyzed and contrasted with the latest available statistics from 2021, as published by the Chinese National Bureau of Statistics (NBS)40. This comparison is concisely presented in Fig. 3.

Figure 3(a) reveals that the gender distribution among the survey respondents is almost even, with males accounting for 49.7% and females for 50.3%. This closely aligns with the actual population proportions in the areas under study, as indicated by the NBS data. For instance, in Liaoning Province, the population is 49.92% male and 50.08% female. In Jilin Province, the proportions are 48.86% male and 51.14% female.

Figure 3(b) displays the age distribution of the survey participants, categorized into various age groups. Notably, a significant portion, nearly 60%, consists of individuals aged between 18 and 40. This is expected, as the online questionnaire was primarily completed by younger respondents. On the other hand, older respondents were more likely to participate in the in-person surveys conducted at selected locations. A direct comparison with the NBS data is not feasible, given that minors were not included in our survey. Consequently, and for the aforementioned reasons, our questionnaire evidently did not engage a substantial proportion of the population over 60 years old. While only 11.1% of our respondents are above 60, this age group represents more than 20% of the overall Chinese population.

Figure 3(c) depicts the educational levels of the survey respondents. In this sociodemographic aspect, we focused on individuals with higher education, as literature suggests that this group tends to be more engaged with policy matters and often exhibits a higher willingness to invest or pay. This approach aligns with the educational trends in the regions under study. Liaoning and Jilin Provinces rank 7th and 8th respectively among China’s 31 provinces in terms of education level. The Northeast has historically led the nation in educational attainment, and it remains one of the areas with the highest proportion of population with higher education. In Jilin Province, 16.64% hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, while in Liaoning Province, the figure is 17.56%. Given these premises, the educational level of the participants surpasses the regional average, with 37.8% holding at least a bachelor’s degree.

Figure 3(d) shows the employment status of the survey respondents. A direct comparison with the NBS data was challenging due to missing categories. Notably, only 5.3% of our respondents are retired, which is significantly lower than the pensioner population in Jilin Province at 16.4%, and Liaoning Province at 20.5%. This discrepancy may be attributed to the survey’s online distribution and in-person locations, which may have limited accessibility for the older demographic.

The monthly net household income analysis presented in Fig. 3(e) could not be directly compared with the NBS data. According to the 2022 statistics from the Statistics Bureau of Jilin and the Liaoning Provincial Bureau of Statistics41, the average monthly wage in Jilin Province is 6,655 CNY, with an average monthly pension for retirees at 2,850 CNY, and a minimum wage standard of 1,880 CNY. In Liaoning Province, the average monthly wage stands at 6,843 CNY, the average monthly pension is 3,198 CNY, and the minimum wage is set at 1,910 CNY. The income categories in the survey were determined based on these real-world figures from both provinces.

Figure 3(f) details home ownership, showing that 73% of the respondents own local housing, while 27% do not. According to the NBS data, the rate of home ownership in various cities within Jilin and Liaoning Provinces exceeds 70%. Therefore, the survey results fall within a reasonable range.

Figure 3(g) presents data on the respondents’ experiences of property damage due to flooding or waterlogging. According to the survey, 30.8% of respondents have experienced property losses due to flood disasters, while 69.2% have not. Although the northern regions of China, including the Northeast, receive less rainfall than the southern regions, with an average annual precipitation of around 800 mm, the Northeast has faced increasing challenges in recent decades. Factors like frequent extreme weather events, rapid urbanization, and aging drainage infrastructure have led to issues such as urban flooding, waterlogging, and non-point source pollution. These disasters have resulted in significant damage to houses and vehicles, and in more severe areas, there are threats of landslides and mudflows, endangering lives.

Respondents’ knowledge of SCSM

The second part of our survey was dedicated to assessing perceptions and knowledge regarding SCSM and preferences for the benefits of SCGI ecosystem services. It was found that a majority of respondents (59.6%) were familiar with the concept of SCSM, while 40.4% reported a lack of understanding. Our null hypothesis (H0) stated that there is no significant relationship between respondents’ knowledge of SCSM and variables such as gender, age, education level, income, current employment status, homeownership, and flood experience. We set a significance level of 95%; thus, if the p-value exceeded 0.05, we failed to reject H0, indicating no significant correlation. Conversely, a p-value less than 0.05 would lead to the rejection of H0. Our analysis revealed that, indeed, there is no statistically significant correlation between respondents’ knowledge with SCSM and their gender, age, education level, household income, homeownership status, or experience with flood waterlogging, as the p-values were all above 0.05.

Evaluation and analysis of the importance and influencing factors of potential ecosystem service benefits of SCGI.

The assessment of respondents’ preferences, as depicted in Fig. 4, indicated a clear prioritization of ecosystem services provided by SCGI, particularly in flooding and waterlogging mitigation, which was identified as the foremost benefit. Additionally, the capacity for water reuse was highly rated, reflecting a growing acceptance and positive perception of non-potable water reuse. This is particularly interesting given that people are often reluctant to embrace water reuse due to perceived health risks, psychological and aesthetic factors, cultural and social factors, and trust issues.

Respondents’ perceived importance of ecosystem service benefits provided by SCGI.

The positive perception observed in our survey may be attributed to several factors. First, there has been a significant shift in attitudes toward water conservation in urban areas, driven by the increasing recognition of the need to conserve freshwater resources. Cities and urban areas are major consumers of water, and as populations grow, the pressure on existing water supplies intensifies42. A continuing growth of population, coupled with a reduction in surface and groundwater resources over the past years, has heightened the demand for secure water supplies and the long-term sustainable management of water resources. The world’s population is expected to grow by 2–3 billion people by 2050, which will further increase the demand for freshwater. Additionally, by 2050, the urban population is projected to be about 2.9 billion more than in 2009 (3.4 billion), with urbanization occurring at the same rate as global population growth, plus migration from rural areas43. This growing demand has led to a greater emphasis on sustainable water management practices, including the adoption of decentralized water recycling systems for non-potable uses.

Moreover, advanced water treatment technologies have played a crucial role in making water reuse more acceptable to the public44. These technologies ensure that water is treated to a high standard, making it safe for various non-potable applications45. For instance, countries like Japan and Australia have successfully implemented non-potable water reuse systems that are well-integrated into daily life, providing benefits such as cost reduction, resource efficiency, and improved water security46. A study conducted on university students in Pretoria revealed their willingness to adopt recycled water systems for non-potable uses, especially if it would lead to lower bills47. In some Chinese cities, rainwater collected from rooftops is used not only for outdoor purposes like gardening, car washing, and firefighting storage but also for indoor uses such as flushing toilets48–50.

The services associated with SCGI’s regulatory functions received widespread acclaim, evidencing the community’s strong appreciation for their role in flood prevention and water management. Supportive services were also well-regarded, emphasizing the community’s value on biodiversity and its enhancement through SCGI. Notably, services directly impacting human welfare, such as flood control, freshwater supply, recreational opportunities, and aesthetic appreciation, garnered widespread recognition and value among the residents. This observation is consistent with analogous results found in other research studies51,52. However, it is noteworthy that more than 40% of respondents rated the restoration of wildlife habitat as “Not at all important,” and more than 20% responded similarly to pollination.

This trend might reflect a broader pattern observed in urban areas, where residents often prioritize services that have immediate and visible benefits to human well-being over those with more indirect effects. For instance, concerns about potential negative impacts, such as an increase in mosquitoes, unkempt aesthetics, and physical safety issues, have been observed in other contexts, such as in Toronto, Canada, where residents worried that brownfield conversions to green infrastructure might expose children to contamination from unknown chemicals53. Similarly, a study found that urban populations tend to undervalue ecosystem services that are less directly connected to their daily lives, such as pollination and wildlife habitat restoration, due to a lack of awareness about their critical roles in maintaining long-term ecological health54. Furthermore, in a review of studies on public attitudes towards the ecosystem service benefits generated by green infrastructure, it was found that in 77% of the studies, people were surveyed or interviewed about their attitudes towards these benefits. The most frequently discussed values were functional aspects like flood protection and water quality improvements, along with aesthetic, health, and safety benefits. Less frequently mentioned were values related to the conservation of biodiversity and financial aspects like property value55. This aligns with our findings, suggesting that services such as wildlife habitat restoration and pollination, which are crucial for the sustainability of ecosystem health but less directly connected to daily human experiences, are often undervalued.

To examine the factors influencing public prioritization of the value attributed to ecosystem services by urban green infrastructure, we employed Pearson’s correlation analysis to discern the impact of socio-economic factors. Pearson correlation coefficients and their significance levels were calculated56.

This comprehensive analysis encompassed a range of socio-economic variables, including gender, age, education level, employment status, household income, homeownership, knowledge of SCSM, and experience of property damage due to flooding or waterlogging. The resultant heatmap presented in Fig. 5 delineates the interplay between the level of interest in ecosystem service benefits (rows within the heatmap) and the diverse socio-demographic categories (columns of Fig. 5). A notable finding was that homeownership exerted a statistically significant influence (p < 0.01) on the valuation of four specific ecosystem services: educational and cultural enrichment, water quality enhancement, aesthetic appeal, and flood control efficacy. This implies that individuals with higher homeownership status tend to place greater emphasis on these particular benefits. Moreover, the analysis uncovered a significant relationship between the experience of flood-related property damage and the valuation of flood control and drainage services—indicating that increased personal exposure to flooding correlates with a heightened concern for these services.

Analysis of public willingness to invest in SCSM construction and influencing factors

The final section of the study examined public willingness to invest in SCSM construction and the factors influencing this willingness. To ensure informed responses, an informational session with a concise overview of SCSM and SCGI was introduced prior to inquiring about investment intentions. This provided participants with essential knowledge about SCGI and its functionality. Subsequently, they were asked to make investment choices regarding SCSM projects. Among the 1,534 respondents, approximately 84.22% supported public investment in sponge city stormwater management projects, 56.71% expressed willingness to purchase green bonds if issued by the government, and 65.97% were agreeable to paying a specific stormwater fee to support these projects.

A logistic regression analysis was employed to explore the relationship between public investment willingness and various factors, including respondents’ demographic characteristics, property attributes, flood or waterlogging experiences, and knowledge of SCSM. The findings, detailed in Tables 2 and 3, and 4, revealed distinct significant influencers for the three types of investments, with statistical significance at the 10%, 5%, or 1% levels. The logistic model’s fit was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow (H&L) statistical test57. This test’s null hypothesis assumes congruence between model predictions and observations; with H&L’s significance values exceeding 0.05 in all three models, this suggests acceptance of the null hypothesis, indicating a good model fit.

As illustrated in Table 2, a significant and positive correlation exists between respondents’ homeownership, experiences of property damage from flooding or waterlogging, and knowledge of stormwater management, and their willingness to support public investment (P < 0.01). Those with homeownership are more likely to back public investment, 88 times more than non-homeowners. Individuals who have experienced property damage due to flooding or waterlogging are 5 times more inclined to support public investment compared to those who haven’t. Moreover, respondents knowledgeable about stormwater management are 245 times more likely to support public investment than those without such knowledge.

Table 3 reveals that the willingness to invest in green bonds is influenced by a wider range of factors, including the ones mentioned above, as well as educational level and household income. Compared to those with primary education, individuals with junior high school, high school or technical school, and university education are 3, 2, and 7 times more likely to invest, respectively, while those with postgraduate education are 6 times more likely. Household incomes within the 7000–9999 and > 10,000 ranges are 9 and 11 times more likely to invest compared to the < 3000 income group. Homeowners’ willingness to invest is three times that of non-homeowners; those who have experienced flood-related property damage are twice as likely to invest; and those knowledgeable about stormwater management are 44 times more likely to invest than those without this knowledge.

Table 4 shows similar patterns in respondents’ willingness to pay additional stormwater fees, where educational level, household income, and knowledge of stormwater management are significant determinants. The educational levels of junior high and high school do not exhibit statistical significance (p > 0.1). In contrast, respondents with a university degree or postgraduate education demonstrate a willingness to pay that is 3 times greater than those with only primary education. Additionally, respondents with household incomes between 7000 and 9999 and over 10,000 exhibit a willingness to pay that is 13 and 17 times greater, respectively, compared to those earning less than 3000. Furthermore, respondents knowledgeable about stormwater management show a willingness to pay that is 26 times higher than those without such knowledge.

Discussion

The socio-demographic traits, homeownership status, and flood or waterlogging experiences of residents do not influence their understanding of stormwater management. This intriguing result suggests that variables like age, gender, education level, income, and other demographic factors are not pivotal in shaping an individual’s comprehension of stormwater management. Additionally, this finding may be linked to the fact that the surveyed areas are sponge city pilot regions, where about 60% of respondents showed an understanding of SCSM. The implementation of numerous pilot projects in these areas has likely heightened the visibility and relevance of stormwater management, enabling residents to gain knowledge through direct experience or community discussions, regardless of their socio-demographic backgrounds. Regardless of the specific reasons, this finding underscores the accessibility and widespread communicability of the SCSM concept, highlighting its high level of transferability and general awareness among the public.

The survey underscores a significant correlation (p < 0.01) between homeownership and the valuation of four key ecosystem services: educational and cultural enrichment, water quality enhancement, aesthetic appeal, and flood control efficacy. Figure 6, a radar chart, displays the average preferences for these services among all respondents, homeowners, and non-homeowners, calculated using a 5-point scale. It reveals a distinct trend: homeowners exhibit a stronger preference for cultural services like recreation, scenic beauty, and education & culture, while non-homeowners lean towards provisioning services such as water reuse, wildlife habitat restoration, and pollination. This pattern resonates with broader findings in urban studies literature. Proximity to city parks, regional parks, and natural areas is a known urban resident priority58,59. North American studies corroborate this, showing a willingness to pay premium prices for properties with superior views, entertainment amenities, and accessible ecosystem services60. The underlying reasons for these preferences among homeowners could stem from their substantial investment in their properties, prompting them to value services that directly enhance their living environments and contribute to cultural and aesthetic enrichment. Additionally, homeowners often adopt a long-term investment perspective, recognizing that benefits in water quality and effective flood control not only ensure the sustainability of their locality but also augment property value and overall life quality. A study by the National Association of Appraisers in 2007 showed that sustainable, environmentally friendly homes can lead to increased property values. Thus, this trend mirrors a deeper engagement and vested interest in services that have direct, tangible benefits to their property and personal well-being.

Regarding the significant correlation between respondents who have experienced flooding and the preference for flood mitigation benefits, this is understandable. Direct experiences with flood-induced property damage significantly raise an individual’s awareness of such risks31,61. This personal encounter often has a greater influence on prioritizing appropriate preventative measures than simple knowledge transmission or indirect experiences. Individuals who have endured flooding events likely possess a more profound understanding and consciousness of prevention and response strategies, subsequently leading them to place a higher value on ecosystem services related to flood or waterlogging management.

Through a binary logistic regression model analysis, respondents’ willingness to support public investments, purchase green bonds, and pay specific stormwater fees were compared. Results indicate a significant positive correlation between homeownership, experiences of property damage due to flooding or waterlogging, and knowledge of stormwater management with public investment willingness. In China’s unique context, most people believe that the government should manage public utilities, with only a minority advocating for allocating more funds to other sectors such as healthcare and education instead of utilities. Those with homeownership and a better understanding of stormwater management are more inclined to support public investment.

Support for purchasing green bonds dropped nearly 30% compared to public investment support, with factors including education level and household income influencing decisions. These factors are known to positively impact willingness to support SCSM initiatives. However, green finance, while an effective way to channel resources toward Nature-Based Solutions, is complex in practice. SCSM projects often struggle to attract green investors due to dispersed and intangible benefits, such as blue-green infrastructure for rainwater retention and recreation. Without clear administrative regulations or mechanisms to ensure returns on private investments, these public goods are less appealing to private investors.

Compared to green bonds, the public seems more willing to pay additional stormwater fees, with a nearly 10% higher rate than those willing to invest in green bonds. Factors like education level, household income, and knowledge of SCSM significantly influence this willingness. Figure 7’s Sankey diagram illustrates the relationship between educational level, monthly household income, and knowledge of SCSM (left side) and public willingness to pay stormwater fees as potential support for sponge city construction (right side). The flow bands represent the proportion of respondents with specific beliefs about stormwater fees as a solution. The data show that with higher education levels, agreement to pay fees increases, with those holding a bachelor’s degree making up the largest group at 30%. Education, proven to be a crucial factor influencing behavior and views62–64, may lead to a better understanding of environmental issues. Higher education often correlates with higher income, impacting willingness to pay28,65. Households earning between 7,000 and 9,999 and over 10,000 showed the highest willingness, each constituting nearly 30% of those willing to pay. The majority of those knowledgeable in SCSM are also willing to pay, with only a small fraction unwilling, a trend opposite in those with less knowledge. This research underscores the importance of raising awareness and acceptance of SCSM initiatives. Only with sufficient public understanding and support can the potential of public investment, green finance, and stormwater fees be fully realized.

Conclusion and policy implications

Sponge city green infrastructure (SCGI) presents a novel, sustainable approach to sponge city stormwater management (SCSM), offering various ecosystem services beyond flood reduction. This study involved surveying 1,534 individuals in five Northern Chinese pilot Sponge Cities, aiming to gauge their understanding and perceived value of SCGI benefits, investment willingness, and influencing factors. The study explores different dimensions of funding compensation methods and potential for SCSM construction, adding a crucial aspect to understanding the multifaceted approach required for the effective implementation of these urban resilience initiatives. This exploration provides valuable insights into financing strategies that are vital for the successful development of SCSM construction. The key findings indicate:

Most respondents (59.6%) were familiar with the concepts of rainwater management in sponge city, with no significant correlation to socio-demographic and socio-economic factors, homeownership, or flood experiences.

To effectively harness SCGI for sustainable urban water management and resilience building, comprehensive policy measures are crucial. These should include initiatives to enhance public awareness and understanding of SCGI’s ecological and socio-economic benefits. Urban planning must integrate public preferences and address concerns regarding ecosystem services. Developing varied financial strategies, such as public investments, green bonds, and rainwater fees, is essential, with considerations for different socio-economic demographics. Implementing educational programs to improve public knowledge about stormwater management will positively influence investment willingness. Prioritizing urban resilience through SCGI solutions tailored to regional needs and ensuring long-term sustainability with effective funding and maintenance plans are also key. These strategies collectively can guide policymakers and urban planners in developing resilient, sustainable urban water management systems.

This study, while providing valuable insights, has certain limitations. One key limitation is the regional focus on Northern China, which may not fully represent the diverse conditions across the country. Additionally, the reliance on survey responses introduces potential biases, as perceptions and attitudes might differ from actual behaviors. The cross-sectional nature of the study restricts the ability to observe changes over time or infer causality. Future research should consider a more diverse geographic scope and incorporate longitudinal data to capture evolving perceptions and practices related to SCSM initiatives.
