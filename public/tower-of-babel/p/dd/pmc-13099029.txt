The awake axillofemoral bypass
Authors: Khalid Nasser Bin Ghali, Raed Sami Altowairki, Muzackir Abubaker Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aortoiliac occlusive disease is the severe form of peripheral arterial disease requiring complex vascular reconstruction, typically performed under general anesthesia (GA). However, GA carries increased risks in patients with significant comorbidities. We report a case of a 61-year-old male with chronic aortoiliac occlusion, uncontrolled hypertension, sepsis, and heavy smoking, presenting with bilateral limb ischemia and sensory-motor deficits. He underwent axillary-to-bilateral femoral artery bypass and right above-knee amputation under thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA) combined with peripheral nerve blocks (PNB) and light sedation. A T4–L2 sensory block was achieved with stable hemodynamics throughout the 7-hour surgery, with minor hypotension managed by ephedrine. Postoperative analgesia with continuous epidural bupivacaine-fentanyl infusion and multimodal therapy provides excellent pain control. The patient required only brief ICU monitoring and was transferred to the ward without complications. This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of TEA with PNB as an alternative to GA in high-risk vascular patients, offering effective anesthesia, hemodynamic stability, and superior pain control while minimizing perioperative risks.

Introduction

Critical limb ischemia (CLI) continues to be a significantly morbid disease process for the aging population. The international consensus on the definition of CLI is the following: any patient with chronic ischemic rest pain, ulcers, and gangrene attributable to objectively proven arterial occlusive disease. Peripheral artery diseases (PAD) patients are at an exceptionally high risk for cardiovascular events and most eventually die of a cardiac or cerebrovascular event.[12] Axillofemoral bypass to treat aortoiliac occlusive disease is an extra-anatomic bypass which is an alternative to aortobifemoral bypass in patients deemed to be unfit to undergo an aortic operation.[3] Administering anesthesia to high-risk patients requires careful planning and specialized strategies. These patients, often with comorbidities or advanced age, face higher perioperative risks, making vigilant intraoperative monitoring and advanced techniques essential. And with growing advantages and safety of awake peripheral nerve block (PNB) in high-risk patients.[4] This case highlights the feasibility and safety of thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA) and PNB in the Awake Axillofemoral Artery Bypass surgery in a high-risk patient.

Case Presentation

61-year-old male with history of chronic aortoiliac occlusion disease, uncontrolled hypertension, sepsis secondary to stage four gluteal ulcer, and heavy smoker. Presented with bilateral impalpable femoral arteries, cold right lower limb, bilateral motor, and sensory deficits. The patient was referred to our hospital for axillary artery to bilateral femoral arteries bypass and right above knee amputation. A computerized tomography angiography revealed complete aortoiliac occlusion extending from below the level of renal arteries. His investigations were within normal limits except for inflammatory markers, white blood cells count, and C Reactive Protein were all high due to sepsis. His cardiac function including echocardiography and myocardial perfusion was within normal limits. His airway examination revealed Mallampati Class II, adequate mouth opening, and full neck range of motion.

On the morning of the surgery, his heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), respiratory rate (RR), and oxygen saturation (SpO2) were 110 beats/min, 154/49 mmHg, 20/min, and 99%, respectively. The patient’s height was 161 cm, and he weighed 41.2 kg.

In the operating room, Standard ASA monitors applied to the patient including the pulse oximeter, electrocardiography (ECG), and noninvasive blood pressure cuff. Baseline values were as follows: HR: 114/min, BP: 133/80 mmHg, and SpO2: 99%. Under full aseptic technique, an arterial line is inserted in the left brachial artery. A dose of 10 mcg of fentanyl and 2 mg of midazolam were given to the patient before inserting a thoracic epidural in the right lateral position at the T7/T8 intervertebral space using an 18G Tuohy epidural needle. The targeted space was identified by loss of resistance to saline. The depth from skin to epidural space was 8 cm. epidural catheter was fixed to 14 cm on skin and adhered to the back with transparent dressing. After a test dose of 3 mL of 2% lidocaine, a loading dose of 5 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine was given slowly. A 7fr triple lumen central line and 8.5fr cordis sheath were inserted in the right internal jugular vein. A right Interscalene, serratus anterior, and musculocutaneous blooks with ultrasound guidance one shot were done to achieve optimum coverage. A dose of 20 mL of 0.375% of ropivacaine, 5 mL of 0.25%, and 5 mL of 0.25% of bupivacaine were injected respectively. Oxygen was administered via a face mask at 6 liters/min throughout the surgery. A sensory block height of T4 to L2 was achieved bilaterally (assessed by pinprick) that allowed the surgery to start. A top up of 5 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine was given by an epidural catheter. A sedation infusion of dexmedetomidine at a rate of 0.7 mcg/kg/h was started before the skin incision. During the surgery, the patient was comfortable and pain free. The patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout the procedure with episodes of hypotension and bradycardia managed with a total of 3 mg of ephedrine. As a part of multimodal analgesia, the patient had 10 mg of ketamine and 1000 mg of paracetamol. At the end of procedure, an additional dose of 5 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine was given by an epidural catheter. The duration of the surgery was around 7 hours. The estimated blood loss was 1 L, and 3 units of packed red blood cells were transfused. The patient was monitored in the ICU for one day then shifted to the ward. The pain was well controlled with 0.125% bupivacaine and 2 mcg/mL fentanyl epidural infusion running on 5–10 mL/h and regular paracetamol 500 mg.

Discussion

Axillofemoral bypass surgery under TEA and PNB demonstrate the safety and feasibility of complex vascular surgery in the awake patient. Classically, these complex surgeries are performed under general anesthesia due to the complexity and potential for significant blood loss and cardiopulmonary events.[56] However, GA carries increased risks in patients with significant comorbidities, such as sepsis, which leads to hemodynamic instability.[7] TEA and PNB offer several advantages in this population, including superior pain control, reduced stress response, and avoidance of airway manipulation, which is especially beneficial for complex surgery.[4]

Conclusion

This case highlights the successful use of TEA and PNB for axillofemoral bypass surgery. TEA provides effective intraoperative anesthesia, hemodynamic stability, and superior postoperative pain control, while avoiding the risks associated with GA. In patients with complex vascular surgery, TEA may serve as a safe and valuable alternative, especially when carful perioperative planning and monitoring are applied. Further studies are needed to establish clear guidelines and broaden the use of regional anesthesia in major vascular surgeries for high-risk populations.
