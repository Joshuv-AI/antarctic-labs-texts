A model testing study of cervical cancer screening behavior using rogers’s protection motivation theory
Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Mohammadi, Zohreh Karimi, Motahareh Shirani, Sona Elyasi, Hadiseh Monadi Ziarat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is a major global health issue, particularly in developing countries. Pap smear-based cervical cancer screening (CCS) is crucial for prevention. The Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) aids in understanding psychological factors influencing CCS behaviors. This study investigated CCS determinants among Iranian women using Rogers’ PMT. This cross-sectional study involved 254 women visiting health centers in Kermanshah and Tehran in 2024. A modified PMT was used with a validated questionnaire measuring fear, perceived vulnerability, perceived intensity, response efficacy, self-efficacy, response cost, demographics, and CCS-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Path analysis was conducted using AMOS. Participants exhibited a mean CCS behavior score of 3.62 (SD = 2.07). Path analysis revealed significant negative total effects on CCS behavior for fear (Standardized Estimate = −0.238, p = 0.009), response cost (Standardized Estimate = −0.111, p = 0.006), and perceived vulnerability (Standardized Estimate = −0.064, p = 0.021). Conversely, positive total effects on CCS behavior were observed for response efficacy (Standardized Estimate = 0.049, p = 0.003), protection motivation (Standardized Estimate = 0.155, p = 0.01), self-efficacy (Standardized Estimate = 0.106, p = 0.007), knowledge (Standardized Estimate = 0.011, p = 0.002), and attitude (Standardized Estimate = 0.052, p = 0.007). Fear, response costs and perceived vulnerability negatively impact CCS behavior among Iranian women, while perceived intensity, response efficacy, protection motivation, knowledge, attitude, and self-efficacy are positive factors. The PMT-based model can guide educational programs to promote CCS behavior, informing targeted counseling and program development to enhance CCS uptake.Keywords: Uterine cervical neoplasms, Early detection of cancer, Protection motivation theory, Proof of concept studySubject terms: Cancer screening, Health services

Background

Cervical cancer continues to be a significant global public health problem, with an estimated 604,127 new cases and 341,831 deaths recorded worldwide in 2020. These figures correspond to a global age-standardized incidence rate of 13.3 cases and a mortality rate of 7.2 deaths per 100,000 women-years. The burden of this disease is marked by profound socioeconomic inequalities; incidence rates in countries with a low Human Development Index (HDI) are three times higher, and mortality rates are six times higher, than those in countries with a very high HDI1. In the context of Iran, a comprehensive analysis of national data reported an age-standardized incidence rate (ASR) as high as 6.0 per 100,000 in population-based registries2.

Despite its significant global burden, cervical cancer is recognized as a preventable and curable disease if detected early and treated adequately. The existence of proven, cost-effective interventions—from HPV vaccination to the screening and treatment of precancerous lesions—has led the World Health Organization (WHO) to establish a global strategy with the vision of eliminating cervical cancer as a public health problem3,4. Increasing cervical cancer screening (CCS) has been shown to enhance early detection and treatment, consequently leading to a reduction in both incidence and mortality rates associated with this malignancy5,6.

The Pap smear is a highly effective method within the context of CCS7. Beyond its affordability, this test serves as a powerful tool for the early detection of cervical cancer among seemingly healthy women8,9. The Pap smear has the potential to reduce the incidence of cervical cancer by up to 79% and its associated mortality by as much as 70%5,10.

The Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) provides a useful framework for predicting and intervening in behaviors related to cancer prevention and early detection11. Rogers introduced this theory in 1975 to explain the impact of fear on health attitudes and behaviors. The model posits that the adoption of recommended health behaviors in response to health threats (protective behaviors) is a direct result of an individual’s motivation to protect oneself. Rogers proposed that fear influences protection motivation (the intention to engage in protective behavior in response to a health threat) through six constructs, ultimately leading to the enactment of health behaviors12,13.

These six constructs are: Perceived vulnerability: an individual’s belief that they are susceptible to a health risk; Perceived intensity: an individual’s belief that the risk is serious; Rewards: an individual’s perception of the benefits associated with maladaptive (unhealthy) behaviors; Response efficacy: an individual’s expectation that an adaptive response (protecting oneself) can eliminate the risk; Perceived self-efficacy: an individual’s belief that they can successfully perform the protective behavior; perceived Response costs: an individual’s estimate of any costs (such as: money, personal, time, and effort) associated with performing the protective behavior12,14,15 (Fig. 1).

Conceptual framework of the study based on PMT.

This theory posits a two-stage process involving threat appraisal and coping appraisal, as well as the construct of fear. Threat appraisal focuses on factors that increase or decrease the likelihood of maladaptive responses, such as avoidance of protective behaviors or denial of the health threat. This cognitive mediation stage comprises perceived vulnerability and perceived intensity16. Coping appraisal emphasizes responses to the health threat and factors that increase or decrease the likelihood of adaptive responses. In other words, it assesses the ability to cope with and avoid the health threat. This cognitive mediation stage consists of perceived self-efficacy, perceived response efficacy, and perceived response cost. Fear serves as an intervening variable between perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and threat appraisal, while protective motivation is synonymous with behavioral intention and drives the initiation and continuation of protective behavior. It acts as an intermediary construct between the two stages (threat and coping appraisal). For protective motivation to be elicited, perceived vulnerability and perceived severity must outweigh the rewards of maladaptive responses, and perceived self-efficacy and perceived response efficacy must outweigh the costs of adaptive (protective) responses17.

The PMT has been employed as a conceptual framework in numerous studies18–20. Xu and Chen, for instance, examined smoking behavior and concluded that PMT is well-suited to guide research aimed at smoking prevention and cessation20. In another study, Nabizadeh et al. utilized PMT to predict vitamin E and C consumption among factory workers19. Chambers et al. also applied PMT to investigate HIV preventive behaviors among American Indian adolescents18.

In the present study, CCS through Pap smear was considered a protective behavior. We aimed to employ PMT as a theoretical framework to predict and further investigate early detection behavior of cervical cancer using Pap smears. Beyond PMT constructs, we also explored women’s knowledge and attitudes regarding CCS. A review of the literature conducted by the researcher indicated a relationship between knowledge, attitudes, and certain PMT constructs in other research areas21,22, thus warranting a more comprehensive investigation of these two constructs in the context of CCS behavior. Considering the aforementioned, the current study aimed to examine CCS behavior based on PMT.

Methods

Study design and participants

For this cross-sectional study, a two-stage cluster sampling method was employed to recruit participants from health centers in Kermanshah and Tehran, ensuring both geographical representation and logistical feasibility. In the first stage, the cities of Kermanshah and Tehran were treated as two distinct geographical strata. Within each city, a comprehensive list of all active health centers affiliated with Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences and Iran University of Medical Sciences, respectively, was compiled to create a sampling frame of clusters. From this frame, two health centers were selected from each city using a simple random sampling technique, resulting in a total of four selected clusters. In the second stage, a systematic random sampling approach was used to select individual participants within each chosen health center. For each of the four centers, a list of all registered women who met the initial inclusion criteria was obtained. A sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of eligible women in each center’s registry by the target sample size allocated to that center. A random starting point was then selected from the list, and every k-th woman was subsequently invited to participate in the study. The selected individuals were contacted during their visits to the health center, where final eligibility was confirmed and written informed consent was obtained before administering the questionnaires. Data collection took place from April 2024 to September 2024.

Sample size calculation

G*Power software (version 21) was used to calculate the sample size23. Considering an α level of 0.05, a power of 0.95, and an effect size of 0.1, the required sample size was determined to be 254.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they provided written informed consent, were married (at least 6 months post-marriage), were aged between 21 and 65 years, had no history of cervical cancer, and had not undergone a hysterectomy.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded from the study if they withdrew their consent or failed to complete the questionnaires.

Research instruments

The following instruments were used in this study:

Demographic information form

This form was designed by the researcher and included questions about age, gender, marital status, duration of marriage, history of cervical cancer, and history of hysterectomy.

PMT constructs measurement tool

This tool was designed and psychometrically tested by Hassani et al. in 2014 to measure PMT constructs in CCS (using the Pap smear test)24. The tool consists of 26 items that fall under the subscales of behavioral motivation (with 3 questions, e.g., “I want to have a Pap smear”), perceived vulnerability (with 3 questions, e.g., “I am worried about having cervical cancer”), perceived intensity (with 4 questions, e.g., “Cervical cancer imposes high costs on me and my family”), fear (with 3 questions, e.g., “I am afraid that the Pap smear will confirm my cancer”), response cost (with 2 questions, e.g., “I feel embarrassed about having a Pap smear”), response efficacy (with 4 questions, e.g., “The Pap smear helps in the early detection of cervical cancer”), and self-efficacy (with 7 questions, e.g., “I have a Pap smear, even though I feel embarrassed about doing it”). The tool was scored using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (score 1) to strongly agree (score 5). The scores for each construct were obtained by summing the scores of each subscale. The validity and reliability of the tool were assessed by Hassani et al. The content validity of the tool was confirmed using a panel of experts (CVI = 0.89, CVR = 0.90), and the reliability of the tool was confirmed through internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70–0.93) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.72–0.96)24.

Knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) questionnaire

The KAP questionnaire was developed and psychometrically tested by Khosravi et al. in 2012 to assess Iranian women’s knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding CCS25. The questionnaire comprised 20 questions on knowledge, 9 on attitude, and 8 on practice related to Pap smear screening. For the knowledge subscale, correct answers were scored 1, while incorrect or ‘don’t know’ responses were scored 0. The attitude subscale employed a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree (score 1) to strongly disagree (score 5). The practice subscale assigned 1 to correct behaviors and 0 to incorrect ones. Subscale scores were calculated by summing the item scores, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge, more positive attitudes, and better practice regarding Pap smear screening. To establish the instrument’s validity and reliability, Khosravi et al. assessed its content validity through expert review, reporting Content Validity Ratios (CVR) of 0.8 for the knowledge subscale and 0.6 for both the attitude and practice subscales. For reliability, they reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.80, 0.82, and 0.93 for the knowledge, attitude, and practice subscales, respectively25.

Data collection and ethical considerations

Adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical principles of research, this study secured approval from the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: IR.KUMS.REC.1402.214). To safeguard participant confidentiality, strict measures were implemented. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point.

Participants were provided with self-rating questionnaires and sociodemographic data sheets to complete independently. To ensure comprehension, the researcher was available to clarify any queries or provide verbal explanations. Adequate time and privacy were afforded to each participant for questionnaire completion, with the average data collection duration being approximately 10 min. At the conclusion of each session, the researcher meticulously verified the completeness of all questionnaires.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and AMOS (version 22.0, Smallwaters Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA). The Pearson correlation coefficient was employed to assess the relationship between each variable and CCS behavior. A path analysis was performed using AMOS. To enhance data quality, we screened for careless responding by calculating the standard deviation of each participant’s responses across all survey items. This approach is a recognized technique for identifying invariant or “straight-lined” responding patterns, which can compromise the validity of the findings26. According to Meade and Craig, a key assumption is that inattentive respondents are more likely to exhibit such uniform response patterns. While no universal cutoff exists, we established a conservative threshold of a standard deviation below 0.2 for our 5-point Likert scale. This value was chosen to effectively isolate the most extreme cases of non-engagement (e.g., using the same answer for nearly all items) without incorrectly flagging participants with genuinely consistent, but attentive, attitudes. These calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel 2007, with custom functions developed to efficiently compute the standard deviation for each participant.

To assess the normal distribution of the data, skewness (± 3) and kurtosis (± 7) indices were used. A box plot was employed to identify univariate outliers; samples identified as outliers in most variables were considered for potential removal. Multivariate outliers were detected using the Mahalanobis d-squared test with Amos version 25 software. Items with (P < 0.001) were considered for possible exclusion.

In order to conduct the path analysis, 5 steps of the modeling process were carried out:

Step 1: model specification

This study utilized the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) as its conceptual framework13. Based on the constructs detailed in the background section, a path model was specified to examine the direct and indirect predictors of CCS behavior. In addition to the core PMT constructs, variables such as knowledge and attitude were incorporated into the model to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the behavior, consistent with prior literature.

Step 2: model identification

Before estimation, the model was checked for identifiability. A necessary condition for model identification is that the number of free parameters to be estimated must be less than or equal to the number of unique pieces of information in the variance-covariance matrix of the observed variables. The number of unique data points is calculated by the formula p( p + 1)/2, where p is the number of observed variables27. For the 10 observed variables in this study, there were 55 unique data points [1011/2 = 55]. The proposed model sought to estimate 33 free parameters. As the number of data points (55) was greater than the number of parameters to be estimated28, the model was over-identified (with 22 degrees of freedom) and therefore could be estimated.

Step 3: model Estimation

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to estimate the model parameters. Specifically, the maximum likelihood estimation method was utilized within the AMOS software.

Step 4: model fit assessment

The fit of the proposed model to the empirical data was assessed using goodness-of-fit indices.

Step 5: model refinement

Given the satisfactory fit of the model as evidenced by the goodness-of-fit indices, no further modifications were deemed necessary.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical profile of participants

A total of 254 individuals participated in this study. Following the data screening procedure for indifferent responding (response standard deviation < 0.2), it was determined that no participants met the criteria for exclusion. Therefore, the final sample for all subsequent analyses was N = 254. The mean age of the participants was 38.07 years (SD = 9.49). Participants had been married for an average of 15.79 years (SD = 10.39), with a mean age at marriage of 21.19 years (SD = 4.17). The average number of children and pregnancies reported were 1.77 (SD = 1.08) and 2.11 (SD = 1.38), respectively. Regarding educational attainment, 50.4% (n = 128) of participants held academic degrees, while the remaining 49.6% (n = 126) had non-academic education. A history of cervical cancer was reported by 3.93% (n = 10) of the sample.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the determinants of CCS protective behaviors among Iranian women using Rogers’ PMT. The findings revealed a mean CCS behavior score of 3.62 (on a scale of 8), indicating a moderate level of screening practice. This finding suggests that the level of CCS behavior among the studied women is comparable to that reported in similar Iranian contexts, such as the study by Malmir et al. (2018), which found a mean score of 3.1 before an educational intervention14. In contrast, our findings indicate a higher screening uptake compared to a study in India by Dsouza and colleagues (2022), where only 10% of women had ever been screened. This difference may be attributed to variations in healthcare system accessibility, cultural norms, or national screening awareness programs between the two countries29.

In the modified Rogers model developed in this study, fear emerged as a significant direct variable inversely influencing protective CCS behaviors. “Fear” is defined as a feeling of apprehension, anxiety, or dread related to an anticipated negative event. This emotion arises from uncertainty and the unknown outcome of a Pap smear. The expressed fears regarding Pap smears were categorized into three main groups: fear of disease (fear of a positive Pap smear result indicating cancer, stemming from the unknown and potential consequences of the disease), fear of pain (fear of discomfort during the procedure, influenced by previous experiences, hearsay, or mental imagery), and fear of other complications (fear of the Pap smear revealing other genital problems that may harm one’s health). The findings of this study demonstrated that as fear levels increased, participants exhibited weaker CCS behaviors.

There was a positive and direct correlation between perceived vulnerability and fear. This relationship was inversely correlated with CCS protective behavior. Furthermore, the perceived intensity was a significant factor in the outcome. This variable directly influenced CCS behavior and indirectly affected fear, with an inverse relationship observed. Consistent with our findings, Khalednejad et al.30 demonstrated a significant statistical correlation between the mean fear score and CCS protective behavior among women.

In a study examining the Health Belief Model to promote skin cancer screening behaviors among rural Iranian women, fear, perceived intensity, and perceived costs were found to be significant predictors of protective behavior. The model accounted for 25% of the variance in behavior31. Similarly, in our study, path analysis of the proposed model revealed that fear was the most significant predictor of changes in CCS behavior. However, while Rogers’ original model suggests that fear initially influences motivational behavior and subsequently cervical cancer screening behavior, our model demonstrates a direct, unmediated relationship between fear and CCS behavior. A noteworthy finding of this study is the significant direct negative effect of fear on CCS behavior, a departure from the traditional PMT framework where fear’s influence is hypothesized to be mediated by protection motivation. Our empirical model, derived from the path analysis, suggests that in the context of cervical cancer screening among Iranian women, fear may operate as a more immediate barrier rather than a cognitive antecedent to motivation. This direct pathway could be interpreted as an “affective override,” where high levels of fear—potentially stemming from the dread of a cancer diagnosis, perceived pain, or social stigma—are powerful enough to bypass the deliberative process of forming a protective intention and instead lead directly to behavioral avoidance. This discrepancy between our findings and the original theory highlights a critical area for future research and suggests that in highly fear-eliciting health contexts, the role of fear may be more complex than originally theorized.

The findings of this study revealed that response costs had a negative total effect on CCS behavior, indicating that higher response costs were associated with poorer CCS outcomes. In essence, an increase in the “response cost” variable in this study signifies that spending money on a Pap smear ultimately results in feelings of discomfort and embarrassment. These findings align with previous studies conducted in Iran by Jowzi et al. in 201332, Karimi et al. in 201133, and Bakht et al. in 201528. The consistency of these results can be attributed to the similarity of the samples and populations studied, which exhibit similar behaviors and attitudes. Interpretation of the results suggests that women seeking healthcare services first analyze and assess the perceived benefits of health behaviors such as cervical cancer screening. The findings of Gao et al. in 2023 also demonstrated that perceived intensity, response costs, and self-efficacy can indirectly influence screening behavior through screening motivations34.

In this study, a positive correlation was observed between perceived response efficacy and CCS behavior among women. In fact, higher perceived response efficacy was associated with better CCS behavior. Perceived response efficacy refers to the belief that Pap smear is effective in early detection, prevention, or treatment of cervical cancer. This variable reflects the belief that regular Pap smears can lead to early diagnosis, prevention, early treatment, and reduced mortality. The results of the relationship between perceived response efficacy and CCS behavior in women are consistent with previous research conducted by Ackerson and Preston, Jowzi et al., and Bakht et al.32,28,35. Moreover, individuals vary in their susceptibility to diseases and protective behaviors, with attitude and knowledge being determining factors. In this study, knowledge was significantly and positively correlated with perceived response efficacy, which in turn influenced women’s CCS behavior. The results of this part of the study are consistent with the findings of Bakht et al. in 201528.

Another significant finding of this study was the positive correlation between the constructs of protective motivation and perceived self-efficacy with CCS behavior. This indicates that higher levels of these constructs lead to improved CCS outcomes among women visiting healthcare centers. Other studies have also examined the impact of self-efficacy on health behaviors, aligning with the results of our research36,37. In essence, high levels of self-efficacy empower women and facilitate the adoption of healthier behaviors such as cervical cancer screening, a factor that should be considered when designing educational programs30.

Similar to prior research, this study was subject to limitations. One notable limitation was the possibility of participants’ reluctance to provide truthful responses due to concerns about confidentiality. To minimize this potential bias, the researcher implemented measures to reassure participants of the anonymity of their data, thereby creating a more conducive environment for honest responses. Additionally, the ‘rewards’ construct, a component of the original Protection Motivation Theory, was not measured in this study. This was a direct result of the methodological decision to use a pre-existing, validated instrument tailored to the Iranian context (Hassani et al., 2014). While this tool has demonstrated psychometric soundness for assessing key PMT constructs related to cervical cancer screening in this population, it does not include a subscale for the rewards of maladaptive behaviors. Prioritizing the use of a culturally validated instrument meant that this variable could not be included in our analytical model. Future studies could aim to develop or adapt instruments that also capture this construct to allow for a more comprehensive application of the theory.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study validates a modified Protection Motivation Theory model for explaining Cervical Cancer Screening (CCS) behavior among Iranian women. Our findings highlight a critical interplay of psychological factors: fear, response costs, and perceived vulnerability act as significant barriers, while response efficacy, self-efficacy, and protection motivation serve as key facilitators. These results provide a clear roadmap for developing targeted interventions. To enhance CCS uptake, healthcare providers and policymakers should design multi-component programs that address these specific constructs. Practically, interventions could include: (1) Fear-reduction campaigns that use patient testimonials and clear, non-threatening language to demystify the Pap smear process and normalize screening. (2) Cost-mitigation strategies, such as offering flexible appointment times, ensuring privacy in clinics to reduce emotional costs, and providing transparent information about any financial assistance available. (3) Self-efficacy enhancement through skills-based training, where health educators use role-playing or video demonstrations to help women build confidence in scheduling appointments and discussing screening with providers. By directly targeting these identified barriers and facilitators, such theory-based programs can move beyond simple awareness campaigns to effectively empower women and promote life-saving screening behaviors. Further longitudinal research is recommended to assess the long-term impact of these tailored interventions.
