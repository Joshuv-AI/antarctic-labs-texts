Spontaneous Production of H2O2 at the Liquid–Ice Interface: A Potential Source of Atmospheric Oxidants
Authors: Junwei Song, Laura Werner, Yoan Carreira Mendes Da Silva, Alexander Theis, D. James Donaldson, Christian George
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We present experimental evidence for the spontaneous production of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at the liquid–ice interface during the freezing of dilute salt solutions. Specifically, sample solutions containing NaCl, NaBr, NH4Cl, and NaI at concentrations between 10–6 and 10–1 M were subjected to freezing–melting cycles and then analyzed for H2O2 content. The relationship between the production rate of H2O2 and the salt concentration follows that of the Workman–Reynolds freezing potential (WRFP) values as a function of the salt concentration. Our results suggest that H2O2 is formed at the liquid–ice interface from the self-recombination of hydroxyl radicals (OH·), produced from the oxidation of hydroxide anions due to the high electric field generated at the aqueous–ice interface under the WRFP effect. Furthermore, the involvement of O2 likely acting as an electron capturer could promote to produce more OH radicals and hydroperoxyl radicals (HO2·), thus enhancing the production of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface. Overall, this study suggests a novel mechanism of H2O2 formation in ice via its spontaneous production at the liquid–ice interface, induced by the Workman–Reynolds effect.

Introduction

In the atmosphere, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) acts as a reservoir species of tropospheric hydroxyl radicals (OH·) and hydroperoxyl radicals (HO2·).1 The levels of H2O2 are closely linked with the atmospheric oxidizing capacity, which determines the lifetimes of many trace gas species.2 Previous studies have reported the detection of atmospheric H2O2 in various phases including gas,3,4 aqueous (e.g., cloud and rain droplets),5−7 and solid (e.g., snow and ice) phases.8−10

It is well-known that in the gas phase, H2O2 is mainly formed by a single pathway involving the recombination of two HO2 radicals.11,12 In the aqueous phase, H2O2 can also be produced by the disproportionation of HO2 radicals, which are predominantly present through gas phase scavenging.11 Several recent studies have reported the spontaneous formation of OH radicals and H2O2 at the air–water interface of microdroplets (1–20 μm in diameter),13−21 sparking a renewed research interest in atmospheric multiphase chemistry. Those studies hypothesized that the strong electric field present at the air–water interfaces promotes the charge separation of hydroxide anions (OH–) to OH radicals, which then recombine to form H2O2 (eqs 1 and 2).13,14 In addition to OH recombination, H2O2 could also be formed in the presence of oxygen (O2) via a more complex scheme (eqs 3–5). Specifically, the electron can be trapped by O2 resulting in O2–, and then, O2– will react with H+ to form HO2 radicals. Eventually, the produced HO2 radical can recombine to lead to the H2O2 formation.12345

Generally, H2O2 in snow and ice primarily originates from the scavenging of gas H2O2 formed in the atmosphere.11,22 A recent laboratory study reported the diffusive uptake of H2O2 into ice grain boundaries directly from the gas phase, which could serve as a source of H2O2 in ice and snow.23

Seventy years ago, Workman and Reynolds observed that during the freezing of dilute salt solutions, ions in the solution become preferentially separated at the ice–water interface.24 For example, Cl– is more preferentially accommodated in the ice crystal lattice upon freezing aqueous NaCl solutions, while Na+ is more likely to be rejected from the ice crystal lattice. This ion differentiation results in a Workman–Reynolds freezing potential (WRFP), an electrostatic potential difference that develops between the ice and the liquid phases during the process of freezing. The WRFP effect has been confirmed by both experimental observations24−28 and molecular dynamics simulations.29−32 The reported values of the WRFP range from few to several hundred volts, depending on salt concentration and type of ions involved in the solution as well as freezing rate.27,28,32 The WRFP generates a localized electric field at the liquid–ice interface, which could exert an electrostatic force that promotes the ionization of molecules or ions present in the solution. Similar to the charge separation processes occurring at the air–water interface of microdroplets, we propose that under the WRFP effect, OH– may undergo charge separation (i.e., oxidation) at the liquid–ice interface during the freezing of salt solutions, leading to the production of OH radicals (eq 1). Once produced, two OH radicals could subsequently combine to produce H2O2 (eq 2). To the best of our knowledge, this hypothesis regarding the formation of oxidants at the liquid–ice interface has not been investigated previously either through experimental studies or molecular dynamics simulations.

In this study, we conducted laboratory experiments to explore this hypothesis. First, we investigated whether OH radicals and H2O2 were spontaneously generated at the liquid–ice interface, induced by the WRFP effect through freezing aqueous bulk salt solutions. Additionally, we examined the effect of salt solution concentrations on H2O2 production during freezing. Finally, we performed a mechanistic investigation to assess the relative importance of different H2O2 production pathways. Our results provide strong evidence for the spontaneous production of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface induced by the Workman–Reynolds effect, suggesting a novel mechanism of H2O2 formation in ice.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were used for the experiments as received without any further purification: disodium terephthalate (Alfa Aesar, ≥99% purity), 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 97% purity), sodium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99% purity), sodium bromide (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99% purity), ammonium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99.99% purity), and sodium iodide (ACS reagent, ≥99.5% purity).

Experimental Procedures

One type of salt solution was freshly prepared using pure deionized water (Elga Purelab Classic, 18.2 MΩ cm) every day. The experiments were carried out at the natural pH of the salt solutions without any external regulation. The pH of the single salt solution used in our experiments was consistently close to 6.0 ± 0.5. For each day’s experiment, 4–5 samples were prepared simultaneously in 15 mL perfluoroalkoxy alkane (PFA) test tubes (BOLA, Germany). For each sample, the salt solution was poured into one PFA test tube and sealed with a cap. All PFA test tubes were thoroughly cleaned with 1 M NaOH and deionized water before being filled with salt solutions. Generally, each PFA test tube was filled with approximately 13 mL of salt solutions and leave a 2 mL headspace to prevent the tube from breaking during the subsequent freezing.

Three methods were used for freezing the sample solution. In the first method, the sample solution was slowly frozen by immersing the tube fully into an ethanol cooling bath, which was precooled to a temperature of −20 °C. With this method, the sample solution was fully frozen after approximately 10–20 min. In some experiments, a second freezing method was used. Specifically, a motor (Zaber Technologies Inc.) was used to control the vertical immersion of the tube into the ethanol cooling bath at a speed of ∼25 μm s–1, allowing the sample solution to freeze more slowly. Previous studies reported the maximum WRFP values for salt solution (e.g., NaCl) at the freezing rate of 25 μm s–1.25,27 With this method, the sample solution from bottom to top was fully frozen after approximately 50–60 min. In the third method, the tube was immersed fully in a liquid nitrogen bath, where the sample solution was fully frozen after 1–2 min. In this study, we define these three freezing methods as direct immersion into an ethanol bath (DI-ethanol bath), motor-controlled immersion into an ethanol bath (MI-ethanol bath), and liquid nitrogen bath (LQ-nitrogen bath), respectively. Regardless of the freezing methods, the PFA test tube was always withdrawn from the ethanol or liquid nitrogen cooling bath after the sample solution was fully frozen and then placed in a water bath at room temperature to melt. In our experiments, we fully froze the sample solutions without specifically monitoring the freezing rate. It is challenging to replicate each freezing cycle with an identical freezing time. Given that the amounts of oxidants formed during a single freezing cycle may be below the detection limit, our best strategy is to freeze the sample solution completely and then repeat the processes to create the freezing–melting cycles. All freezing–melting steps were conducted under room conditions.

Analytical Methods

Although oxidants were expected to be produced at the liquid–ice interface, this study lacked a strategy to directly observe interfacial processes or quantify the concentrations of oxidants at the interface in situ. Therefore, we measured the bulk concentrations of OH radicals and H2O2 formed in the liquid samples after they were subjected to varying numbers of freezing–melting cycles.

A chemical titration method was used to track the formation of aqueous OH radicals at the liquid–ice interface. Disodium terephthalate (TA) is a sensitive probe that reacts with OH radicals to produce a fluorescent product, namely, 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (TAOH), with a yield of 0.315.33 A fluorescence spectrometer (RF 6000, Shimadzu) was used to quantify the concentrations of TAOH in the samples containing the TA solution subjected to different numbers of freezing–melting cycles. The fluorescence spectrometer was set with 310 nm as the excitation wavelength, and the emission was observed with maximum intensity at ∼422 nm for TAOH. The speed rate of all fluorescence spectra was set at 200 nm/min, and the excitation/emission slit widths were set at 5 nm. The calibrations with TAOH standard were performed regularly (Figure S1), which was used for the TAOH quantification.15,18

The concentration of H2O2 produced in the liquid samples following varying numbers of freezing–melting cycles was quantified using a commercial H2O2 analyzer (AL2021, Aero-Laser), whose detection principle is based on a two channel dual-enzyme technique.34 In brief, in one channel, all peroxides including, H2O2 and organic peroxides, are detected by their liquid-phase reaction with p-hydroxyphenylacetic acid catalyzed by horseradish peroxidase. In the other channel, H2O2 is selectively destroyed by the addition of catalase. Hence, the difference in the signal between the two channels represents the concentration of H2O2. The instrument was calibrated daily using a freshly prepared 200 nM H2O2 standard to minimize uncertainties in the measurements. The linearity range of the H2O2 analyzer was characterized by performing the calibrations with H2O2 standard at low concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 nM (Figure S2). The detection limit of the H2O2 analyzer for liquid samples was estimated as ∼2.5 nM based on the measurement of fresh deionized water.

Results and Discussion

Confirmation of OH· and H2O2 Production at the Liquid–Ice Interface

We first investigated whether the production of OH radicals is occurring at the liquid–ice interface by freezing the samples containing TA solution. Figure 1a shows the example of fluorescence emission spectra for the samples containing 0.05 mM TA subjected to different freezing–melting cycles (n = 0–25) by using the DI-ethanol bath method. Note that TA itself is a weakly fluorescent compound, which shows the interferences on TAOH quantification especially when measuring TAOH mixed with high concentrations of TA (>0.1 mM).15,18,33 Thus, we used 0.05 mM TA as the sample solution to minimize this interference. It can be clearly seen in Figure 1a that TAOH at the fluorescence emission peak of 422 nm was observed in the samples that underwent freezing processes (n = 5–25) compared to the solutions of reference TAOH and bulk TA. However, no TAOH formation was observed in the unfrozen sample (n = 0), which was stored under dark room conditions for the same duration as the frozen samples. In our experiments, the frozen samples were only subjected to the freezing and melting without irradiation to minimize the effect of photolysis.35,36 Therefore, our observation confirms that TAOH and, hence, OH radicals were produced at the liquid–ice interface during the freezing of TA solutions.

(a) Example of fluorescence emission spectra of the samples containing 0.05 mM TA solution that subjected to different freezing–melting cycles (n = 0–25) compared to a freshly prepared TA sample and a reference TAOH. (b) Concentrations of TAOH produced in the samples as a function of the number of freezing–melting cycles by using different freezing methods of LQ-nitrogen bath (red), DI-ethanol bath (blue), and MI-ethanol bath (orange). Solid lines represent linear fits.

In our experiments, we adopted the DI-ethanol bath, MI-ethanol bath, and LQ-nitrogen bath methods to freeze the TA sample solutions. With all freezing methods, the production of TAOH always increased linearly with the number of freezing–melting cycles (r = 0.96–0.99, Figure 1b). The amount of TAOH produced in frozen samples was always corrected by subtracting that in the bulk TA solution. The slope of the linear fitting between TAOH concentrations in frozen samples and the number of freezing–melting cycles can be considered as the production rate of TAOH per freezing–melting cycle. The production rate of TAOH using the DI-ethanol bath method (0.069 ± 0.004 nM per freezing cycle) was higher than that using the LQ-nitrogen method (0.043 ± 0.002 nM per freezing cycle). In addition, a higher TAOH production rate was observed for the MI-ethanol bath method (0.098 ± 0.003 nM per freezing cycle). The production of OH radicals at the liquid–ice interface is likely associated with the freezing rate of sample solution or the ice formation mechanism. On the one hand, a slower freezing rate like DI- or MI-ethanol bath methods may provide more time for liquid–ice interfacial reactions, thereby perhaps enhancing the production of TAOH. On the other hand, during fast freezing like the LQ-nitrogen bath method, ice crystals propagate through the liquid forming many small pockets of liquid surrounded by ice, which increases the liquid–ice interface. We normalized the production rate of TAOH by different freezing methods to the time required for the sample solution to freeze completely. As mentioned in Section 2.2, it would take approximately 1–2, 15–20, and 50–60 min for the sample solution to fully freeze by the methods of LQ-nitrogen bath, DI-ethanol bath, and MI-ethanol bath, respectively. The normalized production rates of TAOH were much higher for the LQ-nitrogen bath (0.049–0.098 nM per freezing cycle per minute) compared to DI-ethanol bath method (0.003–0.005 nM per freezing cycle per minute). This indicates that the production of TAOH was faster by using the LQ-nitrogen bath method due to the fact that more liquid–ice interfaces were produced.

Next, we tested whether H2O2 is produced at the liquid–ice interface by freezing samples containing different salt solutions. Figure 2 shows examples for H2O2 concentrations measured in the samples containing either HPLC grade water or 0.2 mM salt solutions of NaCl, NH4Cl, or NaBr subjected to varying numbers of freezing–melting cycles by using the LQ-nitrogen bath method. The concentrations of H2O2 in frozen or unfrozen samples were subtracted from those measured in the bulk solution. It is evident that H2O2 is produced in the frozen samples compared to the unfrozen ones, suggesting that H2O2 is formed during the freezing of both pure water and salt solutions. Similar to the formation of OH radicals, the production of H2O2 linearly increased with the number of freezing–melting cycles of pure water and salt solutions, as well. In addition, we also carried out experiments using NaI solutions. However, no H2O2 was detected for the sample containing 0.2 mM NaI solutions, even though the sample was subjected to 20 or more freezing–melting cycles. This is consistent with our recent study showing a substantial decrease of H2O2 production from the air–water interface of microdroplets generated from NaI solutions compared to NaCl or NaBr solutions.15 Previous studies have shown that I– can react with OH radicals rapidly to form HOI– and atomic iodine.37,38 The reaction rate between I– and OH radicals is 1.0 × 1010 M–1 s–1,39 which is slightly higher than the reaction rate between Br– (4.2 × 109 M–1 s–1) and Cl– (2.8 × 109 M–1 s–1) with OH radicals.40 Since we observed the formation of H2O2 during the freezing of NaCl and NaBr solutions, we can exclude the possibility that H2O2 formation was completely suppressed due to the reaction between halide ions and OH radicals. Pruppacher et al.26 reported that the WRFP values for NaI were lower than those for NaCl and NaBr. Therefore, the production of OH radicals and H2O2 could be lower during the freezing of NaI compared to NaCl and NaBr. In addition, the iodide-catalyzed reaction (2H2O2(aq)→ 2H2O(aq) + O2(g)) could reduce the concentration of H2O2.41,42 In particular, given a higher concentration of NaI (0.2 mM) compared to H2O2 (likely a few nM during a single freezing cycle), this iodide-catalyzed reaction may explain why no H2O2 was detected in NaI solution after freezing. In addition, we used a 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube instead of the PFA test tube to perform the same experiment by using the DI-ethanol bath method. We also observed the concentrations of H2O2 in the samples containing NaCl solution linearly increased with the number of freezing–melting cycles (Figure S3), indicating the production of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface once again. Note that the production rate of H2O2 obtained using the centrifuge tube was lower than that from using the PFA tube. We attribute this difference to the different properties of the materials used to make the centrifuge and PFA tubes to some extent. The centrifuge tube is made of polypropylene, which has a higher reactivity toward OH radicals43 compared to the PFA test tube. As a result, OH radicals may be reduced by reaction with polypropylene in the centrifuge tube, leading to a decrease in H2O2 production. Recently, Eatoo and Mishra44 found that H2O2 could also be formed due to the physical contact of water microdroplets with solid metal surfaces (e.g., aluminum). We cannot exclude the possibility that H2O2 is produced by the liquid–solid interface due to the contact of sample solution with PFA tube surface in our experiments. Nevertheless, the linear increase of H2O2 production with the number of freezing–melting cycles is strongly indicative of H2O2 production attributable to the freezing of salt solutions in this study.

Examples for H2O2 concentrations detected in the samples containing either pure water (HPLC grade), NaCl, NH4Cl, or NaBr solutions that were subjected to different freezing–melting cycles by using the LQ-nitrogen bath method. Error bars represent the standard deviation of each sample measurement. Pink dashed lines show the measurement detection limit of the H2O2 analyzer (∼2.5 nM).

In the case of a 0.2 mM NaCl sample solution subjected to 20 freezing–melting cycles (Figure 2), the production of H2O2 was approximately 32 nM. However, a 0.2 mM NaBr sample solution that underwent 20 freezing–melting cycles produced more H2O2 with approximately 50 nM. Pruppacher et al.26 measured the WRFP values of different types of salt solutions including NaCl and NaBr at different concentrations. At the same salt concentration range of 0.1–1 mM, the WRFP values of NaBr were slightly lower than those of NaCl solution.26 However, bromide anions have lower oxidation potential compared to Cl– and so could undergo enhanced charge separation (Br– → Br·+ e–) at the liquid–ice interface. This may enhance the production of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface by formation mechanisms (eqs 3–5). This is also consistent with our recent study that Br– can act as an electron donor, contributing to the interfacial H2O2 formation at the air–water interface of microdroplets.15

Dependence of H2O2 Production on the Salt Concentrations

We further studied whether the production of H2O2 depends on the concentrations of the salt solution at the liquid–ice interface. These experiments were all carried out using the LQ-nitrogen bath method with sample solutions containing either NaCl, NaBr, or NH4Cl in the concentration range of 10–6–10–1 M. Figure 3 shows the production rate of H2O2 as a function of salt solution concentrations for NaCl, NaBr, or NH4Cl. The production rate of H2O2 is based on the slope of the linear fitting between H2O2 concentrations produced in frozen samples versus the number of freezing–melting cycles. In the case of NaCl, the production rate of H2O2 exhibits the maximum values (∼3 nM per freezing–melting cycle) at a salt concentration of around 10–4 M, while very low values (<1 nM per freezing–melting cycle) were observed at higher salt concentrations of 10–2–10–1 M. This dependence of H2O2 production rate on NaCl bulk concentrations is essentially the same as that of the WRFP values as a function of NaCl concentrations.25,26 Although the values of WRFP for salt solutions at different concentrations were not measured in this study, they were reported by several previous studies.25,26 Note that the WRFP values of salt solution reported by different studies were varied possibly due to different experiment setup. Based on the relationship of WRFP values and NaCl concentrations reported by Wilson et al.,25 we assumed the WRFP values for the different concentrations of NaCl solutions that used in this study (Figure S4). The maximum WRFP values for NaCl have been reported at a concentration of 10–4 M, as experimentally measured by several previous studies.24,25,28 At higher concentrations of NaCl (e.g., 10–2–10–1 M), the WRFP effect almost disappeared, implying no electric field present at the liquid–ice interface. The results displayed in Figure 3 strongly suggest that the production of H2O2 is induced by the WRFP effect at the liquid–ice interface. Note that even at lower salt concentrations or in pure water (Figure 2a), we still observed the formation of H2O2 during the freezing. The production rate of H2O2 during the freezing of pure water was ∼ 3 nM per freezing–melting cycle. Previous experimental studies have observed that diluted salt solutions (<10–4 M) exhibit lower WRFP values and suggest that the WRFP for even more diluted salt solutions (10–6 M) or pure water may be negligible or disappear.25,26 In contrast, one study found during the fast dendritic freezing of water droplets that no decrease toward zero potential for lower salt concentrations (e.g., NaCl) down to 10–6 M but remained near the maximum value.45 More recently, Shavlov et al.32 also pointed out that WRFP can be present during the freezing of pure distilled water. This was related to the capture of H+ and OH– by charge traps, which are the interstices of the ice lattice. In our study, the formation of H2O2 during the freezing of pure water is attributed to the presence of WRFP, likely resulting from the charge separation of OH– and H+. However, it is important to emphasize that the WRFP is badly characterized for pure water, compared to salt containing solutions; this is why we focus here on those conditions.

H2O2 production rate as a function of the salt concentrations of NaCl (blue dots), NaBr (yellow dots), and NH4Cl (red dots) by using the LQ-nitrogen bath method. Production rate of H2O2 was determined from the slopes of the linear fitting between the H2O2 concentration versus the number of freezing–melting cycles. Error bars represent the standard deviation of these slopes. Light blue dashed line represents the assumed WRFP values of NaCl solutions according to Wilson et al.25

Similar to NaCl solutions, the production rate of H2O2 for NaBr exhibited similar dependence behavior as a function of salt concentrations. This is consistent with that the WRFP values of NaCl and NaBr show similar dependence behavior as a function of salt concentrations reported in a previous study.26 It suggests the role of the charge separation induced by the WRFP effect for both NaCl and NaBr. In addition, we observed that within the salt concentration range of 10–6–10–3 M, the production rate of H2O2 was slightly higher for NaBr than NaCl. We also conducted experiments by freezing the solutions of 0.25 mM NaCl mixed with either 0.25 or 10 mM NaBr (Figure S5). The production rate of H2O2 was higher for the solution of 0.25 mM NaCl mixed with 0.25 mM NaBr compared 0.25 mM NaCl alone. This increase is likely attributed to the charge separation of Br– at the liquid–ice interface, which enhances the production of free electrons and consequently promoting H2O2 production. In contrast, the production rate of H2O2 was lower for the solution of 0.25 mM NaCl mixed with 10 mM NaBr compared to 0.25 mM NaCl alone. This reduction is due to the decrease in WRFP at higher salt concentrations.

Besides, the production rates of H2O2 for NH4Cl solutions in the concentration range of 10–4–10–2 M were slightly higher than those for NaCl solutions at the same concentrations. Previous studies have found that during the freezing of NH4Cl solution, ammonium ion (NH4+) is preferentially incorporated into the ice phase, while Cl– is rejected from the ice phase.25,27 Consequently, the ice is positive with respect to the liquid solution of NH4Cl during the freezing processes. Wilson and Haymet25 previously reported that the absolute maximum WRFP values for NH4Cl were slightly higher compared to NaCl, which likely accounts for the increased H2O2 production observed in the freezing of NH4Cl solutions.

Mechanistic Investigation of H2O2 Formation Pathways

Our experimental results provide strong evidence that the production of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface was induced by the WRFP effect. We further explored the pathways participating the production of H2O2 focusing on (1) the self-recombination of OH radicals and (2) the self-recombination of HO2 radicals that formed from the involvement of O2, as suggested in our recent study of air–water interfacial processes of microdroplets.15 To investigate the pathway of OH self-recombination, we froze the salt sample solutions with the addition of TA as an organic OH radical scavenger by using the LQ-nitrogen bath method. If H2O2 formation predominately formed by the pathway of OH radical self-recombination, we expected the reduced H2O2 production in the presence of TA. As shown in Figure 4a, 0.25 mM NH4Cl solutions mixed with varying concentrations of TA (0.001–0.1 mM) were subjected to various freezing–melting cycles. Unexpectedly, no significant reduction in H2O2 production was observed for the samples containing NH4Cl solutions mixed with TA compared with those without TA addition. Similarly, no significant changes in H2O2 production were observed for the samples containing 0.25 mM NaCl solutions mixed with TA compared to those without TA addition (Figure 4b). The concentrations of H2O2 for the samples containing salt solutions mixed with TA linearly increased with the number of freezing–melting cycles. We also observed that the fluorescence intensity of TAOH for the samples of salt solutions mixed with TA linearly increased with the number of freezing–melting cycles (Figure S6). For the samples containing 0.25 mM salt solutions (either NH4Cl, NaCl, or NaBr) with the addition of 0.05 mM TA, the production rate of TAOH was estimated to be 0.02–0.08 nM per freezing–melting cycle. Given the yield of TAOH from TA reacting with OH radicals (0.315), the formation rate of OH radicals was 0.06–0.25 nM per freezing–melting cycle. Notably, this rate is still significantly lower than the production rate of H2O2 with 1.5–2.5 nM per freezing–melting cycle. Previous studies have shown that the self-recombination of OH radicals (kOH·+OH· = 5.5 × 109 M–1 s–1)46 to form H2O2 was slightly faster than the reaction of TA with OH radicals (kOH·+TA = 4.1 × 109 M–1 s–1)36 to form TAOH. Given that TA concentration was much higher than OH radicals, the overall reaction rate of OH radical recombination (kOH·+OH·*[OH]*[OH]) is lower than the rate of TA reacting with OH radicals (kOH·+TA *[OH] *[OH]). However, the actual reaction rates between TA and OH radicals could be limited. On the one hand, similar to some aromatic compounds, e.g., benzene, anthracene, and pyrene,47−49 TA might undergo self-association or clustering due to the poor solvation at the interface. This could reduce the availability of the TA molecules for reaction with OH radicals at the liquid–ice interface. On the other hand, due to the cage effects,50 OH radicals can recombine faster with nearby solvated electron to create a null cycle (eq 1) or react with the another adjacent OH radical to produce H2O2 (eq 2). Thus, the overall rate of OH radical recombination is likely higher than that of the reaction between TA and OH radicals at the liquid–ice interface.

All samples were frozen by using the LQ-nitrogen bath method. Error bars represent the standard deviation of each sample measurement.

To investigate the pathway of self-recombination of HO2 radicals, we purged the sample tube containing NaCl solutions with different gases including nitrogen (N2), argon (Ar), and O2 for 1–2 min, prior to freezing them by using the DI-ethanol bath method. We usually prepared two sets of samples each day: one with the gas purging and another one without any treatment as the control. As mentioned in Section 2.2, a small headspace was always left in the PFA tube to prevent it from breaking during freezing. Thus, the sample solutions were not perfectly isolated from the room air. To minimize this effect on our results, we kept the same headspace in the PFA tube for all samples. All samples were subjected to 15 freezing–melting cycles to ensure sufficient H2O2 production for the detection. The concentration of H2O2 in the control and gas-purged frozen samples was corrected by subtracting the amount of H2O2 detected in the corresponding unfrozen samples. The production of H2O2 under different conditions is depicted in Figure 5, and detailed results for each experiment are listed in Table S1. It can be clearly seen that the average concentration of H2O2 showed a significant increase (p = 0.016) in the samples with O2 purging (N = 6) compared with the control samples (N = 8). This suggests that O2 can enhance the formation of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface during the freezing of NaCl solutions. On the one hand, dissolved O2 could be enriched at the liquid–ice interface,51,52 where it can shift the equilibrium of the reaction (eq 1) to the right. This shift could promote the formation of OH radicals, thus enhancing the production of H2O2 through the self-recombination of OH radicals. Furthermore, O2 reacts with the solvated electrons to form HO2 radicals at the liquid–ice interface. Subsequently, the self-recombination of the formed HO2 radicals promotes the production of H2O2 (eqs 3–5). In addition, dissolved O2 could be trapped in the ice crystal boundaries, which collocates with the solvent electrons to promote the formation of HO2 radicals. Our results are in line with previous studies15,19 that a significant increase of H2O2 production from the microdroplets in the presence of O2, attributing this behavior to the O2 adsorption at the air–water interface. Interestingly, the production of H2O2 was still observed in samples purged with argon or N2, but the average concentrations of H2O2 (p = 0.016) were significantly lower than those measured in the control samples. It suggests that the pathway of self-recombination of HO2 radicals was suppressed in samples purged with argon or N2. Note that in all samples, whether purged with gas or not, a headspace was left in the PFA tube, meaning some O2 remained in the PFA tube even with argon or N2 purging. Therefore, the pathway of self-recombination of HO2 radicals for H2O2 production was not completely deactivated during the freezing of sample solutions.

Finally, we roughly estimated the relative importance of the OH radical and HO2 radical self-recombination pathways in the production of H2O2. The production of H2O2 detected in samples with argon or N2 purging was on average 27.3 ± 12.8 nM, accounting for ∼50% of H2O2 detected in the control samples (54.8 ± 15.6 nM). Some of the O2 remained in the PFA tube headspace, even in samples purged with argon or N2. However, it is reasonable to assume that the contribution of headspace O2-involved production pathways is the same for all samples. Thus, in control samples, almost half of the H2O2 production could be attributed to the self-recombination of OH radicals. Besides, the production of H2O2 in samples with O2 purging was 77.0 ± 18.6 nM, showing an enhancement of ∼40% compared to that in the control samples. These results demonstrate that pathways of both OH and HO2 radical self-recombination are important for the formation of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface.

Environmental Implication

In this study, we present experimental evidence that OH radicals and H2O2 are spontaneously formed at the liquid–ice interface due to the WRFP effect. This interfacial process occurs during the freezing of dilute salt solutions and pure water without the addition of catalysts, oxidants, or irradiation. We observed that the production rate of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface of dilute salt solutions was dependent on salt concentrations, with a maximum of 3–4 nM per freezing–melting cycles observed at a salt concentration of ∼10–4 M. The salt concentrations (e.g., NaCl) used in this study fall within the range (10–7–10–2 M) generally observed in snow and ice under real atmospheric conditions.53−55 The production rate of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface is 2 orders of magnitude lower than compared to that formed from the air–water interface of salt containing microdroplets.15 This difference is expected because of the higher electric field strength at the air–water interface of microdroplet compared to the liquid–ice interface. A recent laboratory study estimated that freshly fallen snow, with a surface area of 59 cm2 per cm3, could yield 2–7 × 1012 molecules of H2O2 adsorbed per cm3 of snow at −10 to −30 °C, corresponding to approximately 0.5–2 nM H2O2.23 Thus, the production pathway of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface could contribute significantly to H2O2 present in ice, probably comparable to the gaseous H2O2 uptake pathway. The spontaneous production of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface represents another example of redox chemistry induced by WRFP at the liquid–ice interface. In the presence of solar irradiation, this interfacial H2O2 formation could further act as a source of OH radicals, which could contribute to the degradation of other compounds in snow and ice. Further research is needed to assess the environmental significance of H2O2 production at the liquid–ice interface, such as mimicking the formation processes of snow and ice in the real atmosphere such as freezing microdroplets directly under both dark and solar irradiation. From the larger liquid–ice interface of microdroplets, there may be higher production rates of H2O2 per volume. Additionally, our observations indicate that the presence of O2 can enhance the production rate of H2O2 at the liquid–ice interface. However, other factors such as pH and the presence of organics or metal ions may also affect the production rate of H2O2 and warrant further investigation.
