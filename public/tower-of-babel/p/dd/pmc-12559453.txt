Na2Fe3(SO4)4: A Zero‐Strain Sustainable Positive Electrode Material for Na‐Ion Batteries
Authors: Anastasia Grebenshchikova, Jacob Olchowka, Loïc Simonin, Sergey Yaroslavtsev, Mathieu Duttine, François Fauth, Lorenzo Stievano, Christian Masquelier, Laurence Croguennec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the challenges in the energy transition is minimization of the battery cost for energy grid storage. Na‐ion batteries are a promising alternative to Li‐based analogues thanks to low cost, abundance of constituents, and an already set‐up industry. However, already commercialized positive electrode materials for Na‐ion batteries (Na3V2(PO4)2F3 and NaxCu1‐y‐zFeyMnzO2) contain critical raw elements such as V, Cu, and Mn, boosting the research towards abundant Fe‐based materials. In this work, we report a novel sodium iron sulfate phase, Na2Fe3(SO4)4 (NFS), synthesized by ball milling, as a positive electrode material. This new compound crystallizes in the orthorhombic Pbca space group with cell parameters a = 9.682(1) Å, b = 8.739(1) Å, c = 29.300(4) Å, and V = 2479.0(5) Å3. Tests of thick NFS positive electrodes (18 mg cm−2) in Na‐half coin cells delivered 62 mAh g−1 at C/30 (corresponding to the exchange of 2 e‐) or 69% of the theoretical capacity (90 mAh g−1), with a good capacity retention of 47 mAh g−1 at a high discharge rate 2C. Operando X‐ray diffraction (XRD) showed minimal (<1%) changes in lattice parameters during cycling. Dominantly octahedra coordinated iron atoms are oxidized/reduced, as confirmed by operando synchrotron Mössbauer spectroscopy.Keywords: Ball milling syntheses, Iron‐based sulfates, Positive electrode materials, Sodium‐ion battery

Introduction

Sodium‐ion batteries (SIBs) do not aim to replace but to be an efficient alternative to already widespread lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) for plenty of applications, such as portable devices, electric or hybrid vehicles, and renewable energy storage. One important goal of new battery technologies is to support a smooth and economically profitable energy transition to reduce the use of fossil fuels. The second goal is to develop technologies based on elements such as Na, which is abundant and not critical due to unlimited localizations and/or political independencies. These new technologies will allow us to relax the pressure on the LIBs, technology and to promote its preferential utilization for applications requiring large energy density, such as electric vehicles. The abundance of Na and its low price promote the development of a large panel of positive electrode materials for SIBs: layered oxides, polyanionic materials, and Prussian Blue analogues. Layered oxides are outstanding in terms of capacity and energy density but are still facing issues such as: i) compositions still containing Ni and facing Na‐deficiency versus the electrochemically active transition metal (Na/TM < 1, TM being the transition metals),[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] ii) optimized syntheses to perfectly control their composition, structure, and morphology,[ 1 , 5 , 6 ] iii) fast reactivity versus moisture or air,[ 7 , 8 ] and iv) short lifetime and bad performance at high cycling rates.[ 9 , 10 ] A lot of efforts are still concentrated on layered oxides in order to successfully integrate them in high‐energy and long‐life SIBs and on Prussian Blue Analogues that are very interesting in terms of price‐to‐performance ratio. For the latter compounds, optimized synthetic routes still have to be developed to control the stoichiometry in Na and transition metal as well as the water content and also to optimize the tap density of these powders.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] Finally, as they are based on cyano anionic groups, their stability remains the main concern to be addressed before their introduction in commercial SIBs.[ 14 ]

Polyanionic TM compounds (phosphates, sulfates, silicates, etc.) are very attractive positive electrode materials thanks to their superior electrochemical cycling stability (a consequence of strong covalent E─O bonds, E═P, S, Si, etc.)[ 15 , 16 ] and to the existence of already available upscale synthesis methods. Among them, Na3V2(PO4)2F3 and Na3V2(PO4)3 are deeply investigated.[ 17 , 18 ] Nevertheless, widely used transition metals such as V, Ni, and Cu are already classified as critical raw materials by the European Union. In 2023 Mn and P, considered less critical until that date, were also added to the list.[ 19 ] Such demands from regulating initiatives are highly motivating for the discovery of new abundant sulfate‐containing phases based on sustainable transition elements such as Fe. Iron sulfate‐based phases are known to adopt different structural types; for example Li2Fe(SO4)2 [ 20 , 21 , 22 ] crystallizes in monoclinic (marinite) and/or orthorhombic symmetries, and both sodium and lithium iron sulfate fluorides, LiFeSO4F[ 23 , 24 , 25 ] and NaFeSO4F,[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] can exist in both tavorite and triplite structural types. Alluaudite Na2Fe2(SO4)3 is regarded as one of the most promising positive electrode materials for SIBs due to one of the highest voltage values reported for the Fe3+/Fe2+ redox process, i.e., 3.8 V versus Na+/Na. Alluaudite‐type sodium iron sulfate compounds, including off‐stoichiometric phases, are usually obtained by solid‐state, ionothermal, or aqueous synthesis methods, including heating of hydrated sodium iron sulfates.[ 16 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ] The simplest synthesis procedure includes a reaction between FeSO4 and Na2SO4 in molar ratio ≈ 2:1. In order to expand the knowledge on phases, which could be obtained by use of the same precursors, in this work we report on a new Fe‐based sodium sulfate, Na2Fe3(SO4)4 (NFS), obtained from FeSO4 and Na2SO4 in a molar ratio 3:1. This discovery was inspired by the work of Ben Yahia on Na2Mn3(SO4)4 [ 35 ] (NMS) and by a theoretical study reporting that, despite no electrochemical activity reported for NMS, the iron‐based polymorph with a structure described in the Cmc21 space group should be electrochemically active with an average voltage for Na+ extraction at about 4.0 V versus Na+/Na.[ 36 ]

The sodium‐based manganese sulfate NMS adopts 2 polymorphs,[ 35 , 37 ] both structures described in orthorhombic symmetry, one in the Cmc21 space group and the other one in the Pbca space group. In both structures the building block [Mn3(SO4)4]2− includes a dimer Mn2O11 of two corner‐sharing octahedra and one MnO5 distorted trigonal bipyramid. SO4 tetrahedra connect these blocks by sharing corner oxygens in order to form a layer, as presented in Figure 1. The polymorphism of these structures could be further expanded, as suggested by the theoretical calculations of Aksenov et al., with a new hypothetical compound of the same composition crystallizing in the Pmcn space group.[ 38 ] The diffusion pathways for the Cmc21 form were estimated using a bond valence sum (BVS) approach[ 35 ] and, depending on the valence mismatch (|∆V|) values chosen, Na+ ions are expected to migrate in one‐dimensional pathways along the c‐axis (0.51 v.u. ≤ |∆V| ≤ 0.6 v.u.), along two‐dimensional pathways parallel to the ac plane (0.6 v.u. ≤ |∆V| ≤ 0.65 v.u.), or along 3D pathways for |∆V| > 0.65 v.u. The Cmc21 polymorph was obtained at low temperature by sequential annealing steps at 300°, 400°, and 450 °C with intermediate grinding steps, whereas the Pbca form was stabilized by annealing at a higher temperature, at 600 °C for 12 h under argon flow.[ 37 ] For both polymorphs no electrochemical activity was reported. As just discussed, Na+ ion diffusion would be more favorable along the c‐axis in the Cmc21 polymorph, and ab initio DFT calculations have predicted a high voltage of 4.48 V versus Na+/Na for the Mn3+/Mn2+ redox couple.

The difference between the two structures comes from the orientation of the Mn2O11 dimers. 3D representations are available as GIF files in the Supporting Information (GIF G1 and GIF G2).

Metastable Fe2+‐rich sulfate‐based phases, such as orthorhombic Li2Fe(SO4)2,[ 20 , 21 ] triplite LiFeSO4F,[ 39 ] and layered LiFeSO4OH,[ 40 , 41 ] have been stabilized by ball milling, which is recognized as an interesting route to explore synthetic ways of new inorganic compounds due to local and very fast heating as well as rapid dissipation of the energy within the particles. For the solid‐state synthesis of sulfates, annealing temperature is often limited to 500°–600 °C at most, due to the relatively low decomposition temperature of the SO4 2− group.[ 16 , 26 , 29 ] The synthesis duration has thus to be increased because of slow diffusion within the solid at low temperature, which promotes the formation of the most thermodynamically stable phases. On the other hand, metastable polymorphs could be obtained by ball milling due to synergy between the decrease of the particle size and the induction of strains and microstructural defects. Furthermore, the absence of solvent is both a synthetical and economical advantage of the ball milling process versus iono‐/solvothermal synthesis methods.

In this work, we report for the first time on a new phase of Na2Fe3(SO4)4, which was synthesized by ball milling. Its synthesis and crystal structure are discussed in detail. The long‐range structure as well as the Fe2+ local environments in the pristine phase are characterized using X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. The newly synthesized phase shows reversible electrochemical activity as a positive electrode material versus Na metal, as also supported by operando synchrotron XRD and operando Mössbauer spectroscopy experiments, performed upon cycling to follow structural and redox evolution.

Results and Discussions

Synthesis and Phase Identification

In order to obtain the targeted Fe2+‐rich compound NFS, FeSO4·0.08H2O, and Na2SO4 were mixed in an Ar‐filled glove box, first in the stoichiometric molar ratio 3:1, before being ball milled. The hermetic jar used in the planetary micro mill PULVERISETTE allows for the prevention of exposure to air of the mixture during operation. The cycle consists of 10 min of milling at a speed of 750 rpm and 5 min of rest for heat dissipation. The recovered powder was slightly pink. The XRD pattern of pristine NFS, shown in Figure 2, reveals the residual presence of the FeSO4 precursor (Cmcm space group, ICSD #23507) next to the main phase, identified to be a new sodiated iron sulfate phase, isostructural to the Mn‐based NMS polymorph (Pbca space group). In order to check if the duration of the synthesis affects the completeness of the reaction, the just‐mentioned product was ground in a mortar, again hermetically sealed in a jar under argon in the glove box for the 2nd step of ball milling in similar conditions to those just described. The XRD pattern of this new sample revealed an additional peak at 11.4°2θ, identified as the most intense diffraction line of the FeSO4 Pbnm polymorph's diffraction pattern (ICSD #167412). Note that the Pbnm form of FeSO4 is known as the “high‐temperature” polymorph, which can be obtained by dehydration of FeSO4·7H2O at temperatures higher than 350 °C (it could be obtained pure at 450 °C).[ 42 ] This is the first time that this polymorph of FeSO4 has been obtained at room temperature, showing that ball milling may represent an interesting alternative route for low‐temperature syntheses of high‐temperature phases. Thus, increasing the synthesis duration of the stoichiometric precursors’ ratio does not eliminate the presence of the crystalline FeSO4 precursor but favors its structural transformation.

Comparison of XRD patterns of samples, obtained after the 1st (violet pattern) and the 2nd (red pattern) steps of ball milling of stoichiometric (FeSO4·0.08H2O:Na2SO4 = 3:1) and different ratios of precursors (10% and 20% of Na2SO4 excess correspond to green and blue patterns, respectively). Only 20 mol% of Na2SO4 excess allows us to avoid the presence of crystalline impurity of the precursor FeSO4 (Cmcm polymorph, ICSD #23507) marked as olive rectangular. FeSO4 Cmcm polymorph remains in the stoichiometric mixture after the 1st step (100 cycles), while after the 2nd step (100 + 100 cycles), the FeSO4 Pbnm polymorph (ICSD #167412) is identified as an impurity (pink rectangle).

We conducted alternative syntheses by varying the molar ratio of precursors with 10 and 20 mol% excess of Na2SO4. As shown in Figure 2, 20 mol% excess of Na2SO4 allows the formation of NFS without detectable crystalline impurities. Note that Figures S1a,b are given in supplementary information in order to support the identification of the phases: the new NFS phase, the residue of the precursor, and the impurities formed. The excess of Na2SO4 (20 mol%) would remain in the sample, despite not being detected by XRD, and would thus be present as an amorphous phase, whose total weight is limited to ∼5 wt% of the total mass.

Figure S1c shows that annealing of the obtained NFS at 200 °C for 10 h in argon flow in order to enhance the crystallinity of NFS leads to the formation of the alluaudite phase Na2Fe2(SO4)3. Fe2+‐based compounds are commonly easily oxidized in air, while the presence of sulfate groups can additionally promote decomposition of the phase exposed to air moisture because of the formation of more stable hydrated phases. Figure S1d compares the XRD patterns of pristine NFS with that stored in air for 1 day and illustrates that only a very little amount of the pristine phase remains, whereas the main phases are FeSO4·H2O, Na2Fe(SO4)2·2H2O (kröhnkite[ 43 ]), and Na2Fe(SO4)2·4H2O (bloedite[ 44 ]).

The inductively coupled plasma‐optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‐OES) analyses of the pure (according to XRD analysis) NFS sample, obtained with a 20 mol% excess of Na2SO4, were conducted to investigate its elemental composition. As reported in detail in Table S1, these analyses are in good agreement with that expected from the precursors’ mixture, i.e., Na:Fe:S≈2.4:3:≈4.2. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images shown in Figure 3 reveal the submicronic size of the primary particles, without specific morphology and assembled into big aggregates, typical for powders obtained by ball milling.[ 20 ]

Structure Determination of Na2Fe3(SO4)4

As illustrated in Figure 4, all the peaks in the XRD pattern of the NFS sample obtained with 20 mol% excess of Na2SO4 (next characterizations and results will refer to this sample) could be indexed in the orthorhombic centrosymmetric space group Pbca (Z = 8), considering a unit cell similar to that reported by Ben Yahia for NMS.[ 35 ] The refined cell parameters for the Fe2+ NFS phase are a = 9.682(1) Å, b = 8.739(1) Å, c = 29.300(4) Å, and V/Z = 309.9(5) Å3, smaller than those reported for the Mn2+‐based phase (a = 9.8313(4) Å, b = 8.7467(3) Å, c = 29.6004(11) Å, and V/Z = 318.2(2) Å3). This shrinkage of the unit cell is expected and may be explained by the difference in ionic radii between high‐spin Mn2+ and high‐spin Fe2+ in octahedral coordination, i.e., 0.83 and 0.78 Å, respectively.[ 45 ] The initial structural model for the new NFS phase is taken for NMS reported by Ben Yahia,[ 35 ] with 25 general 8c positions: 2 for the Na sites, 3 for the Fe sites, 4 for the S sites, and 16 for the O sites. Due to the big number of parameters (>100) to be refined, S─O distances of the SO4 anionic groups were constrained to remain in the range of 1.42–1.49 Å (the average S─O bond length being of 1.473 Å[ 46 ]).

a) Rietveld refinement of the NFS XRD pattern. Iron coordination in the NFS structure and corresponding distances: b) in the Fe2O11 dimers (with FeO6 octahedra) and c) in and between the FeO5 pentahedra.

The isotropic atomic displacement parameters U iso of Fe were found to be around 0.0114 Å2 (Biso = 0.9 Å2) at the early stages of refinements and were subsequently fixed, while for S and O, the values were fixed after being refined at 0.0082 Å2 (Biso = 0.65 Å2) and 0.0155 Å2 (Biso = 1.2 Å2), respectively. Occupancies of both Na and Fe sites were first refined and were found to be close to 1, which allowed us to fix them. The result of the structural refinement is illustrated in Figure 4a and exhibits good agreement between the observed and calculated profiles with low values of reliability factors. Refined cell and atomic parameters are reported in Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Two iron atoms (Fe1 and Fe2) occupy octahedral sites, sharing the oxygen O9 and belonging thus to a dimer as shown in Figure 4b and in Table S4. The Fe1─O9 and Fe2─O9 distances are extremely long (2.40(5) and 2.60(5) Å, respectively) in comparison with the average distance of 2.147 Å expected for the Fe2+─O2− bond[ 47 ] (CN = 6) and with the average Fe─O distances in Fe1O6 and Fe2O6 (2.17(5) and 2.19(5) Å, respectively). Note that the BVS values determined for Fe1 and Fe2 with consideration of this O9 atom are in very good agreement with divalent iron as of 1.99(9) and 1.93(9), respectively.[ 48 , 49 ] The occurrence of the Fe2O11 dimer is very similar to that found for NMS, whose structure was solved based on single crystal diffraction data. Longer than usual distances are also observed for Mn1─O9 and Mn2─O9 with 2.36(1) and 2.56(2) Å, respectively,[ 35 ] while an average Mn2+─O2− distance is estimated around 2.2 Å.[ 47 ] These elongated distances along the dimer M2O11 allow to minimize the strong electrostatic repulsion between the divalent Fe1 and Fe2 cations. The first Fe3 coordination sphere could be considered as a distorted trigonal bipyramid with five oxygen atoms with distances ranging from 2.08(4) to 2.14(4) Å, while the 6th Fe3─O distance with O6 is 2.78(3) Å, i.e., very long, as the mean distance for five coordinated oxygen atoms is 2.097 Å.

Each S atom is surrounded by four O atoms, forming SO4 tetrahedra with S─O distances varying from 1.41(4) to 1.49(4) Å, with the average distance for the S─O bond around 1.473 Å, as reported.[ 46 ] S─O distances in S1O4 and S4O4 tetrahedra are slightly shorter than in S2O4 and S3O4, as already observed for the Mn‐based compound. BVS values for S1, S2, S3, and S4 are in the range of 6.1(5)–6.7(5). The structure has two distinct Na sites, Na1 and Na2. The Na1 site is coordinated by seven oxygen atoms at an average distance of 2.55 Å. The Na2 site is coordinated by five oxygen atoms at distances in the range of 2.29(4)–2.71(5) Å and by three oxygen atoms, O6, O10, and O15, at 2.89(5), 2.86(5), and 2.97(6) Å, respectively. With consideration of the last three, the BVS value of Na2 is 0.82(4), while it is only 0.74(4) for the five closer oxygens. Na diffusion pathways, calculated by bond valence energy landscape (BVEL) and represented by violet isosurfaces at energy −3.4 eV, are presented in the GIF G3 (Supporting Information), which illustrates the 3D character of the ionic diffusion.

Based on the structural determination for the new NFS phase, we were then able to analyze in‐depth all the samples mentioned in the first part of this paper. The cell parameters determined for NFS and the weight contents for both Cmcm and Pbnm polymorphs of FeSO4 are given in Table S5. It reveals that the content of NFS exceeds 80 wt% in all synthesized samples with always the same cell parameters, suggesting that the NFS phase has a constant composition, independently of the synthesis conditions discussed in this paper.

Electrochemical Properties

NFS was ball milled with Ketjen Black to be used for further electrochemical characterization, and no structural changes were observed during ball milling with the carbon additive (as presented in Tables S2, S5, and S6). NFS was tested as a positive electrode material in an Na‐half cell at 25 °C, in different potential windows, at a C/30 rate with C being 89.7 mA g−1. For three cells, different upper cut‐off voltage were tested: 4.3, 4.5, and 4.8 V, as presented in Figure 5.

a) Voltage‐composition profiles of NFS//Na half‐cells cycled at a C/30 rate in different potential windows with increasing cut‐off voltages. b) Performance obtained at different discharge rates for NFS//Na half‐cells (each charge was performed at C/30, the first charge being plotted in a dashed line). c) Capacity retention obtained at different cycling rates. Arrows correspond to discharge profiles presented in (b). d) dQ/dV differential capacity curves of the first five cycles obtained for an NFS//Na half‐cell in the voltage range of 2.2 to 4.5 V.

As NFS is a Fe2+‐based material, the cell was started upon charging up to 4.3 V, delivering 55 mAh g−1, and after being discharged to 2.4 V, only 48 mAh g−1 was recovered, which corresponds to the oxidation of 1.06 mol of Fe2+ (Figure 5a). A higher cut‐off voltage of 4.5 V versus Na+/Na slightly increased the discharge capacity up to 60 mAh g−1, delivered at 2.4 V, suggesting that oxidation/reduction of Fe continues in the 4.3–4.5 V voltage region. Thus, the cell was discharged further to 2.2 V with a slight gain of the discharge capacity. A further increase of the discharge capacity up to 65 mAh g−1 is observed when the cut‐off voltage is increased to 4.8 V, accompanied by electrolyte degradation, as could be seen from the charge curve. Despite the biggest value of the discharge capacity, obtained with a cut‐off voltage of 4.8 V, the preferred one is 4.5 V.

Figure 5b compares charge and discharge profiles obtained in the 2.2–4.5 V potential window at different C‐rates in discharge (from C/30 to 2C), whereas all charges were performed at C/30. NFS electrodes with a loading of 18 mg cm−2 delivered 62 mAh g−1 at a cycling rate of C/30, which corresponds to 69% of the theoretical capacity and to the oxidation of 1.38 mol of Fe2+, at an average voltage of 3.34 V versus Na+/Na, which is less than the 3.8 V reported for alluaudite Na2Fe2(SO4)3.[ 16 ] Despite this high mass loading, a capacity retention of 76% versus the first cycle performed at C/30 was maintained at a high discharge rate of 2 C (i.e., 47 mAh g−1), and then the initial capacity delivered at C/30 was restored, as presented in Figure 5c. The voltage profiles demonstrate a continuous evolution upon Na deintercalation and reintercalation, suggesting a solid solution mechanism with no phase transitions upon electrochemical cycling. The first charge voltage profile (dashed curve in Figure 5b) differs from the subsequent charges, as highlighted from the differential galvanostatic data plotted in Figure 5d. Indeed, broad peaks of low intensity around 4.2 V in charge and 3.65 V in discharge are not visible for the following cycles, suggesting that some irreversible structural changes happen after the 1st cycle. For the next cycles, three broad peaks are noticeable, with the main oxidation/reduction processes occurring at 3.9 and 3.3 V during charge and discharge, respectively. The appearance of the peak around 4.35 V would indicate simultaneous oxidation of Fe2+ in competition with the electrolyte degradation, as already discussed in Figure 5a. Note that these preliminary results were obtained with a high loading for the electrodes (>11 mg cm−2), prepared in dry mode with PTFE as the binder. Despite a reversible discharge capacity limited to 62 mAh g−1 (for a discharge down to 2.2 V versus Na+/Na), these results are promising with consideration that they are obtained for a new phase, never reported until now, and fully sustainable as made of 100% abundant elements, i.e., Na, Fe, S, and O. Without any doubt, powder engineering toward, for example, conformal carbon coating, as well as an optimization of the electrode formulation, are routes (out of the scope of this paper) to prospect in the future to improve the electrochemical performance of NFS.

It is worth comparing these promising results obtained for NFS versus the absence of electrochemical activity reported for NMS.[ 35 , 37 ] This marked contrast may derive from: i) a significant difference in the potential of the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox couple versus that of the Fe2+/Fe3+, which could be incompatible with the current electrolyte available for Na‐ion batteries and the potential windows explored; ii) the inability of Mn3+ ion, a typical Jahn–Teller ion, to be stabilized in the structures of the two polymorphs of Na2‐xMn3(SO4)4 (Cmc21 and Pbca), and thus the impossibility of activating the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox couple.

Structural and Redox Mechanisms Involved upon Cycling

Operando synchrotron X‐ray diffraction (SXRD) was used to follow the structural evolution of NFS during the first charge and discharge, and the corresponding SXRD patterns are shown in Figure 6. The modified coin cell allows operando experiments and was cycled at a cycling rate of C/30 up to 4.3 V: the cut‐off voltage was decreased in order to inhibit electrolyte degradation accompanied by possible beam‐damage, and the lower cut‐off voltage was set to 2.1 V. The open circuit voltage (OCV) of the cell after assembling was 2.88 V, and the discharge capacity delivered in these conditions was 51 mAh g−1. The relatively strong background intensity observed for all the SXRD patterns comes from the electrolyte and the glass windows of the operando‐modified coin cell. Furthermore, several unidentified diffraction peaks are observed, including one sharp peak at around 8.04°2θ, which is most probably due to inner reflections coming from the cell, and a broad unidentified peak at 10.65°2θ. We believe these peaks are not associated with NFS crystal structure changes, as they were not observed for ex situ samples, as shown further. The indexation of these new peaks to check for possible symmetry lowering and structural transformation(s) was not successful. Finally, only very small shifts and changes in intensities of the main diffraction peaks hkl associated to the NFS phase are observed: 111 at 5.61°2θ, 112 at 5.99°2θ, 122 at 9.25°2θ, 214 at 9.7°2θ, and 312 at 12.0°2θ. The lattice parameters decrease during Na+ extraction, as expected from the oxidation of Fe2+ into Fe3+ for charge compensation, the reverse being observed during intercalation (Table 1). These changes are, however, very limited: the a, b, and c cell parameters vary by 0.3%, 0.2%, and 0.3%, respectively, corresponding to a volume contraction during this first charge of less than 1%, which could allow us to consider this material as “zero‐volume change” material.[ 50 , 51 ]

Parallel to this operando study, a series of ex situ experiments were performed in order to support the global picture of the phase diagram observed operando upon charge and discharge and to check for any possible interaction and deterioration of the material under the high brilliance X‐ray beam. These results are available in supplementary information as Figures S2, S3, and S4; Table S7 and are in very good agreement with those reported in Figure 6 and Table 1. As these results appear at first view surprising, and since the limited crystallinity of the sample did not allow precise Rietveld refinements, simulations of XRD patterns were performed in order to check for the impact of Na+ deintercalation on the relative intensities of the XRD peaks, either by emptying the Na1 site, the Na2 site, or both, as presented in Figure S5. It appears clearly that only subtle changes in intensities could be observed, difficult to be detected for patterns such as those reported in Figure 6 or Figure S4. These small changes in lattice parameters and peak intensities would indirectly support the tilting of polyhedra to compensate for changes in Fe─O distances and structural volume due to sodium deintercalation.

In parallel to the structural modifications involved upon Na+ extraction and reintercalation, the associated redox processes were studied by operando 57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy in order to follow changes in the oxidation state and local environment of Fe atoms. The electrode with NFS as active material (loading of 60 mg cm−2) was charged up to 4.5 V versus Na+/Na at a C/30‐rate, and the cell was held for 35 min at 4.5 V. High loading of the electrode is necessary to ensure a high Mössbauer effect, as a non‐enriched sample was used. The OCV of the cell before cycling was 2.93 V, and the charge capacity in these conditions was 80 mAh g−1. After, the cell was discharged at the same C‐rate of C/30 to 2.1 V with a delivered discharge capacity of 58.9 mAh g−1, in good agreement with conventional coin cells. Comparison of the electrochemical curves, obtained for NFS during the operando Mössbauer experiment and in conventional coin cells at the same C/30 rate, is presented in Figure S6. A higher cut‐off voltage was used for the operando Mössbauer experiment versus that for the operando XRD (4.5 V versus 4.3 V, respectively) in order to get similar charge capacity for both despite different polarizations within the in situ cells, as compared and discussed in more detail in Figure S6.

The evolution of the Mössbauer spectra observed operando during the first charge and discharge is presented in Figure 7 and revealed a slight modification in line shape and relative intensities of the two components of the relatively broad Fe(II) quadrupole doublet of pristine NFS. Such changes can be interpreted with the growth, upon oxidation, of a new Fe(III) component at about the same position of the line at low velocities of the pristine Fe(II) doublet.

To check precisely the evolution of iron components, five spectra, presented in Figure 7 on the right, were selected at the same states of charge and discharge as ex situ samples, used for XRD. The 5th spectrum was chosen at a discharged state to correspond to the operando XRD pattern, collected at 2.2 V. The whole series of the spectra can be fitted simultaneously with the three main components (corresponding hyperfine parameters reported in Table 2): two main quadrupole Fe2+ doublets (components A and B), largely predominant in the pristine as well as in the fully discharged state, and a third quadrupole Fe3+ doublet (component C) that grows during charge, reaches its maximum intensity at the end of the process, and gradually disappears during the following discharge. The component C with the lowest isomer shift (δ = 0.52(1) mm s−1) reveals the presence of a minor amount (2.9 at%) of Fe3+ in the pristine sample,[ 52 ] this amount is overestimated due to the usual overestimation of ferrous contribution in phases with the simultaneous presence of Fe2+ and Fe3+ species.[ 53 ] Component B with isomer shift 1.18(1) mm s−1 is attributed to Fe2+ ions in a trigonal bipyramid environment (i.e., located at the crystallographic site Fe3, as discussed before in the structural description of NFS), as a lower number of coordinating oxygen atoms (five for pentahedral versus six for an octahedral environment) induces a smaller value of the isomer shift parameter, which is also influenced by the increase of the Fe─O bond covalency combined with a decrease of Fe─O distances.[ 52 ] Finally, the last component, A, a doublet centered at 1.31(1) mm s−1 is attributed to Fe2+ ions in octahedral environments, i.e., to the sites Fe1 and Fe2 belonging to the Fe2O11 dimer.[ 52 ] As discussed previously, both octahedral environments are very similar, and it is thus impossible to distinguish between them by introducing two independent doublets due to the strong correlations between their hyperfine parameters. Note that the distribution between components A and B was constrained to the ratio 2:1, as expected from the structural description of NFS, and this ratio was shown to give the best description of the experimental spectra. The larger resonance line width for component B may reflect a distribution of Fe2+ local environments, i.e., slight variations in the trigonal bipyramid (Fe3) site distortion and in the quadrupole splitting parameter value.

One should note also that if only two components are considered for the treatment of Synchrotron Mössbauer Source (SMS) data, hyperfine parameters vary and suspicious correlations take place, ensuring use of three components. Alternative data analysis using only two components was considered for the treatment of SMS data but did not lead to satisfactory fits. Moreover, the analysis of the pristine Mössbauer spectrum recorded with a conventional lab‐made spectrometer (with a higher signal‐to‐noise ratio), presented in Figure S7, supports the use of three components, as it could not be fitted nicely with only two quadrupole doublets.

All fits have normalized χ2 close to one, proving the adequacy of the applied model, which means no changes in local environments of Fe ions. Such behavior is very interesting for a battery material, as it minimizes mechanical stress at the electrode level and thus decreases the risks of material degradation with long‐term cycling. Concerning the evolution of the relative intensities of the three components during cycling, component B does not show any evolution, while components A and C are evolving. This observation means that only octahedrally coordinated iron atoms Fe1 and Fe2 are oxidized/reduced, while Fe3 in the trigonal bipyramid (component B) does not participate in the redox process. The evolutions of Fe2+ and Fe3+ contents in Na2‐xFe3(SO4)4, varying linearly with x (obtained from electrochemical data) during charge and discharge, are plotted in Figure S8, proving that, as expected, Na+ extraction and reintercalation, and the corresponding oxidation/reduction of Fe3+/Fe2+, happen gradually. However, it should be noted in Figure S8 that the rate of oxidation of Fe2+ slows down around 4.3 V in charge and that the charge capacity was much higher than the discharge one (82 mAh g−1 versus 58 mAh g−1, respectively). This indicates that part of the charge current is irreversibly consumed because of electrolyte degradation above 4.3 V. A close amount of Fe was oxidized during charge (≈54 at%) and reduced during discharge (≈45 at%), meaning that the material exhibits reversible Na+ deintercalation and reintercalation. The amount of oxidized and subsequently reduced Fe2+ is estimated from electrochemical data (considering the discharge capacity) to be equal to ≈43%. These values are in good agreement with each other within the accuracy of the analyses, as small deviation could be related to statistical errors and corrections of Lamb–Mössbauer factors.[ 53 ]

Conclusions

In the general scope of satisfying the demand for positive electrode materials, based on abundant elements for Na‐ion batteries, we have synthesized a new electrochemically active sodium iron sulfate phase, NFS, by a low‐temperature method of ball milling in an inert atmosphere. This new compound crystallizes in the Pbca space group with cell parameters a = 9.682(1) Å, b = 8.739(1) Å, c = 29.300(4) Å, and V = 2479.0(5) Å3. We have shown that 20 mol% excess of Na2SO4 is essential to obtain a “pure” phase, i.e., without the impurity of the precursor FeSO4. The NFS phase shows attractive electrochemical properties, unlike its structural analogue, the Mn‐based Pbca NMS, for which no electrochemical activity is reported. Thick free‐standing PTFE electrodes during cycling at low‐rate C/30 (for exchange of 2 e−) versus Na metal delivered 62 mAh g−1 (69% of theoretical capacity) with good capacity retention for discharge rate at least up to 2C. Operando XRD revealed a volume change below 1% during cycling, which makes this new phase a zero‐strain positive electrode material. Operando synchrotron Mössbauer spectroscopy showed that only Fe in octahedral coordination is oxidized/reduced during cycling, whereas Fe in pentahedral (trigonal bipyramid) coordination remains inactive.
