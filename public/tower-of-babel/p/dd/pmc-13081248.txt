Blood and body fluid exposure and reporting behaviors among healthcare workers: a decade-long comparative study at a tertiary medical center
Authors: Ziad Kaddour, Rami Abdul-Halim, Zeina Al-Khalil, Lea Al Zoghby, Raafat Alameddine, Reem Mansour, Jack El Sawda, Zeina A. Kanafani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOccupational exposure to blood and body fluids (BBFE) remains a significant risk for healthcare workers (HCWs), yet underreporting persists despite the implementation of institutional policies. This study aimed to compare BBFE prevalence, reporting practices, and HCWs’ knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors, and identify predictors of BBFE and failure to report incidents over a ten-year period at a tertiary medical center.MethodsTwo cross-sectional surveys were conducted at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC) in 2013–2014 and 2023–2024. In 2013–2014, all participants completed paper-based questionnaires, whereas in 2023–2024, medical students, residents, and nurses completed online surveys, and housekeeping/laundry staff completed paper-based surveys. Descriptive comparisons and logistic regression were performed to assess BBFE prevalence, reporting behaviors, and predictors of exposure and reporting using logistic regression.ResultsA total of 299 HCWs participated (157 in 2013–2014; 142 in 2023–2024). Overall, 31.3% reported experiencing a BBFE, with housekeeping/laundry staff (48.6%) and nurses (44.6%) showing the highest prevalence (p < 0.001). The overall reporting rate was 82.6%, exceeding regional and global estimates. Lower attitude/behavior scores independently predicted BBFE occurrence (aOR = 0.665, p = 0.013), while lower perception scores predicted failure to report (aOR = 0.206, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in BBFE or reporting rates between cohorts, though knowledge and perception scores were higher in the 2023–2024 cohort.ConclusionDespite modest improvements in awareness and perception, BBFE remains a persistent occupational hazard across professional categories. High reporting rates reflect institutional progress, yet behavioral and cultural barriers to full compliance persist. Sustained efforts targeting safety culture, reporting accessibility, and psychosocial barriers are needed to achieve lasting reductions in occupational exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01723-w.

Background

Blood and body fluid exposure (BBFE) is a major occupational hazard in healthcare settings worldwide. It most commonly occurs through needlestick injuries (NSIs) or contact with sharp objects, resulting in the penetration of mucous membranes or non-intact skin [1]. Such injuries can lead to the transmission of potentially dangerous pathogens, such as Hepatitis B and Hepatitis C. Following a percutaneous injury, approximately 10 of every 100 exposed individuals are at risk of contracting the Hepatitis C virus. Those at risk are not limited to healthcare workers (HCWs) in direct contact with contaminated needles, but also include janitorial staff and waste handlers [2].

In response to the growing incidence of BBFE, substantial efforts have been made to strengthen prevention and control measures. Policies and regulations now emphasize thorough documentation of each incident and ensure immediate access to post-exposure testing and prophylaxis. Recognizing the occupational risks associated with BBFE, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) developed a set of standard precautions to prevent transmission resulting from sharp injuries [3]. Although these precautions are widely promoted across healthcare institutions, adherence remains suboptimal despite widespread awareness of their importance [2, 4]. Multiple factors, including awareness of exposure risks, compliance with safety protocols, adequacy of HCW training, and frequency of injury reporting, influence the incidence of BBFE [5–8]. Nonetheless, underreporting remains a pervasive issue. For instance, a study from China found that among 131 health science students exposed to sharp objects, only 34.4% reported the incident to their supervisors [9]. This is concerning, as timely post-exposure prophylaxis significantly decreases the risk of seroconversion following an injury [10].

These challenges in adherence and reporting are not uniformly distributed across healthcare roles but rather vary by occupation and work setting. Certain occupational groups within healthcare settings carry a disproportionately high burden of BBFE/NSI risk. For instance, in Lebanon, trainees such as residents and medical students accounted for 13% and 9% of exposures, respectively, in one long-term surveillance study [11]. A later tertiary-care study from Lebanon reported that residents (40%) and nurses (30%) were the most frequently exposed groups, with the operating room accounting for more than one-third of incidents [12]. Similarly, data from Somalia showed that nurses (52.4%) and cleaners (22.3%) were the highest-risk groups for sharps injuries [13].

Despite this clear burden, reporting remains inconsistent. Among undergraduate nursing students, more than half of percutaneous exposures went unreported, primarily because students feared academic or professional repercussions or perceived inadequacy in clinical competence [14]. The literature furthermore identifies inadequate training, older age, seniority, and exposures in high-intensity settings (e.g., operating rooms) as additional barriers to reporting [15].

Collectively, these findings suggest that underreporting reflects entrenched occupational hierarchies, training gaps, and cultural attitudes toward error disclosure. Such underreporting not only obscures the true burden of exposure but also hinders the implementation of effective preventive strategies. Consequently, BBFE remains a major occupational hazard in healthcare settings. Sustained efforts to strengthen HCW training, enforce adherence to safety protocols, and foster transparent reporting cultures are essential to mitigate these risks [12].

In alignment with the above, this study aims to assess the knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors regarding BBFE among selected high-risk healthcare professionals and staff at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC). By identifying patterns and knowledge gaps, the research seeks to inform policy reassessment and guide the development of targeted interventions to reduce accidental exposure events. Furthermore, the study investigates predictors of exposure and reporting, offering valuable insights into HCW behaviors related to BBFE.

Methods

Study aim and design

This cross-sectional study aimed to assess the knowledge, perception, and attitude/behavior regarding BBFE among selected high-risk HCWs at a tertiary medical center, AUBMC. It also sought to examine how these parameters have evolved by comparing two independent cross-sectional surveys conducted a decade apart (2013–2014 and 2023–2024).

The primary objective was to identify temporal trends in BBFE exposure and reporting behaviors within the same institutional setting. In addition, the study aims to determine predictors of BBFE and underreporting among two distinct groups. The first group comprised medical students, resident physicians, and nurses, while the second group included laundry and housekeeping staff at AUBMC.

Findings are based on data obtained from two survey-based questionnaires administered at separate time intervals.

Population and setting

Our study integrated survey data collected at two separate time intervals (2013–2014 and 2023–2024) at AUBMC. Both surveys targeted the same population of HCWs.

AUBMC is one of the largest tertiary medical centers in the Middle East and serves as both a teaching hospital and a hub for various clinical and research activities.

Eligible participants included HCWs aged 18 years or older who voluntarily agreed to participate. The study specifically focused on medical students, resident physicians, nurses, and housekeeping/laundry staff at AUBMC. These groups were selected based on evidence indicating both higher exposure risk to blood and body fluids and a greater likelihood of underreporting due to hierarchical and psychosocial factors (as mentioned in the Background section).

Data collection

The 2013–2014 survey was administered in paper-based form, with written consent obtained from participants beforehand. The 2023–2024 survey was conducted using the LimeSurvey platform and distributed electronically to an anonymous list of AUBMC medical students, residents, and nurses. In contrast, laundry and housekeeping staff completed paper-based surveys.

The online LimeSurvey was sent in English and completed by participants who had previously agreed to an electronic consent form. Physical copies of the survey, translated into Arabic, were distributed to hospital laundry and housekeeping staff by their supervisors. Oral consent was obtained from these participants, who completed the survey anonymously and privately at home. Completed surveys were returned, enclosed in envelopes to maintain confidentiality.

To ensure methodological consistency, the 2023–2024 questionnaire was based on the 2013–2014 version, with only minor modifications (e.g., questions about prior COVID-19 infection and vaccination) and four additional knowledge questions related to infection-control updates.

Questionnaire design and variables

Each survey started with an introductory paragraph outlining the study’s purpose and emphasizing confidentiality. No personal identifiers were collected.

The questionnaire included items on demographics, self-rated health, lifestyle behaviors, sharps exposure, and reporting of exposure incidents. Participants were categorized according to their role within the healthcare system at AUBMC (medical students, resident physicians, nurses, and laundry/housekeeping staff). The survey comprised three main sections addressing knowledge, perception, and attitudes/behaviors related to BBFE.

Knowledge-related questions assessed participants’ understanding of the risk of hepatitis B and HIV, as well as their familiarity with hospital policies for reporting and managing BBFE. Perception questions explored participants’ views on the benefit of reporting incidents, while attitude and behavior items examined their willingness to report and seek treatment following exposure.

Responses were assigned a score of 1 if they reflected a “positive” response, for example, a correct answer in the knowledge section or an indication of awareness regarding the benefit of reporting BBFE. Scores were summed to generate a total score for each section: the knowledge section (maximum score = 6), perception section (maximum = 5), and attitudes/behaviors section (maximum = 7).

Comparisons of perception and attitude/behavior scores were possible between the 2013–2014 and 2023–2024surveys. Knowledge scores were compared only for the identical questions included in both versions, as four new knowledge items were added in the 2023–2024 survey.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Data from the paper-based 2013–2014 survey were double-entered and cross-checked for accuracy before analysis.

Separate analyses were conducted for laundry/housekeeping staff and for clinical staff (residents, medical students, and nurses) due to differences in exposure patterns, job responsibilities, and infection control awareness.

In bivariate analysis, categorical variables (e.g., sex, health rating, etc. ) were examined using the Chi-square test, and continuous variables (e.g., age, knowledge, perception, and attitude/behavior scores) with the independent samples t-test. Variables with p ≤ 0.2 were entered into a multivariable backward logistic regression model to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Assumptions for normality, linearity, and multicollinearity were verified before regression modeling. Results are presented as ORs with 95% CIs. Outcomes assessed in the Chi-square and t-test analyses included the frequency of sharps exposure and failure to report BBFE incidents, analyzed against all demographic and occupational variables.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at AUBMC (IRB approval number SBS-2023-0100). All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 299 participants were included in the study, 157 from the 2013–2014 survey and 142 from the 2023–2024 survey. The distribution of male and female participants was similar across cohorts, with 48.8% males and 51.2% females (Table 1). The mean age of participants was 30.13 ± 9.13 years, with the 2023–2024 cohort being slightly older than those in 2013–2014 (31.82 ± 9.46 vs. 28.52 ± 8.50; p = 0.002) (Table 1). Participants included 78 medical students, 87 resident physicians, 93 nurses, and 38 housekeeping/laundry staff (Table 2).

Most participants were unmarried (68.8%; p = 0.011) and without children (72.9%; p = 0.017). Household composition, smoking status, and exercise habits were similar between survey periods. Alcohol consumption was notably lower in the 2023–2024 compared to 2013–2014 (27.9% vs. 48.1%, p < 0.001). Approximately half of the participants reported having a primary care physician, and nearly all rated their health as good or excellent. Among the 2023–2024 cohort, 97.2% had received the COVID-19 vaccine, and 73.9% had a prior COVID-19 infection (Table 1).

Exposure to blood and body fluid events, knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes/behaviors

Overall, 31.3% of participants reported at least one accidental needlestick or sharp injury, with no significant differences between the two survey periods. Among those exposed, 82.6% reported the incident, and 73.0% underwent the recommended testing (Table 1). BBFE varied significantly by professional category (p < 0.001): 6.4% of medical students, 33.3% of resident physicians, 44.6% of nurses, and 48.6% of housekeeping/laundry staff reported at least one incident (Table 2). No significant temporal differences were observed in incident frequency, reporting, or testing between the two periods (Table 1).

Knowledge

Participants’ knowledge of BBFE improved over time. For multiple-choice questions, correct responses were higher in most items in the 2023–2024 period, notably regarding the risk of hepatitis B infection from sharps exposure (38.7% vs. 24.8%; p = 0.01) and the responsible party for monitoring BBFE at the institution (40.1% vs. 0%; p < 0.001). Yes/no questions indicated that most participants were familiar with institutional policies and preventive strategies, with the highest awareness for “No recapping needles” (59.8%) and “Sharps container” usage (44.1%) in 2023–2024 (See Additional File 1: Table S1). The mean knowledge score in 2023–2024 was 3.29 ± 1.46 out of 6 (Table 1).

Perceptions

The mean perception score was significantly higher than in 2013–2014 (4.09 ± 1.00 vs. 3.69 ± 1.10; p = 0.001) (Table 1), while most participants indicated positive perceptions toward BBFE management. Overall, 83.4% of participants reported adequate knowledge regarding BBFE risks. Participants increasingly felt familiar with the steps to take after an exposure (77.7% in 2023–2024 vs. 62.0% 2013–2014 overall; p = 0.004) and believed that others would report BBFE incidents (66.9% vs. 53.0%; p = 0.016). However, perceptions of the overall impact of reporting on reducing incidents did not differ significantly between the two periods (84.8%, p = 0.277) (See Additional File 1: Table S2).

Attitudes and behaviors

Attitudes toward reporting were consistently positive, with no statistically significant difference in mean attitude/behavior scores between cohorts (6.58 ± 0.80 vs. 6.38 ± 1.00; p = 0.078) (Table 1). Nearly all participants agreed that reporting BBFE is necessary (97.6%) and expressed willingness to report injuries personally (97.9%), endorsing reporting for all injuries (94.4%). However, a substantial proportion of participants reported fear of other healthcare workers’ reactions to injury disclosure (85.3%) and felt intimidated by the BBFE reporting process (82.3%). These trends appeared generally similar in 2013–2014 and in 2023–2024 (See Additional File 1: Table S3).

BBFE incidents by participant category and predictors

Overall, 31.3% of participants reported experiencing a BBFE incident. Rates differed significantly by professional category (p < 0.001): 6.4% of medical students, 33.3% of residents, 44.6% of nurses, and 48.6% of housekeeping/laundry staff reported incidents, highlighting a higher exposure among frontline personnel (Table 2).

Predictors of BBFE exposure and failure to report

Bivariate analysis identified that age, attitude/behavior score, male sex, alcohol consumption, and prior COVID-19 infection were associated with BBFE incidents (p < 0.2). Having children was also significant but was excluded from the logistic regression because no medical students have children. On multivariate analysis, only age (aOR 1.045, 95% CI 1.008–1.083) and lower attitude/behavior scores (aOR 0.665, 95% CI 0.481–0.919) remained significant predictors, indicating higher odds of experiencing BBFE (Table 3).

Among laundry and housekeeping staff, age and knowledge score were the only variables significantly associated with failure to report BBFE in bivariate analysis; multivariate logistic regression was not performed due to limited sample size (n = 38). Discussion

In our study, 31.3% of HCWs reported experiencing a needlestick or sharp injury. This prevalence is higher than the 14% reported among HCWs in Abha, Saudi Arabia (KSA) [16] and 19% in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) [17], but is closer to the rates reported in Iran (47%) [18] and Sudan (46%) [19]. Even higher rates were reported in Egypt (67.9%) [20], suggesting regional or institutional differences in safety practices and preventive measures. A 2020 meta-analysis estimated a global prevalence of NSIs among HCWs of 44.5%, which aligns closely with our findings [21].

Reporting of BBFE incidents was higher in our cohort, with 82.6% of participants submitting reports—substantially exceeding rates observed in KSA (47.3%) [16], Poland (54.8%) [22], the United Kingdom (51%) [23], and the UAE (58.8%) [24]. In Sudan, only 34% of HCWs reported their injury [19]. Despite these high rates, underreporting remains a persistent challenge globally. Barriers to reporting include fear of negative career consequences, lack of awareness of protocols [25], and doubts about the value of reporting. In our study, a lower perception score significantly predicted failure to report. Psychological factors, including shame, guilt, and fear of blame, also contribute alongside structural barriers such as cumbersome reporting processes and potential disruption to patient care [26, 27].

The highest prevalence of BBFE in our cohort was observed among housekeeping and laundry staff (48.6%) and nurses (44.6%), reflecting increased exposure risk. These rates exceed the 35% NSI prevalence reported in a global meta-analysis of nursing students worldwide [28] but are similar to rates reported by Bouya et al. (42.8%) [21]. Medical students had a lower prevalence (6.4%) compared with data from the United States (30%) [29] and Europe (34%) [30], likely due to their limited direct contact with sharps compared to clinical staff [12].

Fear of reporting BBFEs remains a persistent problem across healthcare contexts, influenced by both institutional and personal factors. Pavithran et al. reported that many HCWs refrain from reporting due to fear of blame, administrative consequences, or being perceived as careless, perpetuating silent risk [31]. Similarly, nearly one-fifth of HCWs with NSIs failed to report in another study, particularly among physicians, often due to a lack of awareness of reporting protocols and fear of professional repercussions [32]. A 2023 study from China further highlighted the impact of perceived negative judgment from supervisors and career-related fears, especially among junior staff [33]. Collectively, these findings emphasize that underreporting is not merely a procedural gap. It also reflects behavioral and cultural factors, including fear, stigma, and inadequate institutional support [31–33].

Although the study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, the overall patterns of BBFE occurrence and reporting remained relatively stable over time. This suggests that occupational exposure behaviors are influenced more by enduring structural and cultural factors within healthcare settings than by temporary changes in infection control awareness. Small improvements in participants’ knowledge and perception were observed in the more recent cohort, likely reflecting heightened attention to infectious disease control in healthcare during and after the pandemic. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining consistent educational and organizational strategies to reduce BBFE risk [34–36]. However, knowledge scores were not significant predictors of BBFE exposure or failure to report, consistent with Malaysian data showing no association between knowledge and NSI incidents among medical students [37]. Other studies also suggest that training alone does not necessarily reduce injury risk [38], emphasizing that knowledge must translate into consistent preventive practices and competency in applying safety measures [39, 40].

Perception scores improved in 2023–2024, with more participants reporting familiarity with post-exposure protocols. A low perception score remained the only independent predictor of failure to report BBFE incidents among students, residents, and nurses. This aligns with previous research, such as a 2008 study among paramedics, showing that perceived transmission risk, particularly for deeper injuries, influenced the likelihood of reporting [41]. Belief that reporting would lead to appropriate management was associated, though not significantly, with higher reporting rates.

Attitude and behavior scores did not change significantly over time, but respondents in 2023–2024 were more likely to report fear of colleagues’ reactions to injury. Similar patterns have been reported elsewhere, including among European medical students (45%) [30] and dental professionals in India [31]. However, institutional context matters: in New South Wales, Australia, 90% of nurses described managers as approachable after an NSI [42]. In our sample, 14% of participants reported fear of colleagues’ reactions, demonstrating variability in cultural and institutional norms. Attitude/behavior scores were also independent predictors of BBFE frequency, aligning with findings from Thailand showing that nurses with lower attitude scores were more likely to sustain injuries [43]. Older age was associated with higher BBFE incidence, which may reflect differences in risk perception, cumulative exposure, or other unmeasured factors.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding factors influencing BBFE. Second, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, which may have led to under- or over-reporting of exposures and behaviors. Third, differences in survey administration methods, paper-based in 2013–2014 versus online via LimeSurvey in 2023–2024, could have affected participation rates or response patterns and may introduce response bias related to anonymity or recall. Fourth, as noted in the Methods, the study focused on HCWs directly involved in patient care (students, residents, nurses, and housekeeping/laundry staff), which, along with the relatively young age of the surveyed cohort, limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader hospital population. Fifth, the addition of new knowledge questions in the 2023–2024 survey also prevented direct comparison of knowledge scores over time. Sixth, secular trends and changes in institutional policies, technologies, and education over the decade may have influenced BBFE incidence and reporting, which should be considered when interpreting temporal comparisons. Finally, smaller subgroup sizes and unmeasured factors such as prior training quality, individual risk perception, or institutional culture may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

This study highlights key insights into BBFE among HCWs, with 31.3% reporting needlestick or sharp injuries. The relatively high reporting rate (82.6%) compared with prior studies may reflect increased awareness or improved institutional protocols. Nevertheless, barriers such as fear of career repercussions, lack of awareness of procedures, and cumbersome reporting systems persist. While greater knowledge does not necessarily translate into safer practices, perceptions and attitudes toward exposure remain critical: perceptions influence reporting behavior, and attitudes predict injury rates. A comprehensive approach that addresses both psychological and procedural barriers, fosters a supportive reporting culture, and simplifies reporting mechanisms is essential to reducing occupational exposures and enhancing healthcare worker safety.
