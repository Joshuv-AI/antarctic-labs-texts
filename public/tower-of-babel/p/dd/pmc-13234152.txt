Scalable Ni‑driven synthesis of Pt single‑site catalysts for hydrogen evolution
Authors: Huijing Ma, Haifei Wang, Chengcheng Cai, Hongyu Song, Qianqing Guo, Xia Long, Xufang Qian, Yixin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow-platinum-loading electrocatalysts, offering both high activity and durability under practical conditions, are essential for sustainable hydrogen production. Here we report a scalable synthesis of a platinum single-site catalyst supported on Ni-N-doped carbon nanotubes, achieved via a facile Ni-driven one-step reduction-displacement of Pt4+. The catalyst NCNT-Ni/Pt features a N2-Pt-Cl2 initial coordination, where the dynamic evolution of Pt-Cl bonds regulates the hydrogen evolution reaction performance. Excitingly, the catalyst demonstrates an overpotential of 7.78 ± 0.86 mV at 10 mA cm–2. With a Pt loading of 6 μg cm–2, it enables industrially relevant proton exchange membrane water electrolysis at 1.63 V@1 A cm–2, with a degradation rate of 3.3 μV h–1, sustained over 4500 h. Coupled with a 21%-efficient photovoltaic module, it delivers a 16.06% solar-to-hydrogen efficiency at industrial-level current density. This study presents a practical strategy for minimizing precious-metal use in the synthesis of industrial-grade hydrogen evolution electrocatalysts.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Hydrogen energy, Electrocatalysis, Catalyst synthesis

Introduction

With the global commitment to net-zero carbon emissions and the rapid growth of renewable electricity, green hydrogen has become a crucial vector for clean energy1–3. Proton Exchange Membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE) is particularly promising for coupling with intermittent wind and solar energy, owing to its high current density, compact architecture, and rapid dynamic response4. However, the broad application of PEMWE is hampered by its reliance on scarce and expensive electrocatalytic materials, notably platinum (Pt)5. Conventional Pt/C catalysts, however, face limitations such as poor dispersion, weak metal-support interaction and nanoparticle agglomeration, particularly in the dynamic and acidic HER conditions6. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a scalable synthesis strategy for low-Pt-content HER catalysts that combine high activity with long-term stability under industrial operating conditions.

Considerable attention and effort have been directed toward minimizing Pt usage in HER catalysts over the past decade7. The key scientific challenge lies in achieving controllable and facile synthesis of atomically dispersed Pt sites with intrinsically high catalytic activity and stability. Single-site catalysts in terms of Pt single atom and atomic cluster catalysts, which feature fully exposed Pt centers, maximize atomic utilization and enable fine-tuning of the coordination environment8–10. However, most existing approaches rely on Pt complex precursors (e.g., Pt(acac)2), additional reductants or light/thermal treatments, and harsh synthetic conditions11–14, which impede scalability and raise environmental and cost concerns. Recent studies have demonstrated that Pt single sites anchored on different carbon-based materials represent the most potential substitutes to commercial Pt/C catalysts15–19. The local coordination configuration of Pt is crucial in influencing catalytic performance. In particular, defect engineering and the introduction of heteroatoms (such as N, S, or Cl) can adjust the electronic structure of Pt, suppress aggregation, and improve activity20–23. Among various coordination motifs, the asymmetric N2-Pt-Cl2 structure has been identified as an potent site for HER, as it promotes the formation of the unsaturated N2-Pt-Hx intermediate, which enhances reactant adsorption and transformation, thus maximizing Pt utilization24–26. However, these coordination environments are often realized only on specialized supports or organic frameworks27–31, which are difficult to synthesize at scale and lack long-term electrochemical robustness.

Here, we propose a facile and scalable one-step reduction-displacement (ORD) approach that enables the formation of well-dispersed Pt species under mild conditions. Structural characterizations confirm the presence of Pt single sites with N2-Pt-Cl2 coordination anchored on Ni-N-doped carbon nanotubes (NCNT-Ni/Pt). The catalyst with a low Pt content (1.2 wt.%), delivers competitive HER activity, requiring only 7.78 ± 0.86 mV to achieve 10 mA cm–2 in acidic electrolyte. When integrated into a PEMWE cell, the catalyst achieves 1.63 V at 1 A cm–2 with a low Pt loading and shows a long-term stability for over 4500 h. Furthermore, life cycle assessment (LCA) and cost analysis confirm the environmental and economic viability of this technology, especially when derived from plastic waste-based carbon sources. Our findings demonstrate that rational design of Pt single-site catalyst via in-situ reduction and coordination control offers a scalable pathway toward precious-metal-efficient and durable HER electrocatalysts, bridging the gap between laboratory innovation and industrial implementation.

Results

Physicochemical properties of NCNT-Ni/Pt

Ni-N-doped carbon nanotubes (NCNT-Ni) were synthesized through thermal annealing of Ni ion-anchored melamine-cyanuric acid (MCA) supramolecular aggregates at 800 °C. In the resulting material, Ni exists on the NCNT both as metallic nanoparticles and atomically dispersed Ni sites32,33. Notably, melamine-formaldehyde resin (MFR) waste can be recycled and serve as a carbon and nitrogen source for synthesizing NCNT-Ni34,35. Subsequently, impregnation of H2PtCl6 into a dispersed NCNT-Ni suspension at 25 °C yielded Pt single sites supported on NCNT-Ni. In our synthetic scheme, metallic Ni reduces the Pt4+ species, where they subsequently displace the coordinated Ni atoms on the NCNT to form a N2-Pt-Cl2 coordination structure (Fig. 1a). This one-step reduction-displacement (ORD) strategy enables gram-scale synthesis (>10 g per batch) under mild conditions (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S1), highlighting its scalability and potential for industrial production. The textural properties of NCNT‑Ni/Pt were shown in Supplementary Table S1, Figs. S2 and S3. Details of the synthesis optimization are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4 and S5. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of NCNT-Ni/Pt exhibits distinct peaks at 2θ = 26.2°, 44.5°, 51.8° and 76.4° corresponding to the graphitic 2H (002) and metal Ni (111), (200), and (220) planes. No distinct diffraction peak attributable to Pt was observed (Supplementary Fig. S6), implying the absence of crystalline Pt nanoparticles. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that well-defined carbon nanotube morphologies after annealing, which remained essentially unchanged upon Pt incorporation (Supplementary Fig. S7). Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) mappings also validated the absence of noticeable Pt nanoparticles on the nanotubes (Supplementary Fig. S8). High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM with aberration correction (AC-HAADF-STEM) revealed uniformly dispersed bright spots on NCNT-Ni, corresponding to single atoms (Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10). Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) confirmed that these atomic-scale bright spots originated from Ni, which was likely coordinated with N atoms within the carbon matrix (Fig. 1c, d). Upon Pt incorporation, additional bright spots of varying intensities and diameters appeared on the NCNT, corresponding to Pt single atoms and atomic clusters (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. S11). Statistical analysis of over 300 atomic features yielded an average diameter of 0.25 ± 0.01 nm, with Pt single atoms overwhelmingly predominant (single atoms: atomic clusters ≈9:1) (Supplementary Fig. S12). The HAADF-STEM-EDX elemental mapping confirmed that Ni, Pt, Cl, C, N and O species were uniformly distributed on the nanotubes (Supplementary Fig. S13). Notably, the spatial overlap of Pt with Cl and N suggested a distinctive N2-Pt-Cl2 coordination motif (Fig. 1g), consistent with the designed local structure. The in-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) measurements reveal that NCN-Ni/Pt exhibit strong CO binding at Pt single sites (2150 cm–1), while NCNT-Ni shows weaker CO adsorption at 2140 cm–1 (Supplementary Fig. S14)36–38, which further verifies the presence of Pt single sites and Ni single atoms.

NCNT-Ni/Pt was further analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), confirming the presence of Ni, O, N, C, Cl and Pt elements (Supplementary Fig. S15a). The Pt 4 f spectrum showing 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks located at 72.00 and 75.18 eV, indicating that Pt existed in a low oxidation state. Meanwhile, the Cl 2p spectrum (2p3/2: 198.14 eV and 2p1/2: 199.12 eV) showed clear evidence for Pt-Cl bonding26 (Fig. 2a, b). In the N 1 s region, a distinct peak at 398.66 eV was assigned to metal-nitrogen bond (M-N) (Supplementary Fig. S15b). X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements were conducted to analyze Pt’s coordination environment in the NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst. The X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectrum revealed that the white-line intensity lies between PtO2 and Pt foil (Fig. 2c), corroborating the partially reduced oxidation state of Pt observed by XPS. The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra revealed a major peak at approximately 1.92 Å, corresponding to Pt-Cl bonding26, distinct from Pt-Pt and Pt-O. Additionally, a shoulder peak at approximately 1.5 Å was attributed to Pt-N coordination (Fig. 2d). Quantitative curve fitting of the Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectra (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Table S2) revealed that Pt atom was coordinated with two Cl and two N atoms, establishing a N2-Pt-Cl2 structure, which was further verified by wavelet transform EXAFS (WT-EXAFS). The maximum intensity in k-space was located at 6.7 Å–1 for NCNT-Ni/Pt, lower than those of Pt foil (11.5 Å–1) and PtO2 (7.4 Å–1) (Fig. 2g), consistent with backscattering from lighter atoms (N, Cl)39. When resolved in R-space at approximately 1.92 Å, this feature confirmed the coexistence of Pt-Cl and Pt-N coordination, providing compelling evidence for the N2-Pt-Cl2 coordination in the NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst.

Synthesis mechanism of NCNT-Ni/Pt via the ORD strategy

The intrinsic redox potential difference of Ni2+/Ni0 (E0 = –0.25 V vs SHE) and [PtCl6]2–/ [PtCl4]2– species (E0 = +0.68 V vs SHE) provides the thermodynamic impetus for the reduction of Pt(IV) to Pt(II) by metallic Ni40. In this system, zerovalent Ni nanoparticles supported on NCNT act as local reductants. While such nanoparticles are often removed by acid leaching to isolate Ni single atoms41,42, here they are deliberately preserved to enable the in-situ ORD process (Fig. 1a). The presence of Ni nanoparticle is confirmed by high-resolution TEM (Supplementary Fig. S16) and their metallic nature was further verified by cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Supplementary Fig. S17). In N2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4, pristine NCNT-Ni exhibited two oxidation peaks at 0.64 V and 1.59 V (vs. RHE), corresponding to Ni0 → Ni2+ and Ni2+→Ni3+ transitions, respectively. After nitric acid treatment, these peaks disappeared, indicating the complete removal of exposed Ni nanoparticles. To elucidate the atomic-scale mechanism, we performed density functional theory (DFT) simulations to model the displacement reaction. Given the coexistence of Pt single atoms and atomic clusters observed experimentally, a minimal atomic cluster model was constructed based on EXAFS-derived parameters. Specifically, a dimeric Pt species coordinated by two bridging Cl atoms and four pyrrolic N atoms (denoted as N2-Pt-Cl2-Pt-N2) was constructed and optimized geometrically. We also modeled a Ni-N4 single atom site on the NCNT (Supplementary Fig. S18). The configuration is supported by the N 1 s XPS spectrum of NCNT-Ni, which show a dominant pyrrolic-N contribution associated with Ni-N bonding (Supplementary Fig. S19). The calculated substitution energy for replacing Ni single atom by [PtCl4]2– is –0.44 eV, confirming that the process is exothermic and thermodynamically favorable under ambient conditions. Additionally, Ni K-edge XAS shows that the Ni-N coordination number in NCNT-Ni/Pt is significantly lower than that in NCNT-Ni (Supplementary Figs. S20, 21, and Table S3), indicating that part of the Ni-N coordination is disrupted after Pt introduction, which is consistent with the proposed reduction-displacement mechanism.

To further validate the ORD mechanism, platinum dichloride (PtCl2) was impregnated onto three different supports: NCNT-Ni, NCNT, and pristine CNT under identical conditions, yielding NCNT-Ni-PtCl2, NCNT-PtCl2 and CNT-PtCl2, respectively. XPS analysis detected no Pt or Cl signals in CNT-PtCl2, demonstrating the pivotal role of N in binding Pt (Supplementary Fig. s22a). In NCNT-Ni-PtCl2, Pt existed in mixed oxidation states, including metallic Pt0 (70.96 eV, 74.16 eV) and partially reduced Ptσ+ (0<σ < 2; 71.77 eV, 75.08 eV). In contrast, the Ni-free NCNT-PtCl2 retained Pt exclusively in the +2 oxidation state (72.64 eV, 75.97 eV) (Supplementary Fig. S22b) directly evidencing the reductive role of Ni in the ORD process. The N 1 s spectrum of NCNT-PtCl2 shifted to lower binding energy relative to NCNT-Ni/Pt, indicating stronger M-N interactions (Supplementary Fig. S22c), likely associated with the formation of Pt-NxC3–x or Pt-N4 configurations15,22. Furthermore, compared with NCNT-Ni/Pt, the Pt-Cl signal in NCNT-Ni-PtCl2 and NCNT-PtCl2 shifted towards lower binding energies and showed reduced peak intensity, consistent with partial Pt0 formation and the conversion of Pt-Cl to Pt-N bonds (Supplementary Fig. S22d). The above results demonstrate that the ORD strategy, utilizing NCNT-Ni as a redox active support and H2PtCl6 as a Pt precursor, selectively yields the N2-Pt-Cl2 coordination configuration. This pathway integrates redox displacement and atomic reconstruction, enabling the controlled generation of atomically dispersed Pt species stabilized by dual N/Cl coordination. We also successfully extended this reduction-displacement strategy to other noble metals such as Ir and Ru, both showing good dispersion on NCNT-Ni, which demonstrates the versatility of the synthetic approach (Supplementary Fig. S23).

Electrocatalytic HER performances

The HER performance of NCNT-Ni/Pt and NCNT-Ni was evaluated in acidic media (0.5 M H2SO4) using a standard three-electrode setup, with commercial 60 wt.% Pt/C as the benchmark (see Materials and Methods). The NCNT-Ni/Pt requiring only 7.78 ± 0.86 mV to achieve 10 mA cm–2, significantly lower than commercial Pt/C (27.53 ± 3.58 mV at 10 mA cm–2). This performance demonstrates significant competitiveness among Pt-based catalysts reported in recent years (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S4). To clarify the contribution of Ni species, both NCNT-Ni and NCNT-Ni/Pt were treated with nitric acid to remove Ni nanoparticles (denoted as NCNT-Ni (NT) and NCNT-Ni/Pt (NT)). The HER performance of NCNT-Ni (NT) sample displayed even poorer HER activity than the pristine NCNT-Ni (Fig. 3a), suggesting that the atomic Ni make a negligible contribution to HER process. Conversely, the NCNT-Ni/Pt (NT) retained the good activity, confirming that the catalytic sites responsible for HER are dominated by Pt rather than Ni, and the removal of Ni nanoparticles has little impact on performance (Fig. 3a). The NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst exhibited a low Tafel slope of 9.85 mV dec–1 (Fig. 3b), following the Volmer-Tafel mechanism21. At 50 mV overpotential, the catalyst achieved a mass activity of 69.14 ± 3.94 A mg–1 and a turnover frequency (TOF) of 68.82 ± 3.58 s–1, 86.4 and 85.1 times that of commercial Pt/C (0.80 ± 0.03 A mg–1 and 0.81 ± 0.04 s–1), respectively (Fig. 3c). Normalized to the electrochemical surface area (ECSA)43, the specific activities were 18.82 ± 2.22 mA cmECSA−2 for NCNT-Ni/Pt and 8.26 ± 0.85 mA cmECSA−2 for Pt/C (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. S24). Hydrogen evolution was directly quantified by gas collection under constant current, yielding a Faradaic efficiency close to 100% (Supplementary Fig. S25), confirming that nearly all electrons are utilized for the HER with negligible side reactions. Cost-normalized analysis further highlights the economic advantage of NCNT-Ni/Pt. Its price normalized activity reaches 2138.32 ± 248.05 A dollar–1, approximately 86 times higher than Pt/C (24.74 ± 2.25 A dollar–1) (Supplementary Fig. S26), further highlighting its significant intrinsic activity and cost-effectiveness for industrial H2 production.

Catalytic mechanism of NCNT-Ni/Pt

In-situ Raman spectroscopy was used to investigate the catalyst-electrolyte interfacial structure44. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S28, the relative intensity of the v3 band, corresponding to non-participating water molecules, decreases much faster with increasing cathodic potential in NCNT-Ni/Pt than in Pt/C. This suggests that NCNT-Ni/Pt promotes more efficient activation and hydrogen binding of interfacial water. The corresponding fitting spectra and quantitative analyses are shown in Supplementary Figs. S29–31. In-situ electrochemical impedance spectrum (EIS) revealed the dynamic charge-transfer behavior (Supplementary Fig. S32). NCNT-Ni/Pt exhibits good intrinsic conductivity, enhanced electron transfer during the HER process, and better charge transport and gas release capabilities than Pt/C, as evidenced by its smaller and more stable phase angle changes with increasing potential. The electrical conductivity of NCNT-Ni/Pt powder measured by a four-point probe method was found to be comparable to commercial Vulcan XC-72 carbon black, which is widely used to increase the conductivity of catalysts (Supplementary Fig. S33). This high intrinsic conductivity obviates the need for additional conductive additives, ensuring efficient charge transport throughout the catalyst layer.

We have performed operando XAS experiments to directly monitor the evolution of the local coordination environment of Pt single sites under HER working conditions. At the open-circuit potential (OCP), both XANES and EXAFS spectra closely match those of the pristine catalyst, confirming that the N2-Pt-Cl2 structure remains intact in acidic electrolyte (Figs. 2c, d and 4a, b). Upon applying –0.1 V (vs. RHE), the XANES white-line intensity decreases, indicating a reduction in Pt oxidation state associated with dechlorination. Meanwhile, the main FT‑EXAFS peak shifts from 1.72 Å to 1.60 Å, consistent with coordination environment change (Fig. 4a, b). Upon –0.2 V, the Pt-N coordination contribution further increases, indicating progressive restructuring of the Pt site (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table S6). After the HER operation, the Pt-N peak intensity continues to increase without additional peak shift, consistent with H replacing Cl in the coordination sphere. Importantly, no Pt-Pt coordination peak (~2.54 Å) appears under any operando condition, confirming that Pt remains atomically dispersed throughout operation. The fitting curves of operando FT‑EXAFS and corresponding fitting parameters are presented in Supplementary Fig. S34 and Table S6. XPS analysis of NCNT-Ni/Pt before and after the HER reaction revealed that Pt 4 f peaks shift to lower binding energy post-reaction (Supplementary Fig. S35), indicative of an increased electron density and stronger hydrogen adsorption capability. This observation aligns well with the in-situ Raman results, confirming that H adsorption was enhanced without hindering H2 desorption. Additionally, the Cl 2p signal intensity significantly decreases, and the characteristic peak associated with the Pt-Cl bond (198.1 eV) shifts to a lower binding energy (197.5 eV) after electrolysis (Fig. 4c), indicating the detachment of the Cl ligands. Corresponding with the operando EXAFS result, where the Cl removal generates a coordination environment that may represent the true HER active site. The Cl– concentration in the electrolyte was monitored during electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. S36), corroborating the gradual release of Cl ligands from Pt centers. A vibrational band at ~331 cm–1 was observed in the in-situ Raman spectrum, attributed to the Pt-Cl stretching vibration (Supplementary Fig. S37)26. Upon applying negative potential, this Pt-Cl band gradually disappeared, consistent with Cl removal, while a distinct band at 2326 cm–1 was detected, corresponding to Pt-H formation (Fig. 4d)25. We then performed DFT calculations to assess the hydrogen adsorption free energies (ΔGH*) for various configurations that may emerge from progressive Cl removal and H adsorption, with Pt-N4 serving as a reference (Fig. 4e). The calculated total density of states (TDOS) and d-band centers reveal that ligand electronic effects (Cl withdraws, H donates) dynamically tune Pt’s d-band center shifting from –1.74 eV to –3.01 eV (Supplementary Fig. S38), thereby modulating hydrogen adsorption strength. Among these models, the (N2-Pt)2 structure exhibited the most negative ΔGH* of –0.98 eV, favoring strong H binding to form (N2-Pt-H)2. The latter configuration, with an optimized ΔGH* of –0.21 eV, is consistent with the thermodynamically favorable HER-active site, in agreement with the experimental findings. This may help explain the relatively small Tafel slope observed for NCNT‑Ni/Pt.

PEMWE performances and LCA & cost analysis

Owing to its competitive HER performance and cost efficiency, the NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst was further integrated into practical PEMWE to assess real-world performance. The PEMWE used NCNT-Ni/Pt for the cathode and commercial IrO2 for the anode as catalysts, with both electrodes using catalyst coated membrane (CCM) method (Supplementary Fig. S39). Deionized water served as the electrolyte, even though it resulted in higher ohmic losses compared to acidic electrolyte. At 80 °C, the NCNT-Ni/Pt based PEMWE delivered a current density of 1 A cm–2 at 1.63 V lower than the 1.69 V required by the commercial Pt/C (Fig. 5a). Moreover, the device exhibited good durability, with a degradation rate of only 3.3 μV h–1 over 4500 h (Fig. 5b). Compared with previously reported Pt-based HER catalysts, NCNT-Ni/Pt showed comparable activity and long-term stability (Supplementary Fig. S40 and Table S7). To simulate realistic renewable power fluctuations, the PEMWE parallel sample was subjected to dynamic stress testing after running steadily for 2500 h (Supplementary Fig. S41). Two square-wave protocols were applied: (1) rapid cycling between 100% (40 s) and 10% (20 s) load every 60 s for 120 cycles, and (2) slow cycling between 100% (2 h) and 10% (1 h) load for 35 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S42a–d). After the above tests, the cell voltage increased by 2.33% under steady-state operations, 2.76% and 5.21% under the first and second dynamic tests, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S42e). The rise in ohmic resistance particularly an 85.5% increase after the second dynamic test was attributed to reverse current generated at low load (Supplementary Fig. S42f), which can locally damage the membrane-catalyst interface45,46. Post-operation TEM analyses further confirmed the morphological robustness of NCNT-Ni/Pt. The average Pt particle size on NCNT-Ni/Pt remained at 1.66 ± 0.1 nm, compared with significant growth to 5.8 ± 0.3 nm for Pt/C (Supplementary Fig. S43). Multi-scale TEM imaging and elemental mapping (Supplementary Figs. S44–46) analysis revealed that Pt aggregation was effectively suppressed in NCNT-Ni/Pt. The corresponding SAED pattern (Supplementary Fig. S47) showed diffuse halos alongside weak diffraction rings indicating the coexistence of amorphous and crystalline domains and confirming the preservation of atomically dispersed Pt sites47, AC-HAADF-STEM image further verified the retention of abundant Pt single atoms and atomic clusters (Supplementary Fig. S48). Additionally, SEM imaging demonstrated that the carbon nanotube morphology on the PEM remained intact (Supplementary Fig. S49), underscoring the good structural and interfacial stability. The XAS results of the NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst after 4500 h of PEMWE operation demonstrate that Pt-N coordination remains dominant, with no detectable Pt-Pt coordination signal (Supplementary Figs. S50, S51, and Table S7), indicating preserved atomic dispersion and long-term structural stability. The temperature-dependent PEMWE performance of NCNT-Ni/Pt was further evaluated in the range of 50–90 °C (Supplementary Fig. S52). Increasing the operating temperature leads to improved reaction kinetics and proton conductivity, resulting in lower cell voltage at identical current densities. However, excessively high temperatures can lead to degradation of the membrane or catalysts. Considering both performance and durability, 80 °C was selected as the standard testing condition. For solar-driven H2 production, the NCNT-Ni/Pt based PEMWE was coupled with a commercial photovoltaic (PV) module with a Power conversion efficiency of 21%. Under natural sunlight, the device achieved a solar-to-hydrogen (STH) efficiency of 16.06% and an amperage-level current of 2.82 A (0.71 A cm–2) (Fig. 5c). The coupled system exhibited stable operation during intermittent illumination test (Supplementary Fig. S53). Compared with multi-junction or concentrator PV-electrolysis systems, this configuration offers a low-cost route to achieve industrially relevant current density while maintaining high energy conversion efficiency48, highlighting its promise for scalable, solar-powered H2 production.

To evaluate the environmental and economic benefits of the synthesis strategy, LCA and cost analysis were conducted comparing two management pathways for MFR waste: (1) recycling 1 kg of MFR waste to synthesize NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst for PEMWE hydrogen production, and (2) incinerating 1 kg of MFR waste for electricity generation. The MFR recycling route system boundary was shown in Supplementary Fig. S54. The LCA results (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table S9) indicate that hydrogen production using NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst exhibits negative values in most indicators, confirming its net environmental benefits. Specifically, normalized GWP value is –1.17 × 10–8, and the fossil fuel depletion value is –3.53 × 10–8, indicating the reductions in greenhouse gas emissions and fossil resource dependence. Contribution analysis highlighted the environmental impact of each process component (Supplementary Fig. S55), with PV electricity being the leading contributor, mainly because of the high energy consumption and pollution emissions associated with silicon production. However, the environmental credits from hydrogen and oxygen production significantly offset these effects. Besides, the NCNT-Ni/Pt synthesis step contributes less than 4% to all environmental impact indicators. In contrast, MFR incineration shows positive values in almost all environmental indicators (Supplementary Fig. S56 and Table S9). The above-mentioned manufacturing routes and life cycle inventories are presented in Supplementary Figs. S57–59 and Tables S10–14. The cost analysis further demonstrates the advantages of the recycling path. When hydrogen and oxygen are considered as products, the recycling of MFR for catalyst production yields a net profit of $457,000 (Supplementary Table S15). In contrast, although the waste incineration technology is mature, without subsidies, it incurs a net loss of $–5.37, mainly due to transportation costs and low electricity prices (Supplementary Table S16). Furthermore, when the low-Pt NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst synthesized from waste is compared with commercial 60% Pt/C in a commercial PEM electrolyzer stack for hydrogen production, it enables a total lifecycle cost saving of approximately $750.07 and reduces the levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) by $0.171 kg–1, corresponding to an overall cost reduction of about 3.3% (Supplementary Tables S17 and S18). Overall, the catalyst synthesis path based on recycling provides a technically feasible, economically advantageous, and environmentally sustainable solution for waste valorization and hydrogen production.

Discussion

In summary, we have synthesized a Pt single-site catalyst (NCNT-Ni/Pt) for highly efficient HER via a facile and scalable ORD strategy. The catalyst demonstrates a low overpotential and a small Tafel slope. Mechanistic studies suggest that the initially constructed N2-Pt-Cl2-Pt-N2 motif may dynamically evolves into an active (N2-Pt-H)2 configuration under operation conditions, optimizing the adsorption-desorption kinetics of H* intermediate and thereby possibly contributing to the enhanced HER activity. Moreover, the NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst exhibits good durability in PEMWE. When integrated with commercial PV module, it delivers an ampere-level current density and high STH efficiency, confirming its scalability for solar-driving green hydrogen production. LCA and cost analysis further demonstrate that synthesizing NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst from recycled MFR waste significantly reduces both environmental impact and production cost. Collectively, these findings highlight the technological, economic and environmental advantages of the NCNT-Ni/Pt catalyst, offering practical insights into the rational design of low-loading precious-metal-based catalysts and promoting the practical deployment of efficient, durable PEMWE systems for sustainable hydrogen generation.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Melamine-formaldehyde resin was purchased from Zhongxin Plastic Co., Ltd., Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95.0 ~ 98.0%), isopropyl alcohol (C3H8O, ≥99.7%), nitric acid (HNO3, 65.0 ~ 68.0%) and cyanic acid (C3H3N3O3, ≥98.0%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, ≥98.0%) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Synthesis of NCNT-Ni/Pt powder

First, 1 g of melamine-formaldehyde resin and 1 g of cyanuric acid were ultrasonically dispersed in ethanol at 25 °C, and 0.5 g of Ni nitrate was added and stirred overnight. The obtained product was centrifuged and vacuum-dried and pyrolyzed at 800 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 3 °C min–1 under an Ar atmosphere to obtain a black catalyst powder (named NCNT-Ni). Then, the NCNT-Ni powder was uniformly dispersed in ultrapure water, followed by the addition of H2PtCl6. The target catalyst powder was then obtained through centrifugal drying after the reaction was complete (named NCNT-Ni/Pt).

Material characterizations

SEM images, EDX elemental data, and elemental mapping images were acquired at JSM-7800F (JEOL). The SEM images were captured at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV, while the EDX spectra and EDX mapping images were recorded at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV. TEM images and HRTEM images were obtained using Talos F200X G2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). AC-HAADF-STEM images were acquired with a Spectra300 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at an acceleration voltage of 300 kV. The EELS spectra and spectrum images were collected using a Gatan Continuum spectrometer. XRD was measured by Shimadzu XRD-6100 diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation. XPS for elemental analysis was performed on ESCALAB QXi (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The C 1 s peak at 284.4 eV was used as the reference for calibrating all spectra, with peak fitting and deconvolution performed using the Avantage software. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer, Avio500) was applied to measure the metal concentration of Pt. Ion chromatograph (IC, Metrohm, 930) was used to measure the concentration of Cl–. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area and pore structure of the catalyst were analyzed using N2 physisorption at 77 K with a Physical adsorption analyzer (Micromeritics, ASAP2460). The mass fraction of Ni nanoparticles was determined by a dimethylglyoxime (DMG) spectrophotometric method using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Gary 60). In brief, the catalyst was acid‑leached with HNO3 under controlled conditions, converting exposed metallic Ni species into Ni2+ in solution. The Ni2+ concentration in the filtrate was quantified by UV‑Vis spectroscopy using DMG complexation and a calibration curve based on standard Ni(NO3)2 solution. The in-situ DRIFTS measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 spectrometer (Photograph of the in-situ DRIFTS setup is provided in Supplementary Fig. S60). A confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw, InVia), coupled with a CHI660, was employed for in-situ electrochemical Raman spectroscopy with a 532 nm laser as the excitation source (Photograph of the in-situ Raman setup is provided in Supplementary Fig. S61). XAS measurements at beamline BL14W1, Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. The operando XAS experiment was carried out in a custom three-electrode cell with catalyst-coated carbon paper, graphite rod, and Ag/AgCl electrode as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. The electrolyte was a 0.5 M H2SO4 aqueous solution (Photograph of the operando XAS setup is provided in Supplementary Fig. S62). XANES spectra were collected at the OCP prior to the electrochemical tests. Then, chronoamperometry tests were conducted at different potentials from –0.1 to –0.2 V (vs. RHE) without iR compensation. At each potential, spectra were collected after reaching a stable electrochemical state. Finally, the XANES spectra were recorded 10 min after electrolysis. The Athena, Artemis, and HAMA Fortran software were used for data processing and analysis.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical experiments were carried out on a CHI 660E instrument. A three-electrode system was employed, consisting of a catalyst-loaded glassy carbon electrode (with a geometric area of 0.0706 cm2) as the working electrode, a carbon rod as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode. All measurements were performed in a single-compartment electrochemical cell with a volume of 150 mL (Photograph of the three-electrode cell set-up is provided in Supplementary Fig. S63). Prior to the electrochemical measurements, the Ag/AgCl reference electrode was calibrated against the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) in H2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 solution. A Pt wire was used as the working electrode, and cyclic voltammetry was performed at a scan rate of 1 mV s–1. The potential at which the current crossed zero (the average of the anodic and cathodic scans) was taken as the thermodynamic potential of the RHE. To prepare the catalyst ink, 5 mg of catalyst was dispersed in 1 mL of a mixture containing isopropanol, deionized water, and Nafion (25:25:2 by volume). The solution was sonicated for 30 min and stirred for 2 h to ensure uniform dispersion. Next, 5 µL of the ink was carefully applied to a freshly polished glassy-carbon electrode and left to dry at 25 °C. The catalyst loading (0.35 mg cm–2) is obtained by multiplying the volume of the slurry dropped by its concentration and then dividing by the electrode area. The mixture was treated under ultrasonic for 24 h to ensure complete dissolution of Pt species into the solution. All samples were measured in parallel with appropriate calibration standards. Since a glassy carbon electrode was used for the electrochemical tests, the electrodes were thoroughly cleaned with aqua regia after testing. The electrode surface recovered its original appearance, confirming that no catalyst residues remained. This procedure further verifies that the Pt loading values used for activity normalization originate solely from the catalyst and are not affected by residual deposits.

Computational methods

In this study, all first-principles calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)49 with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. The exchange-correlation interactions were described by the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)50, including the DFT-D3 dispersion correction51. A plane-wave basis with a 500-eV cutoff energy was employed, and a 15 Å vacuum layer was included for all surface models. Brillouin zone integration was carried out with a 2 × 2 × 1 Gamma-centered k-point mesh. Self-consistent calculations were converged to an energy threshold of 10–5 eV, and the equilibrium geometries were optimized with a maximum force criterion of 0.02 eV/Å. Life cycle assessment (LCA)

Given that the catalyst process is currently at a laboratory scale, the system boundary was defined as cradle-to-gate and modeled using a cut-off approach. Foreground data were derived from experimental measurements, while background data (e.g., electricity, water, and precursor synthesis) were obtained from the ecoinvent v3.9.1 database (Supplementary Tables S10–14). For the raw material acquisition stage, data on the synthesis of NCNT-Ni/Pt catalysts were obtained from laboratory experiments and scaled up proportionally. Data on iridium oxide and crystalline silicon PV production were derived from industry surveys and large-scale simulations. Calculations were conducted with CML-IA baseline V3.09 method, SimaPro software, 11 environmental impact categories were assessed.
