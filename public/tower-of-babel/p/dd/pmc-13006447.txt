An integrated framework for proactive deepfake mitigation via attention-driven watermarking and blockchain-based authenticity verification
Authors: Fahima Hajjej, Muhammad Hamid, Ala Saleh Alluhaidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid progress of deepfake generation technologies poses a growing threat to the credibility of digital video content. Most existing solutions rely on reactive detection methods, which struggle to generalize against continuously evolving generative models. To address this challenge, this paper proposes a proactive and verifiable deepfake mitigation framework that secures video content at the point of creation. The proposed system integrates three complementary components: (i) a spatio-temporal attention model based on EfficientNetV2B0 and a two-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to identify perceptually and temporally salient regions, (ii) an attention-guided Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) to embed an imperceptible yet robust watermark, and (iii) a blockchain-based ledger to provide immutable file-level integrity verification via cryptographic hashing. Experimental results show that the attention model achieves 97.20% accuracy, confirming its ability to learn discriminative spatio-temporal representations. The watermarking scheme preserves high visual quality, achieving an average PSNR of 33.67 dB and SSIM of 0.9838. The proposed framework detects 100% of face-swapping attacks generated using DeepFaceLab 2.0 and demonstrates robustness against common video degradations, including recompression (90%), scaling (89%), and blurring (98%). Performance degradation under severe Gaussian noise (80%) highlights the practical limits of the watermark under non-structural pixel perturbations. An ablation study further confirms that both attention guidance and temporal modeling are critical to achieving high robustness. Overall, this work shifts deepfake defense from reactive detection to proactive, content-aware, and verifiable protection.

Introduction

With the evolution of Artificial Intelligence (AI) culture, the deepfake technology has emerged, and making it harder to differentiate between original and fake information. The term deepfake is derived from the Deep Learning (DL) and fake. The term was coined on Reddit in late 2017, when users began to use DL to replace the face of one individual in pornographic video with the face of another individual18. A deepfake is synthetic media generated by DL algorithms that may convincingly alter the appearance of a person face, voice, or actions to make them appear to communicate or behave in a way that they never did18. Such manipulations are motivated by the advanced models, including auto-encoders and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)2,3. Although deepfakes may be used for entertainment, they are increasingly utilized in malicious activities, such as misinformation, identity fraud, political interference, and even compromising diplomatic or military activities4,5.

As a result of these threats, deepfake detection and prevention has become a major field of research. The initial efforts were made for reactive detection, in which models based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) were trained to identify manipulated media hidden artifacts. However, existing methods of detection are already in an ongoing arms race against more advanced generative models such as StyleGAN and DeepFaceLab6, which implies that purely reactive approaches cannot be sufficient over time, a fact that is commonly recognized by several recent studies7,8.

As a result, the focus of research shifted to proactive prevention, where protective mechanisms are implemented on authentic media at its source. The existing proactive mechanisms involve blockchain-based content authentication and DL-based watermarking9. But these methods tend to work independently and have severe limitations. Blockchain systems generally handle videos as black boxes, only ensuring file-level integrity, but not safeguarding the content of the video itself against subtle manipulations9. On the other hand, most of the watermarking methods lack robust content-aware embedding, and they are susceptible to removal, or other basic attacks such as cropping10. Moreover, there is still a major gap in the literature: most of the proactive frameworks are not strictly tested against the state-of-the-art deepfake generators, and their effectiveness in real-life applications remains unproven.

This paper will fill these gaps by proposing a unified framework that integrates three key components into a coherent workflow, namely, a robust spatio-temporal attention model to intelligently guide watermarking, a robust GAN-based watermarking system to ensure the safety of the material on the internal level, and a blockchain-based ledger to ensure the safety of the material on the external level. Combining these defenses, we have changed the paradigm of reactive detection to active and verifiable protection.

Related work

The rise of highly convincing deepfakes has created serious challenges for ensuring digital authenticity. Two main research directions have been followed: detection, where fake content is identified after it has been created, and prevention, where content is protected from being manipulated in the first place.

In the beginning, most studies focused on reactive detection. Advanced AI models were trained to recognize the small flaws left during the generation of fake media. For example, a Hybrid Optimized Deep Feature Fusion (HODFF) method was proposed by Jayashre et al.11where a bio-inspired optimizer was used to combine different features, resulting in more than 90% detection accuracy. However, this type of system was limited because it remained reactive. It represented a constant arms race in which detectors needed to adjust continuously to new and stronger generative models, while no way was provided to proactively confirm the originality of a video. A more advanced system was suggested by Heidari et al.8, where a blockchain-based federated learning framework was created. Capsule networks and SegCaps were used to learn from distributed data sources with maintenance of privacy and 6.6 improvement in accuracy compared to benchmarks was obtained. Although it was sophisticated, this work also remained in the reactive category, as it was only able to detect fake content once it was created, without providing protection of authentic content beforehand.

Due to these shortcomings, the researchers have been compelled to explore proactive prevention strategies, which involve securing content at the origin. One of the most important contributions was made by Noreen et al.12, where steganography GANs were employed to insert watermarks within video frames. The attention model guided the embedding process, and thus the watermark was based on the features of the video itself. One limitation of this design was that the attention mechanism was self-contained and did not have the more detailed spatio-temporal insight that could have been given by a video analysis model like a 3D CNN. Furthermore, Zhao et al.13proposed a more active strategy in which watermarks were encoded into face identity features. This was a very successful approach in countering face-swapping attacks, but it was not very protective since it concentrated on the facial identity and not the entire video.

Blockchain technology has been used as another proactive safeguard measure. Rashid et al.9described in a survey how smart contracts might be used to store video metadata on an immutable blockchain ledger, and thus maintain integrity and provenance. But these systems typically handled videos as black boxes, only paying attention to the fact that the entire file was tampered with, but not to the internal security of the video content itself. Further, Islam et al.14suggested a decentralized global copyright system developed on a consortium blockchain in which a global record of copyright was kept in an immutable form. This primarily focused on legal provenance and ownership, and not on video content protection against manipulation. Besides, Chan et al.15integrated temporal feature extraction using Multi-LSTM network with blockchain verification, which provided a stronger check of authenticity. However, their strategy was not synergistic, as feature extraction and blockchain recording were exploited as two distinct operations. To address these limitations, recent research has explored more advanced integrations. For instance, some frameworks now combine GAN-based watermarking with blockchain for secure traceability7, while others focus on privacy-preserving verification using federated learning to protect user data during the authentication process8. Concurrently, to address the critical issue of privacy, Ghani et al. [29] proposed a framework that uses GANs and blockchain not for tamper detection, but for creating and managing privacy-preserving synthetic facial image datasets. While these approaches are powerful for their specific applications (provenance and privacy), they often do not focus on providing a robust, content-aware internal protection mechanism for authentic video streams, which is the primary goal of our work. Furthermore, Luo et al.10suggested a reversible method of neural watermarking, in which high-capacity watermarks would be stored and then completely erased to recover the original image. The constraint was that the watermark was inserted without knowing the content of the video thus susceptible to attacks like cropping. Also, Wang et al.17also adopted GAN-based steganography model in order to conceal information in a statistically blind manner without caring about robustness, and the watermark was not as resilient to manipulation of deepfakes. In addition, Li, Wang, and Kumari17developed a blockchain-based structure to trace multimedia using cryptographic hashing. Although it was effective in proving file modifications, it was unable to withstand more subtle content-level manipulations.

From progression of research, it is noted that despite the strong detection, watermarking, and blockchain systems being developed, they are largely used independently19–21. Detection approaches are constrained by reactive characteristics, watermarking approaches typically do not provide strong content-based embedding, and blockchain solutions are typically not connected to internal video security22. Nevertheless, an unexplored gap in the literature is the inability to rigorously validate such proactive systems on powerful publicly available deepfake generators. Numerous studies present theoretical models but fail to compare them with the cutting-edge systems such as DeepFaceLab23–26. The gap is the biggest lack of synergistic framework that can combine these safeguards. This gap is bridged in this study by proposing a holistic framework in which deep spatio-temporal analysis with an efficient, state-of-the-art EfficientNetV2B0 and LSTM model will direct a robust GAN-based watermarking system and blockchain will provide verifiable integrity of the whole process.

Proposed methodology

To address the limitations of reactive deepfake detection approaches, this study proposes a proactive and verifiable framework that secures video content at the point of creation. The methodology follows a sequential pipeline in which spatio-temporal attention modeling guides intelligent watermark embedding, followed by immutable authenticity verification through blockchain registration. The core design principle of the framework is to tightly couple content-aware internal protection with external cryptographic verification, ensuring robustness, imperceptibility, and verifiability are achieved jointly rather than independently. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the proposed framework consists of four major stages: (i) spatio-temporal attention modeling, (ii) attention-guided GAN-based watermark embedding, (iii) deepfake prevention and (iv) blockchain-based integrity verification. Each stage builds upon the output of the previous stage, forming a coherent end-to-end pipeline for proactive deepfake mitigation. To operationalize this framework, a structured data preparation pipeline is first established. Authentic video data are collected, preprocessed, and partitioned into training, validation, and testing subsets to provide a consistent and unbiased foundation for all subsequent stages of the methodology. The processed frame sequences obtained through this pipeline serve as standardized inputs for spatio-temporal attention learning, GAN-based watermark embedding, and downstream authentication and verification modules. The detail of each step is defined below.

Data collection process

The UCF-101 action recognition dataset is employed to train and validate the proposed spatio-temporal attention model18. UCF-101 is a widely adopted benchmark comprising 13,320 real-world video clips across 101 action categories, collected from diverse online sources. The dataset exhibits significant variation in motion dynamics, facial appearances, illumination conditions, and background complexity, making it suitable for learning robust and generalizable spatio-temporal representations.

In the proposed framework, UCF-101 is used exclusively for learning attention patterns from authentic video content. This design choice ensures that the attention model captures natural spatial and temporal dependencies without being biased toward specific deepfake artifacts. Videos are partitioned into training and validation subsets following standard practice. Each video is uniformly sampled into fixed-length frame sequences to maintain temporal consistency across samples. All frames are resized to a standardized spatial resolution compatible with the backbone convolutional network.

Framework construction

Following the data preprocessing and partitioning, the next stage involves constructing the multi-component proactive defense framework. The proposed methodology follows a multi-stage framework designed for proactive prevention and powerful verification of digital video content. This framework is built in four synergistic stages each focusing on a vital aspect of the security pipeline. This starts with the building of a deep spatio-temporal attention model to understand the content of the video and followed by intelligently embedding an invisible watermark using a Generative Adversarial Network. The next steps will focus on the validation of the content by extracting watermarks and validation of the integrity of the whole video file with the help of blockchain technology. The detail of each of these phases is described below and architectural representation is shown in Fig. 1.

Stage I: Two-Stage Transfer Learning.

As the foundation of the framework, this component extracts deep spatio-temporal features from the input video. In the first phase of the proposed framework, an “attention model” was developed by conducting in-depth analysis of the video content as shown in Fig. 1. The main objective of this phase is to obtain a rich collection of features that reflects not only the objects in each frame but also the movement and temporal patterns across frames. This deep understanding is necessary for the watermarking phase since it enables the watermark to be embedded intelligently and robustly. To achieve this, a more efficient and powerful two-stage transfer learning approach is employed instead of training a deep 3D CNN from scratch.

A.Spatial Feature Extraction using EfficientNetV2B0.

A.Spatial Feature Extraction using EfficientNetV2B0.

Spatial Feature Extraction using EfficientNetV2B0.

To capture detailed frame-level spatial information, EfficientNetV2B0 is employed as the primary feature extractor. The process begins with spatial feature extraction at the frame level. For this task, a pre-trained 2D CNN, specifically EfficientNetV2B0 with weights from the ImageNet dataset, was utilized. The model was loaded without its top classification layers (i.e. include top=False) so that we could only use it as a feature extractor. At this point, the whole EfficientNetV2B0 model was frozen (non-trainable). This frozen backbone receives each of the 16 frames of a pre-processed video clip and generates a 1280-dimensional feature vector of the frame. This vector is a strong and concise numerical description of the visual content in that single frame. EfficientNetV2B0 backbone was chosen particularly due to its state-of-the-art balance between high accuracy and low-level computing efficiency. EfficientNetV2 also has significantly better performance over older architectures, such as ResNet or VGG, and requires fewer parameters. Although 3D CNNs can perform video analysis tasks with great performance, they are computationally very expensive to train and typically demand significantly larger datasets to be trained. The two-steps framework which employs a small but powerful 2D CNN to extract spatial features and a low-weighted LSTM to model time is a viable and potentially useful alternative that harnesses the potential of transfer learning without requiring expensive computational resources.

B.Temporal Feature Modeling using a Two-Layer LSTM Network.

B.Temporal Feature Modeling using a Two-Layer LSTM Network.

Temporal Feature Modeling using a Two-Layer LSTM Network.

Although spatial features characterize individual frames, it is also essential to model the temporal evolution, which drives the integration of an LSTM network. While the CNN offers a good spatial understanding of separate frames, video is essentially characterized by movement. In order to simulate this temporal change, the sequence of the 16 feature vectors (one per frame) extracted by EfficientNetV2B0 were fed to a two-layer LSTM network. The LSTM architecture was selected due to its capability of capturing long-range dependencies in sequence data which is necessary in order to model the intricate action dynamics of the video clips.

The network is made up of a first LSTM layer containing 512 units used to take the sequence of frame features and a second LSTM layer containing 256 units used to encode more abstract temporal patterns. The output of LSTM stack is passed through a Dense layer which has a ReLU activation function for feature integration before classification.

The whole trained network, which is the EfficientNet spatial extractor and the LSTM temporal model, is the attention model of the framework. The quality of differentiating fine-grained actions has enabled it to correctly point out the most significant spatio-temporal features of a video, the most vital information that should be used to intelligently control the watermarking process in the subsequent stage.

Spatio-Temporal Attention Model.

The output of the LSTM is used to generate spatio-temporal attention model, which encode the relative importance of spatial regions over time. These attention maps highlight areas where modifications are most likely to affect content authenticity. The generated attention maps form the final output of Phase I and are directly passed to the GAN-based watermarking stage. C.Watermark Generation using the GAN Generator.

C.Watermark Generation using the GAN Generator.

Watermark Generation using the GAN Generator.

The watermarking GAN consists of two competing neural networks: a Generator (G) and a Discriminator (D), trained in a minimax adversarial setting. The Generator receives a randomly sampled latent noise vector z and transforms it into a noise-like watermark pattern W. The Discriminator attempts to distinguish between original frames and frames containing the embedded watermark. Through this adversarial interaction, the Generator learns to synthesize watermark patterns that are statistically indistinguishable from the host frame texture, ensuring high perceptual transparency.

The architecture of the GAN is specifically designed to balance imperceptibility, robustness, and computational efficiency, as summarized below and in Table 2.

Depth and filter selection: Three Conv2DTranspose layers with filter sizes (128, 64, 3) are used to progressively upscale the latent representation. This depth is sufficient to capture complex noise distributions, making the watermark difficult to remove or analyze.Choice of 128 × 128 resolution: This resolution provides a practical trade-off between watermark entropy and training stability. Higher resolutions (e.g., 224 × 224) would significantly increase computational cost with marginal gains in watermark robustness.

The GAN was trained using a standard adversarial process to balance imperceptibility and robustness. The training is guided by a composite loss function for the Generator (G):

L_G = L_adv + λ * L_pixel.

This loss makes the watermark invisible by imposing penalties on the large visual variations.Embedding Strength (λ): The trade-off between robustness and imperceptibility is controlled by the hyperparameter λ. In our experiments, the value of λ was chosen empirically and was set to 0.7; this was because we needed to achieve a high visual quality (PSNR > 33 dB) and the watermark had to be powerful to be detected.

Stage 4: Blockchain-Based Integrity Verification.

The final phase of the framework corresponds to the Blockchain-Based Integrity Verification module, which operates as an external and immutable layer of authentication beyond the internal watermarking mechanism. While the watermark ensures content-level protection, blockchain provides file-level, tamper-proof evidence of authenticity. This phase guarantees long-term integrity by recording and verifying the cryptographic fingerprint of each video. The overall pipeline consists of sequential operations Create Block, Add Video Metadata, Retrieve Original Hash, Video Processing, Calculate New Hash, Compare Hash, and Final Verification Result each of which corresponds directly to the block labels in the Phase 4 diagram.

A.Create Block (Hash Generation using SHA-256).

A.Create Block (Hash Generation using SHA-256).

Create Block (Hash Generation using SHA-256).

Once the video has been fully secured through the attention-guided watermarking process in Phase 2, the final watermarked file undergoes SHA-256 hashing. This cryptographic function produces a fixed-length 32-byte digest that uniquely represents the entire video. Due to the avalanche effect and strong collision resistance properties of SHA-256, even a single-bit alteration in the video results in a completely different hash value. This digest acts as the video’s immutable digital fingerprint, ensuring that its originality can always be re-established.

B.Add Video Metadata (Blockchain Registration).

B.Add Video Metadata (Blockchain Registration).

Add Video Metadata (Blockchain Registration).

The generated hash, along with essential metadata such as the video’s unique identifier and timestamp, is written to the blockchain ledger as a new transaction. This registration step creates an immutable record of the video’s authenticated state at the time of creation. Because blockchain operates as an append-only, tamper-resistant structure, the stored hash cannot be modified or erased, making it a permanent proof of the video’s integrity. In our study, this process is implemented using a simulated blockchain environment to replicate real-world immutability while maintaining experimental control.

C.Retrieve Original Hash (Verification Request).

C.Retrieve Original Hash (Verification Request).

Retrieve Original Hash (Verification Request).

During verification, the user uploads a video file to the system, initiating the integrity check. The system uses the video’s identifier to retrieve the original, trusted hash stored on the blockchain during registration. This serves as the reference point for authenticity. Because the blockchain ledger ensures that historical records cannot be overwritten, the retrieved hash represents a guaranteed authentic version of the file.

D.Video Processing and Hash Recalculation.

D.Video Processing and Hash Recalculation.

Video Processing and Hash Recalculation.

After retrieving the registered hash, the system processes the newly submitted video by computing its SHA-256 hash locally. This recalculated hash captures the current state of the file exactly as the user provided it. Any modification whether through deepfake generation, frame editing, compression artifacts, or malicious tampering will inherently alter the cryptographic fingerprint. This step ensures that the integrity check is entirely deterministic and platform-independent, since any compliant SHA-256 implementation will always reproduce the same hash for an unmodified file.

E.Hash Comparison and Final Integrity Decision.

E.Hash Comparison and Final Integrity Decision.

Hash Comparison and Final Integrity Decision.

Authenticity is validated by comparing the newly computed hash with the original hash stored on the blockchain. If both values match, the system concludes that the video has remained untouched since its registration, confirming its authenticity with cryptographic certainty. When the two hashes differ, the mismatch provides irrefutable evidence that the video has been altered. Because deepfake manipulation inherently modifies pixel distributions and temporal consistency, such videos inevitably produce divergent hashes, leading the system to classify the file as manipulated.

F.Scalability and Privacy Considerations.

F.Scalability and Privacy Considerations.

Scalability and Privacy Considerations.

The blockchain module is designed for high scalability and efficient storage management. Only the 32-byte cryptographic hash is recorded on-chain, while the video itself remains off-chain, preventing storage overhead even for large datasets. This architecture supports millions of videos without performance degradation. Privacy is also preserved, as a hash does not reveal video content and only serves as a proof of existence. In scenarios involving sensitive material, the system can be deployed on private or consortium blockchains to restrict access to authorized actors, while ensuring compliance with data-protection regulations such as GDPR. The design remains fully compatible with public chains like Ethereum or private networks like Hyperledger Fabric, making the system versatile for real-world deployment.

Framework robustness against real-world deepfake attacks

A rigorous robustness test was performed to confirm the practical effectiveness of the proposed framework. A heterogenous collection of 50 high-definition video clips was selected in the public stock video libraries, Pexels and Pixabay, where the licenses allow editing and repurposing. The selection criteria provided content diversity, with different subject genders and ethnicities, and a variety of challenging real-life conditions, such as: variable lighting, moving head (stasis to action) and different subject angles (front, profile and three-quarter). Each video had the length of between 5 and 15 s to mimic a normal social media clip. The videos were initially watermarked with our attention-guided scheme and their authentic SHA-256 hash was recorded.

A complex face-swapping manipulation was then performed using the DeepFaceLab 2.0 industry-standard software on each of the watermarked videos. The standardization of the attack was as follows: the destination (target) was the watermarked video, the high quality SAEHD model was applied, and the training was performed with 50,000–100,000 iterations to produce a convincing 256 × 256 pixel face swap. Our two-layer verification system was used to test its ability in tamper detection by processing the resulting deepfake videos.

To demonstrate the mechanism of detection with clear and qualitative evidence, Fig. 12 visually shows the process of watermark destruction. The figure shows that the pixel manipulation in the DeepFaceLab attack permanently destroys the signal of the watermark embedded in the image, resulting in a corrupted extraction by the extractor and confirming the attack.

The performance of both the internal watermark extraction and the external blockchain hash comparison was recorded for every video. The verification results are determined by the output of our watermark extractor, which computes the Bit Error Rate (BER) between the anticipated watermark and the extracted data. As video files typically undergo routine transformations, further tests evaluated resilience under common degradations. In order to evaluate the resilience of the watermark against attacks that are not specific to deepfake, we performed another experiment against a collection of more typical video degradation attacks. The attacks simulate the distortions which might happen in the lifecycle of a video (e.g., sharing on social media) and might unintentionally compromise or delete weak watermarks. This test was carried out on a subset of 10 watermarked videos. The protocol of each attack is as follows.

Recompression: In this attack, the quality of a video is decreased by re-encoding, which often happens when posting it to an online platform. All videos were resized by half (e.g. 1280 × 720 to 640 × 360 pixels) and then resized again to their original size (using bicubic interpolation with OpenCV). It causes a loss of high-frequency information in which the watermark tends to be concealed.Gaussian Blurring: The attack simulates a loss of focus or intentional blurring. OpenCV was used to apply a 3 × 3 Gaussian blur filter to each frame of the videos with a sigma of 1.5. The attack directly targets finer details of the image.Gaussian Noise: This is an attack that tries to replicate sensor noise or transmission errors, by introducing random variation of intensities. OpenCV was used to add zero-mean Gaussian noise of a standard deviation (sigma) of 15 to the pixel values of each frame.

Ablation study

To quantify the contribution of individual components, an ablation study was conducted under identical attack conditions. All the ablated configurations were exposed to the same DeepfaceLab attack scene to give a fair comparison on its detection strength. These findings, which are summarized in Table 8, are strong indicators of our synergistic design selections.

The critical role of attention guidance (Ablation A)

The major finding of this research is the essential part of our attention-guidance mechanism. In Ablation A, the watermark was incorporated by a blindly uniform approach to the entire frame eliminating the intelligence of our spatio-temporal model. This led to a drastic reduction in deepfake detection rate by 100% to 75%. This reduction of performance is due to the fact that a uniform watermark is predictable. These areas can be more easily manipulated by a deepfake algorithm, which essentially re-creates areas of the faces, without entirely destroying an uniformly distributed signal. Conversely, our attention-based strategy puts the energy of the watermark on places of complexity and movement, which in fact are most likely to be perturbed by a face-swap. This experiment empirically confirms that the location of watermark is equally important as that of watermark, which confirms our hypothesis.

The contribution of temporal features (Ablation B)

In Ablation B we separated the role of temporal modeling by decoupling the LSTM layers and only using guidance with spatial features of the EfficientNetV2B0 backbone. Although the performance was still high, the detection rate dropped to 90%. This minor yet significant drop proves the worthiness of considering the time dynamics. A spatial-only attention model is able to detect complex texture and edges in a single frame, but motion is not seen. With the LSTM, our model would be able to recognize and rank areas with complicated time dynamics (e.g., subtle facial expressions, hair movement). These regions are also vulnerable and sensitive to the time discrepancies that are frequently injected by deepfake algorithms.

This finding proves that the combination of the spatio-temporal features offers a stronger and more detailed guidance map, compared to the use of spatial features only. The ablation study proves that the ideal detection rate of our suggested framework is not by chance, but it is a direct outcome of the synergistic design. Intensive spatio-temporal attention (to identify optimal hiding locations) combined with a powerful GAN embedder (to produce a complex signal) ends in a watermark that is very sensitive to the exact alterations of the deepfake generators of the current state-of-the-art.

This empirically validates our central hypothesis: that the feature-learning capability demonstrated by high accuracy on an action recognition task successfully transfers to the task of identifying optimal, robust locations for watermark embedding, a concept we term ‘transfer of saliency’. The ablation study also serves as a failure-case analysis. The drop in detection rate to 85% when attention guidance is removed (Ablation A) demonstrates a key failure mode: the framework is less effective if the watermark is not intelligently placed. A uniform watermark can be partially preserved during a face-swap, allowing some deepfakes to bypass detection.

Table 9presents a qualitative comparison of our framework with a number of state of the art proactive defense schemes. Although these approaches have improved the discipline, our synergistic approach responds to some of their fundamental weaknesses. To provide an example, although our work is similar to Noreen et al.10in the sense that it relies on an attention-guided GAN, the source of this guidance is our strength. Our deep time-varying model (EfficientNetV2B0 + LSTM) is a powerful spatial-temporal model which provides a better basis of embedding watermarks as opposed to an independent, image-only attention model. Likewise, other approaches such as Zhao et al.11target the watermarking of individual facial features. We have an advantage in that we offer holistic protection. The strength of our watermark is that it is a non-reversible, strong watermark specifically designed for tamper detection rather than removal. Lastly, in comparison with provenance-based systems such as Wang et al.15, the two-layer verification mechanism is the clear benefit of our framework. Integration of the blockchain layer offers a decentralized, immutable and publicly verifiable record of the original state of a video a bigger guarantee of authenticity than watermarking itself. This unified design, which is tested against both the state-of-the-art attacks of deepfakes and everyday degradations, is a major leap in comprehensive and realistic solution to proactive deepfake prevention.

Conclusion and future work

This paper presented a proactive deepfake mitigation framework that integrates spatio-temporal attention modeling, GAN-based intelligent watermarking, and blockchain-based integrity verification. Unlike conventional reactive detection approaches, the proposed system secures video content at its source and enables reliable authenticity verification throughout its lifecycle. Experimental results demonstrate that the EfficientNetV2B0–LSTM attention model effectively captures discriminative spatio-temporal features, achieving 97.20% accuracy on the UCF-101 dataset, and provides meaningful guidance for robust watermark embedding. The attention-guided GAN watermark preserves high perceptual quality achieving an average PSNR of 33.67 dB and SSIM of 0.9838, while offering strong resistance to real-world threats, achieving a 100% detection rate against DeepFaceLab-generated face-swapping attacks and have high robustness against common video degradations, including recompression (90%), scaling (89%), and blurring (98%). Reduced performance under extreme Gaussian noise conditions highlights the realistic operational limits of the proposed watermarking strategy. The addition of blockchain-based hash verification further strengthens the framework by providing immutable, file-level proof of authenticity. An ablation study confirms that watermark robustness arises from the synergistic integration of attention-driven spatial and temporal features, as removal of attention guidance or temporal modeling significantly degrades detection performance. Future work will focus on large-scale evaluation across additional benchmarks, extending robustness to more advanced generative attacks, and improving resilience under extreme noise while optimizing computational efficiency for real-time deployment.
