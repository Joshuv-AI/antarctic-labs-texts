Do more postoperative X-rays improve patient perception following total knee arthroplasty? A prospective randomized study
Authors: A. Coelho, N. Carbó, C. Yela-Verdú, S. Gil-González, J. M. Peñalver, D. Martí, M. Viñas-Noguera, X. Pelfort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of two versus five postoperative X-ray protocols on patient perception following total knee arthroplasty (TKA), specifically assessing functional outcomes, quality of life, patient satisfaction and complication detection.MethodsIn this prospective randomized study, 206 patients who underwent TKA were assigned to one of two postoperative radiograph protocols. Group 1 (n = 102) received five X-rays at predefined intervals (immediately postoperative, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year), while Group 2 (n = 104) received only two X-rays (immediately postoperative and at 1 year). Functional outcomes were evaluated using the Knee Society Score (KSS) and Short Form-12 (SF-12). Satisfaction and complications were recorded over a 1-year follow-up period.ResultsAt the 1-year follow-up, KSS-Function scores were comparable between groups (76.07 vs. 74.32, p-value = 0.234), while KSS-Knee scores were slightly higher in Group 2 (85.44 vs. 89.3, p-value = 0.003). Regarding quality of life, SF-12 physical scores were similar (44.01 vs. 43.64, p-value = 0.628), whereas SF-12 mental scores were higher in Group 2 (56.72 vs. 58.57, p-value = 0.048). Mean satisfaction scores were comparable between groups (7.49 vs. 7.99, p = 0.08). No significant differences in complication rates were observed between groups (5 vs. 2, p-value = 0.458).ConclusionIncreasing the number of postoperative X-rays after TKA did not improve patient satisfaction or perception of recovery. Patients who received only two X-rays reported similar or slightly better functional outcomes, mental well-being, and satisfaction levels compared to those who received five. Additionally, no benefit was observed in complication detection. These findings suggest that a more limited radiographic follow-up may be sufficient, reducing costs and radiation exposure without compromising patient confidence or clinical outcomes.

Introduction

TKA is a highly effective surgical procedure for alleviating pain and restoring function in patients with severe knee osteoarthritis. Advances in surgical techniques and implant designs have led to increasingly successful outcomes, improving patients’ quality of life [1]. The survival rate of primary knee prosthetic surgery is estimated to be 82% after 25 years, according to various national TKA registries [2, 3]. As the number of TKAs performed globally continues to rise, optimizing postoperative care and follow-up protocols is crucial to ensure the best patient outcomes while minimizing unnecessary interventions [4].

Patient perception of recovery and surgical success is a crucial factor in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) outcomes and how patients perceive their progress can significantly impact their overall satisfaction and confidence in the procedure [5]. Postoperative follow-up protocols, including the number of radiographic assessments, may influence patient perception by either providing reassurance or causing unnecessary concern [6].

Postoperative radiographs play a pivotal role in the follow-up of TKA, allowing clinicians to monitor the position and alignment of prosthetic components, assess bone integration, and detect early signs of complications such as loosening, infection, or periprosthetic fractures [7, 8]. Routine radiographic evaluations are commonly performed to confirm that the implant is functioning correctly and to identify any issues that may require intervention.

Despite the established importance of radiographic follow-up, the optimal frequency and number of postoperative X-rays remain a subject of debate, particularly within the first postoperative year [9]. Some protocols advocate for a limited number of X-rays, while others recommend more frequent imaging [10]. Beyond the standard immediate postoperative radiograph, two to five additional X-rays are typically performed during the first year [11]. However, the necessity of multiple X-rays must be weighed against their associated costs, patient radiation exposure, and potential influence on patient perception of their recovery [12]. Some patients may feel reassured by frequent imaging, while others may view it as unnecessary or a source of anxiety. Given the high prevalence of TKA and the long survival period of modern implants, it is important to determine the real value of routine radiographs in the first year of postoperative follow-up.

The objective of this study was to evaluate whether reducing postoperative radiographs from five to two (immediate postoperative and one-year follow-up) affects patient perception of their recovery, as assessed through satisfaction, functional and clinical outcomes and quality-of-life scores 1-year after TKA. Additionally, we investigated whether different radiographic protocols influenced complication detection rates.

Materials and methods

A prospective randomized clinical trial was conducted including patients who had undergone a primary TKA, from February 2022 until May 2023, in the same center and were operated by the same surgical team. All patients gave informed consent prior to participating in this investigation. Furthermore, approval was obtained from the institutional review board (20223006) before beginning the investigation. The study was also registered at Clinical Trials under the identifier NCT05944679.

The inclusion criteria consisted of patients with diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis who had undergone primary TKA and were, at least, 18 years old. The exclusion criteria included any patient who underwent TKA following non-osteoarthritic process, such as fractures; patients who underwent partial knee replacement or patients with cognitive disorders that might limit comprehension and the ability to properly assess postoperative scores as well as the ability to modify their lifestyle. Patients who underwent contralateral primary TKA during the follow-up period were also excluded as well as patients who did not accept entering the study.

In the preoperative period, patients were included in the study and, after signing the informed consent, demographic data as well as functional scores - Knee Society Score (KSS) and Short-Form 12 (SF-12) were collected.

TKA was performed using a medial parapatellar approach with cemented fixation of the implants. All procedures were carried out using conventional instrumentation; no navigation or robotic assistance was employed. A mechanical alignment strategy was applied consistently across all surgeries. Patellar resurfacing was performed based on the operating surgeon’s preference. The same antibiotic prophylaxis protocol was used (cefuroxime, or vancomycin in case of penicillin allergy), and a standardized postoperative rehabilitation program was followed by all patients. A total of five orthopedic surgeons participated in the procedures. Postoperative anteroposterior, lateral and axial knee x-rays were performed to all patients.

The randomization was carried out using a simple randomization process, through the “sealed envelope” software, which generated a numbered list that randomized each patient to one of the study branches. Allocation was concealed using opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after patient enrollment. Patients were recruited during the presurgical visit, where they received verbal and written information about the study and provided written informed consent. After enrollment, patients were assigned to either Group 1, which received five postoperative X-rays (immediately postoperative, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months), or Group 2, which received two postoperative X-rays (immediately postoperative and 12 months). All patients attended follow-up visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Functional outcomes (Knee Society Score and SF-12) were recorded at the 6- and 12-month follow-up visits by an independent evaluator (M. V-N.), who was not involved in the surgical procedures and was blinded to patient group allocation throughout the study. Patient satisfaction was assessed at the 12-month follow-up using a numerical rating scale from 0 (worst outcome) to 10 (best outcome).

If a postoperative complication occurred, it was recorded, and the patient was withdrawn from the standardized imaging protocol. In such cases, additional radiographic examinations were performed as clinically indicated, based on the nature of the complication. These patients remained in the study and were included in the final analysis according to the intention-to-treat principle.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographics, quality of life and knee function. Quantitative variables were described through mean and standard deviation. Qualitative variables were described through frequencies table (number and percentage). Differences among weight groups were checked through Chi-square test for categorical variables and T-test analysis (normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney test (non-parametric values) for quantitative variables. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) through RStudio (v.2024.04.2 Build 764). Results were considered as statistically significant at p-value < 0.05.

A difference of 10 points was considered clinically relevant, in accordance with previously published minimal clinically important difference values [13]. Assuming a standard deviation derived from prior literature, a statistical power of 80%, and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, the required sample size was calculated. The final cohort of 198 patients provided adequate power for the primary outcome. This information has been added to the revised manuscript.

Results

A total of 210 patients were initially enrolled in the study. Four patients were lost to follow-up, resulting in a final sample of 206 patients (Fig. 1). The cohort included 140 women (67.9%) and 66 men (32.1%), with a mean age of 73.4 years (SD 6.3). There were no significant differences between groups in preoperative demographics or comorbidities (Table 1).

Functional outcomes

KSS scores showed significant improvement 12 months after TKA compared to preoperative values in both groups (Table 2). At the 6-month follow-up, there were no significant differences in KSS-Knee or KSS-Function scores between groups. By 12 months, KSS-Knee scores were slightly higher in Group 2 (89.37 vs. 85.44), whereas KSS-Function scores were marginally higher in Group 1 (76.07 vs. 74.32).

Quality of life

Both physical and mental components of the SF-12 improved after surgery compared to baseline values (Table 3). At the final follow-up, SF-12 Physical scores were comparable between groups (Group 1: 44.01, Group 2: 43.64). However, Group 2 reported higher SF-12 Mental scores (58.57 vs. 56.72), indicating better mental well-being.

Patient satisfaction

At 12 months, Group 2 (two X-rays) reported higher mean satisfaction scores compared to Group 1 (five X-rays): 7.99 vs. 7.49, respectively. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07), it suggests a trend toward greater satisfaction with fewer radiographic controls.

Complications

No significant difference was observed in complication rates between the two groups (Group 1: 5 complications, Group 2: 2 complications; p = 0.458). In Group 1, the complications included one fat embolism, one septic prosthetic joint loosening, two acute infections requiring debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR), and one case of knee stiffness managed with manipulation under anesthesia. In Group 2, complications included one case of chronic pain and one acute infection treated with DAIR. No cases of implant revision or periprosthetic fracture were reported in either group.

Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that performing additional radiographs at various follow-up intervals (postoperative, 1 month, 3 months 6 months and 1 year after TKA) was not associated with improved satisfaction, functional outcomes, quality of life or an increased detection of complications compared to a more limited follow-up protocol of just two radiographs (postoperative and 1 year). This finding suggests that regular radiographic evaluations may not offer substantial clinical benefit in routine cases where patients are recovering well after surgery. Given the lack of demonstrable advantage in terms of patient outcomes or complication detection, reducing the frequency of X-rays could streamline follow-up care while minimizing risks to patients, its exposure to radiation and lowering the overall economic burden as well as alleviating the pressure on radiology services. Thus, the present study corroborates evidence suggesting that there is no significant difference in functional outcomes or complication detection between patients who undergo just two radiographs and those who receive a more intensive follow-up schedule of five X-rays [7, 12, 14, 15].

There is considerable variability in radiographic protocols for postoperative monitoring after TKA with no clear consensus on the optimal frequency of imaging, with several studies raising concerns about the usefulness of routinely obtaining post-operative radiographs shortly after TKA. Moskal et al. reported that delaying post-operative radiographs after TKA until the first outpatient appointment had no adverse effects at an average follow-up of 4.3 years [16]. Similarly, Lee et al. [17] found that radiographs taken upon admission to acute rehabilitation facilities after THA or TKA identified abnormal findings in only eight of 592 patients, in which neither of these abnormal findings altered or interfered with the rehabilitation plan and follow-up. In a study conducted by Niskanen, limiting the number of x-rays during the follow-up did not impair the postoperative process nor the detection of complications [18]. Other different studies have also demonstrated that radiographic abnormalities are rarely found on early postoperative radiographs and, when present, generally do not result in any change in clinical management [19, 20].

Regarding patient satisfaction, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups. Nevertheless, a trend favoring the 2 X-ray protocol was noted, with slightly higher satisfaction scores in this group (7.99 vs. 7.49, p = 0.07). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, it may suggest a patient preference for a simpler and less burdensome follow-up regimen. Similar findings have been reported in telemedicine and streamlined follow-up studies, where reduced travel requirements and fewer clinic visits were associated with improved patient experience and satisfaction [21, 22]. Furthermore, a large multicenter study including more than 86,000 patients demonstrated that postoperative satisfaction after TKA is primarily driven by functional recovery and the fulfillment of patient expectations, rather than by specific perioperative processes such as imaging frequency [23]. Therefore, even in the absence of clinical differences, perceived convenience and reduced burden may positively shape satisfaction.

According to the results of this study, patients who followed the 2 X-ray protocol demonstrated a statistically higher KSS-Knee at 1 year compared to those in the 5 X-ray protocol group (89.3 vs. 85.4, p-value = 0.00336). However, despite the statistical differences, we believe that these findings lack significant clinical relevance and are not perceived by the patients. Also, the KSS-Function values were comparable between groups (74.32 vs. 76.07, p-value = 0.234). The observed difference in KSS scores likely reflects normal variations in patient recovery rather than the influence of the imaging schedule itself, especially given that both groups followed similar surgical and postoperative protocols. As reported by Lee et al., the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the Knee Society Score after TKA ranges between 5.3 and 5.9 points for KSS-Knee and 6.1–6.4 points for KSS-Function [24]. Lizaur-Utrilla et al. further reported MCID values of 9 points for KSS-Knee and 10 points for KSS-Function, with substantial clinical benefit requiring improvements of approximately 40 points for both domains [13]. In the present study, the observed differences between groups (1.75 points for KSS-Knee and 3.9 points for KSS-Function) fall below these thresholds, reinforcing that the statistically significant findings are unlikely to be perceived as clinically meaningful by patients.

In the present study, quality of life was assessed using the SF-12, a notable difference from previous studies comparing radiograph protocols, which primarily focused on functional scores and complications. Our findings showed significant improvements in both physical and mental quality of life scores after TKA compared to the preoperative period. The improvement in quality of life after TKA aligns with the findings from Shan et al. who reported substantial improvements in both mental and physical components of the SF-12 after TKA [25]. At the final follow-up, both groups exhibited comparable SF-12 Physical scores while Group 2 presented slightly higher SF-12 Mental scores (56.72 vs. 58.57, p-value = 0.048). Clement et al. reported that the MCID in SF-12 1 year after TKA is 1.8 for physical score and 1.5 for mental score [26]. Unlike the rest of the variables described previously, in this case there is a slight, though relevant, clinical difference, with patients who followed a 2-radiograph protocol presenting better results. Nonetheless, we believe that this difference is minimal, potentially negligible, and very close to the threshold of both statistical and clinical significance. Moreover, if these differences were real, it would favor the 2-radiograph protocol, further supporting our hypothesis that reducing the number of radiographs does not negatively impact quality of life.

Concerning complications, the incidence was comparable between the two groups, with no significant differences observed. Group 1 (5 X-rays) recorded five complications: one case of fat embolism, one septic prosthetic joint loosening, two acute infections, and one knee stiffness. Group 2 (2 X-rays) had two complications: one case of chronic pain and one acute infection. Despite a slightly higher, though non-significant, incidence of complications in Group 1, increasing the frequency of postoperative X-rays would not increment the detection or prevent complications, as their diagnosis relies primarily on clinical assessment rather than imaging. This finding is consistent with previous studies, such as Kumar et al. which showed that additional X-rays rarely lead to the discovery of new complications if patients are asymptomatic postoperatively [27]. Additionally, Birir et al. found low rates of positive postoperative radiographic findings and few subsequent changes in management over a five-year follow-up period in over 1000 patients, leading to considerable healthcare costs. Of a total of 3831 postoperative radiographs in 1258, only 13 radiographs (0.3%) yielded any relevant findings [28].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the sample size, while sufficient for the primary outcome, may have limited the ability to detect smaller between-group differences in functional outcomes or complications. A larger cohort could provide more definitive conclusions, especially regarding low-frequency events. Second, the 1-year follow-up period, although appropriate for early postoperative evaluation, may not be sufficient to identify late complications such as aseptic loosening, mechanical failure, or chronic infections, which can emerge years after TKA. A longer follow-up would offer a more complete understanding of the long-term implications of different radiographic protocols. Lastly, the study focused on radiographic findings and standardized functional scores. It did not assess other potentially relevant aspects such as soft tissue pathology, pain syndromes, or patient-reported symptoms not captured by the selected instruments. These areas could be explored in future studies to complement the present findings.

Conclusion

This study suggests that performing additional radiographs at 1, 3, and 6 months after total knee arthroplasty does not improve patient satisfaction, functional outcomes, quality of life, or complication detection compared to a simplified protocol with only two radiographs (immediately postoperative and at 1 year). These findings support the safety and practicality of a more conservative, patient-centered radiographic follow-up strategy in routine TKA cases, within the context of a 1-year follow-up period.
