Do ChatGPT and Gemini provide accurate answers to patient questions about glenohumeral arthritis?
Authors: Suzanna M Ohlsen, Sarah B Pirkle, JaeWon Yang, Albert O Gee, Jason E Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMachine learning, a subset of artificial intelligence, enables large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT and Gemini to analyze data, learn from context, and generate responses without explicit programming. While machine learning and LLMs show promise in orthopedic image analysis and patient education, it is unclear whether LLMs provide accurate responses to patient queries.MethodsOur purpose was to evaluate the alignment of ChatGPT and Gemini’s recommendations for managing glenohumeral arthritis with the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs). Both ChatGPT and Gemini were asked questions based on strong- or moderate-strength recommendations from the AAOS CPG on management of glenohumeral arthritis. Responses were classified by 2 independent reviewers as “agree,” “neutral,” or “disagree” based on their alignment with the CPG recommendations. A Cohen's kappa coefficient was used to assess interobserver reliability, and Fisher's exact test was used to compare the accuracy of responses between LLMs.ResultsOf the 12 recommendations of strong- or moderate-strength evidence, ChatGPT and Gemini provided responses that were in agreement for 6 (50%) and 9 (75%) of recommendations, respectively. There were no significant differences in performance between ChatGPT and Gemini. ChatGPT provided 6 study references, of which 4 were nonexistent or inappropriately cited. Gemini provided 32 study references, of which 5 studies were nonexistent or inappropriately cited.ConclusionBoth ChatGPT and Gemini do not consistently provide responses that align with the AAOS CPG for management of glenohumeral arthritis. Responses frequently included confabulated details or inappropriately referenced articles. Consequently, physicians and patients should use these artificial intelligence platforms with caution when seeking advice on management of glenohumeral arthritis.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Shoulder replacement, Shoulder arthroplasty, Clinical practice guidelines, Glenohumeral arthritis, Shoulder arthritis

Machine learning (ML) is a subset of artificial intelligence (AI) that uses algorithms to analyze and learn from large amounts of data without explicit programming from humans, so that these ML models may adapt to new information and provide more nuanced information over time instead of relying on strict, rule-based programming to provide answers to queries.1,4,22 ML technology is utilized in large language models (LLMs) such as Chat Generative Pretrained Transformer (ChatGPT) by OpenAI (San Francisco, CA) and Gemini by Google (Mountain View, CA), which employ “chain of thought” processing, which allows these AI models to carry on human-like thought processes in decision-making, utilizing context clues.12 ML and LLMs are useful tools within the field of orthopedic surgery for providers and patients alike, showing promise in terms of identifying and classifying fractures and dislocations on imaging and in the creation of patient-friendly informational materials.8,9,14,20 As roughly 80% of patients perform an internet search for health-related questions, it is imperative that healthcare providers understand the clinical accuracy of LLMs and other online resources.5,16 Recent literature has suggested that ChatGPT may serve as a useful adjunct for patient education regarding shoulder arthroplasty in terms of ChatGPT's ability to answer frequently asked questions.7,17,19,21 However, it remains unclear whether LLMs are able to provide patient education that is aligned with the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs). The purpose of this study is to evaluate the quality of recommendations provided by ChatGPT and Gemini regarding the management of glenohumeral arthritis by comparing each LLM's responses to the recommendations of the AAOS CPGs to determine whether these LLMs may serve as valid resources for patients seeking answers to questions about glenohumeral arthritis.

Methods

We did not require institutional review board approval to conduct this study. We reviewed the AAOS CPGs for glenohumeral arthritis, which are evidence-based recommendations formed by a committee of specialists after thorough review of literature regarding management of this pathology.2 These recommendations are meant to guide clinical decision-making and identify areas where further investigation is warranted. Each recommendation was labeled as “strong,” “moderate,” “limited,” or “consensus” in terms of the strength of the recommendation, based on the level of evidence of the literature review for each CPG statement. We included CPG recommendations that were of either “strong” or “moderate” statement strength, excluding those with “limited” strength or those which were “consensus” statements, given the lower level of evidence in the supporting literature. Within the glenohumeral arthritis AAOS CPG, there were 12 recommendations that met inclusion criteria, 6 of “strong” evidence and 6 of “moderate” evidence. Each of these CPG recommendations was rephrased in a nonbiased way that a patient may submit as a query to ChatGPT or Gemini. Each phrase was then submitted as a query to ChatGPT (model GPT-4o) and Gemini (model Gemini 2.5 Pro) on the same day (April 30, 2025), and each LLM response was recorded. Each query was submitted only once—there was no training, fine-tuning, reward modeling, or reinforcement learning with human feedback incorporated. Two authors (S.M.O. and S.B.P) then independently reviewed each response from both ChatGPT and Gemini and classified the responses as “agree,” “disagree,” or “neutral.” When there was discordance between the classifications of the 2 reviewers, a third independent reviewer provided a tiebreaker (J.Y.). Of note, ChatGPT and Gemini were not provided a statement indicating that their performance was being evaluated by comparing their responses to the AAOS CPGs. Fisher's exact test was used to determine the proportion of LLM responses which were categorized as “agree,”, “disagree,” or “neutral” compared to the AAOS CPG. We calculated a Cohen's kappa coefficient to evaluate interobserver reliability.10

Results

Of the 12 recommendations of strong or moderate strength that were queried, ChatGPT provided responses that agreed with the glenohumeral arthritis AAOS CPGs for 6 (50%) of recommendations, while Gemini provided responses that agreed with 9 (75%) of recommendations (Tables I and II). ChatGPT and Gemini provided responses that disagreed with the glenohumeral arthritis CPGs for 5 (42%) and 1 (8%) of recommendations, respectively. Neutral responses were provided to 1 (8%) ChatGPT query and 2 (17%) Gemini queries. The Cohen's kappa coefficient was 0.84 between the raters, signifying “near-perfect” agreement. We found no difference in the overall concordance of ChatGPT and Gemini responses to queries (P = .20); ChatGPT did provide answers that disagreed with AAOS CPG recommendations more frequently than Gemini, but the difference was not significant (5 versus 1; P = .16). While no responses provided by Gemini were in disagreement with any of the strong recommendations provided by the AAOS CPG for glenohumeral arthritis (in fact, Gemini responses were congruent for 5 of 6 queries, 83%), ChatGPT provided answers that were in disagreement with the AAOS guidelines for 3 of the 6 (50%) strong recommendations, representing a worse performance of ChatGPT when queried regarding strong recommendations, though this was not statistically significant (P = .18).

Gemini provided journal references for all 12 (100%) queries. Within its responses, 32 PubMed articles were cited, with links provided. Of these, there were 5 (16%) articles that were inappropriately cited (Tables III and IV). Two (6%) of these articles compared incorrect surgeries (one article evaluated arthroscopic rotator cuff and biceps tenodesis procedures, and another article compared reverse versus anatomic shoulder arthroplasty when the query specifically asked about anatomic versus hemiarthroplasty) yet were inaccurately cited as evidence to support the claims made in response to glenohumeral arthritis recommendations. Three (9%) articles that were included did not support the statement being made by Gemini and sometimes were in complete contradiction. The remaining 27 references were appropriately supportive of the data or claims made by Gemini in each respective response. ChatGPT provided journal references for 4 (33%) queries and provided no references for the remaining queries, referencing in total 6 articles. Within its responses, ChatGPT provided references to nonexistent studies in 3 (50%) of cases, and when asked for the link, responded with an apology and a statement that the article could not be found. A fourth reference was incorrectly cited as being published in “Bone & Joint Journal” when the referenced article had been published in the Journal of Orthopaedics. Only 2 (33%) of the citations provided by ChatGPT led to articles that were both appropriately referenced and provided data supporting the claim made in the LLM's response. Of the articles referenced in the AAOS CPG for management of glenohumeral arthritis, only one of them was included among the 32 articles referenced by Gemini. ChatGPT provided no references from the AAOS CPG for glenohumeral arthritis in its responses.

Discussion

In general, it seems that ChatGPT is capable of supplying satisfactory answers to frequently asked patient questions about shoulder arthroplasty—though often with ambiguity and requiring clarification—thus serving as a reasonable adjunct for patient education in the setting of orthopedic consultation, but not as an adequate stand-alone resource.7,17,19,21 A recent study evaluated the concordance of ChatGPT and Gemini and included consensus statements, which were statements with no evidence to support them or conflicting evidence—because of the lack of evidence to support these consensus statements, we opted to exclude consensus statements from our analysis, as we felt that inclusion of these lower-strength of evidence recommendations would lead to an unfair assessment of Gemini and ChatGPT's ability to provide reliable references.11 Our study demonstrated that 6 (50%) and 9 (75%) of ChatGPT and Gemini responses were congruent with the AAOS evidence-based CPGs for management of glenohumeral arthritis and showed that ChatGPT and Gemini both provided resources which were sometimes confabulated or did not appropriately support the claims of each LLM. When considering only guidelines of strong evidence, ChatGPT significantly underperformed compared to Gemini, disagreeing with AAOS guidelines in 50% of cases (compared to 0 instances of disagreement in Gemini responses; P = .18). This is a concerning level of incongruence and could pose risks to patient decision-making and their confidence in their surgeons, who are more likely to follow evidence-based medicine. Similar studies have been compared LLM chatbots' responses to patient queries to the AAOS CPGs for hip and knee arthroplasty, lower extremity sports pathologies, and for pediatric orthopedic surgery, and the authors found that answers were congruent 60-82% of the time.13,14,20 These studies similarly highlighted concerns with the accuracy of responses and reference transparency. Despite the wide use and rapid evolution of ChatGPT and Gemini, these LLMs still provide answers to medical queries that are incongruent with standard CPGs with concerning frequency.

Another limitation of LLMs is their tendency to confabulate, or “hallucinate” content, confidently providing information or sources that are entirely fabricated.3,6,15 While LLMs often provide a disclaimer about errors in content, without a meticulous review of resources, it would be easy to assume the validity of hallucinated statements or resources, and certainly has ramifications if an unassuming patient were to encounter false information. Previous literature has postulated that online health queries tend to evoke anxiety in patients but also can empower patients by providing them an enhanced understanding of their injury or pathology.18 Consuming confabulated materials from an LLM would lead to the opposite if misinformed patients were to then seek the advice of a human orthopedic consultant who may provide recommendations that are in contradiction to what the patient had received from the LLM, ultimately damaging the patient-physician relationship or fostering mistrust. In our study, we found that 4 (67%) of ChatGPT references and 5 (16%) of Gemini references were confabulated, inaccurately cited, or did not support the claims made by each LLM. Clinicians who consume content from LLMs should confirm the validity of provided references, as there is an unacceptably high frequency of errors in citations provided by these AI models.

Limitations of our study include the naturally subjective nature of reviewer evaluation of ChatGPT’s and Gemini's responses—we utilized 2 independent reviewers and calculated a Cohen's kappa coefficient to determine that we had “near perfect” interobserver reliability. Another limitation is that LLMs are designed to engage in “chain of thought” discussions with the user, and since we submitted all queries on one single day as an anonymous user, there is a chance that later responses were shaped by former responses to queries, and ChatGPT and Gemini may have been able to predict that the query submitter would request links to all reference articles, which could alter the manner in which the LLMs provided responses. It is possible that if queries were to be resubmitted or submitted in a different order that responses would have varied. Similarly, there is an introduction of possible bias in the way that CPG recommendations were rephrased as questions posed in patient-friendly language—each of these rephrased questions was reviewed for appropriateness by 2 members of the team prior to submission to ChatGPT and Gemini. A third limitation is the overall small sample size, which was nonmodifiable unless the authors chose to include AAOS CPG recommendations that were of either limited strength of evidence or a consensus statement alone.

Conclusions

As roughly 80% of patients perform internet searches for health-related questions, it is important that clinicians are aware of some of the pitfalls of LLMs. This study demonstrates that neither ChatGPT nor Gemini consistently provide response that align with the AAOS CPGs for the management of glenohumeral arthritis. We additionally found that Gemini and ChatGPT responses frequently included confabulated details or inappropriately referenced articles. Consequently, physicians and patients should use these AI platforms with caution when seeking advice on management of glenohumeral arthritis. Future development of LLMs should prioritize adhering to evidence-based medicine and transparency of references to mitigate the risk of the distribution of medical misinformation.
