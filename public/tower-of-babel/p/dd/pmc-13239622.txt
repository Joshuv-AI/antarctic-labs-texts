Music Appreciation in Prelingually Deafened Patients: A Scoping Review
Authors: V Peyton Pinkus, Lindsey Herberger, Shanta Mazumder, Lindsay Blake, John Dornhoffer, Robert Saadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMusic appreciation by patients with cochlear implants (CI) is affected by multiple factors. Prelingually deafened CI patients have different expectations and experiences with music that impact their music appreciation. This scoping review aims to describe the factors important for music appreciation and strategies for improved music appreciation in prelingually deafened CI recipients.Data SourcesPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science.Review MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were queried for articles related to music appreciation in prelingually deafened CI users. Inclusion criteria were articles describing music appreciation characteristics and strategies for patients with CIs and prelingual hearing loss. The MINORS criteria was utilized to assess the quality of the studies.ResultsOf the initial 140 citations screened, 52 articles underwent full‐text review, and 32 met inclusion criteria, comprising a total of 599 unilateral and bilateral CI users with prelingual hearing loss. Average age at time of study and implantation were 11.03 and 6.36 years, respectively. Prelingually deafened patients reported increased music appreciation at baseline compared to postlingually deafened patients, but performed worse in melody, instrument, and song identification. Rhythmic music elements are especially important for music appreciation in this population. Additionally, implantation at an earlier age and music training after implantation are associated with improved identification and appreciation of musical elements.ConclusionsMusic is experienced and appreciated differently by prelingually deaf CI patients compared to those who have known acoustic listening. Early implantation and music training can significantly help improve identification of complex musical elements and overall music appreciation.Level of EvidenceLevel 3.Keywords: cochlear implant, hearing loss, music appreciation, prelingual deafness

Cochlear implants (CI) are highly effective at restoring access to spoken language in individuals with profound sensorineural hearing loss. However, music perception in these individuals remains a persistent challenge. Current CI technology remains limited in its ability to accurately deliver complex sounds, such as lexical tones, voice emotion, and music leading to potential implications for quality of life, social development, and interactions with peers. 1

Prelingually deafened CI users represent a unique population for studying music perception. Postlingually deafened individuals often form auditory memories of music they can rely on when listening to music after cochlear implantation. However, pre‐lingually deafened individuals develop their auditory memories solely through electrical stimulation leading to a completely different musical experience. This difference not only impacts objective performance on tasks such as melody or pitch discrimination, but also for subjective enjoyment of music.

This scoping review aims to consolidate current evidence regarding music perception in prelingually deafened CI users. We summarize outcomes across perceptual domains, examine subjective reports of attitudes toward music, review neural processing of music in CI users, and highlight factors that influence performance. We aim to clarify the current knowledge around this topic and identify potential strategies and implementation opportunities to improve music appreciation for pre‐lingually deafened CI users.

Methods

Data Sources and Search Strategies

A comprehensive search of PubMed MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science databases from inception to September 2024 was conducted in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The search strategy was designed and conducted by a trained research librarian with input from the study's principal investigator. A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), other controlled vocabulary, and keywords were used to search for “music,” “prelingual deafness,” “cochlear implant,” “cochlea prosthesis,” “music appreciation,” “music perception,” “music awareness,” “music concept,” “music recognition,” and “music impression.” All studies were limited to the English language only. References of all publications were also subjected to review for potential inclusion criteria. Articles with available full texts were screened, and duplicated articles were removed. A qualitative synthesis of the recognition of musical components, subjective music appreciation, and significant factors affecting music perception detailed in the studies was employed to integrate study results. This review was not registered. The level of evidence is considered level 5.

Eligibility Criteria and Quality Assessment

Eligible studies met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Participants were prelingually deaf with cochlear implant(s); (2) Study focused on music‐related experiences. Exclusion criteria included abstracts, publications that were deemed outside the scope of the study aims, and studies with exclusively postlingually deaf individuals. Two authors utilized the Methodological Index of Nonrandomized Studies (MINORS) criteria (Supplemental Item S1, available online) to independently assess the risk of bias for each of the studies included. Authors resolved disagreements by consensus.

Results

Inclusion Criteria and Study Design

Initially, 140 citations were identified. After removal of duplicates, 80 articles were uploaded to the Covidence review software. Title/abstract review was performed by two independent reviewers leaving 52 for full‐text review. Thirty‐two articles met inclusion criteria for the study. The most common reasons for exclusion were abstract only, inappropriate study design, and inappropriate study population. Among the included studies, 23 were cross‐sectional studies, 6 were longitudinal studies, 2 were prospective cohort studies, and 1 case control study. Eleven studies examined populations from North America, 9 from Asia, 9 from Europe, and 3 from Australia. Figure 1 demonstrates the PRISMA algorithm for article collection and review. In the case of missing data for included studies, it was the authors' intention to avoid bias by reporting only what was explicitly stated in the literature. Statistical analysis was not performed, and therefore, our study lacks statistical biases. Due to variability in study design and outcome measured, formal synthesis in the form of a meta‐analysis was not possible. The following discussion represents a consolidation of findings describing music appreciation in cochlear implant users with prelingual hearing loss.

Risk of Bias Within Studies

The Methodological Index of Non‐Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria were utilized to assess the quality of the studies included. The detailed results are provided in Supplemental Item S1, available online.

Study Characteristics

A total of 599 cochlear implant users with pre‐lingual sensorineural hearing loss were identified over the 32 included studies. Table 1 describes the characteristics of each study group. Of the 21 studies with gender reported, there were 270 male and 245 female cochlear implant users with pre‐lingual sensorineural hearing loss. Twenty‐nine studies provided age at time of the study. The average age of these patients was 11.65 years, with a median of 8.98 years. Twenty‐three studies provided age at implantation. The average age at cochlear implantation was 7.48 years, with a median of 3.16 years. Of note, the dissimilarities between average and median ages are likely due to inclusion of the study by Moran et al, which studied participants with an average age of 51.19 years at the time of the study and 47.29 years at the time of implantation. Twenty‐5 studies included the amount of time the participants had used their implants at the time of the study. The average time of cochlear implant use at the time of the study was 5.48 years, with a similar median of 5.1 years.

Music Perception and Appraisal Tasks

Melody Recognition

Adult cochlear implant users more accurately recognized melodies of familiar American songs than pediatric cochlear implant users. 2 Pediatric cochlear implant users with postlingual sensorineural hearing loss performed significantly better than cochlear implant users with prelingual sensorineural hearing loss of a similar age. 2 Similarly, cochlear implant users performed significantly worse in melody identification than the normal hearing participants with accuracies of 30% and 80%, respectively. 3 Results of a separate study included similar accuracies—37.03% in pediatric CI users and 88.88% in normal hearing children. 4 To add, a separate study revealed the ability of pediatric CI users with prelingual SNHL to identify melody was not better than chance. 5

When examining the relationship between melody recognition and music enjoyment, participants exhibited a positive correlation. Participants who more accurately identified melody reported an increased enjoyment of music 6 .

Tone Discrimination

CI users in an experiment conducted by Vongipasal et al performed better than chance when discriminating between musical tones. A smaller magnitude of change in tone proved to be more difficult for CI users to differentiate. The performance of study participants with normal hearing was not affected by the size of the change in pitch. 7

Tao et al examined the relationship between lexical tone recognition and prelingual or postlingual CI status. Mean recognition performance of the various tones presented was significantly better than chance for both groups (25% correct, P < .001). No significant effect was found between prelingually deafened and postlingually deafened CI users. 8

Pitch Discrimination

Mao et al analyzed the ability to vocally match a target pitch when presented. Children with hearing aid and CI showed poor alignment between singing pitch and target pitch, whereas children with normal hearing showed good alignment between the two pitches. Normal hearing children performed significantly better than those with hearing aids and CI. There was no statistical difference in pitch contour direction between hearing aid users and CI users. The pitch compression ratio in normal hearing children was statistically larger than that of children with hearing aids and CIs, signifying the ability of normal hearing children to better vocalize a consistent pitch while singing. 9

Additionally, while there was a difference in melodic contour identification (MCI) between the 2 groups (18.5% for prelingual, 32.3% for postlingual), there was no significant correlation between lexical tone recognition and MCI performance after correcting for outliers. 8

Rhythm Discrimination

Stabej et al reported a significant difference in rhythm discrimination between CI users and NH peers. 10

Mao et al also analyzed the ability of children with CIs to appropriately vocalize a target rhythm. When compared to normal hearing children, those with hearing aids and those with CIs had significantly larger values of interval deviation. The vocal performance of CI users showed a statistically larger interval deviation than that of those with hearing aids. 9

Instrument Recognition

In the study conducted by Stabej et al, the NH children more accurately recognized various instruments when compared to the CI children (57% vs 88%, P < .001): piano (97% vs 44%, P = .0008), flute (92% vs 44%, P < .001), and violin (97% vs 46%, P < .001). However, both groups recognized the guitar and male singing voice similarly. 10 CI users in the study conducted by Jung et al correctly recognized the guitar most frequently and the flute least frequently. 5

Song Recognition

Vongpaisal et al also studied accuracy of song recognition when presented as different versions—melody, instrumental, and original. For the cochlear implant users included in the study, performance exceeded chance for song identification when presented as melodies (37% accuracy), instrumental (38% accuracy), and the original (65% accuracy). An analysis of variance analysis performed by Vongpaisal et al confirmed that participants more accurately identified the original versions of the songs presented compared to the melody and instrumental versions. It was noted that 2 of the 3 highest performers received their CI much earlier in life than the other participants. In contrast to the CI users, their normal hearing peers included in the study more accurately identified instrumental than melody versions of the songs presented. 3

Volkova et al evaluated the abilities of prelingually deafened children with CIs to identify familiar songs by using pitch or timing cues alone or in combination. Expectedly, the CI children were able to more accurately identify the melodic version of the songs than the timing‐only or pitch‐only versions. However, the normal hearing and CI children did not differ in their abilities to identify the song when presented with the melody or timing‐only; however, the normal hearing children performed significantly better in the pitch‐only condition. Subjective thoughts from two participants were reported. One participant reported recognizing the differences in speed of the songs being helpful in the timing‐only condition indicating a difference in tempo was used to identify the song. Another participant reported counting where the words were supposed to be indicating that he/she linked the rhythm to the lyrics in the timing‐only condition. 11

Harmony Perception

Zimmer et al studied harmony perception in prelingually deafened juveniles with CIs. The study found that the CI users could discriminate chords to a certain extent; however, their performance was significantly worse than that of their normal hearing peers. The CI users preferred to listen to the same chords as the normal hearing listeners. When recognizing cadences and musical syntax, CI users performed at chance level, while normal hearing participants performed much better than chance, and their performance improved with increasing age. 12

Emotional Appraisal

The CI users in the study by Stabej et al identified the selection of music as sadder than did the NH children. 10

Volkova et al studied the ability of children with bilateral CI to identify happiness and sadness in both speech and music. Compared to the children with normal hearing included in the study, the CI children performed significantly worse in identifying the emotion in speech and music. Performance on the speech task was correlated with performance on the music task, and there was a correlation between implant experience and performance on both tasks. 13

Music Enjoyment

Subjectively, postlingually deafened CI users were disappointed when listening to music with their CI; however, prelingually deafened CI users found listening to music to be enjoyable. 14

Vongipasal et al also compared subjective ratings of the melodic, instrumental, and original versions of songs. All participants (CI and NH children) gave favorable ratings to all 3 renditions. Of note, the CI participants assigned lower ratings to the melody versions than to the instrumental and original versions. 3

Gfeller et al surveyed a cohort of pre‐lingually deafened CI users about their attitudes towards music. 87.1% of the cohort reported using their CI when listening to music. When asked about enjoyment of music class in school, 16.1% loved it and only 12.9% did not like it, with the remaining 71% of respondents being somewhere in between. Those with positive attitudes toward music class reported hearing the different instruments, the challenge of listening to different sounds, listening to the beat, playing instruments, excitement of learning new things, and social interaction impacted their response. Those with neutral or negative attitudes toward music class described it as “no big deal,” not interesting or enjoyable, boring, or difficult to understand. Nearly two‐thirds of this cohort indicated that music held a high degree of importance in their lives. They described music as fun, enjoyable, relaxing, a creative outlet, and something that improves mood. There was no significant correlation found between school music class participation and importance of music or reported enjoyment of school music classes and importance of music. However, there was a correlation between those with family members actively involved in music and more involvement in concerts and dances. 15

Moran et al examined music appreciation in prelingually and postlingually deafened CI users. There was no significant difference in time spent listening to music prior to or after receiving the CI between the 2 groups. There was no significant difference between ratings of level of music enjoyment prior to and after cochlear implantation. There was also no significant difference in the impact of CI on music enjoyment between the 2 groups. However, the prelingually deafened group trended toward CI providing greater music enjoyment, and there were large significant correlations with level of music enjoyment and both duration of hearing loss and amount of music listening for the prelingually deafened group. 16

Bruns et al investigated various ways CI users perceive, understand, and appreciate music. Based on subjective reports in this study, music appreciation was reduced in prelingually deafened CI users and relatively preserved in postlingually deafened CI users. 17

Brain Responses

Torppa et al studied singing's impact on auditory perception and attention to accuracy and the development of music‐related sound change detection. Compared to normal hearing individuals, CI users displayed a significantly longer timbre mismatch negativity (MMN) latency period and smaller, later timbre P3a when presented with various timbre changes: piano to violin, piano to cymbal, piano to cembalo. Over time, the timbre MMN amplitude became smaller in the CI singers. In addition, the P3a amplitude increased in the CI singers; whereas the P3a amplitude decreased and the P3a latency lengthened in the CI nonsingers. Further comparisons between CI singers and CI nonsingers were performed. The pitch MMN amplitude was larger in the CI nonsingers, and the pitch P3a was larger and earlier in the CI singers; however, these results were not statistically significant. 18

Peterson et al also utilized MMN to investigate a neural correlation of music perception in a group of prelingually deafened adolescents with CIs. Results showed significant MMNs in adolescent CI users for deviations in timbre, intensity, and rhythm, indicating residual neural prerequisites for musical feature processing. However, only two pitch deviants elicited a MMN in CI users. Overall, MMN amplitudes were significantly smaller in CI users than in NH controls, suggesting poorer music discrimination ability. 19

Marsella et al utilized neuroelectrical imaging of the prefrontal cortex to objectively measure the pleasantness of music perception in prelingually deafened CI users compared to normal hearing individuals. In the normal hearing participants, the EEG alpha rhythm asymmetries were consistent with a positive affective state towards the stimuli; however, this was not displayed in the CI users. A different pattern of alpha asymmetries was noted in the extrafrontal cortical areas in the CI users 20 .

Similarly, Cartocci et al utilized frontal brain asymmetries to investigate music perception in unilateral and bilateral CI users. Researchers found that bilateral cochlear implant (BCI) users, both prelingually deafened adult and pediatric, exhibited brain responses similar to those of normal hearing individuals. The BCI users demonstrated strong approach‐related EEG activity and functional brain connectivity when listening to normal music, especially when compared to distorted or mute conditions. On the other hand, unilateral prelingually deafened CI users, especially children, exhibited much flatter, less differentiated EEG responses when presented with various types of music. 21

Bruns et al further studied neural perception of music. In this study, only postlingual CI users exhibited a typical N400 brain response indicating access to the semantic meaning of music, while prelingual users lacked this neural marker. This important finding suggests prelingually deafened individuals may have difficulties in musical concept formation. 17

Factors Affecting Music Perception

Vongpaisal et al and Jung et al reported age, nor duration of implant use, had an association with task performance. 3 , 5

Chen et al reported no association between age at implantation and pitch perception performance; however, the study found an improved pitch perception performance in pre‐lingually deafened CI users older than 6 years compared to those less than 6 years, signifying a potential positive association between age and pitch performance. 22

Musical training has shown to positively correlate with the correct rate of pitch perception. 20 The earlier musical training, specifically singing training, is implemented after cochlear implantation, the more accurate ability of CI users to sing (Yang et al). The 4 best performers in this study wore hearing aids in the contralateral ear, and the 3 worst performers did not. There was a tendency of better pitch performance with hearing aid usage than without.

Chen et al also aimed to investigate the role of hearing aid use in the non‐implanted ear in pitch perception. The duration of hearing aid use was noted to be a major factor related to pitch perception. The overall correct rate of pitch perception, prime pitch interval, ascending interval, ascending interval over 5 semitones, descending interval, and descending interval over 5 semitones could all be best predicted by duration of hearing aid use. 23

In Tao et al's study, age at deafness was significantly correlated with MCI performance only for postlingual participants. CI experience was significantly correlated with MCI performance for both prelingual and postlingual participants. Duration of deafness was significantly correlated with tone recognition only for prelingual participants. 8

Cheng et al conducted a study to examine the effects of MCI training on music perception in a group of Mandarin‐speaking CI users. After 8 weeks of MCI training, mean scores increased by 22.9% for music perception and 14.5% for lexical tone recognition. Testing 4 weeks after training cessation revealed maintenance of the improvements. 24

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized findings from 32 studies examining music perception in cochlear implant users with pre‐lingual sensorineural hearing loss. Despite heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and outcomes measured, several themes emerged that provide important insight into the capabilities and limitations of CIs in processing of music.

Overall, pre‐lingually deafened CI users demonstrated the ability to perceive and enjoy music. However, when compared to their normal hearing peers, their abilities were consistently reduced. CI users performed above chance levels but below normal hearing controls across the domains of melody, pitch, rhythm, timbre, and harmony. Performance in song identification improved when presented with music containing both pitch and melody, rather than only one or the other. Subjective reports indicate utilization of multiple domains to identify songs. Importantly, performance was often task‐specific. CI users could more easily accurately complete rhythm‐based discrimination tasks compared to more tedious tasks, such as pitch or harmony discrimination.

Music enjoyment varied across studies and participants within studies. Prelingually deafened CI users reported a higher level of pleasure provided by music compared to postlingually deafened CI users. Despite perceptual limitations, most CI users reported finding music meaningful, enjoyable, and socially important. This suggests that music likely maintains its affective nature regardless of how it is perceived.

Several factors have been found to affect music perception in prelingually deafened CI users. While evidence was mixed, many suggested an improved ability to accurately perceive musical components when implantation occurred earlier in life. Similarly, a longer period of CI use was associated with better performance. In addition, hearing aid use in the contralateral ear and longer hearing aid experience were correlated with improved pitch perception, signifying an importance of residual hearing. Finally, musical training was associated with improvements in pitch discrimination and singing ability. Importantly, positive effects seem to remain following cessation of training, indicating long‐lasting benefits.

Studies of neural processing following stimulation by music highlighted important differences in auditory processing between CI users and normal hearing individuals. Among CI users, cortical responses were consistently delayed in response to changes in pitch, timbre, and rhythm. In addition, pre‐lingually deafened CI users lacked neural markers of higher‐level musical concept processing raising questions revolving around formation of an abstract meaning to music. However, early implantation and engagement in musical activities appear to foster more robust neural patterns and ability to appreciate music more closely to that of normal hearing individuals.

Evidence in this study is limited by multiple factors. The musical tasks presented in each study display a high degree of heterogeneity, which leads to difficulty when comparing results. Additionally, the MINORS assessment noted risk of bias across several domains indicating poor methodology of a few of the included studies.

Future research should be conducted to further explore the trajectory of music perception in prelingually deafened CI users over time. Conception of validated standard measures to appraise music perception in CI users should be considered for cohesiveness. Finally, randomized controlled trials evaluating the efficacy of the impact of music‐based training on music appreciation in CI users may be valuable to allow for future guidelines following cochlear implantation.

Conclusion

Prelingually deafened CI users demonstrate limited but meaningful abilities to perceive and enjoy music. While their performance in discrimination tasks generally lags behind those with normal hearing, most CI users find listening to music valuable. Early implantation, contralateral hearing aid use when appropriate, and musical training can enhance musical outcomes, underscoring opportunities for targeted interventions following surgery.
