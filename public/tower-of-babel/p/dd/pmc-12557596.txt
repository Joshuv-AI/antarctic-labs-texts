Macro Fat Graft Application for Preventing Tendon Adhesion After Tendon Repair: An Experimental Study in Rats
Authors: Betha Egih Riestiano, Arif Tri Prasetyo, Almahitta Cintami Putri, Lisa Hasibuan, Ali Sundoro, Selvy Harianti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTendon adhesion is a common postoperative complication following tendon repair, leading to impaired movement and increased morbidity. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of autologous macro fat grafts on preventing tendon adhesion following Achilles tendon repair in a Wistar rat model.MethodsA laboratory experimental study was conducted using a randomized post-test-only control group design. Thirty-six female Wistar rats were divided into two groups: control (no fat graft) and treatment groups (autologous macro fat graft applied circumferentially around the Achilles tendon after incision and repair). Histopathological evaluation was performed 3 weeks postintervention, assessing tenocyte count, collagen density, and both clinical and microscopic adhesion scores. Data analysis was performed using Mann-Whitney U and χ2 tests with a significance level of P < .05.ResultsThe treatment group showed a notable reduction in adhesion scores and adhesion grades compared with the control group. Collagen density was also lower in the treatment group than in the control group. However, no meaningful difference was observed in tenocyte count between the two groups, although the treatment group demonstrated a slightly lower mean tenocyte count.ConclusionsThe application of autologous macro fat grafts reduced tendon adhesion scores, adhesion grades, and collagen density postrepair, suggesting its potential as an effective strategy for preventing tendon adhesion. However, it did not considerably affect tenocyte proliferation.Clinical relevanceThis preclinical study suggests that autologous macro fat grafting may reduce postoperative tendon adhesions and improve surgical outcomes, supporting its potential translation into clinical settings.

Hand injuries constitute approximately 20% of all trauma cases requiring specialized medical care, with soft tissue damage accounting for up to 82% of these cases. Tendon involvement is commonly observed in hand lacerations. Tuncali et al1 reported tendon injury in 54.8% of minor lacerations and up to 92.5% in more extensive injuries.2,3 Tendons function as critical connective structures, transmitting force from muscle to bone to facilitate joint movement. However, tendon injuries and their surgical repair often provoke an inflammatory response, which can lead to adhesion formation and scar tissue development—factors that compromise tendon gliding and hand function.3,4

Tendon adhesion remains a prevalent and challenging complication across multiple surgical disciplines, including abdominal, gynecologic, and orthopedic procedures.4,5 Despite advances in surgical techniques, adhesions remain the most frequent and often unavoidable complication following tendon repair, with reported rates ranging from 10% to as high as 95%, depending on the procedure and anatomical location.6,7 In hand tendon injuries where precise flexion and extension are essential, formation is particularly problematic, often resulting in limited finger movement and reduced hand function.8,9 One study involving 1,031 patients reported adhesion development in 11.45% of cases following hand tendon repair.10 Additionally, the incidence of isolated or combined tendon injuries is estimated at approximately 30%, and up to 4% of patients may require reoperation due to persistent adhesions.10,11 In more severe cases, 6% to 20% of patients undergo secondary procedures to release adhesions, which not only prolong recovery but also contribute to additional physical and psychological burden.12,13

Tendon adhesions can occur in both synovial and extrasynovial tendons, but the underlying mechanisms differ.4 Flexor tendons, such as those in the hand, are enclosed in synovial sheaths that enable smooth gliding but create a confined, poorly vascularized space prone to fibrotic adhesions following injury.14 In contrast, extrasynovial tendons like the Achilles lack a synovial sheath and are surrounded by loose connective tissue, making them more susceptible to external adhesions from surrounding structures.15 Extensor tendons, which are also extrasynovial, are particularly prone to adhesion due to their superficial anatomical position and close proximity to bone.4 These anatomical differences are important when evaluating antiadhesion strategies, as treatments effective in synovial tendons may not directly translate to extra-synovial contexts.4,14

Numerous strategies have been investigated to prevent tendon adhesions following repair. Physical barriers, such as amniotic membranes, Seprafilm, and hyaluronic acid-based gels, are designed to mechanically separate healing tissues and reduce friction-induced scar formation.16 Pharmacological approaches include the use of corticosteroids, mitomycin-C, and anti-inflammatory agents, which aim to suppress fibroblast proliferation and excessive collagen deposition.17 Early mobilization and physical therapy have also been employed to promote gliding and prevent stiffness, although they carry a risk of repair rupture if introduced prematurely.18 More recently, biologic therapies such as platelet-rich plasma, stem cell-based treatments, and gene therapy have shown promise in modulating the inflammatory and fibrotic responses associated with tendon healing.19 However, many of these interventions are limited by high costs, inconsistent results, regulatory barriers, or lack of accessibility in low-resource settings. As such, the search for safe, cost-effective, and biocompatible alternatives such as autologous fat grafting remains a critical area of ongoing research.4,5,10

In recent years, fat grafting has emerged as a promising alternative for adhesion prevention due to its biocompatibility, mechanical properties, and regenerative potential. Beyond its potential in tendon surgery, autologous fat grafting has been widely used in clinical practice for soft tissue augmentation, scar remodeling, treatment of radiation-induced tissue damage, and chronic wound healing.20, 21, 22 These applications leverage the anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and stem cell–rich properties of adipose tissue, making fat grafts a versatile tool in both reconstructive and regenerative medicine.23 This technique involves the harvesting and reinjection of autologous adipose tissue, which is rich in mesenchymal stem cells and paracrine signaling factors that facilitate tissue repair. Fat grafts are inherently biocompatible, readily accessible, and exhibit minimal immunogenicity, making them particularly appealing for both reconstructive and antiadhesion applications.24 Beyond promoting the maturation of collagen from type III to type I, fat grafts can serve as physical barriers that inhibit fibroblast proliferation and excessive scar formation.25 Although commonly used in aesthetic and intra-abdominal procedures, the application of fat grafting for tendon adhesion prevention remains relatively underexplored.26

Fat grafting techniques include macro fat, micro fat, and nano fat, each suited to different clinical purposes. Among the various fat grafting techniques, macro fat—comprising adipose tissue particles larger than 2.4 mm—is primarily used for volumizing large soft tissue defects, such as in breast or gluteal augmentation, and is also suitable as a physical spacer.24 Micro fat (1.2–2.4 mm) and nano fat (400–600 μm) are preferred for finer applications like facial contouring or superficial skin rejuvenation, as they contain higher concentrations of regenerative cells but lack volume for mechanical separation.24,27 In tendon repair, macro fat is more appropriate for preventing adhesions by physically separating the tendon from surrounding tissues. Macro fat grafts possess several advantages, including ease of harvest, ample availability, and mechanical cushioning properties. These attributes suggest potential efficacy in reducing tendon adhesion by providing a gliding surface and enhancing tissue regeneration. In light of the need for effective and accessible antiadhesion interventions, this study investigates the role of macro fat grafting in preventing tendon adhesion following surgical repair in a rat model, with a focus on histopathological outcomes and therapeutic implications.

Materials and Methods

This study was designed to assess the effect of macro fat grafting on both macroscopic and microscopic tendon adhesions following Achilles tendon repair. The evaluation focused on collagen deposition as a marker of fibrotic response and tenocyte count as an indicator of cellular proliferation within the tendon tissue. The aim was to determine whether autologous fat grafting could influence these parameters and potentially support improved healing outcomes.

This experimental study used a randomized post-test-only control group design to develop an extrasynovial tendon adhesion model in Wistar rats. A total of 36 healthy female Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus), aged approximately 12 weeks and weighing 250–300 g. Following a 7-day acclimatization period under standardized conditions (25 ± 2 °C room temperature with a 12-hour light-dark cycle), the animals were randomly assigned into two groups: control (n = 18) and treatment (n = 18). Randomization was performed manually prior to the intervention. Allocation concealment was not applied due to procedural constraints. Outcome assessments, including histopathological grading and collagen analysis, were conducted by an independent, blinded observer using anonymized slide coding to reduce potential bias.

All rats underwent a standardized transverse incision of the Achilles tendon under general anesthesia using intraperitoneal ketamine (100 mg/kg) combined with xylazine 2% (10 mg/kg) diluted in 0.9% sterile saline. After shaving and antisepsis of the surgical field, a sharp transverse cut was made at the middle third of the Achilles tendon using a no. 15 scalpel blades. The tendon was repaired using a modified Kessler suture technique with monofilament non-absorbable polypropylene (Prolene 6-0).

In the treatment group, an autologous macro fat graft approximately 1 cm in length and 1 g in weight was harvested immediately after tendon injury from either the inguinal or abdominal fat pad of the same animal. The selected donor sites were chosen based on accessibility and favorable tissue characteristics. Following disinfection with povidone iodine, a 1 cm incision was made to extract an intact strip of fat tissue. The graft was then carefully wrapped around the repair site in a 360° circumferential manner, covering approximately 0.5 cm proximally and 0.5 cm distally, without dicing or fragmentation.

After surgical closure, both control and treatment group animals were immobilized using a cast to restrict joint movement and maintain tendon alignment during the healing process. Casts were applied immediately after surgery and maintained for the full duration of the 3-week observation period.28 Animals were killed at 3 weeks for histopathological evaluation. Euthanasia was performed using an overdose of anesthetic agents, followed by cervical dislocation for confirmation of death. Tendons were excised entirely from the insertion point at the calcaneus to the musculotendinous junction near the distal femur.

Achilles tendon specimens were immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 24 hours to preserve tissue morphology. Following fixation, the samples underwent standard histopathological processing, including graded ethanol dehydration (70% to absolute), xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding. Embedded tissues were cut longitudinally into 5 μm-thick slices using a rotary microtome, then mounted on coated glass slides. Sections were stained using hematoxylin-eosin to visualize cellular and extracellular components. Hematoxylin stained the nuclei of tenocytes blue-purple, whereas eosin highlighted cytoplasmic and extracellular matrix components, including collagen, in varying shades of pink.

Histological evaluation was conducted using an Olympus light microscope at magnification × 400. The following features were assessed: (1) tenocyte count per high power field, (2) adhesion severity using the Tang scoring system (grade 0: no adhesion; grades 1–2: mild adhesion; grades 3–4: moderate adhesion; grades 5–6: severe adhesion), and (3) collagen fiber structure and distribution.29 To quantify collagen density, image analysis using ImageJ software was employed. Digital micrographs of hematoxylin-eosin-stained sections were processed through the Color Deconvolution plugin to separate collagen (pink), nuclei (purple), and background. Manual area selection was then applied to isolate collagen fibers, and the percentage area occupied by collagen was calculated, representing collagen density. This method, although not collagen-specific, has been validated for semiquantitative analysis of collagen content in hematoxylin-eosin-stained tendon tissues.

The sample size was determined using Federer’s formula: (t − 1)(r − 1) ≥15, which is commonly applied in animal experiments with a completely randomized design, where t is the number of treatment groups and r is the number of replications. For this study with two groups (t = 2), the minimum required replication was 16 animals per group. To accommodate potential dropouts due to mortality or exclusion, an additional two animals per group were included, resulting in a total of 36 rats (18 per group).

The independent variable was the application of macro fat grafting, whereas the dependent variables included clinical adhesion occurrence, tenocyte count, adhesion grading, and collagen density. Statistical analysis was conducted separately for each dependent variable, with appropriate tests selected based on data distribution. Data distribution was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality. Parametric tests (eg, unpaired t test) were used for normally distributed data, whereas nonparametric tests (eg, Mann-Whitney U test) were applied to non-normally distributed data. No corrections were applied for multiple comparisons.

This study followed the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines to ensure transparent reporting of all aspects of in vivo experimentation. The study design, sample size calculation, randomization, blinding, and data handling were performed in accordance with the “Essential 10” components of the ARRIVE guidelines. In addition, the study was conducted with full consideration of the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) to ensure ethical animal use, and the 5F principles (Five Freedoms) were upheld to maintain animal welfare including freedom from hunger and thirst, discomfort, pain, fear and distress, and the ability to express natural behavior. Information on animal characteristics, housing conditions, and experimental procedures was also reported to meet international standards for scientific integrity and animal welfare. The protocol received ethical exemption and review from the Health Research Ethics Committee of our Institution.

All surgical instruments and reagents were standardized. Data were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests depending on data distribution, with a significance threshold set at P < .05. All measurements and assessment tools were selected based on their validated application in previous tendon adhesion research.

In summary, macro fat grafting reduced both visible and microscopic adhesions, mainly by decreasing collagen deposition. Tenocyte proliferation remained unchanged. These results suggest that fat grafting could be a safe and effective method to support better healing after tendon surgery.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effect of macro fat graft application on the prevention of tendon adhesions following Achilles tendon repair in Wistar rats. Based on clinical, histological, and quantitative evaluations, the findings provide preliminary evidence suggesting that autologous fat grafts may play a role in reducing peritendinous adhesions. However, the absence of molecular and mechanistic analyses limits the interpretation of the underlying biological processes. Further studies incorporating immunohistochemical and cytokine profiling are needed to elucidate the potential immunomodulatory and antiadhesive mechanisms of fat grafts.

The use of fat grafts to wrap the tendon repair site may help reduce peritendinous adhesion through several mechanisms. Adipose tissue acts as a biocompatible physical barrier that prevents direct contact between the healing tendon and surrounding tissues, minimizing fibroblast migration and excessive extracellular matrix deposition that contribute to adhesion formation.5,30, 31, 32 Fat grafts are rich in adipose-derived stem cells, which possess immunomodulatory and regenerative capacities. These cells suppress proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α while upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators including IL-10 and TGF-β.5,33 In addition, HO-1 expression within adipose tissue contributes to oxidative stress reduction and modulation of inflammatory responses.34 Fat grafts also inhibit the expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, which is typically elevated in the early proliferative phase and promotes adhesion by limiting fibrinolysis.35 Moreover, adipose-derived stem cells promote the shift from type III to type I collagen synthesis via matrix metalloproteinases, supporting better biomechanical tendon properties and reducing bridging fibrosis.33 Importantly, the angiogenic properties of fat tissue aid in maintaining tenocyte viability during the remodeling phase, which is often compromised due to metabolic stress and hypoxia.34

Clinically, adhesions occurred significantly less in the treatment group (33.3%) than in controls (72.2%) (P = .019; Table 1), and a higher proportion of rats in the treatment group showed no adhesions. Tendon adhesion, both macroscopic and microscopic, was evaluated based on the criteria defined by Tang et al.29 Quantitative scoring in Table 2 confirmed reduced severity of adhesions across all categories in the fat graft group (P < .05), aligning with previous studies such as Laukka et al,5 which demonstrated the antiadhesive potential of fat grafts.5 These results support the idea that fat grafts may serve not only as physical barriers but also influence the healing environment. This hypothesis is further reinforced by similar studies. However, the proposed immunomodulatory effects remain speculative, as this study did not include molecular or immunohistochemical analyses to directly assess such mechanisms.36,37

The study also assessed tenocyte proliferation, a key process in tendon healing. Although tenocytes are vital for collagen production and tissue remodeling, there was no significant difference in their numbers between groups (P = .153; Table 3). This aligns with findings from the study by Viganò et al,38 suggesting that fat grafts may not directly stimulate tenocyte growth but could instead influence healing via paracrine signaling. These signals, mediated by growth factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines, modulate the inflammatory milieu, promote organized tissue remodeling, and suppress excessive fibrogenesis. Although previous studies have proposed that adipose-derived grafts may exert effects through paracrine signaling and anti-inflammatory cytokines, this study did not directly evaluate these molecular pathways. Therefore, such interpretations should be considered preliminary and warrant further investigation. Jiang et al39 also emphasized that optimal tendon healing depends more on matrix remodeling than cell proliferation alone. Thus, although fat grafts did not increase tenocyte numbers in this study, their modulatory effect on the healing environment likely contributes to improved tendon repair.20,40

A key histological finding was the significant reduction in collagen density in the treatment group (P = .0001; Table 3), suggesting a more controlled fibrotic response. This supports the notion that fat grafts can limit excessive scarring, which is often linked to adhesion formation. The reduction in collagen density observed may be influenced by components within the adipose tissue, such as mesenchymal stem cells or bioactive mediators, as reported in prior studies. However, this study did not perform cellular or protein-level analyses; hence, the involvement of these factors remains to be elucidated in future research. Similar mechanisms have been described by Viganò et al38 and Cherubino et al,41 who emphasized the antifibrotic and immunomodulatory properties of adipose-derived grafts. Moreover, adipose tissue contains a range of bioactive elements, including mesenchymal stem cells and adipokines, known to inhibit profibrotic mediators such as TGF-β1 and facilitate balanced extracellular matrix remodeling.42,43 Our findings are consistent with those of Wang et al,44 who observed reduced type III collagen and α-SMA expression following fat grafting—markers associated with pathological fibrosis. Therefore, the observed decrease in collagen density reflects not impaired healing but rather a more regulated and functionally favorable reparative process.

The high proportion of rats with no visible adhesions in the treatment group (66.7%) further supports the potential clinical relevance of macro fat grafts. Similar outcomes have been reported by Cil and Aydogdu45 and Taha et al,25 who demonstrated reduced macroscopic and microscopic adhesions following autologous fat graft application.

In summary, macro fat grafts may reduce tendon adhesions through several mechanisms: acting as a physical barrier, modulating inflammation, limiting fibrosis, and supporting more organized collagen remodeling.5,20,34,35,41,43 Although tenocyte proliferation was not significantly affected, the overall results suggest a more favorable healing environment. These results contribute to the growing body of preliminary evidence suggesting the potential application of autologous fat grafts in tendon repair. However, the lack of mechanistic validation underscores the need for future studies incorporating molecular and cellular analyses to fully understand the underlying biological effects. Further molecular studies, along with long-term and clinical trials, are essential to confirm efficacy and define the broader applicability of this technique.

Although this study demonstrates consistent antiadhesion effects of macro fat grafts, certain limitations warrant consideration. The sample size, although calculated using Federer’s formula, lacked power analysis and effect size estimation, potentially limiting the detection of subtle biological differences. No corrections were made for multiple comparisons, possibly inflating type I error for borderline results. The absence of sham/uninjured controls and assessment at only one postoperative time point further restricts the interpretation of baseline comparisons and healing progression.

Taken together, these findings suggest that macro fat grafts may offer a multifactorial approach to adhesion prevention in tendon repair. Their potential to improve healing outcomes—while maintaining biocompatibility—makes them a promising adjunctive therapy. Continued investigation is needed to refine their use and determine their effectiveness in clinical settings. Future research should also incorporate power analyses, larger sample sizes, and statistical adjustments for multiple testing to further validate and refine the biological and therapeutic significance of fat grafting in tendon healing. Additionally, the inclusion of a sham or uninjured control group is recommended in future designs to provide a clearer histological and structural baseline for comparison and better contextualize postrepair changes.
