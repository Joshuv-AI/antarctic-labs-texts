Smart Microfluidics: A curated dataset of microfluidic liposome formulations with cross-laboratory validation for machine-learning applications
Authors: Leonardo Lavagna, Giorgio Buttitta, Simone Bonacorsi, Chiara Barbarito, Mauro Moliterno, Gabriele Saito, Irene Oddone, Giuliana Verdone, Sergio Raimondi, Massimo Panella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset documents microfluidic production runs of liposome formulations generated across two independent laboratories using standardized lipid compositions and controlled flow conditions. The data include formulation parameters, microfluidic operating settings, and dynamic light scattering measurements of vesicle size and polydispersity, providing structured input–output relationships suitable for data-driven analysis. Raw spreadsheets from each laboratory were harmonized using a reproducible preprocessing workflow implemented in Python, which performs column standardization, fuzzy-matching corrections, physical-range validation, chip-type filtering, and dataset consolidation. The cleaned dataset comprises 304 micromixer-produced liposome formulations, while a separate file contains 12 independent wet-lab validation formulations. Two additional dataset extensions were generated via Gaussian-noise perturbation and SMOTENC-based oversampling to support machine-learning benchmarking and algorithm comparison. Cross-laboratory records enable evaluation of operator variability, equipment reproducibility, and robustness of predictive modeling workflows. Metadata files documenting feature descriptions, naming conventions, and physical bounds facilitate reuse in automated pipelines and FAIR-compliant repositories. All datasets, raw files, preprocessing scripts, and cleaning logs are publicly available on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17867478), enabling regression benchmarking, inverse-design studies, and comparative evaluation of modeling approaches for formulation–property relationships in microfluidic liposome synthesis.

Background

The dataset was generated to support a systematic investigation of microfluidic liposome production inspired by clinically validated formulations. Liposome manufacturing by microfluidics involves multiple formulation and process variables whose combined effects on critical quality attributes, such as particle size and polydispersity index, are not trivially predictable. Traditional experimental optimization approaches, including Design of Experiments, are often limited in capturing nonlinear interactions across high-dimensional parameter spaces. To address this methodological context, a structured experimental campaign was designed to generate a comprehensive dataset suitable for data-driven modeling.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Egg-derived sphingomyelin (ESM; CAS 85,187-10–6); hydrogenated soy phosphatidylcholine (HSPC; CAS 92,128-87–5) and 1,2-Distearoyl-sn‑glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000] ammonium salt (MPEG-DSPE 18:0; CAS 147,867-65–0) were obtained from Lipoid (Ludwigshafen, Germany) as research-grade, high-purity phospholipids. Cholesterol (CAS 57–88–5, ≥99 % purity) was supplied by Carbogen Amcis (Bubendorf, Switzerland). Disodium hydrogen phosphate dihydrate (CAS 10,028-24–7), sodium dihydrogen phosphate dihydrate (CAS 13,472-35–0), d-(+)-glucose monohydrate (CAS 14,431-43–7), and sodium chloride (CAS 7647–14–5) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and were of analytical grade. Ethanol (CAS 64–17–5), HPLC grade, was also obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Ultrapure water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C) was produced using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore Synergy System, Millipore, Fontenay-sous-Bois, France).

Microfluidic system setup

Liposomes were prepared using a controlled microfluidic setup consisting of two programmable syringe pumps (NE-1000, KF Technology, Rome, Italy). Each pump was equipped with Inject Luer Lock syringes (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and connected to a glass microfluidic chip (Dolomite Microfluidics, Royston, UK) via a precision tubing system. To minimize the risk of particulate contamination and prevent microchannel blockage, a 0.2 µm Acrodisc® syringe filter (25 mm Supor® EKV membrane, Pall Corporation, New York, USA) was positioned between each syringe and the inlet tubing. The organic phase was introduced directly into the microfluidic chip, whereas the aqueous phase was routed through a 1/16′’ Micro Tee Assembly (IDEX, Lake Forest, IL, USA) to evenly divide the flow into two symmetrical streams prior to entering the chip. Depending on the experimental requirements, two different glass chip geometries were employed: a Large Droplet Junction Chip (code 3000,158, Dolomite Microfluidics) and a Micromixer Chip (code 3200,401, Dolomite Microfluidics). To ensure steady-state conditions and formulation consistency, the initial 2 mL of each preparation were discarded before sample collection. During operation, flow stability and channel integrity were continuously monitored using a digital microscope (Inspect Holland). Processing time varied according to the total flow rate applied in each experiment. Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined immediately after production using a Zetasizer Advance Red Label instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK) based on dynamic light scattering (DLS). Samples were diluted with Milli-Q water (1:50–1:100) to achieve an attenuator value between 6 and 7. Measurements were performed at 25 °C following thermal equilibration, and each formulation was analyzed in triplicate to confirm reproducibility

Preprocessing workflow

To ensure consistency, reliability and reproducibility of the dataset a structured preprocessing pipeline was implemented. This pipeline harmonized raw experimental data collected from multiple laboratories, standardized feature naming, validated numerical ranges, and filtered configurations to retain only relevant experimental conditions. These steps collectively ensured that the resulting dataset accurately represented comparable liposome formulations, free from labeling inconsistencies and out-of-scope measurements.

Column standardization

raw_data_checker.py reads each raw spreadsheet, aligns column names using features_names_mappings.json, and corrects inconsistent labels using fuzzy matching. Numerical fields are cast to appropriate types.

Physical-range validation

Values are checked against features_bounds.json. Out-of-range measurements (e.g., PDI outside [0,1]) are flagged and removed.

Chip filtering and column consolidation

raw_data_slicer.py extracts only rows with the Micromixer chip type, as droplet-chip configurations did not produce stable vesicles. Redundant columns (ID, ML, CHIP, OUTPUT) are removed.

Dataset merging

Preprocessed spreadsheets from Laboratories A and B are concatenated to form formulations.csv. Validation data from Laboratory A are exported as wet_lab_validation.csv.

Logging

All transformations generate logs stored in code/logs/, documenting corrections and filtering decisions.

Data augmentation

To enhance dataset balance and improve model generalization, synthetic data were generated through controlled augmentation techniques. The augmentation pipeline introduced variability within realistic physical limits while preserving the integrity of categorical parameters. Two complementary strategies were applied: Gaussian noise perturbation to expand the numerical feature space and SMOTENC-based oversampling to address class imbalance in mixed-type variables. The resulting augmented datasets were stored in the data_extensions/ directory, providing enriched and statistically diverse samples for downstream modeling.

Gaussian noise augmentation (data_gn_adder.py)

A subset of rows is selected, numerical features are perturbed using controlled Gaussian noise, and categorical variables are copied intact.

SMOTENC augmentation (data_smote_adder.py)

Categorical features are encoded, mixed-type oversampling is performed using SMOTENC, and generated rows are decoded into the original schema.

Augmented datasets are saved to the data_extensions/ folder.

Software environment

The preprocessing and augmentation tools require Python and the packages listed in requirements.txt, including pandas, numpy, scikit-learn, imbalanced-learn, rapidfuzz, and others. All the code is made available at https://zenodo.org/records/17867478.
