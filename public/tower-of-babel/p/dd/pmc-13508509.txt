An Alginate Hydrogel-Based Novel Oral Contrast Agent Designed for Gastric Computed Tomography Imaging
Authors: Xiaoqiang Shang, Wenyu Ling, Ronghuan Wan, Hongkai Xie, Shiyang Zhang, Weiyu Shu, Yufeng Chen, Hua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In gastric computed tomography (CT), oral contrast agents can enhance the image quality, thereby improving the diagnostic sensitivity and effectiveness for the early detection of gastrointestinal diseases. However, current oral contrast agents are mostly liquid and suffer from rapid gastric emptying rates and short retention times, resulting in unsatisfactory CT results. Here, we present a contrast agent that enables calcium carbonate and sodium alginate to undergo a gelation reaction in the acidic environment of the stomach, facilitating the in situ formation of an iohexol-incorporated calcium alginate hydrogel-based contrast agent. Results from in vivo high-resolution CT scans in mice demonstrate that this hydrogel-based contrast agent remains stable in the stomach for 40 min, whereas liquid contrast agents (like diluted iohexol) were significantly expelled in less than 20 min. After CT scanning, rapid dissolution and excretion can be achieved within 30 min by oral administration of a chelating agent, demonstrating good safety. Compared with traditional liquid contrast agents, this hydrogel-based contrast agent exhibited stronger gastric mucosa adhesion, better gastric distention, and a longer gastric retention time. Therefore, the inherent properties of hydrogel materials facilitate better imaging during gastric CT scans, and they hold potential for application in the diagnosis of gastric disorders.

Introduction

Gastric carcinoma ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 800,000 deaths each year. , Modern medical imaging technologies, such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), offer distinct advantages over conventional endoscopic examination for the early diagnosis of gastric carcinoma and other gastrointestinal diseases, including noninvasiveness, higher patient compliance, and rapid and convenient operation. , Consequently, these techniques have achieved widespread global adoption and are evolving toward increasingly intelligent applications. − For instance, recent studies have employed artificial intelligence (AI) to analyze CT images, enabling the early detection of gastric cancer. , To further improve CT image quality, especially spatial resolution and soft tissue contrast, various CT contrast agents have been developed and employed clinically to enhance diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy in the past decades. −

Currently, clinically used oral contrast agents for gastric imaging are generally divided into two types: positive and neutral. , Positive contrast agents primarily comprise barium sulfate suspensions or iodinated agent solutions, which appear as radiopaque on CT images, whereas neutral agents consist of water or water-diluted contrast agents. , To adequately distend the elastic gastric wall and reveal the fine architecture of gastric mucosal folds, patients are typically required to ingest large volumes (800–1000 mL) of contrast agent liquid in a short time before conducting CT scans, which requires high patient compliance. , However, liquid leaves the stomach very quickly; for example, only 50% of the water remains in the stomach after 10 min, which further weakens the effectiveness of the liquid contrast agent. , Sometimes, gastrointestinal motility-modifying medications, such as Buscopan or glucagon, are used to avoid rapid emptying of oral contrast agents. , However, the consequent side effects, such as allergic reactions, cardiac arrhythmia, and urinary retention, remain a concern. ,

Since administering large volumes of liquid to patients has compromised compliance, including the elderly, it is often impractical in clinical settings. The consequent inadequate gastric distension, coupled with rapid liquid emptying, leads to suboptimal CT imaging, presenting a considerable challenge to radiologists. For example, Figure displays the CT scans of two typical types of patients before and after oral administration of neutral liquid contrast agents (water). As shown in Figure a and Figure b, the gastric walls of both patients appeared to be thickened and nondiagnostic before water administration. After ingesting a large volume of water, patient A exhibited a fully distended stomach with uniformly thin walls, whereas localized abnormal thickening (suggesting gastric cancer) was observed only in a specific region (indicated by the yellow arrow in Figure b). In contrast, patient B, due to poor compliance, presented with suboptimal CT preparation, resulting in a partially distended and thickened gastric wall on CT images and consequently limited information for diagnosis (Figure d). Therefore, the use of liquid contrast agents in gastrointestinal CT has long been associated with certain limitations.

Gastric CT images of two patients (A and B) before (a, b) and after (c, d) oral administration of water. The gastric wall and pathological lesions were visualized by yellow dashed lines and arrows.

Recently, some researchers have designed novel delivery systems for contrast agents, − such as W1/O/W2 double emulsions and metal-oxide nanoparticle-loaded hydrogels. , The hydrogel matrices exhibited targeted and sustained release of contrast agents in the bowel; however, their application in the stomach has seldom been reported and remains a challenge. To overcome the lack of effective oral contrast agents for gastric CT, this study developed a novel agent based on an iohexol-loaded alginate hydrogel. Upon oral administration, sodium alginate rapidly gelated with calcium ions in acidic gastric juice to form a stable X-ray radiopaque hydrogel within the stomach. As a result, this hydrogel contrast agent can fully distend the gastric cavity and reveal detailed structures of the gastric mucosa, thus providing a sufficient time window for CT scanning without being rapidly expelled, as with liquid agents. The stable hydrogel is preloaded with iohexol, providing strong CT attenuation and producing clear, high-contrast images. This approach holds promise for enhancing the early diagnosis of gastric diseases by improving the imaging performance.

Results

In Vitro Calcium Alginate Hydrogel Preparation and Characterization

In this study, the hydrogel was designed to be composed of two liquid components (solution A and suspension B) to facilitate oral administration while enabling the in situ formation of an intact and shape-conforming hydrogel within the stomach. As illustrated in Figure a, solution A was a clear aqueous solution consisting of glucono-delta-lactone (GDL) and iohexol. Suspension B was prepared by suspending calcium carbonate (CaCO3) particles in a sodium alginate solution, resulting in a milky white suspension. Upon oral administration of the two liquids, gluconic acid was produced by the hydrolysis of GDL and gastric acid reacted with CaCO3, resulting in the release of calcium ions. The free calcium ions rapidly induced gelation by cross-linking with alginate, forming a uniform calcium alginate (Ca-alginate) hydrogel within the stomach. As shown in Figure b, after complete gelation, the originally suspended white CaCO3 particles disappeared and the carbon dioxide gas generated was trapped within the gel, yielding a transparent hydrogel containing numerous small bubbles. This in situ prepared hydrogel could quickly adhere to the inner surface of the glass container firmly and would not fall off even when placed upside down, which would be an advantage when applied to gastric CT. As shown in Figure c,d, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images demonstrated that the resulting Ca-alginate hydrogel exhibited a typical porous microstructure, with pore sizes of approximately 200–400 μm. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping revealed that iodine was uniformly dispersed within the hydrogel, confirming the homogeneous incorporation of iohexol molecules within the Ca-alginate network during gelation. −

By simply mixing solution A and suspension B (a), Ca-alginate hydrogel was generated (b); SEM images of the Ca-alginate hydrogel (c, d) revealed its porous structure; EDS mapping of iodine confirmed the uniform dispersion of iohexol molecules within the hydrogel (e, f).

Furthermore, we investigated the influence of various factors on the hydrogel gelation time, including sodium alginate, CaCO3, and GDL concentrations and the reaction temperature. As shown in Figure a–d, by optimization of these parameters, effective control over the gelation time of approximately 1 min was achieved. This is particularly critical for in vivo applications, as excessively high concentrations may lead to overly rapid and inhomogeneous local gelation, whereas excessively low concentrations may prolong the gelation time, resulting in the failure to form a robust gel. Additionally, iohexol was incorporated to yield an X-ray opaque hydrogel. As shown in Figure e, the iohexol-incorporated hydrogel exhibited uniform brightness under X-ray imaging, confirming the homogeneous dispersion of iohexol within the hydrogel. Hydrogels containing 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% (v/v) iohexol yielded a gradient increasing Hounsfield unit (HU) value in CT images ranging from −40 to 5400 HU, which exhibited good linearity with iohexol concentration (Figure f).

Gelation time was modified by varying the concentration of Na-alginate. CaCO3, GDL, and temperature (a–d). Photograph and CT images (e) of hydrogels containing 0–40% (v/v) iohexol and the corresponding HU values (f).

In Vivo High-Resolution Animal Micro-CT in Mice

Subsequently, we applied this hydrogel to gastric CT imaging in mice. As illustrated in Figure , solution A and suspension B were orally administered sequentially into the mouse stomach, where gelation occurred. Gel formation was completed within approximately 1 min, followed by micro-CT scanning. After imaging, a chelating agent was administered orally to competitively obtain calcium ions from the alginate molecular chains, leading to the dissolution of the hydrogel network back into the liquid. The dissolved liquid was easily evacuated from the stomach into the intestines and eventually excreted outside the body. Normal saline and iohexol solutions were used as reference contrast agents.

Flow diagram of a Ca-alginate hydrogel contrast agent applied in the mouse stomach and gastric CT scanning.

The results in Figure a–d showed that while normal saline filled the stomach, it provided no observable signals under X-ray as it has a density similar to that of abdominal organs and soft tissues. Iohexol, a nonionic iodine-containing liquid contrast agent, provided clear contrast but rapidly passed through the pylorus into the intestines owing to gastric contraction and peristalsis. Within 20 min, a significant amount of iohexol entered the intestine (Figure f). In contrast, the iohexol-incorporated Ca-alginate hydrogel not only provided excellent imaging under X-ray but also remained in the stomach for a considerably longer period, owing to its gel nature. As shown in Figure i, nearly all the hydrogels were retained in the stomach after administration. After 20 min, a small amount of hydrogel entered the intestines, and significant gastric emptying occurred only after approximately 40 min. These results confirmed our hypothesis that a gel-based contrast agent can prolong the gastric retention time, potentially facilitating more convenient CT imaging and medical diagnosis.

X-ray images of mice treated with three different oral contrast agents, saline (a–d), iohexol (e–h), and Ca-alginate hydrogel (i–l) in 0–60 min.

Furthermore, three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions of CT scans were performed for imaging evaluation of the three contrast agents. As shown in Figure , images of 3D reconstructions of the stomach from the anterior, left-side, and inferior perspectives are presented. The normal saline group did not visualize the gastric lumen (Figure a–c), whereas both the iohexol and Ca-alginate hydrogel groups clearly outlined the gastrointestinal tract (Figure d–f and g–i). However, due to the rapid flow of liquid agents from the stomach into the intestines, the 3D reconstruction using iohexol displayed both the stomach and adjacent intestinal loops, which were difficult to distinguish separately. Moreover, iohexol only partially filled the stomach and yielded an irregular surface in the 3D reconstruction that did not accurately reflect the inner gastric wall (Figure d–f). In contrast, the hydrogel-based contrast agent filled the entire gastric lumen, forming a smooth, continuous, and shape-conforming gel that clearly delineated the complete stomach structure and morphology (Figure g–i). In addition, owing to the gel nature, most hydrogel agents are constrained in the stomach with only a small amount entering the intestines. Therefore, it is more convenient to image and observe the stomach separately.

3D reconstructions of gastric CT scans of mice treated with three different oral contrast agents, saline (a–c), iohexol (d–f), and Ca-alginate hydrogel (g–i).

Hydrogel Dissolution and Safety Evaluation in Mice

Finally, to safely and easily expel the hydrogel agent from the body, it was dissolved in liquid by introducing calcium ion chelators after CT scanning. Three different chelators (EDTA-4Na, SC, and STPP) were tested by using an in vitro degradation assay. As shown in Figure a, all three agents achieved nearly complete gel dissolution within 25 min, with STPP being the fastest, dissolving approximately 70% of the gel within 5 min. Consequently, STPP was selected for further in vivo degradation testing in mice. Figure b shows that after oral administration of 0.25 mL of STPP solution, the hydrogel in the mice stomach gradually changed from a dense, solid state to a lighter color and a more fluidic state under X-ray imaging, indicating progressive dissolution. Within approximately 30 min, the gel was almost completely liquefied and emptied from the stomach into the intestines within 40 min. A supplementary video (Supporting Information) clearly shows the flow of the dissolved gel within the gastrointestinal tract, confirming that the Ca-alginate hydrogel can be safely and rapidly dissolved via calcium chelation after CT imaging.

In vitro degradation percentage of the Ca-alginate hydrogel by different chelating agents (a) and in vivo degradation process of the Ca-alginate hydrogel by STPP in mice under X-ray (b).

Evaluation of the Hydrogel Contrast Agent in the Rabbit Stomach with Spiral CT

To specifically evaluate the imaging performance of the Ca-alginate hydrogel contrast agent, we applied the hydrogel without iohexol to rabbit gastric CT imaging. The rabbits were orally administered either saline or Ca-alginate hydrogel as a contrast agent prior to gastric CT scanning. As shown in Figure a, in the blank control group without an oral contrast agent, the gastric body showed moderate filling, and the boundaries between the gastric wall and gastric contents were indistinct, making it difficult to delineate the contour of the gastric wall. In contrast, in rabbits that received saline or hydrogel as the contrast agent, the stomach exhibited better overall filling with a relatively larger volume, and the gastric wall was more clearly visualized (as indicated by the yellow dashed outlines in Figure b,c).

Gastric CT images of the rabbit stomach after oral administration with (a, d) nothing, (b, e) saline, and (c, f) Ca-alginate hydrogel. The stomach was outlined with yellow dashes in (a–c). The gastric wall was indicated by yellow arrows, and thickness was measured (e, f).

Because normal saline or hydrogel shows a low-density shadow in CT images, whereas the gastric wall tissue shows an isodensity shadow, the gastric wall can be clearly displayed. As indicated by the yellow arrow in Figure e,f, the gastric wall could be clearly visualized after oral administration of saline or Ca-alginate hydrogel. Furthermore, the thicknesses of the gastric wall were measured to be 3.0 and 1.0 mm, respectively, which implied that the stomach with the Ca-alginate hydrogel was more expanded, resulting a thinner gastric wall. This might be related to the difference in retention time within the stomach between saline and hydrogel. As discussed above, the liquid contrast agent (like saline or iohexol) would be emptied from the stomach in a short time. However, the Ca-alginate hydrogel was more stable in the stomach and gave a better gastric distension, which contribute to favorable conditions for CT scan.

Discussion

Since its first invention in the 1960s, hydrogels have been extensively studied due to their softness, hydrophilicity, and good biocompatibility. − Currently, hydrogels demonstrate broad application prospects in the field of biomedical materials, such as wound dressings, − drug carriers, − and tissue engineering. − However, their research and application in medical imaging are still in the exploratory stage. , In recent years, studies have shown that hydrogels can serve functions such as filling, supporting, and sealing in the digestive tract, hollow organs, or tissue spaces, providing theoretical support for the development of hydrogel-based gastric CT contrast agents. − As a state between solid and liquid, hydrogels possess fluid properties similar to those of liquids, enabling them to flow and fill within the stomach. At the same time, they can maintain shape and volume like solids, thereby expanding and filling the gastric cavity. This makes hydrogels a suitable contrast medium candidate for gastric CT imaging. Furthermore, compared to liquid contrast agents, hydrogels can prolong the gastric emptying time and maintain more stable gastric filling, which is beneficial for clear visualization of the gastric wall.

Among various hydrogel materials, alginate is a natural biopolymer extracted from brown algae. , It is safe, nontoxic, biocompatible, and biodegradable and has been approved by the FDA for use in oral medications and wound dressings. , While used as an oral contrast agent, the preformed Ca-alginate hydrogels present practical challenges such as swallowing difficulties and the loss of structural integrity during oral administration. To overcome these limitations, we have designed an in situ gelling mechanism for this gel-based contrast agent. This system comprises two distinct precursor solutions: solution A containing GDL and iohexol and suspension B consisting of CaCO3 and sodium alginate. Upon oral ingestion, these solutions sequentially enter the gastric cavity and undergo a cascade of reactions: GDL hydrolyzes to gluconic acid, which, in concert with the gastric HCl, reacts with CaCO3 to liberate free Ca2+ ions. These calcium ions promptly cross-link with alginate, forming an Ca-alginate hydrogel matrix that entraps iohexol molecules within its polymeric network, thereby conferring CT imaging capability. , Following the imaging procedure, the patient can ingest a chelating agent solution (e.g., EDTA), which binds to calcium ions within the hydrogel. The resultant chelation destabilizes the hydrogel network, leading to its dissolution back into a soluble state for subsequent elimination from the gastrointestinal tract. Leveraging this reversible gelation characteristic, the controlled formation and dissolution of the Ca-alginate hydrogel under specific conditions can be achieved, enabling tailored functional applications. −

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the controlled preparation of a gel-based contrast agent and explored its application in gastric CT imaging in mice and rabbits. The new designed gel-based contrast agent was capable of undergoing an in situ gelation process quickly within the stomach, forming a stable, wall-adherent, and shape-conforming hydrogel. Owing to the stability and immobility nature of the hydrogel, this gel-based contrast agent exhibited a prolonged gastric retention time and superior gastric distention compared to conventional liquid-based alternatives such as saline or an iodinated contrast agent. As a result, it enabled clear CT imaging and complete 3D reconstruction, enabling independent observation of the stomach without unnecessary signal interference from other organs such as the intestinal tract. On the other hand, the safety issue may need further investigation. After oral administration, hydrogels might adhere to or cause local blockages in the digestive tract. Therefore, an extra chelating agent should be used afterward to help with degradation and excretion of the hydrogel agent. These characteristics suggested its potential as a novel contrast agent for CT imaging of the gastrointestinal tract and for use in the diagnosis and treatment of related diseases.

In summary, this study successfully fabricated an iohexol-incorporated Ca-alginate hydrogel for gastric CT imaging and preliminarily evaluated its in vivo imaging performance. However, comprehensive biosafety assessments remain incomplete, and the contrast agent’s efficacy in disease models (e.g., gastric carcinoma and gastric ulcer) needs further investigation.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Sodium alginate, calcium carbonate, glucono-delta-lactone (GDL), sodium citrate (SC), ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid tetrasodium salt (EDTA-4Na), and sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China. Avertin (tribromoethanol) was purchased from Dalian Bergolin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. Iohexol injection solution (Omnipaque) was purchased from GE Healthcare (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China.

In Vitro Ca-Alginate Hydrogel Preparation

Typically, the Ca-alginate hydrogel was prepared by mixing equal volumes of solution A (0.1 mol/L GDL and 20%v/v iohexol) and solution B (2.5 mg/mL CaCO3 and 2 wt % sodium alginate) at room temperature. The gelation time was recorded. The gelation time may vary from 1 to over 10 min by changing the concentrations of GDL, CaCO3, and sodium alginate. All experiments were repeated three times. For Hounsfield unit measurements, the concentration of iohexol varied from 0–40% (v/v) in solution A.

Characterization by SEM and EDS

The fresh Ca-alginate hydrogel was freeze-dried overnight, followed by Au coating treatment, as reported by previous research. , The images were taken with scanning electron microscopy (JEOL, JSM-7900F) under a 10 kV voltage.

In Vitro Ca-Alginate Hydrogel Degradation Assay

In a typical assay, 0.5 mL of solution A (20% (v/v) iohexol in 0.1 mol/L GDL) was mixed with 0.5 mL of suspension B (2.5 mg/mL CaCO3 dispersed in 2 wt % sodium alginate) in a 2 mL plastic tube and then placed at 37 °C for 10 min to form the Ca-alginate hydrogel. After 10 min, 0.5 mL of the chelating agent solution was added into the tube. Three different chelating agents were tested, including sodium citrate (SC, 0.2 mol/L), ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid tetrasodium salt (EDTA-4Na, 0.2 mol/L), and sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP, 0.2 mol/L). After that, the supernatant was taken out at different time intervals, and the mass of the remaining hydrogel was recorded. The hydrogel degradation percentage was calculated by dividing the remaining mass by the initial mass. All experiments were repeated three times.

In Vivo High-Resolution Animal CT with Mice

Eight-week-old ICR mice (weight 28–32g) were housed at 25 °C under a 12 h light–dark cycle and have free access to food and water. All mice were randomly divided into three groups; each group contained 3 mice. Animals were fasted for 12 h before the experiments but allowed free access to water. First, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection with avertin (tribromoethanol) at a dosage of 20 μL/g. Then, each group of mice was given 0.5 mL of saline, iohexol contrast agent, or Ca-alginate hydrogel (composed of 0.25 mL of solution A and 0.25 mL of solution B), by intragastric administration and ready for X-ray imaging and CT scanning.

For hydrogel degradation and safety evaluation, 0.25 mL of the chelating agent (STPP, 0.2 mol/L) was given by intragastric administration after scanning to digest the hydrogel. The X-ray images and CT scan were taken on a Quantum GX-2 Micro-CT system (PerkinElmer) under conditions of 90 kV and 88 μA. The CT data were analyzed with Bee DICOM Viewer (v2.6.2) software.

In Vivo Spiral CT with Rabbits

New Zealand rabbits (weight 2.25 kg) were housed at 25 °C under a 12 h light–dark cycle and had free access to food and water. Rabbits were given 200 mL of saline or Ca-alginate hydrogel by intragastric administration and were ready for CT scanning. The CT scans were taken on 64-slice spiral CT (Philip Ingenuity CT) under conditions of 120 kV and 220 mA.

All animal experimental protocols for this research were approved and guided by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University (Approval Nos. NCULAE-20260105001 and NCULAE-20260203001).
