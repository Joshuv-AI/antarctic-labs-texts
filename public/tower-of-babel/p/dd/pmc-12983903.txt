ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis review aimed to establish the contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby, defined as unexpected adverse changes in players’ physical, psychological, social or health status.DesignSystematic review.Data sourcesPubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE, SPORTDiscus and CINAHL.Eligibility criteriaStudies were eligible if they investigated a physical, psychological or social factor which results in a negative biopsychosocial outcome in men’s or women’s rugby union, league or sevens. Introduction

Sports participation leads to a range of biopsychosocial outcomes; these can be both positive (eg, improved well-being, quality of life and self-confidence)1 2 and unintendedly negative (eg, injury, burnout, stress). Optimising the interaction between positive and negative biopsychosocial outcomes is a priority for sporting organisations (eg, governing bodies, clubs), to ensure that players can perform while remaining healthy. The rugby codes (rugby union, rugby league and rugby sevens, hereafter referred to as ‘rugby’) are played worldwide by men and women, across all ages and playing levels.3 4 Rugby is a skill-based contact sport,5,7 with a relatively high injury risk, mainly due to the tackle event.8,12 There is growing concern among stakeholders regarding the negative health and performance effects experienced by rugby players, particularly related to the ‘excessive demands’ placed on players. Rugby players are exposed to multiple contributors directly related to playing rugby (eg, exposure to the demands of training and match-play, travel commitments). Depending on age and playing level, players may be required to undertake other rugby-related commitments (eg, media, contract negotiation), which, together with non-rugby activities (eg, work outside of rugby, family commitments, education and socialising), contribute to the overall psychological load players experience.13 14 Considering the range of demands on rugby players, an understanding of the outcomes they contribute to may warrant mitigation.

Exposure to contributors leads to an initial response or outcome that can be positive (eg, changes in fitness) or negative (injury, illness, burnout). Moreover, the initial negative response may be short-lived, leading to an overall positive outcome (eg, an initial increase in fatigue which subsequently leads to an increase in fitness overall). A comprehensive biopsychosocial assessment of these outcomes is necessary to evaluate their impact on players’ physical, psychological and social health.15 As such, in this review, a negative biopsychosocial outcome is defined as any unexpected negative change in a player’s physical, psychological or social health.1 This definition was established using prior biopsychosocial frameworks15 and further developed using clinical expertise, practical experience and support from the literature base2 by the research team to distinguish genuine negative outcomes for players from normal fluctuations typically observed within a standard 1-week microcycle (eg, expected fatigue or stress responses). Negative biopsychosocial outcomes may include reductions in mental health, injury, illness or player burnout. It is currently unknown how factors and exposures collectively experienced by players may positively or negatively influence outcomes.

Governing bodies have previously identified various contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes and enacted changes and guidelines to mitigate them. Examples of this are player match limits16 17 and contact load guidelines18 19 to limit overexposure to components of training and match-play which are associated with negative outcomes. Given the need to consider all contributors, a review which compiles a list of all factors which contribute to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in the rugby codes is required. Establishing all potential contributors that result in negative biopsychosocial outcomes within the current evidence base can increase the impact of the research, driving future research into potentially non-established areas and allowing stakeholders to manage and mitigate identified contributors appropriately. Therefore, this systematic review aims to identify, describe and list the contributors that result in negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby players within the literature, which will then inform further refinement of contributors and mitigation strategies by stakeholders within part 2 of the CoNBO project.20

Methods

The protocol for the CoNBO project has been previously published20 and consists of three parts; part 1: a systematic review, part 2: a three-round Delphi consensus method and part 3: establishing the perceived feasibility and barriers to managing the contributors associated with negative health and performance player outcomes. All parts include general and cohort-specific contributors, feasibility and barriers. This study presents part 1 of the CoNBO project.

The systematic review was carried out in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses protocol.21 The protocol was registered with the PROSPERO international prospective register for systematic reviews (registration number: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42022346751) prior to the commencement of the review.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search of five electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE, SPORTDiscus and CINAHL) was performed from the earliest record available until the search was complete. Primary terms were combined using the AND function. The search terms combined terms relating to rugby and terms relating to negative biopsychosocial outcomes. The search was initially completed in June 2023 and subsequently updated in April 2024 and June 2025. Guided by existing published reviews,14 22 23 search terms were modified20 to adequately reflect the aims of the review (online supplemental table 1).

Eligibility criteria

Each study found within the search strategy was subject to specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. As the aim of the study was to identify and describe the factors which act as contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes within the literature, only papers which assessed and found an association between contributor and outcome were included; examples are provided in figure 1. The following inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. A full list of studies excluded during full-text screening and the reason for exclusion are shown in online supplemental table 2.

Selection process

Records were managed in EndNote throughout the review process. Titles and abstracts identified were screened independently by two researchers (JP and SW) against the eligibility criteria. Any disagreement between the two reviewers was solved by a third reviewer (BJ). Full-text articles were retrieved and reviewed for final inclusion by the same researchers (JP, SW and BJ). The title and authors were not masked to the reviewers.

Data extraction and synthesis

Two reviewers (JP and SW) independently extracted data from selected studies into a predesigned template. Authors were contacted if data were needed to be obtained or confirmed, and WebPlotDigitizer (V.4.5) was used to extract data from figures where needed. Data extracted covered the following points: study details (eg, authors, publication details), study design (eg, type of study, duration), sample (eg, size, rugby code, age grade, playing level, sex), independent (contributor) and dependent variables (negative health and/or performance outcomes), results (eg, descriptive statistics, effect sizes, relative risk, p values) and the key outcome and findings were extracted. As both quantitative and qualitative research was included in the review, independent variables were defined as contributors when risk factors or mechanisms24 that showed either a practical or statistical significant association with a negative biopsychosocial outcome for quantitative research or were described as having implications on negative biopsychosocial outcomes by authors of qualitative research. Independent variables were grouped based on subjective similarities. Groupings were initially made by co-authors (JP, SW and BJ) through an inductive process and were then sent to the wider expert multidisciplinary coauthor group for review and refinement, for example, any contributors relating to contact event (eg, tackle height, tackle proficiency, speed/acceleration into contact) were grouped as ‘factors within the contact event’. Given the heterogeneous nature of the study designs, no summary measure, data exploration or additional analysis was conducted.

Risk of bias

Two reviewers performed a critical appraisal of individual articles independently (JP and SW for quantitative studies, JP and SM for qualitative studies). For quantitative research, the modified version of the Downs and Black checklist25 was used as in previous sport science and medicine reviews.5 26 Quality was assessed over 11 items (question numbers 1–3, 6, 7, 10–12, 16, 18, 20). Question 10 was modified to include the reporting of effect sizes. A score of ‘0’ for ‘absent or insufficient information provided’ or ‘1’ for ‘item is explicitly described’ was assigned to the criteria. Quality scores were expressed as percentages of the highest attainable score for the study type.27 A quality rating based on the percentage score was assigned to each study; ‘excellent’ (≥93%), ‘good’ (≥71%), ‘fair’ (≥57%), ‘poor’ (<57%).28 For qualitative research, individual articles were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) qualitative studies checklist.29 The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the CASP qualitative checklist, in which each item is rated as ‘yes,’ ‘no’ or ‘can’t tell’ in line with CASP guidance.

Patient and public involvement

As part of the need for patient and public involvement and engagement, the protocol for this systematic review was reviewed and approved by a broad range of relevant stakeholders to ensure they have an opportunity to contribute to this research’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plan. There was no patient or public involvement in conducting the review including literature screening or data extraction.

Results and discussion

Identification and selection of studies

Through the original database search, 17 457 records were identified. Following the removal of duplicates and screening for eligibility, 151 studies remained. Figure 2 provides a schematic representation of this process.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses 2020 flow diagram for the identification, screening, eligibility and inclusion for the literature reporting contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby players.

Study characteristics

All rugby codes were investigated (rugby union (n=104); rugby league (n=46); rugby sevens (n=6)) (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). Nine studies investigated multiple rugby codes, while three did not specify the code. Most investigated exclusively men’s cohorts (n=131, ~88%) with only eight (~5%) investigating exclusively women’s cohorts and eight (~5%) assessing both men and women’s cohorts. Five (~3%) did not specify the gender or sex of participants. A range of cohorts and age-grades was investigated, with the majority being at the elite level (n=86, ~57%) and open age (n=115, ~78%) athletes, while 37 (~24%) studies investigated youth populations. A large range of contributor groups (n=29) and negative biopsychosocial outcomes (n=8) were identified (figure 3).

(A) Contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes and (B) negative biopsychosocial outcomes identified by the 151 studies included in the review.

Risk of bias in studies

Online supplemental tables 3 and 4 show the scores and ratings for the assessment of study quality for quantitative studies respectively. Out of the 143 quantitative studies included in the review, 9 (~6%) were rated ‘poor’, 29 (~20%) were rated ‘fair’ and 105 (~73%) were rated ‘good’. No quantitative studies were rated ‘excellent’. Out of eight qualitative studies, two (25%) were rated highly valuable, five (62.5%) were rated moderately valuable and one (12.5%) was rated slightly valuable by the authors within the final question of the checklist.

Negative biopsychosocial outcomes

Eight negative biopsychosocial outcomes with established contributors were identified within the literature. There is a significant focus within the current evidence base on some outcomes, while some remain under-researched. The most cited outcome was injury, which was included in 127 (84%) studies, and burnout was cited in 30 (20%) studies. While symptoms of mental ill-health (n=14), well-being and stress (n=7), illness (n=5), mood states disturbance (n=2), stress urinary incontinence (n=2) and fatigue (n=1) were all included in fewer than 15 studies (0.7–9%). The outcomes highlighted may not constitute an exhaustive list of negative biopsychosocial outcomes experienced by rugby players, as they only include outcomes currently investigated within the literature. Therefore, further research is required to establish links between more outcomes and their contributors. It is also possible that some outcomes subsequently act as contributors. An example of this would be an injury, which has in turn also been associated with subsequent injury and symptoms of mental ill-health and burnout.

Contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes

Within the current literature, 67 contributors were identified; these were then grouped into 29 contributor groups by the research team based on subjective similarities (table 1). The number of contributors per group ranged from 1 to 11. Each contributor group is represented by a heading and for groups with many contributors, each contributor is represented by a subheading. Overall, physical characteristics (32 studies), training and match load (30 studies), injury (27 studies) and factors within the contact event (19 studies) were the most identified contributor groups. Within the contributor groups overall training load, acute increases in training load, previous injury and concussion were the most investigated contributors. The contributors identified within this study may influence multiple outcomes; for example, training and match load have been found to be associated with injury, illness, reductions in wellness, burnout and stress.

Adverse career and life events

Career and life events were identified as contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in three men’s rugby studies.30,32 Across all three men’s professional codes, players who experienced career dissatisfaction and adverse life events (eg, bereavement, marital separation) were more likely to report mental health symptoms and disorders (ie, anxiety/depression) (online supplemental tables 5 and 6).32 Job insecurity and thinking about retirement were identified as contributors to negative mental health outcomes (eg, anxiety and depression) and burnout in professional men’s rugby union.30 31 Career and life events have been found as contributors to negative mental health and burnout in professional men’s rugby, but further research is required to understand this relationship, particularly in women’s and non-professional cohorts.

Environmental conditions

Two studies identified environmental conditions (ie, heat, wind) as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes.33 34 Both studies were in amateur men’s players and injury was the only negative biopsychosocial outcome assessed. In rugby union, a significant negative correlation between heat index and injury rates was identified in Under 16 (U16) age groups; however, a positive correlation was found in the U14 age group.33 In rugby league, temperature and wind conditions were assessed with the greatest injury risk occurring with temperatures between 5°C–7.1°C and during moderate wind conditions.34

Factors within the contact event

22 studies identified factors within tackle or contact events as contributors to injury35,56 (online supplemental table 5 and 6) across different codes (union n=16, league n=6, sevens n=1) and sexes (men n=21, women n=3). Tackle proficiency (n=3), impact location/tackle height (n=9), speed/acceleration into contact (n=5), number of tacklers (n=4) and tackle type and direction (n=3) were the most common factors within the contact event identified as contributors to injury.

Tackle proficiency

In men’s rugby union, lower tackle proficiency of both the ball-carrier and tackler and lower ruck proficiency were consistently observed during injury35 or concussive41 events. Amateur men’s and women’s rugby union players, both open age and youth, perceived that poor tackling technique and inadequate coaching contributed to concussion risk.54 No studies included in the review identified that tackle proficiency may contribute to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby league or sevens.

Impact location/tackle height

In men’s rugby union36,3847 49 56 and rugby league,44 higher tackle height (eg, contact to head or neck of ball-carrier) was associated with injury. However, in men’s rugby union, low tackle heights were also reported to potentially contribute to injury/concussion risk to the tackler.47 50 It is, however, important to note that one of the studies did not examine tackle characteristics directly, but assessed concussion incidence risk when the legal tackle height was lowered.50 In men’s rugby league, low tackle heights contributed to tackler injury, with the highest risk occurring when making contact with the ball carriers’ lower body.52 55

Speed/acceleration into contact

The speed into the tackle contributed to concussion and/or injury risk of ball-carrier and tackler in three studies in rugby union.37 38 40 Increased injury risk occurred when players moved at high speed and/or accelerated into contact (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). This relationship is also present in rugby league, with the speed of the tackler being cited as a tackle characteristic that potentially contributed to injury risk.42 However, whether this relationship translates to women’s rugby populations is yet to be confirmed.

Number of tacklers

In rugby union, the risk of injury varied between studies for the number of tacklers that entered a tackle. One47 and two46 tacklers in men’s rugby union contributed to injury risk, and in women’s rugby union, three52 tacklers were found to have the greatest injury risk. When multiple tacklers were involved, the greatest risk of injury occurred when contact was simultaneous rather than sequential.46 In men’s rugby league, tackle events with one or four tackler involvements contributed to injury risk compared with tackle events with two tacklers.44

Tackle type and direction

In men’s rugby union, tackle type and direction were found to contribute to injury risk (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). Shoulder tackles and collision tackles (ie, tackler stops the progress of the ball-carrier without using their arms) have been reported to increase injury risk.48 Tacklers have been reported to be at increased risk of injury when tackles are made from behind.3 Given the publication year of the study, this may be a reflection of a change in the game around the turn of professionalisation and may affect relevance to today.

Other contributors within the contact event

There were several other contributors to injury risk within the contact event contributor that have been identified across men’s and women’s rugby union and men’s rugby league, which include (online supplemental tables 5 and 6); lower player awareness,36 scrum collapse,48 attacking versus defensive collisions,39 tackler head position43 51 and postcontact tackle characteristics.42 However, these contributors had the lowest evidence base within the contact event (eg, one study per contributor with varying study quality (online supplemental table 2)) and therefore require further research.

Financial

Four studies identified financial matters and contract negotiations as a contributing factor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in men’s rugby union,30 31 57 58 while one assessed rugby league players.59 Money hassles (eg, ‘having enough money for basic needs’ and ‘financial security was a hassle’) and contract negotiations, with concerns about financial commitments, were found to contribute to burnout in professional and semiprofessional players.3057,59 Furthermore, a change in income has been found to contribute to increased feelings of anxiety.31

Fixture scheduling

10 studies in men’s professional rugby found fixture scheduling or the number of matches players are required to play as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes (injury n=5, concussion n=2, stress n=2, fatigue n=1) in rugby players (online supplemental tables 5 and 6).1660,68

Four studies in rugby union found that playing an increased number of matches per season (>31 and >35 matches) increased the risk of injury both on a 12-month average16 and in the following season68 or concussion (>25 games)65 along with a linear relationship between the number of matches played per month and injury risk.16 However, also playing a reduced number of matches may contribute to injury and concussion risk. Playing <15 matches per season had the same injury risk as >3568 and game exposure throughout the season has been negatively associated with concussion risk.60 Shorter microcycle lengths as a result of fixture congestion were found to contribute to higher injury burden of soft tissue injuries in elite men’s rugby union players but not injury incidence overall; however, this was after a prolonged stand down period as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, therefore may be unique to the study’s data collection period.66 In rugby league, two studies focused on the duration of time between game microcycle and injury risk and found that players were more likely to sustain contact training injuries during 10-day microcycles compared with shorter turnarounds.61 Injury risk may also be position-specific with long turnarounds adversely affecting outside backs and hit-up forwards, while adjustables had greater injury risk during a short turnaround.63 One study assessed intensified fixture schedules whereby players played multiple fixtures over a short period, finding that this contributed to cumulative reductions in neuromuscular function of up to 6.9%.67

Fixture scheduling was identified as a contributor to stress in two studies. In rugby union, 50% of players interviewed identified the number of matches and lack of recovery time during preseason as a contributor to stress and negative affective states.64 While in rugby league, social stress significantly increased during a period when fixture scheduling increased.62

Footwear

One study in amateur rugby union showed type of footwear to be a contributor to injury, with the odds of injury being up to four times greater when players wore blade studs, in compared with those wearing other types of footwear (eg, round studs).69

Household Illness

One study in men’s professional rugby union showed household illness (illness of others) was a significant contributor to player illness with a 2.9 times greater risk when illness was present in the household.70

Injury

Injury was identified as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by 26 studies3065 71,93 (online supplemental table 3). Three negative biopsychosocial outcomes were identified: subsequent injury (n=17), mental health outcomes (n=6) and symptoms of burnout (n=4). 19 studies were in rugby union, two in rugby league and one in rugby sevens, while two investigated multiple codes. One study did not specify the rugby code. The studies are summarised in online supplemental tables 5 and 6.

In men’s rugby union, eight studies identified injury as a contributor to subsequent injury risk. Players who had sustained previous injury had an increased likelihood of sustaining subsequent injury.72,7480 84 86 88 89 This was also seen in men’s rugby sevens by one study.92 In men’s rugby league, one study identified previous injury as a contributor to injury risk with the severity of the injury and training time prior to the first injury impacting the subsequent injury risk94 (online supplemental tables 5 and 6).

Four studies identified injury status as a contributor to symptoms of burnout in men’s rugby union players3075,77 (online supplemental 5 and 6). Significant relationships were observed between injury status and all subcomponents of burnout. Additionally, ‘repeated injuries and pressure to play’ have been reported by players as specific contributors to burnout. Injury status has also been reported to contribute to symptoms of mental ill-health, namely, anxiety and depression in two studies, one investigating men’s rugby union and one investigating men’s rugby union, league and sevens players.85 95

Nine studies in this review found previous concussion incidence contributed to increased injury risk compared with players who have not sustained concussion in men’s (n=8)6571 72 78 83 88,90 and women’s (n=1)83 rugby union. Concussion was also associated with subsequent concussion risk, with players who have had two or more concussions in the previous 12 months having increased concussion risk in both rugby union and rugby league,81 87 and it is important to note one of the studies investigated mild traumatic brain injury incidence rate rather than concussion specifically.81

Four studies observed that concussion contributed to negative mental health outcomes. Men’s rugby union, league and sevens players who had sustained a concussion were significantly more likely to develop adverse mental health symptoms and behaviours, namely, distress and adverse alcohol use, than those without concussion.85 Adolescent players in both men’s and women’s rugby union with concussion history reported higher levels of depression and anxiety, compared with those with no history.91 Similarly, men’s rugby league players who reported ≥three concussions had an increased likelihood of developing depressive symptoms79 and in rugby (code not specified) players with a history of concussion were more likely to report poorer somatisation, depression and anxiety scores in both men’s and women’s players.82

Interpersonal relationships

Seven studies included in the review found that interpersonal relationships contribute to negative biopsychosocial outcomes.57,5977 96 Five studies were in rugby union (men’s n=4, women’s n=1), one in men’s rugby league and one was in men’s union and league together (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). Social support was one theme assessed within interpersonal relationships by three studies.57 58 96 One study reported a relationship between low social support and injury incidence,96 while two studies reported a lack of social support networks to contribute to subcomponents of burnout.57 58 In youth players, perceptions of their mother and father’s responsiveness positively related to their global self-esteem,98 and elite rugby league players felt family expectations had a significant impact on mental health. However, this study displayed poor study quality, so further research may be required (online supplemental table 2).59 In women’s rugby union, within-squad competition and pay discrepancies between players contributed to feelings of burnout. A lack of social interaction outside of rugby led to a reduction in connectedness with external peers.97

In rugby union, a negative relationship with coaches contributed to burnout. Men’s professional players attributed absence of openness and honesty, poor communication and a lack of consultation as contributors to burnout.77 While women’s amateur and university players felt autocratic coaches contributed to feelings of burnout by impacting enjoyment and reducing feelings of control. Along with this, players cited adverse reactions from coaches, stigma around burnout and lack of approachability as a source of stress contributing to symptoms of burnout.97

Lifestyle factors

Lifestyle factors were identified as contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby union players by two studies.64 99 Men’s professional rugby union players suggested during interviews that diet, specifically not eating enough food and lack of sleep quality, was a contributor to stress and negative affective states.64 In amateur players, smoking and greater number of hours engaged in strenuous physical activity (>39 hours per week vs 5 hours) were identified as contributors to injury risk.99

Menstrual cycle

One study included in the review assessed players’ perception of the menstrual cycle being a contributor to increased injury risk. Players perceived that they were at increased injury risk during the menstrual cycle due to feelings of reduced athleticism and lack of concentration.100 This warrants greater exploration as a female-specific contributor.

Mental health and well-being

Symptoms of mental ill-health were identified as contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by two studies.79 101 In men’s university rugby union, sport-related anxiety was found to be associated with increased injury rate101 and in men’s professional and semi-professional rugby league, players with a history of mental illness were at greater odds to develop depressive symptoms than those without.79 Along with this, lack of access to adequate mental health provision may act as a contributor to mental ill-health. In men’s professional rugby league, negative attitudes towards existing mental health provision were found to contribute to symptoms of mental ill-health in one study.102

Mood states

Two studies in men’s university rugby union players found mood states to be a contributor to injury and burnout.101 103 Tension/anxiety on the profile of mood states scale has been found to contribute to injury rate while tension/anxiety and anger/hostility and total negative mood state all contributed to injury severity.101 Additionally, all subcomponents of the Stellenbosch Mood Scale were found to be related to symptoms of burnout at seven time points throughout the season.103

Motivation

Motivation was identified as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by three studies.75 97 104 In men’s rugby union, intrinsic, extrinsic and amotivation were all linked to subcomponents of burnout.75 104 Amotivation was associated with sport devaluation and reduced accomplishment across two studies75 104 and was also associated with exhaustion to a lesser extent (online supplemental tables 5 and 6).104 While intrinsic and extrinsic motivation were associated with exhaustion and sport devaluation, while only extrinsic motivation was associated with feelings of reduced accomplishment;75 however, there is conflict within the research. In amateur and university women’s rugby union, reduced intrinsic motivation contributed to burnout, with playing and training obligations impacting non-rugby commitments and leading to ‘resentment’ towards rugby.97

Personality

Personality traits have been identified as contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by three studies. All studies assessed rugby union players, one in open age men’s players, one in youth men’s players and one U21 women’s players.97 105 106 Perfectionism defined by perfectionistic cognitions (ie, automatic thoughts to be perfect) and being overly self-critical was highlighted as a personality trait contributing to symptoms of burnout in both youth men’s106 and U21 women’s players.97 Additionally, lower Mental Toughness Scores using the Mental Toughness Index were found to be associated with anxiety and depression measured using the 12-Item General Health Questionnaire(GHQ-12).105

Phase of the season

12 studies (union n=6, league n=5 and code not reported n=1) identified phase of the season or playing season (ie, summer vs winter) as contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby players.6061 76 80 107,114 11 studies assessed men’s players while one study107 assessed both men’s and women’s players. Negative biopsychosocial outcomes identified included injury (n=9), concussion (n=1), illness (n=1) and burnout (n=1).

In men’s rugby union players, concussion60 and non-contact injury80 incidence were greater in the later phases of the season. Inversely, in amateur rugby union senior and youth players (U18 and U19), injury rates decreased temporally throughout the season.107 114 A phase of season contributed to illness in professional rugby union players with an increase in incidence of upper respiratory illness following periods of reduced game time but increased training loads (ie, following the pre-season period or mid-season breaks).108 In schoolboy rugby players (code not specified), injury incidence per match played was greater at the start of the season and following the mid-season break.112 However, the study possessed poor study quality (online supplemental table 2) and provided limited statistical analysis assessing this relationship; therefore, more evidence may be required in order to support these findings within this cohort. In men’s professional rugby union players, the phase of the season was associated with components of the athlete burnout questionnaire, with the inseason period leading to perceived reduced accomplishment compared to preseason and postseason phases. However, physical exhaustion was reported at a greater frequency during the season compared with preseason and postseason phases.76

In men’s rugby league, injury incidence was greater in the earlier in the season for both professional61 and semiprofessional players.109 The effect of playing season (summer vs winter competition) was investigated as a contributor to injury, with summer competitions contributing to greater injury incidence when compared with winter.110 111 113

Physical characteristics and body composition

Physical characteristics and body composition were identified by thirty-two studies (union n=19, league n=6, both union and league n=2, sevens n=1, union and sevens n=1) as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes.5460 71 73 74 87 93 94 115,138 Within physical characteristics, body composition (n=12), physical capabilities (n=7) and movement competency (n=5) were found to contribute to increased injury risk and concussion, stress urinary incontinence and reduced mental health; studies are summarised in online supplemental tables 5 and 6.

Physical characteristics

Nine studies (union n=5, league n=3 and sevens n=1) reported physical characteristics to be contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby players. Lower physical performance scores were associated with increased injury risk including speed and agility,93 125 aerobic capacity,94 121 strength93 120 130 and between-limb eccentric strength imbalances.74 Stakeholders perceived that insufficient physical conditioning, speed and strength were associated with greater concussion risk in men’s and women’s amateur rugby union players.54

In rugby union, movement competency has been identified as a contributing factor to injury risk by five studies. Lower movement competency scores were related to increased injury risk. Generally, a functional movement screening (FMS) composite score <14 differentiated injured and not injured players,116 118 129 while one study stated that an FMS composite score of 16 led to lower injury incidence.117 Additionally, in men’s academy rugby union, balance contributed to injury risk with a significant correlation between forward right directional control and injury incidence;126 however, this was only identified by one study which was lacking quality; therefore, greater investigation may be required (online supplemental supplement table 2).

Neck range of motion (union n=2)127 135 and neck strength (union n=1, both union and league n=2)87 119 136 were associated with injury and concussion risk. In men’s and women’s rugby sevens players, lower hip flexor flexibility (Thomas Test) contributed to increased injury risk in one study.125 Shoulder and thoracic range of motion was found as a contributor to injury in two studies. In men’s rugby league, differences were present in internal rotation of the shoulder in injured and non-injured players.124 In men’s amateur youth rugby union, reduced upper thoracic mobility and decreased scapular upward rotation angle were significant independent risk factors for traumatic shoulder dislocation.137 In men’s rugby union, active range of motion during lateral flexion in both left and right during preseason testing127 and neck extension strength ≤41 kg119 have been found to contribute to injury and concussion respectively. Similarly, in both men’s rugby union and league, trivial but statistically significant differences in peak isometric neck extension force have been found between participants who were subsequently injured and their uninjured counterparts.87

Body composition

11 studies (rugby union n=8, rugby league n=2, rugby union and sevens n=1) identified body composition as a contributor to injury and concussion risk. In men’s rugby union, greater body mass index (BMI) or body mass was found to contribute to increased injury risk in both open age men73 115 122 and youth players71 and also contributed to contact breast injuries in women’s union and sevens players.132 In contrast, players with a lower BMI were found to have a greater injury incidence than those with a higher BMI.128 One study assessed body mass as a contributor to concussion risk in professional players, finding lighter players were found to be at greater risk of concussion.60 Two studies identified BMI as a contributor to stress urinary incontinence in amateur, semiprofessional and professional women’s rugby union.131 133 One study identified body composition, namely, percentage of lean and fat mass, as a contributor to reduced mental health scores.138

Body type and body measurements have also been identified as contributors to injury risk in men’s rugby union. Amateur players with an endomorphic somatotype and greater chest to waist ratio have been found to be at with a greater risk of injury, while an ectomorphic somatotype decreased the risk of injury.122 Additionally, players with greater waist and hip circumferences associated with a greater risk of injury115 and taller players had an increased risk of concussion.60 Lumbar multifidus size was also identified as a contributor to upper limb injury123 and concussion.134

Playing surface

Nine studies (union n=7, league n=1, sevens n=1) in men’s players identified playing surface to be associated with injury.139,146 Six studies included in the review found playing surface type (grass, artificial or hybrid) to be a contributor to injury risk in men’s professional rugby union. Multiple studies show injury incidence rates or burden were greater on pitches containing artificial grass compared with natural grass.92140 143,145 More specifically, when playing on artificial turf, the risk of minor injuries increased when compared with playing on grass turf.141 146 There was also a greater risk during contact140; this has been found to be specific to players who were tackled on artificial turf in comparison to grass.144 Players were at a greater risk of concussion144 or moderate injury146 when playing on grass. Overall, the research shows that injury risk on playing surface types may be site or severity specific; therefore, more evidence may be required to confirm whether playing surface material acts as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes. Ground hardness may also be a contributor to biopsychosocial outcomes, with harder ground conditions found to contribute to injury in rugby union, league and sevens. Amateur rugby union players and rugby sevens players were more likely to sustain injury on hard ground than soft,92 139 and in semiprofessional rugby league, harder ground conditions were found to lead to greater injury incidence compared with firm, slippery and heavy pitch surfaces.142

Pregnancy and childbirth

Pregnancy and childbirth were found to be a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by one study, with players who had given birth more likely to experience stress urinary incontinence and amateur players more greatly affected than professional players.131

Pressure

Pressure was cited as a contributor to burnout in men’s professional rugby union by two studies. Sources of pressure identified as contributors included expectations and criticisms from the public and media, along with pressures to perform on the pitch due to selection status depending on performance.30 77

Psychological needs fulfilment

Psychological needs fulfilment has been identified by one study as a contributor to burnout in men’s academy rugby union players,147 finding that players who were categorised as high burnout players scored lower for autonomy and competency.

Quality of life

Quality of life was found to be a contributor to injury risk in amateur men’s rugby union players by one study, finding that a lower score on the mental component of the The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey questionnaire (SF-36 V.2) assessing health-related quality of life was associated with increased injury risk.128

Selection status

The selection status of players (eg, starters vs non-starters) has been examined as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in four studies.76 77 148 149 The selection status of players contributed to negative psychological outcomes, namely, components of burnout, changes in mood states and injury. Professional players felt pressure to perform as selection status is performance dependent.77 Non-selected players more frequently reported feelings of reduced accomplishment and increased stress of not being able to perform if they are not selected.76 77 In university rugby union, regular starters more frequently reported greater tension, anger and depression than non-starters/reserve players.148 In men’s rugby union, despite similar injury rates, replacement players suffer greater total injury burden compared with starting players for lower limb injuries.149

Stress

Stress was identified as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by three studies included in the review. In men’s professional rugby league, stress has been found to contribute to injury150 and symptoms of mental ill health102 and in women’s rugby union, stress relating to the perceived demands of rugby outweighing the personal resources was cited by players as a contributor to symptoms of burnout.97

Training age

Training age, or years of experience within rugby, was highlighted as a contributor to negative biopsychosocial outcomes by four studies (rugby union n=2, league n=1, rugby union and sevens n=1) assessing injury (n=3) and concussion (n=1) as negative biopsychosocial outcomes.128151,153 Three studies were in men’s populations and one in women’s rugby. In youth (U11 to U17) community rugby union players, longer duration of rugby participation was linked to an increase in the number of sport-related concussions players experience.152 Meanwhile, in open age rugby union, fewer years of rugby participation were associated with increased injury risk.128 However, these associations are weak; therefore, the findings may lack practical applicability.128 In rugby league, higher training age was associated with increased injury risk, specifically broken down into rugby league training age contributing to bone injuries, resistance training age contributing to hamstring injuries and cardiovascular training age contributing to overall injury risk.151 In community women’s rugby union and sevens, a greater number of seasons played was associated with higher lifetime prevalence of breast injury.153

Training and match load

30 studies reported training and match load to contribute to negative biopsychosocial outcomes including injury (n=23), illness (n=1), reductions in wellness (n=1), burnout (n=4) and stress (n=3) (online supplemental tables 5 and 6).3064 77 86 89 94 97 109 154,175 Studies were in men’s rugby union (n=16), women’s rugby union (n=1) and men’s rugby league (n=13). No studies were identified in rugby sevens.

Both quantitative and qualitative approaches were used to investigate the contribution of training and match load to negative biopsychosocial outcomes. Qualitative analysis found that players believe heavy training and playing demands contribute to symptoms of burnout in men’s30 77 and women’s97 rugby union.5

Training load

In men’s rugby league, greater overall training load was associated with injury incidence in two studies,109 164 although this has been found to be metric and position specific.165 171 In men’s rugby union, high training loads have been found to contribute to injury risk,160 severity156 and symptoms of stress.162 However, a U-shaped curve may exist with both over and underexposing players contributing to injury risk.155 156 162 In men’s rugby league, 1-week170 or 2-week loads161 deemed ‘high’ have been reported to contribute to injury risk.

Acute increases in training exposure compared with longer term averages (ie, chronic load) have been found to contribute to negative biopsychological outcomes (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). Week-to-week changes in training load have been reported to contribute to injury risk in men’s rugby union158 160 and league173 players. Similarly, a high acute:chronic ratio (1.23–>2.11) contributed to injury risk in rugby league.161 169 170 In rugby union players, a high acute:chronic training172 and contact175 load was also found to contribute to injury; however, this link was heavily dependent on the methods used to calculate the load measures.172 As a result, the flaws within acute:chronic ratio calculations should be considered when interpreting these associations.176 177 In rugby league, increases in internal training load have been reported to contribute to increased risk of self-reported illness.157 Periods of intense training (short periods where training load was significantly increased than normal) contributed to reductions in well-being in rugby league159 and symptoms of stress in rugby union.167 Weekly structure and individual session load within a training week were found to be contributors to injury, with gym session intensity, field session volume and intensity contributing to injury risk depending on the day they are performed.174

Five studies (league n=3, union n=2) investigated preseason training exposure as a contributor to injury (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). In men’s rugby league, reduced preseason participation was associated with increased likelihood of injury throughout the competitive season in professional players; there is limited evidence to support this due to poor study quality (online supplemental table 2).173 However, in semiprofessional players, greater preseason training exposure contributed to an increased injury risk.163 178 In amateur rugby union, in one study, every week of preseason, a player was exposed to increased likelihood of injury during the season.86 In addition, qualitative analysis reported heavy training and playing demands to be the most cited contributor to stress and negative affective states in preseason in rugby union players.64

Match load

A greater volume of match minutes played has been reported to contribute to an increased risk of injury in three studies in men’s rugby union.89 166 Running and collision exposure within a match have been found to contribute to injury risk in one study, with high-speed running exposure contributing to musculoskeletal injury and collision exposure contributing to head and neck injuries.154 In rugby league, changes in match intensity, duration and load contributed to injury risk during matches.109 Tackling exposure and injury risk within a match have also been explored within rugby league, showing the number of tackles experienced within 1 min and 10 min periods contributing to injury risk within the tackle.168

Transition between rugby environments

The transition between competitive environments (eg, club and international rugby) was identified as a contributor to burnout in men’s rugby union players by two studies.30 77 The studies suggested transitions between competitive environments led to increases in stress, and limited time between competitions led to feelings of exhaustion and sport devaluation.

Travel and away fixtures

Travel and away fixtures were identified as a contributor in men’s rugby union by three studies.64 179 180 Long-distance travel for fixtures was identified as a contributor to illness, with international travel of more than 5 hours being associated with increased incidence of all illness.180 During interviews, early years professional players highlighted that travel was a contributor to stress and negative affective states, with 4/12 players citing as such.64 Playing away fixtures has been identified as a contributor to concussion risk and severity in school and university players, with a greater number of concussions compared with expected values and also increased number and severity of symptoms 48 hours post injury.6 179 However, this evidence is limited due to only being supported by one study of poor quality (online supplemental table 2).179

Wellness

One study has identified wellness and sleep to contribute to illness in men’s rugby league, with reductions in self-reported total wellness (sleep quality, motivation to train, stress and soreness) identified as possible predictors for upper respiratory tract symptoms.181

Summary

The current literature has focused on certain factors that lead to negative biopsychosocial outcomes within rugby players. This work has primarily focused on physical contributors and outcomes, with physical characteristics and body composition (31 studies), training and match load (30 studies), previous injury (26 studies) and factors within the contact event (22 studies) being the most identified contributor groups. Some contributors within the review have a limited evidence base because of either the small number of studies in this area (eg, quality of life) or limitations due to the methodological quality (eg, playing away as a contributor to concussion severity). The review process revealed that eight negative biopsychosocial outcomes are present in the literature, with injury and concussion the most investigated outcomes. Physical contributors (eg training and match load, physical characteristics and previous injury) and outcomes (eg, injury and concussion) feature prominently within the review, likely due to their objective nature and the quantifiable relationship between contributors and outcomes. It is important that future research seeks to evaluate all aspects of the biopsychosocial relationship when assessing contributors to negative outcomes in rugby players and considers the interconnection between biological, psychological and social factors within the study design to allow a comprehensive assessment of biopsychosocial relationship.

All three rugby codes (union, league and sevens) were investigated in this review. The review found that rugby union was the most researched with 104 studies, followed by rugby league (46 studies) and then rugby sevens (six studies). Some contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes are likely code-specific, due to the unique physical, technical/tactical and socioeconomic demands of codes and cohorts. Of note, most studies included in the review focused on men’s cohorts, with only seven studies investigating solely women’s cohorts and 25 focusing on youth cohorts. Given the physiological and psychosocial differences between male and female athletes and differences between adults and youth players, it is important to ensure female and youth rugby are adequately represented in the literature, as any under-representation in the research may lead to an omission of female-specific (considering both sexes and gendered contributors) and youth-specific contributors.

The inclusion of studies which reported a link between contributors and negative biopsychosocial outcomes allowed for contributors to be compiled for the first time. This can then be refined by stakeholders during part 2 of the CoNBO project,20 which may also highlight contributors who do not currently feature in the literature. It is also important to acknowledge that conflicting findings may exist between contributors and outcomes. For example, the results of the review highlighted that speed into contact is a contributor to injury and concussion incidence 37 38 40 however, in female varsity rugby union, no association was found between speed into contact and concussion risk.51 This further highlights the need for cohort-specific contributors to negative outcomes. It cannot be assumed that since a contributor to negative outcomes in one population exists, that those findings are generalisable to all cohorts and future research should aim to bridge the knowledge gap between cohorts.

It is also possible that the contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes identified in this review may only represent a portion of the contributors that are observed within practice. The multifaceted nature of both rugby as a sport, rugby players within the sport and the interaction between multiple contributors and negative outcomes is potentially difficult to capture within the research. Similarly, some potential contributors may be difficult to evaluate within a practical setting and within certain cohorts and populations. In addition, studies with limitations within their methodological designs may lead to factors being defined as contributors within the research, but practically they may have little impact on the negative biopsychosocial outcomes players experience day-to-day. Part 2 of the CoNBO project will allow stakeholders to add perceived contributors not found within the literature.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this systematic review is that the eligibility criteria only included studies reporting a link between contributors and negative biopsychosocial outcomes. This led to a positive result bias within the review and made best evidence synthesis of contributors not possible. There are potentially subjective elements within the inclusion and exclusion criteria, particularly when determining whether studies have assessed the relationship between contributor and outcomes, which may have also introduced perceived variability in study selection and interpretation. However, all studies were screened by two researchers who were in agreement and a third researcher if required. The agreement may have been attributable to the similarity in background between the researchers. The breadth and size of the current review may also act as a limitation as it may limit the level of rigorous synthesis, due to varying study types, which may be possible in smaller reviews. Furthermore, the research design of studies included within the review includes observational data without causal methods. While a ‘contributor’ has a causal connotation, to date the research has focused on descriptive or predictive methods. Such analyses can be useful in identifying which athletes may be a target population for interventions, but they do not provide information on the causal mechanism that should be intervened with.182 Therefore, further research is required in assessing the causal mechanisms of negative biopsychosocial outcomes. Finally, this review did not include studies relating to retired rugby players. Exploring the experiences of this population would provide valuable insights for rugby-related policy and practice; therefore, future research would benefit from investigating the perspectives and challenges faced by retired players.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to undertake a systematic review of literature to describe the contributors that result in negative biopsychosocial outcomes in the rugby codes and inform a Delphi study as part 2 of the CoNBO project. The review provides an overview of 151 studies, which have assessed the contributors to negative biopsychosocial outcomes in rugby players, identifying a total of 29 contributor groups and eight outcome groups. Future research may benefit from narrowing the scope to enable a more focused and rigorous synthesis of specific relationships. Additionally, the findings within this review are based largely on observational data, with a lack of causal research present. Therefore, future studies should focus on robust study design to demonstrate cause and effect to inform targeted interventions.
