Moving cardio-oncology upstream: Integrating cardiovascular prevention into routine cancer care
Authors: Meiyan Liu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) represents a major adverse prognostic factor and cause of mortality among cancer survivors, particularly those with breast cancer. Shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including tumor biology, chronic systemic inflammation, genetic susceptibility, and the cardiotoxic effects of anticancer therapies, drive this increased risk.1 Despite significant advances in the management of cancer treatment-related cardiovascular toxicity (CTR-CVT), there remains a significant gap between basic research and clinical translation, and many healthcare centers worldwide still lack standardized cardio-oncology management pathways. This review integrates recent mechanistic insights with emerging clinical evidence to propose a practical framework for contemporary cardio-oncology care, and highlights critical unresolved challenges for future research.1 The four core pillars of modern cardio-oncology are illustrated in Figure 1 to clarify its core concepts and key priorities.

Mechanistic complexity of CTR-CVT

Accumulating evidence indicates that CTR-CVT represents a heterogeneous multifactorial syndrome that disrupts cardiac structure, metabolism, and vascular homeostasis through three interconnected core pathophysiological mechanisms.2 One major mechanism involves tumor-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL[interleukin]-1α, IL-6) that drive persistent myocardial inflammation. Notably, empagliflozin mitigates this cytokine-mediated injury, as recently shown in doxorubicin-treated models.3 Another contributing pathway is excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and progressive chronic cardiac dysfunction. This aligns with the emphasis in the special issue article on the multifactorial and interactive nature of the CTR-CVT pathophysiology.4 Withaferin A (WA), a natural steroidal lactone, has demonstrated promising antioxidant and cardioprotective properties in preclinical models. However, well-designed clinical trials are required to validate its efficacy and safety in humans.5 A third mechanism relates to sustained renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation in the setting of cancer, which drives pathological cardiac remodeling. The synergistic effect of angiotensin II and anthracycline chemotherapies exacerbates myocardial fibrosis and ventricular dysfunction, whereas RAAS inhibitors exert broad cardioprotective effects and represent a key intervention strategy for high-risk cancer patients.6

Coronary artery calcification (CAC) in cancer patients

Although coronary artery calcification (CAC) is widely utilized for cardiovascular risk stratification in the general population, cancer patients exhibit a distinct “calcification paradox”. After adjustment for confounding factors, the prevalence of CAC is similar between cancer patients and non-cancer patients. However, the severity, density, and Agatston score of CAC in cancer patients are significantly higher.7 This dissociation—normal prevalence but heightened severity—constitutes the 'calcification paradox". Therefore, reliance solely on dichotomous CAC assessment (presence/absence) may significantly underestimate cardiovascular risk and compromise clinical decision-making. This paradox arises from shared pathophysiological pathways between cancer and CVD, including chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and vascular dysfunction. Tumor-associated chronic inflammation promotes the development of a pro-calcification microenvironment, accelerates advanced vascular calcification, and drives the formation of abundant noncalcified vulnerable plaques that may not be adequately characterized by conventional computed tomography (CT). Therefore, cardiovascular risk assessment in cancer patients requires a comprehensive strategy that extends beyond binary CAC grading. It is recommended to conduct routine baseline CAC screening for elderly patients, high-risk younger individuals, and all patients receiving potentially cardiotoxic anticancer therapies.8 Clinicians should account for the burden of cancer-related inflammation, implement early preventive strategies, and order additional advanced imaging modalities to detect vulnerable plaques, thereby enabling personalized risk stratification and reducing cardiovascular events.

Diagnostic challenges and multidisciplinary management of rare cardiac tumors

Primary cardiac tumors are extremely rare, often presenting with nonspecific symptoms that mimic common cardiovascular disorders, resulting in delayed diagnosis, rapid disease progression, severe hemodynamic compromise, thromboembolic events, and poor overall survival.9 Cardiac intimal sarcoma is a highly aggressive primary cardiac malignancy predominantly affecting the right atrium. It is frequently misdiagnosed as a cardiac metastasis, particularly when concomitant distant liver metastases obscure its primary cardiac origin.10

Timely and accurate diagnosis of cardiac intimal sarcoma relies critically on multidisciplinary imaging-pathology collaboration. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) serves as the first-line screening modality, while cardiac magnetic resonance and computed tomography provide detailed anatomical and tissue invasion assessment. Histopathological evaluation combined with immunohistochemical staining is essential for differential diagnosis. Murine double minute 2 (MDM2)/cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) amplification represents a highly specific molecular biomarker for definitive diagnosis of cardiac intimal sarcoma.11 Prognosis is strongly correlated with early diagnosis and metastatic status. Targeted therapies against MDM2 and platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα) have shown preliminary efficacy. However, large-scale clinical trials are needed to validate their safety and effectiveness.12 This paradigm underscores that multidisciplinary integration is indispensable for the management of rare and complex cardio-oncological conditions.

Psychological stress and CTR-CVT

Psychological stress is an often overlooked yet significant modifiable cardiovascular risk factor in cancer patients, a connection that has also been highlighted in a recent special issue article.4 Mechanistically, chronic psychological stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), resulting in neurohormonal dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, myocardial fibrosis, and systemic inflammation, all of which further exacerbate CTR-CVT.13 Recent systematic evidence confirms that comprehensive psycho-oncological interventions significantly improve treatment adherence, alleviate symptom burden, and enhance health-related quality of life in cancer patients with elevated cardiovascular risk.14 These interventions mitigate the bidirectional link between psychological distress and cardiotoxicity, emphasizing that mental health may represent an important and potentially modifiable contributor to cardiovascular outcomes in cancer patients. To enhance clinical implementation, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scale for anxiety and the Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item (PHQ-9) scale for depression are recommended. A practical model for integrating psychosocial support into the standard cardio-oncology workflow includes: (1) routine baseline and follow-up psychological screening as well as cardiovascular risk assessment; (2) patients with mild distress receive psychoeducational resources, those with moderate distress receive brief counseling interventions, and patients with severe distress should be referred to mental health specialists; (3) the multidisciplinary team integrates psycho-oncologists into cardio-oncology clinics to enable shared decision-making and coordinate care.

Clinical integration of cardio-oncology into routine cancer care

Advances in systemic anticancer therapies have substantially improved long-term cancer survival. However, this success has been accompanied by a substantial increase in cardiovascular complications, which have become the leading non-cancer cause of death in cancer survivors. CTR-CVT, cancer-associated CAC, and rare primary cardiac tumors pose complex clinical challenges that require integrated multidisciplinary management. The integration of cardiology into cancer care is important for the prevention of cardiovascular risk, prevention and early detection of CTR-CVT, preservation of cardiac function, and improvement of overall cancer prognosis. Overview of four key clinical strategies: (1) pretreatment cardiovascular risk assessment, (2) personalized monitoring during cancer therapy, (3) pharmacological cardioprotection, and (4) multidisciplinary care delivery. Pretreatment cardiovascular risk stratification

Pretreatment cardiovascular risk stratification is the cornerstone of cardio-oncology care, enabling personalized treatment and targeted cardiac protection. As recommended by the 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines on Cardio-Oncology, all patients scheduled to receive potentially cardiotoxic anticancer therapies should undergo a comprehensive baseline cardiovascular assessment. This assessment should include clinical evaluation, measurement of cardiac biomarkers, such as high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and cardiac imaging, with TTE with assessment of global longitudinal strain (GLS) as the first-line modality.15 In addition, our special issue article summarizes 10 core biomarkers and proposes population-specific biomarker selection strategies for pediatric and geriatric cancer patients.4

Personalized cardiovascular monitoring

Personalized cardiovascular monitoring should be conducted based on anticancer therapy, cumulative dose, baseline cardiovascular risk, and anticipated timing of cardiotoxicity, as explicitly outlined in the 2022 ESC Guidelines on Cardio-Oncology.15 Serial measurement of biomarkers is the cornerstone of CTR-CVT monitoring. However, the cost-effectiveness of universal serial biomarker testing remains unproven in unselected populations. While serial testing improves risk stratification in high-risk patients, its incremental value is minimal for those who receive low-cardiotoxicity therapy or have lower baseline cardiovascular risk. Indiscriminate serial testing leads to unnecessary healthcare expenditures, false-positive results, and potentially harmful cardioprotective interventions in low-risk individuals. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) has emerged as a sensitive tool for detecting subclinical cardiac dysfunction prior to a measurable decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). However, whether GLS surveillance should be universally implemented for all cancer patients or restricted to high-risk subgroups remains a major area of debate. Universal GLS screening maximizes sensitivity to early cardiac toxicity but is associated with substantial resource utilization. Conversely, restricting serial GLS assessment to patients receiving high-cardiotoxicity treatment or those with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors optimizes resource allocation without compromising clinical outcomes.

Pharmacological cardioprotection strategies

Cardioprotective drugs have been shown to reduce the risk of CTR-CVT without compromising antitumor efficacy, consistent with the 2022 ESC Guidelines on Cardio-Oncology.15 For primary prevention, agents such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), provide sustained cardiac protection for patients receiving high-dose anthracycline regimens, chest radiation therapy, or combination cardiotoxicity regimens, as well as in patients with pre-existing hypertension and left ventricular hypertrophy.16 However, in low-risk patients receiving minimal cardiotoxic treatment, primary prevention may provide limited clinical benefit and may expose them to unnecessary adverse reactions. In addition, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have become an emerging cardioprotective strategy for mitigating CTR-CVT. It was demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce myocardial fibrosis, oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release in patients treated with anthracyclines, while also improving myocardial strain and preserving LVEF.17 In addition, some natural products, nano-targeted agents, and combination therapies for CTR-CVT have been mentioned in our special issue article.4

Multidisciplinary cardio-oncology care delivery

Multidisciplinary collaboration between cardiologists, oncologists, and other relevant specialists is essential, as strongly recommended by the 2022 ESC Guidelines on Cardio-Oncology.15 Specialized cardio-oncology clinics have strengthened risk detection and care coordination. High-priority referrals cover patients who have received high-risk treatment, have intracardiac masses, have pre-existing cardiovascular disease, or have undergone previous cardiotoxicity treatments. Integrated decision-making and shared medical records ensure continuous standardized care for cardio-oncology patients.

Future directions and unmet clinical needs in cardio-oncology

Despite progress in CTR-CVT mechanisms, risk stratification, and management, there are still critical knowledge gaps in translational research, clinical implementation, and global health equity that limit standardized and accessible cardio-oncology care. There is an urgent need for targeted efforts to address unmet needs: (1) optimizing long-term monitoring of cancer survivors, (2) standardizing novel biomarkers for early risk prediction, (3) developing targeted anti-inflammatory and metabolic therapies, (4) advancing precision treatment of rare primary heart tumors, and (5) promoting global equity in cardio-oncology care.

Key priorities for clinical action

Optimized surveillance strategies for long-term cancer survivors

Current guidelines rely on short-term trials, with few studies extending beyond 5 years. There is an urgent need for large prospective cohorts and comprehensive risk models (treatment exposure, genetics, and lifestyle) to determine the late cardiovascular risk window for specific therapies in order to establish cost-effective and risk-stratified monitoring intervals. Validated predictive models should inform dynamic long-term follow-up rather than one-size-fits-all monitoring.

Standardization of novel biomarkers for early risk prediction

Promising markers (microRNAs, exosomes, and multi-omics features) suffer from detection heterogeneity and limited prospective validation. Pre-analytical variables, cut-off values, and incremental value relative to established biomarkers and imaging must be standardized. Prospective trials are needed to confirm their clinical utility in early subclinical injury detection.

Targeted anti-inflammatory and metabolic interventions

Chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation drive CTR-CVT and accelerate atherosclerosis. The repurposing of colchicine requires cancer-specific prospective data on both efficacy and safety.18 Metabolic strategies targeting insulin resistance and dyslipidemia also require rigorous testing to determine their efficacy in reducing myocardial injury. High-risk cancer populations should be prioritized for randomized controlled trials of anti-inflammatory and metabolic interventions.

Precision therapeutics for rare primary cardiac tumors

Primary cardiac sarcomas, particularly MDM2/CDK4-amplified intimal sarcoma, lack standardized treatment and have a poor prognosis. Preclinical data support MDM2 and PDGFRα targeted therapy, but there is a lack of large-scale prospective trials. International collaborative registries are important for verifying efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing of these agents. The role of immune checkpoint inhibitors for metastatic disease also requires urgent investigation.

Global health equity and implementation science: a critical forward-looking perspective

Cardio-oncology care faces profound global disparities. Recent population-based studies have consistently demonstrated that access to cancer supportive care varies dramatically in different income settings. Specialized cardio-oncology services are concentrated in high-income regions and tertiary care centers. Patients in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) may experience a disproportionate burden of CTR-CVT-related morbidity and mortality due to limited access to baseline cardiac screening, inconsistent surveillance strategies, and a lack of multidisciplinary cardio-oncology teams.19 The high prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and chronic inflammation in LMICs magnifies the cardiotoxic effects of anthracycline chemotherapy, chest radiation therapy, and targeted anticancer treatment. Unfortunately, preventive interventions are rarely included in routine cancer care in these settings. Simplified, low-cost, and scalable care models are needed. Priority implementation strategies include: (1) focusing on echocardiographic assessment, (2) implementing single baseline biomarker screening rather than continuous testing, and (3) developing and disseminating standardized digital decision support tools for risk stratification. Addressing global disparities in cardio-oncological care is not only an urgent health policy priority but also essential for translating advances in cancer survival into meaningful and equitable improvements in overall health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for all cancer patients.

Conclusion

Cardio-oncology provides patient-centered, holistic care that integrates cancer management with cardiovascular risk reduction. The substantial improvements in cancer survival have made CTR-CVT a critical long-term health issue that requires sustained multidisciplinary management throughout the cancer treatment trajectory and long-term survivorship. Current clinical priorities include standardized baseline cardiac risk screening, personalized cardiac monitoring tailored to treatment plans and individual risks, and the necessary interdisciplinary collaboration. Key future directions include translating mechanistic research results into clinical practice, identifying and validating novel biomarkers and cardioprotective agents, and promoting equitable healthcare integration. Integrating comprehensive cardiac protection into routine cancer care has the potential to significantly reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, optimize patient quality of life, and improve long-term overall survival.
