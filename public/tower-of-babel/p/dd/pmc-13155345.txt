Analytic Considerations for the Evaluation of Tuberculosis Preventative Therapy Effectiveness Among People Living with HIV
Authors: Lindsey M. Schader, Seth Zissette, Fadimatu Mishara, Macarthur Charles, Sarita Shah, David Benkeser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite data from randomized trials demonstrating the benefits of tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT) for people living with HIV, there remain gaps in the large-scale programmatic provision of TPT and understanding of its impact. The PROTECT study is a multi-country study that aims to evaluate the effectiveness of TPT programs on tuberculosis incidence and mortality. The study leverages electronic health records, which poses challenges in the design, analysis, and interpretation of the data. In this work, we describe the general analysis approach that will be adopted across countries for evaluation of the effectiveness of TPT programs.

1. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality for people living with HIV (PLHIV) (Gupta et al. 2015), accounting for one-third of all HIV-related deaths in 2020 (UNAIDS 2022). TB preventive treatment (TPT) is an intervention to reduce TB among PLHIV and has been recommended by the World Health Organization since 1993 (Churchyard et al. 2007; Akolo et al. 2010; Yanes-Lane et al. 2021). TPT consists of prescribing individuals a course of one or multiple drugs that are active against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Lobue and Menzies 2010). Data from several randomized trials has demonstrated the benefits of TPT in preventing TB and mortality (Salazar-Austin et al. 2019; Chakaya et al. 2021).

The United States President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) is an ongoing global program led by the U.S. government aimed at combating the HIV/AIDS epidemic (PEPFAR 2021c). PEPFAR has contributed over nearly $100 billion since its inception to scaling up services to prevent and treat HIV and related comorbidities through both direct programming and supportive partnerships with national health systems across fifty countries (El-Sadr et al. 2012; Fauci and Eisinger 2018; PEPFAR 2021c; 2021a). One component of PEPFAR’s stated guiding principles is to address the burden of TB among PLHIV in order to save and improve lives (PEPFAR 2021b). To this end, during the United Nations High Level Meeting on Tuberculosis in 2018, PEPFAR committed to providing TPT to all people in HIV care. As a result, PEPFAR-supported countries increased prescribing of TPT to PLHIV, targeting TPT to both people with newly diagnosed HIV and to individuals individuals already engaged in HIV care (Boyd et al. 2020; Peterson et al. 2021).

Despite these efforts, there remain major challenges and gaps in providing TPT to eligible PLHIV in program settings. Moreover, the extent to which the benefits of TPT observed in randomized trials are generalizable to programmatic settings is uncertain. Care programs for PLHIV face practical challenges in administering TPT that may not be observed in randomized trials and that may diminish the effectiveness of TPT in practice. These challenges include broader and more diverse populations of PLHIV than those enrolled in randomized trials and increased lack of adherence to prescribed TPT regimens relative to a controlled trial setting, among other reasons.

This important knowledge gap on the programmatic impact of TPT motivated the Preventing Occurrence of TB by Expanding Coverage of TPT among PLHIV (PROTECT) study. PROTECT includes six countries (Haiti, Kenya, Nigeria, Uganda, Ukraine, and Zimbabwe) that have received PEPFAR funding for expansion of TPT programs. The study aims to utilize health records and surveillance systems in these countries to better understand the effectiveness of TPT in PLHIV in a programmatic context. The study provides a unique opportunity to evaluate this effectiveness in a standardized way thereby generating comparable and robust evidence of the impact of TPT programs.

However, the use of historical health records and/or electronic medical records presents unique statistical challenges for evaluating the effectiveness of medical projects (Hernán and Robins 2016). Moreover, each PROTECT country has a unique protocol for data abstraction, in addition to unique policies pertaining to healthcare in PLHIV. This presents additional challenges to generating evidence on the effectiveness of TPT across countries in a robust and comparable way. In this work, we describe a unified statistical analysis plan that will be applied in the analysis of data generated in each PROTECT country. We also highlight areas where country-specific decisions or modifications may be required to reflect programmatic implementation of TPT, country-specific guidelines for TPT usage, and/or (lack of) data availability.

2. Study objectives

2.1. Study endpoints

The PROTECT study aims to quantify the effectiveness of TPT programs (defined in detail below) at (1) preventing TB and (2) preventing all-cause mortality.

2.2. Effectiveness measure

We define a specific effectiveness measure that is well aligned with expected implementation of TPT programs under PEPFAR and other funding mechanisms. We describe the effectiveness of TPT policies whereby individuals newly diagnosed with HIV with no signs or symptoms of TB are provided TPT in a timely manner after enrollment into HIV care. A detailed description of this effectiveness measure is included in Section 3.

2.3. Primary objective

The primary objective of this study is to quantify the effectiveness of TPT programs that treat individuals with approved TPT regimens within x weeks of enrollment into HIV care on the study endpoints. TPT program success will be defined as a significant reduction in either TB incidence or mortality. Considerations for the choice of x are described in Section 3.3. The PROTECT country protocols were reviewed by CDC, deemed not research, and conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy.1

3. Target trial

Randomized controlled trials are the gold standard for establishing efficacy of interventions. To learn about effectiveness of an intervention, we typically must rely instead on observational data. Nevertheless, it is often constructive to describe a hypothetical randomized controlled trial, the so-called target trial, that the analysis of an observational study is attempting to emulate. Defining target trials can be helpful in avoiding common pitfalls with observational data and to also identify the limitations of observational research (Hernán and Robins 2016; Hernán et al. 2016).

Table 1 summarizes our target trial. Such a trial would enroll participants at HIV clinics seeking care for a new or existing diagnosis of HIV. Individuals who have contraindications for or prior TPT or who have confirmed TB would be excluded from enrollment in the trial. Included participants would be randomized in an unblinded fashion to receive either TPT or no TPT. However, in contrast to a typical randomized trial, these individuals may not initiate TPT immediately. Instead, they are given a grace period of x weeks during which they can initiate TPT. This grace period is included in our target trial to reflect the real-world implementation challenges of TPT programs, where TPT may not be available immediately to individuals. Our target trial would utilize passive follow-up to ascertain outcomes on study participants. Follow-up would begin as soon as an individual’s eligibility is confirmed. This eligibility may be immediately confirmed for some participants; however, confirmation of eligibility in individuals exhibiting TB symptoms may be delayed, as there is a need to confirm that the individual does not have TB. In programmatic settings, where access to rapid diagnostics may be limited, confirmation of TB may take several weeks. The length of follow-up will be capped at T weeks for each individual. Individuals are followed up until first occurrence of death, study termination (T), or until right-censoring due to study dropout.

Table 1 highlights key challenges associated with mimicking our target trial using observational data collected within each country. Our analysis plan is designed to minimize the risk associated with these challenges. For the target trial of interest, a survival analysis using a Cox proportional hazards model would be appropriate to compare the hazard ratio between the intervention and control arms. In our analysis, we will similarly quantify efficacy by modeling hazards, but with a model that is inspired by but distinct from the Cox model, described in Section 4.5.

3.1. Eligibility criteria

Considerable effort will be made to standardize eligibility criteria across PROTECT countries. Where possible, the following eligibility criteria will be standardized and applied: (1) no TB at baseline, (2) no contraindications for TPT, and (3) within the standardized age range for the adult population or the pediatric population. Data may be collected for a variety of age ranges across countries, so a standardized age range for inclusion may be determined based on the age ranges available in all countries.

3.2. Time period for enrollment

We define an appropriate period of calendar time for “enrollment” of participants into the trial. Each country will select their own enrollment period based on data availability and dates of TPT roll-out within the country. During the enrollment period, individuals who enrolled into HIV care during that period are eligible for inclusion into the study. When choosing the appropriate enrollment period it is important to ensure that sufficient numbers of individuals both do and do not receive TPT within x weeks of enrollment into HIV care. An outcome-blinded preliminary analysis may be used to inform this decision, as necessary.

3.3. Defining the grace period

In real-world settings, it may be unrealistic for individuals to initiate TPT immediately after randomization or once eligibility criteria are met. This delay could be due to the need to confirm TB status or specific policies that prioritize achieving adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) prior to initiating TPT. This motivates the inclusion of a grace period in the analytic plan. A grace period is a defined amount of time that is allowed to pass between assignment to and initiation of TPT (Hernán and Robins 2016). The grace period used in our study will be determined a priori by each country of interest. An outcome-blinded analysis in each country could be used to support this selection.

3.4. Study termination time

We will define a cap on the amount of time that individuals are followed in the study (T weeks). Fundamentally, there is a limited period of time during which we expect an initial course of TPT to have any biological efficacy and we wish to restrict the study time to this period. For example, in randomized studies of isoniazid regimens for TPT, a follow-up duration of 12 months or more after treatment completion is common (Yanes-Lane et al. 2021). The decision for choice of T may depend on the length of the observational period over which data is collected in each country as well as the rates of censoring observed in the cohort. Our primary analysis will consider T=104 weeks, yielding a two-year follow-up period. However, if an outcome-blinded analysis identifies that insufficient individuals have at least two years of follow-up data, then the time period may be shortened in the interest of maintaining the stability of the analysis.

4. Primary analysis plan

4.1. Initial descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize baseline covariates for the cohort. Tabular displays will be used for descriptive statistics for the study cohort as a whole and stratified by intervention arm (e.g., individuals who initiated TPT within grace period and those who did not). Mean (standard deviation) and count (%) will be used to summarize continuous and categorical variables, respectively. If a variable is not approximately symmetrically distributed, median (IQR) will be used in place of mean (standard deviation). We may also present descriptive statistics on variables measured after baseline that are useful for characterizing the study cohort and clinical setting. One example of such a variable is HIV viral load, which is often measured after individuals have been taking ART for some period of time to assess whether the ART has sufficiently suppressed viral load.

4.2. Notation

The analysis for the primary outcome is based on approaches described by Cain et al. (2010) that are designed to minimize challenges associated with using observational data to mimic randomized trials highlighted in Table 1. Table 2 provides a key for the notation that will be used throughout the analysis plan. Uppercase letters are used to denote random variables and lowercase letters denote a realized value of the random variable. The lowercase letter z will be used to denote the hypothetical intervention in our target trial. To denote counterfactual outcomes we will use parentheses. For example, Nt(z) denotes the counterfactual event of interest in time interval t under intervention z. Time intervals will be of the form [t−1,t). To denote variables collected from baseline through time period t, we will use bar notation. Thus, for example, L¯t indicates all covariates measured from baseline through time interval t. We assume a natural ordering of variables in each time interval, {Ct,Nt,Dt,Lt,At}, although the true order of the recorded variables may deviate from this in practice. At baseline (the index visit date) we observe {L0,A0} only.

4.3. Selection of time windows

The analysis requires follow-up time to be discretized into time intervals. There are several relevant factors for considering choice of discretization interval. First, the models used in our analysis (e.g., (1)) will implicitly assume constant risk of TB within a given interval. Thus, the interval should be short enough for this assumption to be reasonable. The frequency of measurements may also be considered as special care is required to handle data for individuals with multiple clinic visits within a single time interval, and so we may wish to minimize these occurrences if possible. Lastly, decreasing the width of time intervals will increase computing requirements for the analysis. Longer time intervals will decrease computational needs. We recommend starting with a time window of one week and modifying the time window as appropriate based on the frequency of visits within the data. If there are many encounters within a week, we can shorten the time interval; if encounters are less frequent, we may widen the time window. Time window lengths may also vary based on the time in the study (e.g., shorter intervals earlier in the study and longer intervals after a milestone point). Throughout the text, we use the unit of weeks to describe the grace period and other time variables.

4.4. Intervention definition

As stated above, we are interested in evaluating the effectiveness of a TPT program wherein individuals initiate TPT within x weeks of eligibility relative to a control condition where individuals never initiate TPT. However, there are many possible distributions of TPT initiation times within the grace period and it is plausible that the effectiveness of a TPT program may vary based on this distribution of initiation times. For example, we may expect a TPT program wherein all individuals initiate TPT on the day their eligibility is confirmed to have higher effectiveness when compared with a TPT program wherein all individuals initiate TPT on the final day of the grace period. Ultimately, the most relevant distribution of TPT initiation times to select is the one that most closely mimics the expected natural distribution of TPT initiation times in each country were each TPT program able to enroll all eligible PLHIV in care. In Section 4.10, we suggest a distribution of TPT initiation times for our target trial that is based on the observed TPT initiation distribution during the grace period.

4.5. Estimand of interest

Here, we describe the causal quantity of interest for analysis of the TB endpoint. For this analysis, death is considered a competing risk for the TB endpoint. Relevant adaptations of our approach to analysis of the mortality endpoint are described in Section 4.16.

The causal quantity of interest for quantifying the effectiveness of TPT is a comparison of counterfactual cause-specific hazard curves, under each intervention of interest. We propose to quantify the effectiveness of the intervention on study endpoints using a marginal structural model (MSM). The approach is closely aligned with the quantity that is estimated using a standard Cox proportional hazards regression model in a randomized controlled trial. However, MSMs are designed to model the relationship between a counterfactual outcome and the intervention of interest, and estimation of the coefficients of a MSM requires special care to avoid bias due to non-randomized treatment assignment. Our marginal structural model is based on a pooled logistic regression model, which well approximates a Cox model when the event is rare (Hernán et al. 2000). TB and mortality are not necessarily rare endpoints in PLHIV; however, the incidence in a given week is likely to be low making the comparison to a Cox model appropriate.

Our marginal structural model describes the probability of the event of interest during a given time interval t+1 under intervention z given the participant is event free and still alive during time interval t. We denote this probability by P(Nt+1(z)=1∣Nt(z)=0,Dt(z)=0). Our marginal structural model approximates this counterfactual probability using the following formulation,

(1)logit{P(Nt+1(z)=1∣Nt(z)=0,Dt(z)=0)}=β0∗(t)+β1∗z.

This MSM models the hazard as a logistic-linear function of time (captured through the time-varying coefficient β0∗(⋅) and the intervention of interest z. If the MSM (1) imposes no additional structure on β0∗(⋅), then the model closely mimics a Cox proportional hazards regression model. We will utilize a flexible parametric formulation of β0∗(⋅) based on natural cubic smoothing splines, which should provide a reasonably flexible approach to modeling baseline cause-specific hazards.

Our estimand of interest is exp(β1∗). If the MSM (1) is correctly specified and the event is rare, then this has the interpretation as a counterfactual hazard ratio comparing the TPT program versus control condition. If the MSM is incorrectly specified, we suggest that exp(β1∗) maintains a relevant interpretation as a time-averaged comparison of intervention-specific incidence functions (Struthers and Kalbfleisch 1986). In general, we will adopt this latter view of the estimand and will not require the assumption of proportional hazards. Formally, we are viewing β0∗(⋅) and β1∗ as the values that would be obtained by projecting the true counterfactual cause-specific hazard function onto the working model (1), where the projection minimizes Kullback-Leibler distance (Neugebauer and Laan 2007; Rosenblum and Van Der Laan 2010; Yang et al. 2022).

4.6. Cumulative incidence functions

The above interpretation of parameters of our marginal structural model requires appropriate care. To supplement the counterfactual hazard ratio, we will report estimated cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) and confidence intervals for tuberculosis and all-cause mortality. The counterfactual cumulative incidence at time t under intervention z is denoted P(Nt(z)=1) for t=0,1,...,K. In the presence of competing events we can define the CIF as

(2)P(Nt(z)=1)=∑k=1tλN,k,z∏q=1k−1[1−λN,q,z−λD,q,z],

where

λN,k,z=P(Nk(z)=1∣Nk−1(z)=0,Dk−1(z)=0)andλD,k,z=P(Dk(z)=1∣Dk−1(z)=0,Nk−1(z)=0).

For the purposes of estimating λN,k,z, the MSM (1) could be used. However, (1) is somewhat restrictive in that it makes a proportional hazards assumption. Thus, for the purpose of computing cumulative incidence functions, we may instead consider an alternative formulation of the MSM,

logit{P(Nt+1(z)=1∣Nt(z)=0,Dt(z)=0)}=β0∗(t)+β1∗(t)z.

This formulation allows for a time-varying hazard ratio β1∗(⋅) associated with TPT. By modeling β1∗(⋅) flexibly, we can capture non-proportional hazards over time and have this reflected in our estimates of cumulative incidence. This analysis attempts to emulate what would be seen using classical Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard-based estimates of cumulative incidence estimator (i.e., the competing risks analogue of the Kaplan-Meier estimator) in the context of a randomized controlled trial. Again, we recommend estimating β1∗(⋅) with a flexible parametric approach. λD,k,z can be similarly modeled, but by reversing the roles of N and D in the modeling approach.

4.7. Controlling for confounding

In observational studies a major obstacle to drawing causal inferences is lack of randomization to the intervention, as well as the potential for informative right-censoring. A key assumption for drawing causal inferences from observational data is the sequential randomization assumption which states that treatment during time interval t and the counterfactual outcome of interest at future time points are independent given past covariates (Robins et al. 1999). We also must assume that right-censoring during time interval t and the outcome of interest at future time points are independent given past covariates. For both of these assumptions to hold we must adjust for appropriate confounders. DAG theory will be used to select the appropriate set of baseline and time-varying confounders to adjust for in analyses. These confounders should generally include any measured characteristic that is related to the outcome of interest (active TB/mortality) and either (i) probability of TPT initiation; (ii) timing of TPT initiation; (iii) probability of right-censoring, while a more conservative strategy should be to simply select all covariates that are related to the outcome of interest (VanderWeele and Shpitser 2011). Each country should carefully consider which covariates may be most appropriate given localized knowledge of TPT distribution and TB epidemiology. Table 3 lists confounders that may be generally relevant to control for confounding, depending on the outcome of interest in the analysis (active TB or mortality). When appropriate, these covariates will be included in propensity score models, described in Section 4.9. Time-varying confounders (confounders measured at follow-up visits) may not be available in the data depending on the country of interest. This limitation can be addressed to some extent via sensitivity analysis (Section 6.4).

4.8. Deriving time-varying confounders from irregularly spaced clinic visits

Country-specific policies typically dictate a cadence of clinic visits after enrollment into HIV care. These policies often include frequent (e.g., weekly) visits early after enrollment into care with the frequency of visits lessening over time. However, in practice, visits dates may be irregularly spaced. Given our choice to discretize time intervals into weeks, we require a strategy for mapping irregularly spaced clinic visits into a set of weekly time-varying covariates that can be used to control for confounding. To this end, the following algorithm will be applied. To derive Lt, we will compute the calendar date of the start of week t. We will then identify in the database the k closest visits prior to this date. All relevant covariates obtained in these k visits will be summarized to create Lt. For an explicit example, consider the case that k=3 and that ART adherence is available at each follow-up visit. Thus, Lt could include a component that summarizes ART adherence over the past three visits. For example, we may consider average adherence to ART over the past three visits or we may instead consider whether adequate adherence was achieved at any of the three previous visits. The choice of summary measure should be dictated by conversations with HIV clinicians in each country to determine the specific patterns of adherence that may influence a clinician’s decision to prescribe TPT. We may also include as part of Lt the number of days between the start of week t and each of the three clinic visits. Such variables would allow for appropriate interactions that may reflect the clinical reality of diminishing impact of clinical information from the more distant past influence current treatment decisions. For the primary PROTECT analysis, we will work with in-country partners and HIV providers to define a relevant set of time-varying covariates to be included in the analysis.

4.9. Propensity score model and estimation

In order to estimate the parameters of the MSM, we must first build inverse propensity score weights. There are two propensity scores that we need to estimate: (i) the propensity for TPT during each time interval; (ii) the propensity for right-censoring throughout follow-up. The propensity for treatment in time interval t can be expressed as:

(4)P(At=1∣L¯t,A¯t−1,Nt=0,Dt=0,Ct=0)

The propensity for right-censoring during time interval t can be expressed as:

(5)P(Ct=1∣L¯t−1,A¯t−1,Nt−1=0,Dt−1=0,Ct−1=0)

These propensities may vary over time. We suggest modeling the propensity scores with pooled logistic regression that incorporates smoothing over time. For example, consider the simple situation where only baseline variables L0=(L1,0,L2,0,…,Lk,0) are needed to control for confounding. In this case the propensity score model for TPT initiation during time interval t of the grace period could be

(6)logit{P(At=1∣L0,At−1=0,Nt=0,Dt=0,Ct=0)}=α0(t)+α1L1,0+⋯+αkLk,0.

Note that this model describes the probability for initiating TPT during time interval t as a function of baseline covariates L0 given that an individual: (i) has not previously initiated TPT (At−1=0); (ii) has not previously experienced the study endpoint (Nt=0); (iii) has not previously experienced the competing event (Dt=0); and (iv) is not right-censored (Ct=0). We note that if an individual has previously initiated TPT (i.e., At−1=1) then At=1 with probability 1. This explains why the model (6) conditions on no previous TPT initiation, Ak−1=0, and indicates that the parameters of (6) should be estimated using person-time observations where TPT was not initiated through the prior time point. In (6), α0(t) is a time-specific intercept, which could be flexibly modeled, e.g., using cubic smoothing splines.

More complex models could also be used if desired. In particular, investigators may wish to consider including interactions between calendar time of enrollment and time since initiation of ART. These interaction terms would reflect how the distribution of TPT initiation times after eligibility may have changed in each country over calendar time. The models could also be extended to allow for time-varying confounders.

For the censoring mechanism, we could also use a pooled logistic regression model. For example, again considering the simple case where only baseline covariates L0 are needed to control for confounding, we may consider the model

(7)logit{P(Ct=0∣L0,A¯t−1,Nt−1=0,Dt−1=0,Ct−1=0)}=γ0(t)+γ2L1,0+...+γkLk,0.

Note that this model describes the probability for not being right-censored during time interval t as a function of baseline covariates L0 and treatment history A¯t−1 given that an individual: (i) has not previously experienced the study endpoint (Nt−1=0); (iii) has not previously experienced the competing event (Dt−1=0); and (iv) is not previously right-censored (Ct−1=0). We note that if an individual was previously right-censored (Ct−1=1), then by definition Ct=1 with probability 1. As with model (6), our model for right-censoring (7) includes a time-specific intercept that could be flexibly modeled, e.g., using cubic smoothing splines. For illustration, we have left TPT history out of the right-hand side of the censoring model (7), though such models could also include TPT initiation and time of TPT initiation as adjustment variables. As with the treatment initiation model, the propensity model for right-censoring should be developed in collaboration with country-specific investigators with a detailed knowledge of health records systems.

Standard software packages may be used to estimate models of the form (6) and (7). Hernán et al. (2000) contains explicit examples of pooled logistic regression models used to estimate propensities. The accuracy of the propensity score models will be examined by examining the mean weight over time with values near to one indicating adequate model fit. Propensity scores will be trimmed according to guidelines in the literature to avoid overly large weights (Gruber et al. 2022).

4.10. Defining distribution of TPT initiation time in target trial

Recall that we are interested in an intervention where PLHIV initiate TPT within x weeks of meeting eligibility criteria. As mentioned above, the effect of the intervention of interest may vary depending on the distribution of TPT initiation times within x (Cain et al. 2010). Thus, we must select a target distribution of TPT initiation times within the grace period. We use fz,k(ak∣A¯k−1,L¯k−1) to represent the probability Ak=ak under intervention z in our target trial given past TPT history A¯k−1 and past covariate history L¯k−1.

Note that in our target trial under the control intervention (z=0), participants never initiate TPT so the control distribution is defined by f0,k(0∣A¯k−1,L¯k−1)=1 and f0,k(1∣A¯k−1,L¯k−1)=0 for each k and for all (A¯k−1,L¯k−1). In other words, in our target trial, individuals randomized to condition z=0 have zero probability of TPT initiation at any time.

On the other hand, in our target trial under the TPT condition (z=1), participants will initiate TPT during the grace period according to some distribution. We suggest using the observed data to determine a sensible distribution for TPT initiation. For the primary PROTECT analysis, we will use the logistic regression models described in the previous section to define this initiation distribution. In particular, we will use the same model selected to estimate the probability of initiating TPT at any point to estimate the probability of initiating TPT in the grace period amongst those who are observed to initiate TPT at any point in the grace period, P(At=1∣L0,L¯t−1,At−1=0,Nt=0,Dt=0,Ct=0,Ax=1). Thus, the estimand of our target trial imagines a counterfactual scenario wherein all participants initiate TPT within the grace period and they do so according to the conditional distribution of TPT start times observed in the real data. We believe this choice for initiation distribution yields a target trial that is grounded in the realities of the programmatic implementation of TPT and is more relevant than other common choices of initiation distribution used in the target trials literature. For example, a covariate-unconditional uniform distribution of TPT start during the grace period, a common choice in the target trials literature, would be inappropriate because it would not reflect the reality that the presence of TB symptoms would preclude initiation of TPT. Thus, parameters of the counterfactual distribution under this uniform TPT initiation distribution would not be identifiable.

Irrespective of the specific choice for f1,k, we note that any choice should respect the structural knowledge that for k≥x, f1,k(1∣A¯k−1,L¯k−1)=1 and f1,k(0∣A¯k−1,L¯k−1)=0 for all (A¯k−1,L¯k−1) indicating that by the end of time interval x, all participants assigned intervention in the target trial will have initiated TPT. Because we are interested in an estimand that only considers initiating TPT (rather than also adhering to TPT), once a participant initiates TPT, all future TPT indicators are set to 1, so f1,k(1∣Ak−1=1,…)=1 and f1,k(0∣Ak−1=1,…)=0.

4.11. “Cloning” of participants and “artificial” censoring

During the grace period, it is possible for participants to have data that is consistent with both interventions of the target trial (Hernán and Robins 2016; Cain et al. 2010). For example, an individual who initiates TPT at week t is consistent with both the intervention and the control arm of our target trial up until the t-th week, while after they initiate TPT, they are consistent with the intervention arm only and thus must be “censored” from the control arm.

To appropriately incorporate the information on both interventions that is provided by each individual, each participant is duplicated at baseline, generating two observations for the same individual which we will refer to as two clones. One clone will be “assigned” to the intervention arm (z=1) and the other to the control arm (z=0). Each clone will contribute data until they deviate from their assigned intervention. Here, we define deviation as (i) initiation of TPT for a clone assigned to the control group or (ii) lack of initiation of TPT before the end of the grace period when assigned to the intervention group. Individuals who initiate treatment at baseline do not have a clone under the control intervention, since that clone immediately deviates from the control condition and therefore does not need to be included in the dataset.

Participants who deviate from their observed intervention are artificially censored at the time of their deviation, so their data at future time points no longer contribute to the intervention that they deviated from. This artificial censoring is reflected in the weights that these observations receive. Once a participant deviates from the observed treatment they will receive a weight of zero for future time points. The technical calculation of these weights is described in Section 4.12.

4.12. Calculating weights

The propensity models described in Section 4.9 are used to define time-varying inverse probability weights (IPW) that are used to estimate the parameters of the MSM (1). The general idea of IPW is to up-weight/down-weight certain observations to create a pseudo-population that is balanced across the intervention arms in terms of risk factors for the outcome of interest. For example, if a particular observed individual initiates TPT even though they are unlikely to initiate TPT based on their covariates, then that individual is up-weighted to represent similar individuals who were not observed to initiate TPT. This process can be also be viewed as a re-balancing of covariates across intervention conditions to better mimic a randomized controlled trial. In the present problem, we require weights that appropriately re-balance to our target trial. That is, these weights must re-balance covariates in terms of their relationship to time of TPT initiation and right-censoring.

The equation for the inverse probability of treatment weights is as follows. For individual i, during time interval t, for the clone assigned to intervention z, the weight is calculated as Wi,z,t=Wi,z,taWi,tc, where

On the other hand, the weight Wc is designed to account for actual right-censoring due to individuals being lost to follow up.

4.14. Estimating parameters of the MSM

We can estimate the parameters of the MSM by fitting a weighted pooled logistic regression on the long dataset described in Section 4.13. Using this dataset, we fit a weighted logistic regression where the outcome of interest Nt+1 is regressed on intervention variable z and a smooth function of time since eligibility t (e.g., cubic splines), with weights for each observation given by Wi,z,t.

The nonparametric bootstrap can be used to estimate the standard error of the estimate for the coefficient of the intervention, β1. The nonparametric bootstrap procedure should include all steps of estimation. On the other hand, the bootstrap procedure should include (i) re-fitting the propensity scores on each bootstrap sample; (ii) cloning participants within each bootstrap sample; (iii) generating appropriate weights for each bootstrap sample; and (iv) re-fitting the pooled logistic regression MSM. Re-fitting these models allows the bootstrap procedure to appropriately incorporate all sources of uncertainty in the analysis.

Based on the bootstrap standard error, a level 0.05 Wald test will be used to test whether the cause-specific hazard curves between the two interventions are the same, i.e. H0:β1=0.

We will report the exponentiated parameter estimate of β1, the corresponding 95% Wald confidence interval, and p-value from the hypothesis test. We will also plot the adjusted hazard curves for each intervention over time with 95% bootstrap point-wise confidence intervals and simultaneous confidence bands. We will also compute percentile-based confidence intervals to confirm whether the large-sample normality required for the validity of the Wald-style intervals is approximately satisfied. Both confidence intervals will be reported in situations where they are found to yield significantly different inferences.

4.15. Estimating cumulative incidence functions

The CIF under treatment z, described in Section 6.3, is a function of λN,k,z and λD,k,z. To estimate the CIF we can use the MSMs described in Section 6.3 and the methods described in Section 4.14 to estimate λN,k,z and λD,k,z for all time points k=0,1,...,K. The resulting estimates, λ^N,k,z and λ^D,k,z, may then be plugged into equation (2) to generate an estimate of P(Nt(z)=1) for each time point of interest, t=0,1,...,K. We will plot the resulting cumulative incidence curves under each intervention over the course of follow-up time. In Section 6.3 of the supplementary material, we describe how these curves may be used to assess the presence of unmeasured confounding.

4.16. Modifications for analysis of the all-cause mortality endpoint

Death is a semi-competing event for TB because death precludes the occurrence of TB; however, occurrence of TB does not preclude the occurrence of death. The developments described previously for the TB analysis can be immediately applied to the mortality endpoint by re-defining Nk to be a counting process for mortality and then setting Dk=0 for all individuals and all time windows k. For the mortality endpoint, the MSM may simply be described as a model for counterfactual discrete hazard, rather than a cause-specific hazard.

To compute the CIF for mortality from these hazard models, we define

P(Nt(z)=1)=1−∏k=1t(1−λN,k,z∗)

where λN,k,z∗=P(Nk(z)=1∣Nk−1(z)=0). Again, we can estimate λN,k,z∗ for all time points k=0,1,...,K using MSM (3) and the aforementioned methods described in Section 4.14, by setting Dk=0 for all individuals and all time windows k. The resulting estimates, λ^N,k,z∗, can then be plugged into equation (10) to generate the final estimate of P(Nt(z)=1) for each time of interest, t=0,1,...,K.

An additional practical consideration for analysis of the mortality endpoint is that in many countries, there may be a different definition of right-censoring time for individuals for the purposes of the TB versus mortality analysis. For example, medical records may show that an individual is followed up to a certain time point after which time they do not attend scheduled clinic visits for a year. However, after one year, the individual re-enrolls in care. For the purposes of the TB endpoint, we may wish to censor this individual’s followup at the time of the last clinic visit prior to their temporary discontinuation of care. However, because the individual re-enrolled in care after one year, they should not be censored at this time for purposes of the mortality analysis, as it can be ascertained that they are indeed alive beyond the time point that they temporarily discontinued care. We will work with in-country partners to define appropriate definitions for right-censoring given knowledge of the specific data sources available for each country.

4.17. Handling missing data

The extent of missing data at baseline will be tabulated. Mean and mode imputation will be used for missing baseline data. For baseline variables with large amounts of missingness (e.g., >10%), an indicator variable for missingness will be created and included in models as a main term. Multiple imputation may also be considered as appropriate.

5. Conclusion

Evaluation of the effectiveness of interventions using observational data sources is challenging and requires sophisticated analysis techniques to recover objective inference about the effectiveness of interventions. Our aim here was to describe relevant analytic considerations for the PROTECT study using a target trial emulation framework. However, in doing so, we also present statistical and epidemiological considerations that may be relevant to a range of study topics utilizing treatment initiation grace periods and the target trial emulation.

In future work, we will work with country teams to perform country-specific analyses of TPT program effectiveness, as well as to compare results across countries. Future work may also consider approaches for individual participant data meta-analysis in order to address additional questions related to TPT effectiveness in special populations.

All code developed to implement this analysis plan will be made freely available at https://github.com/benkeser/protectr.
