Non-surgical treatment for displaced midshaft clavicle fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Matias Vaajala, Jeremias Tarkiainen, Rasmus Liukkonen, Antti P. Launonen, Ville M. Mattila
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis review aims to synthesize current evidence by pooling patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and reporting pooled data on pain and complication rates after non-surgically treated displaced midshaft clavicle fractures.Materials and methodsPubMed (Medline), Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases were searched. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adult patients (≥ 18 years) with displaced midshaft clavicle fractures treated non-surgically. The primary outcome was to analyze PROMs at 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month follow-ups. Secondary outcomes were pain, complications, and return to work. The outcomes were synthesized and reported as the weighted mean or rate, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsIn total, 4,908 studies were eligible for screening. After screening and full-text review, 18 RCTs including 1,201 patients were included. For displaced midshaft fractures, the pooled DASH score was 16.1 (95% CI 11.4–20.7) at 3 months and improved to 9.9 (95% CI 5.8–13.9) at 6 months and 6.3 (95% CI 3.0–9.5) at 12 months. The pooled VAS pain score was 4.4 (95% CI 0.0–9.1) at 3 months and 2.6 (95% CI 0.0–8.8) at 12 months. The pooled rates of secondary surgery and nonunion at 12 months were 13% and 14%, respectively.ConclusionsNon-surgical treatment was associated with generally favorable functional outcomes, with substantial recovery observed by 3 months and further improvement by 6 months and only minor changes thereafter. Although secondary surgery was required in approximately 13% of patients and nonunion occurred in 14%, long-term functional outcomes were generally favorable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10208-1.

Background

Clavicle fractures are among the most common skeletal injuries, accounting for approximately 3–4% of all adult fractures [1]. These injuries typically result from a fall onto the shoulder or direct trauma [2]. The midshaft is the most commonly affected region, representing approximately 70–80% of cases, and fracture patterns range from minimally displaced to severely comminuted [3]. Despite the high incidence of clavicle fractures, their clinical course, particularly with regard to non-surgical management, remains incompletely characterized in the literature.

There has been ongoing debate regarding the optimal management of especially displaced clavicle fractures. While non-surgical treatment has been the standard for nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures, surgical fixation has gained popularity due to faster recovery, earlier return to work, and lower rates of non-union [4–6]. In Finland, the incidence of surgical treatment increased from 1 to 11 per 100,000 person-years between 1987 and 2010 [7]. Although non-surgical treatment can be associated with slower recovery and an increased risk of non-union, long-term functional outcomes are comparable to those of surgically treated patients [8–10].

To date, no comprehensive synthesis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) has been conducted specifically to chart the natural healing trajectory of non-surgically managed clavicle fractures. Notably, the most recent Cochrane review compared operative and non-surgical treatments but did not provide a focused analysis on the outcomes of non-surgical treatment alone [11]. Furthermore, it is recognized that RCTs typically include only a limited proportion of the total patient population due to strict eligibility criteria that exclude patients with chronic diseases or old age [12]. For many of these excluded patients, non-surgical treatment remains the only viable option, further emphasizing the need to understand the natural course and outcomes of non-surgically treated patients.

While operative treatment has been extensively studied, little high-quality synthesized evidence exists describing functional recovery, pain development, complication rates, and quality of life following non-surgical treatment alone. A clearer understanding of these outcomes is essential to inform clinical decision-making and support shared treatment planning, particularly when surgical intervention is not indicated or desired. This review aims to clarify the natural healing of non-surgically managed displaced midshaft fractures by (1) pooling patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) at multiple follow-up time points in adult patients treated non-surgically, and (2) reporting pooled data on pain, complication rates, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) across these time points.

Methods

The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under the registration number CRD420251057936. No external funding was obtained for the completion of this review. This is a systematic review and therefore, no ethical approval is required. This study has received no funding.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria for this review were based on the Patient, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework. All search results from the electronic databases were imported into Covidence software, which was used to facilitate screening and to automatically remove duplicate records. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adult patients (≥ 18 years) with displaced midshaft clavicle fractures treated non-surgically in at least one of the study arms. Only full-texts written in English were included, and no grey literature or conference proceedings were considered eligible. As this review was conducted as a single-arm meta-analysis, comparator groups were not extracted or analyzed. Studies were eligible if they included adult patients with displaced midshaft clavicle fractures treated non-surgically and reported at least one validated patient-reported outcome measure (PROM). The focus on RCTs was intended to ensure standardized data collection and reduce the risk of selection bias inherent in purely observational cohorts. Although the original PROSPERO protocol allowed inclusion of all clavicle fracture types, the final quantitative synthesis was restricted to displaced midshaft fractures. This decision was made after full-text screening and data extraction, but before conducting the meta-analyses, because the available RCT evidence for other clavicle fracture locations or non-displaced fractures was too limited to support meaningful pooled analyses.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across three major electronic databases: PubMed (Medline), Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). The search was carried out on May 15th, 2025, and was updated on March 28th, 2026, using the following search strategy: “(clavicular OR clavicle) AND (fracture OR injury OR trauma) AND (non-operative OR non-surgical OR nonsurgical OR conservative)”. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The review adheres to the updated PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and the corresponding checklist is available in the supplementary materials.

Selection process

Title and abstract screening were conducted independently by three reviewers (MV, JT, RL), followed by full-text screening of potentially eligible articles. The final eligibility assessment was independently performed by two reviewers. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion with a third reviewer (RL).

Data collection process and data items

Data extraction was performed using a standardized Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Two reviewers (JT, MV) independently extracted the first 20% of eligible studies to ensure consistency and quality. After establishing adequate agreement and accuracy, the remaining studies were extracted by one reviewer.

The extracted data included general study characteristics (title, authors, year of publication), as well as methodological and population details, such as study period (first and last year of patient enrolment), length of follow-up, mean patient age, sex distribution, the number of included fractures and their classification (if reported), immobilization time, and treatment protocol. In studies with multiple eligible intervention arms, each arm was included as a separate data point in the meta-analysis.

Outcome data included PROMs, pain scores, complications (e.g. non-unions, secondary surgeries), health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and return to work. These outcomes were recorded as reported in validated instruments such as the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH), Constant-Murley Score, and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score.

Study risk of bias

Risk of bias (RoB) was assessed independently by two reviewers (MV, JT) using the ROB 2.0 tool for RCTs [13]. All studies were reviewed independently, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Effect measures, synthetic methods, and certainty assessments

Follow-up assessments were grouped into predefined clinically relevant categories to enable meaningful pooling across studies. Time windows were defined according to the nearest predefined follow-up category (3, 6, or 12 months). Accordingly, outcomes reported between 1.5 and 4.5 months were assigned to the 3-month category, and outcomes reported between > 4.5 and 9 months to the 6-month category. The 12-month category included only assessments performed at 12 months, whereas all assessments beyond 12 months were analysed separately as long-term follow-up. When a study reported multiple assessments within the same predefined category (e.g., both 1.5- and 3-month follow-up), only the assessment closest to the target timepoint was included in the pooled analysis to avoid double-counting participants. In practice, nearly all included studies reported outcomes at the predefined follow-up intervals, meaning that assignment to the nearest category was rarely required and would not have materially influenced the pooled estimates. Had studies reported outcomes during the early post-injury period (e.g., within the first few weeks after injury), these assessments would not have been assigned to the 3-month category, as they reflect the acute recovery phase rather than early functional recovery. Only the most frequently reported and widely validated PROMs were included in the meta-analysis to ensure consistency and comparability of results across studies. Although the original study protocol planned to analyse changes from baseline, this was not feasible, as none of the included trials reported baseline PROM values. As a result, the analysis was limited to the pooled mean outcomes at the predefined time points. Secondary outcomes included pooled estimates of pain intensity, complication rates (e.g. non-unions, reoperations), and HRQoL, assessed at the same follow-up intervals. Adverse events were extracted according to the definitions used in the original studies. Nonunion, malunion, secondary surgery, and other adverse events were recorded separately whenever possible. Because definitions of malunion and other complications varied across studies, these outcomes were not pooled individually. Secondary surgery was analyzed irrespective of the reported indication, as the underlying reason for reoperation (e.g., symptomatic nonunion, malunion, persistent pain, or patient preference) was inconsistently reported across studies. Nonunion and secondary surgery were therefore analysed as separate outcomes and should not be considered mutually exclusive.

All studies were analyzed simultaneously, and effect estimates were pooled using inverse-variance weighting. Study-level variances were calculated as SD2/n, assuming a normal distribution for the outcome measures. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. However, to account for potential differences in treatment protocols and study populations, a random-effects model was applied irrespective of the observed heterogeneity. Pooled estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Exploratory meta-regression analyses were performed to investigate potential sources of between-study heterogeneity. Study-level mean age, duration of immobilization, and male proportion were evaluated as moderators of DASH scores and nonunion rates at 12-month follow-up. Meta-regression analyses were conducted using mixed-effects models. Funnel plots were used to assess publication bias. A sensitivity analysis including only studies with low or moderate risk of bias was performed to evaluate whether the exclusion of high-risk studies influenced the overall effect estimates. All statistical analyses were conducted using the meta package in R (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

In total, 4,908 studies were eligible for screening. After screening and full-text reading, a total of 18 RCTs (21 study arms) [5, 14–30], including 1201 patients with non-surgically treated clavicle fractures, were included in this review (Fig. 1). The weighted mean patient age was 32.2 years (mean range 19.0–41.0 across studies), and the weighted mean duration of immobilization using a sling or figure-of-eight bandage was 4.5 weeks (range 2–6 weeks). The median number of fractures per study was 45 (interquartile range [IQR] 30 to 71), and most of the patients in these studies were male (57.3%, 436/761 among the studies reporting genders). The mean follow-up duration across the included studies was 13.7 months. Pain

Only two studies with three study arms reported pain for displaced midshaft fractures. At 3 months, the pooled mean VAS was 4.4 (95% CI: 0–9.1; I2 = 99.7%), decreasing to 0.86 (95% CI: 0.68–1.03; I2 = 0.0%) at 6 months, and then increasing to 2.6 (95% CI: 0.0–8.8; I2 = 68.4%) at 12 months. However, the 6-month estimate was based on very limited data and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Adverse events

In the pooled analysis of 18 RCTs comprising 1201 patients, a total of three studies reported nonunion or surgeries at three months, a total of 4 studies at 6 months, and a total of 11 studies at 12 months. As the 3- or 6-month timepoints were limited to only a few studies, only nonunion, and surgeries at 12 months were pooled. The overall rate of adverse events following non-surgical treatment at 12 months was 22% (95% CI: 15–30%; I2 = 84%) (Fig. 4). The pooled rate for secondary surgeries at 12 months was 13% (CI 10–18%; I2 = 36%), and 14% (CI 11–19%; I2 = 44%) for nonunion at 12 months (Figs. 5 and 6).Fig. 4The pooled incidence of adverse events at 12-month follow-upFig. 5The pooled incidence of secondary surgeries at 12-month follow-upFig. 6The pooled incidence of nonunions at 12-month follow-up

Health-related quality of life

HRQoL was a predefined secondary outcome. However, none of the included studies reported validated HRQoL measures.

Return to work

Five studies reported return-to-work outcomes, all expressing the outcome as the mean time to return to work. Across these studies, the reported mean time to return to work was 11.8 weeks, ranging from 3.5 weeks in the study by Robinson et al. to 18.1 weeks in the study by Tamaoki et al.

Exploratory meta-regression analyses

In exploratory meta-regression analyses (Supplementary Figs. 1–6), mean age was not associated with DASH scores at 12 months (β = −0.14, p = 0.839) or nonunion rates (β = 0.005, p = 0.759). Similarly, neither immobilization duration (β = 0.83, p = 0.497) nor the proportion of male participants (β = −22.00, p = 0.400) was associated with DASH scores at 12 months. Longer immobilization duration was associated with lower nonunion rates (β = −0.19, p = 0.010). No association was observed between male proportion and nonunion rates (β = 0.48, p = 0.436).

Sensitivity analyses

As there were studies with a high risk of bias included, we performed sensitivity analyses for function and adverse events with studies considered as high risk of bias excluded. A total of 4 studies classified as having a high risk of bias contributed to the outcomes included in the sensitivity analyses and were excluded. The number of studies included in each sensitivity analysis varied according to outcome availability and follow-up timepoint (Supplementary Table 2). In function measures at 3 months, the pooled mean DASH score was 15.8 (95% CI: 10.4–21.1; I2 = 99.5%), while the Constant-Murley was 85.0 (95% CI: 79.4–90.5; I2 = 99.7%). By 6 months, the pooled mean DASH score improved substantially to 9.6 (95% CI: 4.6–14.6; I2 = 99.5%), and Constant-Murley to 88.3 (95% CI: 82.6–94.0; I2 = 99.8%). At 12 months, pooled mean DASH decreased to 6.2 (95% CI: 2.5–10.0; I2 = 99.7%), and Constant-Murley at 91.0 (95% CI: 87.0–94.9; I2 = 99.7%). The pooled rate for secondary surgeries at 12 months was 13% (CI 8–19%; I2 = 55.8%), and 15% (CI 11–20%; I2 = 50.4%) for nonunion at 12 months. (Supplementary Table 2, and supplementary Figs. 7–9).

The funnel plots for DASH, Constant-Murley score, and ASES consistently showed only mild asymmetry across studies, indicating no major publication bias (supplementary Fig. 10–20).

Discussion

The main finding of our study is that non-surgical treatment of displaced midshaft clavicle fractures was associated with generally favorable functional outcomes, with substantial improvement observed by 3 months and further improvement by 6 months, as measured by validated PROMs (DASH and Constant-Murley score). Pain levels also appeared to be low by 3 months among the studies reporting VAS outcomes. In addition, non-surgical treatment was associated with a moderate rate of secondary surgery (13%) and nonunion (14%) at 12 months.

Our findings suggest that non-surgical treatment is associated with low pain levels and favorable long-term functional outcomes. By 6 months, the pooled DASH score approached normative values in the general population, 5 (CI 3–8) for men and 8 (CI 5–11) for women between 30 and 39 years of age [31]. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for DASH in upper extremity conditions has been estimated to be approximately 10 points [32]. When interpreted in this context, the pooled DASH score at 3 months was around 16.1, corresponding to an absolute difference of 8.1–11.1 points compared with normative values. This suggests that functional status at 3 months may already be approaching normative population values for many patients, although this should be interpreted with caution as no clavicle-specific MCID has been established. Surgical treatment is often justified by earlier functional improvement, as shown in recent meta-analyses [33], as well as lower rates of certain complications, particularly malunion and nonunion [9, 34]. However, our findings are highly relevant for shared decision-making, especially in light of the increasing use of surgical fixation over the past two decades [7, 35–37]. While early functional gains may be important for selected subgroups, such as elite athletes or individuals with high physical demands, the long-term outcomes of non-surgical treatment in the general population appear to be sufficient. These favourable functional outcomes should, however, be interpreted alongside the observed complication rates. Although most patients achieved good long-term function, nonunion occurred in approximately 14% of patients and secondary surgery was required in 13% by 12 months. Therefore, non-surgical treatment cannot be considered complication-free, and these risks should be incorporated into shared decision-making discussions. Despite the substantial between-study heterogeneity, exploratory meta-regression analyses did not identify significant associations between study-level mean age, immobilization duration, or the proportion of male participants and functional outcomes at 12 months. Although changes from baseline could not be assessed due to the absence of baseline PROM values in the included trials, the improvement observed across follow-up timepoints suggests that the most clinically relevant recovery occurs during the first 6 months after injury. As no validated clavicle fracture-specific MCID values were identified, these findings should be interpreted using the best available MCID estimates for upper-extremity outcome measures and in relation to normative DASH values. However, at 12 months, the pooled DASH score of 6.3 falls within the confidence interval of normative population values, suggesting that any residual functional deficits are likely to be small for most patients.

Another important finding is the moderate rate of secondary surgical intervention following non-surgical treatment. In the pooled analysis, the proportion of nonunion was 14%, and 13% of patients underwent secondary surgery within 12 months. Importantly, these estimates remained largely unchanged in sensitivity analyses excluding studies with a high risk of bias, supporting the robustness of the findings. Previous studies have reported that reoperation rates following surgical treatment vary considerably, ranging approximately from 7% to over 20%, depending on the indication and follow-up duration [38, 39]. The overall rate of adverse events in the present study was 22% at 12-month follow-up. However, this should be interpreted with caution, as a substantial proportion of these events consisted of potentially asymptomatic findings, such as malunion or cosmetic dissatisfaction, many of which do not require further intervention [40]. Longer immobilization duration was associated with lower non-union rates. Although causality cannot be inferred, this observation may suggest that fracture stability during the early healing phase could influence union rates. However, immobilization duration may also act as a surrogate marker for other study-level differences, and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Also, in our study nonunion and secondary surgery were analyzed as separate outcomes. Because individual studies did not consistently report the indication for secondary surgery, the degree of overlap between these outcomes could not be reliably determined.

Return to work was reported inconsistently and in only a minority of included studies, with substantial variation in reported timeframes (ranging from 3.5 weeks to 4.3 months). Due to this limited and heterogeneous reporting, no formal data synthesis could be performed, and robust conclusions regarding return to work remain difficult. This finding is consistent with previous literature, highlighting a persistent gap in high-quality evidence on return to work following clavicle fractures. This lack of reporting is notable, as return to work is arguably one of the most clinically and economically relevant outcomes, particularly in working-age populations. Future studies should prioritize standardized reporting of return-to-work outcomes to better inform both clinical decision-making and health economic evaluations. In this context, the limited evidence on return to work also complicates the evaluation of the cost-effectiveness of different treatment strategies. Given that the functional advantage of surgery appears to be largely confined to the first 6–12 weeks, its cost-effectiveness becomes uncertain when applied broadly to low-risk patients. This is supported by a RCT by Nicholson et al., which concluded that routine surgical fixation of displaced clavicle fractures is not cost-effective compared to conservative care [38].

Strengths

A major strength of this study is that it is the first extensive review focused solely on patients with non-surgically treated clavicle fractures. Furthermore, we included only RCTs, which reduces the risk of selection bias and provides a high level of evidence on the outcomes of non-surgical treatment. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding studies with a high risk of bias, which did not materially alter the results, supporting the robustness of our findings. Additionally, we analyzed outcomes at predefined timepoints, allowing a more clinically relevant interpretation of the recovery trajectory.

Limitations

This study also has several limitations. First, heterogeneity across studies was high for most outcomes, likely reflecting differences in patient populations, treatment protocols, and outcome reporting. The very high heterogeneity observed for several functional outcomes suggests that the pooled estimates should be interpreted as average recovery trajectories across studies rather than precise estimates applicable to individual patients. Considerable between-study variability likely reflects differences in patient populations, fracture characteristics, rehabilitation protocols, and outcome assessment methods. Second, several clinically relevant outcomes, particularly pain and return to work, were inconsistently reported, limiting the ability to perform comprehensive data synthesis and draw firm conclusions. Furthermore, none of the included studies reported baseline PROM values, precluding assessment of change from baseline. Pain outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, as only three studies reported VAS scores, and different studies contributed data at different follow-up timepoints. Consequently, the apparent increase in pooled VAS scores between 6 and 12 months is unlikely to represent a true deterioration in pain and may instead reflect differences in study populations, follow-up completeness, and outcome reporting. In addition, no validated clavicle fracture-specific MCID values were identified for the outcome measures included in this review, limiting interpretation of clinical significance using condition-specific thresholds. Third, deviations from the original protocol should be acknowledged. Although we aimed to include all clavicle fracture types according to the original protocol, the final analysis was restricted to displaced midshaft fractures due to the limited number of studies on other fracture types. This deviation from the original protocol improves the internal consistency and clinical interpretability of the pooled estimates but limits the generalizability of the findings to other clavicle fracture types. The planned risk of bias assessment using the ROBINS-I tool was replaced with the ROB 2 tool. ROBINS-I was initially considered because the review focused on outcomes within non-surgical treatment arms; however, after finalizing the analytical approach, ROB 2 was deemed more appropriate. Also, none of the included studies specifically addressed older adults, limiting the generalizability of our findings to this population. This is particularly relevant as normative DASH scores increase with age, reaching approximately 10–15 points in individuals over 50 years, suggesting that small residual deficits may be of limited clinical relevance [41]. Furthermore, only studies published in English were included, and grey literature was not searched. Therefore, language bias and publication bias cannot be completely excluded. In addition, evidence from other upper extremity injuries, such as proximal humerus fractures, has shown no clear long-term benefit of surgical over non-surgical treatment, underscoring the need for high-quality RCTs in elderly patients with clavicle fractures [42]. Finally, the absence of validated HRQoL measures also limits conclusions regarding the broader patient impact of non-surgical treatment. The complete absence of HRQoL data represents an important evidence gap.

Future research directions

Future studies should prioritize standardized reporting of baseline PROMs, return-to-work outcomes, HRQoL, and adverse events to improve comparability across studies and enable more robust evidence synthesis. High-quality RCTs are particularly needed in underrepresented populations, including older adults and patients with substantial comorbidities. In addition, future systematic reviews incorporating high-quality observational cohorts may complement randomized evidence by improving the representativeness and external validity of available evidence. Further research is also needed to clarify the relationship between immobilization protocols, fracture healing, and functional recovery.

Clinical implications

The findings of this review are highly relevant for shared decision-making, particularly considering the increasing use of surgical fixation for displaced midshaft clavicle fractures. While surgical treatment may provide earlier functional improvement and may remain appropriate for selected subgroups, such as elite athletes or individuals with high physical demands, the present results suggest that non-surgical treatment provides good functional outcomes and low pain levels for most patients. Furthermore, the observed rates of nonunion and secondary surgery indicate that fracture displacement alone should not necessarily be considered a definitive indication for operative treatment, and that careful patient selection remains essential.

Conclusion

The main finding of this study is that non-surgical treatment of displaced midshaft clavicle fractures was associated with generally favorable functional outcomes, with substantial recovery observed by 3 months and further improvement by 6 months, followed by only minor changes thereafter. Although secondary surgery was required in approximately 13% of patients and nonunion occurred in 14%, long-term functional outcomes were generally favorable. While surgical fixation may offer short-term advantages in selected subgroups, such as athletes or individuals with high physical demands, the long-term outcomes of non-surgical treatment in the general population appear to be sufficient. These findings should, however, be interpreted in the context of the substantial between-study heterogeneity and methodological limitations of the available evidence.
