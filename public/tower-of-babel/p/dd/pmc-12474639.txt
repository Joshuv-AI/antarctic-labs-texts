Identifying Treatable Traits of Patients with Asthma Prescribed an ICS/LABA: A Descriptive Analytic Database Study in England
Authors: Jennifer K Quint, Adrian Rendon, Richard Stanford, Bilun Gemicioglu, Leandro Fritscher, Watchara Boonsawat, Elke Rottier, Anurita Majumdar, Mohamed Hamouda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aimed to understand predictors of moderate-to-severe asthma exacerbations and high short-acting β2-agonist (SABA) use in adults with asthma newly initiating single-inhaler twice-daily inhaled corticosteroids/long-acting β2-agonists (ICS/LABAs) in England.MethodsThis non-interventional, longitudinal, retrospective study used medical record data (Clinical Practice Research Datalink Aurum; Hospital Episode Statistics) in England. Eligible patients with diagnosed asthma were ≥ 18 years old 12 months before the first observed single-inhaler twice-daily ICS/LABA prescription date (December 1, 2017–March 31, 2019). Patients were stratified by occurrence of moderate-to-severe exacerbation and SABA use during 12 months’ follow-up. Study outcomes included exacerbation occurrence and SABA use. Latent class cluster (LCC) analysis distinguished clusters of predictors with high importance (≥ 0.70) identified from random forest analysis and confirmed on clinical relevance. Treatment characteristics were described within each cluster.ResultsMost of 23,567 patients meeting study criteria (80.4%) were white and female (60.3%); mean index age was 50.0 years. Overall, 21 variables were distinguished as key predictors of moderate-to-severe exacerbation (top predictors: comorbid cardiac disease, diabetes, depression, smoking, body mass index [BMI]) and 22 as key predictors of high SABA use (top predictors: comorbid upper respiratory tract infection, cardiac disease, prior SABA use, age, sex). Five clusters each were observed among patients who experienced an exacerbation and those who did not. Among patients with high or lower SABA use, five and six clusters were observed, respectively. Treatment characteristics were similar across clusters.DiscussionThis study distinguished treatable and non-treatable predictors of exacerbation and high SABA use. Distinct asthma phenotypes were detected via LCC analysis. Distinguished treatable traits including comorbidities, smoking status and BMI, may be targeted by healthcare professionals.ConclusionOur findings reinforce the importance of personalized asthma treatments with the goal of improving clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03332-2.Keywords: Exacerbation, Inhaled corticosteroid, Long-acting β2-agonist, Short-acting β2-agonist, Random forest analysis, Latent class cluster analysis

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Approximately 6.5% of the United Kingdom (UK) population have been diagnosed with asthma [1]. Despite the availability of effective treatments, asthma remains uncontrolled in over 2.17 million patients in the UK [2]. Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) treatment recommendations follow a stepwise approach where treatment is refined from the initial diagnosis [3]. Asthma may be controlled using treatment with inhaled corticosteroids/long-acting β2-agonists (ICS/LABAs), as recommended by GINA in treatment Steps 3 and 4 [3]. These patients have symptoms every day or most days, wake with symptoms ≥ 1 night a week, have increased risk of exacerbation and may have poor lung function before the initiation of ICS/LABA treatment, and are typically considered as having moderate-to-severe disease [3]. Regular ICS/LABA therapy is recommended to provide relief, to maintain control of persistent symptoms and to prevent exacerbations [3, 4].

Use of reliever therapy nowadays for patients with moderate-to-severe asthma equates to GINA treatment Steps 3–4 [3]. Two separate asthma management approaches are recommended: preferred maintenance and reliever therapy with ICS/formoterol (FOR) (Track 1) or, alternatively, regular ICS/LABA with as-needed short-acting β2-agonist (SABA) or ICS/SABA (Track 2) [3]. GINA recommends that SABA use should be reduced with appropriate treatment as poor asthma control is linked with high SABA use (≥ 3 per year), risk of severe exacerbations and subsequent mortality [3, 5, 6]. Poor asthma control has been shown to be associated with female sex, increasing age, socio-economic deprivation, smoking history, increased body mass index (BMI) and medical conditions such as atopy, rhinitis, reflux, anxiety, depression and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [7, 8]. An alternate approach to asthma management may be needed to achieve GINA-recommended asthma control criteria [9–11].

Recent research has identified the need for a personalized treatment approach to reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of the disease and treatment response [9, 10, 12]. It is not only important to identify risk factors associated with increased exacerbations and high SABA use but also to identify possible interactions between risk factors, typically associated with poor outcomes and reduced quality of life [13–15]. Individual clinical characteristics may be a key determinant of appropriate patient management, with personalized treatment options dependent on the traits present [9, 10, 12, 14].

The objectives of this study were to identify baseline socio-demographics and clinical characteristics of adults with asthma that predict exacerbations or high SABA use after ICS/LABA initiation and to describe treatment characteristics following initiation of ICS/LABA therapy among asthma risk groups. An exploratory objective was to describe socio-demographic, clinical and treatment characteristics of adults with asthma prescribed budesonide/formoterol (BUD/FOR) or fluticasone propionate/salmeterol (FP/SAL), respectively.

Methods

Study Design

This non-interventional, longitudinal retrospective study is based in part on de-identified data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD Aurum) obtained under a licence from the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. The data were provided by patients and collected by the National Health Service (NHS) as part of their care and support. Furthermore, secondary care administrative data from Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) Copyright © (2024), reused with the permission of The Health & Social Care Information Centre, were linked to CPRD Aurum. CPRD Aurum captures diagnoses, symptoms, prescriptions, referrals and tests for > 19 million patients as of September 2018 [16, 17], while the HES Admitted Patient Care (APC) and Accident & Emergency (A&E) datasets contain details of all admissions to or attendances at English NHS healthcare providers. The 2019 English Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) was used to provide a composite measure of extent of deprivation of each Lower Layer Super Output Area in England [18]. Only practices that were eligible for the linkage scheme were included in this study.

Adults with asthma, newly initiating a twice-daily ICS/LABA therapy within the index period, were included in this study. The index date was the earliest prescription date of twice-daily ICS/LABA therapy between December 1, 2017, and March 31, 2019, inclusive. The index period start date was December 1, 2017, to align with changes to ICS/LABA therapy guideline recommendations from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (November 2017) [1]. The index period end date was March 31, 2020, prior to the first peak of the COVID-19 pandemic in England. The baseline period consisted of a minimum of 12 months before and including index. A fixed 12-month follow-up period after index was selected to allow for seasonal variability across the eligible patient group for occurrences of exacerbations and high use of reliever therapy data to be collected. The study design is shown in Fig. S1 and further details are included in Supplementary Material.

Study Population

Eligible patients with diagnosed asthma were ≥ 18 years old ≥ 12 months pre-index and had ≥ 1 prescription for a twice-daily ICS/LABA therapy within the index period.

The first observed prescription determined the index date; this included a BUD/FOR, FP/SAL, beclomethasone dipropionate (BDP)/FOR or FP/FOR prescription. At least one diagnostic code indicative of asthma (such as International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision [ICD]-10) was required in the 18 months pre-index. Patients were required to have been continuously registered with a general practitioner (GP) ≥ 12 months prior to and including index, and > 12 months post-index, and to have a primary care record in CPRD Aurum eligible for linkage to HES.

Patients were excluded if they had ≥ 1 prescription for single inhaler ICS/LABA therapy prior to index date in medical record history to ensure the sample consisted of new ICS/LABA users only. Patients with ≥ 1 diagnostic code of COPD or any medical conditions incompatible with an asthma diagnosis, ≥ 1 prescription for ICS/LABA/long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) triple therapy or ≥ 1 prescription for a biologic therapy in the 24 months prior to and including index were also excluded. Due to the importance of treatment differences by ICS/LABA class type, patients prescribed more than one ICS/LABA class at index were excluded from the study.

Characteristics of study population included demographic characteristics (age, sex [as self-reported by the patient], region, ethnicity and socio-economic status), clinical characteristics (BMI, smoking status, comorbidities, Quan-Charlson Comorbidity Index [Quan-CCI], eosinophil levels, asthma symptoms, history of exacerbation in the 24 months prior to and including index, or high SABA use) and treatment characteristics (oral corticosteroid [OCS] use, ICS/LABA brand, ICS/LABA device type). Presence of asthma symptoms pre-index was assessed as a binary (yes or no) variable based on information available in medical records within the database. Asthma symptoms evaluated included breathlessness, wheeze, cough (non-acute), chest tightness, fatigue and night-time awakening. The average daily ICS dose within index ICS/LABA considered dose and frequency and was reported in µg/day continuously and categorically as low, medium and high dose, using the GINA 2023 report definitions for adults. Adherence to index ICS/LABA was measured in the 12 months post-index as proportion of days covered (PDC): non-adherent: PDC < 80%; adherent: PDC ≥ 80%. PDC was calculated by dividing the days covered by the prescription by a fixed time interval (i.e. 365 days) multiplied by 100.

Study Outcomes

A moderate-to-severe asthma exacerbation was defined as any of the following events during the 12-month follow-up period: a short course of OCS, a hospital inpatient admission related to asthma, an A&E visit related to asthma or a coded exacerbation event in a medical record. Status was reported as a binary variable (1: ≥ 1 exacerbation/0: no exacerbation) for moderate-to-severe exacerbations.

High SABA use was defined as ≥ 3 SABA written prescriptions in the 12-month follow-up period. This threshold was chosen as patients who use ≥ 3 cannisters of reliever per year are known to have worse asthma control compared with ≤ 2 [5, 6]. SABA use status was reported as a binary variable (1: ≥ 3 prescriptions/0: < 3 prescriptions).

While SABA, BUD/FOR, SABA/short-acting muscarinic-antagonist (SAMA) and SAMA are reliever therapies, only SABA therapy was considered an outcome in this study. BUD/FOR was not considered as it can be used as a maintenance ICS/LABA therapy, while SAMA and SABA/SAMA were not considered as reliever therapies as they are predominantly used in patients with severe asthma or COPD.

Statistical Methodology

A supervised machine learning approach (random forest analysis) was used in this study as it enables the identification of many predictors. A four-step approach was used (Fig. S2). Patients were stratified based on binary outcomes measured in the follow-up period as ≥ 1 moderate-to-severe exacerbation or no exacerbation, and as ≥ 3 SABA relievers or < 3 SABA relievers (Step 1). The relationships between baseline socio-demographic and clinical characteristics were assessed for each outcome using random forest analysis, producing predictors of exacerbation, and high SABA use (Step 2). Variables distinguished in the second step were used to perform a latent class cluster analysis. Criteria for carrying variables forward from random forest to latent class cluster analysis included all variables with a high importance value (≥ 0.7) and all variables known to be predictors a priori based on clinical input. A small number of variables were not carried forward despite meeting criteria due to missingness or not being generalizable to non-UK research (e.g. region). Predictors of each outcome were carried forward to latent class cluster analysis, producing clusters of homogeneous groups regarding socio-demographic and clinical characteristics used to produce clusters (Step 3). Treatment characteristics, including ICS dose, were then described for each homogeneous cluster (Step 4).

Latent class cluster analysis is an unsupervised machine learning approach where patients are assigned to different latent clusters. Data were assumed to be generated from a finite mixture of clusters. The inclusion probability was estimated using the expectation-maximization procedure. Patients were assigned to a cluster that corresponded to the largest inclusion probability. The number of clusters was chosen so that the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was optimal [19]. Overall, the following sets of clusters were produced: exacerbation, non-exacerbation, higher SABA use and low SABA use. Latent class cluster analysis was then conducted to produce 5 (exacerbation) and 6 (SABA use) homogeneous cluster solutions that corresponded to the optimum BIC [20].

Results

Patient Characteristics

Attrition of eligible patients at each inclusion and exclusion step is shown in Fig. 1. Overall, 23,567 patients met all study criteria; the mean age was 50 years, and the mean BMI was 29.6 kg/m2 (Table 1). Most patients were white (80.4%), female (60.3%) and current or former smokers (65.9%) rather than never smokers (25.8%). The most frequent diagnosed comorbidities were cardiac disease (29.2%), upper respiratory tract infection (URTI; 19.3%), anxiety (10.8%) and atopy (10.6%); the mean Quan-CCI [21] score was 0.8. Breathlessness was the most common asthma symptom reported in medical records (10.9%) 12 months pre-index (Table 1).

Predictors of Outcomes—Random Forest Analysis

Predictors of exacerbation (Table S1; Fig. 2) and high SABA use (Table S2; Fig. 3) outcomes in the follow-up period were assessed using random forest analysis. Overall, 21 variables were distinguished as key exacerbation predictors and 22 as key high SABA use predictors. Overall, 18 of the 21 variables distinguished as key exacerbation predators were also key high SABA use predictors. Obesity, current smoking and URTI were key treatable factors predicting exacerbations, while deprived socio-economic status and age were key non-treatable predictors of exacerbation. Comorbid URTI and cardiac disease were factors that predicted high SABA use while increasing age and female sex predicted low SABA.

Homogeneous Clusters of Outcome Exacerbation Predictors and Treatment Characteristics—Latent Class Cluster Analysis

Five clusters were observed among patients who experienced an exacerbation and five clusters among those without an exacerbation (Table 3 [demographic and clinical characteristics]; Tables S3 and S4 [treatment characteristics and dosage]; Fig. 4). The standardized means of predictors distinguished among clusters of exacerbators and non-exacerbators are shown in Fig. 4, representing the distribution of the mean value for each baseline socio-demographic and clinical characteristic from the overall cohort mean. Figure 4 shows the relative distribution of predictors across clusters; a higher standardized mean indicates a higher value relative to other clusters, while a lower standardized mean indicates a lower value. Overall, there were few numeric differences in baseline characteristics between non-exacerbation and exacerbation clusters. A higher proportion of patients in the exacerbator clusters (27.8%) than non-exacerbator clusters (21.2%) overall were considered adherent to therapy (≥ 80% PDC). These clusters are described in more detail below, with descriptive titles representing the most frequent characteristics observed:

Cluster 1, younger (mean < 40 years), smoker and lower BMI (mean ≤ 28 kg/m2)

This exacerbator sub-cluster contained 19.6% (n = 507/2591) of all patients with an exacerbation in the 12-month follow-up period and was characterized by a mean age of 39.9 years, a moderate proportion of current smokers (30.8%), a mean BMI of 28.0 kg/m2 and a mean Quan-CCI of 0.8. In the 12 months pre-index, the mean frequency of moderate-to-severe asthma exacerbations was 0.4.

The non-exacerbator sub-cluster was larger (31.4% of all patients with no exacerbation in the 12-month follow-up period [n = 6604/20,976]). Fewer patients in the non-exacerbator sub-cluster (11.3%) were adherent to therapy versus the exacerbator sub-cluster (18.9%). The non-exacerbator sub-cluster had the highest percentage of patients receiving low-dose FP/SAL (73.6% [in comparison, for the exacerbator sub-cluster this was 41.2%]). Mean daily FP/SAL dose was higher in the exacerbator sub-cluster (429.4 µg) than in the non-exacerbator sub-cluster (300.2 µg).

Cluster 2, older (mean > 50 years), high severity and comorbid

This exacerbator sub-cluster contained 11.2% (n = 289/2591) patients and was characterized by a mean age of 65.1 years, a high proportion of prior clinical severity outcomes (e.g. mean number of moderate-to-severe exacerbations, 2.9; ≥ 1 asthma-related hospitalization, 43.6%) and comorbidities (cardiac disease: 55.7%; diabetes mellitus: 29.4%; gastro-oesophageal reflux disease [GERD]: 12.1%). Most of the sub-cluster had received ≥ 1 OCS prescription (98.3%) in the prior 12 months.

In non-exacerbators, this sub-cluster (12.1%, n = 2531/20,976) had a high proportion of patients with comorbid depression (16.8%) and high socio-economic status deprivation score (IMD quintile 5: 31.3%). Nearly 40% of the exacerbator sub-cluster were adherent to index ICS/LABA in the 12 months post-index. Fewer patients in the exacerbator sub-cluster were receiving low-dose BUD/FOR (84.2%) than in the non-exacerbator sub-cluster (96.0%). Mean daily BUD/FOR dose was higher in the exacerbator sub-cluster (442.1 µg) than in the non-exacerbator sub-cluster (362.3 µg).

Cluster 3, allergic asthma

This exacerbator sub-cluster contained 11.3% (n = 295/2591) patients and was characterized by a high proportion of immune-mediated allergic comorbidities (atopy: 100%).

The non-exacerbator cluster was numerically larger (9.5%, n = 1986/20,976). The mean age in the exacerbator cluster was 48.5 years and in the non-exacerbator cluster was 45.8 years. ICS was the most common treatment change in the exacerbator (100%) and non-exacerbator (5.8%) sub-clusters. Fewer non-exacerbators (17.6%) than exacerbators (30.5%) were adherent to index ICS/LABA in the 12 months post-index.

Cluster 4, older (mean age > 55 years) and cardiac disease

This exacerbator sub-cluster contained 30.7% patients (n = 796/2591) and was characterized by a mean age of 63.1 years, high proportions of comorbid cardiac disease (53.0%) and patients living in areas of low socio-economic deprivation scores (IMD quintile 1: 27.3%).

The non-exacerbation sub-cluster had 15.4% (n = 3231/20,976) patients with a mean of 6.1 SABA prescriptions during the 12 months pre-index. Among patients on FP/SAL, 76.2% were indexed with low dose in the exacerbator sub-cluster, while in the non-exacerbator sub-cluster this was 58.5%.

Cluster 5, female, higher BMI (mean > 30 kg/m2) and deprived socio-economic status

This exacerbator sub-cluster contained 27.2% (n = 704/2591) patients and was characterized by a high proportion of female patients (90.5%), a mean age of 37.8 years, a high proportion of current smokers (53.3%) and a high proportion of patients living in deprivation (IMD quintile 5: 41.1%). This cluster also contained more patients with comorbid depression (15.1%) and URTI (43.0%) compared to other clusters.

The non-exacerbator sub-cluster was numerically larger (n = 6624/20,976), had a mean age of 57.7 years and had a smaller proportion of smokers (19.9%).

Homogeneous Clusters of High SABA Use Outcome Predictors and Treatment characteristics—Latent Class Cluster Analysis

Five clusters were observed among patients exhibiting high SABA use and six clusters among those with low SABA use (Table 4 [demographic and clinical characteristics]; Tables S5 and S6 [treatment characteristics and dosage]; Fig. 4). Four similar clusters/phenotypes were observed in both models, described below compared with the other clusters, with descriptive titles representing the most frequent characteristics observed. The other clusters/phenotypes are described in the Supplementary Results and Table S7. Less than 15% of the low SABA use groups were adherent to index ICS/LABA within 12 months post-index (Fig. 5).

Cluster 1, female, higher BMI (mean >29 kg/m2) and deprived socio-economic status

This high SABA use sub-cluster contained 34.5% (n = 3360/9737) of patients with high SABA use in the 12-month follow-up period) and was characterized by a high proportion of female patients (72.6%), mean BMI of 31.1 kg/m2, wheezing symptoms (14.0%) and having a high socio-economic deprivation score (IMD quintile 5: 27.3%). This cluster also had high primary care resource use (≥ 14 GP consultations: 46.0%) but limited hospitalizations in the 12 months pre-index.

The sub-cluster with low SABA use contained 16.1% (n = 2225/13,830) patients, had a mean age of 35.5 years and a mean BMI of 29.9 kg/m2.

Cluster 2, older (mean age > 50 years), high severity and comorbid

This high SABA use sub-cluster contained 22.4% (n = 2185/9737) patients and was characterized by high proportions of prior hospitalizations (all-cause: 100.0%; asthma-related: 71.7%), primary care resource use (≥ 14 GP consultations: 49.7%) and comorbidities (cardiac disease: 40.0%; GERD: 13.9%).

The sub-cluster with low SABA use was slightly smaller with 14.1% (n = 1953/13,830) patients and had a mean age of 50.8 years.

Cluster 3, allergic asthma

This high SABA use sub-cluster contained 6.5% (n = 632/9737) patients and was characterized by a high proportion of immune-mediated allergic comorbidities (atopy: 100%; allergic rhinitis: 100%). In addition, a high proportion of patients in this cluster were living in the most socio-economic status deprived areas (IMD quintile 5: 27.7%). The sub-cluster with low SABA use was numerically larger (7.4%, n = 1017/9737) and had a mean age of 46.0 years.

Cluster 4, female, older (mean age > 64 years), higher BMI (mean > 32 kg/m2) and severity

This high SABA use sub-cluster contained 0.1% (n = 14/9737) and was characterized by a high proportion of female patients (71.4%), a mean age of 65.4 years, mean BMI of 32.8 kg/m2, respiratory 1 medication (≥ 3 SABA prescriptions: 78.6%; ICS: 85.7%; OCS: 100%) and prior moderate-to-severe exacerbations (mean: 16.6).

The sub-cluster with low SABA use was the same size (0.1%, n = 14/13,830), with a mean age of 64.4 years and mean BMI of 32.7 kg/m2. The mean ICS/LABA therapy durations among the high SABA use and low SABA use groups were 9.9 and 11.4 months, respectively.

Further Treatment Characteristics

There were few differences in ICS/LABA proportion or dose between exacerbators/non-exacerbators and high/low SABA use in the clusters overall (described in the Supplementary Material; Tables S3−6). In the 12 months post-index, dose changes occurred in > 80.0% of patients. Where observed, the most common first change after index was for low-to-medium change. Overall, adherence was low with 20–30% achieving PDC ≥ 80%.

Discussion

This non-interventional, longitudinal, retrospective study used machine learning, via random forest analysis, to identify predictors of moderate-to-severe asthma exacerbations and high SABA use in adults newly initiating twice-daily single inhaler ICS/LABA treatment. Our analysis focused on twice-daily ICS/LABA as these represented the majority of prescriptions in the database. Key treatable predictors of exacerbation were comorbid cardiac disease, diabetes, depression, smoking status and BMI, consistent with findings from previous studies that have investigated factors associated with exacerbation risks [11, 15, 22, 23]. The key treatable predictors of high SABA use detected in this study included comorbid URTI, comorbid cardiac disease and prior SABA use, while non-treatable predictors included age and sex, similarly aligning with findings from previous research [5]. URTI may lead to increased SABA use by inducing or worsening respiratory symptoms such as breathlessness, even if not directly due to asthma activity [24]. This could prompt patients to use SABA for symptomatic relief [24]. Additionally, URTIs are known triggers for asthma exacerbation, which may further explain their association with higher SABA use [6]. Similarly, comorbid conditions such as cardiac disease may also cause breathlessness, potentially contributing to high SABA use [6, 24]. As each characteristic was evaluated independently without adjusting for potential confounders, the variables suggested to be key predictors in our study may be associated with other characteristics, which may partially or fully explain the predictive value of variables that the data have shown to be the strongest predictors. This limitation underscores the possibility of residual confounding and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

We further explored how predictors may interact and form clusters of distinct phenotypes using latent class cluster analysis to identify a range of homogeneous clusters regarding demographic and clinical characteristics. Of the 5–6 distinct clusters distinguished in this study, several represent known asthma phenotypes within the literature and clinical practice, for instance allergic asthma, older, high severity and comorbid, female, deprived socio-economic status and high BMI [11, 15]. Although differences were observed in specific baseline socio-demographic, clinical and treatment characteristics, no clear traits were distinct between patients experiencing an exacerbation or not and between high and low SABA use.

Previous studies have performed cluster analyses in patients with asthma, aiming to identify clinical phenotypes in patients with severe disease [25–27]. In this study, some clusters exhibited both treatable and non-treatable traits, such as the older age and with a history of cardiac disease and the young, smoker and low comorbidity clusters. Therefore, an asthma management approach should focus on addressing treatable traits alongside acknowledging the risk of certain non-treatable traits on poor disease control. The treatable traits distinguished in this study, such as comorbidities, smoking status and BMI, can be targeted by healthcare professionals, with treatment tailored according to clinical phenotypes. Targeting these traits may improve exacerbation and high SABA use outcomes. Evidence suggests that treating obesity in patients with asthma can lead to improved symptom control, reduced exacerbation rates and decreased reliance on reliever medication, supporting the role of obesity as a modifiable treatment target in asthma management [28–30]. Most treatable traits were found in high severity clusters in both exacerbation and high SABA use groups in this study, which is probably the reason for high healthcare resource utilization observed in these clusters.

Healthcare consultation rates have been shown to correlate with increasing levels of socio-economic deprivation [31], while older adults from more socio-economically deprived areas (and conversely, younger adults from less deprived areas) have previously been reported to have higher asthma mortality rates [32]. We found high proportions of patients from areas of deprived socio-economic status (IMD quintile 5) in the exacerbation outcome Clusters 2 (older, high severity, comorbid) and 5 (female, high BMI, deprived socio-economic status) as well as the high SABA use outcome Clusters 1 (female, high BMI, deprived socio-economic status) and 3 (allergic asthma). Interventions targeting these treatable traits in areas of socio-economic deprivation may be associated with potential benefits in asthma control.

The largest cluster in the exacerbation outcome was Cluster 4, the older age and cardiac disease population. This is not unexpected given that previous studies have detected associations between cardiovascular disease with asthma [33, 34]. Moreover, it has been reported that older age is strongly associated with certain potentially treatable traits, such as comorbidities, compared to younger age [26]. Consequently, older populations are at higher risk of worse asthma outcomes [35].

Treatment characteristics, such as index ICS/LABA and device type, were generally similar across clusters in this study, indicating an opportunity for targeted treatments based on clinical phenotypes that may be beneficial for healthcare professions to consider alongside the progressive stepwise asthma recommendations [36]. The most common index ICS/LABA therapy and device type was BDP/FOR and MDI, respectively. Adherence to treatment was generally low, with 20–30% of patients achieving PDC ≥ 80%. Previous research has shown the complex relationship between medication adherence and asthma, highlighting barriers including adverse events, delayed onset of action and complexity of medication regimes [37]. Therefore, improved medication adherence would support asthma control. Potential treatable traits detected in this study may allow personalized management of asthma disease amongst specific patient groups, which may result in better targeted treatment and personalized intervention [12]. Healthcare professional education on current guideline recommendations may be a key to reducing high SABA, particularly as recent updates from GINA and British Thoracic Society/National Institute for Health and Care Excellence/Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network recommend ICS/FOR as maintenance and reliver therapy over ICS/LABA plus SABA regimens [3, 36].

Limitations

The current study has some limitations. The study design and eligibility criteria may have introduced bias, and this could affect the generalizability of results. Misclassification of medical events is possible in CPRD Aurum; while the positive predictive value of diagnoses and symptoms is generally considered to be high, the absence of medical event codes may not accurately reflect the absence of real-world medical events (negative predictive value may be low) [6]. Given the retrospective nature of this database study, symptom reporting may have been incomplete or inconsistently documented, with resultant underestimation in our analyses. In addition, assessment of timely ICS/LABA initiation was not possible and patients with delayed escalation may have experienced different outcomes. Hence, the possibility of introduced misclassification bias cannot be ruled out. Nonetheless, CPRD is generally considered a gold standard for identifying asthma populations, and missed diagnoses are uncommon [38].

The prescription medications may not have been dispensed and/or consumed as prescribed. Therefore, the treatment variables assessed during the follow-up period may not accurately represent true use. Another possible limitation of this study is that ICS dose was not considered or available so assessing the effect of cumulative dose was not possible. Another limitation of this study is the potential underestimation of reliever use in patients prescribed BUD/FOR, which were used by 16.5% of the cohort in this study. Assessment of SABA may have been skewed by the use of ICS/FOR as both maintenance and reliever therapy per GINA recommendations. Including reliever users in the outcome may introduce heterogeneity into this study, as patients using relievers versus those on maintenance therapy were likely to have different characteristics. This may have led to an overestimation of exacerbation rates during the follow-up period, assuming that reliever users have higher underlying risk of exacerbation compared with maintenance users [3, 39]. The current analysis did not evaluate patterns of OCS exposure, such as burst versus chronic daily use, nor did it include data on dosing or treatment duration. Future investigations should consider these parameters to enhance the understanding of OCS associated morbidity.

The high average age at ICB/LABA initiation may reflect delayed diagnosis or misclassification of asthma versus other conditions such as fixed airway disease. This highlights a potential need for improving diagnostic accuracy and public awareness around asthma symptoms and timely care seeking, especially in older patients. Duplication of data from CPRD records could have occurred because of general practices becoming absorbed by other practices. However, in August 2022 only 29 practices were affected (which comprise around 1% of the database), so this duplication was considered to have had a low impact on our findings due to infrequent occurrence [16].

Conclusions

This study distinguished treatable and non-treatable predictors by exacerbation and high SABA use using random forest analysis. Age, obesity, smoking history, URTI and deprived socio-economic status were exacerbation predicting factors. Moreover, comorbid URTI, comorbid cardiac disease, age and sex were high SABA use predicting factors. Distinct asthma phenotypes were distinguished using an unsupervised latent class cluster analysis approach. However, treatment characteristics were generally similar across clusters in this study, indicating an opportunity for targeted treatments based on clinical phenotypes. This study highlights groups of patients who may be at risk of frequent exacerbation and high SABA use, which could be considered by healthcare professionals during annual asthma reviews. Our findings reinforce the importance of personalized treatments with the goal of improving clinical outcomes in patients with moderate-to-severe asthma.
