Sleep and Parkinson’s disease: a population-based study from the CHARLS cohort
Authors: Yunxi Wang, Wenqing Lv, Yaxin Li, Jibao Yang, Anran Lv, Xiran Li, Zishu Yan, Jiahui Jin, Pengwei Zhuang, Hong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo provide precise prevention strategies for Parkinson’s disease (PD), this study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2020 and employed a combination of cross-sectional and cohort analyses to systematically investigate the association between sleep patterns and PD risk. The study revealed, for the first time in a Chinese community-based population, significant age and gender associations between sleep patterns and PD risk. Cross-sectional analysis indicated a significant negative correlation between sleep duration and PD risk in individuals aged ≤60 years, while a U-shaped trend was observed in those >60 years, with the highest risk occurring at approximately 5.2 h of sleep. Cohort analysis further revealed that among individuals with sleep disorders, the incidence of PD was higher in those aged ≤60 years than in those aged >60 years; in contrast, whereas among those without sleep disorders, the incidence was generally higher in women than in men, with this difference being more pronounced in individuals with longer sleep duration. In summary, this study provides a basis for differentiated sleep interventions tailored to specific demographic groups and underscores the public health importance of sleep management in the prevention of PD.Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder with a global prevalence second only to Alzheimer’s disease. The total number of PD patients in China is estimated to reach 3.62 million1, with the fastest-growing prevalence rate worldwide2. The disease primarily affects the elderly population3, with a prevalence rate reaching 1% among individuals over 60 years old and as high as 3% among those over 804.

Sleep is a fundamental element of human life, playing a crucial role in health maintenance and promotion5,6. Sleep health encompasses multiple dimensions, including sleep duration, sleep quality, and the presence of sleep disorders. Sleep disorders (SD) have become a significant health issue facing the elderly7.

In addition to motor symptoms8, PD patients often experience non-motor symptoms9. Among these non-motor symptoms, sleep disorders are particularly prevalent, affecting up to 75% of PD patients. The prevalence and severity of sleep disorders tend to increase with age10–12. However, research on the multidimensional impact of sleep disorders on PD patients remains insufficient, and the epidemiological characteristics of sleep disorders in elderly PD patients have yet to be fully elucidated.

Based on these considerations, this study uses data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2020, employing a combination of cross-sectional and cohort analyses to systematically investigate the association between sleep and PD risk. By clarifying the relationship between sleep and PD across different demographic groups, this study aims to provide a basis for developing targeted prevention strategies for PD.

Relationship between sleep and PD

In a multivariate logistic regression analysis, sleep duration and sleep quality were evaluated as independent variables, with the findings presented in Table 2. Sleep duration of 7–9 h and “Very little or none at all” were used as reference categories. Model 1 did not incorporate any covariates, Model 2 adjusted for Age and Gender, and Model 3 further adjusted for marital status, residence, smoking, drinking, farm work, and nap time. In the Model 3, the risk associated with the > 9 h sleep duration group was reduced by nearly 50% (OR = 0.594, 95% CI: 0.235–1.505, P = 0.272). The risk in the 4–7 h group was comparable to that of the reference group (OR = 0.970, 95% CI: 0.696–1.353, P = 0.859), whereas the risk in the ≤4-hour group was significantly elevated (OR = 1.496, 95% CI: 1.027–2.180, P = 0.036). This trend was consistently observed across all models, reinforcing the association between sleep duration, sleep quality, and the risk of PD.

RCS analysis

To further investigate the nature of this relationship, we selected the highest weighted sleep duration as the exposure variable and conducted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis separately for the age groups ≤60 and > 60, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The results demonstrated a statistically significant linear association between sleep duration and the risk of PD in individuals aged ≤ 60 (P = 0.002), with the risk decreasing as sleep duration increased. Conversely, in individuals aged > 60, a significant nonlinear relationship was observed (P for nonlinearity = 0.018), characterized by a U-shaped pattern in which the highest estimated risk occurring at approximately 5.2 h of sleep (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the degeneration and death of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain, leading to a significant dopamine deficiency within the brain. This pathological process directly gives rise to the characteristic motor symptoms: resting tremor, muscle rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural gait abnormalities. However, the impact of PD extends well beyond motor dysfunction. Several years prior to the onset of motor symptoms, a range of non-motor disorders may appear, primarily including hyposmia, autonomic dysfunction, emotional disturbances, and cognitive impairments. Among the various non-motor symptoms, sleep disorders are especially common13. Patients frequently experience difficulties in falling asleep, reduced sleep duration, frequent awakenings, and daytime sleepiness14. Consequently, the management of sleep disorders has become a crucial aspect of clinical care for individuals with PD.

The mechanisms through which sleep disorders contribute to the onset and progression of PD are multifaceted. Notably, insufficient deep sleep duration or poor sleep quality significantly impairs the brain’s glymphatic system, leading to decreased clearance efficiency of neurotoxic proteins, such as α-synuclein. This impairment facilitates their abnormal accumulation and pathological spread within the brain15,16. Additionally, sleep disorders can induce neuroinflammatory responses in the brain, exacerbating oxidative stress and creating a toxic microenvironment that damages dopaminergic neurons17. These mechanisms are particularly pronounced in the elderly population, who, due to age-related declines in dopamine reserves and reduced neuronal self-repair capacity, are more susceptible to surpassing the body’s compensatory threshold. This susceptibility accelerates dopaminergic neuron degeneration, ultimately promoting the onset and progression of Parkinson’s disease18. To systematically investigate the association between sleep disorders and the risk of PD onset, this study utilized data from the CHARLS covering the period from 2011 to 2020. Through a combination of cross-sectional and cohort analyses, we assessed the impact of sleep duration and quality on PD risk among middle-aged and elderly populations. The findings confirmed that both sleep duration and quality are significantly correlated with PD risk.

Initially, using cross-sectional data from 2020, we conducted LASSO regression analysis to identify sleep duration, sleep quality, age, and gender as significant determinants of PD, with sleep duration emerging as the most critical factor (Fig. 1). Furthermore, a meta-analysis of sleep in patients with PD revealed that total sleep time, slow-wave sleep, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep were significantly reduced in individuals with PD, whereas wakefulness after sleep onset and the sleep fragmentation index were increased19.

PD is a well-documented age-associated neurological disorder, with its prevalence progressively increasing with age. To further investigate the influence of sleep duration on PD risk across different age cohorts ( ≤ 60 years and >60 years), we employed RCS analysis. As depicted in Fig. 2, among individuals aged ≤60 years, there was a significant negative correlation between sleep duration and PD risk (P = 0.002), indicating that longer sleep durations were associated with a reduced risk of PD. Conversely, in the > 60 years age group, a significant nonlinear relationship was observed (P for nonlinearity = 0.018) was observed, characterized by a U-shaped pattern, with the highest risk occurring at approximately 5.2 h of sleep. This suggests significant age-related variations in the impact of sleep duration on PD risk. Moreover, neither sleep duration nor sleep quality demonstrated statistically significant differences in risk impact across subgroups stratified by age, gender, marital status, residence, alcohol consumption, or engagement in agricultural work (all P-values > 0.05, Fig. 3). These findings imply that sleep-based intervention strategies may have wide-ranging applicability.

In the cohort analysis, we followed 8624 baseline participants without memory-related disorders (including PD) starting in 2011. By 2020, 97 new cases of PD were identified, resulting in an overall incidence rate of 1.12% (Table 4). Using a combined exposure model that incorporated sleep duration (using the mean as a threshold) and sleep status (defined by the presence of sleep disorders persisting for two or more years), subgroup analysis indicated that the lowest incidence rate occurred in the group with sleep duration ≤ the mean and no sleep disorders (0.65%). Conversely, the highest incidence rate was found in the group with sleep duration ≤ the mean and concurrent sleep disorders (1.46%). This disparity underscores the significant impact of sleep disorders in elevating PD risk. Importantly, the comparison between the group with short sleep duration ( ≤ the mean) and concurrent sleep disorders (Q3) and those with short sleep duration but no sleep disorders (Q1) revealed a statistically significant difference in PD incidence (adjusted P = 0.018). This finding suggests that among individuals with shorter sleep duration, the presence of persistent sleep disorders is an important contributor to increased PD risk, highlighting sleep disorders as a potential modifiable risk factor in this vulnerable population. Prior research has demonstrated that sleep efficiency in PD patients is markedly lower than that of healthy controls, with sleep quality further declining as the disease progresses20. This phenomenon may be associated with the substantially elevated plasma melatonin levels observed in PD patients relative to healthy controls21. Furthermore, non-motor symptoms frequently experienced by PD patients, such as depression and anxiety, also significantly compromise their sleep quality22.

Subgroup analysis showed that, in the absence of sleep disorders, the incidence of PD was slightly higher among individuals over 60 years of age compared to those aged 60 years or younger (Q1: 0.69% vs. 0.38%; Q2: 0.95% vs. 0.87%). Conversely, in the presence of sleep disorders, the incidence rate among individuals aged 60 years or younger was significantly higher than that of those over 60 years old (Q3: 1.95% vs. 1.40%; Q4: 2.27% vs. 1.04%, Fig. 4B). These observations suggest that, in the absence of sleep disorders, age is the predominant factor influencing PD incidence; however, when sleep disorders are present, the impact of sleep status becomes more pronounced. Additionally, irrespective of sleep status or duration, women consistently exhibited a substantially higher incidence of PD compared to men (Fig. 4C).

Regarding gender differences in PD, existing studies present a complex picture. Data from the Global Burden of Disease study indicate that from 1990 to 2021, the incidence and prevalence rates of PD were consistently remained higher in men than in women23. However, this overall trend does not fully explain gender differences in specific subgroups or under particular exposure conditions. The present study presents a different finding: in the presence of sleep disorders, women aged ≤60 years had a significantly higher incidence of PD than men of the same age group, suggesting that this subgroup may have heightened susceptibility. This finding corroborates existing research on estrogen-mediated modulation of neuroinflammation—estrogen plays an important role in regulating microglial activation and inflammatory responses, which may influence the risk of neurodegenerative diseases in women24,25. Taken together, gender differences in PD appear to be multidimensional: while the incidence is higher in men at the global population level, women may exhibit higher risk in specific age groups and under specific exposure conditions (e.g., sleep disorders). The complexity underlying these differences may result from an interplay of biological factors (such as sex hormones and immune inflammation), environmental exposures, and social behaviors, warranting further exploration in future studies. In examining the correlation between sleep duration and sleep quality in the context of PD, cross-sectional analyses showed a stronger association with sleep duration, whereas the cohort analysis suggested a more substantial impact of sleep quality. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that adequate sleep duration and high-quality sleep represent are two distinct yet complementary core elements essential for health benefits. Insufficient sleep duration often leads to immediate physiological dysregulation due to short-term “under-exposure,” as seen in the rapid onset of cognitive decline26. Conversely, chronic poor sleep quality exerts detrimental effects through a gradual, cumulative pathophysiological process, which is closely linked to a significant increase in the risk of cardiovascular disease and neurodegenerative disorders27.

We further evaluated the predictive value of sleep-related indicators for PD using refined feature engineering and rigorous statistical testing. The results showed that the sleep quality model (AUC = 0.622) outperformed the sleep duration model (AUC = 0.592), suggesting that sleep quality, as an early manifestation of neurodegenerative changes, may better reflect underlying pathophysiological alterations than sleep duration alone. When the two indicators were combined, the predictive performance improved further, achieving an AUC of 0.644. Although the overall predictive ability of the models remained modest, these findings indicate that sleep characteristics may serve as potential indicators for PD risk screening and highlight the potential utility of multidimensional sleep assessment in risk identification. The limited predictive performance may be attributed to the inherent multidimensional complexity of sleep and the multifactorial nature of PD pathogenesis. Future studies could further integrate multidimensional sleep parameters such as sleep rhythm and sleep efficiency, along with multimodal biomarkers, to enhance the accuracy and efficacy of PD risk prediction.

This study highlights a significant association between sleep duration and the risk of PD, suggesting that targeting sleep may be a promising strategy for early prevention and treatment. Currently, commonly used sleep aids include benzodiazepines, non-benzodiazepine GABAA receptor agonists, and melatonin. These agents are limited by issues such as tolerance, dependence, and daytime residual effects with long-term use28. In recent years, research on sleep interventions in PD has increasingly explored the potential for neuroprotective benefits beyond symptom management. Suvorexant is a selective orexin receptor antagonist and belongs to a new class of insomnia medications. Although randomized controlled trials targeting Parkinson’s disease patients are currently underway29, clinical studies have confirmed that suvorexant can increase the proportion of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep while maintaining overall sleep architecture30, which also suggests that optimizing sleep architecture may provide indirect neuroprotective benefits. Levodopa sustained-release formulation (CREXONT) has been shown to improve nighttime motor symptoms while also enhancing sleep quality in Parkinson’s patients, indicating that better sleep may contribute to broader disease management as a synergistic benefit31. Monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) inhibitors have also been found to improve sleep quality in PD32, potentially exerting dual effects on disease pathology by modulating dopamine pathways along with sleep regulation. Together, these findings support the potential clinical value of intervening in PD through sleep improvement, offering a reference for future drug development and clinical research focused on sleep.

This study employed publicly accessible databases to investigate the relationship between sleep and PD; however, several limitations persist. First, the identification of PD in 2020 was primarily based on retrospective inference from questionnaire items rather than directly using confirmed diagnostic information, which may introduce recall bias and misclassification risk. Future studies that combine self-reported data with medical records and clinical assessments would help improve the accuracy of PD identification. Second, the study population was limited to a specific cohort of middle-aged and older adults in China, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic, geographic, or sociocultural contexts. Further studies involving larger and more diverse populations are warranted to validate the robustness and generalizability of the present findings. In conclusion, this study elucidates the pivotal role of sleep in the pathogenesis of PD, affirming sleep as modifiable risk factor across diverse populations and highlighting variations based on age and gender. Notably, we recommended that individuals over the age of 60 maintain at least 5.2 hof sleep per day, and emphasize the need for enhanced sleep monitoring in women under 60 years of age who have sleep disorders. These findings not only underscore the importance of sleep management as a fundamental component of primary prevention for neurodegenerative diseases but also suggest that identifying specific sleep disturbances can enhance early screening efforts for high-risk groups. In turn, this helps facilitate the development of targeted preventive strategies for PD.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data used in this study were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a comprehensive interdisciplinary research project organized and implemented by Peking University. The study covers a wide range of domains, including basic personal information, health status and physical measurements, household structure and financial support, healthcare utilization and insurance, employment and retirement, economic indicators (such as income and personal assets), and community characteristics33. The cross-sectional study utilized the CHARLS dataset, employing 2020 data for cross-sectional analysis. The initial dataset comprised 19,395 participants. After excluding individuals with missing PD data (n = 902), missing sleep data (n = 2086), and incomplete covariate information (n = 4), the final analysis sample size was 16,403 individuals. Owing to excessive missing values in the sleep indicators for 2018, the cohort study was restricted to data from the 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2020 waves. The initial dataset comprised 17,705 participants who participated consistently across the 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2020 waves. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missing data on memory-related diseases present in 2011 (n = 200); (2) memory-related diseases in the 2011 (n = 277); (3) missing PD data at the 2020 endpoint (n = 4382); (4) missing sleep data (n = 4156); and (5) incomplete covariate information (n = 66). Consequently, a total of 8624 participants were included in the final analysis. Figure 6 presents a detailed flowchart of the participant screening process.

Diagnosis of PD or memory-related disease

This study identified PD using question DA002 from the “Health Status and Function” questionnaire in the 2020 CHARLS database: “Compared to [ZIWTime], has [XRName]‘s [XChroDisType] improved, remained stable, or worsened?” Here, [XChroDisType] in DA002_13 refers to PD. Responses “1. Improved”, “2. Worsened”, “3. Remained about the same”, or “99. Did not have this condition at last doctor’s visit” were considered indicative of a prior PD diagnosis at the time of the 2020 survey. In the 2011 data, PD status was assessed using question DA007, “Have you been diagnosed with [conditions listed below, read one by one] by a doctor?” from the “Health Status and Functioning”. Here, (12) denotes Memory-related disease (such as Alzheimer’s disease, brain atrophy, and Parkinson’s disease). A response of 1 indicates a diagnosis, while 2 indicates no diagnosis. Individuals without a diagnosis were included as the initial healthy cohort for subsequent analysis and statistics.

Assessment of SD

Sleep indicators were assessed using data from the Health Status (III) module of the CHARLS. The data source for the 2013–2020 period was consistent across modules: sleep duration was assessed using item DA030, which asked: “In the past month, how many hours did [XRName] usually sleep each night?” Responses ranged from 0 to 24 h. Unknown responses were coded as −1 and subsequently excluded as missing values. Sleep quality was assessed using item DC022: “My sleep quality is poor”. This item offered six response options: 1. Rarely or none of the time ( < 1 day), 2. Some or a little of the time (1–2 days), 3. Occasionally or a moderate amount of the time (3–4 days), 4. Most or all of the time (5–7 days), 997. Unsure, 999. Refused to answer. Responses coded as 997 and 999 were excluded as missing values.

Sleep duration in 2011 was assessed using item DA049, which asked: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)? (This may be shorter than the number of hours you spend in bed)”. Responses ranged from 0 to 24 h; unknown responses were coded as -1 and subsequently excluded as missing values. Sleep quality was assessed using item “DC015. My sleep was restless”. This item offered four response options: 1. Rarely or none of the time ( < 1 day), 2. Some or a little of the time (1–2 days), 3. Occasionally or a moderate amount of the time (3–4 days), 4. Most or all of the time (5–7 days).

The 2020 nap duration was assessed by item DA031: “Over the past month, how long did [XRName] typically nap?”. Responses ranged from 0 to 300 min. Unknown responses were coded as −1 and excluded as missing values.

Covariates

In this study, covariates were categorized into three distinct groups: (1) demographic and social characteristics, which include age, gender, marital status, and place of residence. It should be noted that educational attainment was excluded from the analysis due to a high incidence of missing data; (2) health behaviors and conditions, encompassing factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption; and (3) employment status, with particular attention to engagement in agricultural work.

Specifically, age data for 2020 were derived from the XRAge field and categorized into two groups: “≤60 years” and “å 60 years”. Age data for 2011 were calculated based on four questions: BA002 (“When were you born?”), with components for year, month, and day; BA003 (“Is your answer to BA002 based on the solar or lunar calendar?”); and for respondents with missing values, BA004 (“What is your age?”) Gender information for 2020 was obtained from the XRGender field, and for 2011 from the rgender variable, both containing the categories “male” and “female”. Educational level in 2022 had a high rate of missing data and was not included as a covariate; for 2011, educational level was derived from BD001 (“What is the highest level of education you have attained?”) and categorized as high school or below versus above high school.

Marital status in 2020 was assessed via the BA011 field (“What is your current marital status?”); in 2011, it was obtained from BE001 (“What is your marital status?”), where “1. Married and living with spouse” was defined as “Cohabiting”, while responses 2 through 6 (e.g., married but temporarily separated, separated, divorced, widowed, never married) were collectively categorized as “Separated”. In 2020, residence information was assessed using the BA008 field; options “2. Urban-rural fringe area” and “3. Rural” were merged into a single category, while other options were processed separately. No specific residence information matching this classification was available for 2011.

We utilize the DA. Health Status (Part 3) module to assess smoking and alcohol consumption data35. Smoking data for 2020 were derived from DA046, which asked “Has XRName ever participated in smoking activities, including cigarettes, pipes, or chewing tobacco?”, and DA047, which asked, “Is XRName currently still smoking or has he/she quit?”. Responses were categorized into three groups: “Still smoking”, “Has quit”, and “Never smoked”. Alcohol consumption data were derived from DA051, which asked, “In the past year, has XRName consumed alcoholic beverages such as beer, wine, rice wine, huangjiu, baijiu, or medicinal liquor? How often?”. Participants who reported drinking more than once a month or less than once a month were classified as “drinkers,” while those who reported never drinking were classified as “non-drinkers.”

For the 2011 data, smoking status was derived from “Have you ever chewed tobacco, smoked a pipe, smoked self-rolled cigarettes, or smoked cigarettes/cigars?” with response options of 1. Smoker, 2. Non-smoker. Alcohol consumption data were derived from DA067: “Did you drink any alcoholic beverages, such as beer, wine, or liquor in the past year? How often?”. Responses included: 1. Drink more than once a month, 2. Drink but less than once a month, 3. None of these. Responses 1 and 2 were grouped as “drinkers”, and response 3 was categorized as “non-drinkers.”

Research has demonstrated a correlation between the onset of PD and the use of herbicides and pesticides, such as paraquat36. Consequently, the variable “engagement in agricultural work” was included as a covariate in the analysis. To assess this variable, item FA001 from the FA Work Profile (Part 1) module was used. This item assesses whether the respondent has participated in agricultural work for more than 10 days in the past year. Activities considered as agricultural work include farming, managing fruit trees, collecting agricultural and forestry products, fish farming, fishing, raising livestock, or selling self-produced agricultural products at the market.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 and R version 4.5.1. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate baseline characteristics in both cross-sectional and cohort analyses, including gender, age, marital status, and drinking habits. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the Pearson chi-square test (χ²).

For the cross-sectional analysis, multivariate logistic regression to evaluate the association between sleep duration, sleep quality, and PD, reporting effect sizes as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). In addition, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model was used. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined using 10-fold cross-validation, and PD-related indicators were screened over 1000 iterations. We further applied the Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) regression model to assess its impact on disease risk. The dataset was divided into training and testing sets in a 4:6 ratio. Using the bootstrap method with 1000 iterations, we analyzed component weights to determine each indicator’s relative contribution to PD risk37,38.

Based on the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, a generalized additive model (GAM) was fitted. The sleep duration corresponding to the highest risk point was calculated using the model derivative method. The statistical significance of the nonlinear trend was assessed using a combination of the Wald test, likelihood ratio test, and spline test. In addition to examining the overall relationship between sleep duration, sleep quality, and PD, subgroup analyses were conducted based on variables including age, gender, place of residence, marital status, alcohol consumption habits, and participation in farming activities.

In the cohort analysis, we grouped participants by sleep duration and calculated the average sleep duration for each respondent across 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2020. Participants with sleep duration ≤ the average were coded as 1, and those with sleep duration > the average were coded as 2. For sleep quality grouping, participants who experienced sleep disturbances for ≥ 1 day per year for two or more consecutive years were assigned a value of 2, while the remaining participants were assigned a value of 1.

The combined sleep indicator grouping was defined as follows: Q1: Sleep duration 1 × Sleep quality 1, Q2: Sleep duration 2 × Sleep quality 1, Q3: Sleep duration 1 × Sleep quality 2, Q4: Sleep duration 2 × Sleep quality 2. Following baseline analysis of 2011 data, we calculated the overall incidence of PD, incidence rates stratified by gender, and incidence rates grouped by age ( ≤ 60 years and >60 years). Incidence rates were calculated was the number of cases divided by the total population, expressed as a percentage. The Pearson χ² test was used to compare overall differences in PD incidence among the four groups. When a significant overall difference was detected, pairwise comparisons were conducted, and the Bonferroni method was applied to adjust P-values for multiple comparisons to control the Type I error rate.

Finally, to evaluate the predictive performance of sleep-related indicators for PD, we constructed three logistic regression models: sleep duration alone, sleep quality alone, and a combined model incorporating both sleep duration and sleep quality. The predictive performance of each model was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To obtain robust estimates, we employed a bootstrap resampling procedure with 1000 iterations to calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each AUC. Differences in AUC values between models were compared using the DeLong test, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
