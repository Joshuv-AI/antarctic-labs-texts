Targeting Cell Cycle Vulnerabilities in Cancers: Emerging Strategies for Therapeutic Development
Authors: Nana Kamakura, Minji Jo, Motoko Takahashi, Toru Hirota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCell division is a fundamental cellular process that ensures propagation of genetic material to daughter cells. This process relies on the fidelity of DNA replication and chromosome segregation and is primarily regulated by sequential activity of cyclin‐dependent kinases (Cdks) in the cell cycle program. Mitogenic signals lead to the activation of Cdks and promote cell cycle progression, whereas checkpoints act as surveillance systems to halt cell cycle progression in response to abnormal conditions. In cancers, these regulatory networks are frequently disrupted due to continuous activation of mitogenic/oncogenic signaling and/or loss of checkpoint control, resulting in uncontrolled cell cycle progression and thus genome instability. Nevertheless, cancer cells often maintain robust cell cycle control by virtue of compensatory mechanisms. Counterintuitively, however, this apparent robustness increases sensitivity to perturbations and creates unique targetable vulnerabilities. Intervening in these vulnerabilities in the cell cycle control is therefore the rationale for the effective and selective cancer treatment. In this review, we summarize emerging insights into how we can interfere with cancer cell proliferation by targeting the cancer specific vulnerabilities arising from cell cycle dysregulation. We highlight advances in targeting Cdks, checkpoint kinases and mitotic kinases, as well as recent discoveries from CRISPR‐genetic screens that reveal synthetic lethal genes. Understanding these vulnerabilities provides a framework for developing cancer therapies that exploit specific dependencies in cell cycle control.Keywords: cancer, cdk, cell cycle, kinase, synthetic lethal, vulnerability

Introduction

The cell cycle is governed by the sequential activation of Cdks, which act as the molecular engines driving cell division (Figure 1) [1]. This engine is linked to extracellular proliferative signals (mitogens) that activate Ras and downstream mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, inducing cyclin D expression and activating the Cdk4/6‐cyclin D complex to promote E2F transcription factor‐dependent G1/S transition [2, 3]. Constitutive activation of this signaling sustains E2F activity through RB phosphorylation, promoting uncontrolled S phase entry. In parallel, Cdk1, Cdk2, and Cdk7 regulate mitosis, DNA replication, and transcription, respectively [4, 5].

Cell cycle regulation by Cdk‐cyclin complexes. Schematic overview of the eukaryotic cell cycle showing phase‐specific Cdk‐cyclin activities. Cdk4/6‐cyclin D drives G1 progression and facilitates passage through the R point, while Cdk2‐cyclin E triggers the G1/S transition and S phase entry. DNA synthesis during S phase is maintained by Cdk2‐cyclin A, followed by Cdk1‐cyclin A during G2 phase. Mitosis entry is promoted by Cdk1‐cyclin B, whose activity is regulated by inhibitory phosphorylation by Wee1 and Myt1, and activating dephosphorylation by Cdc25.

Dysregulation of Cdks is frequently observed in cancers. Cancer cells, therefore, become highly dependent on checkpoint pathways to tolerate replication stress [6]. Elevated replication stress often leads to DNA damage, including single‐strand breaks, which are repaired by DNA damage response (DDR) pathways such as poly ADP‐ribose polymerase (PARP)‐mediated repair [7]. To prevent premature mitotic entry in the presence of unrepaired DNA damage, cancer cells must tightly coordinate S‐phase progression with G2/M checkpoint control. This creates a heightened dependence on replication stress response and checkpoint kinases, including ATR, Chk1, and Wee1, thereby becoming therapeutically vulnerable in replication‐stressed cancers [8, 9, 10].

During mitosis, kinetochores‐microtubule attachment is fundamental for faithful chromosome segregation and is regulated by mitotic kinases including Aurora B and Polo‐like kinase 1 (Plk1) [11, 12]. Although these kinases are frequently overexpressed in cancers and therefore are expected to be promising targets, cells can proliferate despite their perturbation. This apparent robustness reflects increased reliance on compensatory mechanisms and thus may create hidden vulnerabilities. To uncover these cancer‐specific dependencies, functional genomic approaches, such as CRISPR‐based screening, are an effective strategy.

In this review, we summarize cancer vulnerabilities arising from cell cycle dysregulation and discuss how they can be used to invent new therapeutic strategies.

Molecular Basis of Cdk4/6‐Dependent Cell Cycle Progression

The fidelity of the cell cycle is maintained by multiple checkpoint pathways that ensure the accurate DNA replication and mitosis [1]. These checkpoints act as surveillance mechanisms, preventing cell cycle progression under conditions of DNA damage, replication stress, or defective mitotic spindle assembly. Four major checkpoints are deployed in the cell cycle: G1/S checkpoint, intra‐S checkpoint, G2/M checkpoint, and spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) (Figure 1). In cancer, however, these regulatory networks are frequently disrupted in association with the inactivation of tumor suppressors and/or the activation of oncogenes [8]. In particular, the expression of Cdk inhibitor proteins such as p16 and p21, which act as tumor suppressors and are essential for checkpoint control, is often downregulated. Consequently, cells lose the ability to control the cell cycle when necessary [13, 14]. Such insufficiencies not only promote genomic instability but also create unique dependencies that can be effective therapeutic targets.

The entry into S phase is primarily mediated by the Cdk4/6‐cyclin D‐RB‐E2F axis [1, 3] (Figure 2). When the cells receive mitogens through epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), these signals are transmitted to the nucleus through the MAPK cascade (Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK). Activation of this pathway induces cyclin D expression, leading to the activation of the Cdk4/6‐cyclin D complex. Cdk4/6‐cyclin D phosphorylates retinoblastoma protein (RB) that binds to transcription factor E2F and suppresses its function, releasing E2F from RB [1, 3]. This leads to the expression of S phase regulators, including cyclin E and cyclin A, thereby activating Cdk2‐cyclin E/A complexes. Cdk2 also phosphorylates RB, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains E2F activity and promotes entry into S phase. At the end of S phase, cyclin A and cyclin B are accumulated and associate with Cdk1 to promote entry into mitosis. Thus, activation of Cdk4/6‐cyclin D initiates a cascade that leads to Cdk2 activation, after which cell cycle progression becomes independent of mitogen signaling. This critical decision point has long been known as the restriction point (R point) [2].

Mitogen and Cdk4/6 control of the G1/S transition Mitogenic signaling through EGFR activates the MAPK pathway, inducing cyclin D expression and activation of Cdk4/6. Phosphorylation of RB by Cdk4/6 relieves active E2F1–3 and promotes transcription of S phase genes, leading to Cdk2 activation. Active Cdk2 also phosphorylates RB, forming a mitogen‐independent positive feedback loop. In parallel, Cdk4/6 activity is also required for releasing a repressive p107/p130‐E2F4/5 complex, constituting a mitogen‐dependent feed‐forward loop.

Although cell cycle progression after R point has been thought to proceed autonomously, several studies have demonstrated that lack of mitogen can still halt cell cycle progression even after the R point. This observation cannot be fully explained by the conventional RB‐only model, which assumes that E2F activity becomes self‐sustaining. To reconcile this discrepancy, it is necessary to consider an additional pathway that activates the Cdk2‐cyclin A complex. While RB restrains activating E2Fs (E2F1, E2F2, E2F3), p107 and p130 form repressive complexes with E2F4 and E2F5 in the promoter region, thereby suppressing transcription of cell cycle genes. Thus, even after RB phosphorylation releases activating E2Fs, transcription of key S phase genes is still restrained by p107/p130‐E2F4/5 complexes. Cdk4/6 activity promotes the relief of this repressive complex and thereby activates Cdk2‐cyclin A. Importantly, this pathway requires continuous mitogen signaling and Cdk4/6 activity to maintain active Cdk2‐cyclin A, forming a mitogen dependent feed‐forward loop. These findings highlight that Cdk4/6 activity remains important beyond the G1/S transition to cell cycle progression [3]. These mechanistic insights reveal why cancer cells are highly dependent on Cdk4/6, providing a therapeutic potential.

Targeting Cdk4/6 to Induce Cell Cycle Brake and Senescence in Cancer

The critical role of Cdk4/6 in cell cycle progression makes Cdk4/6 a promising target for cancer therapy. Inhibition of Cdk4/6 therefore can potently arrest the cell cycle, and many cancer cells show high sensitivity to Cdk4/6 inhibition. Indeed, Cdk4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib have been applied to clinical practice and shown therapeutic efficacy particularly in advanced HR+/HER2 (hormone receptor‐positive, HER2 receptor‐negative) breast cancers. In these cancers, estrogen‐driven mitogenic signaling often results in hyperactivation of Cdk4/6, rendering the cells highly dependent on Cdk4/6 [15, 16]. Cdk4/6 inhibition is expected to block G1/S progression; however, subsequent studies have pointed out that its antitumor effects are not simply due to cell cycle arrest. Mitogens not only promote cell cycle progression but also activate the mTOR pathway to drive cell growth by enhancing the synthesis of proteins and lipids [17]. Cancer cells with hyperactive mitogenic signaling therefore continue to grow and increase the cellular size even after the inhibition of Cdk4/6. Excessive cell enlargement, which occurs specifically in cancer cells, disrupts multiple physiological processes and accumulates DNA damage, ultimately triggering irreversible withdrawal from the cell cycle, namely cellular senescence [18]. Thus, Cdk4/6 inhibitors suppress tumor growth not only by inducting G1 arrest, but also by triggering cellular senescence [17, 18]. These findings demonstrate how cancer‐specific dependencies on Cdk4/6‐cyclin D‐E2F can be exploited for therapeutic purposes, and how targeting G1/S transition can selectively suppress tumor proliferation.

Cdk4/6 inhibitors demonstrate the feasibility of targeting cell cycle drivers in cancers. However, acquired resistance remains a major challenge, particularly with RB loss, cyclin E amplification, or compensatory activation of Cdk2. Combination strategies targeting both cell cycle and growth signaling, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, immune checkpoint, or DNA damage response (DDR) have been explored to enhance efficacy [19].

Targeting Cdk7 to Exploit Senescence Response

As described above, senescence caused by persistent growth signaling can be therapeutically exploited in combination with cell cycle‐targeting strategies. In this context, Cdk7 inhibition is a promising approach, given that Cdk7 phosphorylates and activates multiple Cdks, including Cdk1, Cdk2, and Cdk4/6, as a part of Cdk‐activating kinase [20], thereby promoting cell cycle transitions. In addition, Cdk7 phosphorylates the carboxyl‐terminal domain of RNA polymerase II, at Ser5/Ser7, facilitating transcription initiation. Importantly, recent studies show that persistent oncogenic growth signaling activates a senescence program in cancer cells [10]. However, these cells can evade senescence by Cdk7‐dependent transcriptional programs that sustain oncogenic signaling and proliferation. Thus, Cdk7 inhibition not only prevents cell cycle progression but also collapses transcriptional programs and promotes senescence. Cancer cells are highly dependent on Cdk7 activity [6], and indeed, a CRISPR screen identifies mTOR signaling as a key determinant of Cdk7 sensitivity [5]. In preclinical studies, Cdk7 inhibitors such as THZ1 have demonstrated potent anti‐cancer effects in triple‐negative breast cancer and small cell lung cancer models [4, 5, 6]. Furthermore, another Cdk7 inhibitor, samuraciclib, has been tested in a phase I clinical trial involving patients with triple‐negative breast cancer and HR+/HER2‐negative breast cancer [20]. These findings indicate that Cdk7 integrates oncogenic growth signaling with both cell cycle progression and transcriptional programs, creating a therapeutic vulnerability in cancer.

Targeting DDR Dependencies and Synthetic Lethality

Cancer cells are frequently exposed to elevated levels of DNA damage due to oncogene‐induced replication stress and defective cell cycle checkpoints. To cope with these conditions, they become highly dependent on DNA damage response (DDR) pathways and thus become sensitive to intervening those responses. One of the successful targets is PARP1, which plays a key role in the repair of single‐strand DNA breaks through the base‐excision repair (BER) pathway [7]. Inhibition of PARP leads to accumulation of unrepaired single‐strand breaks and trapped PARP‐DNA complex [21]. During DNA replication, these lesions can induce replication fork collapse and generate double‐strand breaks, thereby increasing the dependence on BRCA1/2‐mediated homologous recombination (HR) repair. In BRCA1/2‐mutant or HR‐deficient cancer cells, PARP inhibition therefore results in synthetic lethality by disrupting compensatory DNA repair mechanisms [22, 23].

In addition to this classical synthetic lethality framework, recent studies have revealed alternative strategies in which DDR‐targeting agents can be used to exacerbate replication stress. For example, loss of SMARCA4, an ATPase subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin‐remodeling complex, is frequently observed in lung adenocarcinoma and is a hallmark of small‐cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type. This loss is associated with altered chromatin states that significantly impact DNA replication dynamics. In this context, PARP inhibition has been shown to increase heterochromatin formation and chromatin compaction, thereby impeding replication fork progression and enhancing DNA replication stress [24]. As a consequence, SMARCA4‐deficient cells become highly dependent on ATR signaling, which stabilizes stalled replication forks and promotes the recovery of replication‐associated DNA damage [9, 10]. This reliance renders these cells particularly vulnerable to ATR inhibition. Thus, PARP inhibitors not only impair DNA repair but also cause chromatin‐associated replication stress, thereby showing DDR‐dependent vulnerabilities that can be therapeutically exploited.

Forcing the Cell Cycle Progression Under Excessive Replication Stress

Since DNA replication machinery is the basic function for cell proliferation, attempts to target DNA replication machinery have been conducted [25]. An alternative strategy is to target replication stress accumulated in cancer cells. These cells are labile to catastrophic failure in progressing through the cell cycle with replication stress‐induced DNA damage.

DNA damage arising from replication stress is sensed by ATM and ATR kinases and their downstream effectors, Chk1 and Chk2. These checkpoint kinases halt the cell cycle progression to allow additional time for repair of DNA double strand breaks and recovery of stalled replication forks [9, 10] (Figure 3). The cell cycle arrest depends on the Chk1/Chk2‐mediated inhibition of Cdc25 phosphatases and activation of Wee1, which suppress Cdk1 activity until DNA damage is repaired. In parallel, ATM activates p53 and subsequently induces p21 expression, inhibiting S phase entry.

DNA damage checkpoint signaling in normal and cancer cells. In normal cells, ATM/Chk2 kinases induce p53‐dependent responses to arrest cells in G1 until DNA damage is completely repaired. In cancer cells, loss of ATM or p53 function causes forced S phase progression with unrepaired DNA damage, promoting replication stress and increased dependence on ATR‐Chk1‐Wee1 signaling. Therefore, inhibition of ATR, Chk1/2 and Wee1 represents promising therapeutic strategies, as these inhibitors induce premature mitotic entry through Cdk1 hyperactivation, resulting in mitotic catastrophe.

In many cancers, these pathways are defective due to multiple oncogenic alterations, resulting in persistent replication stress. For example, loss of ATM or p53 function disrupts the G1/S checkpoint, which normally prevents cells with damaged DNA from entering S phase. In addition, activation of oncogenes such as Myc induces replication stress by promoting premature S phase entry despite the presence of unrepaired DNA damage [26, 27]. Cancer cells therefore heavily rely on ATR/Chk1/Wee1 signaling as compensatory pathways to repair DNA damage before mitotic entry [28, 29, 30].

Inhibition of ATR, Chk1, or Wee1 enforces cells into premature mitosis in the presence of DNA damage, resulting in mitotic catastrophe. From a therapeutic perspective, inactivation of these checkpoint‐mediated compensatory pathways provides a mechanistic basis for exploiting replication stress‐associated vulnerabilities in cancer. Several checkpoint kinase inhibitors have entered clinical trials in combination with agents that exacerbate replication stress, such as Gemcitabine [31].

Drawback of CCNE1 Overexpressed Cancer

Reliance on checkpoint signaling is particularly pronounced in cancers with intrinsically elevated replication stress, such as those with cyclin E (CCNE1) amplification. Amplification of CCNE1 is widely detected in ovarian, uterine, esophageal cancers [32, 33] and often occurs mutually exclusive with other driver alterations, suggesting its pathological significance. Overexpression of cyclin E increases Cdk2 activity and accelerates S phase progression, thereby increasing replication stress and incomplete DNA replication. The development of specific Cdk2 inhibitors is underway [34], however, cells possess a robust backup system that instantly recovers Cdk2 activity after its inhibition [30]. Following Cdk2 inhibition, Cdk4/6‐mediated phosphorylation of RB activates E2F, which induces cyclin A expression and thereby activates formation of Cdk2‐cyclin A, overcoming the effect of Cdk2 inhibitors. The high adaptability in Cdk2 inhibition reflects the robustness of cell cycle regulation. This suggests that directly targeting the core cell cycle enzymes is often challenging, and that therapeutic strategies should instead focus on trade‐off vulnerabilities in cancer.

A comprehensive search of synthetic lethality in cyclin E‐overexpressing cancer cells identified Myt1, a kinase that phosphorylates and inhibits Cdk1 (Figure 1) [35]. Inhibition of Myt1 leads to a premature activation of Cdk1 and triggers mitotic entry before the completion of DNA replication. This causes lethal damage in cancer cells with high Cdk2 activity (Figure 4) [36, 37]. This strategy exploits the overdriven engine to disrupt the coordination between DNA replication and mitosis. Similarly, an inhibitor of Wee1, which is a kinase that also inhibits Cdk1, is effective in CCNE‐amplified cancers and is currently in clinical trials [28, 29, 38, 39].

Vulnerability in CCNE‐overexpressed cancers. In CCNE‐overexpressed cancers, elevated Cdk2 activity promotes premature S phase entry and increases replication stress. Cdk2 inhibitors have been explored, but they are clinically limited due to compensation by Cdk4/6 activity. Under replication stress conditions, cells highly depend on Myt1 and Wee1 kinases to be arrested in G2 until DNA replication is completed. Inhibition of Myt1 or Wee1 leads to Cdk1 hyperactivation, resulting in catastrophic DNA damage and cancer‐specific cell death.

Perturbed Mitotic Progression in Cancer

Recent studies have identified the role of p53 in monitoring M phase progression, in addition to its function in DNA damage response during interphase [40]. When M phase is prolonged, accumulated p53 forms a timer complex with a scaffold protein 53BP1 and the deubiquitinase USP28, which stabilizes p53 [41]. This timer complex induces the transcription of Cdk inhibitors such as p16 and p21 to prevent cells from entering the subsequent G1 phase. The so‐called stopwatch function of p53 represents another critical role of the guardian of the genome in maintaining chromosomal stability [42].

In mitosis, chromosomes align at the metaphase plate and are separated into two daughter cells through universally conserved and ordered mechanisms: kinetochore‐microtubule attachment, silencing of SAC, activation of the anaphase‐promoting complex (APC/C), degradation of securin, activation of separase, and cleavage of cohesion (Figure 5). Separase remains inactive during metaphase and is rapidly activated shortly before anaphase begins and simultaneously across the entire chromosomes [43]. This rapid activation ensures sufficient levels of protease activity to cleave all the cohesin complexes engaging sister chromatids and facilitate chromosome segregation. In many types of cancer cells, silencing of SAC is delayed for various reasons [44]. Significantly, during this sustained SAC signaling, separase becomes prematurely activated, leading to incomplete cleavage of cohesin, chromosome bridges, and genomic instability. These findings suggest that separase undergoes a switch‐like activation and ensures the simultaneous separation of sister chromatids with spindle pole movement during chromosome segregation. Importantly, defects in mitotic surveillance, such as p53 loss or dysregulation of switch mechanisms, seem to create vulnerabilities in cancer cells with delayed SAC silencing and incomplete separase activation. Thus, besides engine and brake, a switch mechanism also ensures proper control of cell proliferation, and these three mechanisms can be a target to intervene in the mitosis of cancer cells.

Regulation of M‐phase progression and the mitotic timer complex. Chromosome alignment and kinetochore‐microtubule attachment are regulated by Aurora B, Plk1, and Kif18A. Unattached kinetochores activate the SAC, which inhibits APC/C and prevents anaphase onset. Proper KT‐MT attachment silences the SAC, allowing APC/C activation, securin degradation, separase activation, and cohesin cleavage to promote sister chromatid separation. During prolonged mitosis, p53 forms a timer complex and induces transcription of CDK inhibitors, p21 and p16, suppressing Cdk1 activity and preventing further cell cycle progression. In cancer cells, weakness of this safeguard causes premature mitotic exit, resulting in chromosome mis‐segregation and genomic instability.

Targeting the Kinetochore‐Microtubule Attachment in Mitosis

The kinetochore‐microtubule attachment is primarily regulated by two mitotic kinases, Aurora B and Polo‐like kinase 1 (Plk1) (Figure 5) [11, 12, 45]. Aurora B destabilizes microtubule attachments and corrects attachment errors by phosphorylating kinetochore substrates such as Hec1. As opposed to Aurora B, Plk1 promotes the stabilization of the attachments by phosphorylating BUBR1, creating the binding site for protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) at the kinetochore that counteracts Aurora B‐mediated phosphorylation [46]. Plk1 and Aurora B are frequently overexpressed in many types of cancers, underscoring their potential as therapeutic targets [47, 48, 49, 50]. However, Aurora kinase inhibitors, e.g., ZM447439, AZD1152, and Hesperadin, and Plk1 inhibitors, e.g., BI‐2536, Volasertib, and Onvansertib, have been limited in clinical use due to severe adverse effects. This is because these two mitotic kinases are physiologically essential for normal cells [51, 52, 53], highlighting the need for alternative strategies to selectively target mitotic vulnerabilities in cancer cells. However, the molecular mechanistic basis underlying these mitotic vulnerabilities remains poorly understood. Recently, genetic CRISPR screens have identified cancer‐specific synthetic lethal genes, such as IGF2BP2, in Plk1‐overexpressing cancers [54]. These approaches may reveal vulnerabilities that distinguish cancer cells from normal cells and provide a foundation for drug development targeting M phase vulnerability in cancers.

As discussed above, synthetic lethality is an effective strategy for targeting the cell cycle. Because the cell cycle machinery is remarkably robust, a single intervention often fails to achieve therapeutic benefit. Therefore, combination strategies have been explored; however, these strategies are often limited by toxicity in normal cells. Instead of rational drug combinations, unbiased CRISPR‐based functional approaches have emerged as a useful means to uncover critical dependencies (the graphical abstract is shown in Figure 6). For example, a screen in CENP‐C deficient cells, which lack a key inner kinetochore component, identified Kif18A as a synthetic lethal gene [55]. Kif18A, a kinesin motor protein, stabilizes the kinetochore‐microtubule attachments and suppresses chromosome oscillation [56]. This vulnerability arises from reduced CENP‐E levels, a key motor protein, as Kif18A compensates for impaired CENP‐E activity for chromosome alignment. Consistently, cancer cells with low CENP‐E expression are often highly sensitive to Kif18A inhibition [57]. These observations highlight the importance of uncovering cryptic cancer vulnerabilities. Such experimental approaches will expand the repertoire of synthetic lethal targets and facilitate development of next‐generation cancer therapies.

Experimental approach to identify cancer‐specific vulnerability. Graphical abstract. Conventional combination strategies using multiple pharmacological inhibitors are often toxic to normal cells. Cancer cells rely on compensation mechanisms that buffer the effect of defective genes. To explore these cryptic vulnerabilities in cancers, experimental approaches combining perturbation of specific molecules (e.g., CENP‐C) with CRISPR knockout screening is an effective approach. Targeting these pre‐existing cancer‐specific vulnerabilities can induce selective synthetic lethality in cancer cells.

Conclusion

Since the cell cycle is robustly regulated and essential for normal cell function, it has long been considered a difficult therapeutic target. However, accumulating evidence indicates that cell cycle dysregulation not only drives tumorigenesis but also creates cancer‐specific vulnerabilities. While normal cells maintain cell cycle fidelity through multiple regulatory mechanisms, cancer cells depend on a limited set of compensatory pathways under oncogenic stress. Identifying these dependencies provides a basis to selectively target cancer cells from normal cells.

Cancer cells develop unique vulnerabilities at each cell cycle stage. At the G1/S transition, cancer cells rely on persistent growth signaling to progress cell cycle. Targeting this dependency can enforce cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence, effectively suppressing the proliferative engine. In contrast, during S phase, cancer cells depend on the replication stress responses for survival, creating vulnerability to checkpoint perturbation, a strategy that does not halt the cell cycle engine but instead forces premature mitosis and lethal genomic instability. Importantly, cancer vulnerability often arises not from the driver mutations per se, but from the compensatory pathways that buffer the effects of driver defect.

Mitotic progression similarly depends on surveillance systems that ensure chromosome segregation, revealing opportunities for synthetic lethal targeting. Advances in CRISPR‐based approaches have identified such dependencies and positioned synthetic lethality as a powerful strategy to exploit the “fragile robustness” of cancer cell cycle networks. Deeper understandings of how cancer cells adapt to and depend on compensatory mechanisms will advance the development of more selective and effective treatments targeting their feet of clay.
