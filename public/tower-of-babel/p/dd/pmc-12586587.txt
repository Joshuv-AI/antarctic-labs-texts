Reconstructing hourly coastal total sea levels and assessing current and future extreme sea levels threats to the Coast of China
Authors: Pengzhen Liu, Ami Hassan Md Din, Mohammad Hanif Hamden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate-driven sea level rise (SLR) will intensify extreme sea level (ESL) events along China’s coast. This study reconstructs continuous hourly total sea level (TSL) by incorporating SLA, tide, storm surge, and wave components, addressing the sparse coverage of tide gauges and their often overlooked extreme events (waves). Using a peak-over-threshold method (thresholds optimised between the 97.00-99.99th percentiles) and a 3-day declustering interval, extreme samples were fitted with generalised Pareto distributions via maximum likelihood estimation. Present-day 1-year return levels exceed 1.6 m at most stations, while centennial return levels surpass 4.1 m at Lusi and Kanmen. Spatial variability is evident, with the East China Sea exhibiting higher extremes (~ 3.9–4.7 m for 100-year events) compared to the Yellow Sea and Bohai Sea (~ 2.7–3.5 m) and the South China Sea (~ 1.7–3.7 m). Under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, centennial return levels rise by 0.83 m by 2100, shifting 100-year events to below 50-year return periods by mid-century and less than 10 years by 2100. This study highlights the urgent need for regional adaptation strategies due to the rising intensity and frequency of extreme events, offering an approach that can be applied to other coastal systems to assess current and future ESL hazards under climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22493-2.Keywords: Reconstructed total sea level, Sea level rise, Extreme value analysis, Extreme sea level, Climate change, Coastal riskSubject terms: Climate-change impacts, Physical oceanography, Natural hazards

Introduction

Climate change is accelerating the global sea level rise (SLR)1,2. According to the IPCC’s sixth assessment report (AR6), the global mean sea level (GMSL) rise trend increased from 1.3 ± 0.7 mm/year from 1901 to 1971 to 3.7 ± 0.5 mm/year between 2006 and 20181. Future SLR will continue for thousands of years, with the rate and extent determined by future greenhouse-gas emissions and the response of polar ice sheets3,4. Natural and anthropogenic subsidence, along with climate-induced global SLR, are collectively altering the relative sea level near world coastlines5. While SLR is important for the coastal risk, the extreme sea level (ESL) resulting from various derived factors (tides, storm surges, and waves) may lead to a more severe risk of vulnerable coastlines6,7. By the end of the century, global “hotspots” for intermittent flooding caused by extreme sea levels are expected to be located primarily in northwest Europe and Asia, with 68% of flooding resulting from tidal and storm surges and 32% due to local SLR8.

Furthermore, SLR further shortens the ESL return period and increases its probability, exacerbating the risk of coastal hazards9–12. By 2100, the current once-in-a-century ESL events may become annual events in many parts of the Earth, even under the lowest SLR scenario13. Therefore, to cope with the impacts of climate change, a better understanding of current and future extreme events in the local areas is needed to plan adaptation measures.

Several studies on the changes in ESL have been conducted by analysing hourly tide gauge observations14,15; however, the limited records and insufficient data completeness lead to large uncertainties in the estimates16,17. To analyse extreme events caused by local factors such as SLR, tides, storm surges, and waves, it is essential to quantify the total sea level (TSL). The linear summation method of defining the TSL as tides, storm surges, and SLR has proven successful7,13,18–20, as the resulting TSL is highly correlated with hourly tide gauges and can reproduce extreme events that are overlooked by tide gauge records21,22. This integrated framework ensures that the potential effects of climate change on various coastal sea level components are correctly evaluated and that appropriate strategies are implemented to mitigate risks and cope with evolving coastal conditions. Although this approach neglects nonlinear interactions between various factors, for example, both storm surges and waves are affected by tides, comparisons with tide gauge observations indicate that such interactions do not notably influence the results8. However, studies based on tide gauge records also indicate that significant but spatially inhomogeneous tide-wave interactions exist along the coast of China14. Furthermore, because waves significantly increase the probability of ESL events and tide gauges are often located in sheltered harbors and can only partially capture wave information20,22, they should be taken into account when projecting regional studies of coastal sea level change23.

Extreme value theory is often employed to determine the magnitude or probability of extreme events24,25, especially in the fields of oceanography, meteorology, and hydrology, and is therefore also applicable to ESL events11,13,26. Both the determination of the extreme samples and the choice of probability distribution will introduce uncertainty and affect the final ESL estimation27. The block maxima (BM) and peak over threshold (POT) have been extensively utilised in extreme value analysis because of their relatively effortless sampling process13,28. The BM method selects a single maximum value based on each equally spaced period, which may ignore other crucial rare events that take place during that period, or the maximum value in this period may be smaller than the low values in other periods19,29. The POT approach considers all values above a certain threshold and is thought to be more advantageous in fitting natural phenomena25,28–30. The generalised extreme value (GEV) and generalised pareto distribution (GPD) are commonly employed limiting models in extreme value analysis13,25,29. Under the BM method, the GEV distribution provides an asymptotically justified representation of sample maxima, whereas the POT approach employs the GPD to characterise exceedances over the threshold19,29. The POT method, which employs the GPD, is preferred in practice because of its superior data utilisation efficiency, making it more accurate, especially when extreme observations are scarce13,25.

The POT method isolates independent exceedances from the continuous sea level record to construct the sample. Threshold selection is critical for sample adequacy and model stability: a low threshold admits excessive nonextreme data, whereas a high threshold yields too few exceedances and destabilises GPD parameter estimates. Common threshold selection techniques include the parametric procedure, nonparametric normality-based hypothesis testing, the K-S statistics, and percentiles, among which the percentile strategy is more widely utilised29. Through optimisation testing, the 99.7th percentile of hourly sea level observations is suggested as an optimal threshold29. Alternatively, when the POT approach was applied to the continuous sea-level record, ESL events were defined as the highest 2% of all observations8,20,22. Additionally, some studies advocate choosing the lowest percentile threshold at which GPD parameter estimates and extreme forecasts stabilise, ensuring consistent and reliable19,28.

In POT resamples, temporal declustering is routinely applied to mitigate serial dependence by retaining only the single largest exceedance in each cluster19,29. Clusters are typically defined by enforcing a minimum exceedance gap, after which declustering ensures that only the dominant extreme event is preserved22. The POT approaches are highly sensitive to the chosen exceedance interval: intervals shorter than 24 h tend to inflate the calculated return levels19,29. On the basis of sensitivity assessments, an exceedance interval of 25 h was recommended as a pragmatic balance between ensuring storm surge event independence and maintaining a robust sample size19. Most studies use a declustering interval of 3 days (72 h), which is the approximate time period during which most storm surge events affect coastal sea levels13,20,22,27. Other studies use a 72-hour wave parameter and a 25-hour storm surge parameter, then perform a joint probability analysis (JPM) to determine the combined ESL return period13.

In statistical modeling, a primary objective is to infer the underlying population distribution from an independent sample29. Under the assumption of sample independence, the unknown statistical parameters of the population can be estimated. Several parameter estimation methods are available, including maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), linear combinations of order statistics (L-moments), least squares, and Bayesian methods19,27. MLE is a suitable approach for estimating unknown parameters of a distribution to fit the GPD to determine the predicted extreme values of the return period19,29,31. GPD fit performance was evaluated by correlating the empirical and model exceedance statistics32.

Under future SLR scenarios, coastal flood hazards driven by ESL will intensify3,4, and a comprehensive assessment of current and future ESL is essential for appropriate policies and planning. This study utilised multi-mission absolute sea level measurements extracted from the radar altimeter database system (RADS) and processed them through a series of optimisation procedures to derive coastal sea level anomalies along China’s coastline. These anomalies were then combined with tide, storm surge, and wave contributions to generate an hourly TSL time series, which was rigorously validated against tide gauge records. Subsequently, optimized extreme value analysis is applied to quantify the intensity and frequency of both present and projected future ESL events. While this methodology offers a comprehensive and practical framework, its limitations must be acknowledged. For example, neglecting nonlinear interactions and land subsidence, as well as potential changes in storm surges, tides, and tropical cyclone behaviour in future projections, may lead to uncertainties in extreme value estimates. These limitations highlight areas for improvement in future iterations of the framework. Nevertheless, the methodology provides valuable insights and a flexible, modular approach that can be applied to other coastal regions worldwide, forming a solid foundation for developing strategies to mitigate coastal flood risks under changing climate conditions.

Materials and methods

Study area and materials

The Chinese coastline, comprising the Bohai Sea, Yellow Sea, East China Sea and South China Sea, extends over 18,000 km and spans approximately 32° longitude (east-west) and 44° latitude (north-south) (Fig. 1a). China’s rapid economic development, especially in its coastal zones, has made people more concerned about the negative implications of ESL events. Between 1989 and 2014, the average annual economic loss caused by extreme sea level disasters was RMB 11.7 billion, 156 people died, and 13.4 million people were affected14. Advancing our understanding of ESL events along China’s coastline is essential for informing coastal defense strategies, guiding future development planning and safeguarding vulnerable coastal ecosystems.

This study first derives Sea Level Anomalies (SLA) with high precision by subjecting multi-mission altimeter measurements from the Radar Altimeter Database System (RADS)33 to a series of optimisation processes. Beginning with the TOPEX/Poseidon satellite in 1992 and continuing with the successive launches of its successors, the Jason-1, Jason-2, Jason-3 and Sentinel-6 A satellites, the TOPEX series of missions has provided uninterrupted high-precision records of global sea surface heights, with the radial accuracy reaching the centimetre and even sub-centimetre level34. The European Space Agency (ESA)’s ERS-1/2, Envisat, CryoSat-2 and Sentinel-3 A satellites, and Geosat Follow-On (GFO), SARAL satellites provide more intensive ground track sampling35. This study improves the temporal and spatial resolution of SLA measurements by fusing these complementary datasets to obtain sea level anomalies along the coast of China (15°−45°N, 105°−135°E) from 1993 to 2023, as one of the components of the subsequent TSL construction. This study utilised the altimeter missions described in Fig. 1b.

Tide gauge station records serve as a reference for assessing the accuracy of altimeter-derived SLA and reconstructed TSL time series. Monthly tide gauge observations were obtained from Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level (PSMSL)36 and are referenced to the Revised Local Reference (RLR), which is located around 7000 mm below average sea level37. Twelve tide gauge stations whose monthly records overlapped with the 1993–2023 altimetry period for at least 10 years were selected to ensure reliable validation of satellite-derived SLAs. Available hourly tide gauge observations for the 12 sites were retrieved from the University of Hawaii Sea Level Centre (UHSLC) dataset38 for direct comparison with the constructed TSL time series. Owing to data availability, the locations of monthly and hourly tide gauge stations are not completely consistent. The spatial distributions of all the tide gauges are shown in Fig. 1a, and their data availability is shown in Fig. 1c, indicating that the available hourly tide gauge data are very limited, which also illustrates the necessity of this study.

SLA derivation from RADS multi-mission altimetry

Altimeter correction and elimination of biases have largely been addressed in RADS, such as atmospheric and geophysical corrections, instrument design, calibration and validation, reference systems, and precise orbit determination, etc. However, optimisation processing is still needed to seamlessly combine data from different satellite platforms. Following RADS processing, all missions are required to have their Sea Surface Heights (SSHs) unified to a “standard surface” to remove residual orbital and frame-system discrepancies39,40. Therefore, the ERS-class satellites were adjusted by making dual-crossover minimisation estimates with reference to the TOPEX-class (TOPEX/Poseidon, Jason-1/2/3, Sentinel-6 A), which are characterised by centimetre-level radial orbit accuracy and uninterrupted observations40–42 (see Fig. 1b for the classification of TOPEX and ERS). Subsequently, ERS‐class and TOPEX‐class observations were merged into a unified dataset. Daily altimeter measurements were then filtered via optimal parameters (sigma 2.0, block size of 0.25°, and cut-off of ± 9 days) and interpolated onto 0.25°×0.25° daily SLA grids40,43,44. Finally, the daily SLAs were aggregated into monthly averages to ensure consistency for validation with the tide gauge data.

Construction of the hourly total sea level series

To assess the ESL on China’s coast in the 21 st century, three crucial components, altimeter-derived SLA, astronomical tide (T), storm surge (S), and wind and swell wave setup and swash (W), are combined into a continuous total sea level (TSL) time series as follows in Eq. (1).

SLA in this study is driven by multi-mission altimeter data, and due to the lack of accurate estimates for land subsidence rates, its potential intensification of SLR has not been included. T is predicted with the Python package pyTMD45 from the FES2014 tide atlas (1/16° grid)46. At each location, we reconstruct an hourly time series by combining each constituent’s complex harmonic constant (amplitude and phase lag) with the astronomical arguments and the standard nodal corrections. The storm surge (S) is represented with the AVISO + DAC v4.0 product (0.25° global grid, 6-hourly, 1992-present)47. DAC merges a high-frequency (< 20 days) barotropic response from the MOG2D-G model, forced by ECMWF winds and sea-level pressure, with a low-frequency (> 20 days) inverted-barometer (IB) correction that assumes a static pressure response and neglects wind. The 20-day cut-off separates the dynamic, wind/pressure-forced variability (MOG2D-G) from the IB component. Wind and swell wave setup and swash (W) are estimated with the empirical parameterisation formula (2)49, which was derived from field observations across a range of beach conditions49. Sensitivity tests further show that the combined contribution of swell and wind waves is most consistent with this formula22.

This choice also aligns with the global median coastal slope (~ 0.04) derived from a global digital elevation model18. It needs to be emphasised that nonlinear interactions, particularly those related to tidal-surge coupling, were not included in the current reconstruction methodology.

Time series of all the components are extracted for each station on the basis of the hourly tide gauge locations employed in this study. Owing to differences in temporal and spatial sampling, these datasets are linearly interpolated into hourly time series. Hourly TSL time series were then constructed via linear addition, and their reliability was confirmed by the co-located hourly tide gauge records.

Validation of components and TSL time series

Before analysis, all tide gauge records were subjected to quality control to remove outliers and erroneous measurements. To verify the reliability of the satellite-derived SLA, the monthly tide gauge measurements were converted to SLAs. For each tide gauge station, a mean sea level was first established by averaging its long-term monthly sea levels. The monthly SLAs were then generated by subtracting this datum from monthly observations. We validated the model tide (T), reconstructed from FES2014 harmonic constants using pyTMD, by comparing it with harmonic tides obtained from hourly tide gauge observations using UTide53 (with the same constituent set and standard nodal corrections).

To validate the reliability of TSL time series and its components, four complementary statistics were computed for each time-series pair: the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), which quantifies the absolute error in amplitude reproduction (Eq. (4)); the Normalized Standard Deviation (NSD), which assesses the fidelity of amplitude reproduction, i.e., the amplitude relative scaling of satellite SLAs relative to tide gauge SLAs (Eq. (5)); the Normalized Centred Root Mean Square Difference (N-CRMSD), which quantifies the relative amplitude difference and phase (time) mismatch of the waveform shape after removing the mean bias (Eq. (6)); and the Pearson correlation coefficient (R)55, which is employed to analyse the similarity between time series, which can characterise the temporal coherence of satellite SLA relative to tide gauge SLA to a certain extent (Eq. (7)).

Extreme sea level analysis based on TheTSL

The validated TSL construction method is extended to the time series of all 12 stations from January 1993 to December 2023. Extreme value analysis is performed on the hourly TSL time series to derive the intensity and frequency of current and future ESL. The specific method flow is shown in Fig. 2.

Flowchart of the current and future extreme sea level return level estimation based on the POT-GDP method and SLR scenarios.

The choice of threshold range, event interval, and probability distribution all introduce uncertainty, affecting the final extreme sea level (ESL) estimate27. In this study, the POT method was first used to extract ESL samples from the hourly total sea level (TSL) series. To determine the most robust exceedance threshold for each station, candidate thresholds from the 97th to the 99.99th percentile were evaluated in increments of 0.1%. In the POT method, a declustering process (i.e., only the single highest exceedance threshold within a cluster was utilised) was also employed to ensure the independence of the samples. The clustering process stipulated that the event interval was 3 days, and then declustering was performed to ensure that only specific events were selected13,20,22,27.

Assuming that the declustered excesses constitute independent realisations of the population, and that the GPD is fitted to the sample via MLE. Then, the current ESL with return periods of 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 75, and 100 years is directly derived on the basis of the estimated GPD parameters. The stability and consistency of the predicted extreme values were identified by the breakpoint method (the total residual error reached a local minimum), and the lowest RMSE ensured the best fit, thereby determining the optimal percentile for each station. Finally, the best fit under the optimal extreme value sample is achieved to obtain a stable and consistent current extreme sea level.

To predict future ESL under SLR scenarios, the current return levels were enhanced via the future SLR scenario extract of the NASA AR6 Sea Level Prediction55–57. The SLR projections of 12 tide gauge stations under 5 shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5) for 3 target years of 2030, 2050 and 2100 were retrieved (Supplementary Table S1) and added to the corresponding current most extreme sea level (100-year return level) to investigate future ESL scenarios.

Results and discussion

Sea level components verification

To ensure reliability, a comprehensive comparison was made between the altimeter-derived SLA and the tide gauge SLA. First, based on the two superimposed SLA time series for each site (Supplementary Fig. S1), it was preliminarily judged that the two curves overlapped closely, with the peaks and valleys basically coinciding at the maximum and minimum values, and the phases and amplitudes were highly consistent.

On the basis of the boxplots and Taylor plots, the SLA performance of the altimeters at the 12 coastal stations is evaluated by comparing them with tide gauge observations. The box plot in Fig. 3a shows that the satellite altimeter-derived SLA performs well overall: a high correlation coefficient (R > 0.60), low RMSE (< 0.12 m) and near-ideal NSD (≈ 1.01), proving that the SLA obtained by the altimeter can reliably reproduce the phase and amplitude of sea level changes. Among them, the RMSEs of the 12 tide gauges are concentrated from 0.0638 to 0.1154 m (median ≈ 0.0956 m, mean ≈ 0.0941, the Interquartile Range (IQR) ≈ 0.0823–0.1070) (Fig. 3a), reflecting that the satellite altimeter has a small absolute error in reproducing the amplitude of sea level anomalies. NSD fluctuates in the range of 0.5528–1.3491 (median ≈ 1.0238, mean ≈ 1.0054, IQR ≈ 0.8841–1.1512) (Fig. 3a), indicating that the amplitude match is high, but some stations have underestimated or overestimated it. R is distributed between 0.6127 and 0.8484 (median ≈ 0.7426, mean ≈ 0.7468, IQR ≈ 0.7032–0.7959) (Fig. 3a), indicating that the time series consistency of most stations is good. N-CRMSD < 0.5 is considered a high-quality fit58, whereas the average N-CRMSD of 0.75 in this study is significantly higher, indicating that there are deviations in short-term detailed fluctuations and phases. However, combined with the comprehensive analysis of the other three indicators, the large-scale trend and amplitude can still be captured, which can meet the requirements of this study, that is, adding a long-term tendency to the construction of the TSL time series.

Hourly total sea level construction and verification

In this study, the time series of each component (tide (T), storm surge (S), SLA, and wave (W)) at the locations of the 12 tide gauges were extracted, and each component was resampled to an hourly resolution via linear interpolation. These interpolated components were subsequently synthesised into the TSL time series for each station by linear superposition. To verify the reliability of the reconstruction method, the total sea level series constructed by three combinations of T + S, T + S + SLA, and full component superposition (T + S + SLA + W) were compared with the original hourly tide gauge observation series. Overall, the three combined sea level time series and original tidal observation series are relatively consistent in amplitude and phase (Supplementary Fig. S3), which requires further statistical analysis for verification.

As shown in Fig. 5, a multi-dimensional comparison of the TSLs reconstructed via the three methods and the original tide level observations was performed at 12 stations. The RMSE of the T + S + SLA scheme is as follows: mean ≈ 0.1886 m (T + S ≈ 0.1992 m, T + S + SLA + W ≈ 0.2453 m), with the lowest absolute error (Fig. 5a). The median and mean of the NSD of the T + S scheme are close to 1 (Fig. 5a), which can accurately reproduce the high-frequency fluctuations in the storm surge but slightly underestimate the overall amplitude. Compared with those of the T + S scheme, the median and mean of the T + S + SLA scheme are closer to the ideal value of 1 (Fig. 5a), reflecting the long-term trend that SLR improves amplitude matching. However, the mean NSD of the full-component scheme is significantly greater than 1, and the IQR is somewhat stretched (Fig. 5a), which shows the limitations of tide gauges in monitoring waves, as they are usually located in closed ports.

Red diamonds indicate means.(b) Taylor diagram of the three TSL series showing statistical metrics relative to the ideal point (red star): RMSE (color), NSD (radial distance), R (azimuthal angle), and N-CRMSD (semicircle).

Regarding detailed fitting, the median and mean of the N-CRMSD of T + S and T + S + SLA schemes are < 0.3 (Fig. 5a), which is considered a high-quality fit58. Moreover, the T + S + SLA scheme outperformed the T + S scheme, with an N-CRMSD mean of ≈ 0.2722 (T + S ≈ 0.2909) (Fig. 5a), and the waveform difference was minimal. However, the median and mean of the N-CRMSD of the scheme with the wave component added reached 0.2743 and 0.4080, respectively, and the IQR was significantly extended (Fig. 5a), indicating that the wave component amplified the waveform difference from the tide gauge station record. In terms of time series consistency, the T + S + SLA scheme has a slightly better R performance than the T + S scheme (the mean and median are closer to 1, and the IQR is slightly shorter) (Fig. 5a), so its overall time series correlation is the highest. After adding waves, the R mean dropped from 0.9571 of T + S + SLA to 0.9105 (Fig. 5a). In summary, the T + S + SLA linear superposition strategy (mean values: RMSE ≈ 0.1886 m, NSD ≈ 0.9584, N-CRMSD ≈ 0.2722, R ≈ 0.9571) achieves the best fit with the original tidal record in amplitude, shape, and phase, validating its reliability in hourly TSL reconstruction (excluding waves). However, it also highlights the limitations of tide gauges in capturing wave components and underscores the importance of including wave contributions in reconstructions.

Figure 5b of the Taylor diagram integrates the metrics of the three reconstructed series for each station into one view. The mean of the three schemes shows that the T + S + SLA is more consistent with the tide gauge records (closer to the five-pointed star ideal point) (Fig. 5b). Some stations, such as Keelung, Kaohsiung, and Shanwei (stations within the red ellipse in Fig. 5b), have large absolute errors and significantly overestimate the amplitude matching (NSD > 1.25), which is consistent with previous studies and is attributed to the fact that tide gauge stations are usually located in sheltered environments20. However, most stations are clustered in the ranges of NSD (0.75–1.10) and R (0.85–0.99), closely around the mean point (Fig. 5b). Overall, while the linear superposition scheme of tide, storm surge, and SLA is highly correlated with tide gauge records, this study neglects nonlinear interactions, particularly in regions like the Chinese coastline with a large tidal range14,59,60, which introduces uncertainty in the validation and subsequent extreme sea-level analysis. This limitation, alongside the constraints of tide gauge measurements in capturing wave components, emphasizes the need to consider wave effects in subsequent reconstructions.

Identifying extreme sea level samples

In this study, the TSL time series (T + S + SLA + W) reconstruction scheme is extended to all 12 stations from January 1993 to December 2023 for extreme value analysis. The POT method was used to extract samples of the TSL time series, and independence was guaranteed by a 3-day declustering interval. To determine the optimal threshold, the 97.0% to 99.99% percentiles of exceedances were evaluated in increments of 0.1% to ensure the robustness and parameter stability of the subsequent GPD fit. For each station, the threshold was determined, the maximum exceedance of each cluster was retained, and the GPD was fitted. Then, on the basis of the fitted GPD parameters, the extreme levels for return periods of 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 75, and 100 years were derived. The model performance is quantified by the RMSE between the empirical and theoretical exceedance distributions.

Supplementary Fig. S4 the fitted return level curves for the 1-, 5-, 10-, 20-, 50-, 75- and 100-year return periods, each computed over a range of exceedance percentiles. For each return level curve, three characteristic “breakpoints” were detected (Supplementary Fig. S4), at which the total residual error reached a local minimum. Inspection of the curves reveals that the slope is relatively flat before the second breakpoint but then rises sharply (Supplementary Fig. S4). Furthermore, at each station, RMSE as a function of exceedance percentile first declines and then rises, with the majority of minima occurring before the second breakpoint (Supplementary Fig. S4). To ensure the consistency and robustness of extreme value predictions, the percentile corresponding to the minimum RMSE before the second breakpoint is used to determine the threshold. The vertical dashed line represents the smallest second breakpoint among all seven curves, and the percentile corresponding to the best RMSE to the left of the line is then selected as the threshold for that station (the red pentagonal star position) (Supplementary Fig. S4).

The selected thresholds ranged from 97.7% to 99.1% (mean = 98.6%) (Table 1), and using these percentiles and a declustering interval of 3 days, extreme value samples for each station were constructed. Calculating the return period extreme sea level

Using the previously determined optimal percentile threshold and a declustering interval of 3 days, extreme samples were extracted for each TSL time series. The GPD shape and scale parameters were then estimated via MLE to obtain the fitted distribution. Finally, the extreme values of the return periods for 12 tide gauge stations along the China coast were obtained (Supplementary Fig. S5). The confidence intervals of Keelung and Shanwei are significantly wider (Supplementary Fig. S5g, h), which is consistent with their smaller number of extreme values (Table 1). Stations with more exceedances have narrower intervals, which highlights the impact of sample size on uncertainty. At the 95% confidence level, the 100-year return period water level exhibits an average uncertainty of ± 0.29 m. While excluding Keelung and Shanwei stations reduces this to ± 0.21 m, suggesting that the overall uncertainty remains within a reasonable and acceptable threshold, supporting the robustness of the regional estimate.

Comparing the return levels based on tide gauge records (from three stations with over 20 years of data) and the reconstructed total sea level (T + S + SLA + W) reveals significant differences, which are largely attributed to the correlation between the time series. At the Hong Kong site, the correlation coefficient (R) between the tide gauge records and the T + S + SLA + W series remained 0.91 after incorporating wave effects (W), and the difference in the 100-year return water level was relatively small (0.1281 m) (Supplementary Fig. S6, Table S2). However, at Kaohsiung and Keelung, the R values decreased significantly (0.8148 and 0.6335, respectively), with the 100-year return water level differences reaching 1.6244 m and 2.7884 m, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6, Table S2). These results further highlight the limitations of tide gauges in capturing wave effects, consistent with previous studies that attribute this to tide gauges typically being located in sheltered environments, where they only partially capture the sea level signal from wave setup and have limited ability to detect wave swash.

Additionally, this discrepancy is closely linked to the parameters used in wave calculations. At Hong Kong, the maximum significant wave height and period were 2.65 m and 13.30 s, respectively, while the maximum wave component calculated from the empirical formula was 1.03 m (Supplementary Table S2). At Kaohsiung, the maximum significant wave height and period were 7.38 m and 18.26 s, respectively, while at Keelung, they were 13.85 m and 18.43 s, resulting in calculated maximum wave components of 2.03 m and 3.72 m, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). These variations directly contribute to the differences observed in the reproduced water levels.

Figure 6 presents the distribution of the extreme levels for return periods of 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 75 and 100 years at the 12 stations. In the Yellow and Bohai Seas, the extreme return levels at Laohutan and Shijiusuo exhibit modesty, with 1-year return levels of 1.93 m and 2.53 m that increase to 2.68 m and 3.47 m, respectively, at the 100-year return period (Fig. 6). In contrast, Lusi, Kanmen, and Xiamen stations in the East China Sea experienced more drastic ESL events, with the 1-year return levels of 2.88 m, 3.02 m, and 2.95 m, respectively, and the 100-year return levels rising to 4.13 m, 4.66 m, and 3.95 m, respectively (Fig. 6). This north–south contrast is consistent with prior analyses of Chinese tide-gauge records showing that ESLs are generally larger and more rapidly varying along the southeastern coast, where tropical cyclones dominate surge statistics and tide–surge interaction is widespread61. In particular, Kanmen and Xiamen have documented upward trends in ESL metrics over recent decades, reinforcing their higher present-day hazard levels, while Yellow/Bohai stations tend to be less cyclone-exposed and more tide-dominated14,62.

Distribution of current extreme sea levels at all tide gauges with return periods of 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 75, and 100. The map was generated by QGIS 3.36 (https://qgis.org/).

On the coast of Taiwan, Keelung has higher extreme values than Kaohsiung, with 1-year return levels of 1.66 m and 1.18 m, respectively, and 100-year return levels of 3.74 m and 2.50 m, respectively (Fig. 6). At the South China Sea stations (Shanwei, Hong Kong, and Zhapo), 1-year return levels are 1.65, 1.18, and 1.87 m, respectively, and 100-year return levels are 2.91, 1.79, and 2.92 m, respectively (Fig. 6). Haikou has the lowest return level of all stations (1.06 m for 1 year and 1.73 m for 100 years) (Fig. 6). Notably, Beihai has the highest return level of all South China Sea stations (2.65 m for 1 year and 3.49 m for 100 years) (Fig. 6), highlighting the significant regional variability caused by local water depth and meteorological forcing.

Prediction of future extreme sea level changes

Future return extremes were projected by superimposing the IPCC AR6 regional SLR scenarios onto the current 100-year return levels (Fig. 7) (the results of the assessments superimposed to other current return levels are available in Zenodo database63. The study adopted SLR medium-confidence estimates from five Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5) in 2030, 2050 and 2100 for each of the 12 tide gauge stations (Supplementary Table S1).

Future 100-year return period extremes evolve at each tide gauge under five SSP scenarios for the years 2030 (gray), 2050 (blue) and 2100 (orange). The solid black line marks the current 100-year return level.

Across all 12 stations, future extremes increase monotonically with both time and emission pathways, consistent with long-term tide gauge studies14,64. By 2030, the mean rises range from 0.1070 m (SSP1-1.9) to 0.1059 m (SSP5-8.5) (Fig. 7); by 2050, these increases reach 0.2015 m (SSP1-1.9) and 0.2495 m (SSP5-8.5) (Fig. 7); and by 2100, they are 0.4156 m under low-emission SSP1-1.9 and even reach 0.8320 m under SSP5-8.5 (Fig. 7). The divergence among scenarios is modest at mid-century but becomes pronounced by 2100, reflecting the compounding influence of long-term SLR projections (Fig. 7).

Spatially, the East China Sea stations (Lusi, Kanmen, and Xiamen) exhibit the highest extreme water levels. Projected 100-year return levels for these stations are expected to exceed 4.0 m by 2030 under the SSP1-1.9 scenario, with Lusi and Kanmen projected to surpass 5.0 m by 2100 under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 7). Keelung is also projected to experience a high extreme value, reaching 4.6 m by 2100 under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 7). Within the South China Sea, Beihai is expected to have the highest future extreme water levels, exceeding 4.3 m by 2100 under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 7). In contrast, Hong Kong and Haikou are projected to have the lowest future extreme values, with levels remaining below 2.7 m by 2100, even under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 7). Extreme return water levels in the South China Sea (Kaohsiung, Shanwei, and Zhapo) and Laohutan in the Bohai Sea are expected to be intermediate, exceeding 3.0 m under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 7). Shijiusuo in the Yellow Sea also experiences high extremes due to its proximity to the East China Sea, with a 100-year return water level reaching 4.1 m by 2100 under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 7). While all sites are projected to experience rising extreme water levels, the magnitude and sensitivity of these increases will vary significantly depending on local water depth and storm surge exposure, particularly in the East China Sea, thereby informing region-specific adaptation strategies.

Coastal management and planning that accounts solely for projected increases in ESL magnitude risk underestimates future flood hazards if changes in the frequency of extreme events are neglected9,24. To analyse how the current 100-year return level will evolve under future SLR scenarios, Hong Kong station is taken as an example, and the low-emissions pathway SSP1-1.9 scenario is selected for specific analysis (Supplementary Fig. S7).

At the Hong Kong station, by 2030, the ESL where the current has a 1% annual probability (100-year return period) will occur with a 2% probability (equivalent to a 43-year return period) (Supplementary Fig. S7a, b). By mid-century (2050), this probability increases to 5% (21-year return period) (Supplementary Fig. S7c, d), and by 2100, it approaches 21% (essentially a 5-year occurrence) (Supplementary Fig. S7e, f). Thus, even under the most optimistic emission pathway (SSP1-1.9), SLR amplifies the ESL frequency by more than an order of magnitude over the next century. In summary, SLR will not only increase the intensity of extreme events but also significantly shorten their return period, with profound implications for risk management and infrastructure design.

Figure 8 summarises how the current 100-year return level accelerates to much shorter return periods by 2030, 2050 and 2100 under the five SSP pathways. Even under the most ambitious emission reduction scenario (SSP1-1.9), by 2030, the return periods of all 12 stations will reach 80 years, with Laohutan, Lushi, Hong Kong and Beihai at 50 years, while Haikou will even reach 35 years (Fig. 8). The shortening of the return period under the other higher emission scenarios is basically the same as that under the SSP1-1.9 scenario, indicating the early reclassification of “once-in‐a‐century” events to multi‐decadal hazards by 2030.

Changes in the return period of the current 100-year return level in 2030, 2050, and 2100 under the five SSP pathways.

By 2050, this shortening intensifies. Under all the scenarios, the 100-year return periods are shortened to less than the 50-year return periods at 11 of the 12 stations, with Laohutan, Lushi, Hong Kong, Beihai, and Haikou falling below the 20-year return periods under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 8). Even under low-emissions SSP1-1.9, only Kanmen, Kaohsiung, Keelung, and Shanwei are above 50 years (Fig. 8). The mid-century shift highlights that, regardless of mitigation pathways, coastal extreme sea levels that occurred once in a century will occur once in 50 years at most stations.

Looking ahead to 2100, under the SSP1-1.9 scenario, the 100-year return period will be shortened to once every 10 years for five stations, while under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, it will be for 11 stations (Fig. 8). Previous studies have also predicted that under the RCP 8.5 scenario, ESL will occur almost every decade by 2100, which is generally consistent with the results of this study9. These results demonstrate that rising sea levels not only increase extreme magnitudes but also, more critically, accelerate their frequency, transforming rare events into routine hazards and demanding regionally tailored adaptation strategies.

Conclusions

Coastal regions worldwide face escalating flood hazards due to the complex interplay of SLR, tides, storm surges, and waves over different timescales. This study validated the contributions of SLA and tides using monthly and hourly tide gauge records, respectively. Subsequently, the SLA, tidal, storm surge, and wave components were linearly superimposed to reconstruct hourly TSL time series. The T + S + SLA scheme exhibited the strongest agreement with field measurements, whereas the inclusion of wave effects resulted in a decrease in correlation to varying degrees. This outcome aligns with previous research, which suggests that tide gauges, typically located in well-protected harbors, generally exclude wave setup and swash from their records20. By incorporating all relevant components, this study overcomes the sparse and discontinuous nature of China’s coastal tide gauge network and can better reproduce extreme events that are often overlooked by tide gauge records, thus providing a solid foundation for regional sea level monitoring and extreme value analysis.

However, the TSL scheme based on all components has certain limitations. Firstly, tide gauges measure sea level relative to a nearby datum, and this study does not incorporate the effects of vertical land motion. Additionally, the model neglects the nonlinear interactions between various factors; for example, both storm surges and waves are influenced by tides. Research has indicated significant but spatially inhomogeneous tidal surge-wave interactions along the Chinese coast14. While wave setup and swash were not verified in this study, sensitivity tests suggest that the results are robust22. Nevertheless, beach slope varies both temporally and spatially, and the use of a fixed value in this study introduces uncertainty in wave calculations, warranting further quantitative research to refine these aspects.

A continuous hourly TSL time series from 1993 to 2023 was reconstructed for 12 tide gauge stations based on the linear superposition of SLA, tides, storm surges, and waves. ESL samples were then extracted via the POT approach, thresholds optimised over the 97.0%−99.99% exceedance percentile range at 0.1% increments, and independence enforced via a 3-day declustering interval. The robustness and accuracy of the threshold selection method, as well as the reliability of the fitted extreme value model, were demonstrated by the RMSE (0.0176 m) between the empirical and the GPD-predicted exceedance distributions. The GPD were fitted to the excess values by MLE to obtain 1-, 5-, 10-, 20-, 50-, 75- and 100-year return level estimates. Due to the small number of extreme value samples extracted, Keelung and Shanwei brought certain uncertainty. However, after removing them, the overall uncertainty of the 100-year return level was ± 0.21 m at a 95% confidence level, indicating the robustness of the estimation in this study. However, it is important to acknowledge that the limited number of extreme value samples at certain sites contributes to increased uncertainty in the fitting process.

The distribution of ESLs across various coastal stations shows significant spatial variability, with higher ESLs observed along the southeastern Chinese coast compared to the Yellow and Bohai Seas. In particular, stations in the East China Sea (Lusi, Kanmen, and Xiamen) exhibit more drastic ESL events, with 1-year return levels exceeding 2.8 m and 100-year return levels reaching up to 4.7 m. This is consistent with previous studies indicating that ESLs are generally larger and more variable along the southeastern coast, where tropical cyclones and tide–surge interactions dominate14,61,62. On Taiwan’s coast, Keelung exhibits higher extreme values than Kaohsiung, while the South China Sea stations, such as Beihai, also show notable regional differences. Beihai, in particular, has the highest return levels (1-year: 2.65 m; 100-year: 3.49 m) in the region, reflecting the impact of local bathymetry and meteorological conditions. These findings highlight the complex interplay of local factors influencing coastal flood risks, which should inform targeted adaptation strategies across regions.

Under the IPCC AR6 SLR scenarios, the future extreme sea levels (ESLs) demonstrate a significant increase in both magnitude and frequency across all 12 stations, with pronounced spatial variability. By 2030, extreme water levels in the East China Sea stations, such as Lusi and Kanmen, are expected to exceed 4.0 m, with Lusi and Kanmen surpassing 5.0 m by 2100 under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. Meanwhile, stations in the South China Sea, like Beihai, will also experience high extremes, surpassing 4.3 m by 2100. In contrast, Hong Kong and Haikou will see lower extremes, remaining below 2.7 m by 2100. Additionally, the frequency of extreme events is set to increase significantly. By 2030, even under the most optimistic SSP1-1.9 scenario, the return periods of all 12 stations will shorten to 80 years, with some stations reaching 35 years, indicating an early reclassification of “once-in-a-century” events to multi-decadal hazards. By mid-century, this trend intensifies, with the return periods at most stations shortening to below 50 years, and in some cases to less than 20 years under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. Finally, by 2100, the frequency of extreme events is expected to increase dramatically, with return periods shortening to as little as 10 years at several stations under the SSP1-1.9 scenario and to 5 years under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. However, these projections do not account for potential changes in storm surge, tidal properties, or tropical cyclone behavior, which may be affected by climate change. This omission introduces uncertainty in the future extreme sea level estimates. Future studies should consider these factors to improve the accuracy of projections.

Collectively, the findings demonstrate that rising sea levels will not only elevate the heights of rare ESL events but also dramatically accelerate their recurrence, transforming “100-year return period” extremes into decadal or annual hazards within decades. This highlights the urgent need for spatially differentiated adaptation strategies that are tailored to specific emission pathways. This study offers new insights into the spatial patterns and intensification of ESL risks under sea level rise, emphasising the critical need for region-specific coastal adaptation strategies. Additionally, it provides a methodology that can be applied to other regional coastal systems, offering a framework for assessing and addressing future flood risks.
