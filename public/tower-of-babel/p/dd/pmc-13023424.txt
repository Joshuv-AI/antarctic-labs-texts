Comparative Analysis of Prenatal Stress Models: Placental and Neurodevelopmental Outcomes in Mice
Authors: Mia Dukle, Faith M. Fairbairn, Sara V. Maurer, Madelyn M. Grueter, Ahmed I. Baig, Rachel Telles, Hanna E. Stevens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prenatal stress affects offspring development. The placenta, an important maternal-fetal mediator, is susceptible to prenatal stress, and its biology affects fetal neurodevelopment, particularly ventral forebrain. Ventral forebrain developmental disruption is linked to neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Currently, multiple mouse models are used to study these links. However, each model may induce unique effects in both placental and neurodevelopmental outcomes, which are rarely studied. To explore this, pregnant mice were exposed 3 times daily to one of three stress models: repeated restraint stress (RS), chronic unpredictable stress (CUS), or repeated footshock stress (FS) beginning at embryonic day (E)12. At E14 or E18, placenta and embryonic brain were collected, and qPCR and brain stereological measurements were performed. Placental immune and ventral forebrain GABAergic gene expression was impacted more at E14, with many changes not apparent by E18, suggesting an acute dysregulated state. Different models had both unique and overlapping effects. E18 analyses suggested adaptation to stress over time, with overall suppression or lack of placental immune factor changes, fewer deviations in the brain, and correlations between placenta and brain, particularly in females. Chronic unpredictable stress showed the most changes at E18. These findings suggest that, while each model has unique effects, as stress exposure progresses, placental immune functioning may promote the normalization of brain GABAergic systems, particularly in females. Further understanding the placenta-brain axis may be valuable in delineating mechanisms that increase and decrease risk of neurodevelopmental disorders.

Introduction

Prenatal stress, or adversity a fetus experiences during gestation, is a well-established environmental risk that interferes with fetal development, increasing likelihood of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and cognitive and behavioral deficits [1-5]. For typical fetal development, appropriate gene expression must be developmentally regulated across the prenatal period. Prenatal stress disrupts normal gene expression trajectories in developing brains, a likely mechanism for increased NDD risk [1,6-9].

The placenta mediates development, including during prenatal stress [10]. It has an important role in fetal immune protection in typical and stress physiology [11] through regulation of placental immune factors which influence neurodevelopment. Across species, dysregulation of placental cytokines, including Il-6, Il-1β, and Tnf-α, has been implicated in neurodevelopmental outcomes [12-14]. Placental biology may also particularly affect ventral forebrain development [1,15-18]. Ventral forebrain, and specifically, dorsal striatum, are implicated in NDDs for their role in social and restrictive, repetitive behaviors [19,20]. Stress-related intrauterine perturbations disrupt development of the GABAergic system, including the almost entirely GABAergic neuronal population of the dorsal striatum and GABAergic interneuron migration, which determines their appropriate locations. Expression of genes such as Gad1, Gad2, and Gabra1 is critical for proper neurodevelopment and is disrupted by prenatal stress [1,15,17,21].

The pathophysiology of NDDs may involve placental function altering fetal neurodevelopment [22-24]; prenatal stress impairs placental function [25-27]. Impacts of placental signaling on neural development, and long-term behavioral effects [22] are mediated in part by sex-specific placental structure and function. Sex-specific developmental origins shape divergent responses to prenatal stress and may help explain male-biased risk for NDDs [28,29]. This inherent interacting biology suggests that prenatal stress studies should report on both placenta and brain alterations to understand mechanisms fully.

The general experience of chronic stress arises from sustained or repeated stressor exposure and when modeled in rodents, can result in adult immunosuppression, reduced body weight, impaired cognition, reduced motivation, and anxiety-like behaviors [30-34]. In mice, prenatal stress has been modeled with a range of methods. Repetitive restraint stress (RS) is commonly used to produce hormonal, immune, and cardiovascular responses present during many human stress states, and when occurring prenatally, results in offspring behavioral, brain, and placental impacts [1,5,35-37]. Chronic unpredictable stress (CUS), in which stressors vary, results in less habituation, and leads to offspring memory deficits, depression-related behaviors, and changes in synaptic, hormonal, and epigenetic processes in the brain [38,39]. Prenatal CUS also affects placenta, specifically DNA methylation of glucocorticoid-related genes [40]. Lastly, prenatal repetitive footshock stress (FS) changes offspring exploratory behavior, fear conditioning, and corticolimbic activity [41,42], but has not been studied for placental outcomes; relatedly, prenatal FS alters hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA) functioning, in which placenta has been implicated [27,28,42]. These prenatal stress mouse models have been mostly used in isolation, with a range of outcomes; however, without direct comparison, the similarity and relevance of outcomes remains unclear.

Direct comparison of different prenatal stress model impacts on offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes remains to be characterized. To evaluate unique effects of prenatal stress on placental function and embryonic brain development, we used three distinct mouse models: RS, CUS, and FS, assessing placental expression of proinflammatory immune factors which contribute to biological stress responses. Given known impacts of prenatal stress and placental inflammation on offspring GABAergic systems development, which are especially relevant to NDDs, we then evaluated expression of GABAergic genes and brain morphological assessments using GAD67GFP mice. Based on previous work, we hypothesized this gene expression would be sex-specifically affected similarly across stress models, modifying prenatal trajectories. We identified unique developmental impacts of each model, generally with acute placental and neurodevelopmental disruptions that normalized as gestation progressed.

Methods

Stress Paradigms

Animal studies were approved by University of Iowa IACUC (Protocol #0121284). Wild-type CD1 female mice were time-mated (using vaginal plug checks) to CD1 GAD67GFP+/- male mice. Dams were randomly assigned (three stress groups or controls): RS [1], CUS [43], and FS [42]. Conditions were: RS dams were placed in a plexiglass restraint, which limited movement, under a bright light; CUS dams experienced one stressor – placement on a moving, orbital shaker, cold temperature, or wet bedding – randomly selected during each stress period; FS dams were placed in a chamber where a 1 sec, 0.75 mA shock was randomly administered at 30-120 sec intervals. No Stress (NS) control dams were moved at the same times but not subjected to stressors. Stress administration began on embryonic day 12 (E12), 3 times/day for 45 minutes. Stressors continued until tissue collection on E14 or E18. Dam/litter numbers were: E14 NS n=6, E14 RS n=7, E14 CUS n=7, E14 FS n=7, E18 NS n=6, E18 RS n=7, E18 CUS n=6, E18 FS n=8.

We administered stress starting at E12 and examined outcomes at E14 and E18 which are similar to timepoints used in other studies [1,5,6,12,17,36]. While each developmental stage has key characteristics, mouse striatal neuronal production starts around E10-11 within the ganglionic eminence. By E18, most neurogenesis has ended, and the focus shifts to neuronal maturation [44,45]. Additionally, the placenta becomes fully functional by about E12, with further maturation by E18 [46]. Due to our specific interest in dorsal striatal neurons, beginning stress paradigms on E12 was ideal, as a timepoint just after development of ganglionic eminence, functional placenta, and fetal neuronal precursor production. E14 has been studied as a mid-gestational timepoint, where neurogenesis is ongoing and placenta is fully functional; in studies with prenatal stress beginning at E12, it is also a timepoint that can demonstrate more immediate, acute impacts. By also examining E18, we assessed prenatal stress effects on more mature neuronal precursors and placenta. In addition, outcomes at later developmental timelines are important to understand temporally specific vulnerabilities and longer-term consequences.

Tissue Collection and Storage

On E14 or E18, dams were euthanized 1 hour after a single stress period. Placentas and embryonic brains were collected. GFP+/- brains were fixed (4% PFA), cryoprotected (30% sucrose), and serially cryosectioned (every 20th) at 25µm in Optimal Cutting Temperature medium (Tissue-Tek) and placentas were microtome sectioned at 4µm. For RNA assessment of placental and microdissected ventral forebrain from GFP-/- offspring, samples were stored in RNALater (-80°C). PCR sex genotyping used Jarid or Rmb31 primers (Table 1).

RNA Isolation, cDNA Synthesis, and qPCR

E14 and E18 placenta and ventral forebrain from one male and one female per litter were randomly selected for RNA isolation (Trizol) and quantified and verified by Spectrophotometer (Nanodrop). cDNA was synthesized (New England Biolabs AMV Reverse Transcriptase; NEB M0227L, Random Primer Mix; NEB S1330S). Ventral forebrain GABAergic system development genes [8,47] and placental immune genes similar to previous findings [25] were assessed with qPCR with samples run in triplicate. As previously, Gapdh and UBC were used as housekeeping genes for ventral forebrain and placenta, respectively (primers in Table 1), assessing expression of these genes for consistency before using in ddCt calculations, normalized to NS.

Cellular and Morphology Measures

One male and one female were randomly selected from each litter for brain volume and migration and placenta stereological assessment, as previously at E14 and postnatal day 0 [1,6]. Slide-mounted coronal brain sections were immunostained with anti-GFP (enQuire BioReagents QAB10251) and secondary (Invitrogen A-11039) antibodies to evaluate GAD67GFP+ cell distribution. H&E-stained placentas were immunostained after antigen retrieval (10 min, 100°C, 10mM EDTA) with anti-Iba1 (1:500, Wako 012-26723) and secondary (1:500, Alexa Fluor 594, A11012) to assess macrophages. An AxioImager M2 epifluorescent microscope (Zeiss) coupled with Stereoinvestigator (Microbrightfield, VT, USA) was used to quantify ventral forebrain volume using standard neuroanatomical boundaries throughout the rostrocaudal extent, GAD67GFP+ cell migration (E14: GAD67GFP+ cell leading edge in dorsal forebrain divided by total circumference; E18: GAD67GFP+ cell stereologically counted density in dorsal forebrain white matter), and placental labyrinth zone macrophages.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed (Prism, Graphpad) with NS paired to each stress condition using two-way ANOVA with independent variables of sex and stress condition (significance p<0.05 and trend p<0.10). Pearson’s correlation coefficients for expression of all placental and forebrain genes assessed in this study were computed separately with individual fold change by sex across individual animals using NS paired with each stress condition or as average fold change for each stress group by sex and embryonic age with significance level adjusted for the number of multiple comparisons made at each time point (significance p<0.003). Outliers were excluded using ROUT analysis (Q=10%).

Results

Outcomes were measured first at E14 and, to investigate the role of developmental stages in prenatal stress responses, then later at E18 (Figure 1a). At E14, fetal weight was decreased in CUS (Figure 1b, F1,116=6.737, p=0.0107) but increased in FS offspring (F1,116=4.277, p=0.0409); at E18, fetal weight now had a CUS stress-sex interaction (F1,44=5.623, p=0.0222, post hoc NS vs CUS females p=0.0435) and a decrease with FS (F1,50=6.146, p=0.0166) (Figure 1b). Maternal weight gain was reduced by all stress types only at E14, and litter size was unchanged except increased at E18 by FS (Appendix A: Supplemental Figure 1a,b,g,h).

E14 placental mass assessment revealed a trending sex-stress interaction for RS (Figure 1C, F1,118=3.410, p=0.0673) and a significant increase for FS (F1,114=11.58, p=0.0009); at E18, only CUS trend decreased placenta mass (F1,40=2.976, p=0.0922). Placental macrophage number was only increased by FS at E14, while placental efficiency was unchanged at E14 and increased at E18 only for CUS (Appendix A: Supplemental Figure 1c,e,f,i).

Ventral forebrain volume (Figure 1d) at E14 showed a trend decrease with RS (F1,20=3.451, p=0.780) and sex-stress interaction for FS (F1,18=6.986, p=0.0165, post hoc NS vs FS males p=0.0106), while at E18, a trend increase with CUS (F1,17=3.737, p=0.0700) and trend sex-stress interaction with effects opposite those at E14 with FS (F1,22=3.284, p=0.08080). However, GABAergic cell migration was unchanged (Appendix A: Supplemental Figure 1d,j).

E14 Gene Expression

Given impacts on placental mass, we assessed expression of the aforementioned placental genes. In RS, there was a sex-stress interaction for Il-6 driven by increased Il-6 expression for females (F1,22=6.269, p=0.0202, post hoc NS vs RS females p=0.0407), similar to a trend sex-stress interaction for Tnf-α (F1,22=3.424, p=0.0777) (Figure 2a,b). Further, RS placentas trend decreased Ccl2 (F1,22=4.132, p=0.0543), decreased Cxcr4 (F1,21=5.748, p=0.0259), and dysregulated Vegf expression, apparent in a sex-stress interaction driven by a trend decrease for females (F1,22=5.266, p=0.0317, post hoc NS vs RS females p=0.0758) (Figure 2d-f). Similar to RS, CUS induced a trend sex-stress interaction in placental Il-6 (F1,22=3.009, p=0.0968) and trend decreased Cxcr4 expression (F1,22=3.620, p=0.0703) (Figure 2a,e). Uniquely, CUS placentas had trending decreased Il-1ꞵ (F1,22=3.774, p=0.0650) (Figure 2c). In FS, there was also a sex-stress interaction for placental Il-6 with increased Il-6 in females (F1,22=6.794, p=0.0161, posthoc NS vs FS females p=0.0109), similar to FS trend sex-stress interaction for Tnf-α (F1,22=3.091, p=0.0926) (Figure 2a,b). FS also dysregulated Vegf, like RS, seen through a sex-stress interaction and increase for males (F1,22=5.015, p=0.0356, post hoc NS vs FS males p=0.0787) (Figure 2f). Overall, Il-6 expression had a sex-stress interaction for all models at E14, RS had the most effects, but each model also had unique gene-specific effects.

To examine how each model also affected offspring neurodevelopmental risk, ventral forebrain (striatal primordium) was examined for GABAergic system development gene expression (Figure 2g-l). In RS, Gad2 and Erbb4 expression increased (F1,17=5.282, p=0.0345, F1,17=5.836, p=0.0272, respectively), and sex-stress interaction for Cxcr4 was driven by a trend increase in females (F1,17=4.800, p=0.0427, post hoc NS vs RS females p=0.0649) (Figure 2h,j,k). In CUS ventral forebrain, there were similar outcomes with trend increased Erbb4 (F1,19=4.310, p=0.0517) and increased Cxcr4 (F1,19=6.845, p=0.0170) (Figure 2h,k). In FS, ventral forebrain also showed trend increased Erbb4 (F1,19=3.953, p=0.0614) and increased Cxcr4 (F1,19=15.85, p=0.0008), and uniquely increased Gad1 (F1,19=5.105, p=0.0358) and trend increased Nxph1 (F1,19=3.621, p=0.0723) (Figure 2g,j-l). Across stress types, Erbb4 and Cxcr4 were impacted similarly (Table 2).

To assess cross-tissue associations, correlational analyses between placenta and brain across all gene expression values were performed at E14. Decreased placental Il-1β correlated with increased brain Gabra1 in NS and FS male offspring (p=0.002939, r=-0.777). No significant outcomes were observed for any other correlation at E14, including across all stress types and sex.

E18 Gene Expression

We also performed the same gene expression assessments at E18. Chronic effects were defined as disruptions present at E14 that persisted to E18, in contrast to acute effects only present at E14 that normalized by E18. RS chronically affected placental Tnf-ɑ from E14 to E18 (F1,19=5.233, p=0.0338) with a trend sex-stress interaction (F1,19=5.233, p=0.0556) (Figure 3b). Placental Cxcr4 was also chronically decreased by RS at E14 and E18 (F1,20=4.749, p=0.0414) (Figure 3e). A trend dysregulation of Vegf expression by RS also occurred at E18 with different male-female effects compared to E14 (sex-stress interaction F1,19=3.510, p=0.0765, post hoc NS vs RS males p=0.0173) (Figure 3f). In CUS, all genes except Ccl2 were impacted. CUS decreased E18 Il-6 (F1,20=6.628, p=0.0181) (Figure 3a), distinct from E14 sex-specific effects. A similar novel pattern of decreased expression at E18 with CUS was additionally found for Tnf-α and Vegf (F1,19=4.294, p=0.0521; F1,20=4.536, p=0.0458, respectively) (Figure 3b,f), which were unchanged by CUS at E14. Two genes showed chronically decreased expression with CUS at E14 and E18: Il-1β and Cxcr4 (F1,19=7.875, p=0.0113; F1,20=6.670, p=0.0178, respectively) (Figure 3c,e). In FS, only Il-6 was significantly decreased (F1,20=5.762, p=0.0262) (Figure 3a). At E18, all stress models, to varying degrees, decreased placental immune gene expression, indicating an immunosuppressed placenta across sex (Table 2).

In contrast to E14, most brain GABAergic system genes assessed were not affected at E18, indicating a possible normalization (Figure 3g-l). The only significant changes seen were increased Nxph1 for RS (F1,20=8.604, p=0.0082) and Cxcr4 for FS (F1,19=5.899, p=0.0252), both observed across sexes.

At E18, significant placental-brain gene expression correlations were observed in females, in contrast to the E14 male-specific finding. Decreased placental Ccl2 was positively correlated with decreased brain Nxph1 expression across NS and FS female groups (p=0.0028, r=0.861). Across NS and RS female groups, decreased placenta Ccl2 was also significantly positively correlated with decreased brain Gabra1 expression (p=0.003, r=0.894). Across females in all stress types, higher expression of three placental genes, Il-1β, Cxcr4, and Vegf correlated with higher brain Gabra1 expression (p=0.001, r=0.728; p=0.003, r=0.656; p=0.0005, r=0.755, respectively) suggesting similar placenta-brain communication across stress types. No other outcomes were significant for any other correlation, including across all stress types and sex.

We also further investigated correlations across groups and time. We found that increased placental Il-6 was negatively correlated with forebrain Gad2 expression (p=0.002, r=-0.790) across both sexes for all models. Additionally, increased placental Il-6 was negatively correlated with forebrain Gabra1 across FS groups (p=0.001, r=-0.999) and with forebrain Gad2 across RS groups (p=0.001, r=-0.999). No other outcomes were significant for other correlations, including across all stress types and sex.

Discussion

This study revealed unique impacts of three stress models, confirming that not all stress is biologically equivalent, and models cannot be used comparably. Across models, prenatal stress biphasically resulted in more deviations at E14 than E18: acute, short-lived gene expression disruptions earlier and normalized states later, particularly in GABAergic systems that play a role in NDDs. Because all stress paradigms began at E12 and were collected at two timepoints, embryos collected at E18 experienced a longer duration of prenatal stress than those at E14. While our age-matched, no stress controls helped account for differences in developmental regulation, in this design, embryonic age and cumulative stress exposure remained inherently linked. Therefore, normalization of changes by E18 may be due to developmental age, cumulative stress exposure, or a complex interaction of the two. Our findings also highlight three other factors in prenatal stress effects: sex differences, fetal growth restriction, and placenta-brain links.

Our data revealed broader deviations at E14 than E18. As development progresses, placental and neural processes may heighten vulnerability or confer protection against stress [48,49]. We saw both normalization and reversal of earlier changes at E18, suggesting placental immune activation was short-lived, and may transition to immunosuppression during prolonged stress. At E18, CUS significantly altered gene expression in all but one tested placental gene, compared to fewer impacted by RS or FS. The unpredictable nature of CUS may contribute to these more chronic effects. Dynamic placental responses may relate to GABAergic system changes, which were transient at E14, but mostly normalized at E18. Transient impacts at E14 may contribute to lasting neurodevelopmental impacts not captured within this study. For example, Erbb4 disruption during embryonic neurogenesis but not postnatally impacted multiple facets of cortical function persistently [50]. Here, Erbb4 was uniformly dysregulated across all stress types at E14, but not E18, which could account for long-lasting and global brain impacts of prenatal stress. Understanding these temporally specific developmental vulnerabilities is essential, as particular neurodevelopmental outcomes may arise from transient versus persistent changes.

Prenatal stress effects differ by sex [51]. Our primary sex-specific findings were E14 female placental (Il-6, Tnf-α, Vegf) and brain (Cxcr4) dysregulations. Interestingly, E14 Vegf was upregulated and ventral forebrain volume decreased in FS only in males. These both align with our previous E13 findings after RS [1]. While small sample size limited our results, they reveal the placenta’s potential role in sex-specific prenatal stress influences on brain [52]. While neurodevelopmental disorders are more common in males, these early embryonic changes do not account for all sex-specific mechanisms that proceed after birth. As such, prenatal stress research should account for the intricacies of different stress paradigms and sex differences.

Across species, prenatal stress reduces fetal body weight [53,54]. Reduced fetal mass is associated with NDD-relevant persistent changes in brain morphology and function [55,56]. Each model manifested distinctive impacts on fetal growth. FS decreased fetal weight at E18 after an unexpected E14 increase. Given weight changes were consistent with sex, but placental gene expression and ventral forebrain volume changes were sex-specific, different mechanisms may impact each outcome. This was further demonstrated by broad E14 gene expression impacts of RS without fetal weight changes. CUS impacted E14 body weight across sex, which persisted to E18 in females, indicating that unpredictable stress may accumulate over time [57]. Broad CUS effects on placental Il-1β and Cxcr4 may have influenced body weight, but only female-specific increased E14 placental Il6 aligned with chronic lower female body weight.

Examining both placental and brain outcomes in the same individuals allowed for placenta-brain axis consideration in stress responses. At E14, a significant correlation emerged between placental Il-1β and brain Gabra1 in males, but not females. By E18, females, not males, displayed correlations between placenta and brain gene expression. Together, these results highlight a sexually dimorphic neuroplacentalogical trajectory, potentially contributing to sex-based differences in neurodevelopment following prenatal stress [28,58]. Correlational analysis demonstrated links of placental Il-6 to forebrain GABAergic systems gene expression across time, sex, and stress types, underlining the important and nuanced role of this immune factor in prenatal stress responses.

In conclusion, this work helps elucidate complex interplays of sex, developmental timing, and placental-brain coordination in shaping offspring responses to varied prenatal stress models. Further placental-brain mechanistic studies will be key in understanding how early stress exposure contributes to NDD risk.
