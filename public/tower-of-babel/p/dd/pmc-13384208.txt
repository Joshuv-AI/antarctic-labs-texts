Blautia coccoides-derived acetate potentiates anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in melanoma by activating cytotoxic CD8+ T cells
Authors: Zhiwen Zhang, Xiaofeng Liang, Yuyang Tian, Chengyi Li, Xueli Cao, Di Wang, Ansheng Xie, Siqi Liu, Kefan Lin, Qing Li, Yunsheng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGut microbiota can modulate cancer immunotherapy and enhance the efficacy of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade in tumors, yet the responsible microbes and underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.MethodsPublicly available anti-PD-1-treated melanoma microbiome cohorts were reanalyzed. Causal validation was performed using oral Blautia coccoides (B. coccoides) supplementation in B16-F10 melanoma-bearing mice. Untargeted and targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry-based metabolomics, CD8+ T-cell depletion, receptor identification and binding analyses, and downstream transcriptomic and biochemical assays were used to identify the key metabolite and investigate its mechanism of action.ResultsWe identified Blautia as enriched in melanoma patients responding to immune checkpoint inhibitors, with higher abundance associated with non-progression. In melanoma-bearing mice, oral B. coccoides suppressed tumor growth and increased intratumoral effector CD8+ T cells. Metabolomic profiling identified acetate as a prominent B. coccoides-associated metabolite. Acetate enhanced CD8+ T-cell effector function. CD8+ T-cell depletion largely abrogated the antitumor effects of both B. coccoides and acetate, supporting a central role for CD8+ T cells. Mechanistically, these findings support a model in which acetate is associated with a TLR3-linked signaling pathway in CD8+ T cells, accompanied by PI3K/Akt activation and enhanced effector function. Notably, B. coccoides or acetate potentiated anti-PD-1 therapy in mouse melanoma models, supporting their potential as adjunctive strategies for melanoma immunotherapy.ConclusionsThese findings support a model of an acetate-TLR3-linked PI3K/Akt signaling axis linking microbiota-associated metabolites to CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Importantly, our study highlights both B. coccoides and its derivative, acetate, as preclinical adjunctive candidates to potentiate anti-PD-1 efficacy in melanoma.Keywords: Melanoma, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immunotherapy, Toll-like receptor - TLR, T-Lymphocytes

Introduction

Melanoma, arising from the oncogenic transformation of melanocytes, is characterized by its aggressive biology and high metastatic potential, representing the most lethal form of skin cancer worldwide.1 2 Although targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved outcomes, melanoma incidence is rising globally, posing a growing public-health burden.1 3 While ICIs, particularly those targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis, have revolutionized the treatment landscape,4,7 durable clinical benefit is restricted to approximately 40% of patients.8 9 This therapeutic ceiling underscores the urgency of identifying novel adjunct strategies to sensitize non-responders and enhance the efficacy of existing immunotherapies.

Accumulating evidence highlights the gut microbiota as a key modulator of ICI responsiveness.10 11 Clinical studies have demonstrated that fecal microbiota transplantation can restore ICI sensitivity in a subset of patients with primary or acquired resistance.12,14 Furthermore, lifestyle and dietary factors, such as high fiber intake, have been linked to superior therapeutic outcomes via the modulation of microbial composition and metabolic outputs.15 Although specific commensals like Lactobacillus reuteri16 and Lactobacillus johnsonii17 have shown promise in preclinical models, the precise identity of the most influential taxa and the molecular dialog between microbial metabolites and the tumor microenvironment (TME) remain largely elusive.

Through an analysis of publicly available microbiome datasets, we identified Blautia as a prominent genus enriched in patients with melanoma who exhibit favorable responses to anti-PD-1 therapy and prolonged progression-free survival (PFS).18 19 Members of Blautia are increasingly recognized as next-generation probiotics due to their diverse metabolic functions.19 For instance, Blautia wexlerae and Blautia coccoides (B. coccoides) have been implicated in maintaining metabolic homeostasis and reinforcing epithelial barriers.20 21 In the context of oncology, B. coccoides has been associated with improved outcomes in breast cancer22 and shown to promote CD8+ T-cell infiltration in bladder cancer models.23 However, its functional relevance and specific mechanism of action in melanoma immunotherapy remain undefined.

In this study, we demonstrate that oral administration of B. coccoides suppresses melanoma growth and enhances the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. Mechanistically, we show that B. coccoides-derived acetate engages a non-canonical, TLR3-linked signaling pathway, leading to PI3K/Akt activation, enhanced effector cytokine production, and increased intratumoral cytotoxic T-cell responses. These findings support a microbiota-metabolite-immune framework and provide a rationale for evaluating B. coccoides or acetate as adjunctive strategies to potentiate checkpoint blockade efficacy in melanoma.

Results

Blautia associates with anti-PD-1 responsiveness, and the Blautia species B. coccoides restrains melanoma growth in vivo

To explore the relationship between gut microbiota composition and immunotherapy outcomes in melanoma, we reanalyzed the publicly available cohort reported by McCulloch et al,18 comprising patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy. Patients were stratified by objective response (responders (R) vs non-responders (NR)) and by disease trajectory (progressors (P) vs non-progressors (NP)).

Community-level analysis revealed a significant difference in β-diversity between R and NR groups (permutational multivariate analysis of variance, PERMANOVA, p=0.004) without evidence of dispersion-driven effects, whereas no significant difference was observed between P and NP groups (PERMANOVA, p=0.079) (figure 1A). Alpha-diversity analysis demonstrated no significant differences between P and NP groups across Shannon, Chao1, or observed richness indices. In contrast, while Shannon diversity did not differ between R and NR groups, Chao1 and observed richness were modestly but significantly higher in NR compared with R patients (online supplemental figure S1A,B).

Genus-level compositional profiling revealed increased relative abundance of Blautia in both R compared with NR and NP compared with P groups (figure 1B). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis (linear discriminant analysis, LDA score >4.0) identified Blautia as a discriminative genus enriched in favorable clinical subgroups. This enrichment was further validated using Wilcoxon rank-sum testing with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction, confirming significantly higher Blautia abundance in R and NP groups (figure 1C–E). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with medium-to-high Blautia abundance exhibited a profound protective effect, featuring significantly prolonged both PFS (HR=0.45, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.83, p=0.010; figure 1F) and overall survival (OS; HR=0.39, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.77, p=0.005; figure 1G) compared with those with low abundance. These dual-endpoint survival benefits robustly support the clinical relevance of this genus. Furthermore, to provide a comprehensive landscape of the prognostic microbiome signatures, detailed survival analyses and statistical metrics for all other clinically significant taxa identified in our cohort are summarized in online supplemental table S1.

We next evaluated whether B. coccoides, selected as a representative Blautia species, could influence melanoma growth in vivo. C57BL/6 mice were orally gavaged with sterile PYG medium, Escherichia coli (E. coli), or B. coccoides beginning 2 weeks prior to subcutaneous inoculation of B16-F10 melanoma cells and continuing until the study endpoint (figure 1H). Notably, B. coccoides treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth, resulting in reduced tumor volume and tumor weight compared with both the PYG control and the non-specific bacterial control (E. coli) (figure 1I), and significantly prolonged the OS of tumor-bearing mice (figure 1J).

To further confirm the specific tumor-suppressive effect of B. coccoides, we established an antibiotic-preconditioned mouse model with depleted native gut microbiota (figure 1K). Consistent with the conventional model, oral gavage of B. coccoides markedly inhibited melanoma growth, with significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in B. coccoides-treated mice relative to controls (figure 1L).

Collectively, these findings indicate that enrichment of Blautia is associated with improved clinical outcomes in patients with melanoma treated with anti-PD-1 therapy, and that B. coccoides suppresses melanoma growth in vivo.

B. coccoides inhibits melanoma growth and enhances intratumoral effector CD8+ T-cell responses

Consistent with its tumor-suppressive effect (figure 1I,L), we next examined the immune changes associated with the antitumor activity of B. coccoides, focusing on T-cell responses within the TME. Flow cytometric analysis (gating strategy detailed in online supplemental figure S2A) revealed that B. coccoides treatment markedly increased intratumoral effector CD8+ T-cell responses, with significantly higher frequencies of interferon (IFN)-γ+ CD8+ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α+ CD8+ T cells compared with controls (figure 2A). Ki-67 expression was also elevated within both IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T-cell subsets, indicating enhanced proliferative activity among effector CD8+ T cells (figure 2B). Immunofluorescence further confirmed increased infiltration of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues following B. coccoides administration (figure 2C), supporting enhanced local effector immunity. Notably, the proportions of granzyme B+ CD8+ T cells and PD-1+ CD8+ T cells within tumors were not significantly altered (online supplemental figure S2B), suggesting that B. coccoides preferentially enhances effector cytokine production and proliferative activity without significantly changing the frequency of tumor PD-1+ CD8+ T cells.

This heightened CD8+ response was accompanied by increased frequencies of IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells and Ki-67+IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells in tumors (figure 2D). In addition, B. coccoides treatment significantly reduced the frequency of regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the TME (figure 2E). Consistent with the reduced overall tumor burden, the proportion of Ki-67+ cells within the tumor-enriched CD45⁻CD31⁻ fraction was also reduced after B. coccoides treatment, suggesting decreased proliferative activity in this population (figure 2F). Furthermore, this immune-permissive shift in the TME was supported by an elevated natural killer (NK) cell frequency (figure 2G) and a significant reduction in Gr-1+CD11b+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs; figure 2H). Gating strategies for these populations are detailed in online supplemental figure S2C,D. These coordinated changes indicate a TME skewed toward immune activation and away from suppression, creating a milieu permissive for CD8+ T-cell effector function.

Peripheral immune profiling provided additional context. In the spleen, most immune parameters were not significantly changed across groups (figure 2I, online supplemental figure S2E), suggesting that B. coccoides does not trigger broad, non-specific lymphoid activation. In peripheral blood, however, intratumoral trends were partially recapitulated: we observed increased frequencies of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells (figure 2J) and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells (figure 2K), accompanied by a reduction in circulating Tregs (figure 2L), an elevation in NK cells (figure 2M), and a decrease in MDSCs (online supplemental figure S2F). Consistently, the proliferative index (Ki-67) was significantly elevated in circulating IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T-cell subsets (figure 2J,K), further supporting a selective systemic immune response associated with enhanced effector CD8+ T-cell activity.

To determine whether B. coccoides administration altered gut microbiota composition, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Although overall β-diversity differences did not reach statistical significance (analysis of similarities, ANOSIM p=0.082) (figure 2N), limited compositional changes were observed (figure 2O), including an increased relative abundance of Lactobacillus. These findings suggest that B. coccoides may subtly modulate the gut microbial community without inducing major global restructuring.

B. coccoides culture supernatant inhibits melanoma cell malignant phenotypes and enhances CD8+ T-cell effector function

To investigate the direct effects of B. coccoides culture supernatant (BCCS) on melanoma cells, A375 and B16-F10 melanoma cell lines, as well as normal human epidermal melanocytes (HEM), were treated with PYG medium, E. coli culture supernatant (ECCS), or BCCS at concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 20%. Compared with PYG and ECCS, BCCS significantly suppressed the growth of A375 and B16-F10 cells in a dose-dependent manner (figure 3A), while no significant change was observed in HEM cells. Based on these growth curves, 20% supernatant was selected for subsequent functional assays.

Consistent with these effects, BCCS significantly induced apoptosis in A375 and B16-F10 melanoma cells (figure 3B). Moreover, BCCS markedly impaired cell migration in the wound-healing assay (figure 3C), induced S-phase cell-cycle arrest (figure 3D), and reduced colony-forming capacity (figure 3E), collectively indicating suppression of multiple malignant phenotypes. Western blot showed that BCCS increased the expression of cleaved caspase-3 and the pro-apoptotic protein Bax while decreasing the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. In addition, BCCS reduced the expression of Vimentin, a marker associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and tumor cell migration (figure 3F). Importantly, HEM cells showed no significant changes in apoptosis, migration, colony formation, or cell-cycle distribution under the same conditions (figure 3B–E), suggesting tumor-selective effects of BCCS under these experimental conditions.

Given that our in vivo data indicated enhanced CD8+ T-cell responses following B. coccoides treatment (figure 2), we next examined whether BCCS could influence T-cell effector responses. IFN-γ, TNF-α and granzyme B (GZMB) were assessed as markers of CD8+ T-cell effector activation. Flow cytometric analysis of splenic CD8+ T cells demonstrated that BCCS treatment significantly enhanced IFN-γ and TNF-α production, whereas GZMB expression was not significantly changed (figure 3G).

Collectively, these findings indicate that BCCS suppresses melanoma cell malignant phenotypes by inhibiting tumor cell growth, migration, and survival while simultaneously enhancing CD8+ T-cell effector responses, with minimal effects on normal melanocytes.

Acetate is a prominent metabolite associated with the antitumor activity of B. coccoides

To begin defining the biochemical nature of the active components underlying this effect, both PYG medium and BCCS were subjected to heat inactivation or proteinase K digestion. Neither treatment impaired the ability of BCCS to suppress melanoma cell growth or to enhance IFN-γ and TNF-α production by CD8+ T cells (figure 4A–D), suggesting that the active factor is heat-stable and unlikely to be proteinaceous.

Having shown that the active factor is unlikely to be a protein, we next sought to identify candidate metabolites associated with B. coccoides. We performed untargeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) of fecal samples from mice treated according to the regimen shown in figure 4E. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation of metabolic profiles among the PYG, E. coli, and B. coccoides groups (figure 4F), indicating that B. coccoides induces a distinct metabolic landscape in vivo. Further differential metabolite analysis identified the top 20 metabolites enriched in the B. coccoides group, as shown in figure 4G.

To determine whether similar metabolic signatures were present in bacterial culture supernatants, we performed untargeted LC-MS/MS metabolomic profiling of PYG medium, ECCS, and BCCS (figure 4H). PCA again demonstrated clear separation among groups (figure 4I), and heatmap analysis highlighted metabolites selectively enriched in BCCS (figure 4J). Among these candidates, acetate emerged as a prominent metabolite. In conjunction with the fecal untargeted metabolomic findings, we next performed targeted quantification of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which confirmed that acetate was markedly enriched in BCCS compared with control medium (figure 4K–L, online supplemental figure S3A).

We next asked whether acetate alone could recapitulate the key antitumor effects of B. coccoides. Targeted SCFA quantification revealed that the BCCS contained approximately 25 mM acetate, meaning that the 20% and 10% BCCS used in prior assays corresponded to roughly 5 mM and 2.5 mM acetate, respectively. To determine whether pure acetate is functionally active at these levels, we performed dose-titration experiments ranging from 0 to 5 mM. We found that acetate dose-dependently induced melanoma cell apoptosis and enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell effector cytokine production, without increasing apoptosis in normal HEM cells (online supplemental figure S4). Based on the optimal functional responses observed in the titration assays, we selected 5 mM acetate for direct tumor-killing evaluations and 2.5 mM acetate for CD8+ T-cell activation assays. In vitro treatment with acetate significantly suppressed the growth of both A375 and B16-F10 melanoma cells (figure 4M). Western blot further demonstrated that acetate treatment simultaneously upregulated the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and cleaved caspase-3, accompanied by the accumulation of full-length Caspase-3, while reducing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and the migration-associated marker Vimentin (figure 4N), indicating a profound activation of apoptosis-associated and migration-associated signaling. Consistent with these direct effects, additional flow cytometry experiments confirmed that acetate markedly induced melanoma cell apoptosis (online supplemental figure S3B–D). Beyond direct tumor inhibition, acetate also recapitulated the key immunomodulatory features of BCCS by enhancing CD8+ T-cell effector responses, as evidenced by significantly increased IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB production (figure 4O).

Having established that acetate could recapitulate key BCCS-associated antitumor and immunomodulatory effects in vitro, we next examined whether acetate could be detected in vivo after B. coccoides or acetate treatment. Acetate abundance was measured in fecal samples, serum, and melanoma tissues using an acetate detection kit. Mice receiving either B. coccoides or acetate showed increased acetate levels in these compartments compared with their respective controls (figure 4P–Q), indicating that acetate was systemically available and detectable at the tumor site after treatment. We then evaluated whether acetate exerted antitumor effects in vivo. Mice received daily oral gavage of acetate (200 mg/kg) beginning 2 weeks prior to subcutaneous inoculation with B16-F10 melanoma cells (figure 4R, online supplemental figure S3E). Compared with the vehicle control, acetate treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth, resulting in reduced tumor volume and tumor weight (figure 4S).

To understand the systemic and local immunomodulatory effects of acetate, we first analyzed the peripheral blood. Acetate treatment significantly enhanced IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T-cell responses (figure 4T) and increased the proliferative fraction of these effector subsets (online supplemental figure S3G–I). This systemic activation was accompanied by an increase in circulating IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells (online supplemental figure S3J), whereas circulating Treg frequencies were not significantly altered by acetate treatment (online supplemental figure S3K).

Flow cytometric analysis further revealed that this immune activation extended into the TME, creating an immune-permissive state. Specifically, acetate enhanced intratumoral IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T-cell responses and increased the proliferative fraction (Ki-67+) within these effector subsets (figure 4U). Intratumoral IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells were similarly increased (online supplemental figure S3F), whereas intratumoral Treg frequencies were not significantly changed (figure 4V). This immune-permissive shift was additionally supported by an elevation in NK cells (figure 4W). Consistent with the reduced overall tumor burden, in vivo tumor cell proliferation (assessed by Ki-67 positivity within the CD45⁻CD31⁻ fraction) was markedly decreased (figure 4X), whereas intratumoral MDSCs were not significantly altered (figure 4Y).

Collectively, these results identify acetate as a prominent B. coccoides-associated metabolite that robustly recapitulates the major antitumor and immunomodulatory effects of the bacterium.

B. coccoides-mediated and acetate-mediated melanoma control depends on CD8+ T cells

To determine whether CD8+ T cells mediate the antitumor effects of B. coccoides and acetate, we performed in vivo depletion experiments using an anti-CD8 antibody (online supplemental figure S5A). Oral administration of B. coccoides significantly suppressed melanoma progression in the IgG isotype control group, leading to reduced tumor volume and weight (figure 5A). This antitumor effect was largely lost following CD8+ T-cell depletion, such that tumor growth in B. coccoides-treated mice became comparable to that in controls (figure 5B). Flow cytometric analysis showed that B. coccoides increased the frequencies of CD8+ T cells in both peripheral blood and tumor tissues under IgG conditions (figure 5C), whereas anti-CD8 treatment efficiently depleted CD8+ T cells in both compartments (figure 5D).

We next investigated whether acetate, a prominent B. coccoides-associated metabolite, likewise requires CD8+ T cells for its antitumor effect. Consistent with the bacterial treatment, oral acetate administration significantly restricted tumor growth in the IgG group (figure 5E), but this effect was largely abrogated following CD8+ T-cell depletion (figure 5F). These antitumor effects were accompanied by increased CD8+ T-cell frequencies in the peripheral blood and tumor tissue under IgG control conditions (figure 5G), along with effective CD8+ T-cell depletion in the anti-CD8 group (figure 5H). While BCCS and acetate exhibited direct tumoricidal effects in vitro, the near-complete loss of antitumor efficacy following CD8+ T-cell depletion indicates that their in vivo effects are primarily mediated by CD8+ T cell-dependent immunity rather than direct cytotoxicity. Collectively, these findings support a critical role for CD8+ T cells in mediating the antitumor effects of both B. coccoides and acetate.

Acetate enhances CD8+ T-cell effector function through a TLR3-linked PI3K/Akt signaling pathway

To identify acetate-responsive targets in CD8+ T cells, we performed a limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry (LiP-MS) workflow (figure 6A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the LiP-MS candidates showed enrichment in cytoplasmic and membrane-associated compartments, particularly membrane protein complexes (figure 6B), suggesting that acetate-responsive proteomic perturbations occur within a cytoplasmic, membrane-proximal signaling landscape. Among these candidates, TLR3 was highlighted in the LiP-MS dataset and was selected for further mechanistic validation (figure 6C; online supplemental table S2).

Using surface plasmon resonance, we observed dose-responsive signals consistent with an interaction between acetate and the recombinant TLR3 ectodomain, with an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 22.6 µM (figure 6D). To assess the association of acetate with TLR3-containing complexes, equal aliquots of cell lysates were incubated with acetate and subjected to immunoprecipitation (IP) using either anti-TLR3 antibody or control IgG. Western blotting confirmed successful TLR3 pulldown. Quantification of the post-IP unbound fraction showed significantly lower residual acetate in the anti-TLR3 group than in the IgG control group, consistent with co-depletion of acetate with TLR3-containing complexes (figure 6E). Molecular docking simulations predicted a plausible binding mode of acetate within a putative TLR3 binding pocket, with a lowest binding energy of –3.36 kcal/mol (figure 6F). Collectively, these orthogonal structural and biochemical analyses support the involvement of TLR3 in the acetate response, even if the precise nature of the receptor mechanics remains to be fully resolved.

To assess the functional relevance of this TLR3-dependent axis, we treated CD8+ T cells with a TLR3 antagonist (CU-CPT4a). CU-CPT4a markedly attenuated acetate-induced enhancement of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T-cell responses (figure 6G). While this pharmacological inhibition strongly implicates TLR3, future genetic validation will be necessary to definitively rule out off-target effects. Furthermore, the KD of 22.6 µm aligns with the physiological accumulation of acetate observed in the TME (figure 4P,Q), suggesting that TLR3 mediates, at least in part, acetate-induced CD8+ T-cell activation under physiologically relevant conditions.

RNA-sequencing analysis revealed significant enrichment of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway following acetate treatment (figure 6H). Immunoblotting further confirmed that acetate enhances phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt in CD8+ T cells, an effect that was attenuated by CU-CPT4a treatment (figure 6I). In summary, our data support a model in which B. coccoides-derived acetate enhances CD8+ T-cell effector function through TLR3-linked PI3K/Akt signaling.

B. coccoides and acetate potentiate anti-PD-1 therapy by enhancing intratumoral CD8+ T-cell effector function

Given the central role of intratumoral CD8+ T cells in mediating checkpoint blockade efficacy, we tested whether B. coccoides or its metabolite acetate could enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in melanoma. In an immunotherapy mouse model (schematic shown in online supplemental figure S5B), combined administration of B. coccoides and anti-PD-1 achieved superior tumor control compared with either monotherapy (figure 7A). This combination successfully reprogrammed the immunosuppressive TME, evidenced by significantly increased frequencies of intratumoral IFN-γ+, TNF-α+, and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells (figure 7B). Notably, systemic acetate supplementation combined with anti-PD-1 largely recapitulated these therapeutic benefits, restricting tumor growth more effectively than either treatment alone (figure 7C) and enhancing multiple intratumoral effector CD8+ T cell responses (figure 7D). Collectively, these results indicate that B. coccoides and acetate can potentiate anti-PD-1 therapy, at least in part through enhancement of intratumoral CD8+ T-cell effector function.

Discussion

Re-analysis of an anti-PD-1-treated melanoma cohort18 showed that Blautia was enriched in clinically favorable subgroups and that higher Blautia abundance was associated with prolonged PFS and improved OS. Consistent with these clinical observations, we functionally demonstrated that oral administration of B. coccoides, a member of the Blautia genus that has been reported in association with antitumor therapeutic responses,22,24 suppresses melanoma growth and enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo.

B. coccoides and its metabolite acetate drive an integrated antitumor program. This mechanism encompasses both direct tumor suppression and robust immune activation. In line with this tumor-intrinsic effect, the reduced Ki-67 positivity within the CD45⁻CD31⁻ tumor-enriched fraction further suggests that B. coccoides and acetate may limit melanoma proliferative activity in vivo. These effects appeared relatively selective for malignant cells under our experimental conditions, with minimal impact on normal human epidermal melanocytes, possibly owing to differences in metabolic requirements or acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family (ACSS)-mediated acetate utilization.25,28 At the same time, B. coccoides and acetate robustly enhanced CD8+ T-cell effector function in vitro and in vivo. Because CD8+ T-cell depletion largely abolished the antitumor effects of both B. coccoides and acetate, our data support CD8+ T cells as the dominant mediators of in vivo therapeutic benefit, with tumor-intrinsic effects providing an additional complementary layer.

Our immune profiling further supports a model in which B. coccoides establishes a TME permissive for effector CD8+ T-cell activity. In tumors, B. coccoides increased cytokine-producing and proliferating CD8+ T cells, whereas splenic immune changes were limited and peripheral blood partially mirrored intratumoral trends, suggesting a coordinated local-systemic immune imprint rather than generalized lymphoid activation. Together with the increased acetate levels detected in feces, serum, and tumors, these findings support a framework in which a gut-derived metabolite becomes systemically available, primes immunity at the systemic level, with this immune response further reinforced within the TME.29,32

Previous studies have shown that acetate can be metabolically used by both tumor cells and T cells through ACSS-dependent pathways, thereby influencing mitochondrial function, histone acetylation, and effector cytokine production.2627 32,35 However, our current study did not directly test ACSS1/2-dependent acetate metabolism in CD8+ T cells. Therefore, these metabolic mechanisms should be regarded as a possible complementary explanation rather than a mechanism directly demonstrated here. In contrast, our experimental data more directly support a TLR3-linked PI3K/Akt signaling axis as a contributor to acetate-enhanced CD8+ T-cell effector function.

Our LiP-MS data indicate that acetate may also engage a membrane-proximal or receptor-proximal signaling landscape. In this context, TLR3 emerged as a candidate mediator of the acetate-induced response. Although TLR3 is canonically recognized as an endosomal sensor of viral double-stranded RNA, the known architecture of the TLR3 ectodomain supports the possibility that non-canonical modulators could influence receptor-proximal events without mimicking classical dsRNA agonists.36,38 Moreover, TLR3 is functionally expressed on CD8+ T cells, where its activation, for example, by poly(I:C), has been reported to enhance antigen-specific responses and effector cytokine production, including IFN-γ.36 39 40 In line with this model, the marked attenuation of acetate-induced CD8+ T-cell activation and PI3K/Akt phosphorylation following TLR3 pharmacologic blockade strongly implicates this receptor pathway. While the exact structural nature of this interaction remains to be fully elucidated, these data support an acetate-driven, TLR3-linked signaling program that promotes CD8+ T-cell effector competence via the PI3K/Akt axis.41

A technical limitation of our study is that the acetate concentrations used in static in vitro cultures are higher than the bulk acetate levels measured in serum or whole tumor lysates. These in vitro concentrations should therefore not be interpreted as direct equivalents of steady-state acetate concentrations in vivo. Instead, our dose-titration experiments support 2.5–5 mM acetate as functional working concentrations for closed culture systems, where continuous metabolite production, replenishment, uptake, and clearance are not fully reproduced.22 42 Thus, while these assays provide mechanistic evidence that acetate can regulate melanoma cell viability and CD8+ T-cell effector function, the precise local acetate concentrations experienced by tumor and immune cells in vivoå remain to be further defined.

In addition to enhanced CD8+ T-cell responses, B. coccoides treatment was accompanied by reduced MDSCs and increased NK cells in the TME. These findings are more appropriately interpreted as part of broader TME remodeling rather than direct effects of acetate on these cell populations. One possible explanation is that enhanced T-cell effector activity and related cytokine changes indirectly alleviate myeloid suppression and support NK cell accumulation or activation.43 44 Regarding the Treg population, canonical studies have established that certain gut microbes and their derived SCFAs, particularly butyrate and propionate, can promote Treg differentiation.45 46 However, the capacity of acetate to promote Treg accumulation appears to be weaker and context-dependent.47 Consistent with these SCFA-specific effects, our in vivo data showed that systemic acetate supplementation enhanced effector CD8+ T-cell responses without significantly altering Treg frequencies in either the melanoma microenvironment or peripheral blood. These findings suggest that, in our melanoma model, acetate-associated antitumor immunity is more closely linked to strengthened effector CD8+ T-cell activity than to modulation of the Treg compartment. In parallel, live B. coccoides treatment reduced Treg frequencies in both melanoma tissues and peripheral blood, further supporting the establishment of an antitumor immune context rather than a Treg-enriched immunosuppressive state.

These findings also have translational relevance. The association of Blautia enrichment with clinically favorable subgroups suggests that microbiome features may contribute to patient stratification during anti-PD-1 therapy, although this requires further validation. In preclinical models, both B. coccoides and acetate potentiated anti-PD-1 efficacy and enhanced intratumoral CD8+ T-cell effector function, supporting microbiota-based or metabolite-based adjunctive strategies for melanoma immunotherapy. Given the favorable safety profile of acetate, optimization of dosing, formulation, and delivery, together with controlled development of B. coccoides as a live biotherapeutic, may provide scalable approaches to improve checkpoint blockade efficacy, particularly in settings with suboptimal baseline responses.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our functional validation focused on a single representative strain (B. coccoides) in limited models, and broader Blautia strain/species generalizability remains to be established. Second, while we provide convergent biophysical and pharmacological evidence for TLR3 involvement, definitive in vivo causality will require CD8+ T cell-specific genetics models (eg, Cd8a-Cre Tlr3f1/f1). Lastly, although acetate supplementation largely recapitulated the B. coccoides phenotype, an acetate-deficient mutant of B. coccoides will be required to further validate the specific contribution of acetate.

Methods

Tumor model and treatment

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n=5 per group), and tumor measurements were performed in a blinded manner.

Mice received daily oral gavage of B. coccoides (1×10⁸ colony-forming units (CFU)), E. coli (1×10⁸ CFU), sterile PYG medium, vehicle (ddH₂O), or acetate (200 mg/kg) for 2 weeks prior to tumor inoculation. Mice were then subcutaneously injected in the right dorsal flank with 5×10⁵ B16-F10 melanoma cells. Gavage treatments were continued throughout the study.

For immunotherapy experiments, mice received intraperitoneal injections of anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (200 µg/mouse) and/or anti-CD8 monoclonal antibody (100 µg/mouse) three times per week for 2 weeks. Control animals received isotype IgG.

Tumor size was measured every 2–3 days using calipers. Tumor volume (V) was calculated as: V=½×length×width2. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 1,500 mm³ or when humane endpoint criteria were reached.

Patient stratification and clinical outcome definition

Microbiome sequencing data and corresponding clinical metadata were obtained from the Pittsburgh melanoma cohort reported by McCulloch et al,18 comprising 94 patients treated with first-line anti-PD-1 therapy. Radiographic tumor responses were evaluated according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 criteria. Disease progression was defined as the first confirmed clinical and/or radiographic progression during anti-PD-1 treatment.

PFS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to the first confirmed disease progression. Patients without documented progression were censored at the date of last follow-up. OS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the time of last follow-up were censored at the date of last follow-up. OS was analyzed as an independent time-to-event clinical endpoint to complement the PFS analysis.

Consistent with the original analytical framework of the Pittsburgh cohort, patients were stratified based on disease trajectory into P and NP, using progression status in conjunction with time-to-event endpoints rather than response-based categorization (R/NR).

For microbiome analyses, stool samples were categorized according to collection timing relative to initiation of ICI therapy. Samples collected prior to treatment or within 4 months of anti-PD-1 initiation were defined as early samples (Pittsburgh early sample cohort), representing baseline-associated microbiota. Samples collected more than 4 months after treatment initiation were defined as late samples (Pittsburgh late sample cohort).

In the present study, a 10-month landmark time point was prespecified prior to analysis, consistent with the temporal framework applied in the original cohort to distinguish durable disease control from early progression. Landmark analysis was implemented to minimize guarantee-time (immortal time) bias by restricting comparisons to patients who were alive and progression-free at the 10-month landmark. Subsequent progression status beyond this fixed time point was used to classify patients as progressors or non-progressors for survival-associated microbiome analyses.

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were minced and digested at 37°C for 1 hour in phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 2 mg/mL collagenase IV. The digest was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer, followed by red blood cell lysis, and cells were finally resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Peripheral blood was collected into anticoagulant tubes, centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min to remove plasma, and then subjected to red blood cell lysis. Spleens were gently homogenized using a glass tissue grinder, and single‐cell suspensions were similarly centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer. Prepared cells were stimulated for 5 hours at 37°C in 5% CO₂ using Cell Stimulation Cocktail with protein transport inhibitors (500×dilution). Surface staining was then performed using afixable viability dye (FVD) and monoclonal antibodies against CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD31, F4/80, CD25, CD206, CD11b, CD86, Gr-1, TCRγδ, CD11c, CD49b, NK1.1, MHC class II (I-A/I-E), and PD-L1 (CD274). After surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using transcription factor buffer, followed by intracellular staining for GZMB, IFN-γ, TNF-α, Foxp3 and Ki-67. For analysis of tumor-enriched proliferating cells, Ki-67+ cells were quantified within the CD45⁻CD31⁻ fraction after sequential gating on singlets, live cells, CD45⁻ cells, and CD31⁻ cells. For Treg analysis, Treg cells were identified as CD25+Foxp3+ cells within the CD45+CD3+CD4+ T-cell population. Unstained cells served as negative controls. Data were acquired on a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo V.10.4 software.

TLR3 immunodepletion assay

Jurkat E6.1 cells were lysed in buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20) supplemented with protease inhibitors. To assess the association between acetate and TLR3-containing complexes, equal aliquots of cell lysates were incubated with the same initial concentration of acetate before immunoprecipitation. A no-acetate sample was included as a background control. The lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-TLR3 antibody (Invitrogen, MA1-25649) or a mouse IgG isotype control (Santa Cruz, sc-2027), followed by incubation with Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen) for 2 hours at room temperature. After antibody-bead capture, the beads were magnetically separated, and the post-IP unbound fraction was collected for acetate quantification rather than the immunoprecipitated eluate. The beads were then washed, and bound complexes were eluted in 50 mM glycine (pH 2.8) and neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) for subsequent sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blotting to confirm successful TLR3 enrichment. Residual acetate levels in the post-IP unbound fraction were quantified using an acetate colorimetric assay kit. Reduced residual acetate in the anti-TLR3 group relative to the IgG control was interpreted as being consistent with co-depletion of acetate with TLR3-containing complexes.

Untargeted and targeted metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomic profiling of fecal samples and filtered cell-free culture supernatants was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China) using a Thermo Vanquish Flex UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.8 µm; Waters) with 5 mM ammonium acetate in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). Quality control (QC) samples prepared by pooling aliquots from all samples were injected periodically to monitor analytical stability. Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode with full MS scans (m/z 100–1,500, 60,000 resolution) followed by MS/MS scans (15,000 resolution) using normalized collision energies of 10, 30, and 60 eV in both positive and negative ion modes.

Targeted quantification of acetate in fecal samples, serum, tumor tissues, and culture supernatants was performed using a calibrated acetate assay, with concentrations normalized to sample weight or volume and expressed relative to controls where indicated. All metabolomic analyses were conducted with at least three biological replicates.

Data processing and metabolite annotation

Raw data files were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard (V.3.0) and processed in R using XCMS (V.3.2) for peak detection and alignment. Isotope and adduct annotation were performed using CAMERA (V.3.16). Metabolic features with a relative SD >30% in QC samples or with >50% missing values across all samples were excluded. Data were normalized using total ion current normalization and corrected for batch effects using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) regression.

Metabolite annotation was first conducted based on accurate mass (m/z tolerance±10 ppm) and retention time (±0.3 min). MS/MS spectra were subsequently matched against HMDB, METLIN, and BiotreeDB databases. Only features with MS/MS spectral similarity scores >0.8 were retained as putatively identified metabolites for downstream analysis.

Multivariate analyses, including partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal PLS-DA, were performed using the ropls package (V.1.26.4) in R, and model robustness was evaluated by permutation testing to assess potential overfitting. Differential metabolites were identified using MetaboAnalystR (V.3.0.3) with thresholds of |log₂ fold change|≥1 and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p<0.05.

Limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry interaction site mapping

LiP-MS was performed to map acetate-associated protein interaction interfaces in acetate-treated mouse CD8+ T cells. Purified mouse CD8+ T cells were incubated with acetate, lysed, and subjected to limited proteolysis with trypsin gradients. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 columns, dried, and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid at a final concentration of 1 µg/µL. Peptide separation was carried out on an EASY-nanoLC 1200 system using an Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 column (75 µm×25 cm, 2 µm) at 55°C and 350 nL/min. A non-linear gradient from 2.2% to 90% buffer B was applied over 54.5 min. MS data were acquired on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 in the m/z 400–1,200 range at 120,000 resolution. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) analysis was performed using 60 isolation windows with higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation at 32% normalized collision energy and fragment acquisition at 30,000 resolution. Raw files were searched against the UniProt Mus musculus reference proteome in Spectronaut 18 with a mass tolerance of ±10 ppm. Protein domains were annotated using InterProScan V.5.57, and enriched domains were identified by Fisher’s exact test (p<0.05). Three biological replicates were analyzed, and only interaction sites detected in at least two replicates were retained for downstream analysis.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.3.2; https://www.r-project.org/) and GraphPad Prism (V.11; GraphPad Software). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, appropriate non-parametric tests (eg, Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were applied. Tumor growth curves were analyzed using a mixed-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood (REML)) implemented in GraphPad Prism V.10, with treatment and time as fixed effects and mouse identity treated as a random effect to account for repeated measures. Tukey’s test was applied for post hoc comparisons. Data are presented as mean±SD unless otherwise indicated. All p values were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The remaining methods are described in detail in the online supplemental file 3.
