Oncogenic Ras drives EED degradation and PRC2 dysfunction to promote aggressive squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Meng-Yen Li, Aubrey Houser, Pradeep Cheema, Xiang Yu Zheng, Paula Restrepo, Pooja Flora, Yudong Zhou, Gil Segal-Dalal, Eldad Silberstein, Xiaotao Zhang, Markus Schober, Deyou Zheng, Idan Cohen, Andrew L Ji, Elena Ezhkova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSquamous cell carcinomas (SCCs) are common epithelial malignancies frequently associated with EZH2 upregulation, which correlates with aggressive growth and poor prognosis. EZH2 functions as the catalytic subunit of Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which deposits the repressive H3K27me3 histone mark, yet PRC2 function in SCC pathogenesis remains unresolved. Here, we uncover a paradox: although EZH2 is elevated, the PRC2-catalyzed H3K27me3 is profoundly reduced in human SCC and complementary murine models. Mechanistically, we identify that oncogenic Ras signaling causes degradation of the PRC2 subunit EED, destabilizing PRC2 and depleting H3K27me3. Loss of EED reprograms keratinocytes into an aggressive tumor-specific cell state and induces paracrine factors that remodel the tumor microenvironment, driving metastasis. Remarkably, reintroducing EED suppresses tumor growth via restoring PRC2 integrity and H3K27me3 levels, revealing the reversibility of this epigenetic collapse. These findings link oncogenic signaling to epigenetic reprogramming and uncover PRC2 reconstitution as a potential therapeutic option in epithelial cancers.Subject terms: Squamous cell carcinoma, Oncogenes, Cancer epigenetics

Introduction

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a common malignancy that arises from stratified epithelial tissues, including the skin, head and neck, cervix, and lung1–5. Among these, cutaneous SCC (cSCC) is the second most prevalent form of non-melanoma skin cancer and originates from basal keratinocytes within the epidermis6,7. Following tumor initiation, cSCC progression involves local invasion of deep structures and regional metastasis via lymphatic invasion8. Aggressive or metastatic forms of cSCC pose significant clinical challenges, with patients experiencing high mortality rates due to resistance to therapy9. However, the mechanism associated with aggressive progression is unclear. This underscores an unmet need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying cSCC progression and develop therapeutic strategies against metastatic cSCC.

cSCC typically arises through the stepwise accumulation of oncogenic mutations in genes such as NOTCH1, NOTCH2, HRAS, and TP53, which disrupt cell cycle regulation, proliferation, and differentiation, leading to clonal selection and expansion5,8,10–14. Notably, metastatic cSCC displays only a minor increase in mutations or chromosomal rearrangements compared with primary tumors, suggesting that non-genetic mechanisms, particularly epigenetic changes, could promote the transition of cancer to become more aggressive15. Yet, we lack a clear understanding of how chromatin regulators and their dependent gene networks influence cSCC tumorigenesis.

Among epigenetic regulators, Polycomb group proteins have emerged as key regulators of chromatin architecture and gene expression, mainly through the activity of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2)16,17. PRC2, composed of the core subunits EED, SUZ12, and EZH2, catalyzes tri-methylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3)—a repressive chromatin mark that compacts chromatin and regulates stem cell identity, differentiation, and tissue homeostasis18–23. Dysregulation of PRC2 is frequently observed in cancer and is associated with the repression of key tumor suppressor genes that regulate the cell cycle, apoptosis, and differentiation24–28. Conversely, recent work in Drosophila revealed that even a transient loss of Polycomb complexes alters the epigenetic landscape and can induce an irreversible cancer cell state, underscoring the oncogenic role of Polycomb dysregulation in tumorigenesis29. In SCCs, including cSCC, elevated EZH2 expression was reported, which correlates with tumor progression and facilitates metastasis30–38. However, the functional state of PRC2 in SCC remains poorly defined, and the contributions of other core subunits have been largely overlooked. This gap is critical, as full PRC2 functionality requires the coordinated assembly of EZH2, SUZ12, and EED39, and disruption of any single component can profoundly affect PRC2 complex composition and reconfigure the epigenetic landscape.

In this study, we identify PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 as a crucial epigenetic gatekeeper of cSCC tumorigenesis. We show that EZH2 expression is uncoupled from H3K27me3 levels across multiple types of SCC. Mechanistically, this EZH2–H3K27me3 decoupling is driven by oncogenic Ras signaling, which promotes proteasomal degradation of EED, thereby destabilizing PRC2 and impairing its function, highlighting how oncogenic activation affects epigenetic regulation by intersecting with chromatin modifiers. Loss of PRC2 integrity reprograms keratinocytes into aggressive, tumor-specific states and remodels the tumor microenvironment to promote metastasis. Notably, restoring EED expression reconstitutes PRC2 function, reverses transcriptional and microenvironmental changes, and suppresses cSCC progression. Together, these findings establish RAS-dependent EED stability and PRC2 integrity as a key barrier to epidermal malignancy and reveal the potential of targeting the EED/PRC2 axis to restore epigenetic homeostasis in SCC.

Results

Uncoupling elevated EZH2 and depleted H3K27me3 through EED loss in SCC

PRC2 is an essential epigenetic regulator of cellular identity during adult tissue homeostasis. In many epithelial cancers, PRC2’s catalytic subunit EZH2 is frequently elevated and correlates with disease severity25. However, whether this elevation reflects increased PRC2 activity remains unknown.

To address this gap, we analyzed the levels of PRC2-mediated repressive histone mark, H3K27me3, in different types of human SCC and correlated them with the levels of EZH2. Strikingly, despite high EZH2 levels (Fig. 1a), we observed that H3K27me3 levels were significantly reduced in both early- and late-stage cSCC samples compared to healthy epidermis (Fig. 1b). A similar EZH2–H3K27me3 uncoupling was observed in head and neck (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), cervical (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d), and lung SCCs (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f), suggesting that this may represent a general phenomenon in different SCCs. This suggests that PRC2 function is compromised in SCC despite EZH2 overexpression, raising the question of how PRC2 dysregulation contributes to tumorigenesis.

To investigate this, we first analyzed levels of PRC2 core subunit, EED and SUZ12, in human cSCC tumors. While the levels of SUZ12 were unchanged between normal skin and cSCC (Supplementary Fig. 2a), we observed a decrease in EED levels across all stages of cSCC samples when compared to healthy epidermis (Fig. 1c). A similar reduction in EED was also observed in head and neck, cervical, and lung SCCs (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d), indicating that EED downregulation represents a common feature across multiple SCC types. We have performed statistical comparisons of EED and H3K27me3 levels between early-stage (Stage I/II) and late-stage (Stage III/IV) human SCC samples, including cSCC, LUSCC, HNSCC, and cervical SCC (Fig.1b-c and Supplementary Fig. 1b, d, f and 2b–d). Interestingly, our analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups. This result suggests that the reduction of EED and H3K27me3 is an early event that occurs during tumor initiation and persists throughout tumorigenesis. To further analyze the correlation between H3K27me3 and EED levels, we employed logistic regression statistical analysis and observed a significant correlation between decreased EED and low H3K27me3 levels in all stages of cSCC (Fig. 1d), linking EED depletion and H3K27me3 loss.

To explore how this affects gene regulation, we performed H3K27me3 Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) using cells from normal human epidermis and human primary cSCC tumors. We identified 4804 epidermal-specific genomic loci that lost H3K27me3 demarcation in cSCC compared to normal human epidermis (Fig. 1e). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that epidermal-specific genes with reduced H3K27me3 occupancy in cSCC are enriched for several critical biological processes related to cancer progression, including cell differentiation, proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Notably, several known SCC-signature genes that are critical for cSCC tumorigenesis also showed a decrease in H3K27me3 demarcation, including those involved in cell proliferation (HMGA240, MYCN41), tumor initiation and progression (PITX142, SOX243,44, and PTHLH45), and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (TWIST146, VIM47) (Fig. 1f). To assess PRC2 chromatin occupancy, we performed CUT&RUN profiling of the core PRC2 subunits EZH2, EED and SUZ12 alongside H3K27me3 in human epidermis and human SCC tumors generated by xenograft transplanation of A431 SCC cells. We isolated SCC tumor and human epidermal cells by performing fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS) and selected for SCC cells (α6-integrinhighβ1-integrinhigh) and epidermal cells (Epi; E-cadherin+α6-integrin+) (Supplementary Fig. 3a and 11a). We observed a marked reduction in the chromatin occupancy of EED, SUZ12, and EZH2 at epidermal-specific genomic loci and SCC-signature genes, which also exhibited loss of H3K27me3 demarcation in SCC, compared to normal epidermal controls (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). Notably, while EED occupancy was also broadly reduced, a subset of loci exhibited relative enrichment of EED despite the concomitant loss of other PRC2 subunits and H3K27me3. These findings indicate that disruption of coordinated PRC2 complex occupancy, rather than uniform loss of individual components, underlies the reduced H3K27me3 landscape observed in SCC.

To determine whether this epigenetic mechanism is conserved, we examined murine cSCC tumors. We induced cSCC in the mouse back skin using a well-established protocol, administering a potent carcinogen, 7,12-Dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA)48,49. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining showed that H3K27me3 was significantly reduced in mouse cSCC compared to normal epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 4a). We also observed that EED levels significantly decreased, while EZH2 and SUZ12 remained expressed, in murine cSCC, similar to those in human cSCC (Supplementary Fig. 4b-d). To examine if the reduction of H3K27me3 levels on cSCC-signature genes also occurs in mouse cSCC tumors compared to normal interfollicular epidermis (IFE), we isolated cSCC tumor cells by performing FACS and selected for cSCC cells (α6-integrinhighβ1-integrinhigh) and interfollicular epidermal cells (IFE; EpCAM+Sca1+α6-integrinhigh) used as controls23 (Supplementary Figs. 4e and 11b). H3K27me3 CUT&RUN revealed a similar depletion of H3K27me3 at SCC-signature genes in cSCC cells compared to IFE (Supplementary Fig. 4f, g). By performing RT-qPCR, we confirmed that loss of H3K27me3 led to upregulation of SCC-signature genes in cSCC compared to IFE (Supplementary Fig. 4h).

Together, these results reveal that EED loss underlies H3K27me3 depletion, explaining the apparent discrepancy between EZH2 overexpression and reduced PRC2 activity in SCC. This finding redefines the question from how loss of PRC2 integrity, mediated by EED depletion, drives epigenetic reprogramming and malignant progression.

Oncogenic RAS activation promotes EED proteasomal degradation and destabilizes the PRC2 complex

Since EED depletion represents a key mechanism of PRC2 dysfunction in SCC, we next investigated whether common oncogenic drivers in this cancer type contribute to EED downregulation. Oncogenic mutations in TP53, NOTCH, and RAS genes frequently occur in human cSCC and in DMBA-induced murine cSCC, and their downstream signaling pathways are critical for modulating various biological processes, including kinase activity, protein expression, and protein–protein interactions 11,50–52. To test if any of these oncogenic mutations contribute to diminished EED levels, we analyzed mice with epidermis-specific expression of active mutant Hras (HrasG12V), p53 knockout (p53KO), and Notch signaling suppression using dominant-negative Mastermind-like1 (dnMAML1)53. EED and H3K27me3 levels were unchanged in the p53KO and dnMAML1 epidermis relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 5a-f), whereas both were significantly reduced in the HRASG12V-expressing epidermis (Fig. 2a-c and Supplementary Fig. 5g). Notably, EZH2 levels were significantly upregulated, and SUZ12 levels remained unchanged in the HRASG12V-expressing epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 5h-i), consistent with our observations in the cSCC tumors. To test whether this mechanism is specific to HRAS or a general feature of RAS activation, we examined epidermis expressing the oncogenic KRASG12D mutant, another common driver in cSCC52. IF results indicated that EED and H3K27me3 were both significantly reduced in the KRASG12D-expressing epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 5j–l), similar to the HRASG12V-expressing epidermis. We concluded that the oncogenic RAS signaling causes EED downregulation without affecting other PRC2 subunits.

To examine the mechanism underlying this regulation, we established an inducible CreER-HrasG12V keratinocyte cell line, where expression of HRASG12V is induced by adding 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4OH-T). We confirmed that induction of HrasG12V activated its downstream ERK signaling pathway as evidenced by increased phospho-ERK levels (Fig. 2d). HrasG12V induction led to a significant reduction in EED levels (Fig. 2d), recapitulating in vivo findings (Fig. 2b). RT–qPCR analysis showed that Eed mRNA levels were unchanged, suggesting that RAS signaling affects EED post-transcriptionally (Supplementary Fig. 5m).

Given that RAS signaling can promote proteasomal protein degradation54,55, we tested whether RAS may similarly drive EED degradation. Treatment with proteasomal inhibitor MG132 but not lysosomal inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1) restored EED protein levels in HRASG12V-expressing cells (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, treatment with Lonafarnib, a RAS inhibitor, blocked RAS activation in HRASG12V-expressing cells and increased EED levels (Fig. 2e), suggesting that active RAS promotes EED decrease via proteasomal degradation. Immunoprecipitation (IP) assays revealed increased EED ubiquitination in HRASG12V-expressing cells compared to control (Fig. 2f), confirming that RAS activation promotes ubiquitin-mediated EED degradation.

Because EED functions as the scaffold protein that stabilizes the PRC2 complex56, we tested whether EED loss affects complex integrity. IP of EZH2 showed that the association of EZH2 with EED and SUZ12 was significantly reduced in HRASG12V-expressing cells compared to the control (Fig. 2g), indicating that EED depletion destabilizes PRC2.

Together, these findings show that the expression of active RAS induces proteasomal degradation of EED, leading to PRC2 destabilization and loss of H3K27me3, thereby connecting oncogenic signaling to epigenetic collapse in SCC.

Loss of EED/H3K27me3 disrupts epidermal homeostasis and cooperates with oncogenic events to drive aggressive cSCC

To determine the effects of EED loss on epidermal homeostasis and tumorigenesis, we used K14-CreERT2 Eedfl/fl (Eed iKO) mice, in which topical application of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4OH-T) efficiently ablates Eed in epidermal progenitor cells. We induced Eed iKO at postnatal day 50 (P50) and collected skin samples at 5 months of age (5 M). We confirmed a significant loss of EED and H3K27me3 in Eed-null epidermal cells compared to controls, while no changes in EZH2 and SUZ12 were observed (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Although Eed iKO mice did not spontaneously develop tumors, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) analysis revealed epidermal hyperplasia in Eed iKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 6c). IF analysis of the proliferation marker, Ki67, showed increased proliferation of basal cells in Eed iKO epidermis (Fig. 3c). We also observed early signs of tissue transformation as marker KRT657 was induced in Eed iKO epidermis (Fig. 3d). To elucidate how the loss of H3K27me3 in epidermal basal cells results in hyperplasia, we tested whether there is an imbalance between basal cell renewal and differentiation in Eed-null epidermis using the EdU-BrdU pulse-chase incorporation method58. We stained for KRT10 (K10), a differentiation marker, and quantified the proportion of K10⁻ progenitors within the EdU⁺BrdU⁻ population (Supplementary Fig. 6d). The progenitor renewal rate was significantly higher in Eed iKO epidermis (~0.8) than in controls (~0.6), indicating that loss of EED/H3K27me3 enhances progenitor renewal and delays differentiation (Fig. 3e).

To identify the transcriptional program deregulated in Eed-null hyperplastic epidermis, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on FACS-enriched IFE cells from Eed iKO and control mice (Supplementary Figs. 6e and 11c). Differential gene expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of 268 genes and downregulation of 61 genes in Eed-null epidermal IFE cells (Supplementary Fig. 6f). H3K27me3 CUT&RUN performed on control epidermal IFE cells showed that 59% of genes upregulated in Eed-null basal cells were H3K27me3 targets (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed enrichment in cell proliferation, consistent with the hyperplastic phenotype observed in Eed iKO epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 6h). Notably, several SCC-associated oncogenes involved in proliferation, cancer stemness, EMT and angiogenesis, including Pthlh and Pitx1, were upregulated in Eed-null epidermal IFE cells (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Fig. 6i).

Although the epidermis in Eed iKO mice did not progress beyond hyperplasia, Eed-null basal cells exhibit an early tumor transformation phenotype, including increasing proliferation and upregulation of several cSCC-associated oncogenes. This suggests that loss of EED/H3K27me3 might create a permissive chromatin state that cooperates with oncogenic mutations to promote tumorigenesis. To test this, we subjected control and Eed iKO mice to weekly DMBA treatment for five months (Fig. 4a). Although both control-DMBA (Ctrl-D) and Eed iKO-DMBA (Eed iKO-D) mice developed visible tumors (Supplementary Fig. 7a), Eed iKO-D mice exhibited earlier onset, higher tumor incidence, and significantly reduced tumor-free survival (Fig. 4b, c). H&E analysis showed that Eed iKO-D tumors presented a more aggressive phenotype when compared to Ctrl-D tumors (Fig. 4d). IF analysis revealed that EED and H3K27me3 were significantly reduced in the Eed iKO-D compared to Ctrl-D tumors (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Differentiation markers, KRT10 and KLF4, were dramatically reduced in Eed iKO tumors (Supplementary Fig. 7d), suggesting that the loss of Eed impairs differentiation during tumorigenesis, consistent with our observation in homeostatic epidermis (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 6d). In contrast, levels of cSCC markers, KRT1757, PITX1, and leptin receptor (LEPR)59, were significantly upregulated in Eed iKO-D tumors compared to Ctrl-D tumors (Supplementary Fig. 7e-g). Moreover, Eed iKO-D tumors exhibited a more disorganized staining pattern for β4-integrin and Laminin 5, and upregulated EMT markers such as TWIST1 and Vimentin (VIM), suggesting disruption of basement membrane integrity and increased metastatic potential (Supplementary Fig. 7e, h–j). To determine whether loss of EED/H3K27me3 enhances tumor-propagating capacity, we FACS-isolated tumor-propagating α6-integrinhigh β1-integrinhigh cells from both Eed iKO-D and Ctrl-D tumors as described in a previous study42 (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Following subcutaneous transplantation into Nude mice, Eed iKO-D–derived cells formed significantly larger tumors than Ctrl-D cells (Fig. 4e). To determine the frequency of tumor-initiating cells (TICs), we performed serial dilution transplantation assays followed by Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis (ELDA)60. The estimated TIC frequency was significantly higher in Eed iKO-D mice (1 in 27.6 cells) compared to Ctrl-D mice (1 in 97.6 cells), indicating that Eed loss enhances tumor-initiating potential (Fig. 4f).

Loss of EED/H3K27me3 increases aggressive tumor-specific keratinocyte cell state and enhances metastatic progression

To identify cellular and transcriptional mechanisms underlying the accelerated cSCC tumorigenesis in Eed iKO-D mice, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq; n = 3 per condition). 18,468 cells were integrated across conditions (Ctrl-D = 7456 cells; Eed iKO-D = 11,012 cells), followed by unsupervised clustering and marker-based annotation, enabling the identification of tumor keratinocytes and various stromal cell types, including fibroblasts, macrophages, endothelial cells, and immune cells (Fig. 5a-c). Upon re-clustering tumor keratinocytes, we identified four major sub-populations in both Ctrl-D and Eed iKO-D tumors, each characterized by distinct representative genes: (1) basal tumor keratinocytes (KCs) (Col17a1, Col4a5), (2) cycling tumor KCs (Mki67, Cdk1), (3) differentiating tumor KCs (Krt1, Krt10), and (4) tumor-specific keratinocytes (TSK) (Mmp10, Lamc2) populations (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 8a). TSK population and TSK-driven genes are implicated in cancer stemness, EMT, and intercellular interaction, and play critical roles in tumor progression, immunosuppression, and metastasis61. Interestingly, we observed a three-fold increase in the TSK population in Eed iKO-D compared to the control (34% in Eed iKO-D vs. 11% in Ctrl-D) and a reduction in the cycling population (24% in Eed iKO-D vs. 45% in Ctrl-D), suggesting altered cell state dynamics in Eed iKO-D tumors (Fig. 5e). To determine whether H3K27me3 directly targets TSK-associated genes in the homeostatic epidermis, we analyzed H3K27me3 occupancy on these genes and observed that 40% of mouse TSK genes and 25% of human TSK genes are demarcated by H3K27me3 in normal epidermal basal cells (Supplementary Fig. 8b). To investigate the mechanistic basis for this specific cell-state transition, we analyzed the expression of H3K27me3 target genes across all tumor keratinocyte clusters. Our analysis revealed that the TSK gene program contains a significantly higher number of H3K27me3-marked genes compared to the basal, cycling, or differentiating compartments (Fig. 5f). Upon Eed depletion, the TSK-specific genes undergo dramatic de-repression and become the dominant transcriptional signature across all tumor keratinocyte clusters (Fig. 5f), suggesting that cells now express this invasive TSK program alongside their proliferative features. In contrast, H3K27me3 target genes associated with the basal and differentiated states showed relatively stable expression profiles across conditions (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Data 1). Indeed, we observed that H3K27me3-marked TSK genes, such as Pthlh, Pdpn and Igfbp2 were upregulated in the TSK population (Supplementary Fig. 8c). We also confirmed the significant upregulation of PTHLH protein in the human SCC and Eed iKO-D tumors (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). To determine the biological processes involved in Eed iKO-D tumors, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on the cycling tumor KC and TSK populations in Eed iKO-D versus Ctrl-D tumors. This analysis revealed that Eed loss drives highly divergent, compartment-specific transcriptional programs rather than a uniform global shift across the tumor KCs. In Eed iKO-D cycling KCs were predominantly enriched for pathways associated with protein transport, endocytosis, and ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic processes, alongside a notable DNA damage response signature (Fig. 5g). These findings suggest that, under conditions of epigenetic dysregulation, proliferative tumor KCs prioritize protein quality control and metabolic adaptation to sustain rapid cell cycling. In contrast, the expanded TSK population in Eed iKO-D tumors adopted a distinct aggressive phenotype focused on microenvironmental remodeling. The top-enriched pathways in these TSK cells focused on extrinsic processes, including cell proliferation, regulation of cell motility, and cell-cell adhesion or angiogenesis pathways (Fig. 5h). This suggests that TSKs in Eed-deficient tumors drive tissue remodeling and invasive behavior. Collectively, these comparative GO analyses demonstrate that epigenetic disruption via Eed loss forces the tumor’s functional landscape into two specialized states: an aggressive, microenvironment-remodeling role for emergent TSKs and a stress-management role for the cycling tumor KCs.

Given the increased aggressive TSK population and the upregulation of EMT-related genes in both Eed iKO epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 6h-i) and Eed iKO-D tumors (Supplementary Fig. 7h-i), we investigated whether the loss of EED/H3K27me3 promotes cSCC metastasis. We collected lymph nodes proximal to cSCC tumors and conducted a histological analysis. H&E staining revealed significantly more metastatic cells in the lymph nodes from Eed iKO-D mice compared to Ctrl-D mice (Fig. 5i). Moreover, 30% of Eed iKO-D lymph nodes contained metastatic tumor cells, whereas none were observed in lymph nodes of Ctrl-D mice (Fig. 5j). The metastatic cells in Eed iKO-D lymph nodes lacked H3K27me3 and expressed epidermal marker KRT14 (Fig. 5k), confirming their origin from Eed iKO-D cSCCs. Together, these results demonstrate that loss of EED/H3K27me3 reprograms keratinocytes into a tumor-specific, aggressive state, expands the TSK population, and enhances metastatic dissemination in cSCC.

EED/H3K27me3 deficiency promotes tumor microenvironment remodeling

Further analysis of scRNA-seq data revealed that loss of EED/H3K27me3 not only expanded the TSK state population but also reshaped the tumor microenvironment (TME) by increasing the abundance of stromal cells (Fig. 5a, b). Differential abundance analysis using a k-nearest-neighbor graph approach62 demonstrated a significant enrichment of endothelial cells, pericytes, fibroblasts, macrophages, dendritic cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) populations in Eed iKO-D tumors compared to Ctrl-D (Fig. 6a). These stromal populations have already been demonstrated to play a crucial role in fostering a pro-tumorigenic environment that promotes tumor invasion63–68. Notably, the IF analysis of macrophage marker F4/80 and CAF markers, α-SMA and PDGFRα, confirmed scRNA-seq data and revealed that macrophage and CAF populations were significantly increased in Eed iKO-D tumors compared to Ctrl-D (Fig. 6b).

To further examine TSK-TME crosstalk, we applied CellChat to conduct differential cell-cell communication analysis to compare paracrine signaling between TSK and TME across tumor types, whereby differences are tested against upregulated and downregulated ligands in sender cells and receptors in receiver cells69. Overall, Eed iKO-D TSK secreted a broader range of paracrine factors targeting five main TME populations: fibroblasts, macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, and MDSCs, compared to Ctrl-D TSK, which mainly interacted with macrophages, T cells, and MDSCs (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 8f). Coupled with the differential abundance of these populations in the Eed iKO-D setting, this observation led us to focus on signaling interactions in these populations. Pathway analysis revealed that many of the paracrine factors, including VEGF, EGF, NRG, and LAMININ-related signaling, were enriched in Eed iKO-D TSK and strongly influenced TME responses (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 8g). Importantly, H3K27me3 CUT&RUN analysis revealed that H3K27me3 normally repressed these factors in homeostatic human epidermal cells but not in human SCC (Fig. 6e). Together, these findings demonstrate that loss of EED/H3K27me3 in TSKs unleashes key paracrine factors, driving TME remodeling and creating a more permissive microenvironment for tumor progression and metastasis.

Re-expression of EED attenuates SCC tumorigenesis by restoring PRC2 function and normal epidermal programs

Our findings indicate that diminished levels of EED and H3K27me3 promote cSCC tumorigenesis, leading us to hypothesize that re-establishing EED/PRC2 function in cSCC could mitigate tumor progression. To test this hypothesis, we engineered mouse cSCC cells derived from DMBA-induced tumors to express GFP, Eed-GFP (Eed), or a mutant form of EED, Eed-R302G-GFP (Eed-R302G), which disrupts the interaction of EED with EZH2 and consequently affects the establishment of H3K27me339,70. To confirm that re-expression of wild-type Eed, but not the Eed-R302G mutant, reconstitutes the PRC2 complex, we performed an IP for EZH2 and observed that both EED and SUZ12 were enriched in the EZH2 immunoprecipitate in Eed-expressing cells but not in GFP or Eed-R302G cells (Fig. 7a). These results suggest that the reintroduction of EED results in the formation of the PRC2 complex in SCC cells.

Next, we conducted proliferation and colony-formation assays to assess whether the induction of a stable PRC2 complex in Eed-expressing cells could attenuate SCC growth. Cells expressing Eed showed significantly reduced cell proliferation and colony numbers compared to those expressing GFP or Eed-R302G, suggesting that re-expression of Eed, in conjunction with functional PRC2 assembly, inhibits SCC cell growth in vitro (Fig. 7b, c). To investigate the impact of Eed re-introduction on tumorigenesis in vivo, we transplanted GFP, Eed, and Eed-R302G-expressing SCC cells into Nude mice by performing subcutaneous injection and monitored tumor size over time. Tumors from Eed-expressing SCC cells showed a significant size reduction compared to those from GFP or Eed-R302G SCC cells after three weeks of transplantation (Fig. 7d). H&E analysis of tumors revealed a marked reduction of tumor keratinocyte aggregation and an increased area of keratinization in Eed-expressing tumors compared to GFP and Eed-R302G (Supplementary Fig. 9a). IF analysis of collected tumors showed that EED was detected in both Eed and the Eed-R302G-expressing tumors, whereas no detectable EED levels were observed in the GFP control tumors (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Additionally, we analyzed if the reduction in tumor size observed in tumors derived from Eed-expressing SCC cells resulted from changes in cell renewal or differentiation. Indeed, we found that the number of Ki67-positive cells was significantly reduced in Eed-expressing tumors compared to those expressing GFP and Eed-R302G (Fig. 8a). In contrast, the key differentiation factor KLF4 was significantly upregulated in Eed-expressing tumors, along with increased expression of the differentiation marker KRT10 (Fig. 8a, b). Remarkably, re-expressing Eed not only restored the balance between proliferation and differentiation in tumor keratinocytes, but we also found that the number of macrophages was significantly reduced in Eed-expressing tumors compared to GFP and Eed-R302G (Fig. 8c). Reduction of α-SMA staining in Eed-expressing tumors revealed that the number of CAFs decreased, while the PDGFRα-only fibroblasts remained in Eed-expressing tumors (Fig. 8d). Altogether, it shows that re-expressing EED in SCC cells reconstitutes PRC2 function, restores H3K27me3-dependent repression, and attenuates tumorigenesis by suppressing proliferation, promoting differentiation, and limiting microenvironmental support for tumor growth.

Re-expression of EED re-establishes H3K27me3 on key oncogenic and paracrine loci in SCC

To identify genomic regions that regain H3K27me3 upon EED re-expression in SCC cells, we performed CUT&RUN with an antibody against H3K27me3 on FACS-isolated GFP⁺ α6-integrinhigh β1-integrinhigh tumor propagating cells from GFP and Eed-expressing tumors (Supplementary Figs. 10a and 11e). Comparative analysis of H3K27me3 occupancy revealed increased levels of H3K27me3 at epidermal-specific gene loci in Eed-expressing cells relative to GFP controls (Fig. 9a). GO analysis showed that these H3K27me3-enriched regions were associated with pathways involved in cell differentiation, cell fate specification, and proliferation (Fig. 9b). Notably, several SCC-associated oncogenes—including Pitx1, Pthlh, Nr2f271, Lepr59, and Twist1—exhibited enhanced H3K27me3 occupancy upon EED restoration (Fig. 9c). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that expression of these oncogenes was markedly reduced in tumors derived from Eed-expressing cells compared to those expressing Eed-R302G mutant or GFP alone (Fig. 9d-f and Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). In addition, key H3K27me3-regulated paracrine signaling factors such as Nrg1, Egf, Tnc, Lama1, and Sema3 were also downregulated upon Eed re-expression (Supplementary Fig. 10d). Together, these findings demonstrate that reintroduction of functional EED/PRC2 restores H3K27me3-dependent repression of oncogenic, proliferative, and secretory programs, thereby effectively suppressing SCC tumorigenesis.

Discussion

In this study, we establish that the scaffolding PRC2 subunit EED—and, by extension, the integrity of the PRC2 complex— acts as an essential epigenetic gatekeeper on SCC. Across diverse human SCCs and complementary murine models, we demonstrate that global H3K27me3 is markedly depleted despite persistently high levels of EZH2, unleashing oncogenic transcriptional programs that drive tumorigenesis. Mechanistically, oncogenic RAS signaling downregulates EED, destabilizes PRC2, and reduces H3K27me3, leading to derepression of key SCC oncogenic loci. Genetic depletion of Eed in epidermal progenitors in vivo disrupts the proliferation–differentiation balance and, upon carcinogen exposure, drives invasive SCC. Single-cell RNA-seq pinpoints the consequences of this epigenetic collapse, resulting in an expansion of aggressive tumor-specific keratinocytes and a paracrine-mediated remodeling of the tumor microenvironment that collectively accelerate invasion and metastasis. Remarkably, re-expressing wild-type EED in SCC reassembles PRC2, reinstates H3K27me3, rebalances proliferation with differentiation, and attenuates tumor growth. Together, these findings position EED/PRC2 as a central epigenetic guardian of epidermal homeostasis and highlight PRC2 restoration as a compelling therapeutic strategy for SCC.

Although elevated EZH2 expression is a hallmark of many solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, our survey of head-and-neck, cervical, lung, and cutaneous SCCs reveals that high EZH2 alone does not ensure functional PRC2 activity. Despite EZH2 abundance, global H3K27me3 is profoundly reduced in SCC, pointing to an underlying destabilization of the complex itself. This paradox echoes recent studies showing that PRC2 plays a more nuanced and context-dependent role in human cancer72–74. Our work shows that the downregulation of EED alone is sufficient to destabilize the PRC2 complex, driving the hyperactivation of oncogenic circuits in SCC. Thus, while EZH2 upregulation is often viewed as oncogenic, our results demonstrate that EED and the H3K27me3 axis, rather than EZH2 expression alone, are the true determinants of PRC2 function and tumor-suppressive capacity.

A growing body of literature highlights the crucial role of activated Ras signaling in initiating and promoting cutaneous SCC, primarily through the canonical MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways that drive proliferation, survival, and metastasis15,75. We show that active oncogenic Ras signaling downregulates EED, which destabilizes the PRC2 complex and reduces global H3K27me3 levels, thereby alleviating repressive constraints on tumorigenic gene networks. Consequently, our study broadens the understanding of how oncogenic Ras not only initiates key growth and survival pathways but also modifies the epigenome to drive malignant transformation.

A recent study by Parreno et al. showed that transient depletion of Polycomb group (PcG) proteins in Drosophila is sufficient to induce stable, irreversible tumorigenesis in the absence of genetic mutations29. These epigenetically initiated cancers persist even after restoration of PcG protein levels driven by sustained upregulation of key oncogenic pathways that promote proliferation and block differentiation. Interestingly, our data reveal that the decrease of EED in mammalian epidermal keratinocytes establishes a permissive epigenetic state characterized by global depletion of H3K27me3 and elevated expression of pro-tumorigenic genes. EED-deficient keratinocytes display disrupted balances between proliferation and differentiation, yet they do not spontaneously form tumors. This underscores that, although EED deficiency removes key repressive barriers, it is insufficient to drive full malignant transformation on its own. When we introduced additional oncogenic lesions via DMBA treatment—which induces mutations in Ras and other cancer-relevant pathways such as p5348,49—EED-deficient epidermal cells progressed to form aggressive and metastatic SCC. This finding suggests a synergistic interplay: while EED/PRC2 deficiency primes basal keratinocytes by lifting epigenetic constraints on oncogenic loci, the subsequent mutagenic insults from DMBA provide a critical “second hit” that fully unleashes tumorigenesis. This cooperative model mirrors findings in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where PRC2 loss synergizes with KRAS activation to promote inflammation, EMT, and tumor progression76. Collectively, these studies establish a conserved paradigm in which oncogenic Ras signaling and PRC2 dysfunction intersect to unlock aggressive tumor states and remodel the tumor microenvironment. Notably, we show that reintroduction of wild-type EED into established SCC restores H3K27me3 levels, reestablishes transcriptional repression, and suppresses tumor growth. Our findings highlight both a conserved tumor-suppressive function of PRC2 and a key mechanistic basis for oncogenic Ras drives EED degradation, thereby destabilizing PRC2. In mammals, PRC2 dysfunction is not an exclusive initiator of cancer but serves as a potent enabling factor within a multi-step oncogenic process, and the potential reversibility of PRC2-driven phenotypes in mammalian cancer.

Our scRNA-seq data show that EED deficiency reprograms both the tumor keratinocytes and the surrounding microenvironment to promote SCC tumorigenesis. EED-deficient tumors are enriched for a TSK population that expresses high levels of EMT genes and has been proposed to promote tumor invasiveness61. While tumor heterogeneity is known, including in SCC, our studies illustrate that the PRC2 complex plays a pivotal role in regulating tumor cell state transitions. Loss of EED disrupts this epigenetic control, fostering a permissive state that promotes a TSK cell state, malignant reprogramming and aggressive cancer phenotypes.

EED-deficient tumors also secrete a distinct repertoire of PRC2-regulated secretory proteins that actively recruit macrophages and CAFs, further reinforcing a tumor-supportive microenvironment. These integrated interactions between epithelial cells and the tumor microenvironment provide insight into how EED/PRC2 normally curbs the mutualistic signals driving cancer progression. The altered secretory profile of EED-deficient tumors might also indicate that PRC2 disruption contributes to immune evasion by recruiting immunosuppressive cells such as macrophages and altering cytokine and chemokine networks. Additionally, these secreted factors could promote angiogenesis, supporting tumor vascularization and growth. Future studies should delve into the molecular mechanisms linking PRC2 status with immune cell modulation and angiogenic processes, as targeting these pathways may further enhance therapeutic strategies against SCC.

Our work underscores that, although EZH2 is frequently overexpressed in various tumor types, tumor cells often fail to maintain wild-type levels of H3K27me3. This finding has critical therapeutic implications. For instance, while EZH2 inhibitors like the FDA-approved Tazemetostat77 are designed to counteract the oncogenic activity of EZH2, their efficacy may be limited or even counterproductive in tumors where PRC2 integrity is already compromised. In such settings, further inhibition of EZH2 could exacerbate epigenetic deregulation, potentially promoting tumor progression rather than suppressing it. Therefore, our data suggest that any therapeutic strategy targeting EZH2 must be carefully tailored to account for the broader epigenetic status in the tumor. Evaluating PRC2 integrity by analyzing EED and H3K27me3 levels in patient samples may serve as a critical prognostic or predictive biomarker and help stratify patients who could benefit from targeted epigenetic therapies.

A significant advancement of our work is demonstrating that the exogenous reintroduction of EED, but not a mutant form that cannot reconstitute a functional PRC2 complex, reinstates H3K27me3 and can reverse SCC tumorigenesis. This finding indicates that the signaling cues necessary to recruit the PRC2 complex and re-establish H3K27me3 are preserved in fully developed SCC tumor cells where EED/H3K27me3 levels are low. Furthermore, we demonstrate that re-establishing EED/H3K27me3 is sufficient to suppress oncogenic gene expression and reduce SCC tumorigenesis. This is important as it highlights the therapeutic potential of the EED/H3K27me3 axis for treating SCC. Preventing EED degradation, especially in tumors where Ras activation drives EED downregulation, can restore global PRC2 repression and alleviate tumorigenesis. This perspective aligns with the growing consensus that selective manipulation of epigenetic complexes can be a powerful strategy for controlling cancer78,79. Moreover, the dynamic epigenetic plasticity revealed in our study emphasizes the potential of integrating epigenetic-targeting strategies with established therapeutic modalities. Since epigenetic reprogramming can promote resistance to treatments such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy80, combining these standard approaches with agents that restore or enhance PRC2 function may yield synergistic benefits. Such combination therapies could reduce the permissive epigenetic environment that supports tumor survival and recurrence, thereby enhancing overall treatment efficacy in aggressive SCC.

Together, these data position the EED/H3K27me3 axis at the core of a critical tumor-suppressive mechanism in the epidermis. By elucidating how EED/H3K27me3 loss synergizes with oncogenic pathways, driving both intrinsic transcriptional alterations in keratinocytes and extrinsic changes in the tumor microenvironment, our study refines the current understanding of PRC2’s context-dependent roles in cancer. The ability to reverse these changes by reinstating EED points toward potential opportunities for targeted intervention in aggressive cutaneous SCC.

Cell lines and cell cultures

Drug treatments and induction of genetic recombination

For topical induction of Eed ablation or HrasG12V induction using K14-CreERT2 mice, 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4OH-Tam, Millipore Sigma) was dissolved in absolute ethanol (Millipore Sigma) to a concentration of 10 mg/ml. Mice at postnatal day 50 (P50) received 100 μl (1 mg/ml) of 4OH-Tam solution applied to the shaved dorsal skin once daily for 4 consecutive days. Control mice received an equal volume of absolute ethanol. For chemical carcinogenesis, 15 mg of 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA; Millipore Sigma) was dissolved in 4.5 ml acetone to prepare 100X DMBA stock solution. A 1X DMBA solution was prepared by diluting 150 μl of the stock in 7 ml of acetone. Mice received 200 μl of the 1X DMBA solution applied topically to the shaved dorsal skin once weekly for up to 20 weeks and were monitored weekly for tumor development. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached or exceeded 1 cm in any dimension.

To induce HRASG12V expression in cultured CreER-HrasG12V- expressing keratinocytes, cells were treated with 1 μM 4OH-Tam (in DMSO) once daily for 5 days, while vehicle-treated (DMSO) cells served as controls. For degradation pathway analysis, CreER-HrasG12V- expressing or control keratinocytes were treated with 10 µM MG132 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 µM bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), or 10 µM lonafarnib (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 24 h, respectively, before cell lysate collection and analyses.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and microscopy

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were deparaffinized in Citrus Clearing Solvent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%, and 50%). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer, pH 6.0. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treating sections with 1% H2O2 in methanol for 10 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked using 2.5% normal horse serum (Vector Laboratories) at room temperature (RT) for 30 min. Sections were subsequently incubated overnight at RT with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-EZH2 antibodies (1:100, Cell Signaling, 5246s), rabbit anti-SUZ12 antibodies (1:50, Cell Signaling, 3737s), rabbit anti-H3K27me3 antibodies (1:250, Cell Signaling, 9733s), rabbit anti-EED (1:100, Cell Signaling, 85322s), and rabbit anti-PTHLH (1:100, Novus Biologicals, NBP1-56322). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with a secondary anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated polymer (ImmPRESS Polymer Detection Kit, Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at RT, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Signal visualization was performed using a DAB substrate kit (Vector Laboratories). Tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei, dehydrated with graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 95% and 100%), and mounted using Permount mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Brightfield images were acquired on a DM5500 slide microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica Microsystems GmbH). Micrographs were processed and analyzed using Adobe Photoshop (v25.7) and NIH ImageJ software (v2.14.0/1.54f).

EdU and BrdU pulse-chase experiment

Mice were injected with 125 μg of 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in solution for 2 h. After 2 h of EdU injection, the mice were injected with 100 mg/kg of bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) for 6 h. Skin samples were collected and embedded in OCT. Sections were fixed and permeabilized as described above and followed by a 30-min incubation with a Click-iT reaction cocktail according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After EdU staining, sections were incubated with 2 N HCl at 37 °C for 30 min and were neutralized with 0.1 M sodium borate buffer for 10 min. Samples were washed twice with 3% BSA before proceeding with IF staining using primary antibodies: rat anti-BrdU (1:200, Abcam, ab6326) and rabbit anti-KRT10 (1:500, BioLegend, 905403). Cells completing the S phase during the first initial 2 h would incorporate EdU exclusively, whereas cells subsequently going through the S phase would have incorporated both EdU and BrdU. This approach involves tracking the fate of progenitor cells through the staining of the differentiation marker KRT10 (K10) to mark, thereby identifying committed cells58. The renewal rate is defined as the ratio of new K10(-) EdU(+) BrdU(-) progenitors to the total count of EdU(+) BrdU(-) progeny cells.

Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) analysis and data visualization

Normal primary human skin samples were washed once in cold 1x PBS, fat layer was removed, and the skin was then incubated in 1.26 U/ml dispase (Gibco) overnight at 4 °C. The epidermis was separated from underlying dermis and kept at liquid nitrogen until use. Human primary SCC tumor biopsies were freshly collected after surgery and kept at liquid nitrogen until use. Next, nuclei from frozen human epidermis and SCC tumor samples were isolated as previously described83. In brief, frozen tissue was placed in 1 mL of cold homogenization buffer and thawed for 5 min. The tissue was then minced into small pieces and transferred to a pre-chilled 2 mL Dounce homogenizer. Samples were homogenized using the loose-fitting (A) pestle until resistance was no longer apparent, followed by 20 strokes with the tight-fitting (B) pestle. The homogenate was centrifuged at 100 g for 1 min at 4 °C to pellet large tissue debris. The supernatant was transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, then centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei. Isolated nuclei were then subjected to H3K27me3 (Cell Signaling) CUT&RUN analysis using the CUTANA™ ChIC/CUT&RUN Kit (Epicypher) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For the H3K27me3 (Cell Signaling, 9733s), EZH2 (Cell Signaling, 5246s), EED (Active Motif, 61203) and SUZ12 (Active Motif, 39357) CUT&RUN in normal human epidermis and xenografted A431 tumors, 400,000 FACS-enriched epidermal cells and SCC cells were isolated from the human skin or A431 tumors. The H3K27me3 (Cell Signaling, 9733s) CUT&RUN in normal mouse skin and SCC, 100,000 FACS-enriched epidermal basal cells were isolated from the back skin of C57BL/6 wild-type mice. 100,000 FACS-enriched SCC cells were isolated from tumors of control-DMBA, SCC-GFP, and SCC-Eed-expressing cells following the FACS protocol described above, optimized for FACS-isolated epidermal keratinocytes23. CUT&RUN was conducted using the CUTANA™ ChIC/CUT&RUN Kit (Epicypher) following the manufacturer’s protocol for FACS-isolated epidermal keratinocytes or SCC cells. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, NEB) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos (NEB) for Illumina following the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR-amplified libraries were purified using a 1X ratio of Agencourt AMPure-XP Beads (Beckman) and eluted in 15 μl TE buffer. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq using 150 bp paired-end reads. Reads were trimmed with Trim Galore (v0.6.7) and aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) or human reference genome (Hg38) using Bowtie2 (v.2.2.5). Deduplicated reads were removed using samtools (v.1.14). Peaks were identified using a sliding window method to determine genomic regions with >1.5 mapping read enrichment over IgG control and adjusted p < 0.05. The BAM file for each replicate was converted to a TDF file for Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software. All visualization presentations were normalized by sequencing depth.

RNA extraction, RT-qPCR, and bulk RNA-seq analysis

A total of 50,000 FACS-purified cells were collected directly into RLT Plus buffer (QIAGEN). Total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (QIAGEN) and paired with on-column RNase-free DNaseI treatment (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent), and samples with RNA integrity numbers (RIN) > 8 were used in subsequent experiments. For RT-qPCR, cDNA was reverse transcribed from total RNA using qScript cDNA SuperMix (Quanta Biosciences). Samples were analyzed by RT-qPCR using LightCycler® 480 SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche) on a LightCycler 480 instrument (Roche). Primer information is available in Supplementary Data 2. For bulk RNA-seq library preparation, libraries were constructed from 50 ng of total RNA (RIN > 8) using the Universal Plus mRNA-Seq with NuQuant® (Tecan). In brief, isolated mRNA was fragmented at 94 °C for 8 min, followed by cDNA synthesis, end repair, adapter ligation, and amplification according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Final library concentration and size distribution were validated using a Qubit (Invitrogen) and Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Constructed RNA-seq libraries were sequenced at GENEWIZ (Azenta) on the Illumina Nova-seq platform, obtaining 150 nucleotide paired-end reads.

Raw paired-end reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using STAR aligner (v2.7.9a)84. Gene expression quantification for both coding and noncoding transcripts was performed using RSEM software (v1.3.3) against the Ensembl annotation (v105) to compute raw counts and transcripts per million (TPM)85. Differential expression analysis was performed for protein-coding genes using DESeq2 (v1.38.2). Low-abundance transcripts with a TPM < 1 across all samples were filtered out prior to statistical testing. Genes reaching adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log2-transformed fold change >1 (or <−1) were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The list of DEGs is available in Supplementary Data 3.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and Western blotting

For Co-IP experiment, cells were harvested in 1 ml PBS and centrifuged at 4 °C. Cell pellets were resuspended in Dignam hypotonic buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH, pH = 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1X protease inhibitor (Roche), 0.5 mM PMSF) incubated on ice for 10 minutes. After incubation, cell lysates were centrifuged for 15 min to pellet the nuclei. Resuspend the nuclei pellet with Dignam high salt buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH, pH = 7.9, 0.42 M NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 25% glycerol(v/v), 1X protease inhibitor (Roche), 0.5 mM PMSF) and rotate lysates at 4 °C for 30 min. Centrifuge cell lysates and collect the supernatant with nuclear extraction. Cell lysates were added Dignam hypotonic buffer to adjust the salt concentration to 150 mM and 0.1% NP-40. Lysates were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The following day, lysates were added Dynabeads Protein G beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and rotated at 4 °C for 2 h. Protein-beads complexes were washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH = 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol (v/v), 1% triton X-100) 3 times and eluted with EZ lysis buffer (60 mM Tris-HCl pH = 6.8, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS). Cells were collected and lysed by adding EZ lysis buffer for 10 min at 95 °C. Samples were run on 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Protein Gels (Bio-Rad). The gel was transferred to PVDF membranes at 0.3 A at 4 °C for 1 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA at RT for 1 h, then incubated with the primary antibody as following: anti-EZH2 antibodies (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 5246s), rabbit anti-SUZ12 antibodies (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 3737 s), rabbit anti-EED (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 85322s), anti-Ras (G12V Mutant) (D2H12) (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 14412s), rabbit anti-pERK (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 9101s), rabbit anti-ERK (1:5000, Cell Signaling, 9102s), mouse anti-tubulin (1:5000, Sigma-Aldrich, T402), mouse anti-Ubiquitin (P4D1) (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 3936s) at 4 °C overnight. After incubation, membranes were washed three times with PBST (PBS + 0.2% Tween-20) and incubated with secondary antibody according to the primary antibody. Membranes were washed three times with PBST, and membranes were developed with Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad).

Cell growth and colony formation assay

Cell growth assay was carried out using the WST-1 kit (Millipore Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, SCC-GFP, Eed, Eed-R302G cells were seeded at a density of 1000 cells/well in 96-well plates in E medium. WST-1 reagent was added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h before collecting the cells at each time point. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan). For colony formation assay, SCC-GFP, Eed, Eed-R302G cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells/well in 6-well plates in E medium. Colonies were fixed with 4% PFA at day 5. Cells were stained and counted with 0.2% crystal violet (Millipore Sigma). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 11.

Quantification and statistical analysis

RT-qPCR analysis

To calculate fold change of mRNA levels in epidermal cells isolated from 5 M control and Eed iKO mice or SCC cells from Ctrl-D, Eed iKO-D, GFP, Eed and Eed-R302G tumors. First, the cycle threshold (Ct) values of technical replicates of each trial were averaged. ΔCt was calculated by subtracting Gapdh Ct average from the Ct average of the gene of interest. Relative expression levels for each individual sample were determined using the formula 2^−ΔCt. To compute relative fold-changes, individual values 2^−ΔCt were normalized to the mean 2^−ΔCt value of the designated control group. The bar graph is presented as mean ± SD. At least three animals from at least two independent litters were used for each group. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 11. Primer information is provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis

Identification of significantly overrepresented functional categories was done using the DAVID tool on the default setting. Selected GO terms using the GOTERM BP DIRECT category were considered significant with p < 0.05. P-value was calculated by Fisher’s exact test. For H3K27me3 CUT&RUN, genes that showed a significant loss of H3K27me3 in human or mouse SCC cells vs. normal human or mouse skin were annotated using the same significance parameters and selected GO terms are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Genes that showed a significant gain of H3K27me3 in Eed SCC cells vs. GFP were annotated using the same significance parameters and selected GO terms are shown in Fig. 9 GO terms are available in Supplementary Data 4. For RNA-seq, genes significantly upregulated in Eed iKO vs. control epidermal basal cells were annotated using the same significance parameters and selected GO terms are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. GO terms are available in Supplementary Data 3.

Quantification of PRC2 subunits and H3K27me3 in human SCC

The mean intensity of DAB signals of EZH2, SUZ12, EED or H3K27me3 signal in the epidermis/larynx/cervix/lung normal tissues and SCC were quantified using QuPath (0.5.1-x64)86. DAB images were separated to analyze the specific emission channel. Immunohistochemistry stains were scored as “Weak” in the complete absence of staining or weak staining in less than 50% of the area under assessment. A score of “Strong” was defined as moderate or strong expression in more than 50% of the area under assessment.

scRNA-seq analysis

Cells passing QC filters (Genes per cell > 200; Percent mitochondrial counts <10; percent hemoglobin counts <10) were retained for further analysis. Using the Seurat v4 integration workflow87, the data was normalized, scaled while regressing out counts per cell and percent mitochondrial expression, followed by PCA and CCA integration across conditions. Next, 21 unsupervised clusters were identified with the Seurat implementation of the Louvain algorithm with a resolution of 0.6. These clusters were then manually annotated based on cluster-defining gene markers and canonical gene expression markers from the literature, yielding 15 cell types. Keratinocytes were then subclustered using the Seurat workflow with a resolution of 0.6, and we identified putative pilosebaceous clusters based on expression of known hair follicle markers Krt14, Lgr5 or transcription factors Krt15, Lhx2, Sox9. Tumor keratinocytes were then subclustered using the Seurat workflow with a resolution of 0.6 and manually curated into 4 populations using the same approach described above. Tumor-specific keratinocytes were identified using markers such as Mmp10, Pthlh, and Il24 as described in Ji et al.61 Differential expression analysis between clusters was performed using the FindAllMarkers function within the Seurat R package. To identify cluster-specific markers, we applied a log2 fold-change threshold of 0.25 and a minimum detection percentage of 0.25, with all other parameters set to their default values. The resulting gene lists were further refined by filtering for adjusted p-values < 0.05 to ensure statistical robustness. For Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, the top differentially expressed genes from specific clusters of interest, such as the tumor-specific keratinocyte population in Eed iKO-D versus control tumors, were analyzed using the NIH DAVID Functional Annotation tool. Biological process terms were specifically identified using the GOTERM BP DIRECT category. The enrichment results were then visualized as bar plots representing the -log10 of the adjusted p-value using the R package ggplot2. To identify differentially abundant populations in the Eed iKO-D versus Ctrl-D samples, we employed miloR, an approach that uses k-nearest neighbor graphs to test differential abundance in single-cell RNA-sequencing data62. Finally, we applied CellChat69 to predict ligand-receptor interactions in the Eed iKO-D and Ctrl-D samples and used the CellChat comparison workflow to identify interactions that were upregulated in Eed iKO-D samples versus Ctrl-D. Briefly, CellChat performs differential expression analysis between Eed iKO-D and Ctrl-D for each cell group and identifies upregulated and downregulated signaling based on the fold change of ligands in the sender cells and receptors in the receiver cells.

Quantification of lymph node metastasis

Two draining inguinal lymph nodes were harvested per mouse from Ctrl-D and Eed iKO-D mice and embedded in OCT. Lymph node OCT blocks were serially sectioned and evaluated using H&E and KRT14 immunostaining to confirm metastatic cells. The analysis included n = 11 mice for the Ctrl-D group and n = 14 for the Eed iKO-D group.

Statistical and Reproducibility

All data from every experiment were included for analysis and measurements, and statistical methods were not used to predetermine sample size and no data were excluded from the analyses. No randomization or blinding was performed in this study. Measurements were taken from independent samples, unless stated otherwise. For statistical analyses, GraphPad Prism 11 was used. Statistical p-values were defined in each figure and figure legend. Sample size is indicated in figure legends, and statistical methods are indicated in the quantification and statistical analysis paragraph. To determine the statistical significance between 2 groups, a two-tailed Mann-Whitney test is used. To determine statistical significance among more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple comparisons. All data in bar graphs are presented as mean ± SD. The number of biological replicates used for comparison is indicated in each figure and figure legend. Male and female mice were used interchangeably, and sex was not treated as a biological variable. Representative images or blots are representative of n ≥ 3 independent experiments performed on different biological samples with similar results.
