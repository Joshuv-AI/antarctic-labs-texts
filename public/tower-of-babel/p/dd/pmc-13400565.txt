Factors associated with acute radiation-induced nocturia in localized prostate cancer treated with proton beam therapy
Authors: Keita Yoshizaki, Yoji Tabata, Koji Yoshioka, Ryosaku Yamada, Yuya Azuma, Kazuya Inoue, Hitoshi Yoshimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe examined factors associated with acute radiation-induced nocturia (ARIN) in patients with localized prostate cancer (LPC) treated with proton beam therapy (PBT). We included 140 patients who underwent PBT for LPC. Of these, 80 patients with implanted hydrogel spacers were treated with moderately hypo-fractionated proton therapy (MHPT) (60–66 GyRBE in 20–22 fractions) and 60 patients without hydrogel spacers were treated with normo-fractionated proton therapy (NFPT) (74–78 GyRBE in 37–39 fractions). In-room computed tomography was performed for each fraction to monitor target position. Prostate–rectum boundary matching was adopted to effectively reduce rectal dose, and position translation and pitch angle corrections were performed depending on rectal filling changes. ARIN was evaluated based on the nocturnal voiding frequencies. We first performed multivariate logistic regression (MLR) analysis to identify significant factors for ARIN. Subsequently, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed as a sensitivity analysis to address potential confounding and to assess the robustness of the findings. The analyses showed that 45/140 patients experienced ARIN during/immediately after PBT. MLR analysis showed that ‘treatment regimen’ (MHPT with spacer vs NFPT without spacer) was statistically significantly associated with ARIN (OR: 0.25, 95% CI: 0.09–0.67, P < 0.05). This association remained significant after PSM. In conclusion, while ‘treatment regimen’ was statistically associated with ARIN, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting a composite treatment strategy within the limitations of a retrospective analysis, rather than a causal relationship.Keywords: proton beam therapy, prostate cancer, acute radiation-induced nocturia, propensity score matching

INTRODUCTION

Prostate cancer has become the most common cancer among Japanese men as of 2022, largely due to the widespread adoption of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening [1–3]. Treatment options for localized prostate cancer (LPC) include active surveillance, surgery, radiotherapy (RT), and hormone therapy. Among these, RT is a well-established definitive treatment that preserves both urinary and sexual functions [4, 5]. However, radiation exposure to the bladder and urethra surrounding the prostate can result in acute genitourinary (GU) toxicity, which may develop during treatment [6–8]. Therefore, new treatment strategies are required to minimize toxicity.

Proton beam therapy (PBT) has rapidly developed in recent years, with two main delivery techniques: passive scattering and pencil beam scanning. Given the physical properties of the Bragg peak, which is a major feature of PBT, doses to organs at risk (OARs) can theoretically be reduced compared with X-ray–based radiotherapy techniques [9–11]. In a comprehensive meta-analysis, Hickam et al. reported that PBT was associated with lower risks of both acute and late GU toxicity compared with intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) [12]. However, its impact on patient-reported quality of life (QOL) remains inconclusive. Some studies have shown that PBT cohorts achieve better gastrointestinal, urological, and sexual QOL outcomes than IMRT cohorts, whereas others have found no significant differences [13–15].

Radiation-induced GU toxicity is commonly evaluated using the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) scoring scale [16, 17]. Urological symptoms include urinary frequency, nocturia, dysuria, urgency, and hematuria. Nocturia is one of the most bothersome symptoms, frequently occurring during or immediately after RT [18]. It is closely associated with sleep disorders, which significantly impair QOL and increase the prevalence of depression [19, 20]. Patients with nocturnal voiding more than twice per night have significantly impaired QOL and well-being [19]. Although several studies have reported acute GU toxicities after PBT for prostate cancer, research on factors inducing acute radiation-induced nocturia (ARIN) is limited.

This study aimed to examine factors associated with ARIN in patients with LPC treated with PBT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient characteristics

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Kouseikai Takai Hospital (reception no.106) and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to treatment. A total of 140 patients who underwent PBT for LPC between January 2019 and October 2023 were included in this study. The inclusion criteria were:

(i) The patient was histologically diagnosed with LPC without metastasis or seminal vesicle invasion (cT1-3aN0M0).

(i) The patient was histologically diagnosed with LPC without metastasis or seminal vesicle invasion (cT1-3aN0M0).

(ii) No previous irradiation in the pelvic area.

(ii) No previous irradiation in the pelvic area.

(iii) No metal femoral head replacement.

(iii) No metal femoral head replacement.

According to National Comprehensive Cancer Network risk categories, patients were classified as low-, intermediate-, or high-risk, respectively. Since January 2020, hydrogel spacers (Space OAR system; Boston Scientific Corp., Watertown, MA, USA) have been implanted to reduce rectal dose during radiation therapy after obtaining patient consent. In this study, two dose-fractionation regimens were used. A total of 80 patients with implanted hydrogel spacers were treated with moderately hypo-fractionated proton therapy (MHPT) (60–66 Gy relative biological effectiveness (RBE) in 20–22 fractions). The remaining 60 patients without hydrogel spacers were treated with normo-fractionated proton therapy (NFPT) (74–78 GyRBE in 37–39 fractions).

CT planning and contouring

The patients were immobilized in the supine position using a body vacuum-pressing bag system (Fujidenolo Co., Ltd., Aichi, Japan). During treatment planning, the following procedures were performed for the bladder and rectum:

The optimal bladder volume to achieve dose constraints is 100 ml and approximately 100 ml of urine is stored in the bladder [21].

The optimal bladder volume to achieve dose constraints is 100 ml and approximately 100 ml of urine is stored in the bladder [21].

The rectal contents were kept as empty as possible [22].

The rectal contents were kept as empty as possible [22].

In all patients, CT (Aquilion ONE; Canon Medical Systems Corp., Tochigi, Japan) images of 2-mm thickness and T2-weighted MRI (Achieva dStream; Royal Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) images of 1-mm thickness with a magnetic strength of 1.5 T were obtained. CT and MRI images were fused to contour the clinical target volume (CTV) and OARs. The CTV was defined as the entire prostate at low risk and the entire prostate and proximal seminal vesicle at intermediate/high risk.

The internal and setup margins were set to 5 and 3 mm, respectively. As it was necessary to consider the range uncertainty arising from uncertainties such as CT values, 3.5% of the range was added as a range margin [23]. Considering these factors, robust optimization was performed for the CTV.

Dose constraints

All treatments were performed at Kouseikai Takai Hospital using a PBT system (Sumitomo Heavy Industries Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with line-scanning proton beam technology. Plan calculations were performed using Version 13.7 of Eclipse (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The overall RBE for the PBT was defined as 1.1. Two opposing lateral beams (gantry angles of 90° and 270°) were used for irradiation. Two treatment plans were created for all patients: an initial plan and a boost plan. The boost plans were created based on the course of treatment and reproducibility of the organs. The NFPT group had an initial plan for the first 25 fractions and a boost plan for subsequent fractions, whereas the MHPT group had an initial plan for the first 14 fractions and a boost plan for subsequent fractions.

In both the NFPT and MHPT groups, the dose constraints for the CTV were as follows: (i) D2% < 107% of the prescribed dose and (ii) D98% > 93% of the prescribed dose. The dose constraints for the bladder, rectum, femoral heads and small bowel in the NFPT group were as follows: (i) V70 GyRBE, V60 GyRBE, and V36 GyRBE of the rectum <10%, 20%, and 30%, respectively; (ii) V70 GyRBE and V60 GyRBE of the bladder <15% and 30%, respectively; (iii) Dmax of the femoral heads <50 GyRBE; and (iv) V60 GyRBE of the small bowel <0.5 cc. The dose constraints for the bladder, rectum, femoral heads and small bowel in the MHPT group were as follows: (i) V60, V50, and V30 GyRBE of the rectum <10%, 20%, and 30%, respectively; (ii) V60 GyRBE and V50 GyRBE of the bladder <15% and 30%, respectively; (iii) Dmax of the femoral heads <45 GyRBE; and (iv) V50 GyRBE of the small bowel <0.5 cc. The maximum dose to the entire body was <110% of the prescribed dose. Dx% was defined as the dose received by x% of the organ volume. Vx GyRBE was defined as the relative volume of the organ that received at least x GyRBE. These dose constraints were achieved using the total plan, which was the sum of the doses of the initial and boost plans.

Treatment

For PBT, in-room CT (Aquilion LB; Canon Medical Systems Corp., Tochigi, Japan) images were acquired for every fraction to accurately monitor the target position and OAR variations. During daily treatment, the following bladder and rectal preparation procedures were implemented:

To the extent possible, bladder filling was adjusted daily during radiotherapy to be greater than the volume at the time of the planning CT.

To the extent possible, bladder filling was adjusted daily during radiotherapy to be greater than the volume at the time of the planning CT.

As in treatment planning, rectal contents were kept as empty as possible.

As in treatment planning, rectal contents were kept as empty as possible.

For patient positioning, prostate–rectum boundary matching was adopted to minimize rectal dose. Position translation and pitch angle corrections were performed as necessary, depending on changes in rectal filling [24–26].

Evaluation of acute radiation-induced nocturia

ARIN was evaluated based on the nocturnal voiding frequencies recorded in the treatment diaries. The toxicities were defined as either:

(a) An increase of two or more nocturnal voids per night after the final day of radiotherapy compared with baseline (pre-treatment);

(a) An increase of two or more nocturnal voids per night after the final day of radiotherapy compared with baseline (pre-treatment);

or

(b) An increase in the acute urinary RTOG grade after the final day of radiotherapy compared with baseline (pre-treatment).

(b) An increase in the acute urinary RTOG grade after the final day of radiotherapy compared with baseline (pre-treatment).

Grading was performed according to the grading system proposed by De Meerleer et al [19, 27].

Statistical methods

For univariate analyses, toxicities between the groups were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression (MLR) analysis was performed to identify significant factors for ARIN. Subsequently, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed as a sensitivity analysis to address potential confounding and to assess the robustness of the findings. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression models. PSM with 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching was performed with a caliper width of 0.2, defined as the allowable deviation in propensity scores between matched pairs. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were applied to examine the balance of covariates between groups. An SMD < 0.1 indicated adequate covariate balance between groups. After PSM, multivariate conditional logistic regression (CLR) analysis was performed to examine the factors associated with ARIN. Twelve variables were selected for univariate and multivariate analyses as follows: seven pre-treatment parameters (age, CTV volume, risk group, diabetes, medication before treatment, treatment regimen [MHPT with spacer or NFPT without spacer] and bladder volume); three dosimetric parameters for the bladder planning target volume (V70 GyRBE / V60 GyRBE, V60 GyRBE / V50 GyRBE, and V36 GyRBE / V30 GyRBE); and two practice-related parameters for IGRT using in-room CT, namely the number of fractions in which pitch angle correction from bone matching exceeded 2° in patient positioning (pitch >2°) and the number of fractions in which bladder volume during treatment was less than at planning (low bladder volume). In the MHPT group, treatment fractions were proportionally converted from 20–22 fractions to 37–39 fractions for evaluation. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R Version 4.3.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [28] and EZR Version 1.67 (Jichi Medical University Saitama Medical Center, Saitama, Japan) [29].

DISCUSSION

In this study, we initially performed MLR analysis to identify factors associated with ARIN in patients with LPC treated with PBT. However, given the observational nature of the study and the potential for baseline imbalance between groups, additional analyses were conducted to account for possible confounding. PSM was therefore applied to reduce bias arising from baseline differences between groups, followed by CLR analysis using the matched dataset. Although MLR is a conventional first-line approach for exploring associated factors, the limited number of events per variable in our cohort raises concerns regarding model overfitting and instability of estimates. Previous studies have suggested that when the number of events per variable is small (e.g. ≤ 7), propensity score–based approaches may provide more stable estimates compared with conventional regression methods [30]. Therefore, PSM was used to balance baseline covariates between groups prior to outcome analysis, and CLR analysis was conducted using the matched dataset to appropriately account for the matched design, thereby improving the validity and robustness of the estimated associations [31–33]. However, it should be noted that PSM does not fully eliminate residual confounding, particularly when important clinical variables are not available. In addition, the relatively small number of events per variable may still limit model stability, even after matching. Therefore, the consistency of the results between the MLR and PSM-based analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Symptoms of radiation-induced GU toxicity include urinary frequency, nocturia, dysuria, urgency, and hematuria. However, only ARIN was evaluated in this study because the incidence of other acute GU symptoms was low. Although no definitive endpoint for radiation-induced GU toxicity has been established, previous studies have assessed toxicity using the RTOG scoring scale [16, 17, 19, 34]. An increase of two or more nocturnal voiding episodes after treatment compared with baseline was used as the primary criterion for ARIN; however, this criterion may not accurately reflect toxicity in patients who already had nocturia before treatment. Therefore, an increase in the RTOG scoring scale grade after treatment was additionally incorporated into the definition of ARIN.

In the multivariate analysis before PSM, two variables—‘treatment regimen’ and ‘low bladder volume’—were identified as potential factors for ARIN during or immediately after treatment. Notably, ‘treatment regimen’ remained significantly associated with ARIN even after adjustment for baseline differences using PSM. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously. The treatment groups differed not only in fractionation but also in hydrogel spacer use and the timing of clinical implementation. Therefore, the observed difference likely reflects a combination of these factors rather than an independent effect of a single variable, and causal inference cannot be established from the present analysis.

With regard to the ‘treatment regimen,’ patients treated with ‘MHPT with spacer’ tended to have a smaller bladder volume at planning than those treated with ‘NFPT without spacer’ (Table 2A). Because MHPT was introduced approximately one year after the first PBT case at our institution, the criteria for bladder volume at treatment planning may have evolved as clinical experience accumulated. PSM helped adjust for these baseline differences. After adjustment, ‘treatment regimen’ remained significantly associated with ARIN in the multivariate CLR analysis (Table 2B), suggesting that ‘MHPT with spacer’ was associated with a lower risk of ARIN compared with ‘NFPT without spacer’. This difference may partly be explained by the effect of fractionation. According to the theory of RBE and the α/β ratio, acute responding tissues such as the bladder are generally assumed to have a relatively high α/β ratio (approximately 7–20 Gy) [35, 36]. Based on this assumption, the BED delivered to the bladder was estimated to be 81.4–100.3 GyRBE in the NFPT group and 69.0–94.3 GyRBE in the MHPT group. Therefore, the BED to the bladder tended to be lower in the MHPT group than in the NFPT group, which may partially explain the observed difference. However, this interpretation remains speculative in the absence of direct dosimetric analysis of relevant substructures. In addition, the higher incidence of acute GU toxicities in the NFPT group compared with the MHPT group is consistent with the findings reported by Takaoka et al. [37].

In the MLR analysis before PSM, ‘low bladder volume’ showed a relatively small odds ratio, indicating only a modest association with ARIN. The odds ratio suggested a positive association between the number of fractions in which bladder volume during treatment was lower than that at planning and the occurrence of ARIN. Although the magnitude of this association was limited, maintaining an adequate bladder volume may help reduce acute GU toxicity during radiotherapy for prostate cancer [38]. One potential concern is that dysuria and urgency during radiotherapy may affect urinary output, making it difficult to maintain adequate bladder filling during treatment. In this study, four of the 140 patients developed urgency during the latter half of PT and had difficulty maintaining adequate bladder filling. When the multivariate analysis was repeated after excluding these four patients, ‘low bladder volume’ still showed a modest association with ARIN. These findings suggest that careful monitoring of bladder volume using IGRT during each PBT session may help minimize acute GU toxicity during PBT for prostate cancer.

This study provides a clinical reference for preventing ARIN in patients with LPC treated with PBT. The statistical results suggested the potential effectiveness of ‘MHPT with spacer’ for prostate cancer. In our hospital, patients choose between ‘MHPT with spacer’ and ‘NFPT without spacer’ as treatment options; therefore, these findings may help inform treatment selection. However, given the non-randomized design and residual confounding, these results should not be interpreted as evidence to preferentially recommend one regimen over another, but rather as hypothesis-generating findings that warrant further validation. Although there is concern that higher rectal doses may increase the incidence of gastrointestinal toxicities, the use of a spacer in all MHPT cases may help mitigate this risk and support the clinical feasibility of this approach. In patients with larger prostate volumes (threshold approximately 40–50 cc), the risk of GU toxicity may increase; careful monitoring of bladder volume is therefore particularly important in these patients [39, 40]. However, the cumulative radiation dose from repeated IGRT should also be considered, and appropriate imaging conditions and the use of ultrasound-based bladder volume scanners are recommended [41]. For patients who have difficulty maintaining adequate urinary output during treatment, supportive measures may be required, including nursing interventions [42]. Patients should be advised to maintain an appropriate daily fluid intake (approximately 1.5–2 L), as both excessive and insufficient fluid intake may worsen urinary symptoms. In addition, fluid intake should be distributed evenly throughout the day rather than consumed in large amounts over a short period. Bladder training, such as scheduled voiding at regular intervals regardless of urinary urgency, may also help maintain stable bladder volume during treatment.

Our study has several limitations. First, the effects of fractionation and hydrogel spacer use could not be disentangled because spacers were implanted exclusively in the MHPT group; therefore, the observed association should be interpreted with caution. Second, key dosimetric parameters potentially relevant to genitourinary toxicity, such as urethral dose and bladder neck/trigone dose, were not available in our dataset. In addition, proton-specific factors such as linear energy transfer (LET) distribution were not evaluated. These limitations restrict the mechanistic interpretation of our findings. Although PBT theoretically reduces the dose to surrounding normal tissues through the Bragg peak effect, the biological effects within the target and adjacent organs may also be influenced by proton-specific characteristics, particularly in regions near the distal edge of the beam where LET may increase and RBE may vary [43, 44]. Recent studies have suggested that LET distribution and variable RBE may contribute to proton therapy-related toxicities [45, 46]. Because these proton-specific parameters were not available in the present study, the current findings should primarily be interpreted as clinical observations derived from a proton therapy cohort rather than evidence of a proton-specific biological mechanism underlying ARIN. Furthermore, the clinical integration of LET- and RBE-based analyses in proton therapy remains an evolving area of research, and standardized approaches for incorporating these parameters into toxicity prediction have not yet been fully established. Nevertheless, given the distinct physical dose distribution of proton therapy, further investigations incorporating LET- and substructure-based dosimetric analyses may help clarify whether proton-specific biological characteristics contribute to acute genitourinary toxicity. Third, baseline urinary function was not adequately captured. Detailed clinical variables such as International Prostate Symptom Score, use of α1-blockers, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and urinary flow parameters were not consistently available, which may have resulted in residual confounding. To partially address this limitation, we additionally evaluated the presence of benign prostatic hyperplasia and the use of urinary medications at baseline, including β3-agonists, anticholinergic agents, and α1-blockers; however, none of these variables were significantly associated with ARIN in univariate analysis. Nevertheless, these variables may not fully capture baseline urinary function, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, the treatment fractions differed between the ‘MHPT with spacer’ group and the ‘NFPT without spacer’ group, resulting in differences in the observation period for nocturia. In addition, the treatment diaries recorded only the daily nocturnal voiding frequency from the start to the end of proton beam therapy, making it difficult to retrospectively assess the peak incidence of nocturia after treatment. At the one-month follow-up, nocturnal voiding frequencies were available in the ‘MHPT with spacer’ group, and five patients developed ARIN at one month after treatment rather than during or immediately after treatment. When multivariate CLR analyses were performed after excluding these five patients, ‘treatment regimen’ remained significantly associated with ARIN (OR: 0.33; 95% CI: 0.11–0.97; P < 0.05). Fifth, the definition of ARIN was based on a composite endpoint, which may introduce heterogeneity, particularly in patients with high baseline nocturia. In addition, validated patient-reported outcome measures were not used, and the peak timing of toxicity could not be precisely determined. Finally, the follow-up period was relatively short; therefore, further long-term follow-up is necessary to evaluate radiation-induced GU toxicity in more detail. Therefore, the present study should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

In conclusion, a treatment strategy involving MHPT with spacer was associated with a lower incidence of ARIN compared with NFPT without spacer. However, this association reflects a composite of multiple clinical and treatment-related factors, and the proton-specific biological contribution remains unclear in the absence of LET- and substructure-based analyses. Further prospective investigations incorporating proton-specific biological and dosimetric parameters are warranted to better characterize factors associated with ARIN after PBT.
