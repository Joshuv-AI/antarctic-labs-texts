The effect of acceptance commitment therapy on the mental health of elderly breast cancer patients
Authors: Yuan Ruan, Wen Xie, Feifei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo explore the impact of acceptance commitment therapy(ACT) to treatment on the mental health of elderly breast cancer patients.MethodsSixty elderly breast cancer patients admitted to a cancer hospital were selected and divided into a control group (n=30) and an intervention group (n=30). The control group received conventional interventions, The intervention group received an additional ACT. Psychological status, quality of life, sense of meaning in life of the two groups were observed and compared.ResultsAfter intervention, the self-rating anxiety scale (SAS) and self-rating depression scale (SDS) scores of the intervention group were lower than those of the control group (p<0.001, p<0.001). In terms of sense of meaning in life, the intervention group scored significantly higher than the control group at post-intervention (p<0.001); Additionally, in terms of quality of life, the scores of the intervention group after the intervention were significantly higher than those of the control group (p<0.001).ConclusionThe effect of ACT in elderly patients with breast cancer can effectively improve the psychological state, quality of life and sense of meaning in life of patients, and the application of this therapy can be further promoted in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04056-x.

Introduction

Owing to advancements in cancer detection methodologies and therapeutic interventions, a remarkable two-thirds of individuals diagnosed with aggressive cancer now exceed the five-year survival mark, thereby resulting in a steadily escalating population of long-term survivors [1, 2]. With the improvement of cancer survival rate,, it has become increasingly evident that mental health concerns constitute a prominent component within the realm of their unmet needs, encompassing a myriad of afflictions, not merely anxiety and depression, but also diverse cancer-related distresses [3, 4]. Even subsequent to treatment, cancer necessitates an extended period of therapeutic management and recuperation. This journey is frequently beset with depression and anxiety, yet these pernicious emotions are often overlooked. Should this oversight persist without amelioration, it will undeniably impair the patient’s adherence to treatment protocols, thereby influencing their cancer survival rates, therapeutic outcomes, and overall quality of life [5–8].

The manifestation of these symptoms can exert deleterious effects, manifesting as a diminished quality of life, adverse implications on patients’ capacity to undergo physiotherapy, and an inability to adhere to prescribed treatments. Consequently, this may lead to a compromised immune system, decreased survival rates, and suboptimal survival conditions. Furthermore, patients may grapple with a profound sense of meaninglessness in their lives [9, 10]. This sense of meaninglessness in life may lead patients to have no purpose in life, leading individuals to abandon the pursuit of a meaningful life [11]. Therefore, enhancing patients’ sense of meaning in life is a critical therapeutic objective. This can be achieved by helping individuals adjust thought patterns, shift perspectives, and recognize that while certain life events—such as terminal illness, cancer, or aging—are unchangeable, they can learn to respond to them differently. For example, though a cancer diagnosis itself cannot be altered, patients can profoundly influence their emotional and psychological experience of the disease. Consequently, interventions aimed at strengthening meaning in life deserve greater emphasis in cancer care, as they may significantly improve patients’ quality of life and treatment outcomes [12, 13].

Traditional treatments primarily rely on pharmacological interventions to address co-occurring symptoms, though these approaches often demonstrate limited efficacy in achieving complete symptom relief [14]. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) represents an important therapeutic approach, demonstrating statistically significant improvements in mental health and quality of life among older adults with cancer [15]. However, there is still inconsistency in the effectiveness of CBT on improving depression in cancer patients [16–19]. For elderly cancer patients, a sense of meaning in life plays a crucial role, therefore, alternative therapies for cancer patients should be explored [20].

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) focuses on altering one’s relationship with thoughts and emotions rather than modifying their content—a key distinction from traditional cognitive therapy. ACT enriches your life by helping you accept things beyond your control and committing to taking action [21]. This approach helps individuals clarify their personal values and commit to actions that cultivate a meaningful existence [22]. Unlike therapies focused on emotion regulation or symptom relief, ACT emphasizes accepting difficult thoughts and feelings while committing to values-driven actions. By teaching individuals to respond to internal experiences with mindful awareness—rather than attempting to avoid or alter them—ACT cultivates engagement in meaningful living despite challenging circumstances. This approach demonstrates significant clinical value for patients with progressive illnesses, improving health outcomes and quality of life through enhanced psychological flexibility.

ACT demonstrates both acceptability and feasibility for alleviating anxiety, depression, and psychological distress in palliative care settings. To advance implementation, rigorous evaluation studies are now needed to: establish the intervention’s effectiveness and cost-effectiveness in enhancing patients’ sense of meaning and quality of life, guide further intervention refinement through development and feasibility studies examining its clinical value within this specialized context [23–25]. Elderly cancer patients often need to simultaneously cope with the challenges brought by aging (such as physical decline, coexistence of multiple diseases, changes in social roles, cognitive function changes) and the pressure of cancer itself (such as prognosis concerns, treatment side effects, and fear of death) when facing the disease. The core of ACT is to accept rather than confront pain, clarify and practice values, which is very in line with the psychological needs of elderly patients [21]. While previous psychotherapeutic interventions for older adults with cancer have yielded inconsistent psychological outcomes, evidence regarding ACT efficacy specifically in older breast cancer patients remains limited. This study therefore examines ACT’s impact on negative emotional states (anxiety, depression) and its potential for enhancing quality of life and meaning-in-life within this population. Our findings aim to inform more effective psychosocial support frameworks for cancer recovery trajectories. This study hypothesizes that compared to the control group, after eight weeks of intervention, the intervention group’s anxiety and depression scores significantly decreased, while their quality of life and sense of meaning in life scores significantly improved.

Materials and Methods‌

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software, selecting repeated measures ANOVA, setting a priori analysis, with an effect size of 0.25, α = 0.05, and statistical power 1-β = 0.8. The calculation yielded a total of 34 participants required, with 17 in each group. Considering potential participant dropouts, we plan to recruit 30 patients for each group, totaling 60 participants for the intervention and control groups.

Participants

A total of 60 cancer patients admitted to Zhejiang Cancer Hospital from October 2023 to October 2024 were selected and randomly divided into two groups, researchers use Research Randomizer software to generate 1–60 random number sequences, which are encoded sequentially and placed in opaque, sealed envelopes for safekeeping by designated personnel. After selecting the research subjects that meet the inclusion criteria, non research team members will open the envelopes in coding order and inform the research team members of the grouping situation. The random numbers from 1 to 30 will be used as the experimental group, and those from 30 to 60 will be used as the control group. To avoid mutual contamination between groups, the intervention of the experimental group was conducted in a separate room. The control group (n = 30) and the intervention group (n = 30). The patients completed the corresponding tests before and after the intervention. All patients and their families who participated in the study signed an informed consent form. All patients had good compliance and completed an eight week intervention. This study was approved by the Anhui University of Chinese Medicine Ethics Committee (2023-99). Intervention

The control group received conventional treatment and routine nursing care, along with general psychological support:. Routine medical care: patients in the control group received standard breast cancer treatment process and relevant routine care, including: Daily medical consultation and health education provided by attending physicians and nurses. Suggestions for managing side effects of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, and other treatments (such as antiemetic drugs, leukocyte elevation, etc.). Issue breast cancer rehabilitation manual.

General psychological support: To control the effects of “attention” and “care”, the researchers also had a 60 min weekly meeting with the control group.

The content of the meeting is: Unstructured supportive conversation: Listen to the patient’s troubles and feelings, provide empathy, encouragement, and comfort (e.g. “I know this period has been very difficult, you are already strong”).

General health education: discusses topics such as nutrition, exercise, sleep, etc. related to cancer, but does not involve any structured psychotherapy techniques (especially mindfulness, cognitive dissociation, value clarification, and other core components of ACT).

Prohibit the use of ACT technology: Researchers should only play the role of a “supportive listener” and avoid any guiding psychological interventions.

The intervention group used ACT as an intervention. The ACT intervention team consists of four intervention therapists (two psychotherapists and two nurses) with college education or above and more than five years of experience in intervening in middle-aged and elderly patients with advanced cancer. The team members are proficient in the theoretical knowledge and procedures related to “acceptance and commitment therapy” and have received corresponding professional training, obtained training certificates, and completed the required competency assessment. Implementation of ACT: Following the principle of not interfering with patient treatment and rest, ACT intervention lasts for 8 weeks, with a total of 8 sessions, each lasting 60 min, completed once a week. Measures

Psychological status

Before and after the intervention, the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) were used for evaluation. Both SAS and SDS consist of 20 items, each scored from 1 to 4, with a total score of 80. A score of < 53 indicates normal emotions, while a score ≥ 53 suggests the presence of anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for SAS and SDS were 0.733 and 0.765.

Quality of life

Before and after the intervention, the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy (FACT) scale was used to evaluate quality of life. It includes four sections with 27 items and a total score of 108. A higher score indicates a better quality of life for patients. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the four dimensions of the scale (physiological condition, social/family condition, emotional condition, and functional condition) are 0.884, 0.821, 0.867, and 0.835, respectively.

Sense of meaning in life

The Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) developed by Steger et al. (2006) was used, consisting of 10 items. The MLQ includes two subscales: Presence of Meaning (MLQ-P; 5 items, e.g., “My life has a clear direction”) and Search for Meaning (MLQ-S; 5 items, e.g., “I am searching for meaning in my life”). A 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree) is used for scoring. A higher score indicates a stronger sense of meaning in life. The MLQ has good reliability and validity and is suitable for research with Chinese samples. In this study, the McDonald’s ω coefficients for the total scale, MLQ-P, and MLQ-S were 0.86 and 0.85 respectively, indicating good internal consistency of the questionnaire.

Control variables

Considering that age, and educational level may affect the intervention outcomes in cancer patients, this study included age and educational level as important covariates for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 software.

Count data is described in frequency and percentage, and chi square test and rank sum test are used for inter group comparison; All measurement data follow a normal distribution, described as mean ± standard deviation. Demographic variables are compared using two independent sample t-tests, and data before and after intervention are tested using repeated measures of variance. A difference of p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a psychological intervention using ACT among elderly breast cancer patients and found that their anxiety, depression, quality of life, and sense of meaning in life were significantly improved. The results indicate that the use of ACT among elderly breast cancer patients is of great value and worthy of further clinical optimization and promotion. This study provides new evidence for the efficacy of ACT in the psychological status of elderly cancer patients, and provides more empirical support for the safety and effectiveness of ACT’s clinical application.

ACT has demonstrated its efficacy for cancer patients by teaching adaptive management of disease-related pain, including uncertainty, anxiety, sadness, and anger, rather than promoting experiential avoidance.

This method cultivates psychological flexibility, enabling one to cope with difficult emotions while pursuing a life of shared values [26]. At the same time, ACT is superior to conventional therapy and behavioral activation therapy in treating anxiety and depression [27],. The foundational phase of ACT cultivates willingness to contact the present moment nonjudgmentally—inviting patients to experience thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations as they naturally occur without attempting to alter their frequency or form, particularly when control efforts prove psychologically costly. Through guided exercises (e.g., mindfully attending to anxiety by noting somatic responses, observing cognitive events, and tracking behavioral urges), patients learn to hold discomfort with openness rather than avoidance. Crucially, this acceptance practice serves not as an endpoint, but as a gateway to developing psychological flexibility—the capacity to consciously choose meaningful actions aligned with personal values despite internal distress [28]. Enhancing cognitive flexibility contributes to the control and improvement of negative emotions [29, 30]. Consistent with the findings of this study, a group intervention using ACT for 8 weeks significantly improved anxiety and depression among breast cancer patients [31], this also means that group intervention has a certain role in treatment, which can greatly improve treatment efficiency. A meta-analysis of 25 treatment-control comparison studies (with a total sample size of 2,256 cancer patients) found that ACT significantly impacted psychological distress among cancer patients, particularly reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety, and greatly improved their quality of life and hope [27]. This also suggests that regardless of whether it is administered individually or in groups, ACT has good clinical effects and is worthy of further promotion.

ACT employs core processes—acceptance, defusion, and present-moment awareness—to dismantle psychological barriers, enabling patients to clarify their deeply held values (e.g., family, relationships, meaningful work). This values identification shifts behavioral regulation away from rule-governed patterns (social compliance, experiential avoidance, cognitive fusion) toward consciously chosen, value-congruent actions. Through committed action, patients implement concrete behavioral changes—structured as short-, medium-, and long-term goals—that progressively align their lives with chosen values. While values themselves are never “achieved” but continually instantiated, these tangible steps foster movement toward what matters most. Crucially, by acting in accordance with their values despite discomfort, patients cultivate richer, more vital lived experiences [28]. When negative emotions improve, patients’ quality of life also tends to improve. A meta-analysis included 19 related studies (1,643 patients) published between 2012 and 2022. The pooled results showed that ACT significantly improved psychological flexibility and quality of life, this is consistent with the results of this study [32]. These results align with our observations and underscore the transdiagnostic potential of the intervention. Notably, our study extends this evidence base by specifically highlighting the relevance of ACT in populations confronting serious illness. A randomized controlled trial conducted in patients with advanced lung cancer revealed that those receiving a 4-week ACT intervention showed significant improvements in quality of life and reductions in negative emotional symptoms compared to a control group receiving usual care [33]. The results of a randomized controlled trial of an ACT intervention tailored to the needs of women with metastatic breast cancer showed significant therapeutic effects of ACT [34]. These findings corroborate our results and suggest that ACT can effectively address unique challenges such as fatigue interference and impaired health-related quality of life in advanced cancer contexts. Beyond symptom reduction, our interpretation emphasizes the role of meaning-making processes in ACT-mediated improvement. Related research has corroborated that ACT contributes to an enhanced sense of meaning in life among cancer patients [35],a notion strongly supported by the value-clarification and committed-action components observed in our cohort. This convergence suggests that fostering purposeful action and valued living may constitute a central mechanism underlying ACT’s benefits in life-threatening conditions.

Research limitations and implications for future research

This study still has some limitations. Firstly, the sample size was not large enough, which would affect the generalization. of the findings; secondly, the study population was relatively fixed and needs to be expanded further; and thirdly, this study did not conduct follow-up after intervention, which may affect the reliability of the results. Future studies will also need to include 3–6 months of follow-up data. Finally, the adaptation of the intervention is a point neglected in this study, considering the economic costs. Future research should continue to optimize ACT treatment models by identifying core, cross-culturally valid processes alongside flexible components that can be adapted to different clinical conditions and cultural contexts. Such a approach may help maximize both efficacy and ecological validity in real-world applications. In terms of research design, priority should be given to efficacy, effectiveness and implementation studies involving patients and cares. Comprehensive evaluations of ACT to support the well-being of patients, carers and staff in palliative care settings are scarce, so evidence on efficacy remains uncertain. Mixed-methods evaluation studies with larger sample sizes and more comparative conditions are needed. In addition, mixed efficacy-implementation studies are recommended to explore how to scale up interventions and how to implement them in medical and social intervention settings. At the same time, long-term follow-up should be conducted after intervention to evaluate the long-term efficacy of ACT.

Conclusion‌

ACT can improve the anxiety, depression, quality of life and sense of meaning of life in elderly patients with breast cancer.It is recommended to include ACT in patient care and psychological support plans during clinical treatment. However, its effectiveness in different patient groups needs further verification, and treatment protocols should be further promoted and refined in the future.
