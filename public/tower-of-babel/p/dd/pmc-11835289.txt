Design of Plastic Binding Lytic Polysaccharide Monooxygenases via Modular Engineering
Authors: Alessia Munzone, Manon Pujol, Majda Badjoudj, Mireille Haon, Sacha Grisel, Anthony Magueresse, Sylvie Durand, Johnny Beaugrand, Jean-Guy Berrin, Bastien Bissaro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe worldwide accumulation of plastic waste in the environment, along with its lifespan of hundreds of years, represents a serious threat to ecosystems. Enzymatic recycling of plastic waste offers a promising solution, but the high chemical inertness and hydrophobicity of plastics pose several challenges to enzymes. In nature, lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) can act at the surface of recalcitrant biopolymers, taking advantage of their solvent-exposed active sites and appended carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs). LPMOs can disrupt the densely packed chains of polysaccharides (e.g., cellulose) by the oxidation of C–H bonds. Given the similarities between these natural and artificial polymers, we aimed here at promoting plastic-binding properties to LPMOs, by swapping their CBM with three natural, surface-active accessory modules displaying different amphipathic properties. The polymer binding capacity of the resulting LPMO chimeras was assessed on a library of synthetic polymers, including polyester, polyamide, and polyolefin substrates. We demonstrated that the plastic binding properties of these engineered LPMOs are polymer-dependent and can be tuned by playing on the nature of the accessory module and reaction conditions. Remarkably, we gained full binding for some chimera LPMOs with striking results for polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA). In the long term perspective of harnessing the unique copper chemistry of LPMOs to degrade plastics, we also provided the first evidence of LPMO-dependent modification of the PHA polymer, as supported by enzyme assays, gel permeation chromatography, and scanning electron microscopy. Altogether, our study provides the first roadmap for engineering plastic-binding ability in LPMOs, constituting a crucial first step on the evolutionary path toward efficient interfacial catalysis of plastic-active enzymes.Keywords: Plastics, Biopolymers, Oxidative enzymes, LPMOs, Bioeconomy

Introduction

Plastics represent a hallmark of the Anthropocene.1 Indeed, with a global production that reaches nearly 400 million tons per year,2 plastics are widely used in various areas such as in the food, agricultural, medical, transport, packaging, textiles and electronic industries. While plastics have undoubtedly allowed great advances in our modern societies, their excessive and ongoing production from fossil fuels, combined with reckless disposal practices, has brought about detrimental effects to the environment and human health.3 Today, the global community is challenged to achieve a transition to a circular plastic economy.4

The biodegradation of plastic waste into the building block monomers (for recycling purposes) or high value chemicals (for up-cycling) offers a promising alternative to conventional (often environmentally inadequate) plastic waste management methods.3,5 However, the recent and fast large-scale use of plastics has not permitted biological systems to evolve assimilation strategies of such artificial and recalcitrant materials. (Micro)organisms are confronted with the inherent physicochemical robustness of most synthetic polymers constituting plastics, marked by limited access to chemical bonds within the polymer backbone, primarily resulting from the elevated polymer crystallinity and hydrophobicity. Remarkably, as these properties are shared with recalcitrant biopolymers (e.g., cellulose and lignin), ongoing research on plastic biodegradation draws inspiration from natural biomass breakdown processes.6,7 One of the most notable discoveries in the field have been the identification and engineering of polyethylene terephthalate (PET)-active hydrolases, produced by the microorganism Ideonella sakaiensis(8−10) or extracted from leaf branch compost (LCC).11,12

Remarkably, most of the enzymes documented to date (i.e., esterases, cutinases, lipases, and proteases)13 have shown activity on PET,8−12 polyamides (PAs),14 and polyurethanes (PUs),15 which are prone to enzymatic hydrolysis, since their backbones contain ester bonds. In contrast, all-carbon backbone polymers, such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP) and polystyrene (PS), are much more difficult to biodegrade, with only a few pieces of evidence reported in the literature,16,17 which remain to be validated.18,19

As plastic polymers are insoluble in water, reaching an efficient enzymatic breakdown in aqueous conditions requires one to engineer the interfacial catalysis at play.20,21 Notably, enhancing enzyme adsorption on the plastic polymers to promote the formation of the enzyme–substrate complex, a prerequisite of enzyme action, appears to be crucial. An analogous problem has already been solved in Nature, notably by saprotrophic filamentous fungi, which secrete an extensive array of multimodular enzymes containing carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) that promote binding to crystalline substrates, and thus catalysis.22 Among those, a peculiar class of copper enzymes named lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases23 (LPMOs; classified as AA9 to AA11 and AA13 to AA17 families in the auxiliary activities (AA) class of the CAZy database24) has evolved dedicated features to overcome the recalcitrant nature of several types of biopolymers, such as cellulose. LPMOs action can disrupt the densely packed chains of recalcitrant polysaccharides by the oxidation of C–H bonds (at C1 and/or C4 carbon of glycosidic bonds),23,25 using a copper-catalyzed chemistry in the presence of a reducing agent and an oxygen donor (O2/H2O2).25 This interfacial catalysis is underpinned by a unique enzyme–substrate interface due to the relatively flat and solvent-exposed substrate-binding surface of LPMOs.

Given the conspicuous similarities between recalcitrant polysaccharides and crystalline plastic polymers, particularly their hydrophobic surface properties, there exists a compelling opportunity to harness the unique oxidative chemistry of LPMOs for the biodegradation of plastics. In that respect, a study has reported the boosting effect of the addition of an (inactive) LPMO (from the AA14 family) on PET depolymerization by PETases.26 However, the molecular basis of such a boosting effect remains unclear. Within the perspective of engineering bona fide plastic-active LPMOs, it is indeed primordial to first ensure that LPMOs bind efficiently to synthetic polymers, as LPMOs are known to suffer from oxidative autoinactivation when poorly bound to their substrate.27 However, seeking too high affinity binding is not necessarily a goal per se, as it may not allow for a dynamic enzyme–substrate interaction. Instead, we should find ways to control the magnitude of the binding strength to reach optimal affinities. This optimum will vary depending on the end goal, which could range from protein immobilization on the polymer surface, to neofunctionalization/degradation of the polymer surface, to complete degradation or re/up-cycling.

Here, we aimed to confer tunable plastic-binding capabilities to LPMOs. To this end, we have engineered bimodular enzymes, where the catalytic module of a cellulolytic fungal LPMO (from the AA9 family) has been appended with noncatalytic, surface-active modules displaying various extents of hydrophobicity, including two fungal hydrophobins (HFB2,28 HFB429) and the substrate binding domain sourced from a bacterial poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) depolymerase30 (PHA-Bd). Upon assessment of the binding of chimera LPMOs toward various synthetic polymers, including polyesters, polyamides, and polyolefins, and under different reaction conditions, we demonstrate that these enzymes possess hitherto never reported plastic-binding abilities. Furthermore, we report evidence of LPMO-dependent oxidation of a synthetic polymer, namely, PHA.

Experimental Section

Chemical and Reagents

Most chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint-Louis, MO, USA), including Avicel, polystyrene (PS; ref.430102; average MW 192,000), low density polyethylene (LDPE, ref.428078), polyethylene terephthalate (PET; ref.429252), Nylon-6,6 (NY; ref.429171), horseradish peroxidase (HRP; ref. P8375) and lactose. Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 reagent used in the Bradford assay was purchased from Bio-Rad (Marnes-La-Coquette, France). Phosphoric acid cellulose (PASC) was prepared from Avicel as described by Bennati-Granier et al.,31 adapted from Wood et al.32 Oligosaccharides substrates were purchased from Megazyme (Wicklow, Ireland). The concentration of H2O2 stock solution was verified at 240 nm (ε240 = 43.6 M–1.cm–1). Poly(hydroxyalkanoate) (PHA PHI 002) was purchased from Natureplast, (Ifs, France); poly(lactic acid) (PLA, 3001D) was purchased from Natureworks (Minnetonka, MN, USA); poly(butylene-succinate) (PBS CapaVC 6800) was purchased from Perstorp (Malmoe, Sweden).

Unless already under the form of powder, the polymers were milled into fine powder, using a cryo-grinder Freezer/Mill 6770 (SPEX SamplePrep, Metuchen, USA) in liquid nitrogen with 15 Hz in 3 cycles of 30 s and an interval cooling time of 1 min. All the polymer powders were passed through a molecular sieve to obtain homogeneous size particles of ca. 800 μm.

Molecular Design and Cloning of Chimera LPMOs

The chimera LPMOs encompassing a catalytic LPMO domain, a linker, and a putative polymer binding region were produced using the in-house 3PE platform (Pichia pastoris Protein Express; www.platform3pe.com) as described in Haon et al.33

The nucleotide sequence encoding the PaAA9E-CBM1 LPMO from the fungus P. anserina (Genbank ID XP_001907069.1) was used to retrieve the LPMO catalytic domain and its linker, which was deprived of the C-terminal CBM1 domain. Three PaAA9E-derived chimera were therefore generated by the molecular fusion of the nucleotide sequences coding the LPMO catalytic domain to that of potential polymer binding domains: the hydrophobins (HFBs) II and IV, sourced from Trichoderma reesei (Genbank IDs XP_006962048.1; XP_006964739.1, respectively) and the substrate binding region of the PHB depolymerase (PHA_Bd) from Alcaligenes faecalis (Genbank ID P12625). In addition, the nucleotide sequence encoding the wild type LsAA9A LPMO from L. similis was fused to that coding for the CBM1 region from the wild type PaAA9E LPMO. The space between the LPMO catalytic domain and the polymer binding domain was maintained constant through a 32 amino acid linker occurring in the wild type PaAA9E enzyme (see Table S1).

The nucleotide sequences of the four chimera LPMOs were codon optimized for heterologous expression in the yeast Pichia pastoris. Gene synthesis was performed by Genewiz (South Plainfield, New Jersey, USA). The region corresponding to the native signal sequence was kept, and the genes were inserted into the expression vector pPICZαA (Invitrogen, Cergy-Pontoise, France) in frame with a C-terminal polyhistidine tag. PmeI-linearized pPICZαA recombinant plasmids were used to transform competent X33 strain yeast cells by electroporation.

Production and Purification of Chimera LPMOs

Zeocin-resistant P. pastoris transformants were screened for protein production (see Table S1 for amino sequence of all produced proteins) via a high throughput robotic system as described in Haon et al.33 The best-producing transformants were grown either in a bioreactor or in 2 L flasks.

For bioreactor cultures, building on an Invitrogen protocol (Pichia Fermentation Process Guidelines), the production of wild type and chimera LPMOs was carried out in a 1.3 L bioreactor (New Brunswick BioFlo 115 bioreactor, Eppendorf, Germany) as previously described for other LPMOs.31,34,35 Preculture was prepared as described previously36 and was used to inoculate at 0.2% (v/v) 100 mL of Buffered Glycerol-complex Medium (BMGY), in a 500 mL flask, incubated (30 °C, 200 rpm) until the OD600 nm reached 4–6. Then 400 mL of basal salt medium containing 4.5 mL·L–1 PTM1 trace salts (both made according to the P. pastoris fermentation process guidelines - Invitrogen) was inoculated with 10% (v/v) of the BMGY culture. Temperature was set to 30 °C. Next 100 μL of Pluronic E8100 (BASF, Germany) was added after 6 h of culture to prevent foam. After full consumption of glycerol (as indicated by a return of dissolved oxygen (DO) level at 100%), the sorbitol-methanol transition phase was initiated by addition of 80 mL of sorbitol (250 g·L–1 stock solution), 1.6 mL of PTM1 traces salts, and 2 mL of methanol. After full consumption of carbon sources, the temperature was lowered to 20 °C and a methanol fed-batch was initiated with a feeding rate of 3.9 mL·h–1·L–1 (mL per hour per liter of initial fermentation volume) of a methanol containing 12 mL.L–1 PTM1 trace salts. New additions of 100 μL Pluronic E8100 were made after 30 and 54 h of fermentation. Methanol feeding rate was increased to 7.8 mL·h–1·L–1 after 52 h of fermentation. Throughout the fermentation, pH was maintained at 5 by automated adjustment with NH3 base. Air flow was maintained at 0.5 slpm (standard liter per minute). A cascade with a set point of 20% dissolved oxygen is maintained through agitation between 400 to 900 rpm and the percentage of pure oxygen addition between 0 to 50%. Fermentation was ceased after 122 h. The culture was centrifuged (10 min, 5,500g, 4 °C). The supernatant was harvested and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), aliquoted, and kept at 4 °C overnight before purification.

For flask cultures, for each protein, 5 mL of cell preculture was grown in a slightly aerated 50 mL Falcon tube containing yeast-peptone dextrose (YPD) media and incubated at 30 °C in an orbital shaker (160 rpm) for 6 h. Upon incubation time, preculture was transferred into 2 L of BMGY media (4 × 500 mL in 2 L nonbaffled flasks) and allowed to grow for 16 h at 30 °C in an orbital shaker (200 rpm) to an OD600 of 6. Expression was induced by transferring cells into 400 mL of Buffered Methanol-complex Medium (BMMY) (4 × 100 mL in 0.5 L nonbaffled flasks) at 20 °C in an orbital shaker (200 rpm) for 3 days. Each day, the medium was supplemented with 3% (v/v) methanol. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the supernatant was filtered on a 0.22 μm membrane (Merck Millipore) and stored at 4 °C. Just before purification, the pH of the supernatant was adjusted to 7.8 and filtered on a 0.45 μm membrane (Merck Millipore).

The CDH from Podospora anserina, PaCDHB, was produced according to the same protocol, as previously described.31,37

For each production, the pH of the culture supernatant was adjusted to 7.8 just before purification and filtered on 0.22 μm filters (Merck-Millipore) and loaded onto a 5 mL HisTrap excel column (Cytiva, Velizy-Villacoublay, France) equilibrated with buffer A (Tris-HCl 50 mM pH 7.8, NaCl 150 mM, imidazole 10 mM) that was connected to an Äkta Xpress (Cytiva). (His)6-tagged recombinant proteins were eluted with buffer B (Tris-HCl 50 mM pH 7.8, NaCl 150 mM, imidazole 500 mM). Fractions containing recombinant enzymes were pooled, concentrated, and dialyzed against a sodium acetate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.2). A fraction of the eluate was loaded onto 10% Tris-glycine precast SDS-PAGE gels (BioRad), stained with Coomassie Blue. The protein concentrations were determined by absorption at 280 nm using a Nanodrop NB-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and using protein parameters computed with the online tool ProtParam (Table S5).

Structural and Hydrophobicity Analysis

Structural models of chimera LPMOs were generated by Alpha Fold2.38 The calculation of hydrophobic patches from the structural models was performed with Protein-sol online software.39 Structure visualization was obtained with PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.0 Schrödinger, LLC). Average hydrophobicity of the selected residues was computed as a GRAVY index score (Kyte-Doolittle method)40 using the EXpasy protparam tool.

Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (DSF) Analysis

Determination of the apparent melting temperature (Tmapp) was made using a protein thermal shift assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The assay monitors protein unfolding using the fluorescent dye SYPRO Orange (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The stock solution of SYPRO Orange (5000×) was diluted in 50 mM of buffer at variable pH values (5.0, 6.0, 7.0 and 8.0) to prepare a 200× solution. Here, 2 μL of this diluted solution was mixed (20× final) with LPMO (20 μM final) in 20 μL total volume. Reactions were heated from 25 to 89 °C in 96-well PCR plates (Bio-Rad) over the course of 60 min in a CFX Connect real-time PCR thermocycler (Bio-Rad). The fluorescence was measured using 515 and 535 nm as excitation and emission wavelengths, respectively. The experiments were performed in triplicates and data analyzed with Bio-Rad CFX Manager 3.0 software to derive the Tm value.

Activity of Chimera LPMOs on Cellulose and Product Analysis

Prior to activity tests, chimera LPMOs and the respective wild type enzymes were copper loaded with 5 equiv of CuSO4. After 2 h static incubation at 4 °C, copper excess was removed by desalting on PD-10 desalting columns (Cytiva) using sodium acetate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.2) as the eluant.

LPMO (1 μM final concentration) and PASC (0.5 mg.mL–1) were mixed in sodium acetate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.2) in a final volume of 500 μL in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. Reactions were started by the addition of ascorbic acid (1 mM) and subsequently incubated in a Thermomixer for 24 h (40 °C, 800 rpm). Reactions were stopped by heat inactivation at 100 °C for 10 min and then centrifuged (12,000g, 10 min) before analysis of the soluble fraction using a high-performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC) coupled with pulsed amperometric detection (PAD) (DIONEX ICS6000 system, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The system was equipped with a CarboPac-PA1 guard column (2 × 50 mm) and a CarboPac-PA1 column (2 × 250 mm) kept at 30 °C. Elution was carried out at a flow rate of 0.25 mL·min–1, and 25 μL of sample was injected. The solvents used were 100 mM NaOH (eluent A) and sodium acetate (1 M) in 100 mM NaOH (eluent B), and the following gradient was applied: 0 to 10 min, 0 to 10% B; 10 to 35 min, 10 to 35% B (linear gradient); 35 to 40 min, 30 to 100% B (curve 6); 40 to 41 min, 100 to 0% B; 41 to 50 min, 100% A. C1-oxidized cello-oligosaccharides were prepared in-house, as previously described.31 Data visualization was performed by using the Chromeleon 7.2.10 chromatography data software.

Pull-Down Assay

Chimera LPMOs (2 μM), and the synthetic polymers PHA, PET, PLA, PBS, PE, PS, and Nylon-6,6 (100 mg·mL–1 each) were mixed in four different buffer solutions including sodium acetate (50 mM, pH 5.0), potassium phosphate (50 mM, pH 6.0), potassium phosphate (50 mM, pH 7.0), and Tris-HCl (50 mM, pH 8.0). Binding reactions were carried out in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes in a final volume of 100 μL. Control reactions were performed analogously (1) in the absence of polymer, (2) in the absence of chimera LPMO, (3) with wild-type enzymes, and (4) with the polysaccharidic substrate Avicel (100 mg·mL–1). After incubation in a Thermomixer for 24 h (40 °C, 1350 rpm), samples were filtered using a 96-well filter plate (Millipore) operated with a vacuum manifold to separate the unbound soluble protein (SNT) from the insoluble polymers. 50 μL of each SNT sample from the pull-down assay was diluted 2-fold with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.0), yielding a 100 μL reaction mixture. The amount of unbound soluble protein was then quantified by two different methods, viz. the Bradford assay and/or by fluorescence spectrophotometry. PaAA9E-CBM1, PaAA9E-PHA-Bd, LsAA9A, and LsAA9A-CBM1 were quantified by the Bradford assay. PaAA9E-HFB2 and PaAA9E-HFB4 did not show a linear response in absorbance with the Bradford assay,41 as already shown for other hydrophobins,42 and were therefore quantified by fluorescence spectroscopy.

Quantification of LPMOs Free in Solution by Bradford Assay

The diluted 100 μL of SNT mixture was transferred into a 96-well plate and mixed with 20 μL of Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 reagent, followed by mixing and 10 min incubation at RT. The absorbance of the samples was then measured on a multiplate reader at 595 nm. Unbound soluble protein was quantified via a standard BSA calibration curve ranging between 0 and 17 μg·mL–1 (in the final 100 μL reaction mixture). Upon absolute quantification, data were normalized on a 100% scale with respect to the control reaction, i.e., the chimera LPMO in the absence of plastic polymer representing the 100% unbound.

Quantification of LPMOs Free in Solution by Intrinsic Protein Fluorescence

For proteins that could not properly be quantified with the Bradford method, here PaAA9E-HFB2 and PaAA9E-HFB4, the diluted 100 μL of SNT mixture was transferred to 96-well Black Clear bottom Microplate (Greiner Bio-One, Les Ulis, France) and quantified by fluorescence spectrophotometry. Sample fluorescence (λex = 280 nm; λem = 340 nm) was read on a multiplate spectrofluorometer (BioTek Synergy H1, Agilent Technologies, Les Ulis, France) and quantified via a standard calibration curve of the same protein at known concentrations. Upon absolute quantification, data were normalized on a 100% scale with respect to the control reaction, i.e., chimera LPMO in the absence of plastic polymer representing the 100% unbound.

Kinetic of Apparent H2O2 Accumulation

The consumption of H2O2 by the LPMO in the peroxygenase reaction was assessed indirectly by monitoring the apparent H2O2 accumulation in the presence and absence of a substrate. To this end, we setup an assay where the CDH-lactose system was used as a stable and continuous source of electrons and H2O2 to, respectively, activate (priming reduction) and fuel (with H2O2) the LPMO peroxygenase reaction. LPMOs (1 μM) were preincubated for a fixed time with plastic polymers (50 mg·mL–1), lactose (3 mM) and potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0, 50 mM) in a final volume of 200 μL, in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Reactions were initiated by the addition of the CDH from Podospora anserina (PaCDHB; 1 μM) and further incubated in a Thermomixer (40 °C, 1350 rpm). Reactions were stopped after 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 min by filtering on a 10 kDa Vivaspin (Sartorious AG, Göttingen, Germany) (4 min, 8500g, 4 °C) to remove the enzymes. The enzyme-free flow-through containing the reaction product was recovered and kept on ice until the end of the kinetics. Then 50 μL of each flow-through sample was added to 50 μL of a premix of HRP (0.1 mg.mL–1) and Amplex red (200 μM) in potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 6.0), for a final reaction of 100 μL, in a 96-well microplate. In the presence of H2O2, HRP oxidizes the Amplex red reagent into resorufin. The formation of resorufin (2:1 ratio with respect of H2O2), was monitored at 575 nm over a 10 min duration. H2O2 solutions, prepared in TraceSELECT water, were used to generate a standard curve under the same conditions as for this assay. Absorbance values were converted to accumulated H2O2.

Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC)

PHA powder (100 mg.mL–1) was mixed with copper-loaded LPMOs (PaAA9E-CBM1 and PaAA9E-PHA-Bd) (2 μM) in the presence of AscA (1 mM), in potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 6.0). In control reactions, the LPMO was replaced with CuSO4 (2 μM) in the presence of AscA (1 mM), or the PHA powder was incubated in buffer only. All samples were incubated in a Thermomixer (40 °C, 800 rpm) for 5 days. Fresh enzymes (2 μM) and reductant AscA (1 mM) were added every 24 h to the reaction mix. AscA and CuSO4 were also supplied in the reaction mixture of the respective control reactions. Prior to GPC analysis, PHA powders were washed via cycles of centrifugation (8500g, 10 min), removal of soluble fraction, and addition of washing solution (500 μL) as follows: twice with 2% SDS, then twice with ultrapure water. and finally twice with 50% ethanol. For each washing step, samples were incubated in a Thermomixer (RT, 800 rpm) for 15 min. At the end of the washing process, residual water/ethanol mix was evaporated in a drying oven (40 °C) for 3 h. PHA powders were then dissolved in a chloroform solution (at approximately 5 mg.mL–1) containing trichlorobenzene (0.15% v/v) as the flow marker, stirred overnight at 50 °C, and then heated to 70 °C for 10–15 min before cooling to room temperature. The samples were then filtered on polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filters (0.22 μm) prior to analysis. GPC was performed on a Malvern Panalytical GPC2155 instrument equipped with GPC Max, TDA 305 and three columns (Agilent PLgel 5 μm Guard 7.5 × 50 and two PLgel Mixed C 7.5 × 300), operated at 30 °C using chloroform as eluent at a flow rate of 0.8 mL.min–1. Polystyrene standards (Agilent PL2010–0301 EasiVial PS-M-2 mL) were used to determine the relative molar masses of the samples.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

PHA thin films (thickness of 100 μm, approximate area of 0.5 cm2) were placed in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes and immersed for 3 h in potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 6.0). Films were afterward treated with copper-loaded LPMOs (PaAA9E-CBM1 and PaAA9E-PHA-Bd) (2 μM) in the presence of AscA (1 mM). In control reactions, the LPMO was replaced by CuSO4 (2 μM) in the presence of AscA (1 mM), or the PHA film was incubated in buffer only. Samples were further incubated and then washed as described above for GPC analysis. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a JEOL JSM-IT500HR. The PHA film samples were placed on a carbon sticker and coated with gold by using a sputter coater (Scancoat6) from Edward. Observations were conducted under high vacuum conditions with an acceleration voltage of 3 kV. Three samples per condition were analyzed, and for each sample, several images were acquired.

Statistical Analysis

All the experiments presented in this paper were performed in triplicate unless stated otherwise. Binding data were subjected to independent two-tailed Student’s t-tests (vs the wild-type enzyme) to evaluate whether differences in polymer binding capacities of each chimera protein were statistically significant (ns, not significant, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

Results and Discussion

Chimera LPMOs with Surface-Active Accessory Modules

To promote the binding of LPMOs to a set of synthetic polymers (Figure 1A), we engineered two cellulose-active AA9 LPMOs, one from the coprophilous ascomycete Podospora anserina (PaAA9E-CBM1)31 and one from the saprotrophic basidiomycete Lentinus similis (LsAA9A), with surface-active accessory modules (more below) (Figure 1B). PaAA9E-CBM1, comprising a catalytic module and a native accessory CBM1, is mainly active on cellulose via a C1 oxidative mechanism, while LsAA9A, naturally devoid of a CBM, mainly acts with a C4 oxidative mechanism toward soluble cello-oligosaccharides (or mixed C1/C4 oxidative cleavage of crystalline cellulose). Regarding the accessory modules, we initially selected three hydrophobins from the filamentous fungus Trichoderma reesei (HFB1,43 HFB228 and HFB429), the substrate binding domain of the Poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) depolymerase (PHA-Bd) from the bacterium Alcaligenes faecalis (syn. Ralstonia pickettii) and the chitin-binding peptide Tachistatine (Ta2)44 from the horseshoe crab Tachypleus tridentatus. All these modules share amphipathic properties (Figure 1C and Figure S1) which could, in principle, enhance the surface adhesion of the LPMO to hydrophobic polymers. We thus designed chimera enzymes composed of a catalytic LPMO domain C-terminally fused with one of the different noncatalytic modules, as follows: PaAA9E-HFB1, PaAA9E-HFB2, PaAA9E-HFB4, PaAA9E-PHA-Bd and PaAA9E-Ta2 were generated from the wild type PaAA9E-CBM1, and LsAA9A-CBM1 was generated from the wild-type LsAA9A, resulting in a total of eight different molecular constructs. Of note, we opted for fusions at the C-terminus because having a free N-terminal histidine in the mature protein, i.e. after secretion and cleavage of its signal peptide, is required to ensure a functional coordination of the copper atom in the catalytic LPMO domain. We also underscore that we used the native linker from PaAA9E-CBM1 in all constructs, which is an important aspect of the protein design further commented on in the Discussion.

Following transformation of the yeast Pichia pastoris (syn. Komagataella phaffii), and by screening of transformants for expression of the protein, we successfully expressed four chimeras (PaAA9E-HFB2, PaAA9E-HFB4, PaAA9E-PHA-Bd, and LsAA9A-CBM1), in addition to the parental enzymes (Table S1). These proteins were first produced in a flask and then produced at a larger scale in bioreactors, and purified to homogeneity by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) (Figure S2). Although hydrophobins are notoriously complicated to produce,45−48 notably for (i) their tendency to precipitate and (ii) the necessity of aggressive purification methods that need surfactants, we managed to obtain PaAA9E-HFB2 and PaAA9E-HFB4, although at rather low yields of ca. 4 and 6 mg·L–1 of culture in bioreactors, respectively. HFB1- and Ta2- containing constructs proved more difficult to express and, under our experimental conditions, were not identified in yeast culture supernatants. PaAA9E-PHA-Bd was remarkably well produced, with a yield of ca. 104 mg·L–1 of culture. Noteworthily, HFB2, HFB4, PHA-Bd and the CBM1 allow coverage of a broad range of hydrophobicity levels, as shown in Figure 1C as well as by computed hydrophobicity indexes (according to ProtParam tool,49 Expasy Web site) of 0.725, 0.642, −0.016, and −0.044, respectively (Table S2).

Plastic-Binding Properties of Chimera LPMOs

To test the impact of the newly appended accessory amphipathic modules, we evaluated the binding capacity of the chimera LPMOs (compared to the parental enzymes) on synthetic polymers and cellulose (Figure 2) by using pull-down assays (Figure S3). To this end, we selected a set of seven synthetic polymers with different extents of biodegradability, including synthetic polymers with hydrolyzable bonds (polyhydroxyalkanoate, PHA; polyethylene terephthalate, PET; polylactic acid, PLA; polybutylene succinate, PBS; nylon-6,6, NY) and all-carbon backbone polymers (low density polyethylene, LDPE; polystyrene, PS) (Figure 2A). For comparative purposes, we also included a natural polymer, namely the microcrystalline cellulose (Avicel, CEL). To ensure eventual scalability of the plastic binding process, we selected a binding condition that closely matches the industrially relevant polymer loading of over 10% w/v.50 Also, as we anticipated that the amphipathic nature of the accessory modules may render the binding efficiency sensitive to the ionization state of amino acids (Figure S4), we tested the polymer binding at four different pH values, spanning from 5.0 to 8.0 (LPMOs are usually used in this range of pH values). For the final series of experiments presented here (i.e., without taking into account the preliminary experiments), a total of ca. 600 binding trials, each requiring individual weighing of the polymer powder, were carried out.

At first, we evaluated the role of the CBM1, a widespread domain in fungal CAZymes promoting interaction with recalcitrant biopolymers, by appending at the C-terminal end of LsAA9A the CBM1 from PaAA9E. The resulting chimera LPMO (LsAA9A-CBM1) was compared to its native single domain counterpart. As expected, addition of the CBM1 improved binding to cellulose for pH values ≥6 (Figure 2B and C), with a noticeable 1.3-fold improvement at pH 8.0 allowing to reach nearly 100% binding. Interestingly, the binding to the polymers PHA, PBS and PE were also significantly improved, while binding to other polymers (PLA, PET, NY and PS) remained virtually unchanged (Figure 2B and C, Figure S5).

To investigate the impact of other surface-active accessory modules on the LPMO plastic binding capacity, we swapped CBM1 in wild-type PaAA9E-CBM1 with the aforementioned modules. Upon a first global observation of the binding heatmap (Figure 2B), we found that the binding of each chimera LPMO was pH-dependent, regardless of the nature of the polymer, albeit the magnitude of the effect varied. We could indeed observe a significant increase in the proportion of bound enzyme as the pH value of the reaction shifted from basic to acidic, with a maximum binding occurring at pH 5.0 (see as an example data for PaAA9E-CBM1 in Figure S6). On closer inspection of each group of polymer, it was remarkable that, on ester-containing polymers (PHA, PLA, PBS, and PET), acidic pH tended overall to confer the best binding capacity to the wild type enzyme PaAA9E-CBM1 (>80% protein bound fraction at pH 5.0) a capacity that, at higher pH, is instead strongly decreased down to 1% (for PBS, at pH 8.0), corresponding to up to 80-fold decrease (from pH 5.0 to 8.0). In contrast, on the same set of polymers, the chimera PaAA9E-PHA-Bd was not as remarkably affected by the pH variation as the wild type enzyme, with only a slight decrease in the fraction of bound protein at pH 8.0 (up to a 1.2-fold decrease compared to pH 5.0). This resulted in a polyester polymer binding capacity for PaAA9E-PHA-Bd significantly higher than that for PaAA9E-CBM1 at pH 8.0 (Figure 2C). Furthermore, the chimera LPMOs featuring the hydrophobin modules, i.e. PaAA9E-HFB2 and PaAA9E-HFB4, also exhibited a similar trend, albeit less pronounced than in the case of PaAA9E-PHA-Bd, with significantly higher binding capacities to PHA and PBS than the wild type PaAA9E-CBM1 (Figure 2B and C). For NY, strong binding was observed for all enzymes, regardless of the tested pH (Figure 2B and C). This behavior may be attributed to the ability of nylon to bind proteins nonspecifically through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic, and hydrophobic interactions, a property that is fundamental to the functioning of Western blot membranes and other immobilization techniques. Also, while such event of nonspecific binding is highly intrinsic to each protein–polymer pair, we cannot exclude that it occurs to some extent for the other polymers, notably at pH 5.0. Thus, for any “protein–polymer–pH” combination of interest, deeper characterization should be carried out.

Regarding all-carbon backbone polymers (PE and PS), the observations were more contrasted. For PS, our data indicate (i) that at pH 5.0, the wild type PaAA9E-CBM1 exhibits the lowest binding capacity compared to the rest of the tested polymers, with a bound fraction below 50% and (ii) that the introduction of the accessory binding modules HFB2, HFB4, and PHA-Bd did not have a positive effect on the polymer binding, as seen instead for the polyesters mentioned above. As for PE, the binding capacity of PaAA9E-CBM1 strongly varied from 94% (pH 5.0) to 11% of the bound fraction (pH 8.0). In contrast, the chimera LPMOs PaAA9E-HFB2 and PaAA9E-HFB4 underwent a mild pH-dependent variation and behaved quite similarly to each other, displaying significantly higher binding capacity at pH 8.0 compared to the parental enzyme. In addition, the binding capacity of PaAA9E-PHA-Bd to PE was also significantly higher (at pH 8.0) than that of PaAA9E-CBM1 (Figure 2C), demonstrating for the first time the binding of PHA-Bd accessory module toward nonester linked polymers.

In control experiments, to account for any loss of protein due to protein instability or unspecific binding, we systematically carried out pull-down assays in the absence of synthetic polymers, throughout all the tested pH values (Figure S3B). These control conditions showed a low to moderate loss of protein (between 0 and 30%), which was accounted for in all calculations. Furthermore, using differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF), we determined, for all the tested pH values, the melting temperature (Tm) of all chimera LPMOs, which ranged between 52 and 60 °C (Table S4), above the working temperature (40 °C) used in our assays. On the basis of this analysis, we underscore that swapping of the CBM1 for hydrophobic accessory modules did not significantly alter the thermal stability of the multimodular LPMO. We also verified that the chimera LPMOs retained a wild-type-like activity toward cellulose (Figure S7). Indeed, activity assays using high performance anion-exchange chromatography coupled with pulsed amperometry detection (HPAEC-PAD) allowed us to show the release of C1-oxidized oligosaccharides from cellulose by PaAA9E-derived chimeras (Figure S7A) and C1-, C4- and C1/C4-oxidized oligosaccharides released from either cellopentaose (DP5) (Figure S7B) or PASC (Figure S7C) by LsAA9A-CBM1. We thus concluded that the fusion of the different accessory modules into the LPMO catalytic domain did not alter its catalytic ability.

Based on the pH-dependent analysis presented above, we found that fusion of the accessory modules to the PaAA9E LPMO catalytic domain introduced significant variability in polymer binding at pH 8.0. This pH condition was also found to be the most effective in promoting the binding of chimeric LPMOs to selected plastic polymers compared with the parental enzyme (Figure 2C). On closer examination of the effect of the accessory modules on binding to the different families of polymers tested, PaAA9E-PHA-Bd stood out from the other chimeras for its high binding capacity toward PET, PLA, PE, PHA, and PBS, with relative increase in polymer binding of 2.3-, 4-, 4.5-, 8-, and 66-fold, respectively , compared to the parental PaAA9E-CBM1 (Figure S8). Interestingly, binding assays of PaAA9E-PHA-Bd toward crystalline cellulose revealed that replacing the CBM1 with PHA-Bd resulted in a dramatic decrease in cellulose binding, down to undetectable binding at pH 8.0 (Figure 2C and Figure S8). This swap in accessory modules has thus allowed to completely shift the substrate binding specificity from cellulose to PHA (and also to PLA, PBS, PET and PE), resulting in novel interfacial properties of the chimera LPMO. Reasoning that PHA could be seen as a (bio)synthetic polymer of intermediate complexity between cellulose and more difficult to degrade all-carbon synthetic polymers, we considered this PHA-binding capacity as a promising step toward the detection of non-natural LPMO activity on plastic (see more below). Furthermore, as PHAs constitute an emerging and promising family of “biodegradable” aliphatic polyesters for the replacement of petroleum-based ones,51 we believe it is important in anticipating the search for ad hoc enzymes.

Assessing the Action of the PHA-Binding LPMO Chimera on PHA

In light of the striking behavior of the chimera PaAA9E-PHA-Bd, we decided to conduct further investigations by first performing pull-down assay experiments at different PHA loadings (Figure 3A). The binding isotherms clearly confirmed a higher binding capacity for PaAA9E-PHA-Bd (ca. 82% maximum binding) compared to the wild type enzyme PaAA9E-CBM1 (ca. 12%), with saturating conditions reached at approximatively 80 and 40 mg.mL–1 of PHA, respectively.

We then tested if PaAA9E-PHA-Bd could display an activity on PHA. To evaluate that, we based our further experiments on the fact that a reduced LPMO involved in a productive binding of insoluble polysaccharides can consume H2O2 in a peroxygenase reaction.27 Therefore, using the cellobiose dehydrogenase (CDH)-lactose system as a dual supplier of both electrons and H2O2,52 we carried out a time course measurement of the apparent accumulation of H2O2 in reactions containing the LPMO in the presence or absence of polymer (Figure 3B and Figure S9). For reactions with PaAA9E-PHA-Bd, we could clearly observe that, upon 40 min of incubation, the concentration of accumulated H2O2 was significantly lower (4-fold) in the presence of PHA than in the absence of polymer (Figure 3B), indicating consumption of H2O2. In contrast, similar reactions carried out with the wild type PaAA9E-CBM1, showed no consumption of H2O2 on PHA. In addition, positive control experiments with cellulose showed no accumulation (i.e., complete consumption) of H2O2 after 40 min (Figure 3B). Looking at the whole time-course (Figure S9B and C), we observed that, after a certain time threshold and in certain conditions (more below), H2O2 began to accumulate, which is usually indicative of enzyme inactivation. For PaAA9E-CBM1 acting on cellulose, such H2O2 accumulation was not observed (Figure S9B), thanks to the presence of the CBM1 that enhances cellulose binding, allowing thereby greater LPMO efficiency and protecting against autocatalytic inactivation. On the other hand, in the reaction with PaAA9E-PHA-Bd (Figure S9C), H2O2 began to accumulate after 40 and 60 min, on PHA and cellulose, respectively, which is indicative of LPMO inactivation. On a side note, one can also observe that the basal production of H2O2 (i.e., in the absence of polymer) is higher in reactions containing PaAA9E-PHA-Bd than those with PaAA9E-CBM1. We speculate that the accessory module may interfere via domain–domain interactions with the oxidase reaction occurring at the catalytic domain as previously suggested for another LPMO.53 Altogether, while the inactivation observed during the reaction on PHA indicates that further engineering would be required to improve the LPMO activity/stability, our experiments indicate that the swapping of the CBM1 by PHA-Bd allowed the PaAA9E-PHA-Bd to catalyze a peroxygenase reaction on PHA.

Based on these results, we investigated whether any degradation of PHA could be directly observed. For this purpose, and in order to generate detectable modifications, we put the polymer in contact with the LPMO for longer incubation reactions (5 days), adding fresh enzyme and reductant (AscA) every 24 h. Using gel permeation chromatography (GPC), we observed a significant decrease (ca. 20–24%) in the molecular weight of the PHA powder after enzymatic treatment compared to controls, which included PHA incubated in buffer only or in the presence of CuSO4 and AscA (Figure 3C and Figure S10). Surprisingly, no significant difference was observed between the wild type LPMO and the chimera with the PHA-Bd module. It is possible that the “pushed” assayed conditions (longer incubation time, more enzyme, and use of AscA) did not allow the positive effect of the accessory module to be detected. Then, to get closer to real conditions of use and formulation of PHA, we investigated whether the LPMO activity observed on PHA powder could also be observed on PHA presented in the film form. To this end, PHA films were subject to reaction conditions analogous to those shown for GPC above and were then analyzed by scanning electron microscopy. Qualitative examination of the surface of PHA films upon treatment with the wild type PaAA9E-CBM1 and chimera PaAA9E-PHA-Bd (Figure 3D) revealed the appearance of holes, with sizes ranging between ca. 2 and 15 μm. Control reactions of the PHA film incubated in buffer only, or where the LPMO was replaced by CuSO4 and reductant, did not show such effect, excluding thus the possible contribution of unspecific Fenton-like reactions (Figure S11). Additional control reactions with heat-inactivated enzymes, for both PaAA9E-CBM1 (Figure S12) and PaAA9E-PHA-bd (Figure S13), did not show the formation of holes as observed with active enzymes. Nevertheless, we point out that it cannot be ruled out that the accessory modules per se may cause structural changes in the polymer surface upon binding. Notwithstanding, we underscore that the size of the holes formed is much larger than that of most enzymes, particularly multimodular LPMOs (<200 Å),54,55 which may therefore allow diffusion of the enzymes into these cavities and further degradation of the polymer.

Overall, our results provide the first experimental indications of LPMO-dependent modification of a plastic polymer, PHA, laying promising ground for future kinetics and mechanistic studies of the suggested enzymatic activity.

Discussion

In the present study, we provide a strategy to engineer plastic-binding properties in LPMOs. The molecular design of bimodular chimeric LPMOs, harboring accessory modules with different amphipathic properties, allows tuning of the binding properties, toward a set of synthetic polymers with different levels of degradability. In particular, we have focused on chimera LPMOs fused with hydrophobins (HFB2 and HFB4)56 and PHA binding domain,57 which are noncatalytic modules that can improve the wettability of hydrophobic surfaces,58−60 and hence the interaction between synthetic polymers and proteins. Importantly, we have also reported the first experimental indications of LPMO-dependent modification of a non-natural polymer, here a biobased aliphatic polyester (PHA).

Previous research by Guo and colleagues demonstrated that the LPMO AA14A from the fungus Pycnoporus cinnabarinus can enhance IsPETaseEHA-catalyzed PET hydrolysis by binding PET,26 suggesting that LPMOs could potentially bind to a wider variety of plastic polymers. Carbohydrate binding modules (CBMs), which can be found in the architecture of some natural modular LPMOs, may be an important factor contributing to the yet unexplored potential of LPMOs to bind plastic polymers. Here, we have investigated the effect of a CBM1 (from PaAA9E-CBM1) by fusing it to the well-known LPMO LsAA9A.61 We have found that, in addition to cellulose, CBM1 has a remarkable positive effect on the binding of some polyesters (PHA, PBS) and PE. Interestingly, Graham et al. showed that the fusion of CBMs (from families 1, 2a, 10, and 64) to the leaf-branch compost cutinase LCCYCCG increased up to 3-fold the affinity of the cutinase for PET.50 By expanding the scope of polymers that CBMs can bind, our results, and those of the latter study, collectively improve our knowledge of CBMs and their potential applications.

Among the other amphipathic polymer binding modules fused here to an LPMO catalytic domain, hydrophobins proved to be good candidates. In nature, hydrophobins play a crucial role in enabling fungi to localize at the interfaces (e.g., growth on waxy surfaces or aerial dispersion of spores).62 Due to their amphiphilic nature, challenges pertaining to the production and purification of hydrophobins have so far limited their applications. Chimeric constructs with hydrophobins have been mainly reported to achieve biosensing63 and enzyme immobilization, including the fusion of hydrophobin DewA64 to a laccase for polystyrene immobilization and the fusion of HFB1 and HFB2 to an endoglucanase65 to facilitate the binding of silanized glass. In the context of plastics biodegradation, fusion constructs of hydrophobins have been once again limited to hydrolytic enzymes as shown for a chimeric bacterial cutinase, reported to degrade PET up to 16 times more efficiently than the free enzyme.66 In the present study, we have seen that LPMO-hydrophobin (HFB2 and HFB4) chimeras display improved binding capacities on PHA, PBS and PE compared to the parental enzyme. HFB2, with the highest hydrophobicity score of all tested accessory modules, was more efficient than HFB4 in promoting PE-binding and could therefore be a good candidate for future engineering studies on polyolefin-active enzymes.

The binding module PHA-Bd also appeared as an interesting candidate. In nature PHA-Bd improves enzyme adsorption and depolymerization of bacterial polyesters, called polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs).67 PHAs polymers are gaining significant attention for their biocompatibility and biodegradability, in particular in the production of single-use plastic packaging.68 Chimeric enzymes incorporating a PHA-Bd module have been shown to enhance the degradation of ester-based polymers, as reported for a cutinase toward PET59 and poly(1,4-butylene adipate) PBA69 or a polyamidase toward polyester polyurethanes,70 suggesting that this polymer binding module can be used to tune enzyme adsorption. In this study, the PaAA9E-PHA-Bd chimera LPMO was the main focus of our investigations due to its striking polymer-binding profile across all of the tested pH values and its high expression levels. It is worth noting that this chimera was the least affected by the pH variation, as supported by the computed charge heatmaps. Notably at pH 8.0, an overall charge (e per aa) of approximately 0 would indicate a main contribution of hydrophobic interactions during polymer binding, explaining for instance, the significant higher binding to PE compared to the native enzyme. Unexpectedly, by creating PHA-Bd containing chimeras we highlighted a way to improve the binding of enzymes to a polyolefin polymer, for which the search for sustainable recycling methods is currently the object of intense research.71

Finding solutions to improve enzyme-polymer proximity is the first challenge on the long road to engineer bona fide plastic-active enzymes. The observation that the fusion of noncatalytic modules to an LPMO led to variable polymer binding profiles, without affecting the catalytic domain, which retained its native capability to oxidize cellulose, constitutes a promising step to further evolve LPMOs toward unnatural substrates. On this path, as evidenced by GPC and SEM analyses, we have shown here the capacity of the PaAA9E-domain containing LPMOs (WT and PaAA9E-PHA-Bd) to modify PHA. As suggested by improved binding capacity and H2O2 consumption capacity on PHA, we anticipate that PaAA9E-PHA-Bd will be a suitable scaffold for future protein engineering. More generally, we think that the design of such chimeras could also prove useful for the degradation of future generations of hybrid copolymers made of cellulose and synthetic polymers. Indeed, these hybrid materials combine the desirable properties of both cellulose, a renewable and biodegradable natural polymer, and synthetic polymers, which often offer enhanced mechanical strength and durability in service of the materials.72,73 However, their biodegradability remains a challenge due to the complex nature of their structure and questions the great promise they represent in terms of material sustainability criteria. In that respect, the development of chimera enzymes (such as the one presented here) that may target both the cellulose and synthetic components of these hybrid copolymers may offer a promising avenue for addressing their end-of-life management.

LPMOs are promising surface-active redox enzymes that will require further engineering. Beyond the introduction of new polymer binding properties, one can envision a deeper study of the linker region connecting the catalytic domain to the accessory binding module. Linkers can indeed vary in length and flexibility allowing the connected domains to move independently of each other and promote a more efficient catalytic activity.54 Importantly, we emphasize that the levels of LPMO affinity for plastics shown here should not be considered as the sole requirement to warrant oxidative activity on these polymers. As shown for hydrolases,74 the strength of substrate binding comes with “costs and benefits” when it comes to processivity, where enzyme–substrate interactions promoting processivity can be at the expense of enzyme speed. In that respect, our study provides means, by playing on the nature of the accessory module and possibly on reaction conditions, to fine-tune the strength of polymer binding on a case-by-case basis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we believe that the presented chimera LPMOs will constitute a starting point for future directed evolution campaigns aimed at creating bona fide plastic-active oxidative enzymes. By analogy with the role played by LPMO in the development of efficient cellulolytic cocktails and in the functionalization of cellulose for new biomaterials, we foresee that access to plastic-active oxidases will allow the design of efficient “plastilytic” cocktails and neo-functionalized plastics and will open new avenues for the bioconversion of synthetic polymers.
