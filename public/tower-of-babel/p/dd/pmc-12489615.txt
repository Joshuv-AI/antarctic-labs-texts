All-in-One Method for Iterative Single-Cell Dielectrophoretic and Optical Characterization: Advancing the OpenDEP Platform
Authors: Florela Gherghinoiu, Tudor Savopol, Ioan Tivig, Mihaela G. Moisescu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An integrated platform for single-cell biophysical characterization is presented. Combining dielectrophoresis (DEP) and optical tweezers (OT) within a single experiment, this approach enables the extraction of both electric properties and optical trap stiffness from individual, living cells in suspension without the need for external calibration beads, labels, or adherence to surfaces. Unlike traditional population-based DEP methods, which average over large cell ensembles and obscure cellular heterogeneity, the presented single-cell approach provides precise DEP spectra and allows direct computation of electric parameters such as membrane conductivity, permittivity, and cytoplasmic conductivity. The method is compatible with structurally and optically complex particles, such as living cells, overcoming the limitations of calibration procedures designed for spherical, homogeneous particles. It supports repeated testing of the same cell, facilitating dynamic studies of cellular responses to chemical or physical perturbations. OT stiffness measurements are performed directly on nonadherent cells that otherwise would be excluded from surface-based assays. The system includes an open-source software for data acquisition, automated image-based analysis, and OT and DEP forces computing. It is compatible with various electrode geometries, making it broadly adaptable to different experimental designs. Overall, this platform offers a robust, label-free method for high-resolution, single-cell electric and optic profiling, expanding the capabilities of DEP and OT in fundamental research, diagnostics, and bioengineering applications.

Introduction

Understanding single-cell biophysics is crucial as it provides insights into cellular behaviors that are fundamental to numerous biological processes, including development, immune responses, disease progression. Traditional bulk measurements often overlook the heterogeneity among individual cells within a population.

Label-free single-cell methods become more and more relevant, since they keep the cells closer to their native state. Most of the presently used techniques for manipulating and characterizing single cells are based on the so-called “gradient techniques”. If the electric properties of the cell are of interest, dielectrophoresis (DEP) is the method of choice, while, if one wants to get information about mechanical or optical properties of the cell, then optical tweezers (OT) may be used. Both methods are based on the observation that when a dielectric particle is placed in an inhomogeneous electric (case of DEP) or light (case of OT) field, a force is generated on that particle oriented parallel or antiparallel to the gradient of the field intensity, depending on the ratio of the electric polarizabilities (in the case of DEP) or refractive indices (for OT) of the particle to those of the surrounding medium. In case of DEP, the force is given by FDEP=2πr3ε0εmedRe[K(f)]∇|E|2 1 where Re­[K(f)] is the real part of the Clausius-Mossotti factor, r is the radius of the particle, ε0 is the absolute electric permittivity of the vacuum, εmed is the relative permittivity of the medium, and ∇|E|2 is the squared electric field gradient. Re­[K(f)] depends on the AC field frequency, and, depending on the particle permittivity, can be positive or negative, determining the orientation of F DEP: in the same direction (positive DEP, pDEP), or opposite (negative DEP, nDEP) the electric field gradient. The dependence of Re­[K(f)] on the AC frequency is called a DEP spectrum. Depending on the frequency of the field, Re­[K(f)] can be positive (positive DEP, pDEP, when the particle will move along the electric field gradient), negative (negative DEP, nDEP, when the particle will move opposite the gradient) or zero (the frequency corresponding to this situation being called the crossover frequency, CO). Knowledge of the DEP spectrum of a particle (e.g., a living cell), allows to compute its electric parameters (membrane and cytosol permittivities and conductivies), based on the model used. − The shape of the DEP spectrum (mainly the CO frequency) can then be used to design strategies for the separation/purification of cells from heterogeneous populations, in a contactless and label-free manner. −

In case of OT, the force is given by FOT=2πr3nc(m2−1m2+2)∇I 2 where r is the radius of the particle, n is the refractive index of the surrounding medium, and c is the speed of light in vacuum. The term (m2−1m2+2) represents the polarizability contrast factor, where m = n p/n is the relative refractive index between the particle (n p) and the medium (n). ∇I is the gradient of the light intensity. Similarly to DEP, this force can be oriented parallel (when the trapping effect occurs) or antiparallel to the light intensity gradient (if the refractive index of the particle is higher or lower than that of the environment, respectively).

The underlying physical principle for both DEP and OT is the polarizability of the particle relative to its surrounding medium. In dielectrophoresis, this relationship is quantified by the Clausius–Mossotti factor (CMF). A direct analogue exists for optical tweezers, where the force is governed by the contrast in refractive indices.

The fundamental correlation between the CMF for DEP and the polarizability term for OT stems from the Maxwell relation, n 2 = ε r , which equates a material’s refractive index n to the square root of its relative permittivity ε r at a given frequency. Thus, the refractive index contrast that dictates the optical force can be considered the high-frequency equivalent of the permittivity-based CMF.

However, it is crucial to recognize that the operational frequency domains for DEP and OT are separated by many orders of magnitude (typically kilohertz to megahertz for DEP, hundreds of terahertz for OT). This vast difference means that distinct polarization mechanisms are probed. While DEP is sensitive to slower phenomena like interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarization, the force in optical tweezers is determined almost exclusively by the much faster electronic polarization, as heavier charge carriers cannot respond to the rapid oscillations of the light field.

A DEP spectrum is obtained by analyzing the behavior of a cellular suspension in an inhomogeneous electric field, at different frequencies. Based on the known geometry of the electrodes, the gradients of the electric field are computed in each point, and the distribution of the cells relative to this spatial field distribution is analyzed. The higher the F DEP, the faster the redistribution of the cells. This populational analysis is easy to perform, and gives robust and reproducible results. − Its limitation consists in that it gives only average values of electric parameters of the cells in suspension, without considering their intrinsic biological variability (as concerns dimensions, electric and optical parameters).

To respond to this limitation, single-cell-based experiments are required. To appreciate F DEP, the method of choice is to compare it with a known force. An example is the force balance between DEP and a hydrodynamic drag force, as used by Su et al. However, this method results in the loss of the tested cell, thus precluding any repeated measurements on the same object. A more popular method is to equilibrate the F DEP with the optical tweezers force. The first attempt was introduced by Papagiakoumou et al. and later refined by Moisescu et al., but in both cases, the measurement was limited to determine the voltage at which a trapped cell escaped, without yielding a full DEP spectrum. Another approach is described by Luo et al., who implemented a force balance system between DEP and optical tweezers. However, their method calibrates OT stiffness using microspheres and extrapolates that calibration to cells. As such, they did not directly measure the OT stiffness on the cells themselves, introducing potential inaccuracies and precluding truly cell-specific optical characterization. Force calibration of the optical tweezers may be done using one of the following categories of experiments: analysis of the Brownian movement of a trapped particle ,− or measuring the drag force exerted on the trapped particle by a fluid flow. , In the case of living cells, which, when in suspension, are almost spherical but inhomogeneous objects, the first method is less adequate, because the trapping point often does not coincide with the geometrical center of the sphere, thus the random collision of solvent molecules with the cell leads to the appearance of a torque that imparts a rotational movement to the cell. This is not the case when calibration is performed by the dragging force analysis: once the trapped object is in a flow, the trapping and geometric points will align along the flow direction and the cell’s displacement is easy to monitor. The OT force is equilibrated by the dragging force of the fluid flow and the spring model can be used: F=kΔx=6πηrv 3 where F is the force applied to the particle, k is the optical trap stiffness, Δx is the displacement of the particle from the trap center, η is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid, r is the radius of the particle, and v is the fluid velocity relative to the particle.

In this study, a novel approach that combines DEP with OT to achieve comprehensive single-cell biophysical characterization, is presented. This integrated method allows not only to determine the electric properties of individual cells but also the stiffness of the optical trap exerted on that cell. The method facilitates further experimentation without sacrificing or losing the initial cell. Cells characterized this way retain their native properties for subsequent investigations, the method providing thus a robust platform to observe how external factors (physical or chemical) influence the cellular behavior in situ.

An integrative research tool is provided, which comprises an experimental setup and the appropriate software for analyzing the movement of an optically trapped cell when a DEP force is applied.

For the band electrodes (Figure A), the electric field gradient vectors are predominantly parallel to the chip surface (except the immediate proximity of the target), exerting forces mainly along the horizontal axis. This orientation facilitates straightforward interpretation of cell displacement from microscopic imaging data, as the observed cell movements directly correspond to the applied DEP forces. The triangular electrodes configuration (Figure B) induces a significant vertical component in addition to horizontal forces, resulting in an upward force component acting on the trapped cells. Hence, microscopic imaging captures only a projected portion of the true three-dimensional DEP force experienced by the cells, potentially requiring additional considerations in force calculation or calibration.

The quantitative analysis of electric field intensities as a function of horizontal and vertical distances from the target region (electrode edge or apex) is illustrated in Figure C,D. For the band electrodes (Figure C), the electric field intensity shows a relatively smooth, near-linear gradient, implying predictable DEP forces with linear positional dependence. This property simplifies the data analysis, ensuring straightforward cell displacement calculations. Conversely, triangular electrodes (Figure D) display a notably steeper, exponential gradient in electric field intensity, showing increased dependence on exact 3D cell positioning.

High quality fit (R 2 > 0.98) for the 3D surface plots validates the numerical accuracy of the simulations, demonstrating the reliability of the simulated electric field gradients to be used in experimental DEP forces calculations.

Method Validation

Figure illustrates a comparative analysis between population-based and single-cell DEP spectral acquisition methods (Figure A), along with corresponding OT stiffness measurements obtained via DEP and drag force methods (Figure B).

(A) DEP spectra obtained by population-based methods (3DEP – green, OpenDEP – red) and single-cell methods (band and triangular electrodes - black and blue, respectively). (B) OT stiffness computed based on measurements done with single-cell DEP (bands and triangular electrodes – black and blue, respectively) and drag force (yellow). Buffer conductivity: 0.02 S/m, laser power 36 mW.

In Figure A, DEP spectra from 3DEP (green) and OpenDEP (red) methods are presented alongside the spectra obtained from single-cell methods (bands – black; triangular– blue). The 3DEP spectra were obtained from previously reported data but processed with the updated OpenDEP Compute software. All the other spectra in Figure A were computed using data obtained using the presented lab-made chips. Although all methods reveal similar overall spectral trends, notable differences appear mainly in the negative DEP plateau region. Specifically, single-cell measurements performed using band electrodes clearly exhibit negative DEP, although the corresponding plateau level has higher values than those of both population-based approaches. In contrast, spectra obtained using triangular electrodes show minimal or absent negative DEP, restricting their use primarily to positive DEP characterization.

The observed discrepancies between population-based and single-cell DEP spectra highlight intrinsic methodological distinctions. Population-based methods measure averages of dielectric parameters from approximately 106 cells per sample, inherently averaging across substantial cell-to-cell variability in size, morphology, and dielectric properties. Conversely, single-cell approaches offer direct measurements of cellular dielectric properties, enabling precise identification of individual cell responses and facilitating sequential testing of the same cell (for instance, before and after a specific treatment). Thus, despite the higher statistical robustness of population-based methods, single-cell methods provide valuable insights into individual cell heterogeneity and real-time cellular responses to external stimuli.

The optical tweezers stiffness measurements presented in Figure B compare values obtained through single-cell DEP methods (bands and triangular electrodes) and the hydrodynamic drag force one. The quality of stiffness values derived from single-cell DEP measurements depends directly on the accurate determination of the Clausius–Mossotti (CM) factor. This happens at frequencies corresponding to pDEP plateau; moreover, when the maximal DEP forces occur, they provide higher displacements of the trapped cell, thus ensuring reliable trap stiffness estimations. Small differences in measured stiffness between the triangular and band electrode configurations highlight methodological sensitivities inherent to each electrode geometry. In contrast, stiffness values obtained using the hydrodynamic drag force approach differ somewhat more noticeably, although still within the same order of magnitude. These discrepancies likely arise due to complexities associated with calculating drag forces in proximity to channel walls, where fluid dynamics are notably influenced. −

Nevertheless, the close alignment in stiffness magnitudes across methods validates the consistency of the single-cell DEP-OT combined approach. Stiffness measurements are typically compared against experimental controls obtained by the same methodology, thus small variations in absolute stiffness values across different methods do not detract from the practical utility of the technique.

Given that a DEP spectra acquisition takes a few minutes, it is interesting to evaluate whether there are time-dependent modifications in the electrical and optical parameters of the cells, as well as in their metabolic viability.

Figure A,B depict the DEP spectra and OT stiffness, respectively, recorded sequentially at multiple time intervals (0, 5, 10, and 15 min from the beginning of the first DEP spectrum acquisition) for the same individual cell. The overlapping DEP spectra across these time points (Figure A), together with the consistent OT stiffness values (Figure B), demonstrate minimal to no variation in the dielectric and optical properties of cells over multiple testing intervals. Repeated measurements on individual cells do not induce significant perturbations of the cellular properties.

Furthermore, cell metabolic viability assays (MTS test, Figure C) were conducted to investigate the potential impact of DEP buffer and duration of exposure on cell health. Results indicate only an immediate minor reduction in cell viability after buffer exposure across the used conductivity range (0.02–0.08 S/m). Importantly, the viability remained consistently above 80%, a threshold considered nontoxic. Additionally, viability did not significantly decrease with longer exposure times (up to 15 min), confirming that the experimental protocol is safe for the cells within the conditions tested.

Overall, these results validate the suitability of the presented DEP-OT method for repeated and sequential single-cell characterization experiments, demonstrating that it does not significantly alter cell properties or viability during typical experimental durations.

Both first and second crossover frequencies (A and B), as well as derived electrical parameters including membrane conductivity, permittivity, and cytoplasm conductivity (C, D, and E), exhibit minimal variation across multiple measurements (0–15 min intervals). This result aligns closely with the stable DEP spectra presented in Figure , confirming the reproducibility and temporal stability of cellular electrical properties measured using the proposed DEP-OT methodology.

DEP spectra (A), along with derived first and second crossover frequencies (B and C), exhibit strong overlap across different laser powers (27, 31.5, 36 mW). However, the observed changes are small due to the relatively narrow range of the laser power. Overall, these results validate the robustness and stability of DEP measurements with respect to trapping intensity, while confirming the anticipated dependence of OT stiffness on laser power.

As anticipated from DEP theory and experimentally observed, increasing buffer conductivity shifts the DEP spectrum toward higher first crossover (CO) frequencies (B), reduces the magnitude of pDEP, and amplifies nDEP forces. As concerns the optical tweezers stiffness (Figure D), a clear increase in stiffness accompanies the rise in buffer conductivity. This effect can be attributed primarily to differences in buffer refractive index changes due to different sucrose concentrations. Higher conductivity buffers contain lower sucrose concentrations, having thus a lower buffer refractive index. Given that the cell refractive index (∼1.40 is constant, this reduction in buffer refractive index increases the ratio of the cell refractive index to that of the surrounding medium, thereby enhancing the optical gradient forces and increasing measured optical trap stiffness.

A central strength of the presented methods is its ability to calibrate optical trap stiffness directly on the cell of interest. In many existing systems, stiffness is estimated using spherical microspheres, whose interaction with the cell surface is then studied. , This standard approach is limited to already-adhered cells. In contrast, our technique uses the freely suspended cell itself as both the calibration subject and the probe, enabling in situ mechanical profiling and interaction force measurements between a specific cell and a chemically functionalized surface.

This design is particularly suited for complex cell morphologies (cells are irregular, deformable, and structurally complex) where conventional calibration techniques fail or yield unreliable results. By directly applying and quantifying the force on each cell, the method ensures that optical stiffness values reflect the true mechanical behavior of that individual biological structure, not an artificial proxy.

Furthermore, our method offers not just precision, but also cell-specific dielectric and optical characterization within a single acquisition. Unlike population-based DEP systems, which average over thousands of cells and mask individual differences, this approach captures the unique electric signature and trapping stiffness of each single cell. This enables high-resolution studies of cell-to-cell variability, phenotypic shifts, or responses to external stimuli.

Critically, the technique allows for tracking the variation of a single cell’s dielectric properties under changing physical or chemical conditions. This includes treatments such as electroporation, chemical permeabilization, or exposure to channel inhibitors, pump modulators, or agonists targeting specific membrane receptors. Hence, this method is a powerful tool for real-time monitoring of cellular biophysical responses, expanding its use beyond static characterization to dynamic experimentation.

Conclusions

We have presented an integrated platform for single-cell biophysical analysis that combines dielectrophoresis and optical tweezers into a single, unified experiment. Its novelty consists in enabling the simultaneous calculation of both electric and optical properties of individual living cells, without the need of external probes or calibration beads. By applying quantitatively characterized electric field gradients and simultaneously measuring displacements of optically trapped cells, the system provides both DEP spectra and optical trap stiffness of the same cell in a noninvasive, label-free manner.

Unlike most used DEP methods, which rely on population-based analyses and yield only average values, this approach generates precise, single-cell DEP spectra, allowing to extract individual electric parameters such as membrane conductivity, permittivity, and cytoplasmic conductivity. On the same cell on which the DEP spectrum was recorded, OT stiffness measurements can be directly performed, making thus possible to measure interaction forces of a trapped cell with various substrates. This makes obsolete the requirement of cell adhesion or use of functionalized beads. This is especially valuable for nonadherent or sensitive cells that cannot be anchored without altering their physiological state.

Our approach is robust across different experimental conditions (buffer conductivity, laser power) and has been validated on optically and structurally complex biological cells, which typically do not conform to idealized spherical models. Additionally, the experimental pipeline supports repeated measurements on the same cell, with no observed compromise in cell viability or its electric/optical behavior.

Importantly, the method is supported by a custom-developed, open-source software suite that integrates automated image processing and force computing. The software is modular and independent of the geometry of the DEP electrodes, allowing users to analyze data from a variety of electrode configurations. This flexibility makes the platform highly extensible for a wide range of custom chip designs or biophysical protocols.

In conclusion, this integrated DEP-OT platform provides a powerful and versatile tool for noninvasive, high-resolution single-cell characterization. It offers significant advancements over existing techniques and lays the foundation for future studies on dynamic cellular processes performed on the same living cell (e.g., receptor affinity analysis, cellular drug response, electroporation-induced phenomena).

Materials and Methods

Simulations

Simulations were performed using COMSOL Multiphysics version 6.2, employing the Electric Currents physics interface in a 3D steady-state configuration. Electric field distributions were computed for the two electrode geometries used in the experimental systems: band electrodes and triangular electrodes.

The band electrodes system is the OpenDEP system described in Tivig et al. It consists of a bottom glass slide patterned with parallel indium tin oxide (ITO) bands (each 250 μm wide, spaced 250 μm apart), and a top slide uniformly coated with ITO, serving as a full counter-electrode (the two slides are 500 μm apart). The region of interest is located between the bands, with the target point defined as a point on the edge of an electrode.

The triangular electrodes system, as described by Yadav et al. in 2025, consists of two glass slides (500 μm apart): the bottom one having photolithographically patterned equilateral chromium triangles (1 μm thick, 100 μm edge length), with 100 μm gaps between opposing apexes. In this configuration, the target point is defined as the apex of a triangular electrode.

In both configurations, the cell suspension is in the chamber formed by the two slides. Simulations included only the fluid domain, excluding substrate materials, as they do not significantly affect the electric field under the conditions tested. The medium was modeled with an electrical conductivity of 0.02 S/m and relative permittivity of 78, corresponding to experimental buffer values. A 1 V potential was applied across the electrodes.

Electric field intensities (|E|) were exported from COMSOL and processed in Python, using the scipy.optimize library. A rational function was used to fit the electric field intensities as a function of position in the x–z plane (defined as a plane perpendicular on the chip surface and aligned to the shortest distance between the bottom electrodes, coordinates (0, 0) are those of the target point): E(x,z)=ax+bz+cdx+ez+f 4

This fitting approach enabled to estimate |E| at any point in the x–z plane, which in turn was used to calculate electric field gradients and dielectrophoretic forces.

Software Tools and Data Analysis

A custom Python-based software tool was developed to automate image-based analysis of both optical trap (OT) stiffness and relative dielectrophoretic (DEP) spectra. The software features a graphical user interface (GUI) built with PyQt5, is open-source, and is released as part of the OpenDEP Platform under the GNU General Public License v3. The source code versions are available via GitHub (https://github.com/IoanTivig/OT-DEP-ForceCalculator). The software outputs processed numerical data in CSV format, along with corresponding PNG-format graphical plots.

The tool supports three main analysis modes: 1.Relative DEP Spectra Acquisition: this mode processes time-lapse images of optically trapped cells subjected to ramping frequency DEP fields. The software detects the trapped cell in each frame using a Hough Circle Transform and calculates its displacement (distance between the initial position of the trapped cell and its current position). For each frame, the distance from the cell to the target point is also computed. The distance cell-target point and the parameters obtained from eq (specific to each electrode geometry) are used to estimate the local electric field intensity. A relative DEP force is calculated from these values, yielding the DEP spectrum. The resulting spectra can be exported to the OpenDEP Compute software (https://github.com/IoanTivig/OpenDEP_Compute) for computing the electrical parameters (e.g., membrane conductivity, permittivity, cytoplasm conductivity) using an appropriate model (e.g., single-shell, homogeneous particle model).2.OT Stiffness Calculation (DEP-based): in this mode, the software analyzes images recorded under ramping voltage at a fixed frequency that induces strong DEP forces (either positive or negative). Displacement of an optically trapped cell and the electric field gradient are calculated as described at subsection 3. The DEP force is calculated using the real part of Clausius-Mossotti (CM) factor previously extracted from DEP spectra fitted in OpenDEP Compute. Further, the software fits the DEP force vs displacement and computes the OT stiffness using Hooke’s law formula.3.OT Stiffness Calculation (Drag-based): this mode analyzes images of optically trapped cells exposed to a ramping flow profile. The cell displacement induced by the flow is computed as before. The Stokes drag force is calculated based on the known flow rate and the cell radius, with optional near-wall corrections applied. − The resulting force vs displacement is fitted to determine the OT stiffness using the Stokes law.

Cells and Chemicals

All experiments were performed using the NIH 3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line. Cells were cultured in 25 cm2 flasks containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Sigma D5796, UK), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma F7524, Brazil), and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were harvested using Trypsin-EDTA solution (Sigma T4174, USA) and resuspended in fresh DMEM. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at room temperature, and the resulting pellet was gently washed 3 times and resuspended in one of the DEP buffer solutions (conductivities ranging from 0.02 to 0.08 S/m).

The DEP buffers were prepared by mixing two base solutions at defined ratios: a saline sucrose buffer (250 mM sucrose, 8 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2; pH 7.4; 286 mOsm/kg; conductivity 1280 (500 μS/cm) and a 300 mM sucrose solution. Final cell concentrations were quantified using a Bio-Rad TC10 Automated Cell Counter (USA) and adjusted according to the experimental requirements: 5 × 106 cells/mL for population-based DEP experiments and 5 × 105 cells/mL for single-cell assays.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the MTS assay (CellTiter 96 AQueous, G3582; Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were cultured to approximately 50% confluency prior to exposure to DEP buffer solutions of varying electrical conductivities (0.02, 0.04, 0.06, and 0.08 S/m). Incubation times were set at 5, 10, and 15 min, respectively. Following exposure, MTS reagent was added, and absorbance was measured using a UV/vis plate reader (Awareness Technology, USA). The resulting absorbance values were normalized against the untreated control group.

Population DEP Spectra Acquisition

The cell suspension was introduced in an OpenDEP chamber and 17 frequencies of AC electric field ranging from 5 kHz to 50 MHz with an amplitude of 10 V peak-to-peak were applied. Microscopic images of the entire chip area were taken before and after 60 s for each frequency. To ensure uniform cell distribution, the suspension was gently agitated with a syringe between each frequency acquisition.

Single-Cell DEP Spectra Acquisition

The cell suspension was introduced in one of the DEP chambers (either triangular or band electrodes) and a cell was optically trapped. In the triangular electrode system, the cell was positioned between two opposing electrodes apexes closer to one of them. In the case of the band electrode system, the cell was positioned between two bands, closer to one of them. In both cases, the image captured both the cell and the electrode.

For both chambers, an initial image was captured to record the cell’s position within the optical trap, prior to the application of the electric field. Same AC field frequencies as in the case of population DEP experiments were then applied, and, for each frequency, an image was acquired. The system was allowed to equilibrate for 5 s before image acquisition. The electric field amplitude was such as to keep the cell optically trapped, while its displacement under the DEP field was clearly observable.

DEP-Based OT Stiffness Acquisition

To obtain the OT stiffness, a cell whose DEP spectrum was previously determined by the described single-cell procedure was used. This cell was optically trapped and positioned at a distance from the target at which the vertical component of the DEP force is negligible (minimum cca. one cellular diameter). The cell was then exposed to a stepwise increasing electric potential (at 2 MHz, belonging to the positive DEP plateau), starting from 1 Vpp, in 0.1 Vpp steps, with a 5-s stabilization period between each increment to allow the system to reach equilibrium. At each voltage step, an image of the trapped cell was recorded, and the corresponding cell displacement was computed. The resulting displacement data were further used to compute the DEP force (using the Clausius-Mossotti factor value at 2 MHz) and derive the trap stiffness via Hooke’s law.

Drag Force-Based OT Stiffness Acquisition

To validate the stiffness values obtained by the single-cell DEP-OT method, the standard hydrodynamic drag force approach was employed. To do this, a custom microfluidic chip consisting of a straight rectangular microchannel (section of 8 × 3 mm) was prototyped. Fluid dynamics were simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics to confirm laminar flow conditions across the full range of experimental flow rates (see Supporting Information Figure S2). This ensured the validity of applying Stokes law, with optional near-wall corrections − , to calculate the drag force exerted on the trapped cell. The fluid flow was controlled by a pressure-controlled microfluidic flow controller (Fluigent Flow EZ).

Once the cell suspension was loaded in the chip, a single cell was optically trapped inside the chip and subjected to an incremental fluid flow (starting from 100 μL/min up to removal of the cell from the trap, with an increment of 10 μL/min), and an image was captured at each step after 5 s of stabilization. The cell’s initial position, recorded prior to flow application, served as a reference point. At each flow rate displacement and drag force were processed using the updated OpenDEP software. Stiffness was then computed by fitting the force–displacement relationship.

Experimental Categories

The study was structured around several experimental categories aimed at validating the reliability, accuracy, and flexibility of the single-cell DEP-OT method: (1) comparison of population DEP (OpenDEP and 3DEP) with single-cell DEP methods; (2) band and triangular electrode systems comparison; (3) validation using drag force experiments; (4) reproducibility in time on the same cell (0, 5, 10, and 15 min from the beginning of the first DEP spectrum acquisition); (5) laser power effects (27, 31.5, and 35 mW); (6) buffer conductivity effects within the range 0.02–0.08 S/m. These categories formed the foundation for a robust evaluation of the proposed system across multiple physical and experimental conditions.

Data Processing and Sample Size

All data analysis was performed using custom Python scripts in conjunction with the OpenDEP software tools. The statistical analysis and curve fitting routines were implemented using NumPy and SciPy libraries. For experiments involving optical trap stiffness or DEP spectra, only data with a correlation coefficient above 0.85 were used for analysis.

For all reported measurements, standard deviation was used to represent error, displayed as shaded areas (for curves) or error bars (for single-point data). Viability data tests were processed using Microsoft Excel, followed by statistical analysis in Python.
