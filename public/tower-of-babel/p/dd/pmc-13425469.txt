Sustainable Synthesis of Quinolones and the Main Based-Drug Ivacaftor by Aquoline
Authors: Marialaura Frisina, Rosa Scarpelli, Giuseppina De Luca, Alessandro Gualtieri, Monica Nardi, Antonio Procopio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The classic drug synthesis methods applied by the pharmaceutical industry are a major source of toxic and environmentally harmful waste. Although the continuous search for increasingly effective drugs is essential, chemists and chemical engineers involved in production processes have a responsibility to adopt practices aligned with the principles of green chemistry. In this context, aquiline, a combination of choline chloride (ChCl) and water in a 1:3.33 molar ratio, representing one of the earliest examples of natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) and water-in-salt (WiS) systems, is explored under different conditions as an environmental reaction medium for the synthesis of several 4-quinolone derivatives. These compounds represent key pharmacophores and essential intermediates in the development of various drugs. Notably, the same reaction system is crucial to obtain Ivacaftor, a commercially available drug for managing cystic fibrosis (CF).

Introduction

Quinolones are a class of compounds characterized by a bicyclic aromatic core, composed of a fused six-membered heterocyclic ring containing one to four nitrogen atoms. Carbonyl group presence in the quinoline nucleus gives rise to oxo-quinolines/quinolones, particularly to 4-oxo-quinoline and 2-oxo-quinoline (Figure ).

2-Oxo-quinoline (also referred to as 2-oxo-1,2-dihydroquinoline or as 2­(1H)-quinolone) and 4-oxo-quinoline (also referred to as 4-oxo-1,4-dihydroquinoline or 4­(1H)-quinolone) structures.

The structural motif derived from a quinolone scaffold is prominent in a wide range of chemotherapeutic and antimicrobial medicines. , Among them, 4-quinolone derivatives are not only a significant class of antibacterial, antidiabetic, anticancer, and antibiotics agents but also a useful structural moiety for other drug candidates.

Ivacaftor [N-(2,4-di-tert-butyl-5-hydroxyphenyl)-4-oxo-1,4-dihydro-quinoline-3-carboxamide] (Figure ) is a quinolinone-based oral FDA-approved medication to treat cystic fibrosis. Genetic alterations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene result in the construction of a defective CFTR protein, which causes cystic fibrosis to manifest through decreased anion secretion and an imbalance in ion and fluid flow leading to accumulation of thick, sticky mucus that can cause blockages, damage or infections in the affected organs. −

The antibiotic and antimicrobic potential of Ivacaftor was predicted from its structural analogy to quinolone antibiotics, ,− which facilitates interactions with binding sites through hydrogen bonding, promoting pharmacological activity. Its bicyclic structure and interactions improve the CFTR control channel by stabilizing its open state. Previous works studied the effect on corrected CFTR protein of different substituents at the phenolic ring and different substituents at the quinolinone positions.

Over the last 30 years, it has become increasingly urgent to address the serious environmental problems associated with industrial production activities, first the pharmaceutical sector which generates abundant quantities of waste associated with most of the classic synthetic methodologies.

Taking this need into consideration, the ACS Green Chemistry Institute Pharmaceutical Roundtable (GCIPR) (Washington, DC, USA) has developed a Reagent Guide to support and direct chemists toward more sustainable reagents to be applied in different chemical transformations. The replacement of toxic and flammable solvents, often fundamental for the synthesis of classic drugs, with eco-sustainable solvents, represents one of the first goals to be achieved for a new policy that looks at protecting the environment.

The industrial processes used to synthesize Ivacaftor do not escape from this logic. Indeed, the synthetic method developed by Vertex Pharmaceuticals to yield the quinolone carboxylic acid ester, a key intermediate in the Ivacaftor synthesis, involves the Gould–Jacobs approach (Scheme A) , which uses high reaction temperature and harmful reagents. Larus pharma and Shen et al. reported the multistep synthesis of Ivacaftor starting from o-nitro and o-halo benzoyl chloride, respectively. The process proposed by Iazzi et al. involves the use of diphenyl ether as reaction solvent in the reflux condition for long reaction time (Scheme B), while Koskinen et al. proposed the synthesis of enamine by condensation of o-nitroacetophenone with N,N-dimethylformamide dimethylacetal in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (Scheme C).

Classic and One-Pot Synthesis of 4-Quinolones

One-pot synthetic processes have been reported recently using expensive catalysts or the Witkop–Winterfeldt oxidation − (Scheme D,E respectively), but these processes involve long reaction times, the use of toxic organic solvents, and drastic reaction conditions. In the same time, recently a TsCl-mediated reaction for the synthesis of 4-quinolone scaffolds using commercially available chromone-3-carboxaldehydes and amines is proposed. The strategy applied for the subsequent coupling and synthesis of Ivacaftor still involves the use of poorly eco-sustainable solvents and the use of coupling agents such as HBTU. Recently acid-amine coupling of 4-quinolone-3-carboxylic acid and 5-amino-2,4-di-tert-butyl-phenol (1 mol), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)­carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl) as a coupling reagent (2.8 molar) was evaluated using methanol as activator hand mechanical grinding in a mortar and pestle under ambient temperature. Although both EDC·HCl and methanol are widely used in organic synthesis, they are associated with known toxicological and safety concerns and are not considered ideal from a green chemistry perspective. Recently, we have developed several synthetic − and extraction − methods of compounds useful for the synthesis of drug candidates that involve the exclusive use of environmentally friendly solvents such as water, even under subcritical conditions by microwave irradiation, and deep eutectic solvents (DESs). ,

DESs have received encouraging interest in a wide selection of chemical transformations , as new green solvents synthesized by mixing a hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) (generally a quaternary salt) and a hydrogen bond donor (HBD) (e.g., organic acids, alcohol, sugars and diols, amines, amino acids, etc.).

Such components are cheap, easily available, and often present in our daily food, − like the nutritional additive component choline chloride, which is the most used hydrogen bond acceptor in the formation of DES.

Now, in this work, we present a new highly efficient synthesis of different 4-quinolones induced by Aquoline, one of the first natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) water-in-salt (WiS) − formed by choline chloride:water at a ratio of 1:3.33 (Scheme ).

Experimental Section

Instruments and Materials

1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded at 25 °C on a Bruker AC 500 spectrometer in CDCl3 solutions at 300 and 75 MHz, respectively, with TMS (tetramethylsilane) as the internal standard. All reactions were observed by TLC on silica Merck 60 F254 precoated aluminum plates. MW-assisted reactions for the synthesis of quinolones were performed on a MW instrument Synthos 3000 from Anton Paar, configured with a 4 × 24MG5 Rotor and an IR probe used to control the external temperature. The MW-assisted reaction synthesis of Ivacaftor was carried out using a CEM Discover microwave reactor in a high-energy density laboratory setup, equipped with an external IR sensor for temperature monitoring. Experiments were conducted in dynamic mode using borosilicate glass vessels fitted with a silicon cap and septum.

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) measurements were performed using a Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, operating in both negative and positive ion modes. The resolving power was set to 35.000 (fwhm at m/z 200), with an ion injection time (IT) of 100 ms and ACG target of 500.000. Full scan analyses were conducted over a mass range of 140–900 amu for 2 samples and 200–1500 amu for other 2 samples. The source parameters were as follows: spray voltage, 2.9 kV; sheath gas, 30 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas, 10 arbitrary units; probe heater temperature, 280 °C; capillary temperature, 320 °C; and S-Lens RF Level, 50. The instrument was calibrated prior to analysis using standard Thermo calibration solutions.

Commercially available reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (Milano, Italy) and used without further purification unless otherwise specified.

Methods

Procedure for DESs’ Preparation

The DESs [ChCl:urea (1:1) and ChCl:water (1:3.33) were prepared as follows: choline chloride (6.98 g, 50 mmol) was combined with either urea (50 mmol) or water (166 mmol) in a round-bottom flask. The mixture was magnetically stirred at 80 °C for 60 min until a clear, colorless liquid was obtained. The resulting DESs were used without further purification.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of 4-Quinolone Esters (1a-14a)

General Procedure for the Synthesis of 4-Oxo-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic Acid (1c)

The ester hydrolysis was performed according to the procedure reported in the literature with only slight variations. 4-Hydroxyquinoline-3-carboxylic acid ethyl ester (1 mol) in suspension in sodium hydroxide solution (2N, 2.2 L) was stirred for 24 h at room temperature. After cooling, the mixture was filtered and the filtrate was adjusted to pH 4 with 2 N HCl. The resulting precipitate was collected via filtration, washed with water and dried under vacuum to afford 4-oxo-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic acid as a pale white solid (1c).

Recycling of Aquoline

The recyclability of Aquoline was evaluated during the reaction of 4-quinolone 1a synthesis under optimized conditions. Upon completion of the reaction, AcOEt (3 mL) was added and the reaction mixture was stirred for 5 min. The extraction process was performed twice more, after which the combined organic layers were washed with water (3 × 3 mL), dried over Na2SO4, filtered, and evaporated under reduced pressure to afford the crude reaction product 1a. Any residual volatile organic solvent remaining present in the aqueous phase was removed by vacuum evaporation. The recovered Aquoline was then reused in a subsequent reaction cycle by adding fresh aniline and DEMM. The resulting mixture was processed following the same procedure described above, and additional cycles were carried out using the recycled DES phase (Aquoline).

General Procedure for the Synthesis of Ivacaftor (1c)

The Ivacaftor synthesis was performed according to the procedure reported in the literature by Romano et al. using Aquoline for direct amidation. The use of ChCl alone does not allow the formation of the desired product. 0.936 mol of acid (1b), 0.936 mol of amine ((5-amino-2,4-di-tert-butylphenol, ATBUF), and 0.936 mol of ChCl in Aquoline were inserted into vessels equipped with a silicon cap and septum.

The mixture was heated in the MW reactor (CEM Discover Sp) at 80 °C for 6 reaction cycles lasting 3 min in “dynamic mode” (P 80W), and the temperature was read with an IR probe.

Solid reagents were heated in an oil bath, up to 80 °C, for 2 min before inserting them into the microwave oven. The reaction was monitored by TLC and LC/MS.

The reaction mixture was then poured in 10 mL of AcOEt and 10 mL of a saturated Na2CO3 solution was added (×3). After phase separation, the organic phase was taken, 10 mL of a saturated solution of CuSO4 (×3) was added, and the organic phases collected were dried over Na2SO4, followed by evaporation under reduced pressure to give the corresponding products (1c).

The same reaction mixture under MW irradiation showed the quinolone ester formation after 10 min but with unsatisfactory yield. Considering previous research on ChCl-based DESs as catalysts and green solvents, our next approach was to study the use of ChCl/urea (1:1 ratio molar) (entries 4–6, Table ) in the same reaction process. The eutectic mixture was prepared by mixing ChCl and urea under heating to 80 °C for 2 h to obtain a colorless, viscous product. In this case, the complete consumption of the aniline was observed; however, after 60 min under conventional heating, the reaction mixture contained both the cyclized product and the reaction intermediate (entries 4–5, Table ). The same outcome was observed under MW irradiation (entry 6, Table ).

Since in our previous research we proved the role of ChCl in creating molecular networks capable of activating and catalyzing synthetic transformation processes, we tested in the same condition the simple ChCl as Hydrogen-Bonding Solvent/Catalytic Medium (HBSCM) of the model reaction in no-solvent condition.

The results obtained confirmed the importance of the presence of water and its hydrogen bonds in the structural system of Aquoline (entries 7 and 8, Table ).

Based on the results obtained using water as green solvent and ChCl/urea as DES (entries 1–6, Table ), we considered it appropriate to investigate the effectiveness of an “unconventional DES” composed of ChCl and water in a 1:3.33 molar ratio. This mixture, named Aquoline, was studied by Triolo et al. and identified as a complex structure arising from the choline cation with water molecules and the chloride anion, dispersed within a bulk phase characterized by extended hydrogen-bonded water networks formed through water–water and water–chloride interactions. Aquoline showed good results already at room temperature after 60 min (entry 9, Table ) producing the formation of intermediate product (40% in yield). The reaction mixture showed only quinolone ester (1a) (entry 10, Table ) at 60 °C after 10 min. The same excellent result was observed under microwave irradiation (entry 11, Table ).

A probable mechanism for the formation of the 4-quinolone ring in Aquoline is proposed as follows. In the hydrogen-bonding solvent/catalytic medium (HBSCM), the carbonyl group of the activated Michael acceptor is polarized through a network of hydrogen-bond interactions involving choline chloride and bound water molecules. This activation increases the electrophilicity of the β-carbon and facilitates the conjugate (Michael) addition of the aromatic amine. Following the C–N bond formation, the resulting enolate intermediate is stabilized by hydrogen bonding within the Aquoline matrix. Subsequent intramolecular cyclization occurs via nucleophilic attack on the carbonyl group, leading to ring closure and formation of the quinolone scaffold. Proton transfer and rearomatization then afford the final 4-quinolone product.

Spectroscopic data, particularly 1H NMR, of product 1a show the product formation and its tautomeric form.

Quinolone derivatives are known to exist in equilibrium between the keto (4-quinolone, 1a) and enol (4-hydroxyquinoline, 1b) tautomers as depicted in Figure (See Supporting Information). In the present study, consideration of this tautomerism is relevant solely to the interpretation of the NMR data. In particular, the 13C NMR spectra display distinct carbonyl resonances that can be rationalized by the coexistence of keto and enol forms, with the ketonic carbon resonating at higher chemical shift values than the ester carbonyl. The observed signal splitting is therefore consistent with tautomeric contributions and supports the assigned cyclic structure.

Surprisingly, the reaction model is shown to be efficient for both reaction times (10–20 min) and yields (85–100%) (Table , entries 1–14).

While m-aminophenol allowed the formation of its respective quinolone (Table , entry 14, 14 a), no trace of the quinolone derived from o-aminophenol was detected. The presence of the hydroxyl group in the ortho position enables the formation of an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the amino group. Such an interaction, together with the different acidity and basicity of the two functional groups, is expected to reduce the nucleophilicity and availability of the amine, thereby disfavoring the Michael addition to the α,β-unsaturated ester.

Aquoline could be recovered and reused for four consecutive cycles in the synthesis of 1a affording high yields (quantitative yield for fresh Aquoline, 98% in yield in the first recycle, 97%, 95%, and 93% in yield in the second, third, and fourth recycle, respectively).

To demonstrate the potential practical applicability of this fast and ecofriendly method, the model reaction leading 1a was performed on a 1 mol scale using 2,42 L of Aquoline at 60 °C under conventional heating. The reaction was completed within 30 min, affording a 90% isolated yield after the addition of water followed by extraction with ethyl acetate.

While the sustainability is only associated with higher AE and RME values, along with lower E-factors and RMC values, the significance of the first molecular weight moment per reaction stage (μ1) requires further clarification. The parameter μ1 reflects the extent of molecular degradation or growth at each step of the reaction pathway leading to the final product. A positive overall μ1 value indicates a decrease in molecular weight across the reaction steps, whereas a negative overall μ1 value indicates an increase in molecular weight during the process.

On the other hand, tree-related parameters also play a significant role in assessing the sustainability of a synthetic route. The P ordinate represents the extent of the mass reduction from the starting materials to the final product; therefore, a lower P value indicates a more mass-efficient process.

Besides, the degree of convergence (δ) indicates many of the starting materials employed in the synthetic pathway are ultimately incorporated into the final product. Its value ranges from 0 to 1: a value of 1 corresponds to a fully convergent process (e.g., a multicomponent reaction), whereas 0 reflects a completely linear sequence in which none of the initial materials are retained in the intermediates of subsequent steps.

With regard to the key metrics, the synthetic strategies appear largely comparable to one another, with the main difference being a significant improvement in overall yield compared to the methods reported by Iazzi et al. and by Gould and Jacobs.

The μ1 value and the RMC of our process are slightly higher than those reported in the literature; however, this drawback is largely offset by the higher yield of the valuable final product. More in detail, considering that the commercial value of Ivacaftor (by Aldrich catalogue) is 26.1 €/mg, starting by 1 mol of aniline, our protocol produces 376 mmol of Ivacaftor corresponding to a value of 3895 MM€, compared to the 286 and 299 mmol of Ivacaftor produced by the method of Iazzi et al. and Gould and Jacobs corresponding to 2931 MM€ and 3067 MM€, respectively. The proposed method, therefore, in addition to promoting the fundamental principles of Green Chemistry with greater energy savings and elimination of toxic reagents and solvents also guarantees significant economic savings.

Conclusions

Here, we present a new economical and environmentally friendly method for the synthesis of 4-quinolones in Aquoline where ChCl plays the role of reaction HBSCM. Our method features no use of organic solvents as reaction media, whereas the purification process employs only eco-sustainable solvents (simple ethyl acetate and water). Furthermore, the role of the same reaction medium, after quinolic ester hydrolysis, has been exploited for the subsequent coupling with 5-amino-2,4-di-tert-butylphenol to obtain the market drug Ivacaftor. The use of Aquoline to afford 4-quinolones improves the easy recovery process of the reaction medium compared to classic dry DES recycle. The advantages of this method (Kernel reaction metric calculations showed the environmental and economic sustainability of synthetic process proposed) makes the synthetic strategy highly sustainable and promotable on a large scale.

The total synthesis of this interesting drug via this sequence reduces the original synthesis from seven/eight steps to three steps, with a high overall yield under environmental conditions.

The use of choline chloride-based Aquoline proved to be an efficient and sustainable reaction medium. Choline chloride exhibits very low toxicity, is readily biodegradable, and is approved for use in food and animal feed as a nutritional supplement, as recognized by regulatory authorities such as the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Its favorable safety profile, combined with its effectiveness in promoting the reaction, highlights Aquoline as an environmentally benign and practical alternative to conventional solvents, which is fully consistent with the principles of green chemistry.
