GNAQ mutation in primary spinal melanoma: illustrative case
Authors: Cristina Hayes Meizoso, Janelle P. Renterghem, Constance J. Mietus, Brittany Owusu-Adjei, Thomas W. Smith, David Cachia, Ali Tasneem, Arno S. Sungarian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPrimary melanoma in the spine is a rare tumor characterized by lack of primary dermal or uveal lesions. The infrequency of this tumor significantly obfuscates both its diagnosis and treatment.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe the case of a 74-year-old male who presented with back pain, lower extremity weakness, and numbness. The diagnosis of primary spinal melanoma was confirmed by histopathology, immunohistochemical studies, and lack of primary lesions on dermatological and ophthalmological examination. GNAQ mutations lead to abnormal β-catenin activation in neural crest cells, resulting in differentiation into melanoblasts and migration to the spinal cord. Primary melanoma in the CNS is a rare neoplasm that accounts for 1% of all melanoma cases,1 as opposed to melanoma metastasis to the CNS with an incidence of 40%–60% clinically and around 80% at the time of autopsy.2 Primary isolated spinal melanoma is even more rare, with fewer than 70 documented cases in the literature. The infrequency of this lesion makes it difficult to diagnose and treat. Primary melanoma of the CNS can be distinguished from metastatic tumors or secondary CNS melanoma according to the criteria proposed by Hayward: absence of melanoma outside the CNS, absence of a lesion in another area of the CNS, and histological confirmation of melanoma.1

Primary spinal melanoma lesions are most frequently found in the mid- or lower thoracic spine in the 5th decade of life.3 Spinal lesions can be classified as intradural or extradural and intramedullary or extramedullary. The incidence of intradural extramedullary primary melanomas is approximately 62% and that of intramedullary is approximately 38% of cases.4–6 MRI is the preferred method for diagnosis with a characteristic hyperintensity on T1-weighted images, homogeneous contrast enhancement on T1-weighted enhanced images and isointense or hypointense signals on T2-weighted images.7 Immunohistochemistry is positive for melanoma markers including human melanoma black (HMB45) and S100.8

It has previously been proposed that primary malignant melanoma of the CNS originates from either differentiation of leptomeningeal melanoblasts9 or faulty migration and differentiation of neuroectodermal crest cells.10 However, the precise mechanism has not been hypothesized. We present a rare case of primary spinal melanoma with a guanine-nucleotide binding protein (GNAQ) mutation. To our knowledge, this is the fifth reported case of primary spinal melanoma with a GNAQ mutation in the literature. We review potential pathomechanisms leading to the formation of these lesions, which may provide insight for possible diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

Illustrative Case

A 74-year-old male with past medical history of diabetes and sciatica presented with a 3-month history of progressive worsening low back pain, bilateral lower extremity weakness, and intermittent urinary incontinence. The patient’s physical examination was significant for weakness in the proximal iliopsoas and quadriceps muscles, gait disturbances, absent ankle jerk reflexes, and paresthesia in the left lateral thigh and bilateral posterior calves. Dermatological and ophthalmological evaluation revealed no primary skin or ophthalmic lesions. MRI of the thoracic spine revealed a 1.5 cm × 1.6 cm × 2.7–cm contrast enhancing, intradural extramedullary lesion at the level of T11–12, causing mass effect on the spinal cord and notable leptomeningeal enhancement of the lower thoracic cord and at L5–S1 (Fig. 1). The patient underwent a T11–12 laminectomy and resection of the intradural extramedullary lesion. Subtotal resection was achieved due to risk of nerve injury and extent of leptomeningeal involvement. The final pathology was consistent with malignant melanoma based on immunohistochemical findings with positive S100, HMB45, MART-1 and SOX10 expression (Fig. 2A–D). The Ki-67 index was approximately 12%. Given the absence of a primary dermal or uveal lesion, next-generation sequencing was performed and demonstrated a GNAQ Q209P missense mutation. Sequencing was negative for BRAF, KIT, or NRAS mutations, which are commonly mutated in melanoma.11 Postoperative MRI demonstrated resection of the thoracic lesion and decompression of the spinal cord, with continued leptomeningeal enhancement in the lower thoracic cord and L5–S1 (Fig. 2E–G). The patient recovered well from surgery, and his lower extremity strength and paresthesia were significantly improved at 1-month follow-up. He received adjuvant local radiation therapy at T10–11 and L4–S3 and immunotherapy consisting of two doses of a combination regimen with ipilimumab (3 mg/kg) and nivolumab (1 mg/kg). Day 1 after completing the second cycle of combination immunotherapy, which was approximately 4 months postoperatively, PET imaging revealed increased uptake at the resection site, concerning for recurrent disease. MRI of the brain and cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine demonstrated progression of leptomeningeal disease. The patient also began to have worsening gait instability. He subsequently declined further therapy, elected to pursue hospice care, and later died. The patient’s overall survival (OS) was 11 months from time of diagnosis. Discussion

Observations

Etiology

We present a rare case of primary spinal melanoma harboring a GNAQ mutation, which represents the fifth reported case of primary spinal melanoma with a GNAQ mutation (Table 1). Primary malignant melanomas of the CNS are believed to originate either from the differentiation of melanoblasts within the leptomeninges, where they are typically found, or from the improper migration and differentiation of neuroectodermal crest cells.10 After the neural plate is formed during embryogenesis, the plate begins to fold to form the neural tube. Neural crest cells are highly migratory,9 multipotent stem cells that emerge from the edges of the neural tube from which both melanocytes and neural cells are derived. Following their formation, the cells undergo an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, after which they begin migrating to different parts of the embryo.12 Trunk neural crest cells arise in the posterior portion of the embryo and eventually generate melanocytes, nervous tissue, and the endocrine system. These cells travel through one of three pathways: a dorsolateral pathway between the ectoderm and the somites, a ventrolateral pathway between somites, and a ventromedial pathway between the neural tube and the posterior sclerotome.13 Typically, neural crest cells migrating along the dorsolateral pathway become melanoblasts and ultimately, melanocytes, and thus travel to the skin and hair follicles. Major signaling molecules and pathways involved in the migration and differentiation of the neural crest cells include Wnt signaling (especially Wnt1 and Wnt3a), bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs, specifically BMP4 and BMP7), fibroblast growth factors, Sonic hedgehog, and Notch.13 The two signaling molecules most closely tied to separation of skin from nervous system tissue during embryogenesis are Wnt and BMP. Wnt signaling stimulates β-catenin translocation into the nucleus, ultimately activating transcription factors for genes that favor epidermal differentiation.15 Inhibition of Wnt is necessary for development of neural tissue, whereas activation of Wnt signaling favors melanocyte formation. Additionally, Wnt signaling plays a crucial role in the migration of neural crest cells by regulating cytoskeletal dynamics and cell adhesion.13

Murine studies have further demonstrated the critical role of Wnt/β-catenin in embryonic differentiation of neural tissues versus melanocytes. β-catenin signal activation in migratory neural crest cells was found to lead to ectopic melanoblast formation in several different areas of the mouse embryo, including the CNS.15 A potential mechanism of melanoma precursor production involves β-catenin signal activation during the neural crest cell migration to or through the dorsolateral pathway, leading to the migration of the cell to one of the ventral pathways and thus causing ectopic melanoblast formation (and eventual melanocyte formation). Once in the CNS, these ectopic cells likely remain dormant and may later become activated to form a melanoma.

GNAQ mutations, particularly in codon 209, have been well documented in the development of uveal melanomas and dermal melanocytic tumors. In an investigation of 19 primary melanocytic lesions of the CNS by Küsters-Vandevelde et al., researchers found somatic GNAQ-209 mutations in 37% of tumors, predominantly around the spinal cord.16 It is a proto-oncogene that codes for a guanine-nucleotide binding protein that activates phospholipase C-beta.17 GNAQ is involved in endothelin signaling, which is essential for melanocyte survival during early development.18

Once mutated, GNAQ causes a constitutively active GTP-bound state, thus activating the MAP-kinase RAS/RAF pathway, which is involved in cell proliferation19 and elevation of levels of activator protein 1 (AP1)20 a transcription factor that promotes gene expression (Fig. 4). Mutated GNAQ also constitutively activates the β-catenin pathway through the activation of ADP-ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6).21 Subsequently β-catenin translocates to the nucleus where it activates T-cell factor (TCF), which, when bound to β-catenin acts, as an additional transcription factor activating the expression of target genes (Fig. 4).21 This increased gene expression contributes to DNA instability, ultimately promoting tumorigenesis over time. Inhibition of GNAQ-mediated β-catenin pathway activation has been shown to reduce cell proliferation. In a study of leukemic stem cells, a selective inhibitor of the GNAQ activated β-catenin pathway was shown to reduce cell number, decrease colony formation, and increase apoptosis.22 Thus, selective molecular targeting of mutated GNAQ or its downstream pathways may present therapeutic potential in tumors that harbor this mutation. We propose that the aberrant Wnt/β-catenin signaling could have been involved in association with our patient’s GNAQ mutation and the development of his primary spinal melanoma. The predilection of primary CNS melanomas with GNAQ mutations to occur within the spine may be secondary to ectopic melanocytes that undergo malignant transformation secondary to aberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin and MAP-kinase RAS/RAF pathways.16

Prognosis and Treatment

Review of the literature revealed a variable survival duration for patients with primary spinal melanoma, ranging from 3 months to 13 years.23 Primary spinal melanoma is treated primarily by excision and commonly involves adjuvant radiation therapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy. In a 2018 PRISMA systematic review of primary malignant spinal melanoma, analysis of treatment for 59 patients revealed that 47.45% (n = 28) of patients underwent surgery only, 45.76% (n = 27) received adjuvant therapy after surgery, and 6.77% (n = 4) received only adjuvant therapy.24 Extent of resection was not associated with mortality; however, surgery combined with adjuvant therapy yielded a longer median overall survival (OS; 72 months) than patients who received adjuvant therapy alone (5 months).24 Another 2024 review on primary spinal melanoma found that the mean survival time for patients who have undergone excision of spinal cord melanoma, with or without additional treatment, was approximately 79 months from the onset of symptoms.23 The longest documented OS for patients with primary spinal melanoma with GNAQ mutation was 2 years; however, reported cases were limited to short interval follow up (Table 1).10,25–27 Thus it remains unclear if the presence of a GNAQ mutation confers worse survival. Due to the rarity of primary spinal melanoma, treatment protocols are not yet standardized. Local recurrence and/or leptomeningeal dissemination are notable concerns for patient outcomes.6 The 2018 systematic review of primary spinal melanoma by Zhang et al. revealed that metastasis or dissemination of primary spinal lesions was a common feature that was associated with worse prognosis. Intramedullary and extramedullary tumors were associated with similar OS, and no survival benefit was observed between cervical, thoracic, or lumbar locations.24

Limitations

Although we hypothesize a potential mechanism by which primary spinal melanomas may develop based on molecular analysis and histopathological stains, our study is limited, as it is a sample size of one. Additionally, the broader implications of our findings are constrained by the low incidence of GNAQ-mutated primary spinal melanoma. This limits the generalizability of our hypothesis and underscores the need for additional cases to confirm our insights.

Lessons

We propose that GNAQ mutations cause aberrant β-catenin activation in melanocytes, potentially derived from GNAQ-mediated ectopic migration of neural crest cells into the ventral pathways and eventually into the spinal cord during embryogenesis. Constitutive activation of GNAQ can yield DNA instability, cellular proliferation, and tumorigenesis, likely via RAS/RAF and β-catenin signaling pathways. Clinically, identifying the mechanism by which GNAQ mutations promote primary CNS neoplasms is important to guide diagnosis, prognosis, and targeted therapy development. Ongoing clinical trials are assessing the efficacy of MEK inhibitors27 and Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors28 in cancer treatment, which could pose potential therapies for primary spinal melanoma harboring GNAQ mutations.
