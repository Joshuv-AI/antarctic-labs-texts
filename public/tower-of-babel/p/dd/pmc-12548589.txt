Understanding the Impact of Early Childhood and Primary Education for Children with Neurodevelopmental Disabilities on Parents in Nigeria
Authors: Basirat Razaq-Shuaib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly childhood is a foundational period for children’s development and readiness for primary education. Yet, children with neurodevelopmental disabilities (ND) in Nigeria, including autism and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, face limited tailored support and interventions during this period. While the presence of ND is known to increase caregiver burden, this remains underexplored in Nigeria, especially in the context of education. This paper aims to discuss how mothers interpret their experiences of educating their children with ND, the multidimensional impact of these experiences on their personal and family lives, and what their experiences reveal about broader structural and systemic inequalities.MethodsA qualitative case study was conducted with mothers as they are the main caregivers. Twelve mothers of children aged 6–13 years diagnosed with ND and accessing primary education in Lagos, Nigeria, participated in the study. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted in English language. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and thematically analysed individually and across cases.ResultsThe findings reveal that the experiences of mothers in caregiving and educating their children with ND have been challenging due to inadequate support and persistent systemic and attitudinal barriers. This lack of support commences in early childhood, from gaining a diagnosis through to finding a supportive educational setting for the child. While mothers navigate these barriers through resilience by developing adaptive measures such as re-educating themselves, this comes at interconnected and multidimensional costs which have a gendered dimension.ConclusionThe paper redirects attention to unsupportive systems, which continually draw on the resilience of mothers, thereby threatening their capacity to protect and support the development and education of their children. It also calls for the prioritisation of disabilities in education agendas, integration of voices of mothers into policy and practice, responsive systemic support for their significant caregiving and education responsibilities, and inclusion of fathers and other stakeholders into future research.Keywords: Caregivers, Children, Mothers, Neurology, Low and Middle Income Countries

Introduction

The importance of early childhood as a critical period that forms the foundation for the development and readiness of children for primary education is well noted within extant literature and in global commitments such as the United Nations 20151 Sustainable Development Goal 4.2 During this period, children with neurodevelopmental disabilities (ND), including autism and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, require tailored support and interventions to improve their outcomes across their lifespan.3 4 ND, which are a group of conditions that manifest in early childhood, often cause impairments and have implications for learning outcomes.5

In Nigeria, despite legislative provisions, the intersection of early childhood development (ECD) and ND remains poorly addressed.6 Early diagnosis, intervention and inclusive education are underdeveloped,7 resulting in the exclusion of children with ND from formal education during their most formative years.8 Consequently, the absence of coordinated systems leaves parents bearing the responsibilities for caring for and educating their children with ND with minimal institutional support.6 9

The presence of ND is known to increase caregiver burden for families.10 11 Yet, only five studies exploring the caregiver burden of children with ND in Nigeria5 9 have been found. These studies were predominantly quantitative and offered limited insight into the lived experiences of parents in accessing education. Additionally, the impact of these experiences on parents and families remains largely undocumented within the Nigerian literature, especially from their perspectives. This gap is significant considering the role of parents, especially mothers, in creating a nurturing and enabling ECD environment for their children with ND in Nigeria.6 12 Moreover, making effective support provisions for parents requires understanding their needs, taking into consideration the different ranges of contextual, political, economic and sociocultural factors.13

This paper addresses these gaps by discussing how mothers interpret their experiences of educating children with ND, the multidimensional impacts of these experiences on their personal and family lives, and what their experiences reveal about broader structural and systemic inequalities. By centring on the voices of mothers, the paper calls for revisiting their resilience as a compelled response to systemic and attitudinal barriers, and as such, deserves recognition, support and policy attention.

Methodology

The underlying research for this paper was the doctoral research of the author. The study was underpinned by critical approaches, such as Southern theory and postcolonial studies14,16 with an Africentric lens. This was considered necessary for reversing, reimagining, reframing and refocusing the canons of knowledge production and dissemination.17

Study design

This qualitative case study18,20 was conducted with mothers as the main caregivers. A case study approach was selected as it is known for exploring topics where there is ambiguity and uncertainty.21 Twelve mothers of children aged 6–13 years, diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder and/or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and accessing primary education in Lagos, Nigeria, were purposively recruited through a network of educational and non-educational service providers. This included health practitioners, who carry out the diagnosis, therapists, parental support groups and charities known to the author. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was received from the mothers before and during the interviews.

Data collection method and analysis

Data collection was done in 2022 through semi-structured interviews, using the timeline interviewing approach that draws on Dervin and Naumer’s22 sense-making theory. This allowed mothers to construct their experiences through space and time, enabled depth and rigour with the mothers, and contributed to addressing the power asymmetry during the interview process.23 Interviews, lasting an average of 3 hours, were conducted at home and at the educational settings of children. The interview schedule covered themes such as gaining a diagnosis, accessing education, impact of education on family relationships and other aspects of family and social life (such as parental well-being, sibling relationships, friendships and parental earning ability), support towards educating the child and interpretation of lived experience.

All interviews were conducted in English, recorded with the consent of the mothers and transcribed verbatim. Data were thematically analysed individually and across cases using the interactive data analysis flow model24 and the reflexive approach of Srivastava and Hopwood.25 Although the study sought to include a diverse range of ND categories, participation responses were only received from mothers whose children had a diagnosis of autism and/or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, thus limiting the focus and subsequent findings to these two categories. Similarly, while the underlying study sought to hear the voices of parents, and despite two fathers initially participating in the interview process, full interviews were only provided by mothers.

Patient and public involvement

Recruitment partners contributed to the study design by providing suggestions for onboarding participants while parents and their children actively contributed to data collection. The dissemination of this research is, in part a direct response to the request by parents for the findings to be shared to ensure that their voices are heard and other families supported.

Findings

Interpretation of the experiences of mothers in educating their children with ND

All the mothers in this study described the educational journey of their children as challenging, using terms such as ‘hell, roller-coaster, struggle and full of ups and downs,’ due to the lack of adequate support in overcoming the systemic and attitudinal barriers faced. This lack of support usually begins in early childhood when trying to gain a diagnosis. Faderera said,

In the beginning it was a struggle because you don’t have enough of the available resources like formal… [inferring support]

Mothers also noted the dismissal they received from their spouses, family members, and teachers and doctors of children at the time of observing a developmental difference or delay in the child. The mothers found this troubling as the significant responsibilities of caregiving and education at home were borne by them. For instance, Amara said,

He said what’s wrong with you? What are you looking for? Everybody says nothing is wrong. But I knew something. So, it got me to point, am I, am I crazy? Am I crazy?

The negative experiences were further noted when finding supportive educational settings for the child and were exacerbated due to COVID-19 disruptions. During this period, most mothers reported limited knowledge and capacity to support their children therapeutically.

Remarkably, all the mothers noted complete absence of support from the government towards educating their children with ND. They described the government as not doing anything and said they had been left on their own to grapple with the issue, especially financially. Importantly, the lack of support at various times during their experiences led mothers to re-educate themselves, develop strategies to support their children educationally and emotionally, and become advocates providing support to other parents. To overcome the barriers they faced, mothers had to be resilient.

Despite their negative experiences, however, mothers still maintained a hopeful outlook and acknowledged how raising and educating a child with ND had made them better individuals. Esther said,

First of all, if my son didn’t have a condition, I wouldn’t have been able to meet other parents who have children with similar challenges. I wouldn’t have been able to help other parents that I have helped so far. I wouldn’t have been as socially active as I am now. I wouldn’t have been as bold and confident as I am now.

Multidimensional costs of educating children with ND

Mothers spoke about the varied, interconnected and multidimensional costs of educating their children with ND and how these intersect across different facets of their lives. These costs, shown in figure 1, have been thematically grouped into three categories, namely: financial cost, opportunity cost, and psychological and physical cost.

The multidimensional costs of educating children with neurodevelopmental disabilities

Financial cost refers to the direct, quantifiable monetary cost of educating the child while recognising the overlap among healthcare, educational and well-being needs of the child. This includes school fees, additional fees for special needs support, therapy costs and transportation costs. While the financial costs were mostly borne by fathers, mothers also contributed or carried significant costs, if the father was unavailable (eg, due to marriage disputes or bereavement) and they had the financial capacity to do it.

Opportunity cost refers to the cost associated with the most valued alternative that mothers had to forgo to educate their children in the light of their limited resources. This includes career truncation, time for other children and spouse, and time for self. Finally, psychological and physical costs refer to the complex weave of emotions that bore on their physical and psychological well-being due to caring for and educating their children with ND. This includes self-doubt, sadness, grief, and physical and mental exhaustion.

Furthermore, the costs have been organised into two distinct categories of visible and hidden costs to highlight how they are usually accounted for within the Nigerian disability legal framework and support systems. Visible costs, made up of the financial costs of educating the child, are costs that are observable and quantifiable in financial terms. Hidden costs, which include opportunity and psychological costs, are those that are relational, temporal, emotional and often go unquantified in financial terms. The impacts of these costs specifically on the personal and family lives of mothers are discussed in the following sections.

Impact of educating the child with ND on the family life of the mothers

Mothers expressed mixed reactions on the impact of educating children with ND on family members. Analysis showed that educating a child with ND in a family setting had both positive and negative impact on their siblings without ND. Positive impacts were seen in siblings trying to build better relationships. The mothers described how the siblings were not only supportive but were also attempting to pick up skills like painting from the child with ND. This is interesting considering that ND is usually portrayed through a deficit lens of the things the child does not have or cannot do. In describing their family experience, Esther said,

It’s helped Chisom (the child’s sister), and it also helped me. It helps their relationship and the communication. It has really helped both of them to connect more. I think it has impacted positively. I’d have to say that even transiting to formal school has helped him a lot in his, on the social side of things.

However, mothers also noted negative impacts, such as not being able to dedicate the same amount of time to support the learning of siblings, both emotionally and academically. Particularly, mothers were concerned about the feeling of prioritisation and exclusion within the family that the children without ND felt and which they feared could lead to resentment among siblings towards parents and the child with ND. Nonetheless, mothers noted their intentionality about carrying different siblings along once they had the realisation. Abiola said,

So, we were thinking that she should automatically understand, but now I see that a child is a child, and they need their parents anyway. So that’s that and it’s not their business. All she wants is when she needs attention, she should also get attention. So, it was a struggle trying to balance that, taking care of him at home and still have your other child and trying to make sure that, you know, you give enough attention, you give enough love, try to make her not feel less than who she is. She felt like in the family she was at the bottom ’cause everything we did was for him.

Furthermore, some parents, due to huge recurring costs, noted the negative impact of dedicating more financial resources to the education and care of the child with ND and adjusting for the other children. For instance, Glory noted how they had to make the difficult choice to change schools to lower-cost schools for siblings to be able to continue to support the education of the child with ND. Beyond the siblings, mothers also noted how the demands of educating their children left them drained and unable to give as much to their husbands, resulting in a negative impact on their marriages.

Impact of educating the child with ND on the careers, relationships and well-being of mothers

This was arguably one of the most emotional aspects of the interviews for the mothers as they had to recount their lost aspirations as well as the pain points that they rarely talk about. All the mothers reported a negative impact of educating their children with ND on their careers. While nine of them had to quit their careers and either become stay-at-home mothers or pick up another skill to be able to earn, three others who did not quit had to either change their work structure or turn down career growth opportunities (table 1). One of the mothers, Esther, who had to quit her job, noted that they were financially worse off as a result of spending all their earnings on educating their children with ND to date. Additionally, 10 mothers noted the negative impact of educating their children with ND on their social relationships as the journey was gradually isolating. For these mothers, they highlighted the difficulty of getting others to understand and support their children. For instance, Abiola said,

At a point I think is as if your life, social life is non-existent. I don't want to think it’s intentional. It’s never intentional for any special experience. In a way, when you just say that autism is isolating, I don't think you even realise at some point that that’s happening to you.

Another theme that came out strongly was the additional feelings of guilt, regret, shame and frustration that mothers expressed. Starting from the initial discovery of the developmental difficulties in their children, they reported feeling confusion, sadness and guilt as well as constantly second-guessing their thoughts, observations and choices as mothers. For instance, Olawunmi said,

At first, I did not understand what was going on him and thought his speech delay was temporary. But when I realised that we were dealing with a long-term condition, though I was relieved to know, I was overwhelmed by sadness. I blamed myself for his condition, that maybe, it was because I did not get enough folic acid. Then, I blamed myself for allowing nausea to prevent me from keeping the folic acid down.

Beyond these, some of the mothers spoke of feeling guilty about returning to work when it seemed like their children needed them the most. However, their inability to openly express their feelings due to shame and for fear of being judged was adding to their emotional burden. Following these inner conflicts, mothers mentioned that when their social, emotional and mental health suffered, they felt drained, exhausted and had nothing to give to their child or their other relationships, including their spouses.

Discussion

The findings from this study provide important insights into how mothers interpret their experiences of educating their children with ND and the impact on their personal and family lives. Despite acknowledging that raising and educating a child with ND had made them stronger and better individuals, mothers still described their experiences as challenging due to a lack of adequate support from facing systemic and attitudinal barriers. This lack of support usually begins in early childhood, from gaining a diagnosis through to finding a supportive educational setting for the child. It also represents an adversity which could significantly impact the capacity of mothers to support the education of their children and ultimately, the educational outcomes of children.26 27 However, by being resilient,28 mothers continue to demonstrate their active agency, adaptiveness and the commitment required to educate their children with ND.29

Based on the findings, therefore, this paper argues that parents, especially mothers, are a key pillar in creating inclusive quality education systems for their children with ND. However, in the absence of a supportive education or social protection system, decision-making for education is tough, comes at significant financial and non-financial costs and has huge implications for the careers, psychological and physical well-being of mothers. To discuss these findings, this paper draws on critical perspectives on resilience30 to examine the impact of resilience on the personal and family lives of mothers, highlighting broader structural and systemic inequalities that need addressing.

Gendered and hidden costs of educating children with ND

The family as a contextual factor is integral to the long-term care and development of the child.10 31 For children with ND, however, scholars have noted the additional caregiving burden that parents and other family members experience.10 11 32 33 This study found that the burden parents and families face extend beyond caregiving to include educating children with ND. This is due to the complex overlaps between the educational, healthcare and general care needs of the child, which are compounded by systemic and attitudinal barriers.4

Also in this study, mothers had significant responsibilities for caregiving34 and education, and this had interconnected and multidimensional cost implications for them. Mothers often feel higher levels of social and economic pressures, multifaceted psychological stress and lower levels of well-being due to the gendered dimension of the costs of educating the child. This is noteworthy considering the absence of a statistically significant association between gender and caregiver burden in previous Nigerian studies such as Olagunju et al5 and Adeosun et al.9 Mothers like Abiola noted constantly renegotiating exclusionary systems while trying to ensure family cohesion. Unfortunately, given the inadequate support received, especially from the government, mothers had to remain resilient and use adaptive measures to overcome the barriers they faced.35 Mothers re-educated themselves, developed strategies to support their children educationally and emotionally and became advocates who are providing support to other parents.10 36 37

However, as Masten28 argues, resilience often takes a toll on individuals as the well of resources they draw from for support is not limitless. This is particularly important within Global South contexts where the nature of adversity does not have a definite starting point within the experience of a person and can be prolonged for extended periods (such as from childhood to adulthood).38 Within these contexts, Van Breda38 posits that though individuals may demonstrate resilience, women are especially vulnerable to the exhaustion associated with repeated reliance on their resilience due to gender-related factors such as caretaking.

These positions were evident in the findings and with the visible39 and hidden costs which also have a gendered dimension. Mothers reported hidden costs such as opportunity and psychological costs. The biggest opportunity cost that mothers noted was related to their careers and personal aspirations.40 It was striking to note that when household decisions had to be made regarding income or career break, it was usually the income or career of mothers that took the hit. Consequently, 75% of the mothers who were previously formally employed had become either unemployed or self-employed. Some mothers also noted turning down career growth opportunities due to lack of organisational support.9 The real-time financial implication of this was visible as half of the mothers in the study were either not earning an income or earning below the minimum wage at the time of the interviews.41 For most of these mothers, this also negatively impacted their overall household income and spending choices.

Beyond the opportunity costs, mothers also noted how gradually isolating the presence of ND in a family could be and how their social lives were almost non-existent. This is due to their caregiving burden and a lack of understanding of ND conditions by strangers, friends and family members.10 Furthermore, mothers noted a complex weave of emotions that bore on their psychological well-being.42 These emotions include confusion, sadness and guilt,43 44 and often result in blame,45 physical, emotional and mental exhaustion, and strained family relationships.46

Remarkably, the mothers appeared to be going through repeated cycles of grief.47 This was linked to the mourning of ambiguous losses48 such as the child and life they had envisioned,49 and the societal barriers that continually reminded them that their children were different and excluded.50 Mothers, such as Abiola, Kikelomo and Olawunmi, reflected on how their lives as parents had turned out compared with what they had planned for themselves.39 However, they expressed frustration about their inability to openly express their feelings because of concerns about perception of people, noting.

You can’t tell anyone because nobody gets it

While the visible financial costs noted were mostly borne by the fathers, difficulties in the spousal relationship, such as parent separation or spouse bereavement, saw costs defaulting back to the mothers, either in part or in full. Meanwhile, these mothers were already disproportionately impacted on by the presence of child disability in the family.

These highlighted gendered dimensions reinforce existing arguments about the presentation of parental involvement in schooling as an ungendered concept when in reality it is not.51 52 They also underscore the need for better psychosocial support11 and responsive systemic and organisational support.53

Examining safety net provisions through social protection policies

Considering the significant financial and non-financial costs noted by the mothers, it becomes pertinent to appraise any safety nets that have been put in place by the government. In Nigeria, the draft National Social Protection Policy54 aims to reduce the impact of poverty, strengthen resilience, sustain economic growth and provide a life of dignity for all her citizens. However, Razaq-Shuaib6 noted that the impact of the costs of disabilities on household income and caregiver well-being remains yet to be fully addressed within the policy. For instance, while the national social protection policy has provisions for psychosocial support, this applies only to survivors of violence against persons, child labour, child abuse, child rape and human trafficking.

Furthermore, in Lagos state, despite social policy provisions and programmes, Festus Fajemilo Foundation55 noted that social protection programmes have failed to reach persons with disabilities (especially those with psychosocial disabilities and children with disabilities) or meet their needs. It was therefore unsurprising that nearly all the parents in this study noted not receiving any form of support from the government throughout their journeys. Unfortunately, the impact of this lack of social safety net by the government is directly borne by parents who are expected to continuously develop adaptive measures to navigate the system.

While the shortcoming in disabilities provisions has been acknowledged within the Nigerian National Social Protection Policy (Section 1.3.4), it points to a much deeper systemic issue of othering of persons with disabilities in policies and practice.6 Moreover, Joseph56 and Garrett57 argue that resilience can be a political tool which places emphasis on preparing individuals to show adaptability in the face of adversities. Used this way, it can be seen to align with neoliberal approaches that focus on tasking individuals to take responsibility for their social and economic well-being with little or no support from the state. Thus, individuals and communities are led to reduce their reliance on the state for their welfare. This leaves the state free to become less accountable for social systems such as quality inclusive education,38 similar to what was found in this study. Importantly, although resilience practices are gaining momentum globally, the failure of resilience thinking to interrogate the triggering point of resilience persists.30 Therefore, while mothers have demonstrated remarkable adaptability, their resilience should not be an excuse for continued governmental neglect in the education of children with ND. This is critical as their well-being has direct implications for the development, well-being and educational outcomes of the child.53 58

Limitations

The data used in this paper was limited to mothers in urban Lagos and may not capture rural or cross-cultural dynamics within Nigeria. This necessitates further enquiry to explore more diverse (eg, fathers’) experiences. Additionally, the insights were provided by 12 mothers and cannot be considered representative of the whole population of interest either in Lagos state or Nigeria. Notwithstanding, they remain useful as the results of an exploration aimed at amplifying marginalised voices in the context.

Implications for research, policy and practice

This paper contributes to the scholarship on disability and education in Nigeria by illuminating the interpretation of mothers regarding their experiences of educating their children with ND, and the multidimensional impact of these experiences on their personal and family lives. By so doing, the paper departs from a deficit framing of mothers of children with ND in a non-agentive light and amplifies their often unacknowledged role in shaping inclusive education systems. Furthermore, it redirects the gaze to unsupportive systems which continually draw on the resilience of motherss, thereby threatening their capacity to protect, support and promote the development, education and well-being of their children.

This paper calls for the prioritisation of disabilities in education agendas, especially at the early childhood stage, to improve the chances of better outcomes for children with ND across their lifespan. It also calls for better psychosocial support, responsive systemic and organisational support, alongside improved social protection systems for mothers who shoulder significant responsibilities for caregiving and education in households. Support systems and parenting interventions, however, must leverage the knowledge of mothers, drawing on their conceptualisation of support in this context. Importantly, the paper re-emphasises the need to recognise mothers as co-architects of inclusive education systems and leverage their knowledge for policy and practice.

While this paper has highlighted the resilience of mothers, it is important to recognise the complex dependencies within the broader context that can be instrumental in building a quality, sustainable inclusive education system. Therefore, future research should broaden the lens to include the voices of fathers, teachers and other community stakeholders, whose roles and insights are also vital to advancing inclusive education for children with ND. This approach will enable a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics, barriers and opportunities shaping the school and home environments in relation to inclusion. It will also reposition the education system not only as a site of implementation but also as a co-participant in the construction of sustainable inclusive practices.
