Characterization of Patient-Reported Loin Pain in IgAN
Authors: Kristina L Newman, Jonathan Barratt, Justyna Szklarzewicz, Lydia Roberts, Róisín Thomas, Lisa Mellon, Alice Smith, Daniel P Gale, Katie Wong, Susan Pywell, Haresh Selvaskandan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLoin pain is a frequently reported symptom in IgA nephropathy (IgAN), but its significance is unclear. No known data exists regarding its prevalence, characteristics, or its impact on daily life, resulting in little support being offered for its management.MethodsA UK-wide mixed-methods study was conducted in collaboration with the National Registry of Rare Kidney Diseases (RaDaR). This included an online survey analyzed using descriptive statistics and group mean comparisons with adjustments for multiple comparisons. In addition, separate semistructured interviews were analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis and applied to the framework of the survey findings.ResultsA total of 358 patients with biopsy-proven IgAN completed the survey. Twety-one completed the semistructured interviews. Loin pain was highly prevalent among those surveyed (n = 254, 70.9%). The pain was described predominantly as “aching” (n = 190, 83%) and intermittent (n = 171, 70.4%), with episodes consistent in character but distinct from other causes of pain. The pain appeared independent of chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage. It was associated with systemic symptoms (assessed using the Kidney Symptom Questionnaire). Those with recent pain (within the last month) reported poorer ratings in health, mental health, and self-efficacy, and felt that health care professionals (HCPs) offered little support to manage their pain effectively.ConclusionLoin pain was highly prevalent among patients with IgAN surveyed in the UK. Pain experience was similar and appeared independent of CKD stage. The pain had negative impact on psychosocial wellbeing, and limited support was provided. A need for better understanding of the pain and suitable management strategies was expressed by patients with IgAN.Keywords: IgA nephropathy, pain, patient reported outcomes

IgAN is the most common primary glomerulonephritis reported worldwide.1 It presents on a wide clinical spectrum ranging from isolated invisible hematuria to rapidly progressive disease; however, it is most often characterized by a slowly progressive kidney disease associated with hematuria and/or subnephrotic range proteinuria.1,2 Most patients are at risk of progressing to kidney failure over their lifetime, unless their rate of estimated glomerular function rate (eGFR) decline is suppressed to <1 ml/min per 1.73 m2/yr.3 Controlling eGFR decline, with the expanding repertoire of therapies becoming available, forms the basis of most clinical consultations.

Loin pain or native “kidney” pain is a poorly studied feature of IgAN, with little understood about its pathogenesis or impact on patients’ lives. An analysis of social media posts in a forum of patients with IgAN highlighted that loin pain, which was described as intrusive and unrelenting, was commonly experienced by patients and was an important issue to address for those living with IgAN.3 In addition, patients posted about the limited support available for managing the pain, and felt that it was often dismissed by HCPs.3 No studies have specifically investigated loin pain in the context of IgAN, and no data exist about its prevalence, characteristics, or its impact on daily life. We aimed to address this knowledge gap by conducting a UK-wide mixed-methods study in collaboration with RaDaR, to characterize loin pain in IgAN and explore its impact.

Methods

This was an exploratory convergent parallel mixed-methods study, informed by a patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) group. This involved a national survey, qualitative semistructured interviews, and follow-up focus groups involving patients with IgAN.

Ethics

Ethical approval was provided by the University of Leicester, UK (survey: 37468, interview: 40331). The British Psychological Society guidelines for ethical research and informed consent were followed.4,5

Survey Design

The survey aimed to identify the prevalence, features, and impact of loin pain in IgAN. Questions were developed with the PPIE group and fell into the categories of demographics, IgAN treatment, IgAN symptoms, loin pain characteristics, and the impact of loin pain on life. Validated questionnaires were incorporated to explore the latter 3, including the Kidney Symptom Questionnaire6; Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire-27; Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire8; and Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire9 (Supplementary Table S1). “Loin pain” was defined for participants with text and images (Supplementary Method S1). The survey was reviewed by the PPIE group and authors in 4 iterations until an agreement for construct validity was achieved. Validated questionnaires were not altered. Data were collected using Likert-scales, multiple-choice or single-choice questions, and free text (Supplementary Method S1).

Survey Distribution

The survey was hosted online on JISC10 between September and November 2023. A survey link was distributed via email through RaDaR, one of the largest rare kidney disease registries in the world,11 to 2462 UK-based adults (aged ≥ 18 years) within the RaDaR IgAN cohort with registered e-mail addresses. The RaDaR IgAN cohort includes patients with biopsy-proven IgAN and proteinuria > 0.5 g/d or eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at any time in their natural history and excludes those with secondary IgAN and IgA vasculitis.12 Recruitment characteristics and potential biases of the RaDaR cohort have been described previously.13 Participation was encouraged from all patients. An explicit request to participate was directed toward those who did not experience loin pain to the reduce the risk of bias. Branch logic tailored questions to participants (e.g., those who never experienced loin pain were not directed to further questions about the pain), and questions other than consent and eligibility were voluntary. The participants were able to pause and return to the survey, and were given a unique code for withdrawal. The survey concluded with a debrief, links to support such as charities, and information regarding the qualitative component of this study. Survey participation was not incentivized.

Survey Analysis

Data were cleaned in preparation for analysis, with consideration of item, variable, as well as overall and individual levels. Responses were categorized into those with recent loin pain (pain within the last month), historical (any previous experience) loin pain, and those who never experienced loin pain. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 2814 with adjustments for multiple comparisons. Descriptive statistics including number, percentage, mean, and SD were calculated. For measures not normally distributed, a median and interquartile range were calculated. Variance across pain groups were analyzed with chi-square and Levene’s test of equal variance, or Fisher exact test, with Bonferroni-adjusted z-tests for post hoc analysis in categorical variables. One-way analysis of variance with Tukey HSD post hoc tests were used to analyze continuous variables, including the Kidney Symptom Questionnaire across the 3 pain categories. Experiences of loin pain, including the Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire-2, the Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire, and Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire were analyzed using independent t tests for mean comparisons between recent and historical pain groups. Free text was cleaned and categorized to reflect similar experiences (e.g., combining “urine,” and “wee”), and word frequency analysis, number, and percentages were calculated.

Interview Design

The interview schedule was developed based on literature reviews, clinical experiences of authors, and lived experiences of the PPIE group. The schedule was reviewed in 3 iterations until agreement. The questions explored the experience of living with IgAN and loin pain with expansion for context (Supplementary Method S2).

Interview Procedure

Qualitative interviews were advertised in the survey, the UK IgAN Facebook group, newsletters, and social media channels. Patients contacted the research team directly via an email contact included in the advertisements. Participants were required to be adults living in the UK with biopsy-confirmed IgAN. Sample size was informed by information power15 and consideration of project scope.16 Purposive sampling was used where possible to increase diversity, otherwise opportunity sampling was employed, and snowball sampling was encouraged. Advertisements included a link to an online information sheet and a consent form, followed by a brief form to collect demographics to screen for inclusion criteria. Interviews were conducted in order of recruitment and participant availability, were 30 to 60 minutes long, and were held remotely between August 2023 and April 2024 with a qualitative researcher (KLN), via telephone or Microsoft Teams, and audio-recorded via an encrypted Dictaphone. The participants were debriefed and offered a £50 voucher, meeting National Institute of Health Research recommendations.17 A further focus group to review findings was recruited from consenting qualitative participants, who were compensated with £50 vouchers.

Interview Analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim. Identifiable data were anonymized and analyzed using NVivo12.18 Exploratory data were coded by KLN and JS, following the procedure of reflexive thematic analysis19,20; familiarization, coding, theme generation, theme review, definition of themes, and write-up. Themes were reviewed with the wider project team and the focus group participants to ensure that the analyses were representative. Coding was applied to the framework of the survey findings to develop the narrative; and reviewed using COREQ (Supplementary Checklist 1). Detailed qualitative findings will be published separately.

Results

Three hundred sixty-six patients with IgAN participated in the survey, and 8 responses were removed because of low survey completion. Twenty-one patients completed an interview (demographics in Tables 1 and 2). Survey results were represented numerically by percentages and statistics (Table 1; Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4), and qualitative data lexically, with themes and quotes mapped to the survey framework in Table 3.

Participants Were Representative of the RaDaR IgAN Cohort and the UK IgAN Population

The 358 survey respondents (14.5% response rate) were broadly representative of the RaDaR IgAN cohort (Table 1) and represented most regions of the UK (Supplementary Figure S1). Of the participants, 66% reported their sex (defined as biological sex assumed at birth) as male. The mean age of respondents was 53.25 (range: 19 – 89) years; and on average, respondents had their diagnosis for 16 years; 90.3% identified as White British.

Of the participants, 65.1% self-reported a recent eGFR (mean = 43 ml/min per 1.73 m2). CKD stage distributions were representative of the RaDaR IgAN cohort (eGFR data within RaDaR are gathered through linked clinical datasets, Table 1). Fewer respondents reported receiving kidney replacement therapy compared with the RaDaR cohort; 38.6% reported a functioning transplant, 5.6% reported receiving hemodialysis, and 1.4% reported receiving peritoneal dialysis (Table 1).

Loin Pain Affected 70.9% of Those Surveyed With IgAN

Loin pain (either “recent,” occurring within the last month, or “historical,” occurring at any other time point) was commonly reported. Of the participants, 70.9% (n = 254) reported loin pain, with 42.7% experiencing recent pain (Figure 1a). Loin pain was more common among females, with 79.3% experiencing pain compared with 66.6% of males (X2 [2, n = 358] = 6.34, P = 0.04). Those experiencing recent pain had a higher body mass index (BMI; 28.5 vs. 26.46; F[2, 345] = 3.54, P = 0.03) and were more recently diagnosed (14 years since diagnosis vs. 18 years; F(2, 350) = 6.01, P = .003).

Participants with kidney transplants were less likely to have reported loin pain. Of the cohort, 38.6% reported having a transplant. Of this group, 31.7% reported recent pain, 28.3% historical pain, and 39.9% reported never having loin pain (P < 0.001). Of those with no history of a kidney transplant, 49.5% reported recent pain, 28.2% reported historical pain, and 22.3% reported never experiencing loin pain. There was no difference in reports of loin pain between those receiving dialysis and those who were not.

The Experience of Loin Pain was Similar Across Most Patients With IgAN

There was high consistency reported in loin pain experiences. Of those experiencing loin pain, 80.4% reported every episode feeling the same (Figure 1b), and this was consistent between those experiencing recent or historical loin pain. In addition, 14.6% experienced loin pain daily, 29.2% once a week or more, and 26.8% once a month or more. Of those experiencing loin pain, 57% believed IgAN was the cause of their pain, and 28.3% felt that the cause was a combination of IgAN and another factor (Figure 1c). Patients with comorbidities who were interviewed felt that they could differentiate between loin pain believed to be IgAN-related and pain from other conditions, based on pain location and description (Table 3).

“Aching” was the most common and highest rated descriptor of loin pain, reported by 83% (Figure 1d), with 40.3% reporting the ache as at least moderately severe (≥ 5/10 on the Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire-2 scale). Tiring or exhausting was the next most common attribute, reported by 54.8%, with 24.9% describing this to be at least moderately severe. Interviewees supported these findings, defining loin pain as “aching,” “sore,” or “bruising,” and associated with fatigue. In addition, participants expanded to describe 2 distinct types of loin pain; a common mild-to-moderate aching pain; and a rarer, more severe, sharp, stabbing pain (Table 3). “Back” and “side” were used most in location description, with reports of similarity in feel and location of a kidney biopsy (Table 3).

Most participants (70.45%) reported the pain as intermittent (“comes and goes” or duration < 24 hours). For those experiencing consistent pain (> 24 hours), the duration was variable, lasting days to weeks (Supplementary Table S2). The most common triggers for the pain were exercise (14.85%) and feeling unwell (7%). Feeling “unwell” or “run down” were exacerbators of loin pain, and some reported SARS-CoV-2 infections made loin pain worse (Table 3).

Loin Pain was Independent of CKD Status, but was Associated With Systemic Symptoms

There was no relationship between loin pain and self-reported eGFR (P = 0.25) or CKD stage (X2 [12, n = 358] = 11.56, P = 0.48). CKD stages based on self-reported eGFRs matched well with CKD stages of the RaDaR IgAN cohort (gathered through clinical dataset linkage) (Table 3).

Patients with loin pain were more likely to experience common kidney symptoms (including itching, sleep disturbance/insomnia, loss of appetite, tiredness, bone/joint pain, poor concentration/alertness, poor muscle strength/power, cramp/muscle stiffness, or restless legs (Figure 2, P < 0.001 for each), and shortness of breath (P = 0.002), feeling cold (P = 0.003), and frequent urination (P = 0.02)). One hundred sixty-four participants self-reported eGFR and completed the Kidney Symptom Questionnaire. Systemic symptoms associated with uremia was associated with CKD stage, including sleep disturbance (P = 0.003), feeling tired (P = 0.003), loss of appetite (P = 0.01), and restless legs (P = 0.01). Patients’ interviews supported these findings reporting fatigue as the main additional symptom (Table 3), although tiredness was reported as a general symptom of IgAN. Additional symptoms reported included cramps, and sleep disturbance. A minority of patients were able to test their urine when experiencing loin pain and reported increases in proteinuria and/or hematuria (Table 3).

When rating the importance of these systemic symptoms, survey patients with recent loin pain scored them higher, suggesting a higher symptom burden of sleep disturbance or insomnia, tiredness, pain in bone or joints, poor concentration or alertness, cramp or muscle stiffness, restless legs, frequent urination (P < 0.001 for each), and additionally, of loss of muscle strength or power (P = 0.005) and shortness of breath (P = 0.004). Interview participants reported being unsure what symptoms to consider important because of perceived unhelpful advice from HCPs (e.g. “kidneys do not feel pain,” Table 3).

Loin Pain had Adverse Psychosocial Impacts on Patients’ Lives

Those experiencing loin pain were more likely to report negative life experiences in both studies. General health and mental health were rated worse among those with recent loin pain (P < 0.001). Although 14.4% of participants with a history of loin pain rated their health as poor or very poor generally, this increased to 23.6% when they experienced loin pain (Figure 3a). Similarly, 15.3% rated their emotional mental health as poor or very poor generally, which increased to 21.2% when experiencing loin pain (Figure 3b). Those with recent loin pain were more likely to report reduced mobility 46.8% (P < 0.001).

Psychological and emotional impacts of loin pain were reported by 57.1% who experienced loin pain. These included feeling anxious (P = 0.02), stressed (P = 0.004), frustrated (P < 0.001), and finding it hard to concentrate (P = 0.02) compared with those without loin pain. Of those who experienced loin pain, 41.7% reported no emotional effect, and this was significantly more reported in historical than in recent loin pain (P < 0.001), reflecting a higher psychological and emotional burden associated with recent pain. The qualitative interviews provided further context regarding the adverse mental impact of the loin pain. Concern of the impact of loin pain on kidney function was emphasized, which for many was the largest negative impact (Table 3).

Of those who experienced loin pain, 17.8% reported loin pain affecting work, 6.7% took time off work because of loin pain, 9.1% took more frequent breaks during their workday, and 2% reported relying on other strategies such as working from home. In interviews, participants added that though they did not take “formal” action at work, they employed similar coping strategies or rested outside of work hours.

Participants with recent loin pain scored lower self-efficacy, scoring lower in enjoyment (t[243] = −2.13, P = 0.03), coping (t[243] = −2.19, P = 0.03), hobbies (t[217.19] = −2.66, P = 0.01), coping without medication (t[221.64] = −3.36, P = 0.001), accomplishing goals (t[221.56] = −2.67, P = 0.01) and activity (t[207.93] = −2.68, P = 0.01). Reflecting these findings, a lower total self-efficacy score was reported among those experiencing loin pain (t[245] = −2.07, P = 0.04). Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire items on chores and socializing were not significantly different between loin pain groups. Interviewees who reported moderate to severe loin pain agreed and felt that the pain impacted multiple life domains, including social, occupational, family, life events, and hobbies, with patients reluctant to engage because of concerns of aggravating the loin pain but concerned about the impact of isolation.

This additionally supports the survey findings because perceptions and burden (Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire) associated with loin pain were rated worse for those with recent loin pain in identity (t[240] = 6.50, P < 0.001), illness concern (t[240] = 3.75, P < 0.001), coherence (t[238] = 2.05, P = 0.04), emotional representations (t[238] = 4.08, P < 0.001), consequence (t[221.55] = 4.74, P < 0.001), and timeline (t[139.70] = 3.71, P <0 .001). The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire items personal control and treatment control were not significantly different between groups.

Patients Felt Unsupported in Loin Pain Management

Loin pain management and professional support were key issues identified by the patients. Interviewees emphasized the lack of pharmacological management options available (Table 3). Both studies report similar management strategies of loin pain, including over-the-counter analgesics (30.7%), prescription analgesics (10.2%), rest (31.5%), heat (17.7%), stretching (15.3%), ice (2.4%), other strategies (7.1%), or with nothing (36.2%), with a mean effectiveness rating of 3.05 (SD = 1.17) out of 5 (Figure 4a). The number of management strategies trialled was higher in the recent loin pain group (t[246] = 2.62, P = 0.01) than in the historical loin pain group. Interviewees report that nonpharmacological management was acceptable during episodes of mild pain; however, during moderate-to-severe episodes, they offered little relief. There was confusion about safe management options, with difficulty obtaining advice online or from HCPs (Table 3).

Of those experiencing loin pain, 43.3% consulted an HCP (Figure 4b), with proportions higher among those experiencing recent loin pain (P < 0.001). Only 34.5% found HCP advice helpful (Figure 4c). This was lower among those with recent loin pain than among those with historical loin pain (X2 [2, n = 254] = 8.38, P = 0.02). Interview patients expanded that they experienced conflicting opinions from HCPs, and felt dismissed when seeking advice, which led to frustration and reduced trust (Table 3). The lack of suitable management options and lack of support from HCPs were the biggest barriers identified by patients to managing loin pain.

Discussion

IgAN is often detected incidentally. Although symptoms outside of visible hematuria, nephrotic syndrome, and advanced CKD are considered rare, loin pain is frequently reported anecdotally. The pervasiveness of this “intrusive” and “unrelenting” symptom was identified as a concern for patients through social media analysis.3 Loin pain in IgAN is understudied, with no data available on its prevalence, features, pathogenesis nor impact. This mixed-methods study aimed to address this knowledge gap.

Loin pain was highly prevalent. It was reported by 70.9% of respondents, and 42.7% experienced it as recently as the preceding month. These figures support insights from social media analysis, interviews, and anecdotal reports indicating that loin pain is a common symptom in IgAN.3

The experience of loin pain in IgAN was broadly universal. The pain was mostly described as an aching and intermittent pain, moderate to severe in intensity. Both survey respondents and interviewees felt able to distinguish pain related to IgAN from pain related to other causes, indicating it to be a distinct experience. The pattern of pain was broadly described as episodic, with little difference in character between each episode. These findings could suggest that loin pain in IgAN is mediated either by a common nociceptive mechanism, or by several mechanisms which converge to a single common nociceptive pathway.

IgAN exhibits a male preponderance in Europe.21 This was reflected in our survey, where 66% of respondents were male. However, loin pain was most frequently reported among female respondents with nearly 80% reporting pain. Women and girls are reportedly more likely to experience chronic pain and experience it at greater severity with more functional impact than men and boys.22 The drivers for this may be both psychosocial and biological. Emerging data from single-cell studies, for instance, demonstrate measurable distinctions between male and female kidney cells.23, 24, 25 This finding is hypothesis generating, but does suggest a need for equitable sex and gender balanced support for managing this symptom.26, 27, 28, 29

Those with higher BMIs were more likely to report loin pain. This aligns with broader epidemiological evidence linking BMI to an increased risk of chronic pain, and several mechanisms may underlie this relationship.30,31 Higher BMIs are associated with states of low-grade inflammation which may sensitize nociceptive pathways.32, 33, 34 Other factors associated with high BMI, including sleep disturbances, mood disorders, and reduced physical activity, may contribute to heightened pain perception30; and indeed were reported here more frequently among those who experienced loin pain. Elevated BMI was associated with poorer outcomes in IgAN and CKD.30 Although it is unclear if these relationships are causative, these findings add further support for the importance of weight management as a component of the comprehensive care that should be offered to those with IgAN.

Although CKD stages were derived from self-reported eGFRs, the distribution of reported CKD stages among respondents was broadly representative of the RaDaR IgAN cohort, which draws eGFR data from linked clinical datasets.35 Loin pain was reported across all stages of CKD, with no association between kidney function and loin pain prevalence. This implies that the mechanisms underlying loin pain in IgAN are distinct from those associated with uremia or progressive renal impairment. This may indicate that loin pain in IgAN arises from factors other than reduced kidney function, possibly involving systemic or local inflammatory or nociceptive processes within or around the kidney.

Loin pain was strongly associated with a broad range of systemic symptoms. Patients reporting loin pain were more likely to experience pruritis, sleep disturbance, reduced appetite, fatigue, poor concentration, muscle cramps, restless legs, and loss of strength; and reported higher overall burden from these symptoms than those who were pain-free. This, in conjunction with its independence from CKD stage, may further support the possibility of loin pain in IgAN being mediated by a complex and potentially systemic process, such as inflammation, which may be exacerbated by higher BMIs.

Those living with a kidney transplant were less likely to report recent native loin pain. Noting the absence of a relationship between loin pain and CKD stage, a possible explanation for this could be the use of immunosuppressive therapy in this group, which may down-regulate inflammatory pathways contributing to pain. Loin pain was more likely to flare during episodes of intercurrent illness, indicating that systemic immune activation or inflammation may contribute to pain.

Loin pain had negative impacts on the lives of those experiencing it. Those experiencing recent pain reported worse general and emotional health, especially during episodes of pain, the impact of pain was linked to its severity. Feelings of frustration were exacerbated by a lack of understanding among HCPs about the pain and a lack of management options for it. As a result, most participants self-managed the pain with analgesics, rest, heat, and stretching. However, these strategies were felt to be moderately helpful at best. These findings support loin pain being an important contributor to the symptom burden experienced by those living with IgAN and supports the need to investigate the biological mechanisms which drive this pain so that it can be better managed.

This mixed-methods PPIE-supported study revealed new insights into loin pain in IgAN, and included one of the largest surveys conducted among patients with IgAN. The survey achieved a response rate of 14.5% from the RaDaR IgAN cohort. This is in keeping with rates from other unincentivized surveys,36,37 and respondents were broadly representative of the wider RaDaR IgAN cohort and of regions within England and Scotland.13 Those identifying as White British and not receiving any form of kidney replacement therapy were overrepresented in the survey.

There are limitations to this work. This study was limited to the UK and predominantly represents a Caucasian cohort. Given that IgAN is clinically heterogeneous and varies with geography,38 these findings may not be generalizable to South Asian, East Asian, African, or Hispanic cohorts. The survey relied on self-reported data, particularly for clinical variables. Although the profile of these data mirrored those collected through automated registry-based data-linkage, a potential for selection and reporting bias remains. Similarly, it is plausible that those currently experiencing loin pain were more motivated to respond, despite a clear call for all with IgAN regardless of experience with pain to respond. This carries the risk of overestimating the prevalence of loin pain in IgAN. Participation in the qualitative arm was facilitated through self-selection and snowball sampling. Although this increases diversity, it may limit representativeness. Participants who engaged in interviews may have also had more severe experiences, potentially skewing the narrative toward negative impacts. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of both the survey and interview components restricts the ability to infer causality. Further work is ongoing using the entire RaDaR cohort to evaluate symptomatology, including loin pain, in all rare kidney diseases included in RaDaR. These data will be analyzed in the context of all laboratory held variables, including eGFR and proteinuria, and trajectory of eGFR changes. In addition, separate studies are exploring the mechanistic drivers of the pain and evaluating the prognostic implications of loin pain in IgAN, so that better management strategies can be developed.

Conclusion

This explorative mixed-methods study represents the first systematic study exploring loin pain in IgAN. The findings support and emphasize that loin pain in IgAN is common, and broadly consistent among those who experience it. Crucially, loin pain had significant psychosocial impacts among those who experienced it, and minimal support was being provided by HCPs to assist with managing the pain. These findings provide hypothesis-generating insights to guide much needed future work to better support the management of this prevalent symptom among those with IgAN.
