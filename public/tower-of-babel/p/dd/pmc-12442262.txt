Acute medial tibial plateau elevation with gradual metaphyseal correction using an Ilizarov fixator for late-presenting infantile Langenskiold stage V and VI Blount’s disease. Short-term results
Authors: Mohamed Abdelaal Hussein, Mokhtar Ahmed Alsayed, Ali H Al-Yami, Eyad A Alakkas, Raad M M Althaqafi, Ahmed Aljahdali, Gamal Ahmed Hosny, Ahmed A Khalifa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCorrecting severe deformities associated with advanced stages of Blount’s disease in adolescents varies in the literature between acute and gradual corrections. We aimed to report the early results (radiological and complications) of performing acute medial tibial plateau elevation combined with gradually correcting the metaphyseal deformity using an Illizarov external fixator in adolescents with late-presenting infantile Langenskiold stage V and VI Blount’s disease.MethodsA prospective case series of 24 patients (24 limbs) with Langenskiold stage V and VI Blount’s disease having a mean age of 11.63 ± 1.74 years were included. The radiological outcomes included hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), joint line convergence angle (JLCA), Posterior proximal tibial angle (PPTA), and medial tibial plateau depression angle (MTPDA). Complications at any time point were reported.ResultsThe Illizarov frame was removed after a mean of 14 ± 2.04 weeks postoperatively. After a mean follow up of 23.33 ± 9.43 months, all the radiological outcomes improved significantly (< 0.001) compared to preoperative values, HKA: 182.17 ± 1.97 vs. 148.29 ± 9.88, MPTA:88.0 (87.0–90.0) vs. 75.5 (70.0–80.0), PPTA 82.0 ± 1.82 vs. 67.67 ± 5.02, JLCA: 2.46 ± 1.77 vs. 19.92 ± 2.75, and MTPDA: 1.96 ± 2.07 vs. 50.08 ± 7.19. No cases of neurovascular complications. The pin tract infection rate was 62.5%, and all were treated conservatively. Varus deformity recurred in 5 (20.8%) patients, all at the metaphyseal level, with no collapse at the elevated medial tibial plateau. Significant Leg length discrepancy (> 2 cm) was encountered in 6 (25%) patients (five were patients with recurrent varus deformities, where the LLD was compensated after deformity correction).ConclusionThe management protocol we adopted, which entailed acute elevation of the medial tibial plateau depression combined with gradual correction of the proximal tibial deformity assisted by the Illizarov external fixator, provided promising results regarding its safety, ability of full deformity correction, and acceptable recurrence incidence.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Blount's disease, Tibia vara, Severe deformity, Illizarov external fixator, Medial plateau elevation, Neglected

Introduction

Tibia vara (Blount’s disease) is a developmental disorder characterized by asymmetric growth disturbance of the proximal medial tibial physis as a result of endochondral ossification defect, leading eventually to various degrees of three-dimensional proximal tibial deformities, including varus deformity, procurvatum, internal tibial torsion, with subsequent knee joint malalignment and instability [1–4].

According to the age at onset, Blount’s disease was classified clinically into either early onset (infantile) type (before the age of four) or late-onset type (which occurs in older ages and before skeletal maturity) [4–8]. It is worth noting that patients with infantile type might present at an older age, and some authors call this a late-presenting or neglected infantile type [9–13]. Furthermore, Langenskiold defined clinic-radiological severity stages based on the degree of epiphyseal-physeal-metaphyseal distortion, leading to classifying tibia vara into six stages (Table 1) [14, 15]. A. A. Khanfour provided an updated comprehensive categorization, where he suggested a cut-off age limit of 4 years old (at which the parents noticed the deformity) to differentiate infantile from late-onset, with further four subcategories relying on the severity stage per Langenskiold classification and if the patient had previous intervention of not, namely de-novo, neglected, relapsed, or mismanaged patients [16].

Managing deformities associated with Blount’s disease could be either conservative or surgical, relying mainly on the patient’s age and deformity severity or progression, which aims to restore the normal limb and joint alignment, obtain leg length equalization, and prevent recurrence possibility [1, 4, 17, 18]. Surgical intervention is the management of choice in cases with higher stages (Langenskiold stage III or higher) or when conservative management fails, which entails various options, including growth modulation techniques (hemi-epiphysiodesis), physeal distraction, physeal arrest resection, corrective osteotomies, and any combination of the previously mentioned techniques [1, 19, 20].

Moreover, selecting the proper management option becomes more challenging for severe deformities (Langenskiold stags V and VI) and in late-presenting (neglected) cases, where the surgeon might perform an acute or gradual correction of the deformities, including elevating the depressed medial tibial plateau and correcting the metaphyseal varus deformity through various types of osteotomies (medial wedge opening or lateral wedge opening), and final fixation could be achieved using either internal or external fixation devices [10, 11, 16, 21–29]. However, in some reports, acute correction was associated with a relatively higher incidence of complications, particularly neurovascular and development of compartment syndrome [30].

In the current study, we aimed to report the early results (radiological and complication incidence) of correcting tibial deformity associated with late-presenting (neglected) infantile severe (Langenskiold stage V and VI) Blount’s disease using a combined approach of acute medial tibial plateau elevation and gradual correction of the proximal tibial deformity using Illizarov ring external fixator.

Patients and methods

We obtained institutional ethics committee approval to conduct this retrospective study (first author institution). We followed the ethical standards per the Helsinki declarations, and all participants’ parents or caregivers gave their informed consent that the patient’s data could be used anonymously for research purposes.

We reviewed all the medical records for patients admitted and treated for deformity correction associated with Blount’s disease over the past seven years. We included adolescents who were diagnosed with late-presenting infantile Langenskiold stage V and VI Blount’s disease, managed by the technique we aimed to evaluate, and having complete data with more than 12 months of follow up. In contrast, infantile type with other disease stages, adolescents with late-onset type, incomplete records, those who refused to participate, and if the correction was performed using different surgical techniques were excluded from the study. During this period, 31 patients were identified with Langenskiold stage V and VI Blount’s disease; however, 24 patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria.

Preoperative assessment

Apart from general medical evaluation and assessing patients for their fitness to undergo surgical procedures. Radiological evaluation (including preoperative planning) was performed by obtaining an anteroposterior (AP) long film, including the hip, knee, and ankle. A lateral view of the knee, including the whole tibia and as much as possible from the femur.

The following measurements were performed [4, 31, 32]: The overall limb alignment was measured using the hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle as the medial angle between the femoral and tibial mechanical axes (180° was considered neutral, and smaller or higher degrees were considered varus and valgus deformities respectively). The medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) is the medial angle between the tibial plateau and the tibial anatomical axis in the frontal plane. The joint line convergence angle (JLCA) is the angle between the tangent line to the distal femur and the tangent to the proximal tibia. Posterior proximal tibial angle (PPTA): measured as the angle between the tibial plateau and the tibial anatomical axis in the sagittal plane. The medial tibial plateau depression angle (MTPDA) was measured as the angle between a tangent to the lateral and medial plateau (Fig. 1a).

Preoperative planning was performed using plain radiographs, and the osteotomy cuts and frame site application were simulated using bone Ninja application (Fig. 1b) [33].

Surgical technique

Positioning and preparation: All patients were operated upon under general anesthesia with the application of an upper thigh tourniquet while they were positioned supine on a radiolucent operating table and under fluoroscopic control.

Surgical Exposure and Osteotomies:

First, we started with a fibular osteotomy (middle third) through a lateral incision to allow easy correction of the osteotomized tibia, where a sector of the fibula was removed (about 1 to 1.5 cm).

Second, the proximal tibial osteotomies (for elevating the medial tibial plateau and performing a metaphyseal correction), which were performed through a single proximal anteromedial curved incision (Fig. 2a), were performed.

A, To elevate the medial tibial plateau, the knee is flexed (to relax the neurovascular bundle), an osteotomy was performed about 2 cm below the ‘‘beak’ of the depressed medial tibial plateau by multiple holes using a 3.2 mm drill bit through the medial tibial cortex, the osteotomy was completed by a straight osteotome to connect the drill holes, and it was angled from distally and extending proximal toward the tibial plateau and at the same time osteotomizing the posterior tibial cortex (Fig. 2b, c, and d). Then, a curved osteotome was used to target the intercondylar area, and great caution was paid not to breach the growth plate or fracture the intercondylar area (Fig. 2e).

After freeing the cortices, a spinal laminar spreader was used to slowly open and elevate the medial tibial plateau, correcting the medial angle and posterior slope (Fig. 2f). Once plateau elevation was satisfactory, the excised fibula was used as a strut to support the elevated medial plateau and keep it in position.

At this point, we proceeded with the Illizarov frame Application as follows: Fixation by two olive wires (1.5 cm below the joint line), one was inserted from the elevated medial tibial plateau to the lateral plateau, and the other from the lateral plateau to the medial plateau (Fig. 2g). Additional wires were added in different directions from the anterolateral and posteromedial surface of the tibial plateau to secure the fixation of the elevated medial tibial plateau to the proximal ring (Fig. 2h). Then, we applied two rings on the distal two-thirds of the tibia, which were connected to the proximal ring by two hinges perpendicular to the plane of maximum deformity, where the primary hinge was placed over the Center of Rotation Angulation (CORA) of the metaphyseal component of the tibial deformity, and with distractor placed medially or posteromedially according to the CORA location.

B, the second tibial osteotomy was then performed at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction below the level of tibial tuberosity. A drop wire from the proximal ring was placed below the level of the elevated medial tibial plateau and above the second metaphyseal tibial osteotomy to secure the fixation of the plateau and prevent movement transmission to the elevated plateau while performing gradual distraction to correct the metaphyseal deformity (Fig. 2i and j).

Typically, the rotation was corrected acutely; however, if adjustment was required postoperatively, this could be achieved during the first week, as there was a seven-day latency period after which angular correction was started. Lengthening was performed if additional length was needed.

Postoperative protocol

During the hospital stay, patients were allowed to start toe-touch weight bearing and active motion of the knee and ankle joints under the supervision of a physiotherapist, with the involvement of the patient’s parents or caregivers. Patients were discharged on the second or third postoperative day after they or their parents were educated regarding the frame care and signs of early infection.

Follow-up visits were scheduled for one week for surgical wound assessment, evaluation of the Illizarov frame, and fine-tuning adjustments if required. Then, at two weeks for suture removal, patients were encouraged to start partial weight bearing as tolerated with the assistance of two crutches. Then, the patients were advised to visit the clinic every two weeks thereafter till frame removal. Then the visits are scheduled every month till the sixth month follow up, then every three months till the end of the first postoperative year, then every six months.

After confirming the osteotomy site’s union, the frame was removed by obtaining serial radiographs during the follow-up visits and by the ability of pain-free full weight bearing (Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 20. The data normal distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median and interquartile range (IQR), or frequencies and percentages, as appropriate. Normally distributed data were compared using the paired t-test, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for non-normally distributed data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Twenty-four limbs (14 (58.3%) right and 10 (41.7%) left) in 24 patients having a mean age of 11.63 ± 1.74 years (ranging from 8 to 14), 15 (62.5%) were males, and the disease grade (Langenskiold stage V and VI) was equally divided between limbs (12 (50%) each). The mean time for Illizarov removal was 14 ± 2.04 weeks (ranging from 12 to 16), and patients were followed up for a mean of 23.33 ± 9.43 months (ranging from 12 to 48).

The overall limb alignment was improved significantly from a mean preoperative HKA of 148.29° ± 9.88 to a last follow up of 182.17° ± 1.97, p < 0.001. Furthermore, all other radiological outcomes significantly improved when comparing the preoperative to the last follow-up measurements (Table 2).

Regarding the complications, no neurovascular injury or compartment syndrome was reported in any of the patients. Furthermore, there were no fibular osteotomy site-related complications. Pin tract infection was encountered in 15 (62.5%) patients; however, all were minor infections (grade1 in 6 (40%) patients, grade 2 in 5 (33.3%), and grade 3 in 4 (16.7%)) according to the Checketts–Otterburn grading system, and all were managed conservatively (daily dressing and oral antibiotics). Varus deformity recurrence was reported in 5 (20.8%) patients, where the deformity ranged from 10 to 17 degrees, all occurred at the metaphyseal level (no collapse occurred at the elevated medial tibial plateau level), and all required secondary corrective osteotomies. LLD was encountered in 6 (25%) patients, where the difference ranged from 2.5 to 5 centimeters (five of which were patients with recurrent varus deformities, where the LLD was compensated after deformity correction), and the sixth patient (LLD of 2.5 centimeters) was managed with a shoe lift.

Discussion

The aim of the current study was not to prove the superiority of the technique and protocol we adopted for managing severe deformities associated with advanced stages of late-presenting or neglected infantile Blount’s disease; however, the promising early results we obtained supported the advantageous role of the technique we described and its suitability to be added as a treatment possibility in the surgical options armamentarium for deformity correction associated with late-presenting or neglected severe Blount’s disease.

The main goals for managing Blount’s disease, especially cases with severe deformity, are to correct all deformity components, which enables near normal load distributions over the joints (leading eventually to proper growth), to guard against deformity recurrence, and a technique allows the child to return early to daily activities [1, 4, 19, 37–39]. In the current study, we achieved the management goals, which is evident by the significant improvement of all the radiological measurements, besides obtaining proper overall limb alignment; the results align with what was reported in various studies after performing acute deformity corrections [11, 21, 22, 29, 30].

Regarding the safety and complication incidence in our patients, we reported no neurovascular complications or compartment syndrome in any of the patients, which is relatively safer than some acute correction techniques, especially in severe deformities. In a systematic review by Gilbody et al., who compared gradual versus acute correction of deformities associated with Blount’s disease, the authors reported that the included evidence failed to prove the superiority of one technique over the other. However, peroneal nerve injury was reported in six out of 10 studies reported on acute correction (10 (6.9%) out of 144 patients), while it occurred in one (1.03%) out of 97 patients in the gradual correction group. Furthermore, compartment syndrome was reported in one patient in the acute correction group and none in the gradual correction [30].

While some reports claimed a 0% recurrence rate [11, 21], we encountered a 20.8% recurrence rate, which might be attributed to not performing lateral hemiepiphysiodesis in relatively younger patients [13, 22]. However, all patients with recurrence were adequately managed by another corrective osteotomy. According to a recent systematic review by Ramella et al., who evaluated 63 studies, the overall recurrence rate (obtained from evaluating 1579 knees in 60 studies) knees after correcting Blount’s disease deformity was 18% (C.I. 95% = 14–22%, I2 = 0.22), regardless of the technique used for management; furthermore, the recurrence incidence was 7% in gradual correction patients versus 22% in those who had an acute correction. They reported the highest recurrence rate (29%) in patients with lateral hemi-epiphysiodesis of the proximal tibia, which was significantly higher than patients who had gradual correction (p = 0.03) [20]. Although we did not perform a concomitant lateral epiphysiodesis, which was the same practice in previous studies [10, 29], it is essential to mention that some authors suggested that performing a lateral proximal tibial hemiepiphysiodesis is a must in neglected cases that had an elevation of the depressed medial tibial plateau [12, 16].

We admit that using an external fixator for stabilizing and correcting such complex deformities has its challenges, including the relatively higher pin tract infection rates (62.5% in the current study), longer duration of frame application, the need for precise hinges site placement, which might need further adjustment, the distraction at various sites at different rates which is amenable for mistakes, and the need for closer follow up visits [4, 23, 27, 28, 40]. However, we believe that the technique we utilized in the current study offers some advantages:

First, we performed acute correction of the articular component of the deformity by elevation and fixation of the medial tibial plateau to its anatomical position in both planes, which is recommended by many authors [10, 21, 41, 42]. By this step, we avoided the hassle originating from the gradual correction of the medial tibial plateau deformity and the need for precise calculations and hinge modification during the correction process. Furthermore, gradual correction at the articular surface might lead to early consolidation with resultant under-correction [43].

Second, the gradual correction of the metaphyseal component of the deformity allows the opportunity for fine adjustment, especially the rotational component of the deformity, which could be adjusted during the first week postoperatively, allowing surgeons to compensate for undue events occurring intraoperatively, hindering proper correction; furthermore, distraction is possible at this site for LLD compensation [23, 26].

Third, the location of the upper tibial osteotomy being below the tibial tuberosity has fewer drawbacks on the patellofemoral joint complex and better osteotomy execution [44].

Fourth, adding fibular osteotomy, combined with the gradual correction, allowed less tension on the neurovascular and soft tissue structures, which might occur with acute corrections [4, 23, 30].

Fifth, using Illizarov as a fixation device allowed operating through relatively smaller surgical incisions compared to extensive soft tissue dissection, which is sometimes required with internal fixation devices; furthermore, external fixators are usually removed as outpatient procedures, unlike internal fixation, which needs secondary surgical intervention for removal [21].

Last, apart from easing the tibial deformity correction, the resected segment from the fibular osteotomy site was used to support the elevated medial tibial plateau and act as an autologous bone graft without considerable complications [4, 45].

The current study has some limitations. First, we did not assess the clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction, or risk factors for the disease development, as this was not within the study’s aims, and we believe these were adequately studied in the literature. Second, we did not provide a rotational profile assessment, as we believe a CT scan is mandatory for proper rotation assessment, which was not obtained in our patients. Third, the study’s retrospective nature and the loss of some patients, which further lowered the study sample size, might affect the results’ robustness. Fourth, we did not offer a control group where other management protocols could be compared to the technique we provided to delineate its pros and cons. Last, owing to the relatively short follow up period, we might have underestimated the actual recurrence rate, especially as some of the patients are relatively young and we did not perform lateral hemiepiphysiodesis.

Conclusion

Correcting deformities associated with late-presenting infantile Langenskiold stage V and VI Blount’s disease by acutely elevating the medial tibial plateau depression, which was supported with fibular autograft combined with below the tibial tuberosity tibial osteotomy for gradually correcting of the proximal tibial varus deformity assisted by the Illizarov external fixator, provided promising results with acceptable complications and recurrence incidence. However, the results we obtained should be cautiously interpreted, and therefore longer follow-ups and conducting a comparative study are advisable to further assess the advantages and drawbacks of such a surgical technique.
