Mechanistic Modeling of Electrochemical Gold Transfer from Gold Leaf to Screen-Printed Electrodes
Authors: Paithoon Prasertying, Thitaporn Sonsa-Ard, Duangjai Nacapricha, Thinnapong Wongpakdee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical deposition of gold onto screen-printed electrodes (SPEs) typically relies on soluble gold precursors, which limits the scalability of low-cost, disposable sensing platforms. Here, we present a mechanistic modeling framework for solid-source electrochemical gold transfer, in which nanometric gold leaf (100–200 nm) serves as the precursor for electrodeposition. The process is described as a sequential system involving potentiostatic anodic dissolution of gold leaf, followed by electrodeposition onto carbon SPEs. The model integrates Butler–Volmer interfacial kinetics with diffusive transport of chloroaurate species, enabling quantitative analysis of how dissolution conditions govern the generation of electroactive species and subsequently control deposition behavior. Simulations reveal that the applied potential dictates the dissolution flux and resulting gold concentration, which directly determines the electrodeposition driving force. The gold transfer efficiency increases with deposition kinetics but exhibits a clear saturation behavior, indicating a transition from kinetically controlled to source-limited conditions. A dimensionless Damköhler-type analysis further demonstrates that system performance is governed by the interplay between reaction kinetics and mass transport, with a well-defined transition between kinetic and transport-limited regimes. This work establishes a predictive framework for solid-precursor electrochemical metal transfer and provides design guidelines for scalable, low-cost fabrication of gold-modified electrochemical sensors.

Introduction

Gold-modified electrodes are widely used in electrochemical sensing, electrocatalysis, and bioanalytical applications , due to their high conductivity, chemical stability, and ability to form well-defined surface chemistries with thiolated molecules. In particular, the modification of carbon screen-printed electrodes (SPEs) with gold nanostructures has become a common strategy for enhancing analytical sensitivity and facilitating surface functionalization in disposable sensing platforms. , Numerous studies have demonstrated that electrochemical deposition of gold onto SPE substrates significantly improves electron-transfer kinetics, surface area, and catalytic activity, enabling applications ranging from environmental monitoring to biosensing. ,−

Electrodeposition of gold on SPEs is typically performed using soluble gold precursors such as chloroauric acid (HAuCl4) which provides the tetrachloroaurate complex [AuCl4]− as the electroactive species. Under cathodic polarization, this complex is reduced to metallic gold, forming thin films or nanoparticles on the electrode surface. − Control over deposition parameters such as potential, precursor concentration, and deposition time is critical for tuning the morphology and electrochemical properties of the resulting gold layer. However, the use of high-purity gold precursor solutions can increase material costs, which may limit the scalability of disposable electrochemical sensors and large-scale electrode fabrication.

An alternative approach is to generate dissolved gold species electrochemically through anodic dissolution of metallic gold in chloride-containing media. − In such systems, metallic gold is oxidized to soluble chloro-complexes, predominantly [AuCl4]− which can subsequently be reduced back to metallic gold at a second electrode. The electrochemical behavior of gold in halide-containing electrolytes has been extensively investigated in corrosion, electro-dissolution, and metal recovery studies, ,, where the formation and transport of chloroaurate complexes play a central role in determining dissolution kinetics and mass transport behavior. These reactions provide a fundamental basis for electrochemical gold transfer processes.

Despite the extensive literature on gold electrodeposition and gold dissolution, relatively little attention has been devoted to understanding the mechanistic process linkage between these two processes in a controlled electrochemical transfer system. In particular, when gold is used as a solid source materialsuch as thin gold leaf, widely used in electrochemical sensor fabrication − the dissolution flux, transport of dissolved complexes, and subsequent deposition behavior become intrinsically linked. Quantitative understanding of this coupled system requires consideration of both interfacial electrochemical kinetics and diffusive mass transport of dissolved species in the electrolyte domain.

Mechanistic modeling provides a powerful framework for analyzing such coupled electrochemical processes. − By combining kinetic expressions describing interfacial charge transfer with transport equations governing species diffusion, modeling approaches can reveal how applied potential, diffusion, and interfacial flux collectively influence dissolution dynamics and material transfer efficiency. These insights are particularly valuable for guiding experimental design and identifying key parameters controlling electrochemical material transfer systems.

In this work, a mechanistic modeling predictive framework is developed to investigate the electrochemical transfer of gold from nanometric gold leaf to carbon screen-printed electrodes under potentiostatic control. Although gold dissolution in chloride media and gold-leaf electrodes have each been studied, no quantitative mechanistic framework has been reported for sequential electrochemical transfer from nanometric gold leaf. Unlike conventional electrodeposition models, which generally assume a predefined soluble gold precursor in the electrolyte, the present framework treats nanometric gold leaf as a finite solid-state precursor and explicitly couples its anodic dissolution with the subsequent cathodic electrodeposition of dissolved gold species onto the screen-printed electrode surface. This sequential solid-source electrochemical mechanism provides a fundamentally different interpretation of gold transfer dynamics under confined reagent conditions and establishes a mechanistic basis for scalable, reagent-minimized fabrication of disposable electrochemical sensors. To quantitatively describe this sequential gold-transfer mechanism, the model incorporates Butler–Volmer interfacial kinetics coupled with diffusive transport of dissolved gold species, while treating dissolution and deposition as temporally linked electrochemical processes. Simulations reveal the transient dissolution behavior of gold leaf, the relationship between dissolution-derived species generation and subsequent deposition behavior, and the parameters governing gold transfer efficiency. These results provide a predictive basis for low-cost gold modification strategies for screen-printed electrochemical sensors. Furthermore, dimensionless analysis provides a general framework for interpreting the relative contributions of reaction kinetics and mass transport. In particular, Damköhler-type formulations have been widely used to characterize transitions between kinetically controlled and transport-limited regimes in electrochemical systems. We hypothesized that gold transfer efficiency is governed not only by dissolution kinetics, but also by the dynamic coupling between precursor generation, mass transport, and deposition kinetics. Accordingly, the model was used to address three specific questions: (i) how the applied dissolution potential determines the generation of dissolved [AuCl4]− from nanometric gold leaf, (ii) how the dissolution-derived precursor concentration controls the subsequent deposition current and deposited gold amount, and (iii) how kinetic and transport parameters define the transition between kinetically controlled, source-limited, and transport-limited regimes.

Methodology

System Description and Sequential Electrochemical Framework

A sequential two-step electrochemical framework was established to describe the transfer of gold from nanometric gold leaf to a carbon screen-printed electrode (SPE). In contrast to coupled or simultaneous systems, the present approach as illustrated in Figure treats anodic dissolution and electrodeposition as two distinct but connected processes, linked through the generation and subsequent consumption of dissolved gold species.

Sequential two-step electrochemical gold transfer process: anodic dissolution of gold leaf in chloride electrolyte and subsequent electrodeposition onto a carbon screen-printed electrode (SPE).

In the first step, metallic gold is electrochemically dissolved from the gold leaf under potentiostatic control in a chloride-containing electrolyte. The gold leaf serves as the working electrode (WE), while a platinum electrode and an Ag/AgCl electrode are employed as the counter electrode (CE) and reference electrode (RE), respectively. Under anodic polarization, gold is oxidized to soluble chloroaurate complexes, predominantly [AuCl4]−, according to 1 Au+4Cl−→[AuCl4]−+3e−

The anodic dissolution process generates a measurable Faradaic current, which directly determines the total amount of dissolved gold through charge integration. The outcome of this step is therefore characterized by the concentration of dissolved gold species in the electrolyte, which serves as a precursor for the subsequent electrodeposition process.

In the second step, the solution containing dissolved gold species is transferred to a separate electrochemical system and used as the deposition medium. A carbon screen-printed electrode acts as the working electrode, and cathodic polarization is applied to reduce the dissolved chloroaurate complexes back to metallic gold: 2 [AuCl4]−+3e−→Au+4Cl−

This step results in the formation of a gold layer on the surface of the carbon electrode. Importantly, the electrodeposition process is governed by the initial concentration of dissolved gold species generated during the dissolution step, thereby establishing a direct mechanistic link between the two stages of the sequential process.

Modeling Assumptions

To develop a tractable mechanistic description of the sequential electrochemical gold transfer process, a series of simplifying assumptions were adopted for each step. These assumptions were introduced to isolate the dominant physicochemical factors governing the sequential transfer process and to provide a first-order mechanistic description. For the anodic dissolution step, the electrochemical system is assumed to operate under ideal potentiostatic control in a three-electrode configuration. The gold leaf was modeled as a planar electrode with a representative initial thickness in the nanometric range of 100–200 nm, , undergoing homogeneous dissolution across its surface. To better define the solid gold source used in the experimental studies, the elemental composition of the commercial gold leaf was characterized by EDX. The analysis confirmed Au as the dominant metallic component, with detectable Ag and minor C/O contributions, as shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). Morphological changes such as surface roughening, pitting, or oxide formation are not explicitly considered. This assumption is most appropriate for thin, relatively uniform gold-leaf domains under controlled laboratory conditions; deviations from planarity, local defects, wrinkling, or heterogeneous contact may alter the local current distribution and therefore affect the absolute dissolution rate. The anodic dissolution kinetics are described using an effective Butler–Volmer formulation, which serves as a phenomenological representation of the overall current–potential behavior in the chloride-containing electrolyte.

For the electrodeposition step, the electrolyte obtained from the dissolution process is assumed to be well mixed, such that the concentration of dissolved gold species is spatially uniform throughout the solution. As a result, mass transport limitations within the bulk electrolyte are neglected, and the deposition process is described using a lumped kinetic model based on the bulk concentration of gold species. Interfacial phenomena such as nucleation, crystal growth, and surface restructuring of the deposited gold layer are not explicitly included in the present framework. Therefore, the model is not intended to predict detailed deposit morphology, particle size, or surface roughness of the resulting gold layer. Instead, it focuses on the overall material balance, deposition current response, and gold transfer efficiency.

The two steps are coupled exclusively through the total amount of dissolved gold generated during the anodic dissolution process, which defines the initial concentration of gold species available for electrodeposition. Additional transport effects, including migration and convection, are neglected, and the model is intended to capture the essential features of sequential gold transfer rather than to provide a complete description of all physicochemical processes occurring at the electrode interfaces. Consequently, the quantitative predictions should be regarded as most applicable within the defined parameter space and experimental configuration considered in this study. Extension of the framework to other electrode geometries, electrolyte compositions, gold-source morphologies, or flow conditions would require recalibration of the effective kinetic and transport parameters. Nevertheless, the qualitative trends revealed by the model, including the dependence of gold generation on dissolution conditions and the transition from kinetically controlled to source-limited transfer behavior, are expected to provide useful design guidance for solid-source electrochemical metal-transfer systems. Accordingly, the simulated trends should be interpreted primarily in terms of relative mechanistic behavior rather than absolute predictions for all possible gold-leaf morphologies, electrolyte compositions, or electrode surface structures.

Gold Transfer Efficiency

The overall efficiency of the sequential electrochemical gold transfer process was quantified by comparing the amount of gold deposited in the second step with the amount of gold dissolved in the first step. The gold transfer efficiency (YAu ) was defined as 9 YAu=NAu,depNAu,diss where NAu , dep is the total amount of gold deposited during the electrodeposition step and NAu , diss is the total amount of gold dissolved during the anodic dissolution step.

This definition provides a physically meaningful metric for evaluating the effectiveness of the sequential process, as it directly reflects the fraction of dissolved gold that is successfully recovered onto the deposition electrode. In this framework, the efficiency is governed by the interplay between the generation of dissolved gold species in the first step and their subsequent consumption during electrodeposition.

It should be noted that the present definition does not explicitly account for potential losses arising from side reactions, incomplete reduction, or adsorption phenomena. Instead, the efficiency represents an idealized measure of gold transfer under the assumptions of the model and is primarily intended for comparative analysis across different operating conditions.

Results and Discussion

Anodic Dissolution Behavior of Gold Leaf

The anodic dissolution behavior of the gold leaf under potentiostatic conditions was first investigated to establish the kinetics governing the generation of dissolved gold species. The simulated current density–time profiles (Figure ) exhibit a characteristic transient response, with an initially high current density followed by a gradual decay over time.

Simulated anodic current density transients for the potentiostatic dissolution of nanometric gold leaf at different applied potentials. Higher applied potentials result in increased initial current density and accelerated decay, reflecting enhanced dissolution kinetics and faster consumption of the gold source.

The initial high current density arises from the strong driving force for gold oxidation under the applied potential, where the electrode surface is fully available for reaction. As the dissolution proceeds, the current decreases progressively, reflecting the reduction in the effective driving force associated with the accumulation of dissolved gold species near the interface and the depletion of readily accessible gold at the electrode surface.

The influence of applied potential on the dissolution behavior is clearly observed in Figure , where increasing the anodic potential results in a significant increase in the initial current density. This behavior is consistent with the exponential dependence predicted by the Butler–Volmer formulation. At higher applied potentials, the dissolution rate is enhanced, leading to a more rapid consumption of the gold leaf and a shorter duration of the high-current regime.

The simulated current transients are consistent with the experimentally observed anodic current density–time responses obtained during gold-leaf electrodissolution (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The experimental profiles recorded at 1.15–1.21 V show an initially high anodic current followed by progressive decay toward the baseline, which is consistent with the gradual consumption of the electrochemically accessible gold source. In addition, the more rapid decay observed at higher applied potentials supports the model prediction that stronger anodic polarization accelerates gold dissolution. This agreement provides experimental support for the essential transient features described by the model, while the comparison specifically validates the anodic dissolution step of the sequential framework.

Generation of Dissolved Gold Concentration

The cumulative amount of dissolved gold, NAu , diss , and the corresponding solution-phase gold concentration, CAu , were evaluated based on the time-integrated anodic current response obtained in Step 1. As shown in Figure a, the total amount of dissolved gold increases monotonically with time for all applied potentials, with higher potentials yielding significantly larger NAu , diss values. This behavior directly reflects the increase in total charge passed during anodic dissolution, as observed from the current density transients in Figure .

Simulated (a) cumulative dissolved gold, NAu , diss , and (b) corresponding dissolved gold concentration, CAu , as functions of time at different applied potentials. Higher applied potentials result in greater amounts of dissolved gold and higher solution concentrations, reflecting increased anodic dissolution rates. These outputs represent the precursor generation step that defines the initial gold concentration available for subsequent electrodeposition.

The approximately linear increase in NAu , diss with time suggests that, within the investigated time window, the dissolution process remains predominantly governed by the applied potential and the associated charge-transfer kinetics. Although the current density exhibits an initial decay due to the gradual onset of source-limited regime (Figure ), the cumulative nature of NAu , diss results in a near-linear growth, indicating that the overall gold dissolution is primarily determined by the time-integrated current rather than instantaneous transport constraints.

The corresponding dissolved gold concentration, CAu , shown in Figure b, follows the same trend as NAu , diss , increasing linearly with time and scaling with the applied potential. Since CAu is directly proportional to NAu , diss through the fixed solution volume, the observed behavior confirms that higher applied potentials lead to a greater accumulation of dissolved gold species in the electrolyte. Notably, the concentration remains in the submicromolar range throughout the simulation, which is consistent with the limited mass of the nanometric gold leaf used as the source material.

Importantly, the dissolved gold concentration generated in Step 1 serves as the initial condition for the subsequent electrodeposition process in Step 2. Therefore, the dependence of CAu on the applied dissolution potential establishes a direct mechanistic link between the anodic dissolution and electrodeposition steps. This process linkage highlights the critical role of the dissolution conditions in determining the efficiency and extent of gold transfer onto the target electrode.

To experimentally support the model-predicted generation of dissolved gold species, ICP-MS analysis was performed on electrolyte solutions obtained after gold-leaf electrodissolution (Section S3 and Table S1, Supporting Information). Under identical electrodissolution conditions at 1.21 V vs Ag/AgCl, the measured Au concentration increased with gold leaf size. This result provides direct experimental evidence for dissolved gold generation during Step 1 and supports the model interpretation that the precursor concentration is governed by the extent of electrochemical dissolution.

Electrodeposition Behavior from Dissolved Gold Precursor

The electrodeposition behavior of dissolved gold species in Step 2 was evaluated based on the initial solution concentrations generated during the anodic dissolution step. As shown in Figure , the temporal evolution of cathodic current density, dissolved gold concentration, and cumulative deposited gold collectively describes the sequential dependence between electrochemical reduction and mass transport in the system.

Simulated electrodeposition behavior of dissolved gold species during the second step of the sequential process: (a) cathodic current density transients, (b) temporal evolution of dissolved gold concentration in solution, and (c) cumulative amount of deposited gold at different initial gold concentrations derived from the anodic dissolution step.

The cathodic current density transients (Figure a) exhibit a pronounced decay with time for all initial conditions. At the initial stage, higher dissolved gold concentrations result in larger cathodic current densities, reflecting an increased rate of electrochemical reduction at the electrode surface. As the deposition proceeds, the magnitude of the current density decreases progressively, indicating a reduction in the availability of electroactive gold species in the vicinity of the electrode. This behavior is consistent with a concentration-dependent deposition process, where the current density scales with the instantaneous concentration of dissolved gold species.

The temporal evolution of dissolved gold concentration (Figure b) further confirms this behavior. For all cases, CAu decreases monotonically with time, demonstrating the continuous consumption of [AuCl4]− during electrodeposition. The rate of concentration decay is strongly dependent on the initial concentration, with higher initial values leading to a more rapid depletion in absolute terms. This trend highlights the role of mass balance in the system, where the consumption of dissolved species is directly coupled to the electrochemical flux at the electrode interface.

The cumulative amount of deposited gold, NAu , dep , shown in Figure c, increases rapidly at the initial stage and gradually approaches a plateau at longer times. The initial steep increase corresponds to the high deposition rate driven by elevated gold concentrations, while the subsequent decrease in slope reflects the progressive depletion of dissolved gold species. The asymptotic behavior observed at longer times indicates that the deposition process becomes limited by the availability of gold in solution, rather than by charge-transfer kinetics.

Importantly, the results demonstrate that the electrodeposition step is governed by a coupled electrochemical–mass transport mechanism, in which the deposition rate is intrinsically linked to the transient concentration of dissolved gold species. The initial concentration, determined by the anodic dissolution conditions in Step 1 (Figure ), directly controls both the magnitude of the cathodic current and the total amount of gold that can be deposited. This establishes a clear mechanistic connection between the two steps of the sequential process and highlights the critical role of solution composition in determining the overall gold transfer efficiency.

To experimentally examine whether the gold species generated from gold-leaf electrodissolution could serve as a precursor for electrodeposition, the electrodissolution-derived gold solution was used to modify a screen-printed carbon electrode. The modification effectiveness was evaluated by comparing the relative change in the calibration slope before and after modification. For reference, the response enhancement obtained using the dissolved-gold method was compared with that reported in our previous study using a pure gold precursor solution from Sigma-Aldrich.

For the dissolved-gold method as presented in Figure S3, the anodic and cathodic slopes increased by 13.3% and 11.8%, respectively, after modification. In comparison, the pure standard gold method produced corresponding increases of 16.2% and 17.2%. Although the absolute slope values cannot be directly compared because the two experimental data sets were obtained under different calibration scales, both approaches produced the same trend of enhanced anodic and cathodic responses after gold modification. These results support the feasibility of using electrodissolution-derived gold species as an alternative precursor for electrode modification. The comparable magnitude of relative response enhancement further suggests that the dissolved-gold method can provide an effective route for gold modification without relying exclusively on a conventional high-purity soluble gold precursor.

Gold Transfer Efficiency

The overall performance of the sequential system was evaluated using the gold transfer efficiency, YAu , defined as the fraction of dissolved gold recovered during the electrodeposition step. As shown in Figure a, YAu increases rapidly at short times and gradually approaches an asymptotic value. This behavior reflects the progressive depletion of dissolved [AuCl4]− during electrodeposition. At early times, high concentrations of electroactive species sustain large cathodic fluxes, leading to a rapid increase in efficiency. As the concentration decreases, the deposition rate declines, resulting in a slower approach to equilibrium.

A clear dependence on the applied potential is observed, with higher potentials yielding consistently higher efficiencies. This trend arises from the increased generation of dissolved gold during anodic dissolution, which provides a larger reservoir for subsequent recovery.

The influence of deposition kinetics is further illustrated in Figure b. The final gold transfer efficiency increases sharply with the deposition rate constant, kdep , at low values, followed by a plateau at higher values. This saturation behavior indicates that the system transitions from a kinetically controlled regime to a mass-limited regime, where the total recoverable gold is constrained by the amount generated in Step 1 rather than the deposition rate.

These results demonstrate that, while faster electrodeposition enhances gold recovery at early stages, the ultimate efficiency is fundamentally limited by the available dissolved gold. Therefore, optimization of the sequential system requires not only efficient deposition kinetics but also sufficient generation of dissolved species during the anodic dissolution step.

As shown in Figure , the final gold transfer efficiency, YAu , exhibits a sigmoidal dependence on Da, clearly revealing a transition between distinct mechanistic regimes. At low Da(Da ≪ 1), the system is kinetically controlled, where the electrodeposition rate is insufficient to effectively consume the dissolved [AuCl4]−, resulting in low transfer efficiency. At intermediate Da, a mixed regime is observed, characterized by a rapid increase in YAu . In this region, both interfacial kinetics and mass transport contribute comparably to the overall behavior, leading to strong sensitivity to system parameters. This regime represents the operational window where optimization of the deposition rate constant yields the greatest improvement in efficiency.

At high Da(Da ≫ 1), the system transitions to a mass transport-limited regime, where the electrodeposition process is sufficiently fast such that the overall efficiency is governed by the rate of diffusive supply of dissolved gold species. As a result, YAu approaches unity and becomes insensitive to further increases in kdep , indicating diminishing returns in kinetic enhancement.

The collapse of system behavior onto a single master curve in Figure demonstrates that the performance of the sequential system can be universally described by the Damköhler number, independent of specific operating conditions. This finding confirms that the interplay between reaction kinetics and mass transport is the fundamental factor controlling gold transfer efficiency.

From a design perspective, the analysis indicates that optimal system performance is achieved when operating in the transport-limited regime (Da ≫ 1), where near-complete recovery of dissolved gold can be attained. Importantly, this implies that increasing the deposition rate constant beyond a threshold value is not an efficient strategy for further improving performance. Instead, greater emphasis should be placed on enhancing the generation of dissolved gold during the anodic dissolution step and minimizing diffusion limitations within the system. Overall, the Damköhler-based analysis provides a unifying mechanistic framework for the sequential electrochemical gold transfer process, enabling rational optimization and predictive scaling of system behavior across different electrochemical configurations.

Conclusions

A mechanistic modeling framework was developed to investigate the sequential electrochemical transfer of gold from nanometric gold leaf to carbon screen-printed electrodes. By decoupling anodic dissolution and electrodeposition into two independent steps, the system enables explicit analysis of the interplay between reaction kinetics and mass transport.

The anodic dissolution process was shown to be initially governed by charge-transfer kinetics, followed by a transition to source-limited behavior as dissolved [AuCl4]− accumulates near the electrode interface. The amount of dissolved gold generated during this step was found to strongly depend on the applied potential, thereby establishing the initial conditions for subsequent electrodeposition. During the electrodeposition step, the reduction of [AuCl4]− leads to progressive depletion of dissolved gold, resulting in a time-dependent decrease in deposition rate. The overall gold transfer efficiency increases monotonically and approaches an asymptotic limit, reflecting the finite reservoir of electroactive species available in solution.

A key finding of this work is that the system behavior can be unified using a Damköhler-type framework. The sigmoidal dependence of the gold transfer efficiency on the Damköhler number reveals a clear transition from kinetically controlled to mass transport-limited regimes. This result demonstrates that the overall performance of the sequential system is fundamentally governed by the competition between interfacial reaction rates and diffusive transport.

Importantly, the analysis shows that increasing the deposition rate constant beyond a threshold value yields diminishing returns in efficiency, as the system becomes limited by the availability of dissolved gold. This highlights that optimization of the sequential electrochemical transfer process should prioritize enhancing the generation and transport of dissolved species rather than solely accelerating interfacial kinetics. Overall, the proposed framework provides a generalized and physically grounded basis for understanding electrochemical material transfer processes and offers a predictive tool for the rational design of low-cost, scalable strategies for electrode modification.
