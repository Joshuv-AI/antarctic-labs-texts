Non-invasive assessment techniques for renal fibrosis: advances and perspectives
Authors: Yumeng Tang, Yixi Ke, Hualin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal fibrosis is a critical pathological process driving chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Early diagnosis is essential for timely intervention, yet traditional methods like renal biopsy are invasive and present significant limitations. Consequently, noninvasive techniques are gaining attention for renal fibrosis assessment. Biomarkers such as transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) and platelet-derived growth factor D (PDGF-D) have been explored for monitoring fibrosis progression, though their specificity and reliability challenges persist. Emerging imaging techniques, including molecular and functional imaging, offer valuable structural and functional insights but remain limited in detecting early-stage fibrosis. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising tool to enhance diagnostic accuracy by integrating imaging and biomarker data. Machine learning algorithms applied to ultrasound, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging have demonstrated improved predictive capabilities for renal fibrosis detection. Furthermore, AI-driven multimodal approaches, combining clinical, imaging, and biomarker data, provide new opportunities for accurate, noninvasive diagnosis and monitoring. Despite these advancements, challenges such as small sample sizes, lack of standardization, and AI model interpretability must be addressed. Future research should focus on refining noninvasive biomarkers, improving imaging techniques, and developing AI-driven models to enhance diagnostic accuracy and clinical applicability in renal fibrosis assessment. This review provides a comprehensive overview of recent advancements in noninvasive approaches for detecting renal fibrosis, highlighting their potential to advance clinical decision-making and ultimately benefiting CKD management and patient outcomes.Keywords: Fibrosis, kidney, biopsy, biomarkers, diagnostic imaging, artificial intelligence

Introduction

Renal fibrosis, characterized by tubular atrophy, interstitial inflammation/fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis, and vascular thinning with excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, is a key pathological process driving chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and irreversible kidney damage [1,2]. Early diagnosis and intervention are critical to delay kidney damage, but renal needle biopsy—the current routine diagnostic method—is invasive, poorly reproducible, and insensitive for early renal fibrosis detection, with reported single ultrasound puncture success rates of 56.3% [3]. While biomarkers and imaging have emerged as noninvasive alternatives, biomarkers lack specificity for renal fibrosis progression, and imaging techniques often fail to detect early microstructural changes, leading to late diagnosis [4]. However, integrating artificial intelligence (AI) technology with imaging modalities holds promise to enhance diagnostic accuracy for early renal fibrosis detection in the significant data era. This review summarizes recent advancements in renal fibrosis biomarkers and discusses the roles of imaging and AI-based techniques in noninvasive renal fibrosis assessment, addressing current evidence gaps in these fields [5–9].

Molecular biology techniques

Blood biomarkers

Profibrotic growth factors

Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1)

TGF-β1, a key pro-fibrotic cytokine in the TGF-β superfamily, drives renal fibrosis by stimulating fibroblast activation and promoting ECM deposition while inhibiting its degradation [10]. Detectable in bodily fluids, TGF-β1 theoretically tracks fibrosis progression but lacks renal specificity due to its role in inflammation, immunity, and wound healing, requiring integration with organ-specific markers for accurate assessment. For example, the urinary TGF-β1/creatinine ratio correlates with interstitial fibrosis in IgA nephropathy (r = 0.62) [6], while renal tissue immunohistochemistry with α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) staining distinguishes renal from systemic fibrosis [11]. Challenges include transient fluctuations from infections, individual variability, and technical limitations. Multi-marker models (e.g., TGF-β1 + urinary KIM-1 + collagen markers) improve diagnostic accuracy, as seen in a 200-patient study where TGF-β1 combined with urinary C3M achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.89 versus 0.72 for TGF-β1 alone [6,12]. Tissue mRNA expression and active TGF-β1 detection enhance organ specificity [10,13]. Clinically, normal serum TGF-β1 rules out severe fibrosis (negative predictive value >90%), while elevated levels (>1000 pg/mL) require imaging for organ localization. As a universal but nonspecific fibrosis marker, TGF-β1 must be paired with urinary or imaging biomarkers for targeted diagnosis. Future directions include TGF-β1 receptor-targeted positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and single-cell sequencing to identify renal-specific co-expressed markers [11,14–16].

Platelet-derived growth factor D (PDGF-D)

PDGF-D is a secreted glycoprotein belonging to the PDGF/VEGF family, functioning significantly in cell proliferation, migration, survival, and angiogenesis. PDGF-D, encoded by the PDGFD gene, exerts its biological effects by binding to cell surface receptors [17]. PDGF-D is measurable in platelets or plasma extracted from the blood. In a study carried out by Boor et al. overexpression of PDGF-D in healthy mice increased the deposition of interstitial collagen, while PDGF-D gene depletion significantly reduced fibrosis in both mouse models of renal scarring, supporting that PDGF-D can work as a driver in the progression of renal fibrosis [18]. PDGF-D is also a potential mediator of liver fibrosis [19]. Compared to several broad-spectrum inflammatory markers, PDGF-D exhibited higher specificity for fibrosis, facilitating a more accurate prediction of kidney damage. However, the expression of PDGF-D may fluctuate with changes in disease stage and other external conditions. Therefore, it may complicate long-term follow-up, and disrupt the determination of a stable threshold for clinical decision-making.

Markers related to transcription factors and gene regulation

Early growth response protein 2 (EGR2)

EGR2 is a multifunctional transcription factor from the early growth response family. Encoded by the EGR2 gene, EGR2 can be rapidly expressed in response to multiple stimulus signals and regulate the expression of a series of downstream genes to mediate physiological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and stress response. EGR2 also regulates immune response and inflammatory reaction, which are essential components of renal fibrosis [20]. Rather than a biomarker detected directly in blood, urine, or other body fluids, EGR2 is more studied by gene expression analysis or protein localization in tissue samples. However, this does not mean that it cannot be obtained from blood samples. In fact, changes in EGR2 expression can be detected by extracting RNA or protein from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). It may be feasible and useful in case of unavailable tissue samples, yet with unsatisfactory sensitivity and specificity when compared with detection directly from the target tissue. In a mouse unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model, Song et al. found that the deletion of the EGR2 gene could alleviate mouse manifestations of renal fibrosis, but exacerbated this process, suggesting that targeting EGR2 may be a potential strategy for renal fibrosis treatment [11]. However, EGR2 is active in multiple tissues and physiological processes, suggesting relatively low specificity. In addition, compared with other more established biomarkers such as TGF-β1, the role of EGR2 in renal fibrosis is less reported currently, necessitating further exploration to promote our understanding and application in this specific pathological state.

Murine double minute 2 (MDM2)

MDM2, encoded by the MDM2 gene, was first discovered in the study of mouse tumor viruses, hence named ‘murine double minute’. MDM2 protein functions in numerous biological processes, most notably as a major negative regulator of p53, a well-studied tumor suppressor [21]. Pericytes are an important source of myofibroblasts in renal fibrosis. The pericyte-endothelial cell interaction is indispensable for the integrity of renal microvessels [22]. Overexpression of integrin β8 can inhibit pericyte transport, endothelial injury, and renal fibrosis. Noticeably, MDM2 may boost integrin β8 degradation, in turn leading to TGF-β1 release and pericyte transformation. Overall, targeting MDM2/integrin β8 may be a potential therapeutic pathway to alleviate renal fibrosis in diabetic nephropathy (DN) [23]. In addition, detection of MDM2 often involves real-time PCR, immunohistochemistry or other molecular biology techniques, which are more complex and costly than traditional blood biochemistry. Despite a recognized association of MDM2 with renal fibrosis, it may be an indirect factor rather than a direct driver, revealing limited value as a monitoring indicator.

Inflammation-related markers

Interleukin-1β (IL-1β)

IL-1β, derived from activated macrophages mainly, is an important pro-inflammatory cytokine in the IL-1 family [24]. IL-1β may trigger proximal tubular injury and fibrosis to drive the development of tubulointerstitial disease [25]. Furthermore, the detection of IL-1β in blood can reflect systemic conditions. However, renal fibrosis is a local process, local levels of IL-1β in the kidney may not be consistent with its levels in serum or plasma, thus weakening its application as a noninvasive biomarker. Moreover, the levels of IL-1β may vary with the advancement of pathologic staging, especially in subjects transitioning from acute kidney injury (AKI) to chronic fibrosis. Therefore, it remains a challenge to determine the optimal time window to monitor this marker. In addition, the role of IL-1β is often intertwined with other cytokines and growth factors. Relying on IL-1β levels alone may be impossible to fully reflect the complex molecular network of the entire process of fibrosis.

Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)

Also known as Chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2, MCP-1 is primarily responsible for attracting monocytes, memory T cells, and basophils to migrate to sites of inflammation in vivo [26]. Wang et al. found that higher uMCP levels were associated with more severe renal fibrosis but not with urinary urinary collagen type IV levels [27]. Moreover, uMCP was proposed as a promising biomarker for noninvasive assessment of the degree of renal interstitial fibrosis, even in subjects with normal renal function [28]. MCP-1 can be detected by multiple well-established and cost-effective methods, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), flow cytometry, and multiplex analysis platforms. However, blood levels of MCP-1, similar to IL-1β, reflect systemic conditions. Its levels in the local kidney may not be consistent with those in serum or plasma, resulting in a compromised application as a noninvasive biomarker. MCP-1 can also be produced in other organs and thus is involved in the disease process of those organs, which further weakens its specificity in diagnosing renal fibrosis.

ECM-related markers

The amino-terminal propeptide of type III procollagen (PIIINP)

Type III collagen synthesized in the body may produce a larger precursor molecule called procollagen first. Before being secreted into the extracellular space, procollagen may undergo a number of modifications, including the excision of specific sequences at both ends, which are called propeptides, of which the amino-terminal prepeptide is excised [29]. Detecting serum levels of PIIINP, a biomarker of type III collagen synthesis, can help to understand the metabolism of type III collagen in the body. As a biomarker of type III collagen synthesis, the serum level of PIIINP can reflect the metabolism of type III collagen in the body. As renal fibrosis progresses, the synthesis of type III collagen increases, and the corresponding PIIINP levels may also increase [30].As a biomarker of type III collagen synthesis, the serum level of PIIINP can reflect the metabolism of type III collagen in the body. As renal fibrosis progresses, the synthesis of type III collagen increases, and the corresponding PIIINP levels may also rise—for instance, in conditions like systemic sclerosis [31], liver cirrhosis [32], or as a marker of active fibrosis in patients with schistosomal liver fibrosis [33]. Additionally, elevated serum PIIINP may indicate severe sepsis or septic shock and could predict the progression to severe sepsis or septic shock in septic patients [34]. Therefore, PIIINP has also been regarded as a biomarker for sepsis. Therefore, in the clinical setting, comprehensive judgment should be made in combination with other clinical information and examination results.

Lysyl oxidase (LOX)

LOX is a copper-dependent amine oxidase which functions significantly in catalyzing the formation of collagen and elastin cross-links during ECM biosynthesis [35]. Upregulated LOX expression has been documented in fibrotic diseases and other pathological conditions. Patients with renal fibrosis had higher serum LOX levels than those without fibrosis in a prior study comprehensively evaluating 202 patients with kidney disease; moreover, serum LOX levels were significantly higher in patients with moderate to severe renal fibrosis than those in patients with mild renal fibrosis [36]. Therefore, serum LOX levels can distinguish mild from moderate to severe renal fibrosis, suggesting that it can be used as a noninvasive diagnostic and stratified biomarker. However, LOX has a short half-life and hence exhibits rapid fluctuation, resulting in varied results at different sampling time points. Given the complex and multifactorial features of fibrosis, a single marker may not be sufficient to fully describe this process. Therefore, to improve diagnostic accuracy, LOX should be monitored in combination with other markers.

Other potential markers

MicroRNA-33 (miR-33)

The small non-coding RNA molecule miR-33 can regulate intracellular gene expression, playing an important role in regulating lipid metabolism processes [37]. Lama et al. found that mice treated with anti-miR-33pHLIP had higher carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A compared to untreated mice, leading to improved renal fibrosis as well as reduced blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels [38]. miR-33, different from some traditional biomarkers, exhibits higher tissue specificity under specific pathological conditions, thus facilitating the monitoring of kidney disease more accurately. In addition, microRNAs are relatively stable in body fluids such as the blood and are not easily degraded, making them ideal biomarker candidates. Common modern techniques such as real-time quantitative PCR, can measure miR-33 with high sensitivity and specificity. However, despite technical maturity, miR-33 still has a limited application compared with traditional biomarkers, largely due to its high detection cost and technical threshold. Like other potential biomarkers of renal fibrosis, miR-33 is affected by individual differences. Therefore, more large-scale prospective studies are needed to confirm its clinical value, especially in different populations and types of renal fibrosis.

Brain-specific homology box/POU domain protein Pou4f1 (Brn3a)

Pou4f1 of the POU domain protein family is a neuron-specific transcription factor that is essential in the nervous system. Myofibroblasts are a subset of activated fibroblasts characterized by α-smooth muscle actin expression. They are the primary cell type for synthesizing and depositing fibrous collagen during tissue fibrosis [39]. Deng et al. demonstrated that silencing of Pou4f1 in bone marrow-derived macrophages can prevent macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT) in infiltrating renal macrophages, thereby significantly reducing renal fibrosis. Thus, Pou4f1 represents a therapeutic target for MMT-driven tissue fibrosis [40]. Pou4f1 can regulate the expression of a range of downstream genes, serving as a sensitive early warning signal or prognostic factor for renal fibrosis. Pou4f1 is primarily known for its high expression in neural tissue, despite its expression detected in the kidney. Pou4f1 levels in serum or urine may be disturbed by disorders involving other organs, thus weakening its accuracy in reflecting specific conditions within the kidneys. In addition to the central nervous system, Pou4f1 may also be found in immune cells in the blood under certain conditions. However, this is relatively rare, and the expression level can be extremely low, making detection difficult. So far, existing studies on Pou4f1 focused on the nervous system mainly, without adequate evidence on its specific role in renal fibrosis. Moreover, there is a lack of sufficient experimental data to prove its effectiveness as a biomarker. Besides, relying solely on Pou4f1 may potentially overlook other important factors that influence the disease process, given that transcription factors often interact with other proteins through complex networks. Pou4f1 is an intranuclear protein, requiring more complex sample processing procedures (e.g., immunohistochemistry or Western blot) for detection, which are more time-consuming and laborious than traditional blood or urine biomarker testing. For these reasons, it may be a significant challenge to establish a reliable standard to measure and compare the expression levels of Pou4f1 between different patients. Large-scale clinical trials are needed to confirm its applicability as a biomarker of renal fibrosis in a broad population and to determine its predictive value.

FK506 binding protein (FKBP)

FKBPs were first discovered for their ability to bind to the immunosuppressant drug FK506 (also known as tacrolimus) [41]. Hence, FKBPs are recognized as an important target for investigating organ transplant rejection and autoimmune disease treatment. In a prior research, the expression of aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear Translocator (ARNT) in the kidney was successfully increased by using nucleic acids targeting FKBP and Yin Yang 1 (YY1), protecting mice from renal fibrosis caused by UUO, highlighting the potential therapeutic potential of intervening in the FKBP–YY1–ARNT signaling axis [42]. FKBP may offer additional biomarker to evaluate renal fibrosis in the future. However, there is still insufficient research on the specific mechanism of FKBP in renal fibrosis currently, with the establishment of a causal relationship required based on more basic scientific research. In addition, simultaneous development and implementation of new biomarker testing often involve high research and development costs and technical thresholds, requiring an evaluation of their cost-benefit ratio relative to existing methods.

Phospholipase D4 (PLD4)

PLD4 is a member of the phospholipase D family involving in a variety of physiological processes, such as membrane remodeling, exocytosis, and signal transduction [43]. PLD4 has been demonstrated to play a mediating role in renal fibrosis by regulating innate and adaptive immune responses, promoting TGF-β signaling, and inhibiting neutrophil elastase expression, which may partially explain ECM protein accumulation in fibrotic kidneys [44]. However, intracellular proteins or enzymes such as PLD4 are not naturally released into the bloodstream, unless the cells are damaged and the contents leak, which obstruct the detection of their presence and activities. However, intracellular LD4 mRNA expression can be measured using techniques such as qRT-PCR or RNA-seq based on the acquisition of PBMCs. However, there is insufficient scientific evidence to support PLD4 as a biomarker considering a poor understanding of the specific function of PLD4 and its role in renal fibrosis.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)

MMPs are endopeptidases that degrade proteins in the ECM to control many cellular processes such as cell-cell interactions and various signaling pathways, and are also involved in the degradation of cell surface substrates. Both MMPs and the tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases mediate the regulation and progression of cell turnover, fibrosis, and apoptosis in tissues. MMPs play a role in renal fibrosis, such as tubular cell epithelial-mesenchymal transition, activation of resident fibroblasts, endothelial-mesenchymal transition, and pericyte-myofibroblast transdifferentiation. For example, researchers have documented the important role of MMP-9 in the upregulation pathway of renal fibrosis [45]. MMPs participate in the degradation and reconstruction of the matrix directly during the pathological process of renal fibrosis. Thus, MMPs can reveal stromal changes in the course of kidney disease. MMPs exhibit dynamic activities, supporting their potential in monitoring disease progression or treatment efficacy. Meanwhile, MMPs are detectable via a series of laboratory techniques, such as ELISA, Western blot, zymography, among others, providing flexible options. However, there may be differences in the measurements of local MMP activity in the kidneys when compared with those in blood or urine, compromising the accuracy of predicting specific renal conditions. Further research is also needed to demonstrate that inhibition of the MMP-9 pathway may be a therapeutic strategy for the treatment of renal fibrosis in CKD.

Circulating MMPs may exist in the form of inactive precursors (such as zymogens) or bound to inhibitors. Detecting only the total concentration cannot distinguish their active states. Techniques like zymography can be used to specifically quantify MMPs with catalytic activity (rather than the total amount), directly reflecting their functional status. In addition, the activity of MMPs is regulated by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), and the ratio of MMPs to TIMPs in the circulation can better reflect the actual functional balance. For example, detecting the ratio of MMP-9 to TIMP-1 can more accurately evaluate its matrix degradation activity in renal fibrosis. Furthermore, the activity and distribution of local MMPs can be detected through renal tissue biopsy, or functional imaging (such as the molecular imaging technology mentioned later in this article) can be used to observe the dynamics of matrix remodeling in the kidney. By correlating the detection results of circulating MMPs with local functional indicators, the interference caused by differences between circulating and local MMPs can be reduced.

Urine markers

Markers of tubular injury

Kidney injury molecule (KIM-1)

KIM-1, which is found mainly in proximal tubular epithelial cells, shows significantly elevated levels when renal injury is present. Under normal conditions, its expression levels are low or undetectable. When AKI or CKD occurs, KIM-1 expression rises. In fact, KIM-1 is more regarded as a biomarker of AKI. At present, there are relatively few large-scale clinical trials focusing on KIM-1 to explore its direct relationship with renal fibrosis, despite its important role in the process of kidney injury and repair. Several studies have indirectly addressed the relationship between KIM-1 and renal fibrosis. For instance, tissue KIM-1 can predict adverse renal outcomes in patients with IgA nephropathy independently [46]. Measurement of urine and renal KIM-1 levels could facilitate the assessment of the severity and prognosis of renal pathological damage in adult patients with Henoch-Schonlein purpura nephritis [47]. Furthermore, although KIM-1 has shown potential as an early diagnostic tool for renal fibrosis, its diagnostic efficacy depends on the specific clinical setting and the type of population studied. In addition, other clinical information needs to be considered when interpreting the results since KIM-1 can be upregulated in different types of kidney injury. At this stage, more prospective studies and large-scale clinical trials are necessitated to confirm the exact application value of KIM-1 in the early diagnosis of renal fibrosis. Future directions of study may clarify the clarification of the optimal cutoff value of KIM-1, the benefits of its integration with other markers, and its specific role in guiding clinical decision-making.

Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL)

Also known as lipocalin-2 or 24p3, NGAL is a small molecule protein of the lipocalin family. Increase in urine NGAL, as an indicator of residual renal function, is directly related to serum creatinine and proteinuria, and inversely correlated with glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [48,49]. Meanwhile, urinary NGAL has been shown to be an independent predictor of ESRD risk and mortality in patients with CKD [50]. In older patients with CKD, serum NGAL would suggest renal impairment, and was regarded an independent risk biomarker for ESRD progression [51]. On the basis of the intimate association of NGAL with renal indicators (e.g., serum creatinine and proteinuria) as described above, NGAL may become an indirect biomarker for the evaluation of renal fibrosis, although no clear evidence supporting the direct relationship of NGAL to renal fibrosis.

Markers of renal tubular function

Microalbuminuria

Proteinuria was reported to be weakly or uncorrelated with tubulointerstitial fibrosis (TIF) in some studies, and the association between albuminuria and TIF was still unclear and less accurate than estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in assessing the degree of tubulointerstitial fibrosis [52–54].

Retinol-binding proteins (RBP)

RBP is a protein responsible for transporting retinol in the body. Trace amounts of RBP can be filtered through the glomeruli into the urine. Normally, most of the filtered RBPs are reabsorbed by the renal tubules, with only a small amount detected in the urine. In contrast, the concentration of RBP in the urine may be elevated in the presence of tubular injury or dysfunction. Urine RBP levels and RBP/creatinine ratios were found to be positively correlated with renal fibrosis development in patients with CKD [55]. Therefore, measurement of RBP levels in urine may be an indicative factor tubular function, especially when monitoring early kidney injury or diseases.

Other biomarkers

Dickkopf-3(DKK3)

DKK3 is a secreted glycoprotein belonging to the Dickkopf family, which is mainly involved in pathophysiological processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and tissue fibrosis by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [56,57]. In the kidney, DKK3 can be secreted by renal tubular epithelial cells and renal interstitial fibroblasts, and its expression level is closely related to the occurrence and development of renal fibrosis.

Under normal physiological conditions, the basal expression of DKK3 maintains the structural stability of renal tissues. It avoids abnormal deposition of extracellular matrix by moderately inhibiting the excessive activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [58]. However, in pathological states (such as chronic kidney disease, obstructive nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy, etc.), the expression of DKK3 is significantly upregulated, becoming a key driver promoting renal fibrosis. In a mouse model of unilateral ureteral obstruction, the levels of DKK3 mRNA and protein in renal tissues were 2–3 times higher than those in normal mice, and were positively correlated with the degree of renal interstitial fibrosis (the area of collagen deposition shown by Masson staining) (r = 0.76) [59]. In in vitro experiments, the H2O2-induced HK-2 cell (human renal tubular epithelial cell) model also showed that DKK3 expression increased with the aggravation of oxidative stress damage, and knockout of DKK3 could significantly reduce the expression of fibrosis markers such as α-SMA and fibronectin [59].

Mechanistically, DKK3 promotes renal fibrosis by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Specifically, DKK3 can enhance the nuclear translocation of β-catenin, form a complex with the transcription factor 4, bind to the promoter region of mitochondrial fission factor, and promote its transcriptional expression. The upregulation of mitochondrial fission factor accelerates mitochondrial fission, leading to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, a reduction in ATP production, and an increase in reactive oxygen species release, which in turn induces apoptosis and oxidative stress of renal tubular epithelial cells, forming a vicious cycle of ‘mitochondrial dysfunction - fibrosis’ [59]. In addition, the abnormally high expression of DKK3 is also related to epigenetic regulation: the methyltransferase METTL3 increases the m6A modification of DKK3 mRNA (3 key modification sites predicted by the SRAMP database), improves its stability, thereby upregulating the protein level of DKK3 in the unilateral ureteral obstruction model and HK-2 cells [59]. In terms of clinical significance, DKK3 can serve as a potential biomarker for renal fibrosis. Studies have shown that the level of urinary DKK3 is significantly elevated in CKD patients and can independently predict the rate of eGFR decline (Hazard Ratio = 1.83, 95% Confidence Interval = 1.32–2.53) [60]. Compared with traditional markers (such as serum creatinine), urinary DKK3 has better performance in identifying early renal fibrosis (Banff score ci1-ci2) (AUC = 0.78 vs. 0.65) [60]. In the diabetic nephropathy model, the expression of DKK3 is also correlated with the degree of proteinuria (r = 0.69), suggesting that it may reflect the dynamic process of renal injury [59].

However, the application of DKK3 has limitations: first, DKK3 is also elevated in fibrosis of organs such as the liver and heart, with insufficient specificity, so it needs to be combined with kidney-specific indicators (such as urinary KIM-1) for joint judgment [57]; second, its detection relies on ELISA or qRT-PCR, and the detection thresholds of different laboratories vary greatly, and the standardization scheme has not yet been unified; third, the interaction between DKK3 and other Wnt pathway inhibitors (such as DKK1) is still unclear, which may affect its reliability as a single marker [56].

In summary, DKK3 is involved in renal fibrosis by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin-MFF axis, and has the dual role of ‘driver’ and ‘marker’. Future studies need to verify its diagnostic value in CKD of different etiologies through multi-center studies, and develop DKK3-targeted intervention strategies (such as the METTL3 inhibitor STM2457), so as to provide new targets for the precise prevention and treatment of renal fibrosis.

Liver-type fatty acid-binding protein (L-FABP)

L-FABP is an intracellular lipid-binding protein primarily expressed by proximal renal tubular epithelial cells. It participates in the uptake, transport, and metabolism of fatty acids, while also exerting a stress-protective role during oxidative stress and cellular damage [61,62]. Under normal physiological conditions, the urinary content of L-FABP is extremely low (the urinary L-FABP/creatinine ratio in healthy individuals is usually <20 ng/mg). Its expression is regulated by the concentration of fatty acids in renal tubules and the level of oxidative stress, with the main function of reducing lipid toxicity-induced damage to renal tubular cells [61].

In pathological states, the urinary level of L-FABP increases significantly, making it a sensitive marker for reflecting renal tubular injury and renal fibrosis. In patients with IgA nephropathy, the urinary L-FABP level is positively correlated with the area of renal interstitial fibrosis (r = 0.35) and increases with the severity of tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis in the Banff score: the median urinary L-FABP is 49.9 ng/mg in patients with mild fibrosis (T1) and can reach 82.5 ng/mg in those with severe fibrosis (T2) [61]. The elevation of L-FABP is directly related to renal tubular cell injury. When proximal renal tubules are damaged due to ischemia, toxins, or inflammation (such as oxidative stress in renal fibrosis), cell permeability increases, leading to the release of L-FABP into urine [62]. Meanwhile, L-FABP can reduce cellular damage caused by lipid peroxidation products (such as 4-hydroxynonenal) by binding to free fatty acids. However, persistent pathological stimulation will deplete its protective capacity, resulting in aggravated injury and forming a ‘damage-release’ vicious cycle [61,62].

L-FABP holds significant value in the assessment of renal fibrosis and AKI. In the multi-marker model for IgA nephropathy, the combined use of urinary L-FABP with DKK-3, EGF, etc., significantly improves the predictive efficacy for the area of renal interstitial fibrosis (the explained variance of the model reaches 60%), with a standardized coefficient of 0.13, suggesting that it can serve as an independent predictor [61]. In AKI, urinary L-FABP reflects changes in renal tubular function earlier than serum creatinine. For example, in patients with malaria-related AKI, its level decreases prior to the improvement of eGFR and can predict the rate of renal function recovery [62]. Additionally, in other renal injuries such as diabetic nephropathy and obstructive nephropathy, urinary L-FABP has also been confirmed to be associated with the degree of tubulointerstitial fibrosis, with AUC values ranging from 0.75 to 0.82 [61].

In summary, as a biomarker reflecting proximal renal tubular injury and oxidative stress, L-FABP has clear value in the staging of renal fibrosis (especially in IgA nephropathy) and the prognostic assessment of AKI. Future studies need to verify its diagnostic thresholds in CKD of different etiologies through multi-center research and explore its combined application with imaging modalities (such as MRI diffusion-weighted imaging) to enhance specificity and clinical utility.

Blood and urine markers

Collagen degradation products (CDPs)

CDPs refers to the specific amino acid sequence fragment produced through the breakdown of collagen by enzymes such as MMPs during ECM remodeling [63]. These fragments in blood or tissue fluid would benefit the monitoring of matrix metabolism in various pathological states, such as renal fibrosis, joint diseases, cardiovascular diseases, tumor metastasis, etc. [64]. Specifically, Collagen Type I Metabolite (C1M), Collagen Type III Metabolite (C3M), etc. are specific degradation products for different types of collagens.

C1M is a degradation product of type I collagen. Elevated C1M levels have established relationship with osteoporosis, poor fracture healing, and certain types of cancer [65,66]. C3M represents the degradation product of type III collagen. An increase in C3M may indicate atherosclerosis or other diseases involving abnormal metabolism of type III collagen [67]. uC3M is a product of type III collagen degradation that was regarded as a predictive marker for early noninvasiveness since its level in urine was measurable before histologically detecting renal fibrosis [12]. Furthermore, C1M and C3M, both detectable in urine, are more likely to indicate the advanced condition of renal fibrosis. It suggests that urinary collagen degradation markers, including types I and III, may be early diagnostic marker for renal fibrosis progression due to different causes. However, the specific predictive value of these markers is till poorly understood that deserves further research. In another study, U-C3M levels increased significantly after reperfusion in the bilateral ischemia-reperfusion injury group, which was associated with histological changes and more fibrosis, and remained elevated after the decrease of creatinine levels in plasma [68]. In addition, in terms of the advantage in renal fibrosis, CDPs can directly reflect changes in collagen metabolism and hence provide specific information about fibrotic activity. CDPs can be quantified easily with reliable results produced by ELISA, chemiluminescence and other laboratory techniques. However, CDP generation and clearance is a dynamic process, imposing a challenge to the interpretation of the results owing to potential variations in the results of sampling at different time points. In addition, CDP levels are not specific to renal fibrosis, which may be disrupted by other diseases involving disorders of collagen metabolism, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, etc.

Although C1M has been involved in some studies, the relevant literature on its application in the field of human renal fibrosis is relatively limited. In contrast, urinary C3M has been more commonly seen in renal fibrosis research literature in recent years, showing promising research prospects and potential for clinical application. Multiple studies have indicated that urinary C3M is closely related to the progression of renal fibrosis. Professor Federica Genovese from the LEO Bioscience Institute in Denmark and other researchers conducted a 3-year study on 145 patients and volunteers. The results showed that the urinary Pro-C3/C3M ratio increased significantly in advanced kidney disease, suggesting that it can serve as a new noninvasive diagnostic and prognostic tool [69]. In another study on patients with diabetic nephropathy, it was observed that the level of urinary C3M in the dulaglutide treatment group increased, which indicated a reduction in renal fibrosis, further suggesting that C3M can be a potential indicator for evaluating the therapeutic effect of renal fibrosis [70]. In addition, some studies have mentioned that urinary C3M gradually decreases with the progression of CKD stages, is associated with the degree of renal fibrosis, and can reflect early pathological changes [71,72].

Based on the above studies, urinary C3M has shown significant value in the early diagnosis, condition monitoring, and evaluation of therapeutic effects of renal fibrosis. Compared with C1M mentioned in the original text, the evidence for urinary C3M in human renal fibrosis research is more abundant and solid. In the future, with the continuous deepening of relevant research, urinary C3M is expected to play a greater role in the clinical diagnosis and treatment of renal fibrosis, providing strong support for precision medicine in patients with renal fibrosis.

Epidermal growth factor (EGF)

EGF is a small molecule polypeptide that can promote cell proliferation, migration and differentiation [73]. It should be acknowledged with regarded to the complicated and double-edged roles of EGF and its receptor EGFR in the progression of renal fibrosis. Specifically, in the early stages of AKI or CKD, EGF may provide protection by stimulating renal tubular epithelial cell regeneration. In this way, it may prevent against tubular atrophy and function to maintain the functional integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier [74]. But over-activated EGFR signaling may also lead to fibroblast activation and over-deposition of ECM in CKD, due to persistent inflammation or injury, eventually accelerating the development of renal fibrosis [13]. In addition, EGF can also induce the transformation of myofibroblasts, a critical step in fibrosis. However, EGF is widely distributed in the human body and may also participate in the development of diseases involving other organs and tissues, resulting in weakened specificity as a biomarker of renal fibrosis, in addition to being affected by individual differences. To utilize EGF stand-alone biomarker of renal fibrosis, there are many challenges to address, despite its potential applications recognized in the field of nephrology.

Pro-collagen type VI (PRO-C6)

PRO-C6 is a specific peptide fragment derived from the C-terminal C5 domain of the α3 chain of type VI collagen, which is released during the synthesis and deposition of type VI collagen in the extracellular matrix [52,75,76]. As a biomarker reflecting the active synthesis of type VI collagen, PRO-C6 not only serves as a surrogate indicator for extracellular matrix remodeling but also exerts biological effects by promoting inflammatory responses, fibroblast activation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby exacerbating renal fibrosis [52,77]. Under normal circumstances, type VI collagen is expressed at low levels in the interstitium, blood vessels, and glomeruli of healthy kidneys, maintaining the structural integrity of the extracellular matrix. In pathological conditions (such as CKD, IgA nephropathy, kidney transplantation, etc.), activated fibroblasts significantly upregulate the synthesis of type VI collagen, leading to its excessive deposition in the renal interstitium. During this process, the C-terminal fragment of the α3 chain is cleaved and released into the circulation or urine, making it a measurable indicator of active fibrosis [52,75]. Functionally, PRO-C6 amplifies the profibrotic cascade by recruiting macrophages, upregulating TGF-β signaling, and inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition in renal tubular epithelial cells [52,77]. This dual role as both a biomarker and a driver of fibrosis highlights its potential clinical value in the assessment of renal fibrosis. PRO-C6 is associated with histological fibrosis. For example, in patients with IgA nephropathy and ANCA-associated vasculitis, serum and urinary PRO-C6 levels are positively correlated with the degree of interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy as evaluated by the Banff score. Specifically, serum PRO-C6 (AUC = 0.799) and urinary PRO-C6/creatinine ratio (AUC = 0.759) can effectively distinguish severe fibrosis from mild to moderate fibrosis, with better performance than traditional markers such as DKK3. This association was confirmed in the IgA nephropathy validation cohort, where serum PRO-C6 could independently predict the severity of fibrosis even after adjusting for serum creatinine [52]. In the AWARD-7 trial, dulaglutide treatment resulted in a 0.2% decrease in serum PRO-C6 levels after 52 weeks, while the insulin glargine group showed an 8.0% increase [78]. This decrease was associated with a slower decline in eGFR, suggesting that PRO-C6 can be used to monitor the efficacy of antifibrotic interventions. In addition, PRO-C6 has significant value in assessing the prognosis of CKD. In CKD patients, elevated serum PRO-C6 is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and progression to ESRD. For example, in patients with type 2 diabetes and microalbuminuria, a higher baseline serum PRO-C6 predicts a 1.5-fold increased risk of cardiovascular events and death within 5 years [79]. In kidney transplant recipients, plasma PRO-C6 measured 6 months after transplantation was prospectively associated with the interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy score at 24 months, and each doubling of PRO-C6 levels was associated with a 1.87-fold increased risk of graft failure [80].

In summary, PRO-C6 directly reflects the dynamic synthesis of type VI collagen and is sensitive to early fibrotic changes before the appearance of histological alterations [79]. Both serum and urinary PRO-C6 show consistent associations with fibrosis, allowing flexible selection of sampling methods according to clinical needs [75]. Moreover, the predictive value of PRO-C6 for outcomes such as graft failure and death remains significant after adjusting for eGFR, proteinuria, and inflammatory markers [52]. However, PRO-C6 may be elevated in non-renal fibrotic diseases (such as liver or pulmonary fibrosis), and clinical judgment is required to confirm its renal origin [77]. In conclusion, PRO-C6, as a promising noninvasive biomarker for renal fibrosis, has sufficient evidence supporting its role in diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and treatment monitoring. Future studies should focus on large-scale validation in different etiologies of CKD and standardization of detection methods to facilitate clinical translation.

β2-microglobulin (β2-MG)

β2-MG is a low-molecular-weight (11 kDa) globulin synthesized by all nucleated cells expressing MHC class I antigens, and it is mainly involved in the process of antigen presentation in immune responses [81]. Under normal physiological conditions, β2-MG is freely filtered by the glomeruli, and more than 99% of it is reabsorbed and metabolized by the proximal renal tubules, so its content in urine is extremely low (the urinary β2-MG/creatinine ratio in healthy individuals is usually <300 μg/g). Its plasma level is mainly regulated by glomerular filtration function, while its urinary level reflects the reabsorptive function of the proximal renal tubules [81]. In pathological states, the urinary β2-MG level increases significantly due to tubular injury or dysfunction, making it a sensitive indicator for reflecting renal fibrosis and tubular damage. In patients with CKD, urinary β2-MG is positively correlated with the degree of renal cortical and medullary fibrosis (r = 0.22 with medullary fibrosis, r = 0.43 with cortical fibrosis), and the urinary β2-MG level in the severe fibrosis group (medullary fibrosis ≥45% and cortical fibrosis ≥25%) (966.0 μg/gCr) is significantly higher than that in the mild fibrosis group (197.0 μg/gCr) [81]. In glomerular diseases such as IgA nephropathy, the elevation of urinary β2-MG is also closely related to the decline of eGFR (r = −0.57), suggesting that it can serve as an early warning indicator for the deterioration of renal function [81].

Galectin-3

Galectin-3 is a lectin containing a β-galactoside-binding domain, belonging to the chimeric galectin family. Its structure includes an N-terminal oligomerization domain and a C-terminal carbohydrate recognition domain. It can bind to cell surface glycoproteins and extracellular matrix components through oligomerization, participating in pathophysiological processes such as cell adhesion, inflammatory regulation, and fibrosis [82,83]. Under normal physiological conditions, Galectin-3 is expressed at low levels in parts of the kidney such as the collecting ducts and proximal tubules, participating in renal tubular differentiation and kidney development, and maintaining the stability of renal structure [82].

In pathological states, the expression of Galectin-3 is significantly upregulated, becoming a key driver of renal fibrosis and various kidney diseases. In patients with IgA nephropathy, the level of Galectin-3 in renal tissue is positively correlated with the mesangial hyperplasia (M) and tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (T) scores in the Oxford classification, and the expression levels in glomeruli and renal interstitium increase with the severity of the disease (the immunohistochemical score of Galectin-3 in patients with severe fibrosis is 2.3 times higher than that in patients with mild fibrosis) [84]. In CKD, the serum level of Galectin-3 increases with the decrease of eGFR, and the level in ESRD patients is 2.7 times higher than that in healthy people [82]. In addition, in hemolysis-induced renal injury, Galectin-3 exacerbates oxidative stress and necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells in response to stimulation by free heme and iron ions, and its expression level is positively correlated with the degree of renal injury (levels of serum creatinine and urea nitrogen) [83].

Noninvasive imaging

Imaging is crucial in diagnosing kidney diseases, particularly in CKD and related complications [4,85]. In the clinical setting, common options generally involve traditional imaging modalities, such as ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, structural abnormalities typically become apparent only after the presence of significant pathological changes, leading to their constrained sensitivities in detecting early-stage diseases. With advances in imaging, emerging techniques such as molecular imaging and functional MRI offer additional diagnostic capabilities with higher resolution and stronger precision. The imaging scope has expanded from traditional anatomical assessments to functional and molecular-level evaluations. Significantly, these advancements enable early detection of microscopic molecular alterations, macroscopic physiological changes, and integrating multimodal data, offering novel approaches for early diagnosis and dynamic monitoring of renal fibrosis [86,87].

Traditional imaging modalities

Ultrasound

Ultrasound generates renal images based on the reflection of high-frequency sound waves, which has be used as a common imaging technique for kidney evaluation [88]. However, ultrasound has a relatively low spatial resolution, which may be impossible to detect early or subtle fibrotic changes in the kidney. Additionally, the image quality of ultrasound is highly operator-dependent, suggesting potential variability in diagnostic outcomes [4]. The advent of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) mitigates these limitations through intravascular microbubble agents, enhancing microvascular visualization with superior spatial resolution (≤100 μm) and enabling quantitative perfusion analysis via time-intensity curves. Crucially, CEUS’s pulmonary-eliminated microbubbles impose no nephrotoxic risk, establishing it as the preferred modality for dynamic perfusion assessment in renal impairment populations where iodinated or gadolinium agents are contraindicated [89–91]. Still and all, CEUS remains limited by operator-dependent variability in quantitative analysis [92].

Computed tomography (CT)

Also serving as a widely used imaging modality, CT employs X-rays to generate high-resolution cross-sectional images of kidneys, which is highly useful for assessing anatomical changes. The primary advantage of CT lies in its high spatial resolution, allowing detailed visualization of renal structures. However, it still has deficiencies in detecting early renal fibrosis and evaluating soft tissue pathology. Furthermore, contrast-enhanced CT poses potential risks to patients with impaired renal function, which contraindicates in individuals with contrast agent allergies. In addition, repeated use of contrast agents may also increase the risk of ESRD [93].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

MRI utilizes strong magnetic fields and radio waves to produce high-resolution images of the kidney, offering excellent soft tissue contrast and detailed anatomical assessments, and hence holding promise for early renal fibrosis detection. However, MRI still face a challenge of limited routine clinical application owing to its high costs, prolonged scan times, and equipment availability.

Collectively, traditional imaging techniques provide valuable structural and functional information about the kidney. Nevertheless, it should be noted with respect to their limited abilities to detect early-stage renal fibrosis and perform quantitative assessments, particularly in identifying subtle tissue alterations. Currently, novel imaging approaches emerge as imaging technology continues to evolve. Considering their potential to overcome existing limitations, emerging imaging modalities may offer more sensitive and precise tools for the assessment of renal fibrosis.

Functional ultrasound techniques

Ultrasound elastography measures shear wave propagation speed or tissue displacement to evaluate tissue elasticity, possessing real-time, noninvasive, cost-effective properties. It has been widely applied in diagnosing and monitoring liver disease, kidney disease, and tumors [94,95].

Among ultrasound-based techniques, shear wave elastography (SWE) adopts Young’s modulus measurements to assess tissue stiffness, demonstrating notable quantitative capabilities. However, it showed varied diagnostic performance across studies due to variations in study design and patient populations. SWE-derived elasticity values have been confirmed to correlate with interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. For example, Chen et al. reported 3.5-fold increase in the risk of moderate-to-severe fibrosis for every 10 kPa decrease in cortical elasticity (Odds Ratio = 4.54) [96]. Similarly, Leong et al. found a correlation coefficient of ρ = 0.44 between SWE and tubulointerstitial fibrosis scores [97]. However, Wu et al. reported a conflicting finding, without the observation of significant association between SWE-derived stiffness and CKD staging or Masson’s trichrome-stained fibrosis area (p > 0.05) [98]. Therefore, the accuracy of SWE may be compromised by several confounding factors such as microvascular density and operator experience [95]. Spatial heterogeneity of fibrosis is speculated to be one possible explanation for these inconsistencies. Using time-harmonic elastography, Grossmann et al. found significantly lower cortical shear wave speed in patients with early-stage glomerulonephritis than healthy individuals (1.88 vs. 2.08 m/s), whereas medullary stiffness differences were less pronounced. This result highlights the critical role of measurement site selection in detecting early pathological changes [99].

In order to improve diagnostic performance, Ge et al. integrated multimodal radiomics combining 2D ultrasound texture features with elasticity parameters, achieving an AUC of 0.93 in differentiating mild-to-moderate from severe interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy [100]. Similarly, Yoon et al. combined SWE with CEUS to assess elasticity and perfusion changes in a rabbit model of obstructive nephropathy [101]. Furthermore, with a viscoelastic imaging introduced, Yuan et al. documented stronger correlation of viscosity coefficient (Vmean) with interstitial inflammation (r = 0.81) than traditional elasticity parameters, with a combined model (Vmean + Dmean + Emean) achieving an AUC of 0.91 [102].

Functional MRI techniques

Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE)

MRE acts as another imaging modality that quantifies tissue stiffness (elasticity) noninvasively by generating shear waves via external mechanical vibration and analyzing wave propagation using phase-contrast MRI techniques. MRE has a full-kidney coverage and exhibits high spatial resolution, enabling the detection of medullary fibrosis signals, which are difficult to capture using conventional imaging modalities [103,104].

In pigs with acute renal artery stenosis (RAS) induced by vascular occlusion, cortical stiffness would be decreased significantly by reduced renal blood flow (RBF). In contrast, in chronic RAS, there was no significant cortical stiffness reduction due to compensatory blood flow mechanisms [105]. Hence, relying solely on cortical stiffness may underestimate the severity of fibrosis. In comparison, medullary stiffness is less dependent on perfusion and remains significantly elevated in chronic RAS and atherosclerotic RAS, correlating directly with histological fibrosis scores [104]. In DN, MRE-derived cortical stiffness reduced with worsening renal function, which might be attributed to the loss of tissue elasticity resulting from glomerulosclerosis and reduced renal perfusion. To address this, a ‘surrogate filtration fraction’ model was modified with the incorporation of arterial spin labeling (ASL) and eGFR, generating indirect evidence for determining fibrosis progression [106]. Further histological validation revealed a negative correlation of MRE-derived stiffness with ECM volume; and it, in conjunction with eGFR, allowed for quantitative prediction of fibrosis severity [107]. Additionally, in glomerulonephritis and amyloidosis, MRE stiffness also exhibited negative correlations with both glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis.

Indeed, MRE has significant advantages in three-dimensional, noninvasive medullary fibrosis assessment. It is not widely used clinically due to its high sensitivity to perfusion-related cortical stiffness variations. One solution to the diagnostic accuracy may be the combination of MRE with ASL or diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) [108,109].

Viscoelastic imaging can quantify both elastic and viscous properties, beyond conventional tissue stiffness measurements. It may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of tissue mechanical characteristics. This imaging modality can indicate fibrosis and inflammation progression in CKD more accurately. Recently, Yuan et al. demonstrated remarkable alterations of Emean, Vmean, and Dmean in patients with CKD, with positive correlation of Vmean and Dmean found with fibrosis and inflammation severity. Similarly, with the use of tomoelastography in a CKD rat model, Krehl et al. also established the relationship of SWS and wave penetration rate with histological fibrosis markers, further supporting viscoelastic imaging as a promising tool for early renal disease detection [102,110].

Besides, a major ongoing challenge lies in the standardization of elastography techniques. As for the reason, variations in measurement protocols may produce negative impact on diagnostic threshold applications across different anatomical locations (e.g., cortex vs. medulla) and clinical contexts (e.g., native kidney vs. transplanted kidney) [111–115]. These discrepancies likely arise from differences in technical parameters (e.g., frequency, shear wave propagation pathways), tissue properties (e.g., perfusion, fibrosis degree), and reference standards used in studies [95,103,108,109]. In order to enhance diagnostic consistency and accuracy, this technical heterogeneity underscores the need for developing adaptable reference standards tailored to specific clinical scenarios.

Diffusion imaging

DWI techniques offer a noninvasive approach to assess renal fibrosis by evaluating changes in water molecule mobility, which becomes restricted due to the deposition of ECM and tubular atrophy characteristic of fibrotic kidneys. Standard DWI quantifies this restricted water motion primarily through the Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC). Multiple studies indicate that ADC values are typically lower in patients with CKD and tend to decrease as renal function deteriorates [103,108,116,117]. This makes ADC a potential marker for tracking fibrosis severity, though its measurements can be influenced by perfusion changes independent of fibrotic structural alterations. Recently, molecular mechanisms and spatial localization of fibrosis-related diffusion changes have undergone great advancements technologically. For instance, ultra-high b-value DWI in a RAS model demonstrated a strong negative correlation between ultra-high diffusion ADC (ADCuh) and interstitial fibrosis (ρ = −0.782). Moreover, ADCuh was directly correlated with aquaporin (AQP1/AQP2) expression, revealing fibrosis-induced restrictions in transmembrane water movement [118].

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI), a more advanced DWI modality, provides insights into tissue microstructure by measuring the directionality and anisotropy of water diffusion. The Fractional Anisotropy (FA), a key DTI metric, has been consistently reported to decline with worsening renal function in both animal models and human studies [119–121], reflecting disorganization of the renal parenchyma’s normal architecture due to fibrotic remodeling. Moreover, in an oxalate-induced mouse model, DTI combined with whole-kidney voxel-based analysis enabled precise localization of medullary fibrosis. FA values exhibited moderate-to-strong correlations with collagen III deposition, serum hydroxyproline levels, and fibrosis-related gene expression, allowing for a multidimensional validation of pathological, imaging, and molecular markers [122].

Diffusion Kurtosis Imaging (DKI) represents a further extension of DWI, moving beyond the assumption of Gaussian water diffusion. DKI quantifies the degree of non-Gaussianity, which reflects tissue heterogeneity and structural complexity from fibrotic processes and can be captured by kurtosis metrics. Notably, DKI has shown promise in evaluating renal fibrosis severity and predicting clinical outcomes in patients [123–126]. Furthermore, DKI outperformed traditional ADC measurements profoundly in the aspect of diagnosis. In patients with CKD, cortical mean kurtosis values could be referenced to distinguish early-stage (CKD 1–2) from advanced-stage (CKD 3–5) disease, with an AUC of 0.93 [127].

Recent preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the value of Intravoxel Incoherent Motion (IVIM) in characterizing renal fibrosis [127–130]. IVIM imaging is designed to differentiate between true molecular diffusion in the extravascular space and the pseudo-diffusion effects arising from microvascular blood flow (perfusion) within a voxel. By modeling the DWI signal decay across multiple b-values, IVIM can estimate parameters such as the true diffusion coefficient (D), the perfusion fraction (f), and the pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*). This allows for a more specific assessment of alterations in both tissue diffusivity and microcirculation, potentially offering a clearer view of capillary loss (a key pathological feature of renal fibrosis) than ADC alone. For example, in a cisplatin-induced rat fibrosis model, standard ADC values initially declined before transiently increasing; conversely, a continuous decrease in f value was observed in a bi-exponential IVIM model [131].

Despite the promising findings, the application of these diffusion MRI techniques for routine clinical assessment of renal fibrosis faces several challenges. These include limitations in specificity, as diffusion metrics can be affected by other pathological processes like inflammation or edema, and sensitivity, particularly in detecting early-stage fibrosis. Furthermore, standardization of acquisition protocols, post-processing methods, and establishing robust correlations with histological fibrosis grades are necessary to enhance their practical utility and facilitate broader clinical adoption.

Oxygenation and perfusion assessment

Renal fibrosis is typically accompanied by tissue hypoxia and impaired blood flow perfusion. Through the measurement of magnetic susceptibility differences between deoxygenated and oxygenated hemoglobin, blood oxygen level-dependent MRI (BOLD-MRI) offers an indirect approach for renal oxygenation assessment. Reduced tissue oxygenation can be determined when there is a rise in deoxygenated hemoglobin suggested by a decrease in T2* values (effective transverse relaxation time, reflecting signal decay from magnetic field inhomogeneities) or an increase in R2* (1/T2*, effective transverse relaxation rate) generally [132–134]. Woo et al. observed decreased T2* values in the early fibrosis stages, which correlated negatively with fibrosis severity [135]. However, in a UUO model, Zha et al. used a longitudinal histogram analysis and found a progressive decline in R2* values during fibrosis progression, which correlated with the extent of fibrosis. Therefore, considering confounding factors such as altered microvascular density and perfusion changes, BOLD-MRI may exhibit relatively poor performance in long-term fibrosis assessment [136].

Furthermore, ASL and IVIM imaging quantify RBF and perfusion f-value, offering insights into early microcirculatory dysfunction in fibrosis. In a reversible UUO (rUUO) model, ASL-derived RBF as well as IVIM-derived ADC and f-values decreased during obstruction but partially recovered after relief, suggesting fibrosis progression resulting from early perfusion deficits possibly [128]. Additionally, Mao [137] and Liang [127] independently demonstrated strong negative correlations of RBF and f-values with interstitial fibrosis in patients with CKD, reinforcing the role of perfusion abnormalities in fibrosis progression. In addition, in renal transplant recipients, ASL exhibited superior sensitivity over IVIM in differentiating no fibrosis (ci0) from mild fibrosis (ci1), and demonstrated predictive value for long-term allograft dysfunction [138].

Metabolic imaging

Metabolic reprogramming is also common during renal fibrosis, underlining the value of metabolic imaging for evaluating pathological changes in the kidney [139].

Iron metabolism dysregulation

Emerging evidence positions iron dysregulation as a pivotal metabolic driver of renal fibrosis, mediated through ferroptosis and senescence-associated pathways [140].

Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), based on phase-sensitive gradient-echo sequences, effectively detects paramagnetic substances like hemosiderin. In a rabbit UUO model, 2D-SWI demonstrate dynamic signal ratio changes (renal/muscle) that correlate strongly with fibrosis progression (R2=0.91 cortex, 0.81 medulla) [141]. However, SWI remains qualitative and suffers from 2D acquisition artifacts while being unable to differentiate iron forms.

Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) complements SWI by leveraging magnetic susceptibility differences to quantify tissue iron content. In a rabbit renal fibrosis model, QSM revealed biphasic cortical iron dynamics from initial decrease (reflecting ferroptotic iron release) to subsequent increase (indicating lysosomal sequestration in fibrotic niches) [142]. While as a Level 2 animal study (Technical Efficacy Stage 3), clinical translation requires validation in humans, with limitations including motion artifacts and the need for standardized postprocessing protocols.

Macromolecular remodeling

During fibrosis, the accumulation of macromolecules (e.g., collagen) may alter the interaction between macromolecules and water, thus displaying changes in magnetization transfer. Significantly, magnetization transfer imaging (MTI) can detect these interactions and, indirectly, ECM metabolic abnormalities. In a murine model of surgical RAS via vascular clipping, Jiang et al. (2017) first demonstrated a strong correlation of magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) with tissue collagen deposition, which can be used for longitudinal tracking of fibrosis progression. Therefore, macromolecular accumulation may be a core feature of fibrosis [143]. On this basis, in a two-pool model, the same group applied quantitative magnetization transfer imaging to quantify the bound pool fraction (f-value). In a porcine RAS model, f-values were observed to be directly correlated with fibrosis severity in the renal cortex and outer medulla, accompanied by a negative correlation with renal perfusion, reinforcing the link between ECM metabolism and fibrosis [144].

Molecular metabolism shifts

To characterize fibrosis-related metabolite-specific changes, chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) imaging emerges and has been applied to detect the chemical exchange signals of free metabolites (e.g., glucose, creatine). It can facilitate the assessment of struc­tural and metabolic alterations comprehensively when combined with MTI. For instance, in a rat UUO model, asymmetric MTR derived from CEST correlated negatively with [18F]-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, indicating fibrosis-associated suppression of glucose metabolism. MTR also exhibited a strong negative correlation with α-SMA expression, suggesting an association between myofibroblast activation and ECM remodeling, further highlighting the role of metabolic reprogramming in structural remodeling [145].

Amide proton transfer-weighted imaging (APT) is a direct approach to determine abnormal protein metabolism in fibrosis by detecting chemical exchange between amide protons in proteins and water. For example, in an ischemia-reperfusion injury model, Zhao et al. reported significant increase of APT values in early-stage fibrosis, outperforming conventional DWI in sensitivity. Furthermore, the diagnostic performance could be further enhanced obviously when combining APT with ADC measurements, indicating the potential of APT for early, noninvasive fibrosis assessment [146].

Additionally, fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) provides optical-level metabolic insights by detecting fluorescence lifetime changes of coenzymes such as NADH and FAD. In DN patients, Ranjit et al. found increasing trend of phase lifetime (τ(phase)) with fibrosis progression, indicating oxidative-redox imbalance and cellular energy depletion, two major hallmarks of fibrotic tissue microenvironment [147].

Molecular imaging

Molecular imaging in nephrology primarily focuses on the noninvasive assessment of renal physiological and pathological processes, particularly in detecting and monitoring fibrosis in CKD. Different from conventional anatomical imaging, molecular imaging can visualize pending on specific target molecules, which can be categorized into targeted and label-free (non-targeted) types. By integrating imaging techniques, molecular imaging enables direct visualization of fibrosis-associated biomolecules or pathological markers at the microscopic level. It can generate high-resolution and functional information, beyond the capabilities of traditional imaging modalities.

Targeted molecular imaging

Targeted imaging techniques allow precise visualization and detection by employing specific molecular probes that bind to distinct targets within cells or tissues. Rather than a uniform distribution across the renal parenchyma, the fibrotic niche in renal fibrosis refers to a localized microenvironment where fibrotic lesions develop. This specialized microenvironment generally comprises resident and infiltrating inflammatory cells, ECM networks, exosomes, soluble factors, and metabolites, all playing crucial roles in modulating fibrosis progression [14,15]. Notably, targeted molecular imaging can visualize these fibrotic niches and their key components [e.g., fibrosis-associated molecules like fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and collagen] in a real-time and noninvasive manner, thereby aiding in the early identification of fibrosis.

FAP is a transmembrane glycoprotein predominantly expressed in fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and stromal cells of solid tumors. In addition to promoting ECM degradation and remodeling, FAP plays a crucial role in myofibroblast activation, migration, and proliferation in the process of fibrosis [148]. FAP has been regarded as a potential early diagnostic marker. In response to this, FAP-targeted imaging has gained increasing research interest for the early detection and dynamic monitoring of renal fibrosis. FAP inhibitors (FAPI) have been applied in various animal models and clinical studies, often conjugated with radionuclides such as Ga-68 or F-18 for PET imaging [149–151]. In a hypertensive mouse model, Byun et al. observed a progressive increase in FAP expression as fibrosis advanced using [68Ga]FAPI-46 PET imaging, demonstrating its ability to detect renal and cardiac fibrosis at early stages [152]. [18F]FAPI-42 and [18F]FAPT (FAP-targeted PET tracer) in combination with PET/CT imaging was also employed and suggested sustained renal uptake in an AKI mouse model, correlating with fibrosis progression [16]. Similar imaging patterns were observed in patients with AKI-to-CKD transition. Eventually, FAP upregulation has been documented to be a marker of maladaptive renal repair by this noninvasive imaging approach, highlighting its potential in predicting fibrosis within the clinical intervention window. Additionally, Zeng et al. developed an FAP-sensitive polymeric nanoprobe-based fluorescence imaging modality, enabling efficient detection of FAP expression for early diagnosis of renal fibrosis in a UUO mouse model [153].

During the development of renal fibrosis, there may be significant alterations in ECM components. Elastin, which is minimally expressed in healthy kidneys, exhibits marked upregulation in fibrotic kidneys, particularly around renal tubules and within the cortex and medulla. In a prior research, noninvasive staging of renal fibrosis and treatment monitoring were achieved using an elastin-specific MRI agent (ESMA) [154]. Similarly, pathological collagen deposition is a hallmark of renal fibrosis. The CNA35-Cy7 collagen-binding probe, in combination with CT-fluorescence molecular tomography, has been used to quantify collagen accumulation in both murine and human samples, demonstrating high probe accumulation in fibrotic regions [155]. Additionally, MRI techniques utilizing Gd-OA-based probes have shown high sensitivity in detecting collagen crosslinking changes, further enhancing the noninvasive evaluation of fibrosis [156].

Label-free molecular imaging

Indeed, targeted molecular imaging techniques exhibit high specificity and sensitivity for disease diagnosis, but they possess an important flaw of depending on the expression changes of known molecular markers or receptors [14]. In contrast, label-free imaging techniques offer imaging information based on the physical or chemical properties of biomolecules, independent of specific target molecules, offering a broader range of molecular data and the potential for multimodal analysis [157].

Endogenous molecules imaging

Among label-free molecular imaging techniques, imaging endogenous molecules has opened up new avenues for studying renal fibrosis. For instance, Hysi et al. employed photoacoustic imaging (PAI) to directly image collagen, enabling noninvasive fibrosis quantification with high accuracy and operational simplicity. This technique has highlighted potential for clinical translation as itis also feasible in simulated renal transplantation environments [158]. Similarly, techniques such as matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization imaging mass spectrometry, infrared spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy also leverage the intrinsic properties of biomolecules for high-resolution imaging. These methods enable precise mapping of macromolecular changes (e.g., collagen distribution), which are informative in fibrosis research [157]. In another study, a multimodal approach was adopted, which integrating FLIM, which is highly sensitive to molecular microenvironments, with second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging, which exhibits high specificity for collagen fibers. It allowed for efficient and noninvasive quantitative analysis of fibrosis in a mouse UUO model, further advancing the potential of label-free imaging in renal fibrosis assessment [159].

Metabolic molecular imaging

Metabolic molecular imaging offers another useful solution for detecting early disease changes, as it can capture physiological metabolic abnormalities at both tissue and cellular levels. TGF-β signaling can enhance glycolytic flux by activating glycolytic enzymes, promoting lactate production, and exacerbating fibrosis progression [160]. Alanine is another key metabolic product that indicates fibrosis-related metabolic shifts, which is essential in the progression of renal fibrosis. In diabetic and ischemic renal fibrosis mouse models, hyperpolarized [1]³C-pyruvate MRI revealed a reduced conversion of [1]³C-pyruvate to [1]³C-alanine, accompanied by an increased flux toward lactate, both of which correlated with fibrosis biomarkers [161]. This metabolic shift was further validated in a porcine renal fibrosis model. and subsequently observed in human renal fibrosis tissues [162]. Eventually, the aforementioned evidence reinforced the potential of alanine-based metabolic imaging as an early diagnostic tool for renal fibrosis clinically.

Aggregation-induced emission (AIE) probes for renal imaging

The clinical use of traditional imaging probes for renal imaging is often limited due to their drawbacks, including poor clearance efficiency, nonspecific accumulation, and potential toxicity. Unlike conventional fluorophores that suffer from aggregation-caused quenchin, AIE probes exhibit enhanced fluorescence upon aggregation due to restricted intramolecular motion, which promotes radiative decay [163]. This unique mechanism allows for high-contrast imaging with minimal background interference, making them particularly suitable for sensitive renal imaging. With advantages including small size, efficient renal clearance, excellent biocompatibility, and high specificity, AIE probes are particularly suitable for precise kidney disease monitoring.

For instance, Yan et al. developed an AIE-4PEG550 probe, which, due to its nanoscale particle size and superior renal clearance property, combined short-wave infrared fluorescence imaging with PAI to track renal fibrosis progression accurately. Similarly, with the design of BSA-Ag2S quantum dot probes with AIE properties, Zhou et al. integrated AIE with targeted molecular imaging. These probes enabled targeted imaging of the kidney and myofibroblasts owing to their modification with chitosan and hyaluronic acid. Consequently, their investigation provided a marker-independent, highly tissue-specific, and sensitive imaging approach with clear signal output. This novel imaging strategy established a safer, more efficient, and simplified method for early renal fibrosis diagnosis [164]. Beyond fibrosis, AIE probes have demonstrated outstanding performance in detecting AKI and other renal disorders. Based on their sensitivity to metabolic alterations, AIE probes has been considered a promising tool for early identification of renal dysfunction [165,166], expanding the landscape of noninvasive diagnostic techniques for kidney diseases.

Key challenges in clinical translation of molecular imaging

Despite encouraging preclinical and early clinical results of molecular imaging in renal fibrosis detection and monitoring, overcoming systemic barriers from development to implementation is essential for clinical translation.

The foremost challenge lies in comprehensive human safety validation, which extends well beyond standard biocompatibility assessments. Taking FAPI-PET as an example, radiotracers such as 68Ga-FAPI-04 have demonstrated clinically observed renal retention [149,151]. This necessitates systematic characterization of their long-term biodistribution patterns in renal insufficiency patients through dynamic SPECT/CT imaging, with quantitative analysis of retention rates across eGFR-stratified populations. Meanwhile, metal-based MRI contrast agents require strict evaluation in renal-impaired populations to avoid unforeseen toxicity [154,156]. Furthermore, nanoprobe systems including polymeric probes and AIE fluorophores necessitate in-depth investigation into their clearance kinetics, long-term accumulation risks, and immunogenicity, particularly given their novel chemical structures that may interact uniquely with diseased tissues.

Scalable production and cost control present another major hurdle. Short-lived isotopes like Gallium-68 require specialized on-site manufacturing under strict quality standards, significantly increasing costs and limiting accessibility. Complex probes—such as those needing surface modifications for targeting or specialized chemical structures for AIE properties—face challenges in batch-to-batch consistency and reproducibility, which are critical for regulatory approval and reliable clinical deployment. Moreover, for innovative agents lacking precedents, regulatory pathways remain uncertain, potentially requiring adaptive strategies under exploratory frameworks.

Demonstrating clinical utility demands extensive validation beyond proof-of-concept. Technologies like hyperpolarized [1]³C-pyruvate MRI and targeted probes must undergo rigorous testing in large-scale, prospective, multicenter trials involving patients with diverse etiologies of chronic kidney disease and varying fibrosis stages [153,161,167]. This validation must not only establish diagnostic accuracy against the current gold standard (histopathology) but also prove their ability to provide clinically actionable insights. While label-free techniques avoid exogenous probe risks, operator-dependent variability must be minimized through standardized protocols and automated analysis tools to ensure reproducibility across centers.

Only by systematically addressing these interconnected challenges can the full potential of molecular imaging in renal fibrosis diagnosis and management be realized, ultimately translating into measurable patient benefits.

Emerging trends in multimodal imaging

Inter-study variability in trends may occur owing to different disease models, analytical methodologies, and fibrosis stage. Consequently, optimizing imaging parameters combined with multimodal integration has emerged as a critical direction to enhance precision in renal fibrosis assessment. For example, diffusion-relaxation correlation spectroscopic imaging (DR-CSI) has shown promise in distinguishing moderate-to-severe fibrosis by differentiating low-diffusion/long-T2 (V(B)) and high-diffusion (V(C)) signals [168]. In another comparative analysis of multiple diffusion models (IVIM, DKI, DR-CSI), a combined V(B) and IVIM f-value model achieved an AUC of 0.90, and further improved value of 0.96 when combined with eGFR [169].

Existing multimodal MRI studies in CKD have highlighted the importance of integrating multiple functional parameters with pathological validation. By integrating T1 mapping (a quantitative technique generating pixel-wise maps of longitudinal relaxation time), ASL, and DWI, Buchanan et al. demonstrated that cortical T1 values, ADC, and corticomedullary difference correlated significantly with eGFR and interstitial fibrosis [170]. Similarly, Zhang et al. validated MRI-derived fibrosis parameters (MRE and DWI) against histological findings in patients withe CKD, establishing a fibrosis assessment model incorporating MRI and eGFR [107]. Besides, the melanin-based MNP-PEG-Mn nanoprobe (2.7 nm) enables dual-modality photoacoustic/MRI detection of renal fibrosis, showing peak signals at 6h post-injection with stage-dependent contrast (stronger in early 7-day fibrosis vs attenuated in advanced 28-day fibrosis). While demonstrating passive renal targeting and antioxidant properties, this proof-of-concept study (animal model) highlights the need to address targeting specificity and safety before clinical translation [171].

For other kidney disease models, multimodal MRI also shows wide applicability: When combining IVIM-DWI, BOLD, and T1 mapping, a cortical ADC + ΔT1 composite model showed accurate diagnosis of fibrosis in renal transplantation [172]. Furthermore, parameters such as pool size ratio and spin-locking relaxation imaging (R1ρ and Sρ) could also assess fibrosis and tubular atrophy following AKI effectively [173]. In DN models, combining Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) with DWI enabled precise differentiation of fibrosis severity in miR-29a transgenic mice [174].

Collectively, the growing potential of multimodal functional imaging has been underscored in noninvasive fibrosis assessment. It may enable more comprehensive characterization of renal tissue microenvironment alterations, thereby improving early fibrosis detection and disease monitoring. To boost more precise, noninvasive renal fibrosis assessment, scholars can continue to make efforts in the following areas, such as developing AI-driven automated analysis tools, and establishing a comprehensive structure-function-metabolism evaluation framework.

AI technology

While biomarkers (serum and urinary) and imaging techniques provide noninvasive approaches for assessment of renal fibrosis [175,176], they face limitations in sensitivity, specificity, and early detection capabilities. Recent advances in AI have opened new possibilities for overcoming these challenges and improving fibrosis evaluation.

Artificial intelligence combined with ultrasound and renal fibrosis

Ultrasound elastography techniques, such as SWE, allowing the measurement of tissue elasticity to indicate the degree of renal fibrosis, have been concerned widely for their noninvasive and easy-to-operate properties [177]. However, SWE reveals slightly inferior diagnostic efficacy, disturbed by various factors, such as individual differences, renal heterogeneity, etc., and hence limited clinical application. AI technology, especially machine learning algorithms, is highly efficient in analyzing and uncovering the potential patterns in massive amounts of data, and can effectively handle complex nonlinear relationships, making up for the deficiencies of traditional methods.

Zhu et al. [176] constructed a model for predicting renal fibrosis by combining the support vector machine (SVM) with the ultrasonic features of SWE, B-ultrasound, and color Doppler flow imaging. When diagnosing tubulointerstitial fibrosis (>10%), the AUC reached 0.8303, which outperformed traditional ultrasound or SWE alone significantly. Moreover, it also had good diagnostic performance for different grades of renal fibrosis. In another prospective study in 162 patients with CKD Chen et al. [177] established four machine learning models, namely eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), SVM, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). The XGBoost model performed the best in distinguishing mild and moderate-to-severe renal fibrosis. In the original dataset and the five-fold cross-validation dataset, the AUCs reached 0.97 and 0.85, and the average precisions were 0.97 and 0.90, respectively. Further model explanation, based on the SHapley Additive exPlanations algorithm, demonstrated that eGFR contributed the most to the model output, followed by the elastic modulus, renal length, renal resistance index, and hypertension.

AI combined with CT and renal fibrosis

Based on high-resolution imaging, CT can clearly present the anatomical structure and tissue characteristics of the kidney [178]. However, there are certain limitations in evaluating renal fibrosis and accurately quantify the degree of fibrosis relying solely on CT images. AI technology, especially machine learning and deep learning algorithms, has powerful data processing and pattern recognition capabilities. This modality may faclitate a precise assessment of renal fibrosis by excavating hidden characteristic information in CT images. In an AIRS study, Chantaduly et al. analyzed 152 abdominal CT scan images of 92 patients using convolutional neural network (CNN), aiming to distinguish severe (≥50%) from mild/moderate (<50%) renal fibrosis [178]. Two types of CNN classifiers were employed at the slice and the voxel levels, with fibrosis score in the kidney biopsy report as the gold standard for model training and validation. Consequently, both models performed excellently in distinguishing severe from mild/moderate renal fibrosis, with AUCs of the slice- and voxel-level models of 0.917 and 0.922, respectively. Moreover, the model performed stably after using different CT scanners and excluding some interfering factors. Therefore, AI combined with CT technology has high accuracy and reliability in the assessment of renal fibrosis, providing a potentially noninvasive assessment for clinical practice.

AI combined with MRI and renal fibrosis

MRI acts as a multi-parameter and multi-sequence imaging to unveil the structural and functional information of the kidney from different perspectives [179]. For example, the T1 mapping technique can reveal pathological changes of tissues sensitively by measuring the T1 spin-lattice relaxation time of renal tissues, and the corticomedullary difference (ΔT1) has a significant correlation with the degree of renal fibrosis [180]. However, traditionally, regions of interest (ROIs) on MRI images aremanually drawn to measure cortical and medullary parameters, which is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and highly subjective. Significant differences in the measurement results may generate among different operators, seriously weakening the accuracy and reproducibility of the measurements.

A promising solution has begun to surface owing to the rapid development of AI technology. Machine learning and deep learning algorithms offer powerful tools for medical image processing. By automatically extracting complex features from images, both algorithms can achieve precise analysis of medical images. In the assessment of renal fibrosis, MRI integrated with AI is mainly reflected in the following aspects.

First, image segmentation can be effectively performed using deep learning algorithms, such as CNN, enabling the automatic segmentation of the cortex and medulla in renal MRI images. The Residual Channel Attention Mixed U-Net (RCM U-Net) model proposed by Aslam et al. performed excellently in the segmentation of the cortex and medulla of T1 map images of transplanted kidneys in patients with CKD. Its mean absolute error decreased by 50%, the Dice coefficient score increased by 16%, and the sensitivity increased by 12%, when compared with traditional networks such as VGG16, VGG19, ResNet34, and ResNet50. Moreover, it significantly improved the accuracy and efficiency of segmentation, owing to the saving of manual operations [180]. This achievement further enhance the reliability of ΔT1 measurement based on automatic segmentation, and provides a more accurate data reference for the assessment of renal fibrosis.

Second, the prediction of the degree of fibrosis relies on an in-depth analysis of MRI image features using machine learning algorithms, enabling the development of predictive models for renal fibrosis assessment. Combining AI algorithms with multiple parameters of MRI has been documented to gain deeper insights into the pathological state of the kidney, thereby improving the accuracy of prediction. For example, by learning from a large number of MRI images and renal biopsy results, an AI model can explicit the associations of some image features that are difficult to detect by traditional methods with renal fibrosis, thus achieving precise prediction of the degree of renal fibrosis [181].

Third, the monitoring of disease progression is effectively facilitated by the integration of AI and MRI, allowing for dynamic tracking of renal fibrosis progression. By analyzing the MRI images of patients at different time points, an AI model can promptly detect the subtle changes in the structure and function of the kidney, which may provide clinicians with instantaneous data to adjust the treatment plan. It may contribute to early intervention, delaying disease progression, and improving the survival and quality of life of patients [180].

AI combined with multimodal technology and renal fibrosis

Multimodal imaging technologies draw on the strong points of multiple imaging modalities, benefiting the presentation of the structural and functional information of the kidney from different levels. Ultrasonography is featured by convenience, noninvasiveness, and manifests high reproducibility. To be specific, gray-scale ultrasonography can observe the size, shape, and echogenicity of the kidney; superb microvascular imaging (SMI) can clearly display the microvascular status of the kidney and evaluate the changes of blood vessels during renal interstitial fibrosis; while strain elastography (SE) can suggest the degree of renal fibrosis by quantifying the tissue hardness [182]. MRI has high soft tissue resolution and multi-parameter imaging capabilities. For example, T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) can reflect the morphological structure of the kidney; DWI can evaluate the microscopic structure of tissues by measuring the diffusion movement of water molecules; and BOLD imaging can reflect the oxygen metabolism of the kidney [183]. In addition, CT has a high density resolution and can clearly present the anatomical structure of the kidney, which may also helpful in assessing renal fibrosis [178].

Through automatic feature mining in multimodal imaging data, machine learning and deep learning algorithms in AI technologies enable precise assessment of renal fibrosis. Qin et al. constructed a deep learning fusion model based on multimodal ultrasound by integrating the image features of gray-scale ultrasound, SMI, and SE. This model performed excellently in predicting early renal fibrosis in patients with CKD, with an AUC of 0.86, significantly better than single-modal deep learning models and clinical models [182]. Among them, SMI played a major role in the model’s decision-making. It can be interpreted that SMI can sensitively capture these changes in the context of renal microvascular changes during renal fibrosis, providing key information for the assessment. Moreover, SE also functioned to confirm the correlation between renal elasticity and the degree of fibrosis.

In the field of MRI, Chen et al. used machine learning algorithms to conduct texture analysis on T2WI, DWI, and BOLD images. They also constructed a multimodal MRI texture analysis (mMRI-TA) model for evaluating the renal function and degree of fibrosis in DN patients [184]. This model performed excellently in distinguishing patients with different renal function states. In the training cohort, the AUCs for identifying severe renal impairment, non-severe renal impairment, and normal renal function groups were 0.978, 0.852, and 0.972, respectively. Corresponding AUCs in the test cohort were 0.961, 0.809, and 0.850, respectively. Commonly, the texture analysis of T2WI can quantify the changes in the signal intensity of the kidney during fibrosis; while ADC measured by DWI and R2 (transverse relaxation rate) by BOLD can reveal renal fibrosis and hypoxia, respectively. Information integration on these basis can effectively improve the performance of the model in evaluating renal function. In addition, the progression of renal fibrosis can be monitored by combining AI with multimodal imaging technologies. By analyzing the multimodal imaging data of patients at different time points, AI model can promptly detect subtle changes in the structure and function of the kidneys, which may also be a robust tool to assist clinicians’ adjustment of treatment plans [180].

AI combined with biomarkers and renal fibrosis

Biomarkers play an important role in the assessment of renal fibrosis. At the genetic level, 24 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were discovered, such as ISG20, SERPINA3, etc., after analyzing GEO-sourced gene expression profiles of renal fibrosis and control samples. The expression changes of these genes established strong relationship with occurrence and development of renal fibrosis, providing potential biomarkers for renal fibrosis diagnosis. In terms of the application of machine learning algorithms, machine learning methods such as SVM-recursive feature elimination and the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression model were used to screen and analyze DEGs, with the identification of 9 key genes. These genes benefited a lot to the distinguishing of renal fibrosis samples from normal samples. The diagnostic potential of these genes was confirmed through the analysis of the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve [184].

Immune cell infiltration analysis is also one of the important means for evaluating renal fibrosis. The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to estimate the fractional composition trends of immune cells in renal fibrosis tissues, and ISG20 and SERPINA3 were associated with various immune cell subtypes, such as T follicular helper cells, neutrophils, activated memory CD4+ T cells, etc. Therefore, immune cells are also pivotal in the occurrence and development of renal fibrosis, which may also provide new biomarkers for the assessment of renal fibrosis.

In addition, some protein biomarkers have also been studied in the assessment of renal fibrosis. For example, a renal fibrosis cell model was established by treating the human renal tubular epithelial cell line (HK-2) with TGF-β1. This in vitro experiment revealed sharply increased expression of ISG20 in renal fibrosis samples, and ISG20 siRNA could significantly inhibit the progression of renal fibrosis. ISG20 may be a potential therapeutic target for renal fibrosis [184].

The approach of combining artificial intelligence with biomarkers has significant advantages in the assessment of renal fibrosis. The accuracy and specificity of diagnosis can be further elevated when machine learning algorithms are utilzied to extract valuable information from a large amount of biomarker data. Simultaneously, the combined application of biomarkers may provide more comprehensive evidence to determine the pathophysiological process of renal fibrosis, significantly supporting the diagnosis and treatment of the disease.

AI combined with renal pathology

In recent years, AI deep learning technology has shown great potential in renal histopathological analysis, providing an innovative tool for the precise assessment of renal fibrosis. By processing digital whole-slide images (WSIs), AI can achieve automatic segmentation of renal structures, quantitative analysis of fibrosis severity, and prediction of disease progression, significantly making up for the defects of subjectivity and low repeatability in traditional manual assessment.

Deep learning models (such as U-Net and Mask R-CNN) can accurately segment key structures in renal tissues, laying a foundation for fibrosis assessment. For example, algorithms based on convolutional neural networks can automatically identify structures like glomeruli, renal tubules, renal interstitium, and blood vessels, and even distinguish sclerotic glomeruli from normal ones as well as areas of tubular atrophy [185,186]. In the analysis of multi-stained sections (such as PAS, Masson’s trichrome, and Jones silver staining), AI models have realized multi-category segmentation of glomeruli, proximal/distal renal tubules, arteries, and peritubular capillaries in the renal cortex by integrating features from different stains. Among them, the segmentation accuracy of peritubular capillaries, whose damage is closely related to the progression of fibrosis, can reach a clinically practical level [187]. In terms of quantitative analysis, AI can objectively measure interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy through pixel-level identification of fibrotic areas (such as collagen deposition areas stained by Sirius red). The results are highly correlated with the interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy scores in the Banff classification, but with significantly better repeatability than manual assessment [185,186]. For instance, in renal transplant biopsies, the quantitative error of AI in fibrosis assessment can be controlled within 5%, and it can capture tiny fibrotic lesions that are easily overlooked manually [185]. Pathomics technology based on deep learning can further correlate with clinical outcomes by extracting microscopic features of fibrotic areas (such as collagen fiber texture and cell infiltration density). In IgA nephropathy, the prediction model constructed by AI through analyzing glomerular morphological features (such as glomerular capsule dilation and mesangial hyperplasia) and the degree of interstitial fibrosis can effectively distinguish the risk of disease progression, and its performance is superior to the traditional Oxford classification [186]. In lupus nephritis, AI can not only automatically identify active inflammatory and chronic fibrotic lesions but also predict the risk of progression to end-stage renal disease within 10 years by integrating fibrotic features with clinical data (such as eGFR and proteinuria) [185]. In addition, multi-modal data integration is an important advantage of AI. For example, after combining the features of renal biopsy WSIs with serum fibrosis markers (such as PRO-C6) and imaging data (such as renal ultrasound), the accuracy of AI models in predicting the progression of renal fibrosis can be increased to over 85% [186].

Currently, the core issues restricting the clinical application of AI include data standardization (such as staining differences among different laboratories and the impact of scanner models) and insufficient clinical validation. The generalization of models can be significantly improved by performing staining normalization through generative adversarial networks or adopting multi-center data for training (such as data from 29 centers in the NEPTUNE project) [187]. In the future, with the introduction of foundation models, AI is expected to achieve ‘zero-annotation’ rapid adaptation to different fibrosis scenarios, and the development of green AI technology will also reduce its computational energy consumption [186].

In conclusion, through automatic segmentation, quantification, and multi-dimensional integration, AI is reshaping the paradigm of pathological assessment of renal fibrosis, providing a new tool for achieving precise typing, prognostic prediction, and monitoring of treatment response.

Challenges in AI-driven renal fibrosis assessment

While AI-ultrasound combinations demonstrate promise in enhancing diagnostic accuracy for renal fibrosis, most evidence comes from single-center studies with small samples. Large, multi-center trials are required to validate model reliability and generalize results, which would facilitate clinical implementation and advance CKD management.

Besides, current AI-CT research is limited by small sample sizes and retrospective designs, necessitating larger prospective studies to validate model generalizability. Additionally, improving the interpretability of AI models—such as explaining their decision-making processes—is critical for clinical acceptance. Addressing these challenges will accelerate the clinical translation of AI-CT, revolutionizing the assessment and management of renal fibrosis.

Furthermore, most current studies on AI-MRI integration are based on small sample data sets, requiring larger multi-center trials to validate model generalizability and optimize performance across diverse populations and clinical contexts. The interpretability of AI models remains a hurdle, as enhancing clinicians’ understanding and trust in AI-derived diagnostic results is critical for adoption. Additionally, the high cost of MRI equipment and lengthy examination times may limit widespread implementation. Despite these barriers, AI-MRI holds promise for future clinical use, offering robust support for precise diagnosis and personalized treatment of kidney diseases by enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of renal fibrosis assessment.

AI technology combined with multimodal imaging has made progress in renal fibrosis assessment. However, data quality and standardization pose significant hurdles, as varying imaging devices and scanning parameters produce heterogeneous datasets, necessitating unified data protocols to ensure model accuracy and generalizability. The interpretability of deep learning models, rooted in their complex architectures, remains a major barrier, as clinicians often struggle to trust results from ‘black-box’ systems. Additionally, most studies rely on small, single-center datasets, lacking validation in large, multi-center cohorts, which undermines the reliability of findings for widespread application. To address these, future research should prioritize multi-center, large-sample studies to optimize data collection/processing and enhance standardization. Concurrently, developing visualization tools to decode AI decision-making processes will improve model transparency and clinician trust. As technology advances, AI-multimodal imaging integration is poised to become a cornerstone of renal fibrosis assessment, driving precise diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies for kidney diseases.

As for AI-biomarkers combinations, there are still some limitations in current research. Firstly, owing to the retrospective nature of most studies, further prospective studies are needed to verify the diagnostic value of these biomarkers. Secondly, multiple confounding factors, such as the stage of the disease, treatment interventions, etc., exist to disturb the expression of biomarkers, necessitating in-depth investigation to uncover their variation patterns under different circumstances. In addition, due to the challenging interpretability of artificial intelligence models, it is necessary to further develop visualization tools and interpretation methods to sharpen clinicians’ understanding of the decision-making process of these models. Generally, AI combined with biomarkers may be a more accurate and comprehensive method for the assessment of renal fibrosis, and is expected to be widely applied in clinical practice. To improve the accuracy and reliability of renal fibrosis assessment, further attempts should be made to optimize the selection of biomarkers and the application of machine learning algorithms.

Generally, the current lack of standardization in AI model validation significantly impacts the comparability of results, mainly manifested in three aspects as shown in Tables 1 and 2: Firstly, heterogeneity in data preprocessing: There are substantial differences in the processing of imaging/clinical data across various studies. For instance, variations exist in the truncation range of Hounsfield Unit (HU) values for CT images (Chantaduly et al. adopted [−100, 300] HU, while other studies did not specify the range) [178], and in whether ultrasound elasticity parameters are standardized (Qin et al. used 0–1 range normalization, whereas Zhu et al. did not) [182]. These discrepancies may lead to shifts in feature distribution, directly affecting model performance.Secondly, inconsistency in the definition of gold standards: There is a lack of uniformity in fibrosis grading criteria (e.g., Zhu et al. used ≥10% as the cutoff, Chen et al. adopted a 25% threshold, and Chantaduly et al. applied a 50% cutoff) [176–178]. Additionally, different pathological staining methods (Masson single staining vs. HE+Masson combined staining) result in inherent differences in the ‘target labels’ learned by the models. For example, models based on the Banff score [178] may focus more on transplant kidney-specific fibrosis, while those based on the Katafuchi score [182] are more suitable for chronic kidney disease, making direct performance comparison between the two impossible.Thirdly, variations in cross-validation methods: The rigor of validation strategies differs. Holdout validation [176] does not consider sample stratification, which may lead to overestimation of AUC due to uneven data distribution. In contrast, cross-scanner validation [178], although more clinically relevant, has not been implemented in most studies (only 1 out of 5 studies conducted equipment stability testing), resulting in insufficient evaluation of the model’s generalization ability.

To promote the standardization of artificial intelligence models, the following improvements can be attempted: Firstly, establish a unified fibrosis grading standard: It is recommended to adopt the universal Interstitial Fibrosis-Tubular Atrophy grading (0–3 grades) developed by the International Society of Nephrology, and clarify pathological staining specifications (including at least Masson staining for evaluating collagen deposition). Secondly, standardize data preprocessing procedures: Issue industry guidelines (e.g., MRI images need to correct for respiratory motion, ultrasound elasticity values should be standardized according to the reference range of healthy populations), and require studies to report key parameters (such as HU value range and normalization methods). Thirdly, promote multi-center stratified cross-validation: Prioritize the use of stratified sampling (stratified by etiology and stage), incorporate cross-equipment/cross-center data validation, and require reporting of model performance in different subgroups (e.g., diabetic nephropathy vs. hypertensive renal damage) instead of only the overall AUC.

Discussion

In the diagnosis and monitoring of renal fibrosis, the assessment of renal fibrosis biomarkers in blood and urine allows for frequent repeated sampling in view of its noninvasive, cost-effective nature (Table 3). It promotes dynamic monitoring of patients’ conditions and, in some cases, enables real-time acquisition of results. For example, specific biomarkers such as KIM-1 and NGAL have demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity in revealing kidney injury. However, these biomarkers may also indicative factors for other renal injuries or systemic diseases (as shown in Table 2), which should be used in combination with other indicators, rather than as the sole diagnostic tool.

Furthermore, these biomarkers may exhibit fluctuations in their levels, which may be attributed to the disruption of inflammatory state, nutritional status, the function of other organs, etc., leading to difficulties in interpretation. Moreover, inter-individual physiological and genetic differences may also endow the same biomarker levels with different meanings in different populations. At the same time, many emerging biomarkers may have limited value for application owing to the lack of unified detection methods and reference ranges at the current research stage. Currently, no single biomarker has been widely accepted as the gold standard.

Key directions in future development may include the combination of multiple biomarkers to construct prediction models, formulation of personalized medical programs, discovery of new and more specific biomarkers, establishment of automated integrated detection platforms, further in-depth verification of the effectiveness of biomarkers through long-term follow-up studies, and interdisciplinary cooperation to accelerate the transformation from basic research to clinical practice. Despite potential limitations of the currently used biomarkers, with the progress of scientific research and technologies, we can expect to witness the emergence of more efficient, accurate, and personalized diagnostic tools, thus enabling a more accurate assessment of renal fibrosis.

Significant progress has been made in the application of imaging techniques for noninvasive diagnosis of renal fibrosis recently. There have been multi-dimensional solutions supported by functional upgrading of traditional imaging techniques as well as in-depth integration with emerging molecular imaging and functional imaging techniques, resulting in more accurate assessment of renal fibrosis (Table 4). Besides making up for the limitations of traditional biopsy, these techniques also offer new perspectives for disease mechanism exploration and personalized treatment development.

In the field of molecular imaging, targeted molecular imaging techniques (e.g., FAPI, ESMA, CNA35-Cy7, Gd-OA) can visualize key fibrotic pathways by specifically binding to fibrosis-related targets (e.g., fibroblast activation protein, elastin, and collagen). However, these techniques still face challenges, such as complex probe pharmacokinetics and local blood flow interference on targeting efficiency. Significantly, by tracking natural metabolites or using microenvironment-responsive fluorescence, label-free molecular imaging techniques (e.g., hyperpolarized 1³C-alanine MRI, AIE probes) can avoid the interference of exogenous probes. However and notably, further verification is required to unveil the quantitative relationship between their signal intensity and the stage of fibrosis.

In the field of functional imaging, techniques (e.g., SWE, MRE) for tissue stiffness assessment provide important evidence for fibrosis staging by quantifying the mechanical properties of the kidney. SWE is cost-saving and provide real-time data to facilitate clinical screening, but with inter-operator difference in the measurements potentially. MRE is another widely selected modality with high repeatability and accuracy, yet with influenced popularity due to the cost of the equipment and the long scanning time. In addition, by analyzing water molecule diffusion, oxygenation status, and blood perfusion, renal microenvironment assessment techniques (e.g., IVIM, BOLD MRI, DCE MRI) reveal the pathological characteristics of microcirculatory disorders and hypoxia during fibrosis. Metabolic assessment techniques (e.g., CEST, APT, FLIM) further analyze the metabolic reprogramming related to fibrosis at the molecular level.

Existing technical systems have their inevitable limitations (Table 2). Firstly, the first priority should be given to parameter standardization of different imaging modalities, such as the poor comparability of Young’s modulus of SWE and the perfusion fraction of IVIM among different devices. Secondly, most emerging technologies (e.g., hyperpolarized 1³C-MRI, AIE probes) are still in the laboratory stage. Bottlenecks such as high cost and poor signal stability should be overcome first prior to their clinical translation. In addition, efficient algorithms are required to integrate and analyze multimodal data. The application of the promising AI-driven radiomics research is restricted by its unverified model interpretability and generalization ability, with further refinement required.

Technological innovation, multimodal integration, and interdisciplinary collaboration will be our future directions. It is necessary to combine AI-driven data analysis and build interpretable prediction models, and establish cross-vendor parameter calibration protocols. Meanwhile, the achievement of seamless integration of clinical translation, diagnosis, and treatment also relies on the development of low-cost screening solutions (e.g., portable SWE devices), formulation of multimodal diagnostic procedures (e.g., T1 mapping + IVIM + APT), and investigation of the clinical application of integrated diagnosis and treatment probes (e.g., drug-loaded targeted nanoparticles).

AI technology has demonstrated profound potential and remarkable advantages in assessing the degree of renal fibrosis, while also facing some challenges. AI, combined with various imaging techniques (e.g., ultrasound, CT, MRI) and biomarkers, provides a novel solution for noninvasive and accurate assessment of renal fibrosis, further improving the accuracy and efficiency of diagnosis (Table 1). Specifically, machine learning algorithms can make up for the deficiencies of traditional methods by uncovering patterns in complex data, especially excelling in quantifying subtle tissue changes.

However, relevant models require further verification for their generalization ability and reliability through large-scale, multi-center studies. In addition, one key for expanding the clinical application lies in the improvement of the interpretability of AI models. In the future, researchers can devote to do a good job in the implementation of more prospective studies and technological advancements, especially in the optimization of data collection process and development of visualization tools to enhance model interpretability. In this way, AI is expected to become an important tool for renal fibrosis assessment. Eventually, it may facilitate the formulation of personalized treatment plans and the monitoring of disease progression, bringing significant breakthroughs to the management of patients with CKD.
