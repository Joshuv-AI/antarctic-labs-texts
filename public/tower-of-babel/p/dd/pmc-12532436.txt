Household health-seeking behaviour and response to Informal payment: does economic status matter?
Authors: Chukwudi Nwokolo, Obinna Onwujekwe, Martin McKee, Iheomimichineke Ojiakor, Blake Angell, Dina Balabanova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCorruption is a major factor that influences health seeking behaviour. However, there is paucity of empirical evidence from research on how corruption affects different population groups when they seek healthcare services from formal healthcare facilities. The paper presents new evidence on how informal payments, which is a major form of corruption, affect health-seeking behaviour people and how household economic status has sustained it in Nigeria.MethodsWe used a pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire to conduct interviews in 1,652 households in Enugu and Kano states, in the south and north of Nigeria, respectively. Descriptive statistics was used to estimate household health-seeking behaviour and Ordinary Least Square, binary logistic and multinomial logistic regression analyses to assess how experience of informal payment and economic status (quintiles: extremely poor, poor, average, rich, extremely rich quintiles) affect household health-seeking behaviour.ResultsPoorer households were most likely to attend health posts and health centres, while extremely rich households disproportionately used hospitals (59%). Household economic status determines the likelihood of paying informally, with richer ones paying more (p < 0.05). Household size, age of the patient, sex, years spent on formal education and state were other identified determinants of informal payments. Experience of informal payment in public facilities significantly reduces household use of tertiary hospitals compared to primary health centres or health posts by 58% (p < 0.05). The choice of tertiary hospital compared to a primary health centre or health post is significantly reduced by 31% because of informal payments (p < 0.01).ConclusionInformal payments in public facilities negatively affect health seeking, driving the poorest households to use low-quality care services. This problem needs to be widely recognised and sufficiently tackled in order for the country to reduce the economic burden of health seeking and achieve equitable access and utilisation of high-quality health services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-025-00654-3.

Introduction

Health-seeking behaviour is influenced by both the resources available and constraints on households and the characteristics of the healthcare system [1]. Household characteristics, such as socio-economic status, education and access to information, impact the decision to seek care and where to seek it [2]. To take one of many examples, Li and colleagues have reported how economic status significantly influences healthcare-seeking behaviour in China, with better-off households more likely to utilise high-quality health services [3], findings that have been replicated in many low- and middle-income nations [4–6]. Among these characteristics, household wealth is relatively under-researched, but an understanding of its role in health-seeking behaviour and how it interacts with other household characteristics is vital if we are to tackle inequalities in care-seeking seeking [7].

In Nigeria, the setting for this study, many people, especially those who are poor, struggle to access affordable, quality healthcare. High out-of-pocket payments, geographic inaccessibility of health facilities, cultural norms, and a general mistrust of public health institutions contribute to low rates of care-seeking in the formal sector [8, 9]. These challenges often lead individuals to delay or avoid seeking care altogether and turn to informal providers or self-medication, both carrying significant public health risks [10]. In these circumstances, effective policy responses are urgent.

In designing appropriate responses, one issue is now coming to the fore: corruption in the health sector [11]. There is growing acceptance that it creates major barriers to equitable access to health services [4, 12]. The Health Belief Model (HBM), which is one of several frameworks used to understand this phenomenon, views health-seeking behaviour as balancing the perceived threat of disease against the cost and benefit of any intervention [13]. The cost here involves many components, among them direct costs of care, expenditures on transport and loss of income from taking time off, and payments for corrupt actions.

The last of these is a known problem in the Nigerian health sector. It includes a range of unethical actions such as informal payments for services that are officially meant to be free or subsidised, demanding bribes before providing care, or diverting public medical supplies for private gain [14, 15]. Such informal, unofficial, and unapproved payments are demanded directly from patients by healthcare workers. This process is typically covert, with no receipt being issued and it can place a significant financial burden on households, particularly those already struggling economically [10]. The Nigerian government has responded to the high cost falling on households in several ways, including the establishment of a Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF) and the creation of anti-corruption units within relevant ministries. However, the effectiveness of these efforts remains constrained by weak enforcement mechanisms and persistent challenges to achieving transparency at the point of service delivery [9, 15]. Attempts to improve this situation confront a lack of substantial evidence on how households across different economic strata respond to health sector corruption when seeking care [7, 16].

This paper provides new insights into the relationship between economic status, perceptions of corruption, and household health-seeking behaviour. First, we explored how household economic status influences health-seeking behaviour and how households across different economic quintiles respond to corrupt public healthcare providers. Second, we examined how economic status and perceptions of corruption affect the size of informal payments and the likelihood that households normalise corruption in healthcare services. Finally, we investigated the impact of informal payments on health-seeking behaviour and the potential moderating role of economic status.

Methodology

Following pre-testing to exclude redundant questions and simplify complex terminology, we used an interviewer-administered questionnaire to survey 1,652 households in Enugu and Kano states, Nigeria, using a multi-stage sampling approach. First, Kano and Enugu states were purposively selected based on their proximity to the research teams, differences in health systems, societal contexts, and relative security. Next, local government areas (LGAs) were categorised as urban, semi-urban, or rural based on their characteristics, and LGAs were selected within each category. Finally, households that had accessed care from a public facility within six months prior to the interview were purposively selected.

Data collection took place between November and December 2022, conducted by 80 research assistants supervised by 12 supervisors. Research assistants worked in pairs: one used a tablet loaded with the questionnaire via ODK software to ask and record responses, while the other used a paper version of the questionnaire to tick responses simultaneously. At the end of each day, the two versions were compared and reconciled to ensure accuracy. Supervisors oversaw the reconciliation process before the final dataset was synchronised to the central server.

Research assistants were assigned to local government areas (LGAs) where they were familiar with the community and spoke the local language. Interviews were conducted in English, Hausa, Igbo, or Pidgin, depending on household preferences. To measure household economic status, we constructed an asset index based on self-reported ownership of items such as televisions, radios, refrigerators, air conditioners, computers/laptops, mobile phones, tablets, bicycles, motorcycles, cars, kerosene lamps, generators, and rechargeable lamps. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to generate the asset index.

The selected assets reflected the resources of households that largely operated in the informal sector, with variable incomes, limited expense records, and typically low savings. Under these conditions, economic status is better captured by asset ownership rather than income or savings. Households were classified into five wealth quintiles: extremely poor, poor, average, rich, and extremely rich. To tackle the possible endogeneity issue around the economic status variable, we used the Hausman test which produced a non-significant probability value (p > 0.05). Based on the result, we conclude that the estimates are similar, and therefore there is no strong evidence to suggest that the economic status is endogenous.

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse household health-seeking behaviour, payment strategies, responses to informal payments in healthcare, and the distribution of the asset index. To assess the impact of household economic status on the size of informal payments to healthcare providers, we used an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model, appropriate because the dependent variable was continuous and normally distributed. To identify factors influencing the likelihood of making an informal payment, we applied a binary logistic regression model, with the dependent variable coded as 1 for households that made an informal payment and 0 otherwise. Finally, we used multinomial logistic regression to analyse the effects of informal payments and their interaction with household economic status on health-seeking behaviour, as the dependent variable was categorical and unordered, requiring a multinomial probability model.

In the OLS model, the dependent variable was the total amount of informal payments made by households when seeking healthcare. For the binary logistic regression, the dependent variable was a binary indicator: households that reported making an informal payment at a public healthcare facility within the past six months were coded as 1, while those that did not were coded as 0. To capture health-seeking behaviour for the multinomial logistic regression, we categorised the choice of healthcare facility as follows: 1 for primary healthcare centres or health posts, 2 for district, general, or cottage hospitals, and 3 for tertiary hospitals. This categorical variable served as the dependent variable for the multinomial model. The independent variables were largely consistent across all models. Aside from the asset index, all quantitative independent variables were logarithmically transformed in the OLS regression to reduce the risk of spurious results [17]. The independent variables included household economic status, number of healthcare facility visits (taken as a proxy for severity), household size, years of formal education, age of the person receiving treatment, sex (male = 1, female = 2), state (Enugu = 1, Kano = 2), and who made the decision to seek care (self = 1, another person = 2). Payments for healthcare services

Table 5 highlights how many people paid for services that were supposed to be free in public facilities based on their economic status. Among the extremely poor, 26.32% paid for consultations, while this figure was higher among the rich (36.36%) and the extremely rich, at 26.96%. Similarly, 22.66% of the poorest households paid for medications despite knowing they shouldn’t have, compared to 27.13% of the extremely rich. Regardless of economic status, most households made extra payments for medical supplies, even when they knew such charges were unapproved. However, there was no significant difference across income levels. Regarding diagnostic tests, 25.64% of the poorest households paid for them despite knowing it was unapproved, while 27.48% of the extremely rich did the same. Administrative fees, such as payments for certificates, showed the largest disparity. 31.71% of the poorest households paid for these services, but the numbers jumped significantly among the rich (53.45%) and extremely rich (55.40%). These findings suggest that informal payments for supposedly free public services are widespread, with wealthier households more likely to make such payments across most categories.

Association between economic status and households engaging in corrupt behaviour while utilising healthcare services

Table 6 reveals a clear relationship between household economic status and the likelihood of making informal payments, even when such payments were not approved. Households with an average economic status were significantly more likely to engage in informal payments, with a 42% higher likelihood than extreme poor households. This trend intensified as wealth increased; rich households were 116% more likely to make informal payments, while those classified as extremely rich had an even greater likelihood, at 186%. Beyond economic status, other factors also played a role. Larger households were far more prone to making informal payments, with a 93% higher probability. Similarly, household members who had spent more years in formal education were 98% more likely to participate in such payments. However, age and gender appeared to have the opposite effect. Older patients were slightly less likely to engage in informal payments, with a 1% decrease in likelihood. In comparison, female household members were 48% less likely to do so than their male counterparts. The state where the respondents reside also influenced informal payments. Households in Kano had a significantly lower likelihood by 37% of making informal payments than those in Enugu. These findings suggest that socioeconomic and demographic factors contribute to informal payment prevalence in different household contexts. The association between household economic status and the level of informal payments

Table 7 shows how economic status and other factors influence the amount of informal payments made by households when seeking care at public healthcare facilities. Households classified as extremely rich paid, on average, 46% more in informal payments than those in the extremely poor quintile. The frequency of healthcare visits also had a significant impact: each additional visit increased informal payments by 23%. Household size had a smaller effect, with each additional member associated with a 6% increase in informal payments. In contrast, education had a mitigating effect: each additional year of formal education reduced the amount paid informally by 36%. Age also slightly reduced informal payments, with each additional year of age associated with a 0.2% decrease. Gender differences were observed as well: female respondents paid 24% less in informal payments compared to male respondents. Overall, these findings suggest that wealthier and larger households, as well as those with more frequent healthcare visits, are more likely to make higher informal payments. Conversely, higher education levels, older age, and being female are associated with lower informal payment amounts. Health-seeking behaviour in response to corruption in public facilities and the role of economic status

Table 8 explores the impact of corruption on household healthcare choices and the role of economic status in shaping these decisions. The likelihood of a household making informal payments did not significantly influence whether they chose a district, general, or cottage hospital over a primary health centre or health post. Similarly, when considering economic status, informal payments had no significant effect on the choice between these facility types. However, informal payments significantly reduced the likelihood of households seeking care at a tertiary hospital instead of a primary health centre or health post. Households that made informal payments were 42% less likely to choose a tertiary hospital. When economic status was taken into account, this effect became even stronger: households making informal payments based on their wealth were 69% less likely to seek care at a tertiary facility. These findings suggest that corruption and the burden of informal payments may discourage households from accessing higher-level healthcare services, potentially limiting their access to specialized medical treatment. Discussion

This study examined how household economic status influences health-seeking behavior and responses to informal payments within the public healthcare systems of two Nigerian states, Enugu and Kano. The findings reveal significant differences across income levels, reflecting broader structural inequities present in Nigeria and much of sub-Saharan Africa. Households in the lowest economic quintile predominantly relied on health posts and primary health centers, whereas those in the highest quintiles were more likely to utilize tertiary hospitals. This pattern aligns with expectations, as tertiary hospitals—with more skilled personnel and better resources—are perceived as offering higher-quality care and are therefore more desirable [18]. However, access to such facilities often depends on a household’s financial capacity, explaining why wealthier households are more likely to seek care there. These disparities undermine the Nigerian government’s objective of promoting equitable access through its three-tier healthcare system. In practice, access to quality care is largely determined by the ability to pay. Although tertiary hospitals are theoretically open to all, for many poorer households they remain functionally inaccessible due to high transportation costs, long wait times, and the prevalence of informal payments. This finding supports previous research suggesting that wealthier households invest more in healthcare, while financial barriers limit healthcare options for poorer households [19].

Richer households were significantly more likely than poorer ones to make informal payments, even when aware that such payments were unofficial. This suggests that households in higher economic quintiles are more willing to engage in informal transactions to access services, despite knowing that these payments are not sanctioned. In the Nigerian context—characterized by systemic underfunding and limited oversight—such practices are especially concerning. These findings are consistent with previous literature indicating that wealthier households are more inclined to make informal payments when faced with barriers to healthcare access [20, 21]. Unfortunately, informal payments are often normalized and used to expedite service delivery within public health facilities. [22]

Contrary to findings suggesting that poorer households are more likely to make informal payments when seeking healthcare, our results present a different narrative [23, 24]. This discrepancy may be explained by the relatively faster access to care experienced by wealthier households [25]. Nonetheless, our findings indicate that poorer households still engage in informal payments and bear a disproportionately heavier financial burden compared to wealthier households. For many, these additional costs compound existing struggles to meet basic needs. This reality reflects a broader pattern of health-related impoverishment observed in Nigeria and other low- and middle-income countries, where out-of-pocket payments continue to dominate healthcare financing [24, 25].

Our findings also reveal that excessive informal charges drive households away from public facilities toward private healthcare providers, where such unapproved payments are less common. Although this shift affects all socioeconomic groups, it highlights the urgent need to address informal payments because of their widespread impact on healthcare access. Additionally, the financial pressure associated with informal payments often forces poorer households to seek lower-cost alternatives, which may offer inferior quality care and pose serious health risks [26]. In contrast, wealthier households can afford private healthcare, further exacerbating disparities in access and health outcomes. Addressing these challenges is critical to achieving equitable access to quality healthcare for all.

Economic status plays a major role in determining the amount households spend on informal payments in public healthcare facilities. Our findings show that wealthier households make significantly higher informal payments than poorer households, reinforcing the idea that financial capacity drives informal healthcare expenditures. This result is consistent with previous research suggesting that economically privileged households are more likely to make informal payments for services such as malaria treatment than their poorer counterparts [14].

Beyond economic status, other factors—including household size, education level, age, gender, and geographic location—also influence the likelihood of making informal payments. Larger households were more likely to make informal payments, likely because greater healthcare needs and more frequent interactions with providers increase their exposure to informal payment demands. This is consistent with earlier studies indicating that larger households incur more informal healthcare costs [27]. Education level similarly affected informal payment behavior; more educated individuals were more likely to make informal payments, possibly due to greater familiarity with informal financial systems. In developing economies, educated individuals may exploit informal practices for convenience or to lower transaction costs [28]. Some may also prefer informal payments due to distrust of formal institutions or bureaucratic inefficiencies [29], challenging the assumption that education reduces informal payments [30]. Older individuals were less likely to make informal payments, possibly because they visit healthcare facilities less frequently. Similarly, female household members were less likely than males to engage in informal payments, supporting previous findings [27, 31]. Geographic differences were also observed, with households in Kano State being less likely to make informal payments compared to those in Enugu State, likely due to higher poverty levels in Kano, which may limit the ability to pay.

Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insights into household health-seeking behaviour and responses to informal payments in relation to economic status, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the research relied solely on quantitative survey data from 1,652 households. While the large sample size strengthens the statistical validity of the findings, the lack of qualitative data limits a deeper understanding of the contextual and subjective factors that influence individual behaviours and perceptions of informal payments. Second, as with most survey-based studies, the results may be affected by recall bias or social desirability bias, especially given the sensitive nature of the topic. Respondents may have underreported or misrepresented their experiences, potentially affecting the accuracy of the data. Third, we lacked data on severity of the conditions for which care was sought. In this context, severity includes a combination of elements, including pain, disability, and prognosis. This would be difficult to capture even if all respondents had the same health problem but is virtually impossible when they can have a wide variety of different ones. The degree of severity may be judged differently by different respondents. Also, in some cases, the respondent reported on behalf o the person who had been sick so may not be able give accurate information on severity as experienced by the patient. Given these complications, we used the number of hospital visits as a proxy.

Conclusion

Informal payments in public healthcare facilities significantly influence households’ choice of care providers, with economic status playing a key role in sustaining these payments and shaping health-seeking behaviour. While wealthier households are more likely to make informal payments, the widespread prevalence of this practice poses serious barriers to equitable healthcare access. Addressing this issue requires targeted reforms that recognise the extent to which informal payments undermine health-seeking behaviour and health outcomes. More evidence is needed to identify effective strategies. Since most informal payments are made in cash, introducing automated payment systems in public healthcare facilities could help curb the practice. Additionally, the economic disparities in healthcare access highlight the urgent need for comprehensive health insurance that covers all households, regardless of income or occupation. This study contributes to the growing call for governments to prioritise the elimination of informal payments by expanding access to quality healthcare, an essential step toward achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), a critical goal for many countries, including Nigeria.
