Cytochrome P450 1a (CYP1A)‐Knockout Javanese Medaka Fish Exhibit Diabetic Traits and Reduced Reproductive Capacity
Authors: Suhaila Rusni, Yuki Yoshioka, Hilda Mardiana Pratiwi, Toshiyuki Takagi, Muhammad Rahmad Royan, Yuya Hojo, Seiichi Uno, Shinji Kanda, Yusuke Takehana, Masato Kinoshita, Koji Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCytochrome P450 1A (CYP1A) is an enzyme localized in the endoplasmic reticulum of liver cells. Although its roles in detoxification of exogenous substances have been investigated, its endogenous functions remain poorly understood. Here, we compared liver transcriptomes of CYP1A‐knockout (KO) Javanese medaka ( Oryzias javanicus ) with those of wild‐type (WT) fish to determine the physiological roles of CYP1A. We identified 508 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including genes involved in glucose metabolism that were highly upregulated, such as insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin. KO fish exhibited elevated blood glucose levels, increased liver mass, and higher hepatic triglyceride levels, together with upregulation of glucose‐regulatory genes, suggesting insulin resistance and a diabetic‐like condition. In addition, some genes associated with reproduction, including zona pellucida sperm‐binding proteins 1 and 3 (ZP1 and ZP3), were downregulated. Decreased spawning rates and fertilization rates confirmed reduced reproductive capacity in KO fish. Thus, this study demonstrates that CYP1A has endogenous functions in glucose homeostasis and reproduction. The CYP1A‐KO strain is expected to serve as a model for investigating detailed physiological processes related to diabetes and reproductive mechanisms, as well as the relationship between these processes mediated by CYP1A.

Introduction

Cytochrome P450 1A (CYP1A) belongs to the Cyp superfamily, a group of heme‐thiolate monooxygenases. Fish and amphibians generally have a single CYP1A gene whereas mammals have two paralogs, CYP1A1 and CYP1A2 [1]. CYP1A is involved in the metabolism of various exogenous and endogenous compounds. It is important in phase I metabolism of drugs and xenobiotic substances from the environment [2]. The CYP1A gene has also been utilized as a marker for exposure to harmful water‐borne pollutants, such as PAHs [3, 4], microplastics [5], heavy metals [6], pesticides [7], and sunscreen compounds [8]. As for its endogenous function, previous studies have reported its involvement in the metabolism and synthesis of lipids, cholesterols, steroids, linoleic acid, retinol, phosphatidylcholine, melatonin, and uroporphyrinogen [9]. However, the functions of this gene relative to these compounds remain poorly understood, and it is possible that important functions of CYP1A are yet undiscovered.

In this study, we explored endogenous functions of CYP1A through transcriptomic analysis of Javanese medaka, Oryzias javanicus , a strain whose CYP1A gene is knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9. Oryzias javanicus is related to the well‐known experimental model, Japanese medaka, O. latipes , but it is more useful for analyzing physiological processes in marine environments [3, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] because it prefers seawater to freshwater [15, 16]. We have already established a CYP1A‐knockout (KO) strain and demonstrated that wild‐type (WT) and CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus respond differently to exogenous polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), i.e., KO fish were more sensitive to pyrene, than WT fish, but the result was reversed when fish were exposed to phenanthrene [4]. Hence, we used the CYP1A‐KO and WT strains to understand differences in toxicity modes of environmental contaminants. We also sought to clarify metabolic differences between the KO and WT strains in order to understand differences in their responses to pollutants.

Liver was targeted for the transcriptome analysis, because the primary localization of CYP1A is in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of liver cells [17]. In vertebrates, the liver is a major site of lipid metabolism, protein synthesis, sugar balance regulation [2, 18, 19, 20], formation of new cellular membranes [21, 22] and blood production [23, 24, 25], as well as detoxification of xenobiotics and drugs [26]. In addition, in fish, liver synthesizes egg components, including vitellogenin and egg shell proteins, and also governs temperature adaptation [27]. Therefore, we expected that disruption of CYP1A might affect a wide variety of hepatic functions.

Interestingly, upregulated genes in the CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus included insulin and glucagon genes. As these hormones are known to be expressed in pancreatic islets and regulate blood sugar levels antagonistically, their expression in the liver and co‐upregulation are unexpected and suggest glucose metabolism dysfunction in KO fish. Therefore, we also assessed blood glucose levels and liver morphology. Results indicated that KO fish manifest a diabetes‐like condition, suggesting the importance of CYP1A in glucose metabolism. Moreover, we also report the indirect effects of CYP1A KO on reproduction.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

We used WT Javanese medaka, O. javanicus (strain ID: RS831), obtained from the National BioResource Projects (NBRP) Medaka at the National Institute for Basic Biology (NIBB), Okazaki, Japan, and the CYP1A‐KO strain, generated from the WT strain using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Detailed information on the genome editing procedure, genotyping strategy, and mutation detection of CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus were described in a previous study [4]. The KO strain has been maintained in a separate room to avoid cross‐contamination, and the existence of the deletion has been monitored by a PCR assay that amplifies deleted and WT sequences specifically [4]. For both strains, adult fish (approximately 3.5 cm, total length) were used for all experiments, unless otherwise specified. Fish were maintained in aquaria with recirculating water under a 14/10‐h day/night cycle at 26°C. Seawater salinity was maintained at ~31 ppt, and fish were fed twice a day with brine shrimp, Artemia salina , larvae. The animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). Care and use of animals were conducted according to Guidelines for the Care and Use of Animals of The University of Tokyo, and experimental procedures approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Atmosphere and Ocean Research Institute of The University of Tokyo (permission no. P18‐13).

RNA Extraction and RNA‐Sequencing of WT and KO O. javanicus

After euthanasia on ice, livers were removed from three adult male fish (mean total length, 3.50 ± 0.00 cm for WT and 3.43 cm ± 0.067 for KO), taking care not to contaminate samples with pancreatic or islet cells [28]. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermofisher Scientific) and a RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN) along with DNase treatment (QIAGEN). The concentration and quality of total RNA were checked using a NanoDrop. Integrity of RNA samples was assessed using the 5200 Fragment Analyzer System and an Agilent HS RNA Kit (Agilent Technologies). Qualified samples were used to construct cDNA libraries using an MGIEasy RNA Directional Library Prep Set (MGI Tech) and a QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega), with 100‐bp, paired‐end sequencing using a DNBSEQ‐G400 (MGI Tech) at Bioengineering Lab. Co. Ltd. Low‐quality reads (quality score < 20 and length < 20 bp) and sequence adaptors for forward: 5′‐AAGTCGGAGGCCAAGCGGTCTTAGGAAGACAA‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐AAGTCGGATCGTAGCCATGTCGTTCTGTGAGCCAAGGAGTTG‐3′ primers were trimmed with CUTADAPT version 4.3 [29]. Mapping to Oryzias javanicus gene models (mRNA) from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), BioProject PRJNA505405 [30] was performed using SALMON v1.10.0.

Transcriptomic Analysis of WT and KO O. javanicus

To identify DEGs, gene expression levels (CPM) in KO fish were compared with control samples (WT). Normalized gene expression levels for each KO sample were calculated using edgeR version 3.41.8 in R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023) relative to WT O. javanicus . Resulting p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method in edgeR. Genes were considered DEGs when their expression levels were significantly different (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05), and change was ≥ 2× different than WT. DEGs were then visualized using volcano plot analysis.

DEGs were annotated by BLASTX homology searches with an e‐value cutoff of 1e−3 against the Swiss‐Prot database (downloaded on 20 May 2023). Then, to evaluate DEG involvement in functional and metabolic pathways, the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) was utilized to perform Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and KEGG Pathway Analysis. The background used for these analyses comprised all UniProt IDs from gene models of O. javanicus . Terms exhibiting p < 0.05 were considered “enriched”.

Measurement of WT and KO O. javanicus Blood Glucose Levels

Blood glucose levels of both WT and KO O. javanicus strains were measured following protocols established by [31, 32], with some modifications. Adult fish (n = 6) were kept in a fasting state for 20 h. Fish blood from the caudal peduncle area was drawn using a heparinized glass needle attached to a silicone capillary by mouth suctioning. After obtaining the blood, the glass needle tip was broken using forceps and was blown onto a piece of parafilm. Then, a OneTouch Verio Reflect Glucose Meter (LifeScan, Tokyo, Japan) with a test strip prepared in advance was immediately brought into contact with the blood, and the reading displayed by the glucose meter was recorded. Sampled fish were then returned to their aquaria.

Measurement of O. javanicus WT and KO Liver Sizes

To compare liver of WT and KO fish, six adult male O. javanicus fish with average standard lengths of 3.00 ± 0.17 cm and 2.98 ± 0.54 cm were selected from both WT and CYP1A‐KO fish. After euthanasia on ice, body lengths and masses were measured. Subsequently, livers were excised and weighed, and maximum liver diameters were measured using an all‐in‐one fluorescence microscope (BZ‐X800, KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan) at 4× magnification in bright‐field mode. To visualize adipose tissues on the liver surface, excised livers from WT and CYP1A‐KO fish were fixed and stored in 10% formalin, then sequentially immersed in 50% isopropanol/phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) for 1 h, Oil Red O (Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) for 2 h, 50% isopropanol/PBS for 2 h, and PBS for 1 h, followed by observation under a stereomicroscope (Leica S9D).

Lipid Analyses of Livers

Livers were excised from 14 WT and 14 KO adult fish (average standard lengths: 2.76 ± 0.13 cm and 2.78 ± 0.15 cm, respectively), as described above. The livers were weighed, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Due to the small size of individual livers, two organs were pooled into a single sample. Each sample was homogenized in a mixture (500 μL) of chloroform, methanol, and ultrapure water (130:265:105, v/v/v), followed by the addition of chloroform (330 μL) and ultrapure water (105 μL). After shaking and centrifugation (12 000 g, 10 min, 4°C), the lower organic phase was evaporated under N2 gas and weighed. The resulting lipid extract was dissolved in 100 volumes of isopropanol. Triglyceride and phospholipid concentrations were determined using LabAssay Triglyceride and LabAssay Phospholipid kits (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan).

Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species Levels

Five adult males each were selected from the WT and KO groups. After euthanasia, livers were placed in 20 mM HEPES solution and homogenized using a pestle and pipetting. All liver samples were then centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. Supernatants were kept on ice and subjected to ROS measurement using a Dojindo's ROS Assay Kit—Highly Sensitive DCFH‐DA‐Dye with 10× loading buffer (R253, Dojindo, Japan). Ten‐μL liver samples were brought up to 100 μL with phosphate‐buffered saline solution and placed in a 96‐well black microplate. A microplate reader (Spectra iD3, Molecular Devices LLC, San Jose, CA, USA), was utilized to measure ROS content at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. Obtained values as percentages were then normalized to total protein content, quantified using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay (06385, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan).

Comparison of Spawning Activities and Fertilization Rates

To compare reproductive activities, 15 male–female pairs of WT and CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus were maintained in separate 1 L tanks for mating with the same breeding condition. Pairs that produced eggs at least once in 2 weeks were counted. Subsequently, fertilization rates of eggs were compared using three pairs of actively spawning parents from each of the WT and KO groups. Collected eggs were cultured in 0.6 ppm methylene blue solution [33] and kept in an incubator at 27°C–28°C, with continuous rotation on a rotary shaker (10 rpm) and the illumination period was similar to that for adult fish. Eggs that did not develop after 1 day were considered unfertilized.

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.3.1, 2024) was used for statistical analysis and Student's t‐test was performed to compare WT and KO fish. Data were summarized as means ± standard errors of the means (SEM). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 in all analyses.

Results

Differential Expression Analysis

To investigate the impact of CYP1A deficiency on liver function, we performed transcriptome analysis using RNA‐seq. Differential expression analysis between WT and KO detected 508 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 225 upregulated and 283 downregulated genes. Genes for which transcripts increased significantly in the KO strain included insulin (INS), somatostatin (SMS1) and its receptor (SST2), glucagon (GLUC1), ryanodine receptor 2 (RYR2), claudin (CLDN8), glutamate receptor NMA2A (GRIN2A), GPI‐linked NAD(P)(+)‐arginine ADP‐ribosyltransferase 1 (ART1), and rRNA N‐glycosylase (STXA) (Figure 1). These seven genes are all related to glucose regulation, although it is puzzling that both insulin and glucagon, which serve opposite functions in regulating blood sugar levels, as well as somatostatin, which inhibits secretion of both hormones [34], were upregulated. Ryanodine receptors (RYR2) and glutamate receptors (GRIN2A) are also thought to be connected to insulin‐related function [35, 36]. Insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin genes were generally considered to be expressed in pancreatic islets. However, these transcripts are not a result caused by contamination of islet cells because islet cells of the Oryzias fish are separated from the liver [28] and we collected liver samples carefully not to include pancreatic tissues. In addition, TPM (transcripts per million) values, which indicate expression levels, of the three genes were nearly zero in wildtype fish (Table S2), indicating that our sampling method did not cause contamination because islet cells are considered to express such genes under normal conditions [37]. Therefore, we propose that expression of such genes was ectopically induced by disruption of the CYP1A gene in the liver.

Downregulated genes in the KO strain included sushi‐repeat containing protein (SRPX2), T‐cell surface glycoprotein (CD4), transposon Ty3 (YI31B), germ cell‐specific gene 1‐like protein (GSG1L), LYR motif‐containing protein 5A (LYRM5A), RNA‐binding Raly‐like protein (RALYL), zona pellucida sperm‐binding protein 3 (ZP3), extracellular serine/threonine protein kinase (FA20C), solute carrier family 41 member 1 (S41A1), and zona pellucida sperm‐binding protein 1 (ZP1). These downregulated genes were dominated by germ cell‐related genes.

The knocked‐out gene, CYP1A, displayed a fold change of only 0.1082 (p = 0.8129), possibly reflecting expression of mRNA lacking the targeted knockout region. This result is unlikely to be influenced by the presence of a CYP1A paralog, as only a single CYP1A gene is present in the whole‐genome sequence of Javanese medaka [30]. In addition, transcripts of the CYP1B gene, which is also expected to be regulated by aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), were not detected in WT livers and were barely detectable in KO livers (Table S3).

Gene Ontology

Next, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs in KO O. javanicus , showing that upregulated genes are associated with endoplasmic reticulum, Ca2+ transport activity, and cell signaling (Figure 2A). Detailed analysis of GO terms for upregulated DEGs (Table 1) indicated that the functions suggested above involve genes commonly annotated as ryanodine receptor 2 (RYR2). Loss of CYP1A enzyme in the ER significantly upregulates the RYR2 gene, suggesting an increase in calcium‐induced calcium release in hepatocytes. Additionally, suramin expression was greatly enhanced. Suramin inhibits acrosin, a sperm protein that helps sperm penetrate the egg zona pellucida; thus, suramin upregulation likely suppresses fertilization.

GO analysis of downregulated DEGs (Figure 2B) shows significant disruption of processes such as phosphorus metabolism, osteoclast maturation, MHC class I peptide loading complex, and aminoamylase activity. In particular, aminoamylase is crucial for xenobiotic metabolism.

Likewise, KEGG pathway analyses of upregulated DEGs showed that most are involved in cell adhesion, P13K–Akt signaling, prion disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, Ras signaling, and Apelin signaling (Figure 3A). Downregulated DEGs are related to functions such as linoleic acid metabolism, inositol phosphate metabolism, GABAergic synapse, glutamatergic synapse, biosynthesis of amino acids, and drug metabolism via cytochrome P450 (Figure 3B).

Blood Glucose Analysis

As the transcriptome analysis detected increased expression of both insulin and glucagon, which are antagonists in blood sugar regulation, we analyzed blood glucose levels of KO and WT fish. Blood glucose levels were significantly higher in CYP1A‐KO fish than in WT fish (p < 0.01) (Figure 4). Blood glucose levels of KO fish correspond to those of human diabetic patients, higher than 54–120 mg/dL [38] or other fishes, higher than 40–90 mg/dL [39, 40] can be considered hyperglycemic.

Comparison of Liver Sizes of WT and KO Fish

As disorders in glucose metabolism were suggested, we compared liver morphology of CYP1A‐KO and WT fish (Figure 5; Figure S1). Liver lengths and weights revealed that livers of CYP1A‐KO fish were significantly larger (Figure 5A,B). These differences were not due to differences in body size because total lengths and body weights did not differ significantly between WT and KO fish (Figure 5C,D). In addition, livers of KO fish appeared more irregularly shaped (Figure S1), and adipose tissues were observed on their surface (Figures S1 and S2). Lipid analyses of the liver indicated that triglyceride (TG) content in the liver of KO fish was significantly higher than that of WT whereas phospholipid (PL) levels were similar (Figure S3). TG/PL ratio was not significantly different but tended to be higher in CYP1A‐KO fish (p = 0.075, Figure S3).

Numbers of pairs that spawned eggs and fertilization rates of spawned eggs between WT and CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus were compared (Figure 7). From 15 pairs in each group, 12 pairs actively produced eggs in WT and seven in KO (Figure 7A). Then, eggs obtained from three actively spawned pairs in each group were checked for fertilization. Most eggs were successfully fertilized in the WT group, unlike the KO group, which contained a high number of unfertilized eggs which failed to develop (Figure 7B).

Discussion

CYP1A is thought to be important in oxidative metabolism of various exogenous and endogenous compounds, mainly lipophilic compounds. In our previous study, we established CYP1A‐knockout O. javanicus , which was confirmed to have a four‐base deletion in its CYP1A gene sequence, and demonstrated a difference in sensitivity to two exogenous compounds, phenanthrene and pyrene [4]. However, the effects of gene knockout on endogenous metabolites had not been examined yet, though the loss of CYP1A is expected to cause enormous changes in fish physiology. In this study, we attempted to understand CYP1A function by comparing transcripts in the liver of KO and WT fish. Transcriptome analysis detected a number of DEGs in KO fish liver (Figure 1). An expectation before starting analyses was influences on genes related to xenobiotic metabolisms. Actually, a decrease in xenobiotic metabolism was suggested by GO and pathway analyses (Figures 2 and 3). However, the most remarkable effects involved increased expression of genes related to glucose regulation. Upregulation of the insulin gene (INS) and those involved in its release through calcium release (RYR2 and GRIN2A) suggests an increased insulin level. However, it is puzzling that the glucagon gene (GLUC1), which antagonizes insulin, and genes of somatostatin (SMS1) and its receptor (SST2), which inhibit the secretion of both hormones, were also upregulated.

To unravel this relationship, we compared fish blood glucose levels (Figure 4) from both groups. We found significantly higher blood glucose levels of CYP1A‐KO fish, classifying them as hyperglycemic. Although the function of CYP1A in glucose regulation has not been well established, it has been discussed in relation to an aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) that regulates CYP1A gene expression [41]. Moreover, CYP1A‐KO fish exhibited hepatomegaly (Figure 5 and Figure S1), fragility of the liver, and high triglyceride levels (Figure S3). Differences in liver mass and size were unrelated to non‐significant differences in fish total length and weight (Figure 5C,D). High blood glucose levels despite increased insulin expression suggest that CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus is insulin resistant, as frequently observed in Type 2 diabetes mellitus [42]. Increased blood glucagon levels are also frequently observed in diabetes patients [43]. Therefore, CYP1A‐KO fish are considered to be in a diabetic condition. In mice, knockout of the CYP1A2 gene, which is the major CYP1A isoform in the liver, causes changes in expression levels of genes related to insulin action, lipogenesis, fatty acid, and cholesterol biosynthetic pathways [44]. Therefore, the involvement of CYP1A in insulin action seems common in vertebrates, and the present results provide deeper insights into the CYP1A function. In mammals, CYP1A2 is also suggested to be involved in diseases including diabetes by interacting with heme oxygenase‐1 [45], although its gene was not detected as a DEG in the present study.

Possible mechanisms responsible for insulin resistance are upregulation of the PI3K‐Akt pathway and cell adhesion molecules (Figure 3A). The PI3K‐Akt pathway is essential to mediate signal transfer for various cellular functions, such as protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and glucose homeostasis [46, 47]. In the liver, the PI3K‐Akt pathway regulates hepatic glucose production [48]. Direct involvement of this pathway in insulin regulation and insulin resistance has been demonstrated in multiple studies in mammals [49, 50, 51] and fish liver [52, 53].

Knockout of CYP1A downregulates genes related to the metabolism of important polyunsaturated acids such as linoleic acid (Figure 3A) and reduces activity related to inositol phosphate metabolism (Figure 3B). Cyps are involved in the generation of lipid signaling mediators, including inositol phosphates (IPs) and lipid‐derived metabolites that are highly charged [54]. IPs are small molecule messengers and participate in various activities that involve signal transduction, such as the activation of Akt, calcium homeostasis, adhesion, and metabolism of steroid hormones [55, 56].

In this study, CYP1A was not detected among DEGs, suggesting that the gene harboring the deletion is still transcribed. Neither AHR nor ARNT appeared in the DEG list. In addition, transcription of the CYP1B gene, which is also regulated by AHR, showed minimal change (Table S3). These results suggest that transcriptional regulation of the CYP1A gene is not affected by deletion of its coding region, and that the KO strain is more suitable for elucidating downstream events caused by the absence of CYP1A rather than mechanisms regulating its expression. However, because CYP1A functions as a key effector of AHR signaling and represents only one branch of a broader transcriptional network, an important future direction will be to determine whether disruption of CYP1A affects other components of the AHR–CYP1A signaling system, including potential compensatory or feedback responses. Knockout of the AHR gene and the use of pharmacological AHR agonists or antagonists also represent promising future approaches for elucidating the entire regulatory network. It is also likely that CYP1A malfunction leads to stress in the ER, the main location of RYR2, which was upregulated in CYP1A‐KO fish. Although RYR2 functions mainly in cardiac tissues, its expression and potential impact are also notable in other tissues, such as liver and adrenal gland [57]. Increased Ca2+ uptake by mitochondria may also increase ROS production and ATP generation [58, 59]. The ROS levels of KO fish were found to be higher in the whole liver compared to the WT (Figure 6A), although it is not significant when normalized by milligrams of protein (Figure 6C). These results suggest that ROS levels elevate in KO fish due to increase in liver mass (Figure 5; Figure S1). Related study of CYP1A‐knockdown zebrafish proposed functions of CYP1A in oxidative metabolism and redox regulation, besides demonstrated inhibited growth and reduced hypoxia tolerance [60]. Functions of CYP1A in oxidative metabolism is an important future study using the KO strain.

In addition to dysfunction of sugar regulation, diminished reproductive fitness is also a major effect of CYP1A‐knockout O. javanicus ; reduced spawning pair numbers and fertilization rates were observed in CYP1A‐KO O. javanicus (Figure 7). Increased RYR2 activity enhances suramin binding (Table 1) [61] which subsequently affects acrosin binding (Table 2) and decreased efficiency of sperm penetration into the zona pellucida of eggs [62]. Expression of related genes, ZP1 and ZP3, was also significantly downregulated (Table 2 and Figure 1). A study by Yokokawa et al. [63] supported the importance of liver‐derived ZP proteins in formation of the egg envelope in a related medaka species, Oryzias latipes . The observed ZP1 and ZP3 reductions indicate potential disruptions in oocyte envelope formation and fertility. Further studies at protein levels are needed to clarify how reduced expression of the ZP proteins affect the reproduction processes of the CYP1A‐KO strain. i.e., Anyway, correlation of reduced reproductive capacity and diabetes have been suggested by He et al. [64], and CYP1A may link the two phenomena.

By comparative transcriptome analysis of CYP1A gene‐knockout O. javanicus with the normal WT strain, we found that CYP1A is deeply involved in homeostasis of sugar. The CYP1A‐knockout individual tends to have higher blood glucose and insulin levels compared to normal wild‐type individuals, indicating the diabetic‐like condition. Figure 8 illustrates the internal cellular processes that potentially occur in both groups. Thus, the CYP1A‐KO strain has potential as a model organism to understand diabetes, in addition to metabolism of drugs and chemical pollutants. In addition, we also detected that CYP1A contributes to reproductive processes, including gametogenesis and fertilization. For future research, we suggest conducting metabolomic studies to identify changes in metabolite profiles due to CYP1A knockout, not only in the liver but also other organs such as the pancreas and brain, which are involved in sugar metabolism, as well as gills and intestine, where CYP1A mRNA levels are second highest after the liver [3] and where tissues are exposed to exogenous compounds.
