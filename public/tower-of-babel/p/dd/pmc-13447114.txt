Synthetic image evolution for affective science
Authors: Darius Valevicius, Celine Haddad, Gurgen Soghoyan, Aurelio Cortese, Vincent Taschereau‐Dumouchel
Published: 2026

How does the brain generate the experience of emotions? Are the neural mechanisms of subjective emotional experience distinct from those governing autonomic and reflexive responses? Historically, defensive autonomic and behavioral responses have been considered central to emotions such as fear, often in relation to brain structures such as the amygdala. However, this close relationship between the experience of fear and measurable “fear” behaviors and physiological activation has recently been called into question. 1 , 2

To better dissociate the neural correlates of different components of emotion, it would be useful to have a method to drive the subjective and physiological processes in a targeted and independent way. Closed‐loop approaches are one such way of targeting specific patterns of neural activity. 3 , 4 In these experiments, data are analyzed in real‐time, and metrics derived from these data are used to change the experimental stimulus in order to produce a target outcome.

A recent innovative closed‐loop brain imaging experiment in macaque monkeys used generative AI models to produce images that maximized specific neural targets. 5 By iteratively selecting, recombining, and mutating the “latent embedding vectors” of generated images based on neural recordings, this method successfully converged to synthetic images that maximally activated neurons in higher visual areas. Importantly, this approach can map the ‘receptive fields’ or ‘tuning characteristics’ of neural targets in a manner similar to classical experiments mapping receptive fields in the primary visual cortex. 6

Here, we investigated whether this method could be leveraged in humans in order to study the neural mechanisms underlying different aspects of emotion, including subjective feelings and physiological responses. We present a proof‐of‐concept study in which we used self‐reported fear and physiological measurements of arousal as two different targets in an image evolution experiment.

Twenty participants were recruited to a pilot study where a series of AI‐generated images were presented, following which participants made ratings of subjective fear on a 10‐point scale (Fig. 1a,b). Simultaneously, measurements of skin conductance responses (SCRs) – a proxy for autonomic arousal– were collected using an Emotibit device. 7 Images were evolved using a simplified version of the Covariance Matrix Adaptation algorithm. 8 After each generation, the target measures were used to select, recombine, and mutate the latent embedding vectors of the images and produce a new generation (Fig. 1c). Altogether, we tested three conditions: self‐reported fear, SCRs, and a control condition where fitness values were randomly generated.

(d) Average fear ratings per generation for three conditions. Thin transparent lines show individual subject trajectories; bold lines with error bars show group mean trajectories. Error bars = 95% CI. (e) Mean fear ratings by generation in the SCR condition for subjects with positive and negative concordance values. Interpretation of lines and error bars is identical to (d). (f) Density plots of the images resulting from the three conditions. (g) Example images for a typical subject.

Images were generated using the Stable unCLIP model. 9 We found that, when evolving images in CLIP's latent space, the generated images failed to converge on target categories. To address this, we used Principal Components Analysis (PCA) on the embeddings of 2700 animal images to define a subdomain of the model's latent space. Through this method, we were able to generate images on a coherent “animal manifold” without the need for fine‐tuned models and were able to rapidly converge on target categories within six generations.

We found that targeting self‐reported fear resulted in images that were far more subjectively frightening than the SCR condition (d = 0.84, P < 0.001) and the control condition (d = 0.87, P < 0.001), while images generated during the SCR condition were, on average, not more frightening than controls (Fig. 1d). In addition, the occurrence of SCRs did not increase in any condition, possibly due to habituation effects. Nonetheless, we found that the self‐report and SCR conditions converged on similar image distributions (r = 0.69, q < 0.001), with the self‐report condition being relatively more dissimilar to the control condition (r = 0.27, q = 0.18) and the SCR condition being more similar to controls (r = 0.64, q < 0.001; Fig. 1f).

By measuring the degree of correlation between fear ratings and SCRs across subjects, we saw that the mean correlation between the two measures was near zero (d = 0.0, P = 0.97). This correlation, however, varied between subjects in an apparently trait‐like manner. We found that subjective fear‐SCR concordance could explain whether participants reported elevated fear in the SCR‐target condition (r = 0.49, P = 0.027; Fig. 1e), but could not significantly explain the similarity in the resulting image distributions (r = 0.43, P = 0.059).

Having completed this proof‐of‐concept study, we are now applying this method to study the tuning characteristics of patterns of neural activity in fMRI, such as a subjective fear decoder and an extended amygdala network. We hope that this method can continue to produce insights about the mechanisms of the subjective and physiological components of emotions such as fear, in order to better understand mood and anxiety disorders.

Figure S1. (a) The experimental setup consisted of a computer for stimulus presentation and image generation, as well as a wirelessly connected Emotibit device attached to the left index finger. (b) In a single trial of the experiment, an image was presented for 3 s, preceded and followed by a 1‐s fixation. Afterwards, participants made a fear rating on a visual analog scale. (c) The structure of the full experiment. A generation consisted of 9 images, and each condition (self‐report, SCR, and control) consisted of 10 generations. The order of the conditions was randomized per subject. (d) The progression of image embeddings in a latent space as a result of the evolutionary algorithm (taken from one self‐report run) to illustrate the progression of the synthetic stimuli in the latent space. Starting from a wider distribution, the images should converge on a preferred stimulus for the target metric.

Figure S2. Analysis of dimensionality reduction effects on unClip image generation and representation stability. (A) Schematic of the re‐encoding process: original images were embedded using CLIP, compressed via PCA to various target dimensions, reconstructed back to 1024 dimensions using inverse PCA, then processed by the unClip model to generate new images, which were re‐encoded. (B) Euclidean distances between original and re‐encoded embeddings across PCA sizes. Contrary to initial expectations, higher‐dimensional reconstructions did not reduce distance; instead, smaller distances were associated with lower PCA dimensions that preserved more image complexity (C) Robustness of image generation to Gaussian noise added to PCA embeddings prior to reconstruction. Intermediate PCA dimensions (40–150) showed highest stability across noise perturbations. (D) Impact of PCA compression on downstream semantic decoding: Lasso regression performance predicting categorical fear levels from PCA‐reduced embeddings. Predictive accuracy declined at very low PCA sizes, stabilizing after size 40.

Figure S3. (a) The results of a simulated version of the image evolution algorithm. Each category evolution was run once, with a line representing the average logit score per generation. On average (black line), the logit scores plateaued after about 6 generations. Error bars = 95% CI. (b) Some categories appear systematically easier to evolve than others. For one of the better‐performing classes from the first sim (Cockroach, orange), and one of the worst‐performing classes (Whale, blue), we ran additional sets of 10 evolution runs. At least in the context of Google ViT as an observer, the algorithm consistently achieved higher logit scores for cockroaches than for whales. Error bars = 95% CI.

Figure S4. (a) The average fear ratings per generation for the three conditions, with fear ratings Z‐scored within subject. Only the self‐reported fear condition produced images that were significantly different from the control condition. Transparent lines show individual subject trajectories, while opaque lines show group average trajectories. Error bars = 95% CI. (b) Average SCR scores by generation for each condition. No significant differences or interactions were observed. Transparent and opaque lines are to be interpreted as in (a). Error bars = 95% CI. (c) The five categories with the highest average fear ratings based on the pre‐experiment screening questionnaire (see Supplementary Materials). (d–f) Synthetic images were classified into one of 30 natural image categories based on their CLIP embeddings using a K Nearest Neighbors algorithm. The top five predicted classes at the end of each condition (last three generations) are shown.

Figure S5. (a) Kernel density estimates for the images in the last three generations of each condition are plotted over a two‐dimensional space derived via multidimensional scaling (MDS) of CLIP embeddings. (b) Pearson correlations between the predicted class frequencies for the three conditions, as well as the mean fear rating per category in the pre‐screening questionnaire (“Mean fears”). (c) Predicted class frequencies of the synthetic images from the last three generations, across all conditions. The top five classes for each condition are depicted in darker colors.

Figure S6. (a) Example images for a typical subject. The first image (i.e. the dummy image generated from the mean embedding of the previous generation) from each generation after the first (x‐axis) is displayed, for each condition (y‐axis). In this instance, the self‐report condition generated threatening insectoid creatures, while the SCR condition produced ambiguous, brightly‐colored birdlike or insectoid creatures, and the control condition produced equine and feline mammals. (b) Another subject for whom average fear ratings were low, but SCR activity was high. This subject was exceptional in having SCR‐generated images that appeared much more frightening than self‐report‐generated images.

Figure S7. (a) Permutation test of between‐condition reliability of fear‐SCR concordance scores. The concordance scores for all subjects and conditions were shuffled 10,000 times, and the average deviations from the subject means were compared to the actual data. (b) Distribution of concordance scores by condition (F(2) = 1.44, P = 0.24). (c) Mean fear ratings by generation in the SCR condition for subjects with concordance values above zero (blue) and below zero (red). (d) Correlation between concordance and the change in fear ratings between the first generation (all conditions) and the last three generations of the SCR condition. The gray dotted line represents the y‐axis mean of the same metric for the control condition (also for (e)). (e) Correlation between concordance scores and the mean euclidean distance between the SCR‐evolved and self‐report‐evolved images for each subject. (f) Correlation between concordance scores and age.

Figure S8. Flowchart illustration of the evolutionary algorithm. This represents the code that is executed in the generator.py script in the GitHub repository.

Figure S9. Average values of skin conductance responses (SCR), heartrate (HR), electrodermal activity/level (EDA), and skin temperature (Temp) for each generation of the experiment. The readings streamed in real‐time from the Emotibit for the four variables were averaged within each stimulus presentation window (0–4s post stimulus onset). All variables were z‐scored within‐subject before plotting. No significant effects of condition or condition * generation interaction effects were observed using the repeated measures ANOVA models. Error bars = 95% CI.
