Intraoperative Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound Versus Traditional Intraoperative Ultrasound for Detecting Lung Nodules During Robotic-assisted Thoracic Surgery
Authors: Gaetana Messina, Giuseppe Vicario, Vincenzo Di Filippo, Davide Gerardo Pica, Beatrice Leonardi, Noemi Maria Giorgiano, Angela Iovine, Riccardo Vinciguerra, Francesca Capasso, Paolo Chiodini, Annafrancesca Smimmo, Giovanni Vicidomini, Alfonso Fiorelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

INTRODUCTION

Intraoperative identification of solitary pulmonary nodules (SPNs) during minimally invasive surgery still represents a difficult challenge, especially when the lesions are sub-centrimetric and deeply located.1,2 Intraoperative ultrasound (Io-US) has been reported as a diagnostic tool for detection of SPNs during video assisted thoracic surgery (VATS),3–8 but its use during robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) remains limited to a few case series.9,10 The lack of experience with US by surgeons to detect lesions and the fear of increasing operative time are possible explanations. However, the development of new ultrasound probe system dedicated for robotic surgery and the use of a contrast-enhancing agent may facilitate the visualization of lung nodules and reduce procedural time.11

Herein, we evaluated the feasibility and the diagnostic accuracy of intraoperative-contrast enhanced ultrasonography (Io-CEUS) in comparison to traditional Io-US to detect SPNs during RATS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

It was a retrospective single-centre study performed in the thoracic surgery unit of the University of Campania Luigi Vanvitelli and included all consecutive patients undergoing RATS for management of SPNs from May 2024 to June 2025. All data were recorded in a dedicated database and then extracted and evaluated retrospectively. The clinical data of patients (i) with indeterminate SPN; (ii) with nodules smaller than 10 mm; and (iii) with nodules located distant from the pleura were included in the analysis. We excluded the data of patients (i) with peripheral nodules; (ii) with nodules larger than 10 mm; (iii) with definite preoperative diagnosis; and (iv) undergoing thoracoscopy or upfront thoracotomy.

In all cases, the nodules were first evaluated using traditional Io-US and served as the control group (Io-US group); if Io-US failed to detect the lesion, then a contrast-enhancing agent was administered (Io-CEUS group). The first end point of the study was to evaluate whether the use of a contrast-enhancing agent improved the diagnostic accuracy of traditional US to detect the lesion. Then, the operative and perioperative outcomes between the 2 study groups were also compared to assess whether CEUS affected operative outcomes (secondary end point of the study).

This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by local ethics committees of the University of Campania Luigi Vanvitelli (protocol number 18296/2025). All patients gave written informed consent for the treatment, and the data were anonymously used for scientific purposes only.

Study population

In all cases SPNs was diagnosed by whole body positron emission tomography–computed tomography (CT). The lesion size and the distance from the pleura were measured by CT scans. Patients were reviewed for a non-surgical biopsy (ie, CT-guided biopsy and/or transbronchial needle aspiration biopsy were performed. Patients without a preoperative diagnosis of malignancy were scheduled for RATS oncological resection, including wedge resection for the diagnosis and treatment of suspected lung malignancies based on the current recommendations.13,14

Variables

US findings

All lesions were detected based on the following standardized US parameters before and after the use of contrast-enhancing agents: echogenicity, sharpness of the edges and presence of necrotic areas or calcifications.

Surgical outcomes

Operative time (min), output drainage (mL), length of chest drainage (days), length of hospital stay (days) and major complications based on the Clavien-Dindo system were recorded.

Intraoperative US procedure

All patients underwent RATS under general anaesthesia and single-lung ventilation. A standard 4-arm approach was used for the procedure. All procedures were performed by the same thoracic surgeon with experience in thoracic US. A newly developed ultrasound probe (L51K Robotic Probe, 15 MHz, 13 mm Linear Probe) dedicated for da Vinci Systems (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) and a diagnostic colour Doppler US scanning device were used. The probe was inserted into the chest through the assistant port and fixed with a bipolar clamp to scan the pulmonary surface. The surgeon at the console moved the probe and scanned lung parenchyma, taking into account the CT anatomical landmarks for nodule detection. After the lesion was identified, the images obtained from using the probe were reported on the console screen and the US findings were recorded.

In the case of a clearly evident hypoechoic lesion (Video 1), CEUS was not performed; the nodule surface was scared by bipolar forceps and removed in a standard manner by wedge resection. Then, the specimen was sent to pathologists for frozen section analysis.

If the lesion was isoechoic (and not poorly detected, then 2.4-5 ml of a contrast-enhancing agent (SonoVue, Bracco, Milan, Italy) followed by 10-20 ml of NaCl were administered. The lesion was evaluated by CEUS for at least 3 min, and any changes in relation to vascularization were recorded. During the “wash-in” phase, the contrast agent entered the lesion and enhanced it, whereas in the “wash-out” phase, the contrast agent left the lesion, thereby becoming less evident (Video 2). After the lesion was identified, it was resected and sent to pathologists for frozen section analysis as reported above.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and ranges (min, max) and compared between 2 study groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test. The diagnostic accuracy of Io-US and of Io-CEUS to detect lesion was compared using the McNemar test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria, version 4.4.1). A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Our study population included 33 patients; 8 of these underwent a preoperative non-surgical biopsy that was inconclusive for a definitive diagnosis of malignancy; the other 25 patients were considered not suitable for a less invasive biopsy and were scheduled for an upfront surgical biopsy. In all cases, the lesions were detected intraoperatively and successfully resected. After frozen section analysis, 31 out of 33 (94%) lesions were diagnosed as primary lung cancer, and a lobectomy with lymph node resection was performed; the remaining 2 lesions (6%) were metastases from breast cancer and colon carcinoma, respectively; because the surgical margins were free from tumour cells, only lymph-adenectomies were performed.

DISCUSSION

The increasingly widespread use of low-dose CT scans in lung cancer screening and in the follow-up of patients with lung disease has concomitantly increased the detection rate of SPNs. The diagnosis is still challenging, and VATS resection remains the main strategy when a less invasive biopsy fails to obtain a definitive diagnosis.1,2 RATS has become an alternative to thoracoscopy for management of lung nodules because of the availability of several advantages such as 3D operative vision, lack of tremor filtration and wristed instruments. Despite all, the detection of small, deeply located nodules may be more difficult during RATS than during VATS due to the lack of tactile feedback. Several approaches14–16 have been used over the years to facilitate the intraoperative detection of lung nodules, but each approach presents advantages and limitations, and the best strategy is still debated. The preoperative marking of a lung nodule with staining methods or using a foreign body such as a hook wire or a microcoil may be associated with an increased risk of complications (ie, pneumothorax, haemoptysis) and is unsuitable for a lesion localized close to mediastinal structures. More sophisticated techniques such as bronchoscopy-guided placement of markers or electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy can overcome these limitations, but they require specific equipment not widely available. Io-US has been used to locate SPN since the 1990s during VATS with a detection rate that ranged from 76% to 100%.17 Nevertheless, its use during RATS is limited to case series,9,10 probably due to the difficulty encountered by surgeons with limited experience interpreting thoracic US findings. CEUS has changed the world of US, reducing the US artifacts and facilitating the detection of lesions. It has been used to guide transthoracic US lung biopsies18,19 and intraoperatively to define the resection margins during hepatic or thyroid operations17 or to detect lung nodules during VATS.20 However, no researchers have investigated whether Io-CEUS added significant information to standard Io-US for detecting lung nodules during RATS, as occurred in this study.

Our results indicated that Io-CEUS significantly improved the rate at which lung nodules were detected compared to that at which traditional Io-US were detected. Io-US correctly identified hypoechoic nodules while missed isoechoic lesions were then correctly identified after administration of a contrast agent. The different US characteristics of the lesions explained these results. Hypoechoic lesions appeared as well-defined solid nodules, darker than surrounding lung parenchyma and thus easily detected by Io-US, also without the evaluation of blood flow. Conversely, isoechoic lesions had the same echogenicity of surrounding lung parenchyma without defined boundaries; thus they were difficult to see using traditional Io-US. CEUS assessed in real time the vascularization of the lesion; the enhancement phase increased the difference of impedance between the nodule and the lung parenchyma that facilitated the identification of the lesion. Similarly, Wang et al.18 and Di Vece et al.19 found that assessment with CEUS before a transbronchial biopsy increased the detectable rate of lesions hidden in atelectasis of the lungs compared to traditional US.

Despite the use of contrast enhancing agents, Io-CEUS remained a safe procedure because no complications were observed during and after the operation. Io-CEUS required almost 3 min, representing the mean “wash out” time,17 but it did not significantly affect the whole operative time of resection. However, Io-CEUS should not be routinely performed for identifying lung nodules, but the lesion should first be evaluated with standard Io-US and, if detected (ie, hypoechoic lesion), it should be directly resected without additional evaluation. In this clinical scenario, the administration of a contrast agent was only a time-wasting procedure that increased the health costs without adding significant clinical advantages. On the other hand, if the nodule was poorly defined by Io-US (ie, isoechoic lesion), then CEUS should be performed because the enhancement phase obtained using the blood flow analysis facilitated the detection of the lesion.

Compared to other strategies for nodule detection, Io-US offers several advantages such as the possibility of obtaining images in real-time without needing sophisticated equipment such as a hybrid operating room and without risk of complications such as pneumothorax, haemothorax and lung haemorrhage. On the other hand, the presence of air within the lung parenchyma could act as a barrier to ultrasound waves and limit the ability of US to visualize structures, especially in emphysematous patients. To overcome this limit, from a technical point of view, we suggested the suction of the airways during the procedure and pressing the visceral pleura with the probe to deflate the lung. Furthermore, the use of a saline solution could improve the adhesion of the US probe with lung parenchyma, enhancing the transduction of ultrasound waves and limiting the artifacts. Because US was operator-dependent, the results depended heavily on the skill of the surgeons. Thus, the first procedures performed by surgeons with limited experience with US should be supervised by radiologists or by thoracic surgeons with US skills until all surgeons on the surgical team have mastered the basic skills of US, generally acquired after 50 procedures.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered before drawing definitive conclusions. (i) The results could be affected by the retrospective nature and the small sample size of the study. (ii) All lesions were malignant, and it was unclear whether CEUS could also be useful for the detection of benign lesions due to different vascular profiles. (iii) All US evaluations were performed by the same thoracic surgeon with US experience to overcome the interobserver variability; thus the reproducibility of our results by other surgeons with different US experience was unclear.

CONCLUSION

Our preliminary results support the use of Io-CEUS as an additional tool to help thoracic surgeons to detect small, deeply located lung nodules during the RATS procedure. The administration of a contrast-enhancing agent could be useful, especially in the case of an isoechoic lesion, which was poorly defined by traditional Io-US. Our results, although encouraging, should be corroborated by future larger prospective, randomized studies.
