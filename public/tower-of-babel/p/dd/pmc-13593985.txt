Maternal Isocaloric Monosaccharide Diets Reorganize Dorsal–Ventral Hippocampal Maturation and Ventral Plasticity in Rat Offspring
Authors: Kacper Witek, Karolina Wydra, Luca Bonfanti, Małgorzata Filip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early-life nutrition is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of brain developmental trajectories. Because the dorsal (dHIP) and ventral (vHIP) hippocampus regulate distinct cognitive and emotional functions, understanding how maternal diet shapes maturation along this axis may provide insight into later behavioral vulnerability. However, the impact of maternal diet on the spatial organization of hippocampal maturation along the dorsal–ventral axis remains unclear. Here, we tested whether maternal isocaloric monosaccharide diets containing glucose (GLU) or fructose (FRU) are associated with age-, sex-, and region-dependent molecular remodeling along the dorsal–ventral axis of the hippocampus. In an established Wistar rat cohort, we quantified mRNA expression of genes associated with neuronal maturation and plasticity (Bdnf, Dcx, Gfap, Nes, Rbfox3) and protein levels of DCX, GFAP, and NeuN in the dHIP and vHIP of dams and male and female offspring during adolescence (PND28) and young adulthood (PND63). In dams, FRU exposure was associated with modestly increased Bdnf expression, and both GLU and FRU diets were associated with region-dependent differences in DCX, GFAP, and NeuN protein levels. In offspring, perinatal exposure to GLU or FRU diets induced sex-dependent patterns of hippocampal gene expression. Diet-related molecular differences were more prominent in the dHIP during adolescence, whereas additional vHIP-associated alterations emerged in young adulthood. Several proteins exhibited region-dependent differences, with the vHIP generally displaying higher protein abundance, although diet-induced alterations differed between hippocampal subregions. Guided by PCA and a composite RNA–protein molecular index highlighting persistent molecular remodeling within the vHIP, subsequent analyses focused on the vHIP in male offspring. Targeted analysis of the male vHIP at PND63 revealed that maternal FRU exposure was associated with increased Mc4r expression and elevated MC4R protein levels, whereas both maternal GLU and FRU exposure were associated with increased ARC protein levels. In contrast, maternal monosaccharide exposure was associated with reduced Npas4 expression, and maternal FRU exposure was additionally associated with reduced NPAS4 protein levels. Collectively, maternal monosaccharide diets were associated with age-, sex-, and region-dependent remodeling of hippocampal maturation- and plasticity-related molecular profiles. Introduction

The prenatal and early postnatal periods represent critical windows of brain plasticity, during which environmental factors can influence long-lasting changes in brain structure, function, and behavior (Barker 2007; Gluckman et al. 2008, 2016). Maternal nutrition represents one of the most influential modulators of this developmental trajectory (Margolis and Gabard-Durnam 2025). Dietary exposure to monosaccharides derived from added sugars, including glucose (GLU) and fructose (FRU), has increased and remains high in many populations (Russell et al. 2023; Walton et al. 2023). Although GLU and FRU provide equivalent caloric value, they differ substantially in their metabolic and neuroendocrine effects (Qi and Tester 2019; Merino et al. 2020). Compared with GLU, FRU induces weaker insulin- and satiety-related signaling and differentially modulates brain regions involved in reward, motivation, and cognition (Page et al. 2013; Luo et al. 2015; Page and Melrose 2016). Emerging evidence further suggests that FRU exposure may impair hippocampal synaptic plasticity and hippocampus-dependent function (Cisternas et al. 2015; Merino et al. 2020). Observational studies in pregnancy cohorts have linked higher maternal intake of sugar-rich foods and sugar-sweetened beverages to poorer cognitive and behavioral outcomes in offspring, including impairments in memory and nonverbal intelligence, as well as increased externalizing and attention-related behaviors (Jacka et al. 2013; Steenweg-de Graaff et al. 2014; Cohen et al. 2018). Given its high energetic demands and central role in integrating stress and metabolic signals, the hippocampus may be particularly vulnerable to prenatal monosaccharide exposure (Maniam et al. 2016; Jacques et al. 2019).

The hippocampus is highly sensitive to early-life perturbations due to its prolonged postnatal maturation, sustained maturational and plastic capacities, high metabolic demands, and central role in learning, memory, emotional regulation, and stress responsivity (Poppenk et al. 2013). A defining feature of hippocampal organization is its long-axis functional specialization. In rodents, the dorsal hippocampus (dHIP) primarily supports the processing of spatial and cognitive memory, whereas the ventral hippocampus (vHIP) is more closely linked to emotional behavior, anxiety, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis regulation (Fanselow and Dong 2010; Strange et al. 2014). Beyond long-axis functional specialization, hippocampal maturation is influenced by experience-dependent circuit remodeling during postnatal development, potentially biasing the organization of maturation programs along the dorsal–ventral axis (Cushman et al. 2021). Human neuroimaging and developmental studies demonstrate a comparable anterior-posterior organization, with posterior regions contributing to high-resolution memory and anterior regions involved in affective and motivational processes (Poppenk et al. 2013; Nichols et al. 2023). These subdivisions differ in connectivity, gene-expression profiles, and neurogenic niche properties (Anacker and Hen 2017), suggesting region-specific vulnerability to early dietary exposure. Importantly, hippocampal maturation also exhibits marked sex-dependent organization across the dorsal–ventral axis. Experimental studies have demonstrated sex-specific differences in hippocampal neurogenesis, inflammatory signaling, and plasticity-related processes across development, suggesting differential vulnerability to environmental perturbations between males and females (Yagi and Galea 2018; Hodges et al. 2022).

Hippocampal maturation is commonly assessed using molecular markers associated with neurogenic maturation, neuronal differentiation, and structural plasticity, including glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), nestin (NES), doublecortin (DCX), and neuronal nuclear antigen (NeuN) (Denoth-Lippuner and Jessberger 2021; Simard et al. 2025). Importantly, these markers do not serve as a binary index of neuronal birth. In many mammalian species, DCX-positive cells label immature, non-dividing neurons that persist into adulthood and contribute to structural plasticity rather than ongoing neurogenesis, highlighting heterogeneity in postnatal brain maturation and a continuum of maturational and plastic states (Bonfanti and Peretto 2011; La Rosa et al. 2020; Benedetti et al. 2023).

Beyond structural markers, activity-dependent regulators provide functional insight into hippocampal circuit maturation. Activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein (ARC) and neuronal PAS domain protein 4 (NPAS4) are immediate early gene products that couple neuronal activity to transcriptional programs governing synaptic plasticity and circuit refinement (Lin et al. 2008; Zhang et al. 2013). Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) acts downstream of these activity-dependent pathways and plays a central role in dendritic maturation, synaptic stabilization, and long-term plasticity within the hippocampus (Lu et al. 2014). Together, these molecules provide sensitive readouts of how early-life environments shape hippocampal functional maturation.

Preclinical studies indicate that maternal FRU exposure interferes with hippocampal development. Perinatal FRU intake has been associated with impairments in hippocampus-dependent learning and memory, as well as reduced Bdnf expression and altered neurotrophic regulation (Wu et al. 2016; Yamazaki et al. 2018). Other studies report structural and cellular alterations in the offspring hippocampus, including neuronal loss, glial activation, and transcriptional dysregulation of pathways involved in neuronal differentiation and synaptic maturation (Erbas et al. 2018; Zhong et al. 2023). Emerging evidence further suggests that early-life FRU exposure may disrupt microglia-mediated developmental processes during critical postnatal windows (Wang et al. 2025). Experimental evidence further indicates that postnatal FRU and sucrose exposure induce sex-dependent alterations in hippocampal plasticity and cognition, with several studies suggesting greater vulnerability in males (Abbott et al. 2016; Kloster et al. 2021; Hyer et al. 2022). However, most available maternal dietary studies rely on liquid sugar supplementation and non-isocaloric designs, whereas postnatal studies often focus on a single sex and analyze whole-hippocampus homogenates.

An additional pathway of interest is the melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R), a metabolically sensitive G-protein-coupled receptor expressed not only in hypothalamic nuclei but also in extra-hypothalamic regions, including the hippocampus and postnatal neural precursor populations (Kishi et al. 2003; Carniglia et al. 2024). MC4R signaling has been implicated in hippocampal plasticity (Caruso et al. 2014) and emotional regulation through activity- and metabolism-dependent pathways, linking metabolic state to hippocampal plasticity-related mechanisms (DiFeliceantonio and Kenny 2018). Despite growing evidence that maternal dietary sugars influence offspring brain development, it remains unclear whether maternal isocaloric monosaccharide exposure shapes offspring brain development and postnatal maturation along the dorsal–ventral axis in a sex-dependent manner, and how changes in gene expression relate to protein-level maturation profiles across postnatal development. Here, we examined gene expression (Bdnf, Dcx, Gfap, Nes, Rbfox3) and protein levels of DCX, GFAP, and NeuN in the dHIP and vHIP of dams and male and female offspring at PND28 and PND63 following maternal GLU or FRU diets. Using multivariate integration approaches, we characterized region-, sex-, and age-specific maturation signatures and subsequently examined activity-dependent regulators (Arc, Npas4) and MC4R-related molecular pathways in the male vHIP. We therefore hypothesized that maternal isocaloric monosaccharide exposure is associated with differential age-, sex-, and region-dependent alterations in hippocampal maturation-related molecular profiles along the dorsal–ventral axis.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals, Maternal Diets, and Tissue Collection

Brain tissues analyzed in the present study were obtained from dams and their offspring (male and female) at adolescence (postnatal day 28, PND28) and young adulthood (PND63) from a previously characterized cohort of Wistar rats in which maternal body weight and food intake during pregnancy and lactation, litter characteristics, pups’ body weight, as well as metabolic parameters and MC4R signaling had already been described (Witek et al. 2025). The present analyses addressed a distinct biological question by examining spatially resolved hippocampal molecular maturation and plasticity at both the RNA and protein levels. Use of this established cohort enabled the reuse of biological material and minimized additional animal use in accordance with the 3Rs principles.

Wistar rats were obtained from a licensed breeder (Charles River, Sulzfeld, Germany) and housed in standard transparent plastic rodent cages in an animal breeding room under standard conditions (21 ± 2 °C; 40 ± 10% humidity; 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to water and VRF1 chow (Special Diets Services, London, UK; Cat. No. 801900; hereafter referred to as SD). Twenty-four virgin female Wistar rats (201–225 g), after a 14-day acclimatization period and during the proestrus phase of the estrous cycle (determined by daily vaginal smears), were mated with six randomly assigned males (226–250 g). After sperm presence was confirmed in vaginal smears, females (n = 8 dams per group) were randomly assigned to one of three maternal isocaloric solid diets: an SD or two custom-formulated synthetic diets based on the SD composition, in which the starch–sucrose component was selectively replaced with either glucose (GLU) or fructose (FRU), as previously described (Witek et al. 2023, 2025). During pregnancy (21 ± 2 days) and lactation (21 ± 2 days), dams were given ad libitum access to water and food (administered by feeder). On PND2, litter size was recorded, mean pup body weight per litter was measured, and litters were standardized to 10 pups whenever possible, as previously characterized (Witek et al. 2025). At weaning (PND21), offspring were separated by sex, individually marked, transitioned to an SD, and housed in standard transparent plastic cages (five animals per cage; 38 × 59 × 20 cm) until PND28 or PND63.

Dams (at weaning; n = 8 dams per group) and naïve offspring (PND28 and PND63; n = 8 rats per group) were euthanized by rapid decapitation without prior anesthesia to avoid molecular confounds, 3 h after lights-on, in accordance with ethical approvals. To minimize litter effects, only one male and one female offspring per litter were randomly selected using a computer-generated randomization schedule at PND28 and PND63, as previously described (Witek et al. 2025). The same animals were subsequently used for molecular and neurochemical analyses. Brains were rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold saline to facilitate dissection. The hippocampus was bilaterally dissected under cold conditions using razor blades and separated into dorsal (dHIP) and ventral (vHIP) subdivisions along the septo-temporal axis based on anatomical landmarks and the characteristic curvature of the hippocampal formation, as described previously (Ropireddy et al. 2012). The intermediate hippocampal subdivision was not analyzed separately, and tissues were classified into dHIP and vHIP domains (Jaszczyk et al. 2022). All tissues (Table 1) were snap-frozen on dry ice and stored at − 80 °C until molecular analyses. Samples were coded before analyses whenever possible; however, investigators were not fully blinded to group allocation during all experimental procedures and data analysis. Sample sizes were determined based on previous studies using the same maternal dietary paradigm and assessing comparable molecular, neurochemical, and behavioral outcomes (Witek et al. 2023, 2025, 2026); therefore, no a priori power analysis was performed.

Molecular Analyses

RNA Extraction

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, a frozen brain tissue fragment (20–50 mg) was collected from each sample before protein homogenization and then processed for RNA isolation. Each tissue was homogenized in the presence of a phenol solution using bead-beater tubes (zirconia/silica beads; A&A Biotechnology, Gdynia, Poland; Cat. No. K-PKCM-100) in the TissueLyser III (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Next, the lysates were incubated at 50 °C for 5 min and mixed with chloroform. Then, the lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was transferred to the MagnifiQ™ 16 Total RNA Plus instant kit plate (A&A Biotechnology; Cat. No. 614 A-16 V-256) and total RNA was isolated automatically using the magnetic Auto-Pure Mini device (A&A Biotechnology). The total RNA concentration was measured using a NanoPhotometer® NP80 (IMPLEN, Munich, Germany), and the absorbance ratio at 260/280 nm was used to assess sample purity.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR) Analyses

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania; Cat. No. 4368813) in a T100™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., California, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the QuantStudio™ 3 system (Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Foster City, CA, USA), TaqMan® Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems™, Vilnius, Lithuania; Cat. No. 4369016), and TaqMan® Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems™) for the target genes listed in Table 2. PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. ΔCT values were calculated by normalizing target gene CT values to the housekeeping gene 18 S rRNA and were used for subsequent statistical analyses. Expression stability of the 18 S rRNA was verified across diet, sex, age, and hippocampal region groups, and no systematic differences in CT values were detected. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative CT (2^−ΔΔCT) method (Schmittgen and Livak 2008) for graphical presentation only. Fold change values were normalized to the mean ΔCT value of the corresponding control (SD) group (set to 1.0).

Neurochemical Analyses

Tissue Homogenization

Frozen hippocampus tissue was disrupted and homogenized using ceramic beads in TissueLyser III (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; TaKaRa Bio Inc., Cat. No. T9181, Shiga, Japan) supplemented with a cOmplete™ (Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany, Cat. No. 11697498001). Next, the homogenate was centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 14,000 × g. The resulting supernatants were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat. No. 23225) at a wavelength of 562 nm, measured in the Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Following the manufacturer’s protocol, the protein levels of doublecortin (DCX; ng/L; BT LAB, Zhejiang, China, Cat. No. E2904Ra), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; pg/mL; BT LAB, Cat. No. E0538Ra), and neuronal nuclear antigen (NeuN; ng/L; BT LAB, Cat. No. E1594Ra) in the vHIP and dHIP of dams and offspring were quantified using the ELISA method. Briefly, each sample (40 µL) and standards were applied in duplicate into 96-well ELISA plates. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer. The protein concentration was calculated from a standard curve and expressed as ng/mg of protein for analysis.

Electrophoresis and Western Blotting

Protein samples from the vHIP of male offspring at PND28 and PND63 were prepared for Western blotting. Equal amounts of total protein were loaded per lane, depending on the target: 30 µg for MC4R, ARC, and the corresponding BDNF measurements, and 60 µg for NPAS4 and the corresponding BDNF measurements. Samples were mixed with 4× Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA, Cat. No. 1610747) containing β-mercaptoethanol (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Cat. No. 1610710) and distilled water. Samples were denatured at 70 °C for 10 min (MC4R, NPAS4, and BDNF) or at 95 °C for 5 min (ARC and BDNF). Proteins together with Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standards (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Cat. No. 1610374) were separated on 14-well SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Cat. No. 5671083) in 1× Tris-Glycine-SDS buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Cat. No. 1610732). After equilibration in Pierce™ 1-Step Transfer Buffer (Thermo Scientific™, Cat. No. 84731), proteins were transferred onto previously methanol-activated (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland, CAS: 67-56-1) PVDF membranes (0.45 μm, Thermo Scientific™, Cat. No. 88518) using a Pierce G2 Fast Blotter (Thermo Scientific™) at 25 V for 7 min (MC4R/ARC series) or 12 min (NPAS4 series).

MC4R, ARC, and NPAS4 were quantified in three blot series, each including BDNF as an additional plasticity-related target protein (MC4R/BDNF/α-TUBULIN, ARC/BDNF/HSP90, and NPAS4/BDNF/α-TUBULIN). Different loading controls were selected based on molecular weight compatibility and membrane configuration within each blot series. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (BioShop, Burlington, ON, Canada, Cat. No. ALB001) in TBST (Thermo Scientific™, Cat. No. 28360) at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table 3) diluted in 5% BSA in TBST. Antibody specificity was assessed based on manufacturer validation data, expected molecular weight, and absence of major non-specific bands. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit IRDye® 680RD (1:1000, LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA, Cat. No. 926-68071), goat anti-rabbit IRDye® 800CW (1:1000, LI-COR Biosciences, Cat. No. 926-32211), or goat anti-mouse IRDye® 680RD (1:1000, LI-COR Biosciences, Cat. No. 926-68070) for 1 h at room temperature under dark conditions. After washing with TBST, membranes were scanned using the Odyssey® CLx Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences) at 87 μm resolution with the default settings for the 700 nm channel (MC4R, ARC, NPAS4, BDNF) and 800 nm channel (α-TUBULIN, HSP90). Signal intensities were quantified in Image Studio (v2.1) using the Trim Signal metric and raw, unadjusted images. Each protein target was analyzed using two separate electrophoresis runs and Western blot membranes. Membrane strips corresponding to individual proteins were analyzed separately using identical ROI sizes and positions across all lanes. To minimize inter-membrane variability, fold change normalization was performed independently for each membrane relative to the mean value of the corresponding control (SD) group (set to 1.0), after which normalized fold change values from both membranes were combined for subsequent statistical analysis and visualization. Original Western blot membranes are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Python (v3.13) unless stated otherwise. Gene expression data obtained by RT-qPCR (ΔCT values; Figs. 1A and 2) and protein levels measured by ELISA (ng/mg total protein; Figs. 1B and 3) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (statsmodels). Separate models were fitted for each age group. For offspring, fixed effects included diet (SD, GLU, FRU), sex, and hippocampal region (dorsal, ventral), including all interaction terms, while animal identity was included as a random effect to account for repeated regional measurements within the same subject. For dams, models included diet and region as fixed effects, with animal identity included as a random effect. Model significance was assessed using Wald z-tests. Significant diet effects and diet-related interactions were followed by exploratory post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Welch’s t-tests with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Diet comparisons were performed within sex and hippocampal region, when applicable. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d. Supplementary boxplots showing raw ΔCT values and absolute protein levels for individual animals across diet, sex, and hippocampal region groups (Supplementary Tables; S1-S3). All statistical analyses and biological interpretation were performed using raw ΔCT values for RT-qPCR data and raw protein concentrations for protein analyses. For visualization only, qPCR heat maps are presented as relative fold change values calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method, whereas protein heat maps display mean raw protein concentrations.

Results

Maternal Monosaccharide Diets Induce Selective Region-Dependent Hippocampal Molecular Remodeling in Dams

To determine whether maternal isocaloric monosaccharide diets directly affect hippocampal maturation- and plasticity-related molecular profiles, we quantified mRNA expression of Bdnf, Dcx, Gfap, Nes, and Rbfox3 (Fig. 1A), together with the corresponding protein levels of DCX, GFAP, and NeuN (Fig. 1B), in the dHIP and vHIP of dams.

Maternal monosaccharide diets were associated with relatively modest and selective alterations in hippocampal gene expression in dams (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Tables; S1). Linear mixed-effects model analysis revealed a significant main effect of diet for Bdnf expression, with FRU exposure associated with modestly elevated overall expression levels relative to SD controls (β = −0.49, p = 0.01), independent of hippocampal region, as no significant region or diet × region interaction effects were detected. Maternal monosaccharide diets did not significantly affect Dcx expression. However, a significant main effect of the hippocampal region was observed (β = −0.69, p = 0.003), indicating differential Dcx expression between the dHIP and vHIP. Likewise, maternal monosaccharide diets did not significantly alter Gfap expression. Nevertheless, a significant regional effect was detected (β = 0.50, p = 0.02), indicating differential Gfap expression between hippocampal regions. No significant effects of diet, hippocampal region, or diet × region interaction were detected for Nes expression. In contrast, Rbfox3 expression showed a significant main effect of diet, with FRU exposure associated with modestly elevated Rbfox3 expression levels relative to SD controls (β = −0.30, p = 0.04), without significant regional or interaction effects.

At the protein level (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Tables; S1), DCX abundance in dams varied significantly by diet and hippocampal region, with significant effects of both GLU (β = 1.50, p < 0.001) and FRU exposure (β = 1.18, p = 0.004) relative to SD controls, together with significant diet × region interactions. Significant GLU × region (β = −2.57, p < 0.001) and FRU × region (β = −1.19, p = 0.01) interactions showed region-specific effects along the hippocampal longitudinal axis. Post-hoc analyses revealed significantly lower DCX levels in the vHIP of GLU dams compared with SD dams (p = 0.02), indicating that diet-related effects on DCX protein levels differed between dHIP and vHIP regions. GFAP protein levels were significantly elevated in FRU dams compared with SD dams (β = 0.72, p < 0.001). A significant main effect of region was observed (β = 0.45, p = 0.02), with higher GFAP levels in the vHIP than in the dHIP. A significant GLU × region interaction (β = 0.69, p = 0.01) further indicated region-specific effects on GFAP abundance. Post-hoc analyses confirmed significantly higher GFAP levels in the vHIP of both GLU- (p = 0.03) and FRU-exposed dams (p = 0.002) compared with SD dams. Maternal FRU exposure was associated with altered NeuN protein abundance relative to SD controls (β = 1.79, p < 0.001). Independent of maternal diet, NeuN levels differed significantly between hippocampal regions, with higher levels observed in the vHIP than in the dHIP (β = 2.48, p < 0.001). Moreover, a significant GLU × region interaction (β = 2.47, p = 0.003) indicated that the effects of maternal monosaccharide diets on NeuN abundance varied across hippocampal regions. Post-hoc analyses demonstrated significantly higher NeuN levels in the dHIP of FRU dams (p = 0.005) and in the vHIP of GLU dams (p = 0.04) compared with SD dams.

Overall, these findings indicate that maternal isocaloric GLU and FRU intake during pregnancy and lactation induced selective hippocampal molecular adaptations in dams. FRU exposure was associated with increased Bdnf and Rbfox3 expression, whereas maternal monosaccharide diets produced region-dependent alterations in DCX, GFAP, and NeuN protein abundance across hippocampal regions.

Maternal Monosaccharide Diets Induce Age-, Sex-, and Region-specific Hippocampal Gene Expression Changes in Offspring

To assess the impact of maternal isocaloric monosaccharide diets on hippocampal maturation- and plasticity-related molecular profiles during adolescence (PND28; Fig. 2A) and young adulthood (PND63; Fig. 2B), we quantified mRNA expression of Bdnf, Dcx, Gfap, Nes, and Rbfox3 in the dHIP and vHIP of male and female offspring.

In adolescent offspring (Fig. 2A), several diet-, sex-, and region-dependent effects on hippocampal gene expression were detected (Supplementary Tables; S2). Bdnf expression showed a significant sex effect (β = 0.48, p = 0.04), together with significant sex × region interactions and diet × sex × region interactions for both GLU (β = 0.98, p = 0.03) and FRU exposure (β = 1.16, p = 0.01). Post-hoc analyses revealed significantly higher Bdnf expression in the dHIP of male offspring exposed to maternal GLU (p = 0.03) or FRU diets (p = 0.02) compared with SD controls. Dcx expression also showed a strong sex effect (β = 0.75, p < 0.001). Significant diet × sex interactions were detected for both GLU (β = −0.90, p = 0.002) and FRU exposure (β = −1.23, p < 0.001). In addition, a significant FRU × sex × region interaction was observed (β = 1.17, p = 0.004), indicating sex- and region-specific effects on Dcx expression following FRU exposure. Post-hoc comparisons revealed significantly higher Dcx expression in the dHIP of male offspring exposed to maternal GLU (p < 0.001) and FRU diets (p < 0.001) compared with SD controls, with the strongest effect observed following FRU exposure. For Rbfox3, a significant regional effect was detected (β = 0.70, p < 0.001). A significant FRU × sex interaction was also observed (β = −0.58, p = 0.02). Post-hoc analysis revealed significantly higher Rbfox3 expression in the dHIP of FRU-exposed male offspring compared with SD controls (p = 0.002). No significant effects of diet, sex, hippocampal region, or their interactions were detected for Gfap expression at this developmental stage. For Nes, a significant regional effect was detected (β = 0.37, p = 0.03). In addition, a significant sex × region interaction was observed (β = −0.76, p = 0.002), suggesting region-specific sex differences in Nes expression. However, post-hoc analyses did not reveal significant diet-related pairwise differences.

In young adult offspring (Fig. 2B), gene expression differences showed prominent region- and interaction-dependent patterns (Supplementary Tables; S2). For Bdnf, significant diet × region interactions were detected for both GLU (β = −0.99, p = 0.002) and FRU exposure (β = −1.04, p < 0.001), together with a significant GLU × sex × region interaction effect (β = 1.08, p = 0.02). Post-hoc comparisons revealed significantly higher Bdnf expression in the vHIP of female offspring exposed to maternal GLU (p = 0.04) and FRU diets (p = 0.01) compared with SD controls. No significant main or interaction effects were detected for Dcx expression. For Gfap, the mixed model revealed a significant sex × region interaction (β = 0.72, p = 0.049), indicating region-specific sex differences in Gfap expression. Maternal monosaccharide diets did not significantly affect Gfap expression, and no significant main effects of diet, sex, or hippocampal region, or diet-related interaction terms, were detected. Maternal GLU exposure was associated with altered Nes expression (β = 0.57, p = 0.04), independent of offspring sex and hippocampal region. Finally, Rbfox3 expression was significantly influenced by sex (β = −0.64, p = 0.001). Significant sex × region (β = 0.97, p = 0.001) and GLU × region (β = −0.66, p = 0.03) interactions were also detected, indicating region-specific effects on Rbfox3 expression. No significant post-hoc pairwise differences in Rbfox3 expression were identified between diet groups at PND63.

Maternal monosaccharide exposure altered hippocampal molecular profiles in an age-, sex-, and region-dependent manner. Diet-related effects were predominantly detected in the dHIP during adolescence, whereas in young adulthood, significant diet-related differences were mainly observed in the vHIP, suggesting a maturation-related shift in hippocampal molecular organization along the dorsal–ventral axis.

Maternal Monosaccharide Diets Produce Persistent Sex- and Region-Dependent Hippocampal Protein Alterations in Offspring

To evaluate the impact of maternal diet on hippocampal maturation-related protein profiles in offspring, DCX, GFAP, and NeuN levels were quantified in the dHIP and vHIP during adolescence (PND28; Fig. 3A) and young adulthood (PND63; Fig. 3B).

During adolescence (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Tables; S3), DCX levels showed a significant main effect of sex (β = 1.23, p = 0.006), together with multiple diet-, sex-, and region-dependent interaction effects. Significant interaction effects included FRU × region (β = 2.75, p < 0.001), GLU × region (β = 2.15, p < 0.001), FRU × sex (β = −1.35, p = 0.03), GLU × sex (β = −2.30, p = 0.0003), FRU × sex × region (β = −2.00, p = 0.03), and GLU × sex × region (β = −3.45, p < 0.001) interactions. Post-hoc comparisons revealed marked region- and sex-specific dietary effects. In male offspring, both FRU and GLU diets decreased DCX levels in the dHIP compared with SD controls (both p = 0.02). In the vHIP, GLU-exposed males showed lower DCX levels than SD animals (p < 0.001), whereas FRU exposure did not significantly alter DCX levels relative to SD controls. In female offspring, DCX levels in the vHIP were significantly increased in both GLU- (p = 0.002) and FRU-exposed animals (p = 0.03) compared with SD controls. GFAP levels showed significant main effects of sex (β = 1.12, p = 0.004) and hippocampal region (β = 3.02, p < 0.001). Significant GLU × region (β = 1.27, p = 0.02), GLU × sex (β = −1.17, p = 0.03), and GLU × sex × region (β = −2.37, p = 0.002) interactions were detected, indicating sex- and region-specific effects on GFAP levels following maternal GLU exposure. Post-hoc comparisons revealed divergent vHIP responses between sexes. In female offspring, GLU exposure increased GFAP levels in the vHIP compared with SD controls (p < 0.001), whereas FRU exposure did not significantly differ from SD. In contrast, male offspring exposed to maternal GLU showed lower GFAP levels in the vHIP than SD animals (p = 0.008). NeuN levels were significantly influenced by maternal FRU exposure (β = 4.37, p < 0.001) and hippocampal region (β = 7.97, p < 0.001), although these effects varied according to offspring sex and hippocampal region. Significant FRU × sex (β = −6.38, p < 0.001), FRU × region (β = −3.48, p = 0.03), sex × region (β = 3.20, p = 0.04), and GLU × sex × region (β = −6.44, p = 0.004) interactions were also found, indicating complex diet-, sex-, and region-specific effects on NeuN levels. Post-hoc analyses revealed significantly higher NeuN levels in the dHIP of FRU-exposed female offspring compared with SD controls (p = 0.02). In contrast, male offspring exposed to maternal GLU or FRU diets exhibited significantly lower NeuN levels in the vHIP compared with SD animals (GLU: p < 0.001; FRU: p = 0.004).

In young adulthood (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Tables; S3), significant region-dependent differences in protein levels remained evident, with consistently higher levels detected in the vHIP. DCX levels were significantly influenced by sex (β = −0.69, p = 0.01) and hippocampal region (β = 2.76, p < 0.001), with higher DCX levels in the vHIP than in the dHIP, together with multiple sex-, diet-, and region-dependent interaction effects. Significant GLU × sex (β = 0.78, p = 0.048), FRU × sex × region (β = −2.30, p < 0.001), and GLU × sex × region (β = −1.53, p = 0.02) interactions further indicated sex- and region-specific effects on DCX levels. Post-hoc comparisons revealed significantly higher DCX levels in the dHIP of FRU-exposed male offspring compared with SD controls (p = 0.03). In contrast, in the vHIP, both GLU- and FRU-exposed male offspring exhibited lower DCX levels than SD animals (GLU: p = 0.048; FRU: p = 0.004). GFAP levels were significantly influenced by maternal FRU exposure (β = 0.38, p = 0.01) and hippocampal region (β = 3.71, p < 0.001), with higher levels detected in the vHIP than in the dHIP. However, these effects were strongly modified by sex and hippocampal region, as evidenced by a significant FRU × sex × region interaction (β = −1.85, p = 0.002). Post-hoc comparisons revealed region-specific alterations in male offspring. In the dHIP, both GLU- and FRU-exposed males exhibited significantly higher GFAP levels compared with SD controls (GLU: p = 0.04; FRU: p < 0.001). In contrast, in the vHIP, both GLU- and FRU-exposed male offspring showed lower GFAP levels than SD animals (GLU: p < 0.001; FRU: p = 0.01). NeuN levels were significantly influenced by maternal FRU exposure (β = 3.14, p < 0.001), sex (β = 2.12, p = 0.01), and hippocampal region (β = 15.89, p < 0.001), with higher NeuN levels generally observed in the vHIP than in the dHIP. Significant FRU × region (β = −5.11, p = 0.01) and sex × region (β = −4.68, p = 0.02) interactions were found, indicating region-specific effects on NeuN levels. Post-hoc comparisons revealed significantly higher NeuN levels in the dHIP of both female (p < 0.001) and male FRU-exposed offspring (p = 0.008) compared with SD controls. In contrast, in the vHIP of male offspring, both GLU- and FRU-exposed groups exhibited lower NeuN levels than SD animals (both p = 0.03).

Overall, DCX, GFAP, and NeuN protein levels showed pronounced region-specific patterns across development, while several markers exhibited significant diet-, sex-, and region-dependent interactions. Maternal monosaccharide exposure produced marked sex- and region-specific alterations during adolescence, particularly for DCX and GFAP, whereas in young adulthood, pronounced hippocampal regional differences persisted and were accompanied by diet- and sex-dependent alterations in DCX, GFAP, and NeuN levels. These findings indicate sustained remodeling of maturation-related hippocampal protein profiles during postnatal development.

Maternal Monosaccharide Diets Alter Hippocampal RNA–Protein Integration and Behavioral Measures in Offspring

To examine the effects of maternal diet, sex, and developmental stage on hippocampal RNA–protein integration, we analyzed the NDMI derived from the PC1 of the combined gene–protein PCA separately for the dHIP and vHIP (Fig. 5). In parallel, behavioral outcomes previously reported for an independent cohort exposed to the same maternal diet paradigm (Witek et al. 2023) were summarized using effect-size analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Group differences in NDMI were evaluated using permutation tests with FDR correction (Supplementary Tables; S5). In the dHIP, NDMI exhibited a significant age-dependent decline, with lower values at PND63 than at PND28 across several groups (q < 0.05). At PND28, males showed higher NDMI than females under the SD condition. Diet-related differences showed marked sex- and region-specific patterns. Specifically, in the dHIP, FRU-exposed males displayed lower NDMI than SD controls at PND28, whereas at PND63 FRU males exhibited higher NDMI values than SD males, indicating an age-dependent reversal of the FRU-associated NDMI pattern despite the overall age-related decline (q < 0.05). In the vHIP, female offspring exposed to maternal GLU or FRU diets exhibited higher NDMI values than SD controls at PND28 (q < 0.05), whereas GLU-exposed males showed lower NDMI than SD males (q < 0.05). This sex divergence persisted at PND63, where males exposed to both GLU and FRU displayed lower NDMI than SD males (q < 0.05). Age-related reductions in NDMI were also observed across several groups, particularly among males (q < 0.05). Previously published behavioral outcomes obtained using the same maternal diet paradigm are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1 for descriptive comparison with the present molecular findings.

Maternal Monosaccharide Diets Alter MC4R-, ARC-, and NPAS4-Related Molecular Profiles in the Ventral Hippocampus of Male Offspring

To explore molecular alterations associated with the persistent behavioral dysregulation observed in adolescent (PND28) and young adult (PND63) male offspring, Mc4r, Arc, and Npas4 were assessed at the mRNA (Fig. 6) and protein levels (Figs. 7 and 8) in the vHIP. Previously obtained Bdnf mRNA data were additionally included to complement the analysis of BDNF protein levels in the same cohort.

In adolescent male offspring (Fig. 6A-C), maternal diet did not significantly affect mRNA expression of Mc4r (F(2, 21) = 2.50, p = 0.11), Bdnf (F(2, 21) = 0.80, p = 0.46), or Arc (F(2, 21) = 1.59, p = 0.23). In contrast, maternal diet significantly influenced Npas4 expression (F(2, 21) = 9.39, p = 0.001), with increased expression observed in FRU-exposed males (Fig. 6D). In young adult males, maternal diet significantly affected Mc4r expression (F(2.0, 14.92) = 3.71, p = 0.049), with FRU-exposed rats showing higher expression than SD (Fig. 6E). No diet effects were detected for Bdnf (F(2, 21) = 2.15, p = 0.14) or Arc (F(2, 21) = 1.74, p = 0.20) mRNA levels (Fig. 6F-G). Notably, Npas4 expression was significantly reduced in both GLU and FRU groups relative to SD (F(2, 21) = 6.69, p = 0.006; Fig. 6H).

At the protein level, Western blot analyses (Figs. 7 and 8) revealed significant effects of maternal diet on several neuroplasticity-related proteins in the vHIP of male offspring. In adolescent males, maternal diet significantly affected MC4R protein levels (H = 6.30, p = 0.04, N1, 2, 3 = 8; Fig. 7A-B), although no post-hoc comparisons reached significance. No diet effects were observed for ARC (H = 4.38, p = 0.11, N1, 2, 3 = 8; Fig. 7D-E) or NPAS4 (F(2, 21) = 1.51, p = 0.24; Fig. 7G-H). BDNF protein levels were significantly influenced by maternal diet when normalized to HSP90 (H = 6.24, p = 0.04, N1, 2, 3 = 8; Fig. 7F), with reduced levels in GLU males. A significant main effect was also detected when BDNF was normalized to α-TUBULIN (F(2, 21) = 3.96, p = 0.03; Fig. 7I), although post-hoc comparisons did not reach significance. In contrast, no diet effect was observed when BDNF was quantified in the MC4R blot series (F(2, 21) = 1.12, p = 0.34; Fig. 7C). Thus, BDNF protein differences at PND28 depended on the normalization strategy used for quantification and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In contrast, MC4R, ARC, and NPAS4 showed no diet-dependent differences at this stage. In young adult males, maternal diet significantly increased MC4R protein levels (F(2, 20) = 4.56, p = 0.02; Fig. 8A-B), with FRU offspring showing higher levels compared with SD. ARC protein levels were significantly increased in both GLU and FRU groups relative to SD (F(2, 20) = 5.72, p = 0.01; Fig. 8D-E). NPAS4 protein levels showed a significant effect of maternal diet (H = 6.08, p = 0.048; N1, 2, 3 = 8, 7, 8), reflecting reduced levels in FRU offspring compared with SD (Fig. 8G-H). In contrast, maternal diet did not significantly affect BDNF protein levels in either the NPAS4 blot series (F(2,21) = 0.04, p = 0.96; Fig. 8I) or the MC4R blot series (F(2, 21) = 1.44, p = 0.26; Fig. 8C). Likewise, no maternal diet effect was detected when BDNF was normalized to HSP90 (F(2, 21) = 0.14, p = 0.87; Fig. 8F), although a similar trend was visually apparent.

Taken together, these findings indicate that exposure to maternal monosaccharide diets is associated with persistent alterations in NPAS4-, MC4R-, and ARC-related molecular profiles in the vHIP of male offspring.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that maternal isocaloric monosaccharide exposure is associated with age-, sex-, and region-dependent alterations in hippocampal maturation-related molecular profiles, particularly along the dorsal–ventral hippocampal axis. In particular, maternal FRU exposure was associated with particularly prominent vHIP molecular alterations in young adult male offspring,although dHIP protein changes were also observed.

Hippocampal Molecular Maturation and Plasticity Under Maternal Monosaccharide Diets

Maternal monosaccharide diets were associated with region-, sex-, and age-dependent alterations in hippocampal maturation- and plasticity-related molecular markers. In dams, FRU exposure was accompanied by modest increases in Bdnf and Rbfox3 expression, along with region-dependent modulation of GFAP, DCX, and NeuN protein levels, indicating that maternal monosaccharide intake was associated with measurable molecular changes in the maternal hippocampus. At the transcript level, diet-related effects were more prominent in the dHIP during adolescence, whereas additional vHIP-associated alterations emerged in young adulthood. At the protein level, persistent differences were observed across both hippocampal subregions. Notably, males generally exhibited more pronounced molecular changes, particularly within the vHIP during young adulthood, whereas females displayed distinct molecular profiles that varied according to developmental stage and hippocampal region. These findings suggest that maternal monosaccharide exposure influences hippocampal molecular organization in both dams and offspring, although the functional significance of the maternal hippocampal changes remains unclear.

This developmental pattern is consistent with the well-established functional segmentation of the hippocampus. The dHIP predominantly supports contextual and cognitive processing, including pattern separation mechanisms, whereas the vHIP is more closely linked to emotional regulation and HPA-axis modulation (Fanselow and Dong 2010; Sahay et al. 2011; Snyder et al. 2011; Kheirbek et al. 2012; Strange et al. 2014). These dorsal–ventral differences are further reflected in regional variation in cellular composition, connectivity, and plasticity-related molecular organization (Anacker and Hen 2017). The apparent age-dependent pattern, characterized by predominantly dHIP-associated transcriptomic alterations during adolescence and greater vHIP molecular involvement in young adulthood, may be consistent with the environmental sculpting hypothesis of hippocampal maturation, in which early-life conditions may bias hippocampal circuit specialization toward later cognitive and affective demands (Cushman et al. 2021). Indeed, hippocampal maturation remains highly sensitive to environmental influences during critical periods of postnatal plasticity, including dendritic remodeling and integration of immature dentate neurons (Tronel et al. 2010; Bergami et al. 2015; Huckleberry et al. 2018). These observations are consistent with the possibility that early-life monosaccharide exposure may influence dorsal–ventral hippocampal organization through plasticity-related developmental processes. Comparable functional specialization along the hippocampal longitudinal axis has also been described in humans, where posterior regions are linked more strongly to cognitive processing and anterior regions to affective regulation (Poppenk et al. 2013; Strange et al. 2014; Nichols et al. 2023; Angeli et al. 2025).

In the present study, maternal FRU exposure was associated with opposing molecular changes at the protein level in the hippocampus of young adult male offspring, characterized by increased DCX and NeuN protein levels in the dHIP but reduced DCX, GFAP, and NeuN levels in the vHIP. In addition, both maternal GLU and FRU exposure increased GFAP protein levels in the dHIP. Together, these findings support a dorsal–ventral divergence in hippocampal maturation- and plasticity-related molecular profiles following early-life monosaccharide exposure. Previous studies similarly demonstrated that maternal FRU exposure alters multiple aspects of hippocampal neurogenesis, neuronal maturation, and plasticity in offspring, including reductions in DCX-, BrdU+/NeuN+-, and Ki67-positive cell populations together with altered BDNF–TrkB signaling (Yamazaki et al. 2018), increased GFAP immunoreactivity and neuronal abnormalities (Erbas et al. 2018), impaired neuronal differentiation accompanied by reduced DCX expression (Liu et al. 2020), altered hippocampal BDNF signaling (Wu et al. 2016), and broader transcriptional dysregulation of pathways associated with synaptic plasticity and memory (Zou et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2023). However, direct evaluation of sex-dependent effects has been limited because several studies examined only male (Yamazaki et al. 2018) or only female offspring (Wu et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2020), whereas studies including both sexes generally did not perform detailed sex-specific analyses (Zou et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2023).

Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating age-, sex-, and region-dependent molecular adaptations, with males exhibiting particularly pronounced alterations within the vHIP during young adulthood. Consistent with previous reports of disrupted hippocampal BDNF signaling following maternal FRU exposure (Wu et al. 2016; Yamazaki et al. 2018; Zou et al. 2023), Bdnf expression in the present study showed region-, sex-, and age-dependent modulation, with increased dHIP expression in adolescent males and enhanced vHIP expression in young adult females. Importantly, most previous studies either used non-isocaloric FRU paradigms or analyzed the hippocampus as a single structure, limiting insight into dorsal–ventral and developmental dynamics. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to systematically examine dHIP–vHIP and sex-dependent RNA–protein organization across postnatal stages following maternal isocaloric monosaccharide exposure, revealing a developmental shift from predominantly dHIP-associated molecular alterations during adolescence to greater vHIP molecular involvement in young adulthood. Similar developmental reprogramming has been described following other early-life environmental perturbations, including prenatal stress and altered maternal environments (Alachkar et al. 2017; Marchisella et al. 2021; Grieco et al. 2026).

Our PCA analysis revealed age- and region-specific multivariate organization among hippocampal markers associated with neuronal immaturity, maturation, and plasticity. In adolescence, variance in the vHIP reflected coordinated contributions of both gene expression and protein variables. In young adulthood, dHIP PC1 reflected coordinated RNA-protein contributions, whereas vHIP PC1 displayed opposing loadings for mRNA expression and protein levels, suggesting partial RNA–protein uncoupling within vHIP circuits. These RNA–protein coupling and dissociation patterns may be broadly consistent with region-specific regulatory configurations associated with prolonged maturational plasticity and heterogeneous immature neuronal states across postnatal development (La Rosa et al. 2020; Pattaro et al. 2026). Collectively, these observations suggest divergent dHIP and vHIP maturation- and plasticity-related trajectories across postnatal development. We therefore integrated PCA-derived RNA and protein signatures into a region-specific NDMI, enabling comparison of diet-, sex-, and age-dependent differences in hippocampal molecular maturation states. Recent studies increasingly employ integrative RNA–protein approaches to characterize the spatial molecular organization of hippocampal circuits and regulatory relationships between transcriptional and proteomic profiles (Wu et al. 2025; Kaulich et al. 2025).

Importantly, partial dissociation between mRNA expression and protein levels was observed for several markers. Although the mechanisms underlying this discordance were not directly examined in the present study, previous transcriptomic–proteomic studies indicate that mRNA levels only partially predict protein abundance because of post-transcriptional regulation, translational efficiency, protein turnover, and temporal delays between transcription and protein synthesis (Vogel and Marcotte 2012; Samih et al. 2026). In neural tissue, regional protein trafficking and neuronal compartmentalization may further contribute to RNA–protein divergence (Moritz et al. 2019). Together, these observations suggest that maternal monosaccharide exposure may influence hippocampal maturation-related profiles through regulatory mechanisms extending beyond transcription alone.

A critical methodological consideration concerns the interpretation of the markers used. DCX, Rbfox3 (NeuN), GFAP, and Nes are not exclusive markers of newly generated neurons but may instead reflect broader immature and maturing cellular phenotypes associated with hippocampal postnatal maturation and plasticity (Bonfanti and Peretto 2011; La Rosa et al. 2020; Pattaro et al. 2026). Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as direct measures of canonical adult neurogenesis, but rather as indicators of broader region-specific maturational and plasticity-related states.

Associations Between Hippocampal Molecular Organization and Behavioral Outcomes Under Maternal Monosaccharide Exposure

Although the behavioral data were obtained from an independent cohort exposed to the same maternal dietary paradigm (Witek et al. 2023), several behavioral patterns align conceptually with the regional NDMI profiles observed in the present study. Importantly, the behavioral assessments were performed within developmental windows corresponding closely to the molecular analyses conducted at PND28 and PND63. Young adult males exposed to maternal GLU or FRU diets exhibited reduced vHIP NDMI, whereas independent behavioral analyses revealed increased anxiety-like behavior during young adulthood, most pronounced in FRU-exposed males. Conversely, FRU-exposed males displayed relatively higher dHIP NDMI at PND63, together with increased recognition index values in the novel object recognition test previously reported at PND61 (Witek et al. 2023). FRU males also displayed increased sucrose consumption during early adulthood (PND65), consistent with altered reward-related and stress-related behavioral domains associated with vHIP function (Witek et al. 2025). Notably, these associations were most evident in males, consistent with the more pronounced vHIP molecular alterations observed in male offspring relative to females. Consistently, maternal or early-life FRU exposure has been associated with hippocampal molecular and structural alterations accompanied by behavioral abnormalities, including impaired spatial and recognition memory, deficits in contextual learning, reduced sociability, increased stereotypic behaviors, anxiety-like and depressive-like phenotypes, reduced motivation, and altered reward-related behaviors across multiple rodent models (Wu et al. 2016; Yamazaki et al. 2018; Erbas et al. 2018; Mucellini et al. 2019; Trujillo-Villarreal et al. 2021; Zou et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2025). Although the specific behavioral domains affected varied across studies, these findings collectively support a link between early-life FRU exposure, hippocampal dysfunction, and long-term behavioral alterations. Notably, the behavioral consequences of maternal FRU exposure appear to be sex-dependent, with behavioral abnormalities reported in both male and female offspring, although the affected behavioral domains vary across studies. Collectively, these observations are broadly consistent with region-specific behavioral phenotypes associated with dorsal–ventral hippocampal organization.

In the present study, FRU-exposed males exhibited the most pronounced vHIP alterations, including reduced vHIP NDMI values, conceptually aligning with previously reported behavioral abnormalities. Females generally displayed weaker or partially distinct vHIP molecular profiles, consistent with previous reports of sex-dependent effects of sugar exposure on hippocampal plasticity and behavior. Estrous cycle stage was not monitored in female offspring at the time of tissue collection, and potential influences of fluctuating sex hormones on hippocampal gene and protein expression cannot be excluded. Experimental studies further suggest greater male vulnerability to hippocampus-dependent cognitive and structural alterations following FRU or sucrose exposure, whereas females may show partially preserved molecular or behavioral profiles, potentially related to sex hormone-dependent regulation of hippocampal plasticity (Abbott et al. 2016; Yagi and Galea 2018; Kloster et al. 2021; Hyer et al. 2022; Hodges et al. 2022). Overall, these observations suggest that maternal monosaccharide exposure may contribute to dorsal–ventral alterations in hippocampal plasticity associated with stress-related and contextual behavioral phenotypes (Eisch et al. 2008; Kheirbek et al. 2012; Toda et al. 2018).

Maternal Monosaccharide Exposure Alters Ventral Hippocampal Plasticity-Related Phenotype

Given the established role of the vHIP in affective regulation and stress-related behavior, together with evidence linking altered vHIP excitation–inhibition balance to anxiety-related phenotypes (Ghasemi et al. 2021; Volitaki et al. 2024; Cerquetella et al. 2025), the vHIP-specific molecular alterations identified by PCA and NDMI analyses prompted further examination of plasticity-related pathways in this region.

During adolescence, the targeted molecular effects of maternal diet in the vHIP were relatively modest, with partial dissociation between gene expression and protein levels, suggesting early priming of plasticity-related pathways rather than a coordinated molecular programme. In contrast, young adult male offspring displayed the most pronounced vHIP plasticity-related phenotype characterized by increased Mc4r expression and MC4R protein levels, increased ARC protein abundance, and reduced Npas4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. These alterations were most pronounced following maternal FRU exposure, whereas GLU exposure primarily affected ARC levels without consistently altering the full molecular profile. Importantly, BDNF effects at PND28 depended on the normalization strategy and were therefore interpreted with caution, whereas changes in MC4R, ARC, and NPAS4 were directionally consistent across analyses.

Although MC4R is not traditionally considered a central regulator of hippocampal maturation, increasing evidence suggests that melanocortin signaling contributes to hippocampal synaptic plasticity and neuroplasticity-related processes. Previous studies demonstrated that MC4R signaling modulates dendritic spine maturation, AMPA receptor trafficking, and long-term potentiation (Shen et al. 2013, 2016; Griffin et al. 2024). MC4R expression has also been detected across hippocampal regions, including the ventral subiculum, where MC4R-expressing circuits were implicated in motivational and affective regulation (Kishi et al. 2003; Singh et al. 2023). More recently, MC4R signaling was additionally linked to maintenance of hippocampal precursor populations and broader neuroplasticity-related processes (Carniglia et al. 2024), while earlier studies demonstrated nutrition-sensitive regulation of BDNF downstream of MC4R signaling (Xu et al. 2003). In this context, the altered MC4R molecular profile observed in young adult FRU offspring may be consistent with dysregulated melanocortin-associated processes within vHIP circuits. Offspring from the same maternal dietary model previously displayed altered glucose homeostasis, accelerated postnatal growth, increased sucrose consumption, and elevated mesocorticolimbic MC4R expression following FRU exposure (Witek et al. 2025). Importantly, FRU and GLU differ substantially in metabolic handling and insulin-related signaling, with FRU exerting stronger effects on lipid metabolism and metabolic stress pathways (Johnson et al. 2026), which may contribute to the more pronounced vHIP alterations observed following maternal FRU exposure. Together, these observations support a potential link between early metabolic dysregulation, melanocortin-associated signaling, and vHIP plasticity-related remodeling.

A prominent component of the PND63 molecular phenotype was reduced NPAS4 expression occurring alongside increased ARC levels in GLU and FRU offspring and elevated MC4R levels in FRU offspring within the vHIP of young adult males. NPAS4 is an activity-dependent transcription factor involved in hippocampal circuit homeostasis, inhibitory synapse regulation, excitation–inhibition balance, and memory-related plasticity (Lin et al. 2008; Bloodgood et al. 2013; Sun and Lin 2016; Heroux et al. 2019). Previous studies further implicated NPAS4 in stress responsivity, structural plasticity, anxiety-related behaviors, and neuropsychiatric vulnerability (Yun et al. 2013; Fu et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2021; Brito et al. 2024). In the present study, maternal monosaccharide exposure produced an age-dependent pattern of Npas4 dysregulation, characterized by increased Npas4 mRNA expression in FRU adolescent offspring, followed by reduced Npas4 expression in GLU and FRU young adult males, together with decreased NPAS4 protein levels in FRU offspring. These findings are consistent with persistent alterations in molecular profiles associated with vHIP plasticity.

In line with the present findings, previous studies indicate that early-life environmental perturbations, including maternal dietary imbalance and prenatal stress, can induce long-lasting alterations in hippocampal neuroplasticity and activity-dependent gene regulation. Perinatal nutritional manipulations and prenatal stress were previously shown to disrupt Npas4-associated pathways together with hippocampal maturation and synaptic plasticity processes (Alachkar et al. 2017; Nätt et al. 2017; Ryabushkina et al. 2020; Marchisella et al. 2021). Reduced Npas4 signaling has additionally been linked to maladaptive circuit organization following developmental perturbations (Drouet et al. 2018; Heslin and Coutellier 2018). These findings support the interpretation that maternal monosaccharide exposure may influence coordinated alterations in activity-dependent vHIP plasticity rather than isolated molecular changes.

The observed increase in ARC protein abundance at PND63 further supports this interpretation. ARC is a key mediator of synaptic plasticity involved in AMPAR trafficking, synaptic scaling, and structural remodeling, and its expression is strongly regulated at translational and post-transcriptional levels (Shepherd and Bear 2011). This may explain the dissociation observed here between unchanged Arc mRNA and increased ARC protein levels. Previous studies further demonstrated that coordinated hippocampal Npas4 and Arc dysregulation may accompany memory impairment, stress-related behavioral vulnerability, and maladaptive plasticity-related states (Qiu et al. 2016; Drouet et al. 2018). In the present study, increased ARC protein levels together with reduced Npas4 expression seem to reflect a molecular profile potentially associated with enhanced excitatory remodeling and reduced inhibitory stabilization. Although synaptic function was not directly assessed here, our observations support a molecular profile consistent with altered plasticity. Thus, FRU males previously tested at PND61 displayed an increased recognition index in the novel object recognition test (Witek et al. 2023), temporally corresponding to the ARC alterations observed here at PND63.

Collectively, our present findings demonstrate a pattern of altered vHIP plasticity characterized by increased ARC protein abundance together with reduced NPAS4-associated inhibitory regulation, particularly in FRU offspring, where elevated MC4R levels were also observed. Behavioral alterations previously reported in independent FRU-exposed cohorts were detected during developmental periods corresponding to those examined in the present molecular analyses (Witek et al. 2023, 2026). Although direct functional relationships cannot be established from the present data, these molecular alterations may be broadly consistent with the anxiety-like and motivational phenotypes previously observed in FRU offspring. Consistently, our cocaine self-administration study demonstrated preserved cocaine intake but reduced motivation under high-effort conditions in FRU male offspring (Witek et al. 2026), in line with evidence that vHIP dysregulation may influence effort-based behavior (Yoshida et al. 2021). Reduced hippocampal NPAS4 signaling has also been associated with impaired stress adaptation and altered excitation–inhibition balance (Zhang et al. 2013; Hughes et al. 2023), whereas MC4R signaling has been implicated in anxiety-related regulation (Micioni Di Bonaventura et al. 2022). Accordingly, the present molecular profile may be consistent with dysregulated activity-dependent plasticity within the vHIP.

Further, alterations in MC4R, ARC, and NPAS4 suggest involvement of melanocortin-associated plasticity mechanisms in the hippocampal circuit function. However, the analyses rely on molecular markers and do not directly assess neuronal activity or synaptic physiology, which would require further circuit-level approaches such as electrophysiological or activity-dependent analyses (Lin et al. 2008; Sorrells et al. 2019; Brito et al. 2024).

Since perinatal exposure to isocaloric monosaccharides was associated with age-dependent differences in dorsal–ventral hippocampal molecular organization, characterized by a relative predominance of dHIP-associated molecular alterations during adolescence (PND28) and greater vHIP molecular involvement in young adulthood (PND63), we present multidimensional conceptual models illustrating the dorsal–ventral redistribution of maturation-related molecular organization (Fig. 9A) and the male-biased vHIP plasticity phenotype (Fig. 9B). Together, these observations support the interpretation that early-life exposure to isocaloric monosaccharides may promote region- and sex-specific vHIP plasticity associated with affective vulnerability. Importantly, NDMI and NDPI should be interpreted as descriptive integrative indices of coordinated molecular organization rather than validated biomarkers of neurodevelopmental state, and therefore primarily serve to enable multivariate comparison of diet-, sex-, and age-dependent molecular patterning across hippocampal regions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that maternal isocaloric monosaccharide exposure is a potential modulator of dorsal–ventral hippocampal molecular organization across postnatal development. Early-life GLU and FRU exposure was associated with persistent region-, sex-, and age-dependent alterations in markers associated with hippocampal maturation and activity-dependent plasticity, with molecular changes becoming more prominent in the vHIP during young adulthood, particularly in male offspring. Targeted analyses identified persistent vHIP plasticity-related molecular alterations involving MC4R, ARC, and NPAS4 in young adult male offspring. Notably, the molecular effects of maternal monosaccharide exposure were sex-dependent, with male offspring generally exhibiting more pronounced vHIP alterations than females. Our findings suggest that early-life exposure to isocaloric monosaccharides may influence dorsal–ventral hippocampal developmental organization and activity-dependent plasticity associated with affective vulnerability. Future studies should determine whether the dHIP–vHIP molecular alterations observed here extend to other interconnected limbic regions involved in metabolic and emotional processing. In addition, functional and pharmacological investigations will be necessary to clarify how early-life dietary environments influence long-term hippocampal plasticity and circuit remodeling.
