Antiviral Monocytes Increase Prior to Detectable HIV-1 Rebound Viremia
Authors: Anna Farrell-Sherman, Natalia de la Force, Cecilia A Prator, Renan Valieris, Walker Azam, Israel Da Silva, Steven G Deeks, Cassandra Thanh, Ronald J Bosch, Timothy J Henrich, Lillian B Cohn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe persistence of human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) proviruses in latently infected cells allows viremia to resume upon treatment cessation. To characterize the resulting immune response, we compared plasma proteomics and single-cell transcriptomics of peripheral blood mononuclear cells before, during, and after detectable plasma viremia. We observed unique transcriptional signatures prior to viral rebound, including a significant increase in CD16++ monocytes with increased antiviral gene expression. Inflammatory proteins were identified in plasma after detectable rebound. Identifying early signals of imminent viral rebound after treatment cessation will aid in the development of strategies to prolong time to viral rebound and cure HIV-1.

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) prevents the infection of new cells by human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) and prevents HIV-1 replication in people with HIV (PWH); however, viremia returns rapidly after ART cessation. Viral rebound occurs due to a persistent reservoir of latently infected cells within which virus can replicate following treatment interruption [1]. We previously demonstrated that the frequency of CD4+ T cells expressing the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) super family receptor, CD30, increases after ART is stopped but prior to detectable HIV rebound [2]. This observation suggests that low-level viral replication may initiate an immune response to recrudescing HIV-1 before viremia is detectable in the peripheral blood. Understanding this immune response could uncover biomarkers to predict HIV-1 viral rebound that would greatly benefit HIV-1 cure studies.

We hypothesize that the immune response to low-level viral activity before it is detectable by clinical assays could potentially generate signals that could serve as an indicator for imminent rebound. Here, we characterize immune signatures during treatment interruption of 24 PWH who participated in the placebo arm of 3 separate Advancing Clinical Therapeutics Globally (ACTG) vaccine trials [2]. We performed high-dimensional plasma proteomics on samples from 23 participants and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) on 10 participants who were chosen because they demonstrated the greatest fold-change in CD4+ T-cell CD30 expression after stopping ART in our prior study and therefore had the highest likelihood of other detectable pre-rebound immune signaling. Examining the viral-immune dynamics during rebound is essential to understanding mechanisms of HIV immune control.

METHODS

Cohort

We accessed samples from 24 individuals who participated in observational analytical treatment interruption (ATI) studies as part of previously published ACTG trials (A5068, A5024, A5187, A371, A5197) [2]. A5187 and A371 enrolled early treated participants, while A5197, A5024, and A5068 participants were treated in chronic phase. Consistent with the parent ACTG trials, 21 (87.5%) of participants were male and the median age was 42 years (interquartile range, 37–48 years). Fourteen participants were on nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-based regimens, 6 were on protease inhibitor (PI)-based regimens, 3 were taking both PI and NNRTIs together, and 1 was on an nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) only regimen. All studies were approved by on-site institutional review boards and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Plasma Proteomics

We assayed plasma proteomics through 3 Olink targeted panels (inflammation, immuno-oncology, and biological processes) with plasma samples from 23 of our 24 participants. These panels analyzed 188 soluble proteins (Supplementary Table 1) in plasma at each study time point. Differences in plasma protein abundance were assessed with a paired Wilcoxon test and P values were adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing

Live peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from a subset of 10 participants were processed through the 10x Genomics 5′ Single Cell assay on a 10x Genomics chip K according to manufacturer's instructions. Library products were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq. Three libraries failed, resulting in a total of 27 samples. Reads were processed with Cell Ranger 6.1.1. with a depth of 17 068–92 846 reads per cell after filtering.

scRNAseq Analysis

Preprocessing was performed using Seurat version 4.1.1 in R. Low-quality cells were removed if they were associated with < 2500 transcripts, < 900 genes, or > 15% reads mapping to mitochondrial genes. SCTransform was used to normalize samples, and integration was performed using SCTIntegration across time points, donors, and batches. Seurat's multimodal PBMC reference dataset [3] was used to assign cell-type labels, which were then visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) to confirm cell-type annotations (Figure 1B). Principal component analysis clustering of samples identified donor 6's post-rebound sample as an outlier and we removed it from further analysis (Supplementary Figure 1). Cell-type composition changes were assessed using a paired Wilcoxon test between cell-type proportions with FDR-corrected P values. For each cell type, paired-pseudobulk DESeq2 normalization was performed to determine differentially expressed genes using FDR < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold-change (log2FC) > 0.25 [4, 5]. The MSigDB Hallmark 2020 gene set was used to test for gene set enrichment analysis pathway enrichment [6]. FDR values and normalized enrichment scores were used to identify the top pathways per cell type. More detail on the scRNAseq analysis can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

RESULTS

To investigate the immune-viral interactions during rebound, we compared samples from 3 time points: on-ART (median, 4 days [range, 0–147 days] before ATI), pre-rebound (during ATI, median, 11.5 days [range, 6–56 days] before detectable plasma viremia), and post-rebound (during ATI, median, 12 days [range, 0–84 days] after detectable viremia) (Figure 1A). The median time to rebound (> 50 copies/mL) was 35 days (range, 19–82 days) and the median time from ATI start to pre-rebound sampling was 25 days (range, 11–56 days).

We performed 10x scRNAseq on cryopreserved PBMCs from 10 participants (8 participants had both an on-ART and pre-rebound time point) (Figure 1). We analyzed cell-type abundance and found that the median proportion of CD14++ monocytes increased from 16.6% of total PBMCs at baseline to 19.0% pre-rebound (P = .054; Figure 2A). We observed a significant increase in the median proportion of CD16++ monocytes from 2.57% of PBMCs on-ART to 4.38% of PBMCs pre-rebound (P = .008; Figure 2A). The baseline on-ART level of CD16++ monocytes varied from 1% to 5% across donors; however, the increase in CD16++ monocytes was consistent with a median difference of 1.9% (standard deviation = 0.6). Among the other cell subsets, Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and doublets significantly increased; however, these subsets were small fractions of total cells (less than 0.3% of PBMCs) (Supplementary Figure 2).

To assess changes in gene expression between on-ART and pre-rebound, we performed a pseudobulk differential gene expression analysis. CD14++ and CD16++ monocytes had 375 and 34 differentially expressed genes, respectively, which included inflammatory, antiviral, and antigen-presentation genes (Figure 2B). Pathway analysis showed upregulation of inflammatory pathways, including tumor necrosis-factor-α (TNF-α) via nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), oxidative phosphorylation, interferon response, inflammation, and interleukin 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (IL-2/STAT5) signaling in monocytes as well as other cell subsets (Figure 2C). Specifically, 23 genes from the interferon-α (IFN-α) response pathway showed significant upregulation in monocyte subsets but did not significantly vary in CD4 or CD8 subsets (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). Expression of these pathways increased in multiple cell subsets, but we observed the greatest fold-change differences in monocyte subsets.

While the scRNAseq analysis revealed an increase in inflammatory cell states prior to detectable rebound, plasma proteomic analyses on a broader cohort of 23 participants showed no significant changes between on-ART and pre-rebound. Instead, we observed an incremental yet significant post-rebound increase in plasma levels of LAG3, TNF, GZMH, CRTAM, CXCL10, IL12, TRAIL, CD27, MIC-A/B, CD5, MMP12, GZMB, IL-18R1, CXCL9, PD-L2, GZMA, NCR1, CD83, SLAMF1, and IL-12B from on ART to after viral rebound (FDR < 0.05; Figure 2D and Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Here, we evaluated changes in the immune landscape of PWH throughout ATI to determine whether immune responses are detectable in peripheral blood prior to clinically detectable viral rebound. Our results, which show an increase in antiviral monocytes prior to detectable viral rebound, complement our earlier finding that these same donors experienced an increase in CD30+ CD4 T cells prior to detectable viremia [2].

We hypothesize that the increase in CD16++ monocytes represents shifting monocyte composition towards a migratory antiviral phenotype in response to undetectable HIV-1 recrudescence. About 80% of the monocyte compartment is made up of CD14++ monocytes, known as classical monocytes, which are poised for differentiation into monocyte-derived macrophages but can also differentiate through an intermediate category into CD16++ monocytes, known as nonclassical monocytes [7]. While recent literature separates intermediate monocytes from classical monocytes, scRNAseq cell labeling has difficulty differentiating the two, so we did not examine intermediate monocytes directly in this study [7]. CD16++ nonclassical monocytes are functionally distinct from the other subsets, with higher expression of antiviral genes and a patrolling phenotype, which helps alert the immune system to viral replication in tissues [7, 8]. The maintenance of the CD14++ classical monocyte population combined with a significant increase in CD16++ monocytes, observed here, indicates activation of the innate immune system for antiviral defense.

The increase in antiviral CD16++ monocyte frequency was consistent across all participants, with CD16++ monocytes making up 2% more of total PBMCs at pre-rebound than they did on-ART. Previous studies suggest that nonclassical monocytes are a first-line immune response to viruses, including HIV-1, as they increase in frequency after HIV acquisition and fluctuate in concordance with viral load through HIV progression [7, 9, 10]. Migration throughout the body after detecting HIV-1 activity in tissues could explain why this subset was measurable before detection of viremia in our study. Notably, we did not observe continued elevation of the CD16++ monocyte population post-rebound: we believe there are several potential explanations, including (1) the variation in time to sampling post-rebound, (2) an increase of other cell subsets responding to systemic viremia, or (3) a downregulation of inflammatory pathways to protect the body from sustained inflammation. The increase in CD16++ monocytes before detectable viremia could represent a relatively universal response to HIV-1 activation in localized tissues before systemic viral dissemination, which then goes on to diversify based on individual immune phenotypes.

The increasing expression of specific inflammatory pathways within expanding monocyte subsets supports the hypothesis that these cells not only increase in frequency but may be engaged in suppressing viral replication in tissues. In fact, at pre-rebound, both classical CD14++ and CD16++ monocytes were highly enriched in inflammatory pathways and responses related to IFN-γ and IFN-α. These pathways are an integral part of the antiviral response and are necessary for the production of cytokines to recruit immune cells to sites of viral replication [11]. Out of the 90 genes in the MSigDB hallmark gene pathway for IFN-α response, 23 were individually significantly upregulated in monocyte subsets (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4) [6]. Additionally, CD14++ and CD16++ monocytes increased expression of pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, indicating that energy requirements may increase in response to proliferation and pathogenesis. While none of the inflammatory proteins we measured were detectable before viral rebound, their presence post-rebound confirms the inflammatory state observed in the scRNAseq.

We were unable to measure the abundance of CD30-expressing lymphocytes in the present work because their frequency was too low to accurately quantify by scRNAseq. In addition, Olink does not have an assay that detects soluble CD30. Instead, our data suggest that host immune responses are detectable prior to HIV-1 viremia. While we only identified increases in inflammatory circulating proteins following HIV-1 recrudescence, we observed a significant increase in CD16++ monocytes and a significant increase in the expression of inflammatory pathways in monocyte subsets before HIV-1 RNA was detected in plasma.

Our study has several potential implications for HIV cure, most importantly in identifying a signal of imminent viral rebound. There is currently no noninvasive method for monitoring viral rebound in PWH undergoing treatment interruption [12]. Especially as the number of post-treatment viral controllers increases, and if current efforts to achieve durable virus control succeed, monitoring individuals for viral rebound will become paramount [13]. An assay that detects or predicts early virus spread would be particularly useful, as suggested in an earlier case study of sustained post-ART control [14]. While no specific genes assayed here show a big enough fold change to serve as an individual biomarker, they provide insight into the types of immune mechanisms that could potentially produce a biomarker in future studies. Additionally, our data have implications for the development of novel interventions. Theoretically, the early immune response to replicating HIV following treatment interruption might prove to be an essential factor in determining who does or does not control the virus [15].

This work demonstrates the need for more in-depth ATI studies examining immune responses in response to rebound viremia. While the current study is based on a small number of participants sampled infrequently during ATI, our results suggest that future studies with more frequent longitudinal sampling and intensive tissue sampling would greatly benefit the field. These studies would have the potential to examine early viral activity in tissues, to explain variation in time to HIV-1 rebound, and shed light into potential etiologies of post-treatment and post-intervention control of viremia.
