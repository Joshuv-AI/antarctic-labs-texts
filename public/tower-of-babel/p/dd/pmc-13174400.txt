Investigation of surface properties of various parts of Angelica sylvestris by inverse gas chromatography
Authors: Melek Kubra Habiboglu, Birol Isik, Fatih Cakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere are nine different varieties of Angelica sylvestris plants found in Asia, America, and Europe, and all of them, especially their roots, have medicinal properties. It is important to determine the surface properties of medicinal A. sylvestris that can be easily found in nature. In this study, it was aimed to investigate the surface characteristics of the root, stem, and flower parts of the A. sylvestris. For this purpose, gas chromatography columns were prepared by separating the root, stem, and flower parts of the plant, and the surface properties were determined by the inverse gas chromatography (IGC) method. The IGC study was performed at infinite dilution with polar and non-polar solvents. Surface characteristics of different parts of the plant were determined through analysis of retention diagrams. From IGC experiments, the changes in the acidic and basic character of the parts of the plant were determined and compared with each other. As a result of this comparison, it was found that all KD/KA values were less than 1 and changed as flower > root > stem.

Introduction

There is a growing demand for industrial goods made from renewable resources. Many plant species are utilized as sources of non-food industrial products in addition to their use in food production (1). The utilization of natural products possessing therapeutic properties dates back to ancient times. Throughout human history, natural products have held a significant role in mystical-religious practices, whilst different cultures offer unique perspectives on health and disease. Plants, as the primary group of organisms on Earth, possess multifunctional and large surfaces, yielding a plethora of intriguing properties (2). The noteworthy features of plant surfaces are super hydrophilicity and super hydrophobicity. The notion of surface energy “γ” is commonly deemed an excess energy term as a surface can be considered a layer that has a bound energy (3).

Comprehending surface energy is crucial for all industrial procedures involving the interactions between particles, such as coating, wetting, agglomeration of solids, powder processing, dispersion of liquids, and catalysis (4–6). Coatings may alter surface energy, which has a significant impact on various industrial applications and processes involving particulate and fibrous materials' surface-free energy. However, binding to solid surfaces is not always desirable (4, 7).

Contact angle and wetting procedures are the gold standards for comparing surface quality among the many contemporary surface analytical technologies available (8). Additionally, inverse gas chromatography (IGC) at infinite dilution has gained widespread acceptance for evaluating the non-specific surface free energy of solid materials (9). IGC is a versatile vapor probe technique that may be used to analyze powders and fibrous materials. It works by reversing the traditional use of gas chromatography (GC). It is a flexible tool for surface analysis since it can handle sample porosity, uneven surface topographies, and surface inhomogeneity (10).

Since most practical surfaces are heterogeneous, to solve this problem, IGC analysis is performed by infinite dilution on mixtures of known heterogeneity (11). IGC can be used to examine the surface energy of solid materials and has been effectively employed in physiochemical studies of solids for more than 50 years (12, 13). In IGC investigations, the solid sample that is being studied is put inside the column. The surface characteristics of the sample are next investigated by inserting known vapor probes into the column via an inert carrier gas. Studies on dispersive interactions usually involve non-polar solute molecules, such as alkanes, whereas studies on acid–base interactions require the use of polar solute molecules, such as water or ethanol (12, 13).

In this study, the surface properties of A. sylvestris roots, stems, and flower parts were studied using IGC at infinite dilution. The surface properties of the plant parts were then compared with each other. Moreover, the dispersive surface energy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document}) values were calculated according to the Dorris-Gray, Donnet, Hamieh, and Schultz methods and the results of these methods were compared.

Materials and methods

Roots, stems and flower parts of A. sylvestris were obtained from an herbalist in Istanbul, Turkey. The polar (ethyl acetate (EA), chloroform (TCM), acetone (Ace), dichloromethane (DCM), diethyl ether (DEE), and tetrahydrofuran (THF)) and non-polar solvents (n-hexane (Hx), n-heptane (Hp), n-octane (O), n-nonane (N), n-decan (D)) used in the study were obtained from Sigma Aldrich and utilized in their purest form (>99%) without the need for any further purification process. All organic solvents polar and non-polar solvents were used to pass through the powdered plant material packed into the chromatographic column as the stationary phase.

The surface properties of the materials were evaluated using an Agilent Technologies 6890 N gas chromatograph with a thermal conductivity detector. Silane-treated glass wool obtained from Alltech Associates Inc. was utilized to seal the ends of both the chromatographic column, which had dimensions of 1/8″ outer diameter and 2.10 mm inner diameter. The 0.5-meter-long chromatographic column was washed with distilled water and then dried in an oven at 110°C. The A. sylvestris plant was cleaned to remove soil and dust residues and then thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove impurities and contaminants. After cleaning, the plant material was dried in a vacuum oven at 110°C for 48 h. After drying, the materials were ground using a grinder. The particles were then reduced to 80 to 100 mesh sizes with the help of appropriate sieves. Adsorbents with these particle sizes were loaded onto the chromatographic column to weigh ~0.6 g. The filling process was carried out without any problems by adding plants from the other end of a 0.5-meter chromatographic column, one end of which was closed with glass wool, and by applying vibration during filling. After filling, the other end of the column was completely closed with silanized glass wool. Subsequently, each chromatographic column was subjected to a 20 h conditioning process at 120°C. By conditioning the completely dry plants in the column, the possibility of moisture in the column was reduced to a negligible level. After conditioning, the chromatographic columns were sequentially connected to an Agilent Technologies 6890 N gas chromatograph, and experimental studies were performed. Throughout the study, ultrapure helium, which served as the carrier gas, flowed through the column at a constant flow rate of 3.6 mL per minute. Helium was used as carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 6 cm3/min measured using a soap bubble flowmeter. The chromatographic column temperature varied across the experimental sessions, set at 303.2, 308.2, 313.2, 318.2, 323.2, and 328.2 K. To ensure accuracy and proper data determination, thermodynamic equilibrium must be reached within the selected temperature range and linear graphs must be obtained. Therefore, the temperature range between 303.2 and 328.2 K, where thermodynamic equilibrium is reached, was selected for preliminary studies. Each experiment was performed a minimum of five times to ensure consistency. Administration of organic solvents was executed using a 1.0 μL Hamilton syringe, while a 10 μL Hamilton syringe was utilized to obtain the air peak. To ensure infinitely dilute concentration, 0.1 mL was drawn into a 1 mL syringe, then aspirated into air and injected into the device, creating peaks. Injection of all solvents, including air, into the device was carried out manually with the specified syringes.

The surface properties of a solid material can be characterized by determining several physicochemical parameters. This parameter provides insight into the dispersion interactions between organic solvents and the stationary phase. Discussion

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values offer insights into the force field characteristics of high-energy sites within materials. Typically, these values are directly related to surface density metrics like atom polarizability and ionization energy (40, 41). Moreover, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values tend to decrease as temperature rises, inversely proportional to it. Table 1 illustrates that, except for the Hamieh method, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values calculated by various methods are consistent with each other. When the results of the model proposed by Hamieh are examined, it is seen that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values ​​are ~50% higher than the other models. The reason for the difference in these results is explained by Hamieh as the change in the surface areas of alkanes with temperature (27). Additionally, it's observed that across all methods, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values diminish with higher temperatures. In this study, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values of the A. sylvestris roots, stems, and flower parts were calculated. As seen in Table 1, it was found that the surface energy varied as root > stem > flower for all methods.

When all parts of the A. sylvestris plant were examined, it was found that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $-\Delta{G}_A^S$\end{document} values found as a result of the study showed the highest values in amphoteric probes such as Ace and EA. In addition, when the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $-\Delta{G}_A^S$\end{document} values of basic solvents (THF and DEE) and acidic solvents (TCM and DCM) were compared, it was found that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $-\Delta{G}_A^S$\end{document} values were higher with basic solvent (42).

As seen in Table 2, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta{H}_A^S$\end{document} values for each polar solvent were negative for A. sylvestris roots, stems, and flower parts. This shows that the adsorption of polar solvents on various parts of A. sylvestris occurs exothermally. Stronger acids have better conjugate bases than weaker acids. By losing a proton, acids become a more stable molecule (usually the conjugate base is stabilized by (more) conjugates) and have lower energy than the acid. This energy gap makes them a good acid. A strong acid is one which ionizes completely to produce hydrogen ions when in water. The strength of an acid determines the amount of hydrogen ions present for every mole of acid dissolved in water. In other terms, the stronger the acid the greater the amount of hydrogen ions present in the solution, and hence the more alkali that should be required to neutralize the solution, hence increasing the enthalpy. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${K}_D/{K}_A$\end{document} value for the flower region of the angelica plant was found to be 0.998. This value corresponds to an almost neutral value. Therefore, the enthalpy values were slightly lower than the other parts of the plant.

Lewis acid–base interactions are prevalent in various chemical processes and have diverse applications, including synthesis (43), solar cells (44), semiconductors (45), batteries, (46), and catalysis (47). These interactions enable precise tuning of material properties, influencing everything from molecular structures to more complex, organized assemblies. When the examined parts of the angelica plant were examined, it was found by IGC that the surface of the flower part had values ​​close to an amphoteric behavior. However, it was determined that it showed acidic properties, although very little. This situation also showed itself in the changes in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta{H}_A^S$\end{document} values ​​of the studied polar solvents. The fact that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta{H}_A^S$\end{document} values ​​of the flower part of the A. sylvestris have lower values ​​than the root and stem regions can be explained by this acidic property close to amphoteric. It was observed that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta{H}_A^S$\end{document} values ​​of three different parts of the A. sylvestris decreased in the following order: THF > DEE > EA > Ace > DCM > TCM.

Conclusion

This study aimed to examine the surface characteristics of the root, stem, and flower parts of the A. sylvestris. The surface properties of A. sylvestris were assessed through IGC experiments conducted at infinite dilution. Subsequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values were calculated using the Dorris-Gray, Donnet, Hamieh, and Schultz methods based on the results of the IGC experiments. When these methods were compared, it was found that R2 values changed as Hamieh > Schultz > Donnet > Dorris-Gray. It is supported by the results of this study that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values effect with temperature on the surface area of n-alkanes, methylene group (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $-{CH}_2-$\end{document}), and polar molecules, which is the basis of the Hamieh method. In all four calculated methods, it was determined that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\gamma}_S^D$\end{document} values of the plant parts decreased as the temperature increased. Negative adsorption enthalpy values indicate that the adsorption of all solvents is exothermic within the three parts of the A. sylvestris examined. Besides, from the acidity-basicity constants, it was determined that the surfaces of the three parts of the plant examined were basic. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${K}_D/{K}_A$\end{document} values of A. sylvestris roots, stems, and flower parts are 0.988, 0.962, and 0.903, respectively. These results showed us that the flower has an almost neutral structure, and the root and stem show near-neutral basic properties.
