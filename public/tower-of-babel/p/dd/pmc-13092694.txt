From deficit to recovery: postoperative neurological improvements in patients with severe cubital tunnel syndrome
Authors: Anne-Kathrin Uerschels, Mehdi Chihi, Angelina Olbrich, Annika Lenkeit, Tim Hagenacker, Ben Stolte, Ulrich Sure, Oleh Danylyak, Džiugas Meška, Karsten H Wrede, Oliver Gembruch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCubital tunnel syndrome (CuTS) is a prevalent focal neuropathy causing motor and sensory deficits in the hand. Some patients present with marked muscle atrophy and impaired hand function, indicating significant nerve damage.Research questionThe aim of this prospective study was to ascertain the long-term benefits of surgical intervention for these patients.Material and methodsPatients diagnosed with CuTS who underwent surgical treatment in the authors' department between January 2018 and December 2021 were screened. Patients with visible muscle atrophy and severe hand weakness, classified as grade IIb or III according to the modified McGowan classification, were included within this prospective study. A comprehensive data set was collected and analysed, encompassing clinical data, demographics, high-resolution ultrasound examinations and electrophysiological examinations. These were collected and analysed preoperatively, postoperatively, and at six and twelve months after surgery.ResultsA total of 40 patients (26 male (65%), mean age: 55.02 ± 1.88 years) were successfully enrolled in the study. Of those, 18 patients (45%) underwent endoscopically assisted in situ decompression, while open decompression and transposition were performed in the remaining 22 individuals (55%). Neurologically, 24 patients (58.54%) were classified as grade IIb. Postoperative hand power significantly improved (p < 0.001) at six and twelve months after surgery. Neurological improvement based on modified McGowan classification was also significant six (p = 0.016) and twelve months (p = 0.002) after surgery.Discussion and conclusionPatients with severe CuTS neurologically recover after decompression. Therefore, surgical therapy could be offered to those patients.Keywords: Cubital tunnel syndrome, Ulnar neuropathy, Modified McGowan classification, Surgical treatment, Postoperative recovery

Introduction

Cubital tunnel syndrome (CuTS) was first described by Feindel and Stratford in 1958 (Feindel and Stratford, 1958). It is the second most common upper extremity compressive neuropathy, affecting 20.9 to 24.7 per 100,000 people annually (Bartels and Verbeek, 2007). Men are affected nearly twice as often as woman (Assmus et al., 2011). It can be classified into primary (idiopathic) and secondary (symptomatic) forms, the latter comprising a heterogeneous group of pathologies. The most frequent cause of compression is the humeroulnar arcade (Osborne's ligament). However, the aetiology of symptomatic CuTS appears to be a combination of compression, traction, and friction of the ulnar nerve (Graf et al., 2023).

Elbow movement from full extension to 135° of flexion leads to a 30% to 41% reduction in cubital tunnel volume with a corresponding 7-fold increase in intraneural pressure (Gelberman et al., 1998).

Known risk factors for CuTS are comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, rheumatic diseases, obesity, older age and economic distress (Zhang et al., 2023).

Symptoms include numbness and tingling (at night) of the 4th and 5th finger, especially when the elbow is bent, aching pain on the inside of the elbow, weak grip and clumsiness due to muscle weakness in the affected arm and hand, and muscle atrophy. In late stages, clawing of the 4th and 5th finger is typical, showing the axonal loss of ulnar nerve branches of the intrinsic hand muscles (Graf et al., 2023). Activities requiring elbow flexion, and forced positions such as holding the phone nearby the ear, can exacerbate symptoms (Andrews et al., 2018).

Patients with CuTS are more likely to present with muscle atrophy initially compared to those with carpal tunnel syndrome (Mallette et al., 2007). Muscle atrophy, indicative of severe and long-lasting nerve damage, is found in 13-79% of patients (Assmus et al., 2011; Goldberg et al., 1989; Keiner et al., 2009).

The severity of symptoms was first classified by McGowan in 1950 (Mc, 1950) and later modified by Goldberg in 1989 (Goldberg et al., 1989). The modified McGowan classification focuses on intrinsic hand muscle atrophy:

Frantz et al. demonstrated in a retrospective study analysing the neurological outcome of 156 patients with both mild and severe ulnar neuropathy that 88% of patients improved at short-term follow-up (Frantz et al., 2019). However, Goldberg et al. were unable to detect neurological improvement in patients with poor preoperative hand function (Goldberg et al., 1989).

Conservative therapy may be considered for mild and intermittent symptoms, including nocturnal elbow immobilization with splints, local corticosteroid injections, nerve gliding exercises, and behavioral modifications (e. g. avoiding putting the elbow on the desk) (An et al., 2017; Padua et al., 2002; Shah et al., 2013). Previous studies have reported success rates ranging from 50% to 88% using nonoperative approaches (An et al., 2017; Padua et al., 2002; Shah et al., 2013).

Svernlöv et al. observed 90% clinical improvement in patients with early-stage disease, regardless of whether they received education alone, wore nighttime orthoses, or performed nerve glides (Svernlov et al., 2009).

Surgical treatment of CuTS includes open or endoscopic in situ decompression of the nerve, as well as subcutaneous, intramuscular, or submuscular transposition and medial epicondylectomy (Graf et al., 2023).

To date, however, there has been a paucity of studies specifically addressing the postoperative neurological outcome of surgically treated CuTS patients with intrinsic hand muscle atrophy.

The objective of this study was to analyse surgical therapy and postoperative outcomes in patients with CuTS and severe neurological deficits (grade IIb and grade III according to the modified McGowan classification) and to evaluate the potential benefits of surgery in these patients.

To the best of our knowledge, no prospective study has addressed the long-term outcomes of patients with high-grade ulnar nerve damage (grade IIb and grade III according to the modified McGowan classification) in order to draw meaningful conclusions regarding the neurological prognosis for patients with this condition.

Material and methods

This prospective study included patients with surgically treated CuTS between January 2018 and December 2021.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients with severe neurological deficits (Grade IIb and Grade III according to the modified McGowan classification) were included (Fig. 1). In contrast, those with no or mild neurological deficits (Grade I and Grade IIa) were excluded. The inclusion and categorisation criteria were based on the presence of clearly visible muscle atrophy of the intrinsic hand muscles and hand weakness. This manifests as difficulty in abduction and adduction of the thumb and fingers (grade IIb) or, at an advanced stage, a clawed hand position (grade III). C8 nerve root involvement was ruled out in all cases by electrophysiological testing prior to surgery. In cases there electrophysiological testing was not sufficient to rule out a C8 nerve root involvement, MRI of the cervical spine was performed. One patient, who postoperatively developed a new complex regional pain syndrome, cancelled its consent to participate within the prospective study and was therefore excluded from analysis.

Data acquisition

Clinical data, demographics, high-resolution ultrasound examinations, and electrophysiological examinations were collected and analysed preoperatively, postoperatively, and at six and twelve months after surgery. Photographic and video documentation was conducted during all inspection periods.

DASH score

The DASH (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand) Score is a well-established questionnaire for upper extremity disorders. The scale does not differentiate between individual joints of the upper extremities; rather, it employs 21 questions to assess the extent to which patients have experienced difficulties performing specific activities in the previous week. The questions are answered irrespective of which arm or hand is required for the respective activity. The DASH Score is not joint-specific, but rather measures the function of both extremities as a whole. The scale ranges from 0 to 100, with 100 representing severe disorders (Hudak et al., 1996).

Evaluation of hand strength using Martin Vigorimeter

Hand strength testing is a simple method for assessing general muscle strength and identifying functional deficiencies. The Martin Vigorimeter hand strength test was performed for comparison. The Martin Vigorimeter is a device that utilises pressure balls (medium and large rubber balls), which are adjusted to correspond to the size of the patient's hand in order to measure maximum strength. The device can register and display pressure increases, with results presented in kilopascals (kPa), millimetres of mercury (mmHg), or pounds per square inch (psi). The interpretation of the Martin Vigorimeter results was conducted following the normal values established by Neumann et al., in 2017 (Neumann et al., 2017).

High-resolution nerve sonography

Routine high-resolution nerve sonography (HRNS) of the ulnar nerve was performed at baseline and repeated at six and twelve months. The most frequently analysed parameter is the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the ulnar nerve at the level of the medial epicondyle (ME). The maximum CSA in the area 4 cm proximal and distal to the ME is also of diagnostic importance (Smith and Gandhi Mehta, 2023). Numerous publications and reviews have presented a cut-off value of >10 mm2 as a diagnostic criterion for CuTS, with a sensitivity and specificity of approximately 88% (Beekman et al., 2011; Chang et al., 2018; Thoirs et al., 2008). Furthermore, studies have demonstrated a correlation between CSA values and electroneurographic lesion grading (mild, moderate, and severe), with grading ranging from 11 to 18 mm2 (Volpe et al., 2009). Additionally, HRNS visualization of ulnar nerve changes at the elbow allowed comparison of the affected and unaffected sides.

Electrophysiological examination

Electromyographers have utilized motor, mixed nerve, and sensory conduction velocity and compound motor action potentials (CMAP) across the elbow, and compared it with segments of the ulnar nerve in the forearm and upper arm in order to confirm the clinical diagnosis. In the majority of cases, there is greater involvement of the motor than the sensory nervous system. Consequently, studies of motor conduction are the most useful in the evaluation of cubital tunnel syndrome (Werner, 2013). A nerve conduction velocity (NCV) of <50 m/s across the elbow or when conduction velocity from the above-elbow to below-elbow segment is slowed to >10 m/s compared with the below-elbow to wrist segment is considered pathological (Practice parameter for electrodiagnostic studies, 1999). A reduction in ulnar nerve deflections by more than half during nerve conduction velocity testing indicates a pathological condition (Podnar et al., 2017).

Surgical technique and postoperative care

Patients were surgically treated either by endoscopically assisted in situ neurolysis or by circular open neurolysis, followed by subcutaneous transposition to the flexor side. In cases without bony changes or subluxation, endoscopically assisted decompression was performed. In cases where anatomical changes in the bone structure or subluxation were present, ulnar nerve transposition was performed.

The surgical procedure was performed under general anaesthesia, with the patient in a supine position and the affected arm abducted and resting on an arm table. A tourniquet was applied with a cuff pressure of 80 to 100 mmHg above the intraoperative systolic pressure.

A 1-2 cm straight incision was made in close proximity to the sulcal ridge and the ulnar nerve was identified. The retractor was then introduced into the subcutaneous pocket. The fibrous ligament overlying the ulnar nerve and the fascia of the flexor carpi ulnaris muscle were meticulously dissected longitudinally over a length of 10 to 12 cm distal to the sulcus. Proximal decompression was performed up to the exit point of the ulnar nerve under the medial intermuscular septum. Haemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed in two layers.

For open decompression and transposition of the ulnar nerve, a 6 to 8 cm long slightly curved skin incision was made around the ME. The subcutaneous tissue was dissected, and the ulnar nerve was identified in the proximal cubital tunnel. The Osborne ligament was completely dissected longitudinally, followed by circular exposure of the nerve over its entire course. Care was taken to release the ulnar nerve from the intermuscular septum proximally to the deep floor aponeurosis distally.

Postoperative neurological examination was performed directly after surgery. Patients were discharged well-being at the day of the surgery. Neurological follow-up and wound control were performed one week after surgery at our outpatient centre. Additional neurological examinations were performed at routine follow-up six and twelve months after surgery.

Statistical analysis

All analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29, for MacOS (Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data are presented as frequency and percentage. The paired-sample t-test was used to determine whether the mean difference between two continuous variables was statistically significant. The McNemar test was used to determine differences on a dichotomous variable between two related groups. Spearman's correlation was used to assess associations between an ordinal and a dichotomous variable, while the Mantel-Haenszel test of trend evaluated associations between two ordinal variables. P-values <0.05 in two-sided testing were considered significant.

An idiopathic CuTS was present in 83.3% (25 of 30 patients), while an elbow joint trauma was detected in 16.7% (5 of 30 patients).

Symptom duration from beginning of the symptoms until the diagnosis of CuTS varied between one and 60 months. Mean symptom duration was 13 ± 16.2 months. In 30% (9 of 30 patients) symptom duration until diagnosis of CuTS was shorter than 6 months. Symptom duration of 6 to 12 months was detected in 30% (9 of 30 patients), while in 40% (12 of 30 patients) symptom duration until diagnosis was longer than 12 months.

Every patient suffered from hypoaestesia affecting the ulnar part of the 4th finger and the 5th finger. Postoperative improvement of sensory deficits were detected in 76.7% (23 of 30 patients), while sensory deficits remained unchanged in 23.3% (7 of 30 patients).

A Froment sign was preoperatively positive in every patient, while it was negative at last follow up in 56.7% (17 of 30 patients).

A Tinel sign was positive preoperatively in 64.3% (18 of 28 patients), while it was negative in 35.7% (10 patients of 28 patients). In two patients a Tinel sign was not described within the neurological examination. Postoperatively, Tinel sign was not dectetable at 12 months follow-up in 3.6% (1 of 28 patients), while it remained unchanged in 96.3% (27 of 28 patients).

Hand power

The operative treatment elicited a statistically significant improvement of the hand power at six months postoperatively compared to preoperatively of 15.19 kPa (95% CI: 8.06 to 22.32, t (31) = 4.34, p < 0.001) and at 12 months postoperatively compared to preoperatively of 20.51 kPa (95% CI: 12.94 to 28.07), t (29) = 5.54, p < 0.001). Additionally, improvement of hand power was also significant at 12 months postoperatively compared to hand power at six months postoperatively (p < 0.05). However, the clinically relevant improvement of the hand power after surgical treatment was more significant within the first six months after surgery (mean = 15.19 kPa) than from six months to 12 months after surgery (mean = 4.68 kPa). Therefore, surgical treatment led to ongoing improvement of hand power over the follow-up period of 12 months. More information can be find in Supplementary Data, Table 2.

Electrophysiology

Statistically significant increase of the CMAP of 0.42 mV postoperatively compared to preoperatively was detected (95% CI: 0.14 to 0.71, t (11) = 3.35, p < 0.05, see Supplementary Data, Table 3).

Additionally, the postoperative NCV-improvement of 9.99 m/s was statistically significant compared to preoperative (95% CI: 5.76 to 14.221, t (27) = 4.84, p < 0.001, see Supplementary Data, Table 3).

High-resolution nerve sonography

Statistical analysis revealed significant increase of the CSA of 0.01 cm2 six months postoperatively compared to preoperatively (95% CI: 0.00018 to 0.01982, t (14) = 2.185, p < 0.05), while twelve months postoperatively CSA showed a significant decrease of - 0.02 cm2 compared to preoperatively (95% CI: −0.03452 to −0.0730) cm2, t (21) = −3.195, p < 0.05, see Supplementary Data, Table 4).

DASH score

The operative treatment elicited a statistically significant decrease of the DASH score postoperatively compared to preoperatively of −10.25 (95% CI: −16.23 to −4.28 points, t (29) = −3.51, p < 0.05, see Supplementary Data, Table 5).

Modified McGowan classification

Postoperative changes according to the modified McGowan classifications are described in Table 1. Using the McNemar test, the operative treatment elicited a statistically significant improvement in ulnar motor function, as assessed by the degree of muscle atrophy, at six months (p < 0.05) and at twelve months (p < 0.05) compared to preoperatively. The improvement was not significantly relevant between six and twelve months postoperatively (p = 0.250, see Supplementary Data, Table 6).

Changes in hand power according to the surgical technique

Endoscopic decompression of CuTS led to significant improvement in hand power six months after surgery with a mean improvement of 17.38 kPa (p = 0.006). Mean improvement of 18.51 kPa twelve months after surgery was also significant (p = 0.003) compared to preoperative hand power. Significant changes in hand power were also detected in patients treated with open decompression. Mean improvement in hand power of 13.27 kPa was detected six months after surgery (p = 0.012), while mean hand power improvement of 22.51 kPa was also significant twelve months after surgery (p = <0.001, see Supplementary Data, Table 7).

However, there was no significant difference between the two decompression methods according to the mean changes in hand power at six months (p = 0.455) and at twelve months after surgery (p = 0.775).

Patient's profession

Surgery did not affect the patient's profession in 22 cases. Of those, six patients were pensioners, four patinets were “long-term unemployed” and not able to work due to different circumstances, 7 patients had a physically demanding job (nurse, technician, cleaner, etc.) and five patients worked in majority in an office.

Interestingly, 7 patients were “long-term sick leave” prior to surgery. Of those, three patients were able to work after surgery in a full-time job (technician, accountant, machine operator), while one changed the profession with the aid of the “job centre” (nurse to computer scientist). An early pension was applied by two patintes and one patint was not able to work due to a severe depression not associated with the surgery. Profession of one person was not available.

Lost to follow-up

One patient died before the six months control and one patient cancelled its consent to participate within the prospective study and was therefore excluded from analysis. Eight patients were lost to follow-up due to the COVID pandemic. Therefore, final analysis was performed with 30 included patients.

Discussion

Our prospective study showed that motor recovery was significant over a follow-up period of twelve months in patients with severe muscle atrophy (grade IIb or grade III according to the modified Mc Gowan classification after CuTS surgery.

The CuTS is the second most prevalent upper extremity compressive neuropathy, following carpal tunnel syndrome (Bartels and Verbeek, 2007) This condition, if left untreated, can significantly impair an individual's quality of life, necessitating accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment to mitigate further damage and prevent symptom exacerbation (Assmus et al., 2011). Nevertheless, considerable controversy persists regarding the optimal diagnostic and treatment approaches for CuTS.

A cautious clinical approach is generally recommended for patients presenting with mild symptoms of CuTS in the absence of neurological deficits. This typically involves a watchful waiting strategy supplemented by conservative management techniques such as nocturnal elbow immobilization using splints, administration of local corticosteroid injections, nerve gliding exercises, and behavioral modifications aimed at minimizing ulnar nerve irritation.

Conversely, more severe presentations—particularly those involving motor deficits—may warrant surgical intervention to avert irreversible neurological damage. In cases where significant deficits are already present, surgery may be necessary to prevent secondary complications such as joint contractures.

A range of comparative studies have been conducted, showing the effectiveness of all three techniques, but eventually none has proved to be superior (Bacle et al., 2014). In our study, open in situ decompression and endoscopic in situ decompression did not show any significant differences in postoperative hand power changes.

The German Guideline “Diagnosis and therapy of cubital tunnel syndrome – state of the art” (Assmus et al., 2017) advocates a patient-centered approach, encompassing a thorough medical history and comprehensive clinical examination. While clinical assessment often suffices for diagnosing ulnar nerve compression at the elbow, preoperative electroneurographic diagnosis is strongly recommended to confirm the diagnosis, facilitate differential diagnosis, monitor disease progression or treatment efficacy, and detect recurrence (Assmus et al., 2017).

The severity of the condition before surgery and the neurological outcomes after surgery were systematically monitored in our study that was conducted on a prospective basis. Electrophysiological follow-up examinations demonstrated statistically significant improvements in CMAP and NCV at six and twelve months postoperatively. These electrophysiological improvements are concomitant with a marked postoperative enhancement in hand strength and observable aesthetic recovery of the affected hand. These findings call into question a commonly held, albeit as yet unsubstantiated, assumption that patients with high-grade ulnar nerve palsies do not experience substantial improvements in NCV or motor nerve amplitude that would indicate functional recovery. However, the findings of this study contradict this hypothesis, providing prospective evidence to the contrary. Nonetheless results of electrophysiological examination may be negatively affected by aging and comorbidities, limiting postoperative improvement. Anker et al. investigated the relationship between the result of preoperative electrophysiology and postoperative treatment success twelve months after CuTS surgery. They concluded that older age, male sex, and concomitant diabetes mellitus are associated with more severe preoperative electrophysiological nerve affection and that this, in turn, may be associated with a poorer treatment outcome (Anker et al., 2022).

Furthermore, Cuneo et al. observed that clinical symptomatic, but electrodiagnostic-negative patients may experience earlier disease progression. Therefore, early identification and treatment could prevent demyelination and fibrosis, leading to better clinical outcomes. The authors highlighted the importance of establishing standardized diagnostic criteria for CuTS that include electrodiagnostic-negative patients, as these individuals may also benefit from surgical treatment and experience significant improvement (Cuneo et al., 2024).

In recent years, imaging techniques such as HRNS have gained prominence as valuable adjuncts to electrophysiology. Unlike neurography, these modalities enable direct visualization of morphological changes under movement and provide more precise localization of compression sites.

An increase in CSA of the ulnar nerve was observed in the study cohort, consistent with values reported in the existing literature (Beekman et al., 2011). Interestingly, CSA continued to increase at the six-month postoperative follow-up, followed by a reduction at twelve months after surgery. These fluctuations may represent aspects of the normal postoperative healing process. The initial postoperative nerve swelling, evidenced by increased CSA, could be attributable to intraoperative manipulation, whereas the subsequent physiological reduction observed at twelve months may reflect the resolution of wound healing. Despite an extensive review of the current literature, no additional clinical studies were identified that specifically describe postoperative changes in CSA. Statistically significant neurological improvement (hand power) was observed at both six (p < 0.001) and twelve months postoperatively (p < 0.001) in comparison to the preoperative baseline. The disparity in neurological outcomes between the six and twelve months postoperative intervals was also found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). Consequently, patients exhibiting severe neurological deficits derive substantial benefit from surgical intervention, with neurological recovery being a feasible prospect even one year post-surgery. Nevertheless, the modified McGowan classification did not demonstrate a significant correlation with postoperative improvement between six and twelve months following surgery. (p = 0.250).

However, studies have demonstrated that patients with lower preoperative McGowan grades (e.g., grade I or IIA) tend to experience better postoperative recovery, including improved sensory and motor function, compared to those with more severe grades (IIb or III). However, while the modified McGowan grade correlates with objective neurological measures, it does not consistently align with patient-reported outcomes, indicating a potential discrepancy between clinical improvement and perceived disability (Choi et al., 2022; Esen Hasturk et al., 2012).

Neurological improvement was additionally assessed using DASH score, which revealed significant functional enhancement at both six and twelve months postoperatively. Similarly, Yeoman et al. reported postoperative neurological improvement using the Quick DASH questionnaire, with mean scores improving significantly from 39 to 30 following surgery. Patient-reported postoperative satisfaction was high, with 86% indicating favorable outcomes (Yeoman et al., 2020). Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that individuals presenting with severe symptoms — classified as Grade IIb or Grade III according to the modified McGowan classification — were not evaluated as a distinct subgroup. This omission suggests that the observed postoperative outcomes in these patients may be comparatively poorer, likely due to their substantially more compromised baseline status. Nevertheless, measurable improvement in functional hand use in daily life was still evident within this cohort.

In our study, eight patients (26.7%) with severe muscle atrophy (Grade III) followed-up 12 months after surgery did not improve according to the modified Mc Gowan classification. These success rates are similar to those published by Bartels et al. (2005) and Biggs et al. (Biggs and Curtis, 2006). Revision surgery rates vary between 0% and 41% in a meta-regression analysis with a mean revision surgery rate of 3.6% to 11.9% (Reichenbach et al., 2024). In our cohort, revision surgery was performed in one case (3.3%) two years after the initial surgery due to recurrent symptoms.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The number of patients included at a single centre was limited due to the rarity of CuTs operations at a tertiary medical centre. Therefore, statistics might be influenced by the small sample size. However, due to the global impact of the coronavirus epidemic, it was necessary to discontinue the inclusion of subjects in 2020.

Conclusion

Surgical treatment showed significant motor improvement in atrophic changes of the hand, as well as, favorable clinical outcomes in majority of the patients. Therefore, surgical treatment may be offered to those patients independently of severity of atrophic changes.
