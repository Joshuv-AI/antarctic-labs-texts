Efficacy of Sirolimus Versus Paclitaxel-Coated Balloons for Coronary Revascularization: An Updated Meta-Analysis with GRADE Assessment
Authors: Matthew A. Wijayanto, Christopher D. Tristan, Erlangga M. Kynaya, Annisa A. Rahma, Putri S. Harimurti, Arwin S. Mangkuanom, Amir A. Alkatiri, Doni Firman, Bambang B. Siswanto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drug-coated balloons are an emerging strategy for coronary revascularization. Despite their increasing use, the comparative efficacy of sirolimus-versus paclitaxel-coated balloons (SCB vs PCB) remains uncertain. This updated meta-analysis evaluated clinical and angiographic outcomes of SCB and PCB in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect was performed on August 28, 2025. Studies directly comparing SCB and PCB were included. The primary endpoint was target lesion failure (TLF), which was defined as cardiac death, target vessel myocardial infarction (TVMI), or target lesion revascularization (TLR). On follow-up angiography, secondary endpoints consisted of minimal lumen diameter (MLD), late lumen loss (LLL), and diameter stenosis. Pairwise meta-analyses, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses were performed. The certainty of evidence was evaluated with GRADE. Thirteen studies with 5947 patients (4097 SCB and 1850 PCB) were analyzed. SCB and PCB showed no significant differences in TLF (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.44), cardiac death (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.56 to 2.53), TLR (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.37), and TVMI (RR 1.82, 95% CI 0.58 to 5.72). Subgroup analyses by lesion type and study design were consistent. Angiographic outcomes were comparable between groups, except for smaller insegment MLD with SCB (MD −0.10 mm, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.02; p = 0.01), although this finding was not robust in sensitivity analysis. Certainty of evidence was moderate for clinical outcomes and low to moderate for angiographic outcomes, primarily downgraded due to imprecision and inconsistency. In conclusion, SCB and PCB demonstrated comparable efficacy in PCI, with no significant differences in clinical and angiographic endpoints, irrespective of lesion types.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) continues to be the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally [1,2]. The Global Burden of Disease 2022 study indicates that ischemic heart disease represented the leading cause of death and disability-adjusted life years worldwide, highlighting its significant impact on public health [3]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) utilizing drug-eluting stents (DES) has emerged as a fundamental approach in the management of CAD [4]. Considering advancements in drug-eluting stent (DES) technology, such as thinner stent struts and enhanced polymer formulations, PCI failure remains a clinical problem, with in-stent restenosis (ISR) accounting for approximately 10% of PCI procedures in the United States [5].

Drug-coated balloons (DCB) were created as an alternative revascularization method to prevent additional stent deployment, offering a cage-free option to maintain normal vessel anatomy and function [6,7]. In contrast to stents, DCBs administer antiproliferative agents directly to the artery wall without leaving a permanent metallic scaffold, thereby mitigating the risks of late stent thrombosis and neoatherosclerosis [8]. Among the existing DCB, paclitaxel-coated balloons (PCB) were the first to exhibit efficacy owing to the drug’s lipophilic characteristics and sustained tissue retention [9]. Recently, sirolimus-coated balloons (SCB) have been introduced, supported by the advantageous safety and efficacy profile of sirolimus and its analogs in DES systems [10]. Nonetheless, the administration of sirolimus in DCB presents technical difficulties due to its reduced lipophilicity and fast tissue washout, necessitating advancements like crystalline coating and phospholipid encapsulation technologies [11].

Several meta-analyses have previously compared SCB and PCB for coronary interventions [12–14]. However, these analyses were limited by smaller sample sizes, inclusion of unpublished conference data, and the absence of formal certainty of evidence analysis. Therefore, we performed an updated systematic review and meta-analysis, incorporating contemporary evidence to provide a more robust and clinical comparison of SCB versus PCB.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in compliance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) reporting standards [15]. As a comprehensive protocol, the protocol number CRD420251126139 has been registered in the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews).

2.1. Search strategy

Systematic literature searches were conducted to identify relevant studies published by 28 August 2025 through PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect, utilizing the pre-defined search strategy detailed in Supplementary Table 1 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha). To ensure comprehensiveness, citation searching of all included studies and relevant reviews were performed to identify additional eligible articles. The screening and identification of relevant studies were performed by two independent researchers (MAW and CDT), with any disagreements addressed by a third investigator (ASM) in a blinded manner. No language limitations were imposed, and any titles or abstracts deemed potentially suitable for inclusion by either investigator were acquired for further evaluation of full-text review.

2.2. Study eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they fulfilled the following prespecified criteria: (1) RCTs or observational studies (including cohort, case—control, cross-sectional, longitudinal, or post hoc analyses of RCTs) conducted in human patients undergoing PCI; (2) studies that directly evaluate clinical and/or angiographic outcomes following SCB-only versus PCB-only PCI. In contrast, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-observational studies, including review articles, letters, comments, case reports, and case series; (2) irretrievable full-text publications; or (3) articles not published in English.

2.3. Data extraction and study outcomes

The following data were extracted from each included study: (1) publication details, including the study setting and location, study identifier number, year of publication, and the last name of the first author; (2) patient, lesion, and procedure characteristics; (3) follow-up duration; and (4) outcomes.

The outcomes of this study were divided into primary and secondary endpoints. The primary endpoint comprised clinical outcomes, including target lesion failure (TLF), which is a composite of cardiac death related to the target vessel, target vessel myocardial infarction (TVMI), and target lesion revascularization (TLR). The secondary endpoint comprised angiographic outcomes, including in-lesion and in-segment minimal lumen diameter (MLD), in-lesion and in-segment late lumen loss (LLL), and diameter stenosis on follow-up angiography.

2.4. Quality assessment and certainty of evidence

The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0) and the risk-of-bias in non-randomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I) instrument were used to evaluate the risk of bias of RCTs and observational studies, respectively. The bias evaluations were conducted by an additional pair of independent authors (AAR and PSH). Any disagreements were addressed independently by a third author (AAA). Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE approach [16]. Two main independent panelists (DF and BBS) assessed the overall outcomes synthesis; any discrepancies in the investigation were resolved through discussion and consensus among all authors.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses were conducted using RStudio version 4.4.1 (PositPBC, Boston, United States) with the ‘meta’ package. A random effect model was implemented in all statistical analyses, considering the variability between trials. The restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator was implemented in the meta-analysis model. Pairwise meta-analysis with mean difference (MD) and risk ratio (RR) effect sizes was conducted for all eligible outcomes. In order to quantify heterogeneity, I2 was employed, with values of <25%, 25–50%, and >50% corresponding to low, moderate, and high levels, respectively. Publication bias was not formally assessed due to the limited number of studies available for the majority of outcomes, which precludes reliable interpretation of funnel plot asymmetry.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted according to lesion type (ISR, de novo lesions, and mixed populations) and study design (RCT and observational studies) to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, in line with methodological guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis [17]. Sensitivity analyses were subsequently performed using a leave-one-out approach to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the effect of the estimates.

Results

3.1. Study selection and risk of bias assessment

Through database searches, a total of 615 records were identified (111 in PubMed, 375 in Scopus, and 129 in ScienceDirect). Following the removal of 127 duplicates, 488 articles were screened. Of these, 473 were excluded after conducting a title and abstract review, resulting in 15 articles that were eligible for full-text evaluation. Two studies were excluded during the full-text review: one due to exclusive reporting of optical coherence tomography-derived results, without relevant clinical or angiographic outcomes, and one because it was available only as a conference abstract. No additional studies were identified through citation searching.

A total of 13 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [18–30] (Fig. 1). A study by Scheller et al. [27] reported on two RCTs that had identical study designs, one of which had been previously published by Ali et al. [25]. We extracted only new RCT results from the report by Scheller et al. to prevent the repetitive pooling of the same data, unless only combined results were available.

The risk of bias assessment using RoB 2.0 for RCTs showed that six out of eight studies had some concern, mainly related to the deviations from the intended intervention. Among the non-RCT studies, three were rated as having some concerns, while two were assessed as having low risk of bias. Specifically, the primary sources of bias were the selection of participants (e.g., registry-based heterogeneity), deviations from intended interventions (e.g., variation in balloon inflation protocols), and incomplete outcome data (e.g., less than complete follow-up), which resulted in downgrading in certain studies. Further details of the risk of bias are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha).

3.2. Baseline study, patient, and procedural characteristics

This updated systematic review and meta-analysis included 5947 patients (4097 treated with SCB and 1850 treated with PCB) [18–30]. These studies comprised cohorts, registries, and RCTs that were conducted in Asia and Europe between 2019 and 2025. Varying lesion types were examined throughout the studies; some studies focused solely on ISR, while others examined de novo lesions or both lesions. The most frequently used devices were MagicTouch™ for SCB and SeQuent® Please Neo for PCB, with sirolimus doses typically ranging from 1.25 to 4.0 μg/mm2 and paclitaxel doses from 2.2 to 3 μg/mm2. The majority of studies reported angiographic follow-up at 6 months and clinical follow-up up to 12 months, with overall follow-up durations ranging from 6 months to 3.8 years (median 45.6 months in one study [30]). Detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. Patient and procedural characteristics of each study are presented in Supplementary Tables 2–3 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha).

3.3. Clinical outcomes

The pooled analysis of clinical outcomes shown in Fig. 2 revealed no significant differences between SCB and PCB regarding TLF (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.44; p = 0.47, I2 5.3%), cardiac death (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.56 to 2.53; p = 0.65, I2 0.0%), TLR (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.37; p = 0.28, I2 0.0%), and TVMI (RR 1.82, 95% CI 0.58 to 5.72; p = 0.30, I2 0.0%). Table 2 provides detailed results of the clinical outcomes meta-analysis.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses stratified by lesion type (ISR, de novo lesions, and mixed populations) and study design (RCT and observational studies) demonstrated consistent results. No significant subgroup effects were observed between TLF (p = 0.23; p = 0.89, respectively), cardiac death (p = 0.59; p = 0.34, respectively), TLR (p = 0.27; p = 0.63, respectively), or TVMI (p = 0.95; p = 0.62, respectively) (Supplementary Figs. 2–9 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). These results were deemed robust based on the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figs. 10–13 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). Collectively, these findings suggest that the efficacy of SCB and PCB is overall comparable, irrespective of the lesion characteristics.

3.4. Angiographic outcomes

A total of eight studies reported follow-up angiographic outcomes, ranging from 6 to 12 months [18,19,23–27,29]. Comparable differences were observed between SCB and PCB at follow-up in-lesion LLL (MD 0.07, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.14; p = 0.08, I2 74.0%), in-segment LLL (MD 0.09, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.18; p = 0.06, I2 42.8%), in-lesion MLD (MD −0.05, 95% CI −0.15 to 0.05; p = 0.30, I2 0%), and diameter stenosis (MD 3.43, 95% CI −0.07 to 6.92, p = 0.05, I2 54.5%). Significant differences were observed in follow-up in-segment MLD (MD −0.10, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.02; p = 0.01, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3). Subgroup analysis revealed higher values of in-segment and in-lesion LLL, along with lower values of in-segment MLD in the sirolimus group for de novo lesion (Supplementary Figs. 14–16 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). However, the overall p-value of the subgroup comparison of both analyses did not reach statistical significance, suggesting no evidence of a true subgroup effect. Details of the subgroup analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 4 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha) and Supplementary Figs. 14–18 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha).

In the subsequent sensitivity analysis, several findings did not remain robust. For in-segment MLD, omission of Ninomiya et al. [19] attenuated the effect to non-significance, confirming this study as the primary confounder (Supplementary Fig. 19 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). For in-segment LLL, Ali et al. [25] emerged as the main confounder, as its omission shifted the effect toward significantly higher in-segment LLL in the SCB group (Supplementary Fig. 20 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). In the diameter stenosis analysis, Liu et al. [18] was the key confounder, with its exclusion resulting in significantly higher diameter stenosis in the SCB group (Supplementary Fig. 21 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). Moreover, Ninomiya et al. [19] also emerged as the major contributor to heterogeneity in in-lesion LLL, in-segment LLL, and diameter stenosis analyses, as its exclusion markedly reduced heterogeneity (Supplementary Figs. 20–22 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). The detailed results of the meta-analysis and sensitivity analysis are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Figs. 19–23 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1508&window=additional_files&context=jsha).

3.5. Certainty of evidence

The detailed GRADE summary of findings are presented in Table 3. For clinical outcomes, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate. These outcomes were downgraded in the imprecision domain due to wide confidence intervals (CI). For angiographic outcomes, the certainty of evidence for in-lesion LLL, in-segment LLL, and diameter stenosis were rated as low, primarily due to notable visual inconsistency and wide CI observed. In-lesion MLD and in-segment MLD were rated as moderate certainty, which was downgraded due to a wide CI.

Overall, although the certainty of evidence did not reach a high level, the estimated absolute effects indicated little to no difference between SCB and PCB, supporting their comparable efficacy. Furthermore, subgroup analyses did not demonstrate any significant effect modification by lesion type, reinforcing that SCB and PCB provide comparable therapeutic benefits in the management of CAD, irrespective of lesion type.

Discussion

Compared to prior meta-analyses [12–14], the present study provides an updated synthesis incorporating recently published studies, resulting in a larger pooled sample size of nearly 6000 patients. Our study expands on the existing evidence by using pre-specified subgroup analyses based on lesion characteristics and study design, as well as comprehensive sensitivity analyses, to better explore heterogeneity and assess the robustness of findings. Furthermore, the application of the GRADE methodology allows for a more transparent assessment of the certainty of evidence. Across the expanded dataset, our pooled results demonstrate no significant differences between SCB and PCB in major clinical outcomes, including TLF, cardiac death, TLR, and TVMI. Likewise, angiographic parameters such as LLL, MLD, and diameter stenosis showed no clinically meaningful differences between the two devices across a broad spectrum of coronary lesion types.

Paclitaxel has historically been the most widely used antiproliferative agent for DCB due to its high lipophilicity, which enables efficient drug transfer and prolonged drug retention at the target site [31,32]. Paclitaxel acts primarily through binding to the β-subunit of tubulin and stabilizing the microtubule structures, thereby inhibiting their disassembly and arresting the cell cycle at the G2/M phase. This mechanism prevents vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation and migration as key drivers of neointimal hyperplasia and restenosis following angioplasty [33,34]. However, transient tissue concentrations exceeding 100 ng/mg following DCB application have been linked in preclinical studies with vascular injury, including intimal hemorrhage, fibrin persistence, and heightened inflammation, thereby raising important safety considerations and restricting the therapeutic margin for clinical use [35–37]. These limitations have prompted growing interest in exploring safer and more effective cytostatic agents for DCB.

Sirolimus and its derivatives inhibit the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a key serine/threonine kinase involved in cell growth, proliferation, and survival [38,39]. This agent has been long established in DES technology and provides a more predictable pharmacological profile via cytostatic inhibition of the mTOR, resulting in VSMC cell-cycle arrest at the G1/S phase while concurrently modulating endothelial proliferation and inflammation [40,41]. This balanced cytostatic effect contributes to more stable vessel healing and a wider therapeutic margin than paclitaxel [9]. The main limitation of sirolimus has been its lower lipophilicity and reversible binding, leading to challenges in tissue penetration and drug retention when delivered by balloon technology [42]. To address this, advanced coating strategies such as phospholipid encapsulation and crystalline carrier platforms using magnesium stearate have been developed, improving vascular wall uptake and sustained local bioavailability [43].

The clinical performance of DCBs is significantly influenced by procedural and lesion-specific factors aside from their pharmacologic properties. In order to ensure homogenous drug transfer, it is necessary to implement optimal lesion preparation, adequate balloon-to-vessel sizing, and sufficient inflation time. The local drug uptake and retention can be substantially influenced by vessel morphology, including small vessel diameter, calcification, and residual stenosis. Additionally, the rate of drug absorption and washout is influenced by the composition of the coating and the technology of the excipient [44]. The variability in these parameters across trials may partially account for the differences in angiographic outcomes, regardless of the drug type used.

Among angiographic outcomes, including inlesion and in-segment LLL, in-lesion MLD, and diameter stenosis, there was no significant difference between SCB and PCB. Although pooled analyses revealed a statistically significant difference in in-segment MLD favoring PCB, the significance was largely driven by a single study [19], as sensitivity analysis confirmed the effect was no longer significant once this study was excluded. Moreover, the heterogeneity observed across angiographic outcomes was largely driven by the TRANSFORM I trial, which exclusively enrolled a de novo smallvessel disease population. In such lesions, even minimal neointimal thickening accounts for a proportionally greater reduction in luminal area, exaggerating angiographic parameters such as LLL and MLD without reflecting true differences in pharmacological efficacy. Additionally, sensitivity analyses identified Ali et al. [25] and Liu et al. [18] as contributors to variability, with opposite directional shifts in in-segment LLL and diameter stenosis. These variations likely stem from differences in lesion-specific factors and disease burden rather than from intrinsic differences between sirolimus and paclitaxel. Furthermore, differences in vessel size between studies may have contributed to residual heterogeneity, which was approximated by balloon diameter. However, since balloon sizing is primarily based on angiographic estimation and operator discretion, it may not accurately reflect true vessel dimensions, especially given the absence of intravascular imaging.

The main strength of this meta-analysis lies in its comprehensive and contemporary evidence synthesis, incorporating the largest cumulative patient population to date. Rigorous subgroup and sensitivity analyses, low heterogeneity for clinical endpoints, along with a GRADE-based certainty assessment, further enhance the reliability of the results. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, variations in lesion type, procedural protocols, and follow-up durations among included studies may introduce clinical heterogeneity, and the ability to further investigate these sources of variability was limited by the absence of meta-regression analyses due to the small number of studies per outcome. Second, angiographic outcomes were reported in a smaller subset of studies, limiting the precision of pooled estimates and precluding long-term evaluation of late lumen dynamics. Third, the majority of included studies reported follow-up data limited to only 6–12 months, with only one study extending beyond one year, thereby restricting the evaluation of long-term outcomes and vessel patency. Fourth, while additional databases such as EMBASE and the Cochrane Library were not investigated, comprehensive citation searching was performed, and no additional eligible studies were discovered. Despite these limitations, the consistency of results across sensitivity analyses supports the overall robustness and clinical relevance of the findings.

This updated meta-analysis reinforces that SCB and PCB provide comparable clinical and angiographic efficacy in coronary revascularization for both de novo and ISR lesions. In practice, the selection between SCB and PCB should therefore be guided by procedural considerations, such as lesion characteristics, patient profile, and operator familiarity. These results also strengthen the position of DCB as a stent-free strategy, particularly in patient subsets where permanent stent implantation is undesirable, such as small-vessel disease, ISR, and high bleeding-risk patients requiring abbreviated dual antiplatelet therapy.

An ongoing study comparing SCB with another DCB in de novo small-vessel lesions (NCT06345209) is expected to provide updated head-to-head data on efficacy and safety. Similarly, the bifurcation lesion trial (NCT06618248) is evaluating SCB versus PCB in complex bifurcation anatomy, addressing an underrepresented lesion subset, and may further refine the clinical positioning of SCB. Furthermore, real-world registries may help define specific clinical subsets that derive unique benefit from one device over the other. Future investigations should also address long-term outcomes beyond one year and evaluate the next generation of sirolimus coating technologies, which may offer an improved vascular healing process. Collectively, the present study will consolidate and extend the evolving evidence base on DCB therapy, providing clinicians and interventional cardiologists with updated guidance for device selection and refining the position of DCBs within future coronary revascularization guidelines.

Conclusion

SCB and PCB demonstrated comparable efficacy in PCI, with no notable differences in clinical or angiographic outcomes. Current evidence supports both SCB and PCB as effective treatment options across a broad spectrum of coronary lesions.
