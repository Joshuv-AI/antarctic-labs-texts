Multigene panel analysis of connective tissue and vascular genes in patients with a bleeding disorder of unknown cause or a cerebrovascular event
Authors: Mona Florquin, Christine Van Laer, Cyrielle Kint, Chris Van Geet, Quentin Van Thillo, Kathleen Freson, Veerle Labarque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC) refers to patients with a significant bleeding phenotype despite normal hemostatic evaluation. Cerebrovascular events (CVEs) are neurologic deficits from intracerebral hemorrhages to hemorrhagic or ischemic strokes. A multigene panel for inherited connective tissue and vascular disorders (BLEED_panel) may improve diagnostic assessment.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic yield and clinical value of the BLEED_panel in patients with BDUC or CVE and to characterize their bleeding manifestations.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center study, all patients tested with the BLEED_panel between January 2019 and August 2025 were included. Demographics, bleeding phenotype, family history, and genetic results were extracted. Variants were classified using American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria. Descriptive statistics assessed the diagnostic yield and genotype-phenotype correlations.ResultsIn the BDUC cohort (n = 69), 61% were children (median age, 13 years) and 61% were female; 75% had a positive International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis Bleeding Assessment Tool score, and 54% had a positive family history. Mucocutaneous bleeding was most common, and 36% had surgery-related bleeding. Diagnostic yield, including variants of uncertain significance, was 15%, with (likely) pathogenic variants in ENG and COL5A1; no significant genotype-phenotype associations were found. In the CVE cohort (n = 38), 89% were children (median age, 0 years) and 47% were female; 74% had a positive International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis Bleeding Assessment Tool score, and 16% had a positive family history. Most presented with central nervous system hemorrhage (79%). Diagnostic yield, including variants of uncertain significance, was 29%, with (likely) pathogenic variants in COL4A1, COL4A2, and ENG.ConclusionThe BLEED_panel adds no diagnostic value in patients with BDUC. It may be useful in selected CVE cases, particularly for COL4A1 and COL4A2.Keywords: bleeding disorder of unknown cause, cerebrovascular disorders, connective tissue diseases, high-throughput sequencing, vascular disorders

Introduction

Bleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC) represents a diagnosis of exclusion, characterized by an objectively confirmed bleeding tendency ranging from spontaneous to trauma-related bleeding, despite normal results on comprehensive hemostatic laboratory testing [1,2]. A diagnosis of BDUC can only be made after systematically excluding all known coagulation and platelet function disorders. Consequently, diagnostic workup should comprise routine coagulation testing in addition to detailed platelet function analyses. By definition, BDUC represents a clinically heterogeneous entity. Its true prevalence remains unknown as only limited number of specialized centers formally register this diagnosis, and inconsistent terminology in the literature complicates comparisons across studies.

Given the clinical heterogeneity and poorly understood etiology of BDUC, genetic studies have primarily focused on known genes associated with coagulation and platelet disorders, both to explore potential underlying causes and to exclude missed diagnoses that may not be detectable with conventional hemostatic laboratory testing. The ThromboGenomics study, the largest diagnostic high-throughput sequencing study to date, investigated gene panel testing in patients with BDUC [3]. However, the diagnostic yield was low (3.2%, including variants of uncertain significance [VUS]) when screening known genes associated with platelet and coagulation disorders, compared with substantially higher yields (up to 60%, including VUS) in patients with defined coagulation or platelet disorders. These findings underscore BDUC as a genetically unresolved patient group. Following participation in the ThromboGenomics study, we incorporated multigene panel testing into routine clinical practice [4]. In addition to the platelet and coagulation disorder subpanels, an additional subpanel, referred to as the “bleeding of unknown etiology panel (BLEED_panel),” was introduced specifically for patients with a BDUC phenotype. This panel includes 11 genes associated with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), various types of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS), and brain small vessel disease (BSVD; see Supplementary Table S1) [[5], [6], [7]]. Since genes associated with BSVD are included in this panel, the BLEED_panel is requested not only for patients with a BDUC phenotype but also for those presenting with a cerebrovascular event (CVE).

HHT, also known as Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome, is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by abnormal vascular morphogenesis, leading to telangiectasias, recurrent spontaneous epistaxis, and arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) in multiple organ systems, including the brain, lungs, and liver [5,8]. Dominant pathogenic variants in ENG, ACVRL1, SMAD4, and GDF2 are the primary genetic causes of HHT, and these genes have been included in the BLEED_panel. EDS represents a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of hereditary connective tissue disorders defined by joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility, and tissue fragility [9,10]. The EDS genes included in the BLEED_panel are COL1A1, COL3A1, COL5A1, COL5A2, and CHST14—genes selected for their association with EDS subtypes that can present with a bleeding tendency in addition to connective tissue manifestations. Variants in COL5A1 or COL5A2 are associated with the classical EDS subtype, which is mainly characterized by marked skin hyperextensibility, joint hypermobility, atrophic scarring, and easy bruising. Mutations in COL3A1 cause vascular EDS subtype, which predominantly presents with arterial rupture at a young age and spontaneous bruising. COL1A1 variants may cause either classical or arthrochalasia-type EDS—the latter typically associated with congenital bilateral hip dislocation. One study has also suggested a link between pediatric pulmonary hemorrhage and variants in COL3A1 and COL1A1, although this finding is based on a small patient population [11]. CHST14 mutations are associated with musculocontractural EDS in which congenital contractures are prominent and large subcutaneous hematomas may occur [10]. Pathogenic variants in COL4A1 and COL4A2, which encode components of the cerebral vascular basement membrane, lead to BSVD [7,12]. This condition is associated with cerebral vascular fragility and susceptibility to intracerebral hemorrhage, which may present in utero, during birth, or later in life. Retinal or renal vascular anomalies may also be present. In addition, cerebral degeneration may result in porencephaly or schizencephaly due to cavitary lesions. These genes have also been included in the BLEED_panel to identify patients with a BDUC phenotype associated with cerebral vascular fragility, as well as individuals presenting primarily with CVE.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed 107 patients who underwent genetic testing using the BLEED_panel between January 2019 and August 2025. Our aim was to assess the diagnostic yield and clinical utility of the panel in patients presenting with either a BDUC phenotype or CVE and to explore genotype-phenotype correlations related to bleeding manifestations.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a single-center retrospective study at UZ Leuven, which was approved by the Ethics Committee of UZ Leuven (S70654). A total of 167 patients were screened using the BLEED_panel between January 2019 and August 2025 (Figure 1). Of these, 52 were external requests and were excluded from the analysis. An additional 8 patients were excluded due to insufficient clinical data or an alternative diagnosis (von Willebrand disease), established via another subpanel. Ultimately, 107 patients were included in this retrospective analysis.

Clinical data collection and bleeding phenotype assessment

Clinical data were extracted from the patients’ electronic medical records and entered in a pseudonymized database. Collected clinical variables included demographic characteristics, age at the time of testing, relevant family history, specific bleeding characteristics, hemostatic laboratory assays, the presence of hyperlaxity, and AVM screening by imaging (including AVM status). The bleeding phenotype was assessed using the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis Bleeding Assessment Tool (ISTH-BAT), a standardized questionnaire that quantifies bleeding symptoms by scoring their severity to aid in the diagnosis of inherited bleeding disorders [13]. The cutoff values used for an abnormal ISTH-BAT score were ≥3 for children, ≥4 for adult men, and ≥6 for adult women, as recommended by the guidelines. When available, the ISTH-BAT score at the first clinical visit was extracted from the electronic medical record; in cases where it was not documented, the score was determined retrospectively based on the information recorded in the electronic medical record from that visit. In addition to the overall ISTH-BAT score, individual symptom scores contributing to the ISTH-BAT score were recorded. Major bleeding was defined according to the ISTH as clinically overt bleeding associated with at least 1 of the following: a fatal outcome, a fall in hemoglobin level of ≥2 g/dL, transfusion of ≥2 units of red blood cells, or bleeding in a critical site (intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, pericardial, intra-articular, intramuscular with compartment syndrome, or retroperitoneal) [14]. Minor bleeding was defined as any overt bleeding event, not fulfilling the criteria for major bleeding.

Multigene panel testing

The “Leuven Thrombosis and Haemostasis multigene panel” uses whole exome sequencing to generate exomic data, from which a predefined virtual panel of 108 TIER1 genes is analyzed for variants associated with inherited thrombotic and bleeding disorders. The 108-gene panel is organized into 3 subpanels: a coagulation/anticoagulation panel for bleeding and thrombosis, a platelet disorder panel, and a bleeding of unknown etiology or BLEED_panel (Supplementary Table S1) [4,15]. The BLEED_panel specifically comprises 11 genes associated with hereditary connective tissue disorders and vascular diseases. Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria as (likely) pathogenic variant ((L)PV), VUS, or (likely) benign [15]. (L)PV and VUS were included in the genetic report, allowing for potential reclassification of VUS.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort. Continuous variables are presented as median with the first and third quartiles, and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The ISTH-BAT score was analyzed both as a continuous variable (compared between genetically positive and negative groups using the Mann-Whitney U test, 2-tailed; exact P values) and as a dichotomous variable (positive/negative according to established age- and sex-specific thresholds) using 2×2 contingency tables with Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. In addition, frequencies of individual ISTH-BAT bleeding symptoms were analyzed descriptively. For sex-specific bleeding symptoms, such as menorrhagia and postpartum bleeding, percentages were calculated using an estimated relevant subgroup denominator (girls and women aged 11 years or older for menorrhagia and women aged ≥18 years for postpartum bleeding). To systematically evaluate the relationship between the genetic panel outcome and a dichotomous variable, 2×2 contingency tables were constructed. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad (GraphPad Software). All tests were 2-tailed, and P values of <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical presentation and demographic features of the BDUC and CVE cohorts

The BLEED_panel was performed for a total of 167 patients, of whom 107 were ultimately included in this retrospective analysis (Figure 1). These patients were stratified into 2 distinct cohorts. The first, referred to as the BDUC cohort (n = 69), comprised patients meeting BDUC criteria, all of whom had normal platelet count and function, von Willebrand factor, and coagulation screening results (at minimum normal prothrombin time/international normalized ratio [PT/INR], activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], and fibrinogen and factor VIII [FVIII] levels), with the exception of a single patient with hemophilia A who, despite prophylactic treatment with emicizumab, experienced spontaneous joint bleeds and was therefore included. The second, designated the CVE cohort (n = 38), included patients presenting with a CVE phenotype (predominantly intracranial hemorrhage), without necessarily exhibiting additional bleeding manifestations. This stratification was essential to allow separate analyses, as the 2 cohorts represent clinically distinct phenotypes. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the included patients.

Most patients in both cohorts were children (ie, 61% in the BDUC cohort and 89% in the CVE cohort; Table 1). The median age was 13 years (range, 5-31 years) in the BDUC cohort and 0 years (range, 0-3.8 years) in the CVE cohort due to the predominance of perinatal CVEs in the CVE cohort, resulting in further testing being performed at a very young age. Median age at bleeding onset could not be determined due to missing documentation. Sex distribution was relatively balanced with 61% being female in the BDUC cohort and 47% being female in the CVE cohort. A positive family history was reported in 54% of patients in the BDUC cohort, whereas this was less common in the CVE cohort (16%). The ISTH-BAT score was positive in 75% of the BDUC cohort and in 74% of the CVE cohort (Table 2), indicating that the BLEED_panel is predominantly used for patients with clinically significant bleeding. In the BDUC cohort, the median ISTH-BAT score was 5 among patients with a negative genetic result (n = 59) and 3.5 among those with a positive result (n = 10); however, this difference was not statistically significant (Mann-Whitney’s U test, 2-sided; P = .1059; Table 2). In the CVE cohort, the median ISTH-BAT score was 4 in both groups when stratified by genetic test results (Mann-Whitney’s U test, 2-sided; P = .3560; Table 2).

Regarding clinical presentation, Figure 2A provides an overview of the bleeding symptoms in the BDUC cohort, according to the 14 subcategories used in the ISTH-BAT score. Most of the patients exhibited mucocutaneous bleeding, particularly cutaneous bleeding (68%), epistaxis (36%), and menorrhagia (66% of females aged ≥11 years). A substantial proportion of tested patients (36%) had a history of surgery-related bleeding. Other bleeding symptoms such as gastrointestinal, urological, or central nervous system (CNS) bleeding were much less frequently reported. Of note, musculoskeletal bleeds were only reported in 1 patient, which involved the known hemophilia A patient. In the CVE cohort, most patients (79%) experienced a CNS hemorrhage, predominantly in the perinatal period (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table S2). This likely accounts for the relatively elevated median ISTH-BAT score observed in this cohort despite the young median age. This included 5 children who developed unexpectedly extensive intracranial hemorrhages after uncomplicated term pregnancies, 4 late preterm infants (34-36 weeks’ gestation), and 4 individuals outside the perinatal period (ages of 10 days to 40 years) with a spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage. One patient is not included in Supplementary Table S2, as she was tested at the age of 38 years for a perinatal CNS hemorrhage, with the gestational age and exact timing not specified in her medical records. Of note, in 5 patients, brain imaging or autopsy revealed porencephaly and/or schizencephaly. The remaining patients included 4 individuals (11%) who experienced a CNS stroke and another 4 (11%) who were classified under the “other” category, encompassing alternative cerebrovascular phenotypes such as periventricular leukomalacia, hydrocephalus, and internal carotid artery thrombosis.

Analysis of the BLEED_panel for 69 patients in the BDUC cohort

The overall diagnostic rate in the BDUC cohort was 15%, including VUS findings (Figure 3A). A total of 11 variants across 6 genes could be identified in 10 patients with 1 individual carrying 2 VUSs in 2 different genes (COL3A1 and COL5A1; Figure 3B). Details on individual genotypes, bleeding manifestations, ISTH-BAT scores, family history, and subsequent investigations are summarized in Table 3. Only 2 variants (3%) were classified as (L)PV, and in both patients, the identified variant fully explained the clinical phenotype. The first patient (number 1 in Table 3) exhibited a clear HHT phenotype, characterized by recurrent epistaxis and multiple AVMs in the brain and lungs. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic variant in ENG, c.-127C>T, a substitution in the 5′ untranslated region. This variant has been previously described and it disrupts translation initiation of ENG, leading to degradation of the mutant transcripts and reduced endoglin expression [[16], [17], [18]]. Variant cosegregation studies were performed for the affected father and 2 affected sisters of this index patient. The second patient (number 2 in Table 3) concerned a 2-year-old boy who was referred to pediatric hematology for extensive bruising, with a history of germinal matrix hemorrhage at birth. Genetic testing identified a likely pathogenic frameshift variant in COL5A1, p.(Pro1304Thrfs∗29), resulting in a truncated or absent protein. Loss-of-function variants in COL5A1 are established as pathogenic in classical EDS. There was a clear genotype-phenotype correlation reflected by extensive bruising, joint hyperlaxity with mild motor difficulties, and lax skin and excessive scarring. Segregation analysis confirmed paternal inheritance, with the father showing easy bruising and joint hypermobility on targeted examination. VUS findings were identified in 8 patients, most frequently in COL3A1, COL4A2, and COL5A1 (Table 3). The corresponding phenotypes ranged from isolated cutaneous bleeding to combined mucocutaneous manifestations. Additional studies, including family cosegregation analyses and further clinical investigations, were undertaken to enable potential reclassification of the VUS findings; however, this was only possible for 1 variant, which was ultimately reclassified as likely benign. (last column in Table 3). A potential splice variant (c.3258+19C>T) in COL5A1 was detected in patient 5, a 15-year-old female, who presented with a history of easy bruising and menorrhagia; on re-examination, joint hypermobility was scored (Beighton score, 6/9). Segregation analysis was unfortunately not possible, as the biological parents’ identities and therefore their genetic statuses are unknown. While her phenotype could align with classical EDS—particularly given the characteristic mucocutaneous and connective tissue features—the absence of further diagnostic evaluation necessitates keeping the variant classified as a VUS. This variant may nevertheless explain her bleeding diathesis, consistent with the well-documented predominance of menorrhagia and easy bruising as hemorrhagic manifestations among patients with EDS [19]. Patient number 9, a 4-year-old boy, was referred after severe postoperative bleeding following circumcision and adenotonsillectomy, both requiring surgical reintervention, alongside a history of easy bruising. Routine functional coagulation studies yielded normal results, prompting further evaluation. Genetic testing revealed 2 missense variants: COL3A1 p.(Gly1198Asp) and COL5A1 p.(Thr348Met). Segregation analysis showed paternal inheritance of the COL3A1 variant (father with childhood epistaxis but no current phenotype) and maternal inheritance of the COL5A1 variant (mother with easy bruising and minor wound bleeding, yet uneventful wisdom tooth extraction). Neither the proband nor the mother showed marked hyperlaxity (both with a Beighton score of 2/9) and the father scored 0/9. Although each variant alone may not suffice to explain a bleeding phenotype, a combined or additive effect on vascular fragility remains possible. However, the variants remain VUS.

We next examined whether bleeding severity or a positive family history in the BDUC cohort correlated with genetic findings. A positive overall ISTH-BAT score was not predictive of finding a genetic variant (P = .7). Moreover, no association was identified between the severity of bleeding symptoms (major vs minor phenotype) and genetic findings (P = 0.13), nor between the involvement of multiple and single subcategory scoring in the bleeding assessment tool (P = .73). Finally, no significant association was observed between the genetic findings and the presence or absence of family history (P > .99; Table 4).

Analysis of the BLEED_panel for 38 patients in the CVE cohort

The diagnostic yield in the CVE cohort was 29%, with most findings classified as VUS (18%; Figure 3C). Among patients with a positive genetic result, 9 carried variants in genes known to be associated with brain hemorrhage, including those linked to HHT and BSVD. In 3 patients (8%), an LPV was identified in COL4A1 and COL4A2, and in 1 patient, a PV in the ENG gene was found, while VUS were found in ACVRL1, COL1A1, COL4A1, COL5A1, and ENG (Figure 3D, Table 5). Patient 2 was a term-born girl after an uncomplicated pregnancy presenting with delayed motor development and left-sided hemiparesis. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain at the age of 8 months showed sequelae of a hemorrhagic infarction with marked ventricular dilation. Genetic testing identified the COL4A1 frameshift variant p.(Gly423Trpfs∗19), leading to a premature stop codon. This specific variant is absent in mutation and population databases but aligns with known loss-of-function disease mechanisms for COL4A1. Segregation analysis showed paternal inheritance without the BSVD phenotype. Two patients (siblings represented as patients 3 and 4 in Table 5) suffered from a severe perinatal intracranial hemorrhage with fatal outcome. The first developed a CNS hemorrhage at 36 weeks and deceased 3 days postpartum, whereas the second experienced CNS hemorrhage at 21 weeks’ postmenstrual age and deceased at 25 weeks, 1 day after birth. Autopsy revealed porencephaly and schizencephaly, respectively, in these patients, and both carried the COL4A2 frameshift variant, p.(Gly1426Argfs∗30). This variant is not described in the literature or mutation databases, but several other frameshift variants in COL4A2 have been reported in association with similar patient phenotypes [20,21]. Segregation analysis revealed that their mother, currently 49 years old, carries the same variant, although no associated phenotype has been identified to date. As for the BDUC cohort, additional studies, including family cosegregation analyses and further clinical investigations, were performed to potentially reclassify the VUS findings (Table 5, last column). Reclassification of VUS is very challenging, as variants in COL4A1 and COL4A2 exhibit incomplete penetrance and marked variability in expressivity [22]. Interestingly, 1 VUS could be reclassified as an LPV because it was found to be de novo (patient number 11 in Table 5). This patient presented prenatally with severe hydrocephalus, suspected to be of vascular origin, and subsequently deceased at the age of 10 months due to the severity of the underlying condition.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective study, we evaluated the diagnostic yield and clinical utility of the BLEED_panel, a targeted multigene subpanel for inherited connective tissue and vascular disorders, in patients presenting with a BDUC or CVE.

According to bleeding severity, BDUC is classified as a mild-to-moderate bleeding disorder. Notably, 1 study reported that patients with BDUC account for around 60% of all moderate bleeding disorders [1]. In our BDUC cohort, the majority of patients also exhibited mild to moderate bleeding, most commonly manifesting as mucocutaneous features including cutaneous bruising, epistaxis, and menorrhagia. One limitation of our study is the estimation of the occurrence rates of menorrhagia and postpartum hemorrhage. For these calculations, the denominator included all female patients aged ≥11 years for menorrhagia and >18 years for postpartum hemorrhage. However, due to the retrospective nature of the data, information on which women had experienced childbirth was unavailable, making this approach somewhat arbitrary. This limitation is less pronounced for menorrhagia, as the majority of women in this age group are expected to have experienced menstruation. Additionally, a subset of patients (36%) presented with a more severe phenotype, namely, surgery-related bleeding. Considering that this panel is designed for patients with an unexplained bleeding disorder, most of our patients had a positive ISTH-BAT score. However, 25% had a negative ISTH-BAT score, suggesting that their reported bleeding symptoms fall within the expected range for the general population. Nonetheless, certain well-known limitations are associated with a BAT: in children and men, underestimation may occur when only a limited number of hemostatic challenges have taken place. Next, it remains important to keep in mind that the severity of bleeding may have been underreported in certain patients due to the retrospective nature of the study (symptoms not documented in the medical records were scored as zero, and when an uncertainty within a subcategory occurred, the lowest score was assigned). Moreover, the ISTH-BAT score does not capture several key features of vascular or connective tissue disorders—such as joint hyperlaxity, impaired wound healing, or AVMs—that may still warrant consideration of this panel, even in patients with a negative ISTH-BAT score. For example, patient number 1 in Table 3 has a “negative” BAT score of 2 (based on epistaxis); however, given the very clear HHT phenotype alongside (AVMs), requesting the panel was clearly justified despite the negative BAT score. We could not identify any factors predictive of detecting a genetic variant in BDUC patients: no associations were observed between family history, the overall ISTH-BAT score, or the presence of specific phenotypes. It is likely that the number of positive genetic results was too small to detect statistically meaningful associations. Alternatively, this may also reflect the inherent clinical overlap between patients with and without an identifiable genetic defect, as well as the contribution of yet-unidentified genes or non-Mendelian mechanisms in BDUC. In our BDUC cohort, the overall yield of the BLEED_panel was 15% (including VUS). However, the panel yielded a molecular diagnosis ((L)PV) concordant with the clinical phenotype in only 2 patients, indicating limited clinical utility. This reflects again the substantial heterogeneity of BDUC and the fact that its underlying genetic contributors remain unknown. Consequently, the cost-effectiveness of routine use of the BLEED_panel in patients with BDUC is highly questionable. Nonetheless, a thorough clinical assessment remains essential for patients with increased bleeding tendency. This should include systematic evaluation for joint hyperlaxity and (familial) AVMs, alongside a detailed bleeding history and comprehensive physical examination, to avoid overlooking potential underlying vascular or connective tissue disorders. Genetic testing using targeted gene panels for HHT or EDS can then be considered based on these clinical findings.

The BLEED_panel for patients of the CVE cohort shows an overall diagnostic rate of 29% (including VUS). In 5 patients, the genetic panel could identify (L)PVs in genes that aligned with the clinical phenotype, facilitating targeted clinical management. Of note, segregation analysis of variants in COL4A1 and COL4A2 (Table 3) illustrates the incomplete penetrance in these genes and highlights the need for nuanced counseling when such variants are detected [23,24]. In this cohort, it remains uncertain whether these patients will exhibit an increased bleeding tendency later in life. Moreover, intraventricular hemorrhage occurs in 15% to 20% of premature infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. Its etiology is multifactorial, involving immaturity of the germinal matrix vasculature, hemodynamic instability, perinatal asphyxia, inflammation, and sepsis. A hereditary cause may not play a role in these bleeding episodes, and genetic testing should not be part of routine workup in these patients [25]. On the other hand, in case of atypical severity or location of the bleed, distinctive phenotypes, or a positive family history, genetic evaluation using the BLEED_panel may be relevant. Systematic testing for COL4A1 and COL4A2 variants is recommended in the literature for neonates with porencephalic cysts, hemiparesis at birth, or unexplained intracranial hemorrhage, especially when cerebral lesions are severe, multifocal, or of combined ischemic-hemorrhagic nature [22]. In our cohort, 17 out of 25 patients experienced CNS bleeding in the perinatal period (with 1 patient not mentioned here because the timing of the CNS hemorrhage was unclear), occurring after 32 weeks of gestation. In the remaining 7 patients with CNS bleeding in the perinatal period, the reason for requesting genetic testing was more difficult to ascertain due to the retrospective study design; however, it may have been prompted by an apparent discrepancy between gestational age and the extent of the hemorrhagic lesion. Of note, 2 of these 7 patients had porencephaly or schizencephaly, which likely represented the specific reason for requesting the panel. This observation supports the notion that genetic evaluation is only justified in selected relevant cases with atypical severity or location of bleeding, disproportionate findings relative to the degree of prematurity, or a positive family history, and should not be performed systematically.

In our cohort, the BLEED_panel detected genetic variants in only a minority of patients with BDUC, indicating that its utility for clinical testing, even in selected cases, is limited and not advised. Additional studies investigating the genetic underpinnings of BDUC are needed to uncover novel genes and improve the clinical diagnostic impact in this patient population. However, when the phenotype is suggestive of HHT or EDS, targeted genetic testing is of course indicated. Within the CVE cohort, genetic testing for COL4A1 and COL4A2 may be indicated in patients where the phenotype appears disproportionately extensive for the gestational age, or when specific findings are present (eg, porencephaly).
