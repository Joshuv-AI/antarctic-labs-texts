Occupational status and outcomes in acute myocardial infarction: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yalin Zhu, Jianyi Wang, Qiujing Cai, Yaxue Qiao, Jingang Yang, Haiyan Xu, Shuhong Su, Xiaojin Gao, Wei Li, Yuejin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile Western studies often show an inverse association between occupational status and cardiovascular mortality, its impact on acute myocardial infarction (AMI) characteristics and prognosis in Eastern middle-income countries remains unclear.MethodsUsing data from the multi-center prospective China Acute Myocardial Infarction (CAMI) registry from January 2013 to January 2016, 11,318 admission AMI patients aged 18–60 years were stratified by occupation: white-collar (22.1%), blue-collar (23.4%), farmers (44.3%), and unemployed (10.1%). White- and blue-collar workers comprised the “stable employment” group; farmers and unemployed comprised “unstable employment.” We compared risk factors, treatments, and clinical outcomes. Multivariable Cox regression assessed occupational impacts on in-hospital and 2-year post-discharge all-cause mortality, progressively adjusting for demographics, self-reported risk histories and clinically measured parameters, treatments and hospital level.ResultsStable employment groups (particularly white-collar patients) had higher baseline cardiovascular risks but less severe clinical presentation and higher reperfusion rates than unstable groups. No significant in-hospital mortality differences were observed after full adjustment. At 2-year follow-up, white-collar patients initially demonstrated 50% lower post-discharge mortality versus unemployed after demographic adjustment (HR 0.487; 95% CI 0.257–0.924). This association weakened with adjustment for risk histories/clinical characteristics (HR 0.506; 95% CI 0.266–0.964) and became non-significant after including treatment/hospital-level factors (HR 0.519; 95% CI 0.271–1.021). Neither blue-collar workers (fully adjusted HR 0.848; 95% CI 0.474–1.516) nor farmers (fully adjusted HR 1.164; 95% CI 0.702–1.931) showed significant mortality differences versus unemployed. Critically, unstable employment independently predicted higher 2-year post-discharge mortality after full adjustment (HR 1.639; 95% CI 1.169–2.298).ConclusionStable employment (White- and Blue-collar) was associated with significantly higher cardiovascular risk factors but better long-term outcomes compared to farmers and the unemployed. This trend was particularly pronounced among White-collar workers.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24564-5.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of global mortality despite significant therapeutic advances and declining death rates over the past century [1]. Socioeconomic status (SES)—encompassing income, education, and occupation—is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease incidence and outcomes [2–5]. However, comprehensive SES metrics have fundamental limitations in AMI populations. Educational attainment largely reflects early-life experiences that shape health literacy, yet its temporal disconnect from AMI onset—typically occurring decades later—limits relevance to acute clinical presentation. Similarly, self-reported income data are subject to recall bias and temporal fluctuations, often failing to capture household-level socioeconomic dynamics. Critically, occupational status not only correlates with education and income but also reflects ongoing socioeconomic exposures through job-related stressors, healthcare access patterns, and current lifestyle behaviors [6–8].

While prior studies predominantly examine composite SES metrics [9–14], occupation-specific analyses in AMI populations remain limited. Existing research often focuses on isolated aspects (e.g., cardiovascular risk factors, reperfusion strategy, medication adherence) [7, 12, 15, 16] lacking comprehensive assessments of how occupational groups differ in clinical profiles and outcomes. Furthermore, most evidence derives from populations in western high-income countries [2, 12–14, 17, 18], where occupational class effects may differ fundamentally from those in Eastern nations due to divergent healthcare systems, risk factor distributions, and work cultures. For instance, Japanese studies notably identify higher smoking and alcohol use in higher occupational groups—a pattern contrasting with Western findings [19, 20]. Thus, investigation of the occupational impact on AMI patients in Eastern middle-income countries, such as China, is needed.

Therefore, this study examines whether there are significant differences across occupational groups among Chinese AMI patients regarding risk factor profiles, quality of care, and in-hospital and long-term all-cause mortality.

Methods

Overview of the CAMI registry and data collection

This study is part of the CAMI registry study (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT number: NCT01874691, Registered 11 June 2013), a nationwide prospective program aimed at gathering real-world data on AMI patients in China [21], approved by the Fuwai Hospital institutional review board and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study involved the enrollment of AMI patients from 108 hospitals across 31 provinces and municipalities throughout mainland China, with all participants providing informed consent.

Comprehensive clinical data were collected through a secure web-based system, with standardized variable definitions and strict data entry protocols. Trained physicians conducted real-time enrollment and follow-up at each site, while senior cardiologists oversaw quality control to ensure accuracy. The database was periodically checked, and random audits of hospital sites were performed by cross-referencing medical records to verify data accuracy. Clinical follow-up assessments were conducted at 1, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. Trained staff performed follow-up via phone interviews and electronic medical record reviews to identify adverse outcomes. All self-reported events were validated by accessing or requesting medical records and adjudicated according to standard criteria. The 2-year mortality follow-up rate exceeded 95%.

Study population and classification

Patients consecutively admitted to participating hospitals between January 2013 and January 2016 with a primary diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), including ST-segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non–ST-segment–elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), within 7 days of ischemic symptom onset were enrolled. The AMI diagnosis adhered to the third universal definition of myocardial infarction. To focus on the actively employed population (statutory working age: 18 years; standard retirement age: 60 years for most occupations in China), enrollment was restricted to patients aged 18–60 years. After excluding patients with undefined AMI type or indeterminate occupational status, 11,318 patients were included.

Occupational status was documented at hospital admission through physician-administered interviews using standardized case report forms, capturing employment circumstances at AMI onset. Occupations were classified using a Chinese-context adaptation of the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-88), yielding four categories: (1) White-collar (ISCO-88 major groups 1–4: e.g., scientists, teachers, professionals, technicians, government officials, civil servants, healthcare workers, engineers, senior managers, entrepreneurs); (2) Blue-collar (ISCO-88 major groups 5, 7–9: e.g., laborers, machine operators, craft workers, transportation/moving occupations, service employees, drivers); (3) Farmers (agricultural and fishery workers, categorized separately due to their distinct semi-manual, low-mechanization farming practices during the study period, differing from ISCO-88 Group 6 ‘Skilled Agricultural Workers’); and (4) Unemployed (individuals without employment). White- and blue-collar workers were categorized as “stable employment” based on standardized labor protections and near-universal enrollment in Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), ensuring systemic access to tertiary care. Farmers and the unemployed comprised “unstable employment”, reflecting absence of formal labor contracts, income irregularity, and fragmented coverage under lower-reimbursement schemes (predominantly New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme [NRCMS]) or self-pay.

Variable definitions and outcomes

Key variables were defined as follows: Overweight (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 24 kg/m² per China standard); low educational attainment (illiteracy or primary school completion only); and high-risk GRACE score (> 155 for STEMI or > 140 for NSTEMI patients). The GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) score - an internationally recommended risk stratification tool for acute coronary syndrome - incorporates age, Killip class, systolic blood pressure, ST-segment deviation, cardiac arrest at presentation, serum creatinine, positive initial cardiac biomarkers, and heart rate.

The primary outcomes were covariate-adjusted all-cause mortality during hospitalization and within 2 years post-discharge. Secondary outcomes included: (1) in-hospital major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs), defined as composite of all-cause death, reinfarction, and stroke; and (2) post-discharge all-cause mortality at 30 days and 1 year.

Statistical analysis

Baseline conditions, clinical characteristics, treatments, and outcomes were compared across occupational groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) and analyzed using ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables are reported as counts with percentages and compared by χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for descriptive data, with SMD >0.1 indicating imbalanced groups.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models evaluated adjusted associations between occupational classes and all-cause mortality (in-hospital and 2-year post-discharge). Three models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for demographics (age, sex); Model 2 additionally adjusted for self-reported risk histories (smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, family history of premature coronary artery diseases [CAD], prior chronic heart failure, prior stroke) and clinical parameters (BMI, onset-to-arrival time, anterior myocardial infarction, GRACE score); Model 3 further adjusted for primary PCI (PPCI) therapy and hospital level. The unemployed group served as the reference in four-group comparisons (white-collar, blue-collar, farmers, unemployed), while unstable employment was the reference in two-group comparisons (stable vs. unstable employment).

Stratified subgroup analyses assessed effect modification by sex, AMI type, education, health insurance, and PPCI status through interaction terms in Cox models. Missing data were imputed based on missingness proportion: for variables with < 10% missingness, qualitative variables were imputed to the highest-frequency category and quantitative variables to the mean; for variables with ≥ 10% missingness, multiple imputation was applied in Cox models. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05.All analyses used SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Figure 1 shows the selection process. After excluding patients with uncertain AMI type, missing occupation data, or age < 18 or > 60 years, 11,318 AMI patients with complete occupation data were included. Patients were stratified into occupational groups: white-collar (N = 2,506), blue-collar (N = 2,652), farmers (N = 5,013), and unemployed (N = 1,147). For employment stability analysis, white-collar and blue-collar workers were classified as the stable employment group, while farmers and unemployed were classified as the unstable employment group. The comparison of baseline conditions across occupations are presented in Table 1. White-collar and Blue-collar patients had markedly lower rates of low educational attainment (4.2% and 9.8%, respectively) compared to Farmers (46.3%) and the Unemployed (40.7%) (SMDs: 0.979 and 0.760 vs. Unemployed). Similarly, Urban basic medical insurance coverage was substantially higher in White-collar (78.9%) and Blue-collar (80.2%) groups versus Farmers (11.5%) and the Unemployed (44.6%) (SMDs: 0.787 and 0.829 vs. Unemployed). White-collar patients exhibited the highest prevalence of key cardiovascular risk factors: smoking (75.5% vs. 66.2% Unemployed; SMD > 0.2), overweight (68.9% vs. 59.3%; SMD > 0.2), and dyslipidemia (15.5% vs. 6.7%; SMD > 0.2).

Clinical characteristics and treatments

As shown in eTable 1, White-collar patients reported the highest rate of recently unhealthy behaviors as a trigger (37.5%) among the minority with identifiable AMI triggers. Moreover, notable disparities were observed across occupational groups regarding healthcare access, clinical severity and treatments (Table 2). White-collar patients had significantly greater access to province-level hospitals (47.2%) than Blue-collar (34.1%), Farmers (22.4%), or the Unemployed (37.1%) (P < 0.0001). Conversely, Unemployed patients presented with the most severe clinical status: highest rates of Killip class ≥ II (19.7% vs. 14.6% White-collar; SMD = 0.136) and high-risk GRACE scores (15.2% vs. 11.2%; SMD = 0.117). Farmers received PPCI for STEMI at markedly lower rates (42.0%) compared to White-collar (60.4%) group.

Discussion

The main findings of our study are as follows: (1) White- and blue-collar workers exhibited higher baseline cardiovascular risk profiles yet less severe clinical presentations upon admission and received reperfusion therapy at higher rates compared to farmers and unemployed patients. (2) While crude mortality rates aligned with Western patterns—showing lower in-hospital and long-term mortality in higher occupational classes [2, 12, 14, 17, 18] —these differences became non-significant after comprehensive multivariable adjustment for demographic, clinical, and treatment/hospital-level factors. (3) The long-term survival advantage observed in patients with stable employment (white- and blue-collar) may be largely due to their greater access to medical resources.

Contrary to patterns in Western nations where socioeconomic advantage associates with lower cardiovascular risk [2, 12, 13, 15], our study found higher occupational status groups (white- and blue-collar workers) exhibited increased prevalence of CVD risk factors (e.g., overweight, smoking, dyslipidemia). This pattern aligns with previous studies from other Asian settings. For example, a study in Japan reported elevated smoking and heavy drink in higher occupational status [20]. Studies from Korea observed greater metabolic syndrome incidence [22], and India reported higher dyslipidemia rates [23] among patients in higher occupational classes. A large-scale epidemiologic study demonstrated the inconsistent trends of CVD risks across different socioeconomic classes in countries with different income levels [24]. These findings highlight the necessity the critical influence of regional context on CVD risk stratification.

Conversely, farmers and unemployed patients presented with more severe clinical conditions, prolonged prehospital delays, and significantly reduced PPCI rates—consistent with prior studies [2, 12, 17, 25]. These disparities likely reflect China’s occupational geography. Urban-based white- and blue-collar workers typically benefit from proximity to advanced medical resources. In contrast, farmers and unemployed individuals, predominantly residing in rural areas, face systemic barriers—including limited access to preventative care and lower health literacy [26] —which may contribute to delayed hospital presentation (> 12 h post-symptom onset) and restricted access to emergency medical services.

Crucially, the markedly lower PPCI utilization among farmers and unemployed patients may stem from two intersecting systemic barriers. First, geographic constraints limit access: county-level hospitals (their primary care source) have substantially lower PPCI capability (44% vs. 93–100% at prefecture/province-level facilities) [21]. Second, financial burdens from insurance inequity exacerbate these disparities: predominantly covered by the lower-reimbursement NRCMS (40–50% inpatient coverage), farmers and unemployed patients face significantly reduced benefits compared to UEBMI-enrolled urban residents (50–70%) [27, 28]. Third, prolonged prehospital delays—partly attributable to these barriers—frequently cause missed therapeutic windows for emergency reperfusion, further reducing PPCI eligibility.

Contrary to most prior studies [2, 12, 13, 17], our fully adjusted analysis revealed no significant differences in in-hospital and 2-year post-discharge mortality across occupational groups. This finding likely stems from counteracting effects of divergent risk profiles and healthcare access disparities. While existing studies mainly associated lower SES with higher CVD mortality—align with fewer risk factors in high-SES groups—our cohort demonstrated a higher prevalence of CVD risk factors (e.g., smoking, overweight, dyslipidemia) among white- and blue-collar workers. This elevated risk burden appears to counterbalance their socioeconomic advantages in healthcare access (e.g., proximity to PCI-capable centers, comprehensive insurance). Supporting this hypothesis, a mediation analysis confirmed that occupational disparities in CVD incidence are largely attributable to risk factors like smoking and BMI [29]. Jakobsen et al. [12]and Emberson et al. [30] further substantiate that mitigating risk factor inequalities reduces mortality gaps across occupational classes. Additionally, a multinational study also demonstrated that variations in CVD mortality align with occupational inequalities in risk factor distributions [31].

Notably, unadjusted mortality remained higher among farmers and unemployed patients, reflecting their more severe clinical presentations (higher Killip grades and GRACE scores), prolonged prehospital delays, and reduced PPCI utilization. These disparities could stem from aforementioned limited access to PCI-equipped hospitals and financial constraints under lower-reimbursement insurance schemes. These factors collectively constrain timely reperfusion—a proven mortality determinant [32–34]. Our analysis supports this: occupational mortality differences attenuated after adjusting for baseline risk and disappeared when further accounting for hospital level and PPCI receipt.

To further substantiate our findings, white- and blue-collar workers were classified as “stable employment” reflecting their shared institutional protections within China’s urban formal sector—including standardized labor contracts guaranteeing regular wages and predominant enrollment in high-coverage UEBMI. This facilitates consistent access to tertiary hospitals centralizing > 90% of AMI specialty care [35]. Conversely, farmers and the unemployed (“unstable employment”) exhibit structural vulnerabilities: absence of formal labor protections, irregular income, and fragmented health coverage (predominantly lower-reimbursement NRCMS or self-pay).

Patients with unstable employment demonstrated a 30% higher adjusted risk of in-hospital mortality after demographic and clinical adjustment—a disparity that attenuated following adjustment for primary PCI and hospital-level factors, indicating therapeutic access primarily mediates short-term outcomes. This aligns with established socioeconomic gradients in reperfusion therapy and subsequent mortality rates [5, 36, 37]. Crucially, unstable employment independently predicted > 60% higher 2-year mortality despite full adjustment. This enduring risk likely stems from compounding barriers: lower PPCI rates increasing post-infarction complications, compounded by reduced medication adherence [16], limited access to guideline-directed therapies and follow-up care [38–40], and disadvantaged neighborhood contexts impairing recovery [41].

While these findings derive from China’s unique healthcare context—a middle-income country with residence-linked insurance systems—they offer transferable insights for nations with comparable socioeconomic profiles. The observed occupational disparities necessitate stratified interventions: for stable employment groups (white/blue-collar workers), targeted risk-factor mitigation such as workplace-based obesity and smoking cessation programs is warranted. Conversely, addressing systemic care gaps for unstable employment groups (farmers/unemployed) remains paramount, including establishing standardized reperfusion networks to expand PPCI access, implementing telemedicine-enabled post-discharge support to bridge rural-urban divides, and promoting policy reforms for equitable insurance coverage across occupational strata.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, comorbidity histories (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia) relied on self-report, potentially leading to underreporting due to incomplete disease awareness. Secondly, assessment of oral medication adherence was limited to the first six months post-discharge, precluding evaluation of long-term persistence and its inclusion as a covariate in survival analyses. Thirdly, the occupational classification used broad categories, which may obscure heterogeneity within groups, and lacked data on specific work-related exposures (e.g., working hours, shift patterns, job strain, physical environment) or employment stability (short/long-term status). Fourthly, the cohort included relatively few female patients, limiting generalizability to women. Finally, the study utilized data collected between 2013 and 2016; consequently, findings may not fully reflect contemporary AMI management practices, evolving socioeconomic patterns, or current occupational health landscapes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals distinct characteristics and outcomes among Chinese AMI patients across occupational groups compared to Western populations. Notably, patients in stable employment, particularly white-collar workers, demonstrated a higher burden of cardiovascular risk factors yet experienced more favorable long-term prognoses than farmers and the unemployed. These findings underscore the need for tailored interventions: enhanced risk factor management for white- and blue-collar workers, and improved healthcare access and quality for farmers and the unemployed. Future research should investigate occupational disparities in AMI risk and prognosis across diverse national and social contexts, specifically examining the impact of job-related characteristics such as stress levels, physical work environments, and interpersonal dynamics.
