Modeling the Impact of Ensitrelvir on SARS-CoV-2 Dynamics and Its Application for Assessment of Transmission Mitigation of Patients with COVID-19
Authors: Daichi Yamaguchi, Masaya M Saito, Ayano Hata, Ryosuke Shimizu, Shogo Miyazawa, Takamichi Baba, Ryuji Kubota, Yoshitake Kitanishi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMathematical modeling can provide quantitative understanding of the viral dynamics and viral reduction effects of drugs and enable simulations of the dynamics in various scenarios. In this study, a drug effect model of ensitrelvir was developed to describe the viral reduction effect. Using the model, we also estimated the impact of treatment with ensitrelvir on the reduction in the number of infected patients at the population level in Japan.MethodsThe drug effect model of ensitrelvir was developed based on a viral dynamic model for the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and a population pharmacokinetic model of ensitrelvir using 10,477 samples of viral load from 1447 patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in a phase 2/3 study. It was assumed that the drug effect on SARS-CoV-2 promoted the viral clearance depending on the free plasma concentrations. We estimated the impact of ensitrelvir treatment on the reduction in the number of infected patients at the population level in Japan using the susceptible–infectious–recovered–susceptible (SIRS) model including transmission mitigation.ResultsThe viral reduction effect of ensitrelvir was characterized as a promotion of viral clearance depending on the plasma ensitrelvir concentrations using the Emax model. The maximum reduction effect was considered to depend on the time from symptom onset to treatment. The maximum transmission mitigation effect was observed when treatment was initiated within 12–24 h of symptom onset, and secondary infections could be reduced by administering ensitrelvir as soon as possible after symptom onset.ConclusionThe viral reduction by ensitrelvir could be characterized based on the viral dynamics, and the dynamics could be estimated using the drug effect model. Furthermore, the drug effect on population level transmission based on the dynamics could be estimated. Thus, the simulation could be conducted for various conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-024-01046-6.Keywords: COVID-19, Ensitrelvir, SARS-CoV-2, Transmission mitigation, Viral dynamic model

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Quantitative understanding of viral dynamics based on mathematical modeling is useful for the appropriate use of drugs, and the relationships between viral amounts in humans and transmission have been reported for influenza. However, modeling and simulation studies for the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) with ensitrelvir have not yet been reported.We developed a drug effect model of ensitrelvir which can describe the viral reduction effect of ensitrelvir and estimated the impact of treatment with ensitrelvir on the reduction in the number of infected patients at the population level in Japan. What was learned from the study? We characterized the viral reduction by ensitrelvir based on the viral dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 including the time effect (time from symptom onset to treatment) and showed that secondary infections could be reduced by administering ensitrelvir as soon as possible after symptom onset.This drug effect model structure including the time effect can be applied to other types of antiviral drugs. Furthermore, population level prediction of patients with the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) based on information from the patients was performed, showing that it would be possible to conduct the simulation for various conditions.

Introduction

As one of the therapeutic antiviral drugs against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), ensitrelvir (a 3C-like protease inhibitor of SARS-CoV-2) is used for the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection in Japan [1]. Viral RNA level (viral load) data on human patients were collected in a phase 2/3 study on ensitrelvir (Japan Registry of Clinical Trials identifier: jRCT2031210350) [2–4]. This clinical trial was conducted from late 2021 to the end of 2022, when the Omicron variant was mainly prevalent, with patients having mild-to-moderate coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), i.e., they had at least one moderate or severe symptom or worsening of an existing symptom among the 12 COVID-19 symptoms defined by the US Food and Drug Administration guidance [5]. Participants in Japan, South Korea, and Vietnam were randomized to three groups (125-mg group: ensitrelvir 375 mg on Day 1 followed by 125 mg on Days 2–5, 250-mg group: ensitrelvir 750 mg on Day 1 followed by 250 mg on Days 2–5, and a matched placebo group) at the ratio of 1:1:1. In this trial, the viral reduction effect by ensitrelvir was confirmed. The viral titer data were also collected for mainly selection of enrolled patients in this clinical study; however, more than 90% of the samples were below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) since the decrease of viral titer in the active (ensitrelvir) groups was faster than in the placebo group.

Mathematical modeling can provide quantitative understanding of viral dynamics in humans or the viral reduction effect of antiviral drugs. Viral dynamic models with drug effects have been reported for the human immunodeficiency virus, herpes simplex virus-2, influenza virus, and other viruses [6–8]. These models have been used for various purposes, e.g., optimization for the dose regimen of antiviral drugs, evaluation of the relationship between viral cycle and dose timing, and drug discovery [9, 10]. Moreover, therapeutic drugs are one of the major factors in reducing infection risk, as described for influenza infection [11]. Many studies have been conducted on the relationship between influenza viral load and its transmission [12–15]. However, the relationship between viral load and transmission in COVID-19 remains unknown. It is known that the routes of human-to-human transmission in COVID-19 and influenza are similar, such as respiratory droplets, aerosol transmission, and direct contact with contaminated surfaces [16].

We developed a viral dynamic model for SARS-CoV-2 based on a target cell-limited model with immune function, which could describe the SARS-CoV-2 dynamics in a population mainly including vaccinated and Omicron-infected patients with COVID-19, using data from the placebo group in this clinical trial [17]. In addition, we developed a population pharmacokinetic (PK) model for ensitrelvir, which could characterize its PK profile and simulate the concentration–time profile after dosing with ensitrelvir [18]. In the present study, we integrated these models to construct a drug effect model of ensitrelvir which could describe the impact of the time from the start of the infection to the treatment by using viral data after administration of ensitrelvir. We also estimated the impact of treatment with ensitrelvir on the reduction in the number of infected patients at the population level in Japan using the drug effect model in order to investigate the possibility of antiviral drugs contributing to a decrease in the number of infections.

Methods

Data for the Drug Effect Model

To develop the drug effect model of ensitrelvir, we used the SARS-CoV-2 viral load data from the patients with COVID-19 in the 125-mg and 250-mg groups of the phase 2/3 study for ensitrelvir [2–4]. We included patients with detectable SARS-CoV-2 viral titer at the baseline in the phase 2a and 2b parts, and patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection confirmed by a qualitative/quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test based on a nasopharyngeal swab sample in the phase 3 part; asymptomatic patients were excluded from our analysis. The samples for measurement of the viral load were collected from a nasopharyngeal swab, a nasal swab, or saliva. The viral load in the phase 2a part was measured by RT-PCR at LSI Medience (Tokyo, Japan) and LLOQ was 3.40 log10 copies/mL. In the phase 2b and 3 parts, the measurements were performed by ViroClinics Biosciences (Rotterdam, The Netherlands) and the LLOQ was 2.08 log10 copies/mL.

This study was conducted according to the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. It used anonymized information from the clinical study data of participants who provided written informed consent and assented to participate in the study. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Please see the referenced articles for ethics relating to the original studies.

Drug Effect Model

The drug effect model was developed based on the viral dynamic model for SARS-CoV-2 [17] and the population PK model of ensitrelvir [18]. It was assumed that the antiviral effect on SARS-CoV-2 of ensitrelvir was promoting viral clearance (c) of both the infectious virus and the non-infectious virus depending on the free plasma concentrations. The scheme of this drug effect model is illustrated in Fig S1, and the model parameters of the viral dynamic model and the population PK model are summarized in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. where c and c’ (day−1) are the viral clearance rates without and with ensitrelvir, respectively, Emax is the maximum effect constant, EC50 (µg/mL) is the free plasma ensitrelvir concentration achieving 50% of Emax, fu is the unbound fraction of ensitrelvir, Cp (µg/mL) is the plasma ensitrelvir concentration, Teff is the time effect, and Tot (h) is the time from symptom onset to the start of ensitrelvir treatment. In our model, the time effect for the maximum effect was considered, which means that maximum effect of promoting c was Emax when Tot = 0 and Emax decreased with the time elapsed from the start of ensitrelvir treatment. In this analysis, fu was fixed at 0.023 based on an in vitro protein binding study [19], and EC50 was also fixed based on in vitro studies (in-house data which had been evaluated in a previous study [20]). The antiviral activities of ensitrelvir against SARS-CoV-2 were evaluated using human primary airway epithelial cells with various viral variants, and various EC50 values were obtained depending on in vitro study conditions. For developing our drug effect model, the minimum and maximum EC50 values (0.0114 and 0.127 µmol/L) among these studies, which assumed that ensitrelvir would have most and least antiviral effects in the human body, were used. The unit of EC50 was changed to µg/mL using the molecular weight of ensitrelvir fumaric acid (647.95 g/mol). The Cp of each patient was simulated using the population PK model of ensitrelvir considering the patients’ characteristics. In this clinical trial, the time from symptom onset to randomization was reported as categorical data every 24 h, and the median value for each category was set as Tot. The value of Tot for the patients who were randomized more than 120 h after symptom onset was assumed to be 120 h.

Calculation of Transmission Mitigation

The transmission mitigation of ensitrelvir was evaluated using the drug effect model. The viral dynamics were simulated for 100 vaccinated virtual patients, with a uniform distribution of body weight (35–125 kg), with and without a 5-day treatment regimen. For each patient, the viral simulations were performed for every situation in which the first dose was given within 12–72 h (every 2.4 h for Tot) after symptom onset. where α is set to 10–4 as in the previous study [12]. As in previous studies [12, 15], the area under the curve (AUC) of the viral load was calculated for each scale for the patients who received ensitrelvir treatment in comparison with the AUC of the patients without ensitrelvir treatment to estimate the relative reduction in AUC, which was defined as transmission mitigation. We plotted the infectiousness using each scale and estimated the transmission mitigation from Tot divided into 12-h intervals across 12–72 h.

SIRS Model

Using the secondary infection reduction rates (i.e., transmission mitigation) estimated in the previous section, a long-term scenario analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of ensitrelvir on the number of infections at the population level.

In order to perform a long-term scenario analysis of the number of infections, it was necessary to consider the duration of immunity after reinfection. Therefore, we adopted the age-structured susceptible–infectious–recovered–susceptible (SIRS) model, which is a mathematical model of infectious diseases that can express the epidemic dynamics of infectious diseases while taking reinfection into account.

The mathematical model was based on a reported study [21], with λEi added as a new parameter to account for the reduction rate of secondary infections due to ensitrelvir treatment, as shown in Fig. 1. The details of the original mathematical model can be found in the original paper [21]. In the SIRS model, the following differential equations are used to express the evolution of the numbers of persons in the susceptible group (Sip), first-time infected group (Iip), recovered group (Ri), infected susceptible group (Sinp), and second or later infected group (Iinp) for each age category i (5-year intervals from 0 to 80).

The first-infected cohorts and the second- or subsequent-infected cohorts became a recovered cohort after an average duration of infection of 1/γ. The recovered population lost immunity after an average duration of 1/ω and became a susceptible population with experience of infection and re-infection thereafter. Based on these definitions, we performed a scenario analysis of the number of patients with COVID-19 infection over 20 years. The estimated numbers were used to calculate the percentage of decrease in the number of infections at each treatment rate relative to the number of infections with no treatment for each year. Also, the average reduction in the annual number of infections for the total population in Japan was estimated per incubation duration and treatment rate. It was assumed that there were 48 million infected patients per year [26, 27], and the reduction rate was calculated using the average reduction rate over the 20-year period calculated in this study.

Software

The parameter estimation in the drug effect model was performed by the likelihood maximized using the stochastic approximation expectation–maximization algorithm implemented in NONMEM ver.7.4.4 [28]. The other simulations and graphical analyses were performed using R ver.4.1.2.

Drug Effect Model

To describe the antiviral effect of ensitrelvir, the Emax model with the time effect for the maximum effect of Tot was selected, and we developed two drug effect models (low EC50 model and high EC50 model). The parameter estimations except for EC50 of the drug effect models were performed using the observed viral load data and the estimated values are shown in Table 2. The estimated values of Emax in low and high EC50 models were 13.9 and 12.9, respectively, and Emax decreased over Tot in both models (Teff in low and high EC50 models were –0.0294 and –0.0233, respectively). The individual predicted values from both models were approximately similar to the observed values based on the goodness of fit plots, as shown in Fig S2. The models which inhibit the production rate of infectious virus from productive infected cells and promote the loss rate of productive infected cells were also tested. These models were not selected based on BIC or visual prediction accuracy. Inhibiting virus production rate might be more appropriate than promoting viral clearance as a viral reduction effect of ensitrelvir based on the pharmacological action of ensitrelvir, however that model was not selected for describing observed viral load appropriately due to the structure of target cell-limited model. The simulation results using the developed high EC50 model for each virtual patient with vaccination in the 125-mg group are illustrated in Fig. 3. These results for each virtual patient with or without vaccination in both dose groups are shown in Fig S3 for the low EC50 model and Fig S4 for the high EC50 model. It was confirmed that the simulated viral load from these models could be well described by the observed dynamics. In addition, viral reduction was rapid if treatment was started soon after infection.

Transmission Mitigation

To simulate transmission mitigation, the high EC50 model of ensitrelvir was used as the conservative condition. The mean viral load in the patients without ensitrelvir treatment estimated by the drug effect model are plotted in Fig S5. The natural scale showed a dramatic increase in infectiousness, with no infectivity after about 5 days. On the other hand, the log and dose–response scales showed that the risk of secondary infection peaked at 2 days after symptom onset, indicating a long period of secondary infection risk. The log scale was modeled to indicate a certain level of infectivity even after 10 days from symptom onset. We found that infectious viruses could be detected in the nasopharynx even after 10 days from symptom onset with Omicron variants, and, taking this into account, the log or dose–response scale seemed to fit with the previous findings [29]. The effect of mitigation was estimated by calculating the relative reduction in AUC of patients with the ensitrelvir treatment compared to patients without the treatment at 12-h intervals (12–24 h, 24–36 h, 36–48 h, and 48–60 h) from symptom onset. The sooner the treatment was started after symptom onset, the greater was the efficacy of ensitrelvir in transmission-mitigation, as illustrated in Fig. 4. In the natural scale model, the median reduction of infection was 17.6%, 8.5%, 3.8%, 1.7%, and 0.8% when treatment was initiated at 12–24 h, 24–36 h, 36–48 h, 48–60 h, and 60 -72 h after symptom onset, respectively, as shown in Table S4. When the log scale model and dose–response model were used, the transmission–mitigation effects were 9.4%, 7.9%, 6.5%, 5.2%, and 4.1%, respectively, for the logarithm scale model, and 14.9%, 13.0%, 11.2%, 9.5%, and 7.9%, respectively, for the dose–response model. In all models used in this study, the greatest transmission–mitigation effect was observed when treatment was initiated within 12–24 h of symptom onset. The natural scale which showed a dramatic increase in infectiousness would be considered to be inappropriate, and we assumed that secondary infections could be reduced by up to 10–15% by taking ensitrelvir as soon as possible after symptom onset based on the transmission–mitigation effect estimated from these two models (log and dose–response scales). Based on this assumption, we simulated the impact on the number of infections in the population under several scenarios with different treatment rates with ensitrelvir.

SIRS Model

In this scenario analysis, the number of patients with COVID-19 for 20 years after the pandemic was estimated, taking into account differences in the duration of immunity after infection (6 months and 1 year) and the proportion of infected persons taking ensitrelvir (10%, 40%, and 70%). The SIRS model simulation, which took into account the demographics of the Japanese population, showed a gradual transition to a steady state (a certain percentage of the population being infected) approximately 2 years after a pandemic, as illustrated in Fig. 5. During the endemic period (approximately 10 years after the pandemic), it was estimated that approximately 2% of the population would be infected if the duration of immunity was 6 months, and approximately 1% if the duration was 1 year, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Furthermore, we calculated the reduction rate in the number of infections at each treatment rate relative to the number of infections when no treatment was given, as shown in Table S5. Assuming a transmission–mitigation rate of 10%, the reduction rate in the number of infections was approximately 0.3%, 1.2%, and 2.1% for 10%, 40%, and 70% of the treatment rate, regardless of the duration of immunity. When the transmission mitigation was 15%, the reduction rate of infection was about 0.4%, 1.8%, and 3.2% for each treatment rate with ensitrelvir. These reduction rates remained almost constant regardless of the number of infected patients until 20 years after the pandemic. Also, the estimated reductions in the annual number of infections for the total population in Japan with a 70% treatment rate were approximately 1 million and 1.5 million for 10% and 15% of mitigation, respectively, as shown in Fig. 6.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed a drug effect model of ensitrelvir based on the viral dynamic model for SARS-CoV-2 and the population PK model of ensitrelvir to describe the viral reduction effect of ensitrelvir using the observed viral load dynamics. Furthermore, the impact of treatment in a largely vaccinated population with ensitrelvir on the reduction in the number of infected patients at the population level in Japan was estimated by simulation using this drug effect model.

The Emax model with the time effect for the maximum effect of Tot was selected as the drug effect model of ensitrelvir. In the clinical trial of ensitrelvir, the efficacy has been confirmed for patients with COVID-19 who were randomized within 72 h from symptom onset [3]. The findings indicated that higher effectiveness could be expected by timely treatment. A similar relationship between the efficacy of the antiviral drug and the time from symptom onset to treatment has been confirmed for other viruses: oseltamivir for influenza virus and antiretrovirals for human immunodeficiency virus [30, 31]. In our study, the relationship between the time from symptom onset to the start of treatment and the maximum viral reduction effect could be characterized using a linear (exponential) model, which is applicable to other viruses. The results suggested that a higher viral reduction effect could be expected by starting treatment before the viral load reaches the maximum value (about 5.5 days after infection, as shown in Fig. 3); the viral load is also reduced to a lower level than in cases without treatment. The incubation period, which is defined as the time from infection to the onset of signs and symptoms caused by the Omicron variant, was reported as 3.42 days (95% confidence interval was 2.88–3.96 days) [32]. Our findings suggested that a greater reduction rate of viral AUC could be attained by starting treatment within about 2 days after symptom onset. These tendencies could be calculated for transmission mitigation, especially using natural and log scales, as shown in Fig S5.

Although there were certain limitations, as described below, the simulation could provide some indication of the long-term trends of emerging infectious diseases such as COVID-19 and the degree of effectiveness of countermeasures such as therapeutic drugs. We next estimated the number of infected patients in Japan using the simulations in this study. In Japan, the number of infected patients with the Omicron variant has increased since January 2022, and the number of infected patients had increased more than in the case of outbreaks of other variants [33]. The annual cumulative number of infectious patients in Japan in 2022 was estimated to be about 27 million based on the number of reported patients [34]. However, it could be assumed that SARS-CoV-2 is characterized by a certain degree of subclinical infection, or that there are a certain number of infected patients who have not been tested and thus not reported. The true annual cumulative number of infected patients in Japan in 2022 was estimated to have been about 39–48 million people, based on the results of this study. If 70% of the patients were to take antiviral drugs such as ensitrelvir, a reduction of about 1–1.5 million infections per year, or a 2–3% reduction in the number of infections, could be expected. These results were simulated based on the data from a largely vaccinated population which is the current situation in Japan and many countries, and it showed the impact that ensitrelvir could have even in a vaccinated population.

One major limitation of this study is that we used data on viral load rather than viral titer as a starting point for translation to infectivity. It was difficult to describe viral titer dynamics in the drug effect model due to low amount of data above LLOQ. It has been noted that a high viral load leads to high infectiousness in SARS-CoV-2, and some studies supported this correlation [35–37]. In this study, the correlation between viral load and infectiousness was estimated assuming that the log scale or dose–response scale model fits, based on the fact that the Omicron variant remained infectious even 10 days after symptom onset [27]. Regarding influenza, many studies have been conducted on the relationship between viral load and its transmission, and the logarithm scale was found to be particularly compatible with the data from the BLOCKSTONE study [13]. Other previous studies also employed the logarithm scale for the relationship between viral load and the secondary infection rate [14, 15]. In the case of SARS-CoV-2, no paper has yet reported the model with the best fit. On the other hand, taking into account the estimated distribution of intrinsic generation times of the Omicron strain [38] and the reported incubation period of approximately 3.61 days [39], it can be assumed that secondary infections occur even 5–10 days after symptom onset. In light of this, models that assume secondary infections occur even after a certain period of time has passed since symptom onset, such as the logarithm scale and dose–response scale, are considered more persuasive than the natural scale. To decide which model best fits the correlation between viral load and infectiousness, data on viral load over time and household transmission rates are needed, as has been done in previous studies [12]. Data from future clinical trials of ensitrelvir could aid clarification. Another limitation of this study is that the observed data below LLOQ (27.2%) were excluded from this analysis, the same as in the previous study [17]. Viral load data were collected over a long period in the clinical trial for confirming that viruses have been removed from patients. Including below LLOQ by M3 methods [40] made the model parameters irrelevant because a small amount of viruses exist persistently through a long period after infection. Therefore, the data below LLOQ were excluded from the analysis. Analysis using below LLOQ data with proper handling might be performed for evaluation of viral dynamics including a low range of viral levels. The other limitation of this study is that some parameters in the SIRS model, such as the basic reproduction number and the average infection period, were set based on previous studies to fit the characteristics of the Omicron variant. The simulation conducted in this study was simplified to assume that the epidemic strain would not change even 20 years after the pandemic. In reality, multiple variants with different characteristics should emerge after a pandemic and have a considerable impact on the epidemic situation. Furthermore, the simulation did not take into account artificial interventions such as lockdowns that were in place in various countries at the time of the pandemic outbreak. As mentioned above, there were various conditions of an epidemic and its control that were not considered in this model. However, we believe that this is a helpful model that can be improved with further clinical and empirical studies.

Conclusion

We have characterized the viral reduction by ensitrelvir based on the viral dynamics of the SARS-CoV-2 and PK profiles of ensitrelvir. The viral reduction effect of the various treatment periods could be evaluated and this model structure including the time effect should be applicable to other types of antiviral drugs. An important finding is the time to treatment and its impact on infectivity, showing the importance of future public health action to improve access to testing and treatment. As an application of the model, the transmission–mitigation effect was translated from the viral reduction, and the impact of treatment with ensitrelvir on the reduction in the number of infected patients at the population level in Japan was estimated. The population level prediction was based on information from human subjects and allowed for simulation under various conditions.
