Applications of spectral CT calcium suppression for percutaneous bone tumor ablation procedures
Authors: Juna Musa, Thomas D Atwell, Daniel A Adamo, Ahmad Parvinian, Nathan R Huber, Setayesh Sotoudehnia Korani, Brian T Welch, Christopher P Favazza, Rebecca Hibbert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo cases are presented in which spectral CT calcium suppression was perceived as beneficial for percutaneous cryoablation of bone tumors. Cases include cryoablation of a lytic sacral/iliac bone metastasis and cryoablation of recurrent pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) at the posterior aspect of the right knee. Calcium suppression images have a potential role in the visualization of bone lesions for treatment planning and the visualization of ice ball growth for treatment monitoring.Keywords: Spectral CT, Calcium suppression, Cryoablation, Bone tumors, Ice ball visualization, Interventional oncology

Introduction

Percutaneous cryoablation is an effective technique for palliative or curative treatment of bone lesions [[1], [2], [3]]. A typical procedure involves placement of cryoprobes at or near a lesion to induce tumor destruction by freezing. Risk of major complication during bone cryoablation is low (2.5%), with the most common complications being skin frostbite, nerve injury, secondary fracture, and hemorrhage [4,5]. Associated risk factors include age (greater than 70 years) and use of more than 3 cryoprobes [4]. Contraindications of this procedure include active infection, bleeding disorders, spinal instability, and multiple medical comorbidities [6,7]. Percutaneous bone cryoablation is typically performed using CT-guidance; however, inability to radiographically monitor the ablation zone in bone during treatment remains a limitation [8]. Follow-up imaging (CT, MRI, or PET) at 3-12 months post-procedure is typically recommended to assess local tumor progression and risk of fracture [9].

Spectral CT, also referred to as dual-energy CT, uses the differentiation of high and low energy x-rays to provide additional information about the properties of the tissues being scanned. Multiple approaches to spectral CT have been described including dual-layer or photon counting detectors, dual x-ray sources, rapid switching of x-ray tube potential, and temporarily sequential scanning at 2 x-ray tube potentials [10]. In addition to the conventional CT images, spectral CT enables multiple visualization modes including virtual monoenergetic images, material-specific maps (ie, iodine, uric acid), material-specific removal (ie, virtual-non-contrast, calcium suppression), and additional tissue characterization (ie, electron density, z-effective). Calcium suppression is the result of interest for this report.

Calcium suppression images can be used to visualize soft tissue and bone marrow in the absence of calcium signal [11]. Diagnostic applications of calcium suppression include assessment of bone marrow edema [12], multiple myeloma lytic lesions [13], and osteoporosis [14]. Within the context of interventional oncology, dual-energy CT calcium suppression has been demonstrated in cadavers for monitoring of cryoablation zone growth in spinal column and bony pelvis [15]. This report provides in-vivo demonstration of spectral CT calcium suppression result for cryoablation of a lytic sacral/iliac bone metastasis and a recurrent pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) at the posterior aspect of the knee.

Case presentation 1

Case summary

A 77-year-old female with a history of lung adenocarcinoma and metastatic disease presented with a painful lytic lesion in the sacral/iliac region. Given her extensive medical history and the location of the metastasis, CT-guided percutaneous cryoablation was selected as the treatment modality to achieve local tumor control and pain relief. Motor evoked potentials were monitored throughout the procedure to confirm integrity of motor pathways.

Ablation procedure

Ablation procedures were performed in a dedicated hybrid procedural suite with patients under general anesthesia. Initial CT scans were performed to assess the extent of the metastasis and plan the placement of cryoprobes. Six probe trajectories were selected to ensure complete coverage of the tumor while avoiding injury to the sacral nerve roots. The cryoprobes had a shaft diameter of 2.1 mm (14 G) and shaft length of 17.5 cm (3x IcePearl, Boston Scientific and 3x IceForce, Boston Scientific). A ten-minute freeze, 5-minute passive thaw, and 8-minute refreeze ablation technique was used. Ice ball growth was monitored with spectral CT imaging (Spectral CT 7500, Philips Healthcare) every 2 minutes throughout each freeze cycle. During the second freeze cycle, there was a slight increase in the latency of L5-S1 nerve root motor evoked potentials, indicating transient nerve irritation. This was closely monitored, and the latency returned to baseline values rapidly following the active thawing of the probes, suggesting no permanent nerve damage. Following cryoprobe removal, a limited post-procedural CT was performed in order to screen for any immediate complications.

Twenty-four hours following cryoablation, percutaneous stabilization of the pelvis was performed using a photodynamic bone stabilization technique (IlluminOss) and cement-augmented fixation of the ilio-sacral joint. Four months post-procedure imaging confirmed a stable, healed fracture, indicating a successful intervention.

Role of spectral CT calcium suppression

The spectral CT calcium suppression result (suppression index: 76) yielded a more conspicuous ice ball in the image and provided the capability to monitor ice ball growth. Due to the proximity of the sacral nerve roots, tracking the ablation zone (ie, ice ball size) was paramount to a successful treatment. In the conventional CT image result, the high signal attenuation from bone obscured the ice ball making it difficult to accurately assess margins. The calcium suppression result enabled improved visualization of the target lesion during planning and ablation margins during ice ball monitoring and post-ablation assessment, as shown in Fig. 1. Case presentation 2

Case summary

A 27-year-old male presented with a recurrent PVNS nodule measuring 1.2 cm in diameter, located deep to the lateral head of the gastrocnemius muscle at the posterior aspect of the right knee. The lesion’s proximity to the bone resulted in suboptimal visualization during procedure planning and ice ball monitoring.

Ablation procedure

Under general anesthesia, a single cryoablation probe was placed using a combination of ultrasound and CT guidance. Ultrasound provided soft tissue localization of PVNS nodule probe placement; however, intraosseous involvement could not be visualized with ultrasound [16]. CT provided superior visualization of the bone involvement and helped confirm accurate needle position prior to ablation [17]. The cryoprobes had a shaft diameter of 1.5 mm (17 G) and a shaft length of 17.5 cm (1x IceSphere, Boston Scientific). A 4-minute freeze, 5-minute passive thaw, and 2-minute refreeze ablation technique was used. Ice ball growth was monitored with spectral CT imaging every 2 minutes throughout each freeze cycle.

Post-procedure imaging confirmed that the ablation zone effectively encompassed the targeted PVNS nodule, indicating successful treatment. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged with no immediate adverse effects observed. Post-procedure MRI of the knee at 6 months did not indicate any signs of recurrence.

Role of spectral CT calcium suppression

During the planning stage, calcium suppression (suppression index: 76) enabled improved visualization of the lesion which was partially obscured by the bone. Additionally, calcium suppression enabled better assessment of possible cortical destruction by the tumor. During the cryoablation monitoring stage, there was improved visualization of ice growth near the bone and lesion using the calcium suppression result. Calcium suppression subtracted out the bone signal which was perceived as helpful for informing appropriate treatment coverage, as shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

These example cases demonstrate the potential value of spectral CT calcium suppression for cryoablation of bone tumors. Importantly, calcium suppression in these cases improved visualization of the ice ball formed during cryoablation. Ice ball visualization is crucial for ensuring complete coverage of the tumor and avoidance of adjacent critical structures. Inadequate ice ball coverage could lead to residual tumor cells and compromised curative or palliative treatment efficacy [18,19].

To our knowledge, this is the first in vivo demonstration of dual-energy calcium suppression for improving ice ball visualization during bone cryoablation. Prior work in porcine and human cadavers concluded that calcium suppression can enable ice visualization in near real time leading to potentially greater confidence in treatment [15]. The type of dual-energy technique differed from the cadaveric study (dual-source) and the current report (dual-layer detector). One practical advantage of dual-layer detector CT is that spectral results are always available without protocol modification. For the cases described, spectral results were reconstructed along with conventional CT results. In a spectral CT viewer (Advanced Visualization Workspace, Philips Healthcare) when the calcium suppression result is selected the optimal suppression value can be tuned for a given scan. These workflow efficiencies and adaptable calcium suppression implementation are helpful for the user to optimize ice ball visualization during an intervention.

Calcium suppression index (range of 25-100, default of 76) allows for user adjustment to optimize visualization based on individual bone composition. Reducing the calcium suppression index is recommended for the assessment of trabecular or spongy bone and increasing calcium suppression index is suitable for dense cortical bone. In prior works, a lower calcium suppression index (25) was used for vertebral metastasis detection [20] whereas a higher index (80) was used in the assessment of cortical endplates [21]. For the 2 cases in this paper, the vendor-supplied default setting of 76 was deemed suitable.

There are opportunities to further improve spectral CT calcium suppression for bone tumor cryoablation. Metal artifact from the ablation probe commonly obscures soft tissue and ice ball visualization during treatment monitoring [15]. Improved handling of metal artifact, such as using an AI-based spectral CT metal artifact reduction technique, could potentially be employed to improve this result [22]. Additionally, ice ball margins in the presence of dense cortical bone may be difficult to visualize in the calcium suppression result. Reducing noise level may also be beneficial for lesion visualization and ice ball monitoring.

Conclusions

In conclusion, spectral CT calcium suppression presents an opportunity to improve visualization of the ice ball during bone tumor cryoablation. By providing improved delineation of ice ball margins, and enabling near real-time procedural feedback, this imaging technique has the potential to improve the effectiveness of bone tumor cryoablation.
