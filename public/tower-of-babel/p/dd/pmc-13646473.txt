Smartphone Applications in Dermatology With Special Focus on AI: A European‐Continent‐Based Review
Authors: Tobias Sangers, Ahmad Naweed Shifai, Davide Giovanni Jacob Pasqualini, Catarina Barata, Ralph Braun, Titus Josef Brinker, Alessandra Cartocci, Ben Esdaile, Simone Goldinger, Pascale Guitera, Holger Haenssle, Harald Kittler, Jilliana Monnier, Cristián Navarrete‐Dechent, Sebastian Podlipnik, Laura Serra‐García, Wilhelm Stolz, Kenneth Thomsen, Philipp Tschandl, Linda Tognetti, Elsemieke Plasmeijer, Remco van Doorn, Josep Malvehy, the EADV AI Task Force
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSmartphone applications for dermatology are widely available across Europe, yet evidence on their characteristics, validation, and regulatory compliance remains limited. This review, conducted by the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) Artificial Intelligence (AI) Task Force, systematically searched app stores in 44 European countries, identifying 1746 apps in 2024, of which 420 met inclusion criteria. Specifically for AI‐based skin cancer screening apps, the search was updated in June 2026. Apps were categorized by function, with metadata extracted on cost, target audience, AI presence, General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance, and CE (Conformité Européenne) marking. Most apps (61%) were available on both Google Play and Apple App Store; 94% were free, and 60% targeted laypersons. Twelve percent reported AI functionality in 2024, 41% of these focused on skin cancer diagnosis. Only 2% reported CE marking and 10% GDPR compliance. Scientific validation was limited. Twenty percent of apps linked to peer‐reviewed publications, 24 apps had clinical trial evaluations, 5 underwent randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and 6 had real‐world post‐deployment studies. The 2026 search identified 38 AI‐based skin cancer screening apps. Adherence to EADV recommendations was poor. Key principles including explainability, inclusivity, and data sharing were met by ≤ 37% of apps. Most dermatology apps target laypersons for teledermatology, education, and self‐diagnosis. Major gaps exist in regulatory compliance, clinical evidence, and transparency, raising patient safety concerns. These shortcomings raise concerns about reliability and safety, underscoring the need for stronger quality assurance, real‐world validation, and inclusion of diverse skin types, in line with EADV AI Task Force recommendations. Keywords: applications, artificial intelligence, dermatology, Europe, skin cancer, smartphone

Introduction

Over the past decades, the widespread adoption of smartphones has transformed access to health information and digital health tools. As of 2025, approximately half a billion individuals in Europe own a smartphone, representing nearly 90% of the adult population [1]. In parallel, the development and availability of mobile health software applications (apps) have expanded significantly, with a growing focus on dermatology [2]. Since a decade, apps for dermatology have been available for different types of smartphones and tablets with software that facilitates access to everyone. This trend is driven by the digitization of health care systems, increasing public interest in skin health, and technological advancements enabling rapid and user‐friendly access to dermatological information and services. In some areas, this increase in medical app use is also due to a lack of dermatologists leading to long delays to get an appointment. Dermatology‐related apps target the general public, primary care providers, and dermatology healthcare professionals. These range from educational platforms providing insights into skin conditions and treatment options to artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven tools for self‐diagnosis [3]. The advent of smartphones combined with apps, AI and high quality of images taken with smartphones led to complete tools easy to use for everyone.

Although the amount of dermatology‐related smartphone apps continues to grow, and some countries start to allow digital tools to be reimbursed and prescribed to patients, an overview of dermatology‐related apps specifically available to the European public is still lacking [4]. Recent studies have mapped dermatology‐related apps available in the United States of America (USA) and Australia [3, 5]. Importantly, the availability of these apps may vary substantially between countries and regions. To the best of our knowledge, no review is available that comprehensively summarizes the apps available in Europe. Additionally, there is limited insight into how accurately AI‐powered tools align with emerging professional standards, such as those issued by the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) AI Task Force [6]. There is a clear need for an up‐to‐date systematic catalog of dermatology‐related smartphone apps, including their functions, target audience, supporting evidence, and integration of AI where relevant. This review, authored by the EADV AI Task Force, addresses this need by providing a comprehensive overview of publicly accessible smartphone apps focused on dermatology available in European countries.

Methods

App Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted for dermatology‐related mobile apps available on the Google Play Store and Apple App Store. Both platforms were searched for active, downloadable apps on January 18, 2024, by using automated app store scraper scripts (Data S1). Specifically, the open‐source libraries app‐store‐scraper (https://github.com/facundoolano/app‐store‐scraper, MIT License, version 0.18.0) and google‐play‐scraper (https://github.com/facundoolano/google‐play‐scraper, MIT License, version 10.0.0) were employed within a JavaScript environment. Script development and execution were carried out using Visual Studio Code (Microsoft, 2024; version 1.98.2). The search strategy incorporated the following keywords to identify potentially relevant apps across both app stores: dermatology, dermatological, dermatologic, mole, naevus, nevus, eczema, psoriasis, rosacea, acne, skin cancer, melanoma, hidradenitis suppurativa, UV, teledermatology, vitiligo, and dermoscopy. These terms were adapted from a previously published US‐based review by Ouellette et al. [3]. The scripts facilitated the extraction of app data from app stores across 44 European countries (full list is provided in Data S1). Given the rapid expansion of AI technologies, the search for smartphone applications employing AI for skin cancer detection was updated on May 7, 2026.

Literature Search Strategy

In parallel to the app store screening, a systematic PubMed search was conducted to identify peer‐reviewed publications related to dermatology‐related apps. The aim of this search was twofold: (1) to determine whether any of the identified apps had been evaluated or mentioned in the scientific literature, and (2) to detect additional dermatology‐related apps which have not been captured through app store scraping. The search strategy was developed in collaboration with a medical librarian of the Leiden University Medical Center library and was executed on April 24, 2024 (full search criteria are reported in Data S2).

Eligibility Criteria

For the literature search, study eligibility was assessed in a two‐step process. Title and abstract screening were manually performed by two reviewers (N.S. and T.S.), followed by full‐text screening conducted by three reviewers (N.S., T.S., and D.P.). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Studies were included if they (1) referenced a mobile application or (2) mentioned an application already identified through the web‐based app store searches.

Mobile apps were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) were available and active status in at least one of the 44 European countries, and (2) included functionalities related to skin health monitoring, dermatologic disease diagnosis, clinical decision support, dermatology education or awareness, or communication within the field of dermatology. Apps in any language were eligible, provided that an English version was also available. Apps were excluded when their primary purpose was entertainment, general medical information without dermatologic relevance, general cosmetic advice without reference to skin disease, or photo editing. Weather apps were included only if they provided dermatology‐relevant information, such as ultraviolet (UV) protection guidance.

Analysis of Included Apps

Metadata for each app included was initially extracted using the automated capabilities of the app‐store‐scraper and google‐play‐scraper libraries. Also, extracted variables such as Apple application identifier, average user rating, number of ratings, developer name, initial release date, latest version date, and cost were included. In addition, keyword‐based queries were incorporated into the JavaScript scripts to identify whether the app description reported (1) having a Conformité Européenne (CE) marking, (2) utilization of AI functionalities, or (3) compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Although registration in the European Database on Medical Devices (EUDAMED) is not yet mandatory, CE marking claims were verified using the database to enhance the reliability of reported regulatory compliance [7]. All extracted metadata was verified subsequently through manual review of the corresponding app listings on the web‐based versions of the Google Play Store and Apple App Store. During this verification process, apps were categorized to their intended target audience (laypersons or healthcare providers) based on their descriptions.

Further categorization of apps was conducted using a modified framework adapted from previously published studies by Ouellette et al. and Brewer et al. [3, 8]. The 15 categories included: general dermatology knowledge, educational aids, disease guides, self‐diagnosis tools, teledermatology platforms, calculators or measurement tools, conference and event platforms, scientific journals, photography tools, research tools, cosmetic dermatology, dermatology patient platforms, clinical decision‐making tools, nonphotographic documentation tools, and self‐treatment or awareness apps.

Scientific articles were included and categorized based on the nature and depth of their discussion of dermatology‐related mobile apps. The categories were defined as follows: (1) Mention only, if the app and perhaps its functionalities were named without further elaboration; (2) Commentary, if the article discussed app functionalities or clinical implications; (3) Systematic review; (4) Randomized controlled trial (RCT); (5) Clinical trial (nonrandomized); (6) Development paper; (7) Qualitative study; (8) Accuracy study without direct patient involvement with in silico experiments, if pre‐existing images or datasets were used without new data collection; (9) Accuracy study without direct patient involvement in head‐to‐head tests with health professionals, if pre‐existing images or datasets were used without new data collection; and (10) Post‐deployment real‐world study.

For apps that incorporated AI functionalities specifically intended for skin cancer diagnosis, compliance with the EADV AI Task Force position statement on AI‐assisted smartphone apps was assessed (Table 1) [6]. App developers were contacted by email to request relevant documentation and information. Nonresponders received one follow‐up reminder after 15–30 days. If no response was received after the reminder, publicly available data from the application's website was reviewed to complete the assessment. Developer‐supplied information concerning a shared AI platform was applied to apps distributed by the same developer. Third‐party apps using that platform were assessed separately unless app‐specific documentation was available. All data presented in the tables, figures, and main text pertains to the core dataset derived from the 2024 app and literature search, unless explicitly stated that the reported data encompasses apps from both the 2024 and the updated 2026 search.

Results

App Selection

A total of 1746 smartphone apps available on the European market, including countries of the World Health Organization (WHO) European Region, were initially identified through our search: 916 on the iOS App Stores and 830 on the Google Play Stores (Figure 1). After screening, 1098 apps were excluded, mainly due to nondermatology content (e.g., general health apps such as pedometers, fitness trackers, or diet coaches without dermatology‐related features, and children's games using “skin” in unrelated contexts). In parallel, a systematic PubMed search of scientific literature initially identified 1784 articles, of which 204 were included after title, abstract, and full text screening. An additional 31 apps were identified through the literature search. The final dataset comprised a total of 420 apps, which formed the basis for further analysis. These included 255 apps available on both platforms, 117 available only on iOS, and 48 available only on Android. The updated search conducted on May 7, 2026 identified an additional 17 apps focused on AI‐based skin cancer detection.

App Characteristics

The identified apps were classified into 15 categories based on their primary functionalities and intended use (Table 2). The most common categories were teledermatology (n = 54), general dermatology knowledge (n = 51), disease guides (n = 47), and calculators or measurement tools (n = 37). Overall, a greater number of apps were available on the Apple App Store (89% of apps) compared to the Google Play Store (72% of apps), with 61% of apps available on both platforms. The majority of apps (94%) were free to download. Most (60% of apps) were targeted toward laypersons, while the remainder were designed for use by healthcare professionals. Among all included apps, 2% reported having obtained CE marking, and 10% indicated compliance with the GDPR within their app descriptions. From the 2024 app search, seven apps with a CE marking identified themselves as Class I medical devices whereas one app obtained CE marking as a Class IIa medical device (Table S1). The updated 2026 AI app search detected one more app with a Class IIa CE marking.

Adherence to the EADV AI Task Force Recommendations

Including the updated 2026 AI‐based skin cancer screening app search, 38 dermatology apps utilizing AI for skin cancer detection were identified and systematically evaluated based on the EADV AI Task Force recommendations (Table 4, Table S3). Publicly available information remained limited across most of the recommended criteria. Despite repeated attempts to contact app developers through email, only a minority responded to requests for additional data, which restricted the completeness of the assessment. Consequently, the evaluation was largely based on publicly accessible sources such as app store listings and developer websites. Among the criteria most frequently fulfilled were cost transparency (92%) and data privacy (87%). Explicit user consent regarding data monetization (37%) and the level of clinical validation (32%) were documented less often. Only 21% of apps described inclusion of diverse skin tones in training data, 8% described mechanisms for post‐clearance monitoring, and 13% addressed the requirement for comparable performance across different user groups and devices. Criteria concerning algorithm explainability, accessibility for diverse populations, avoidance of exclusion, and error reporting were each fulfilled by 18% or fewer of the assessed apps. Multidisciplinary involvement, such as input from dermatologists, was reported in 42% of cases.

Discussion

This review provides a systematic overview of the wide range of dermatology‐related mobile apps currently available in European Apple App Stores and Google Play Stores. Most apps were categorized under teledermatology, general dermatologic education, and disease‐specific guidance. Only 5% of the 2024 dataset apps focused on skin cancer detection with AI. Sixty percent were designed for layperson use, while the remainder targeted healthcare professionals. Importantly, our findings indicate persistent gaps in regulatory compliance or in the documentation of compliance accessible to the public. Very few apps indicated having obtained a CE marking. Scientific evidence was scarce as only 20% of apps were linked to a scientific publication. In most cases, the apps were briefly mentioned in the referenced publications. The adherence to the EADV AI Task Force recommendations was highly variable and often difficult to assess due to a lack of data. The apparent scarcity of regulatory compliance and peer‐reviewed evidence raises concerns regarding the reliability, safety, and effectiveness of these widely accessible apps to the whole population providing diagnosis assistance and medical advice.

Contextualization Within Existing Literature

Previous reviews have assessed dermatology‐related mobile apps from varying geographical perspectives. Ouellette et al. conducted a review of dermatology apps available in the United States, identifying 632 apps, of which 63% were directed toward laypersons and 16% reported incorporating AI functionalities [3]. In the present analysis of apps available in Europe, we identified 420 apps in 2024, with 60% targeting laypersons and 12% utilizing AI. The relative proportion of apps incorporating AI appears comparable, despite the rapid global growth in AI implementation in recent years [9].

As the integration of AI in dermatology apps is gaining more interest, recent studies have focused specifically on AI‐assisted apps in dermatology. Wongvibulsin et al. recently conducted a study of 41 dermatology‐related apps that utilize AI [10]. Only 12% of these apps had supporting evidence published in peer‐reviewed journals, none of the apps were approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and only 5% of the apps possessed CE marking. Similar to these findings, our study finds that only 20% of available apps were reported in at least one peer‐reviewed scientific paper and only 2% of all apps in our study reported CE marking in app store descriptions.

A recent review by Oloruntoba et al. on Australian mobile apps for AI skin cancer diagnostics included 21 apps and reported limited scientific support and a lack of transparency in the training data of the available apps [5]. This lack of transparency regarding demographic diversity in the development of AI algorithms for skin disease was previously highlighted in a scoping review by Daneshjou et al. [11]. They showed that only 24% of images used to develop AI algorithms for dermatological purposes were publicly available, only 20% of studies included descriptions of ethnicity or race, and only 10% of studies included any information about skin tone. These findings, in line with the results of our study, highlight a consistent lack of transparency and widespread validation not only within Europe, but also across diverse international settings.

Medical Device Regulation and CE Marking

A particularly concerning finding of our study was the remarkably low proportion of AI skin cancer screening apps identified from both the 2024 and updated 2026 search, reporting having obtained CE marking (11%). Whilst CE marking does not assure high performance of the apps, it does signal that the app meets the European legal, safety, and quality standards for medical devices. Under the European Medical Device Regulation (MDR), which came into force in May 2021, mobile apps intended for the diagnosis of disease are medical devices. Therefore, they are required to obtain CE marking. Medical devices are classified according to the potential harm to users: Class I for low risk, Class IIa or IIb for moderate risk, and Class III for high risk. Software that supports clinical decision‐making typically falls under Class IIa unless incorrect decisions may result in serious harm or death, when it is categorized as Class III. Dermatology apps intended to autonomously diagnose skin cancer may therefore fall into Classes IIb‐III due to the potential consequences of diagnostic errors. For devices in these classes, the MDR requires a clinical evaluation to demonstrate safety and performance, often based on formal clinical investigations, in addition to continuous post‐market surveillance. Notably, only two of the apps were classified as IIa, while it appears that developers from other apps assisting in skin cancer diagnosis may try to minimize their CE marking requirements through the use of disclaimers.

The low number of CE‐marked dermatology apps for AI‐based skin cancer screening identified in this study raises concerns about whether many currently available diagnostic apps comply with MDR requirements. In principle, such apps are expected to obtain CE marking before being marketed under EU regulations. As mentioned before, one potential explanation for their continued availability is the use of disclaimers by developers, asserting that their apps are not intended for diagnostic or medical use. Under the current MDR, the determination of whether a digital health product qualifies as a medical device is primarily based on the intended purpose as defined by the manufacturer [12]. In response, Ludvigson et al. have advocated for an expanded interpretation of “intended purpose” that incorporates not only explicit claims but also inferred intent. The latter includes, for instance, the app's technical features, the nature of the data it acquires, and the type of processing or analysis it performs [13]. This could prevent app developers from circumventing the MDR by merely adding a disclaimer. This issue still represents a “legal grey area”. Another possible explanation for the low number of CE‐marked dermatology apps is insufficient enforcement, as regulatory authorities may lack the capacity and/or the resources to systematically monitor and act on noncompliant apps in European app stores. Dermatology associations can complement regulatory oversight by contributing to the evaluation of app quality and performance.

However, while autonomous mobile apps for skin cancer diagnosis should be classified under MDR Classes IIb‐III from a patient safety perspective, obtaining CE marking for these higher risk categories imposes a substantial financial burden on developers. These costs could discourage innovation by limiting competition and impeding bottom‐up initiatives from individual researchers, smaller organizations, and start‐up companies. When combined with a strict regulatory approach, this may reduce the availability of mobile apps for laypersons that support awareness of potentially malignant skin lesions. The role of the current legal gray area in shaping access to and use of these apps warrants further investigation in real‐world settings.

Scientific Publications on Apps Not Available in App Stores

Many currently available dermatology‐related mobile apps that need clinical validation, such as self‐diagnosing apps, lack supporting scientific evidence. However, our literature review conversely identified numerous publications describing apps that were either never brought to the European market, have since been withdrawn from app stores, or could not be linked to a specific commercial app due to the absence of identifiable naming in the publication. This disconnect between scientific reporting and app availability complicates efforts to screen for the clinical validation and regulatory compliance of apps in real‐world settings. A large proportion of these studies evaluated apps aimed at detecting premalignant lesions and skin cancer [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Additional publications investigated AI‐based apps for general dermatologic diagnosis beyond oncologic indications [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. A smaller number of studies addressed tools designed for remote monitoring and treatment [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Furthermore, two papers examined apps developed for wound management and care [35, 36]. Lastly, studies focused on educational/informative apps were published [37, 38]. These findings illustrate that many apps studied in literature either never reached the European market, have been removed from app stores, or lack sufficient identifiers to be matched to a current commercial product.

Key Priorities for App Development and Implementation

As noted in a closely related position statement of the EADV AI Task Force, smartphone apps have the potential to significantly support patients throughout their dermatology journeys between consultations with dermatologists [6]. Nevertheless, this potential can only be realized if apps meet appropriate standards of clinical robustness, safety, explainability, and usability. Based on the findings of this review, several key areas warrant particular attention in future app development and evaluation efforts, including data privacy, inclusion of diverse skin types, AI transparency, scientific validation, and real‐world applicability.

Data Privacy

The popularity of dermatology‐related mobile apps varies considerably, and understanding user preferences may inform the development of more widely adopted tools. Prior research indicates that secure image storage and communication with healthcare providers are highly valued by users and enhance their confidence in the application's safety and reliability [39]. These findings highlight the relevance of data protection standards, such as compliance with the GDPR. Improving GDPR compliance may improve confidence among potential users and therefore improve the adoption of smartphone apps in dermatology.

Real‐World Validation

Recent work by Brancaccio et al. highlights that the clinical performance of apps including AI functionalities in dermatology may differ substantially between controlled research settings and routine clinical practice [40]. Their review explored the utility of AI technologies across various practical contexts, considering perspectives from consumers, general practitioners, and dermatologists. While certain algorithms have outperformed dermatologists under controlled experimental conditions, their diagnostic accuracy has been shown to be inferior to experts in a real‐world setting or after including independent test data [41, 42, 43]. This discrepancy highlights the critical need for prospective real‐world studies to rigorously evaluate the clinical validity and reliability of AI‐driven dermatologic apps. In our review, 24 apps of the 2024 search have been included in a clinical trial. Only six apps have been subject to a real‐world post deployment study. These findings highlight a significant gap in the real‐world validation of dermatology apps currently available in Europe, underscoring the critical need for such studies to assess their actual clinical effectiveness.

Representation of Different Patient Groups

Adequate representation of diverse patient groups is essential to ensure the safety, equitability, fairness, and reliability of AI‐based mobile health apps for skin cancer detection. Nevertheless, a recent qualitative study among healthcare professionals raised concerns that some apps only work with lighter skin tones, thereby excluding patients with darker skin tones [44]. Gordon et al. additionally noted that many convolutional neural networks are primarily trained on images of lighter skin types, resulting in underrepresentation of Fitzpatrick types V and VI and reduced diagnostic accuracy for these groups [45]. Notably, our own analysis showed that accuracy across diverse groups of patients was limited (Table 4, Table S3). Addressing these gaps is crucial to ensure diagnostic accuracy regardless of skin tone and maintain trust in the expanding market of AI‐assisted self‐diagnosis apps. This lack of diversity also affects other dermatology apps. For example, Dore et al. found limited skin tone representation in educational tools for students and health care practitioners [46].

Adherence to Regulatory and Expert Standards

A key area for improvement in the development of dermatology‐related apps, specifically those employing AI, is adherence to regulatory and expert standards. While regulatory clarity has been improved since the implementation of the MDR in 2021, expert groups have also issued detailed guidance to support responsible development of AI‐assisted dermatology apps. Two of the most comprehensive documents in this area are the CLEAR Derm consensus statement and the EADV AI Task Force position statement, both of which address the full AI lifecycle from development to post‐market oversight [6, 47]. The EADV recommendations are specifically intended for app developers and provide practical guidance on clinical validation, transparency, data representativeness, and post‐deployment monitoring. The CLEAR Derm guidelines, developed by the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) AI Working Group, focus on the evaluation of imaging‐based AI research. Although CLEAR Derm is aimed primarily at peer reviewers, it also serves as a useful reference for app developers seeking to align with current AI development standards in dermatology. Greater adherence to these recommendations may help standardize development practices and improve the clinical robustness of dermatology‐related AI tools.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this review is the comprehensive search strategy employed to identify both dermatology‐related mobile apps available in 44 European countries and corresponding scientific publications. Initially, a web‐scraping method was applied to systematically extract data from both the Apple App Store and Google Play Store, ensuring broad coverage of currently available apps. To complement this, a secondary literature search was conducted using PubMed, allowing identification of scientific articles associated with the apps found in the stores, as well as detection of additional apps not captured by the scraping process. The PubMed search strategy was intentionally broad, incorporating general dermatology‐related app terms, which enabled the inclusion of publications referring to earlier or alternative names of the apps. Furthermore, all included publications were systematically classified by study design, providing insight into the extent and type of scientific evaluation conducted for each app. This revealed that most apps were merely mentioned in the literature, with only a minority being evaluated in clinical validation studies or trials. Lastly, due to the low proportion of apps reporting CE marking or GDPR compliance, evaluating the regulatory integrity of AI‐based skin cancer diagnostic apps was limited. Therefore, the EADV AI Task Force recommendations were employed as an alternative framework to assess the development quality and functional performance of these AI‐driven tools.

A limitation of this study is the low response rate from app developers when contacted to provide information regarding compliance with the EADV AI Task Force recommendations, underlining that some mandatory data such as the number and type of training and test images should be provided before commercialization. Additionally, many of the data elements necessary to assess adherence to these recommendations were not publicly accessible, limiting a comprehensive evaluation. With respect to app costs, only initial download fees were assessed. Potential in‐app purchases were excluded, as such costs were not consistently disclosed prior to installation. Moreover, while CE marking demonstrates adherence to regulatory standards, it does not provide evidence of an app's functional performance, such as its diagnostic accuracy. Evaluating these capabilities for all available apps was beyond the scope of this review. Nevertheless, prior studies have undertaken such assessments, and extending this evaluation to all dermatology apps at the European level could offer valuable insights into their overall quality [48]. Moreover, the Google Play Scraper may be subject to geographic access restrictions, representing a potential technical limitation that could account for failure to detect certain applications. Furthermore, the GDPR compliance assessment may underestimate actual compliance, as it relied primarily on app store descriptions rather than privacy policies or terms of service.

Future Research

The use of large language models (LLMs) in the form of chatbots has recently gained attention in dermatologic research, both for layperson‐facing applications and clinical decision support. These models have the potential to influence various aspects of dermatology, from education and triage to diagnostic assistance [49, 50]. Healthcare professionals have shown growing interest in their potential benefits, including improved accessibility of information and preliminary guidance for laypersons [51]. Initial studies have explored the diagnostic accuracy of chatbots based on LLM in identifying skin cancer and its ability to address layperson questions regarding common conditions such as pediatric atopic dermatitis [52, 53, 54]. As LLMs are continuously updated, their performance and functionality may evolve substantially across versions. A recent comparative study evaluated several LLMs in a general medical context, though dermatology‐specific benchmarking remains limited [55]. Given the broad accessibility and general‐purpose nature of LLMs, laypersons may increasingly turn to these tools for dermatologic information, potentially even more so than to dedicated dermatology apps. Further exploration of their performance and limitations in dermatology‐specific scenarios is therefore warranted. Until there is clear regulation on the use of LLM in health care, there is a responsibility for dermatologists and other health practitioners in using them cautiously.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review provides a comprehensive overview of dermatology‐related mobile apps currently available in the European continent. Most apps are targeted at laypersons or the general public and fall into categories such as teledermatology, dermatologic education, disease‐specific guidance, self‐assessment, and cosmetic dermatology. Our findings highlight substantial gaps in publicly available documentation on regulatory compliance, clinical validation, and transparency. This is reflected by the lack of reported CE marking in the majority of cases. Developers are encouraged to follow established guidance, such as the EADV AI Task Force recommendations, to involve dermatologists and their insights during the development. Furthermore, it is strongly advised to obtain the CE marking as a means of strengthening regulatory compliance, improving transparency, and fostering trust among users and healthcare professionals.
