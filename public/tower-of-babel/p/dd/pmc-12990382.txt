Effects of different low-phosphate formulations on the quality of prepared pork patties under repeated freeze-thaw cycles: Water retention, texture, and sensory properties
Authors: Chengju Zhang, Yi Yang, Ke Chen, Yiyu Liao, Mingfeng Xu, Jiaxin Chen, Liang Liang, Rongrong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the impact of a low-phosphate water-holding system on the quality attributes of pork patties subjected to repeated freeze-thaw (F-T) cycles. The experimental formulations included: a control group (without water-retaining agents), a full-phosphate group (FP), low-phosphate group I and II (LP-I, LP-II). Results demonstrated that LP-II effectively maintained a higher pH value within the system and significantly inhibited moisture migration. After the seven F-T cycles, the thawing loss and cooking loss of LP-II group were reduced by approximately 12% and 15% respectively compared to the control. Concurrently, the LP-II group exhibited better microstructure and protein secondary structures. LP-II demonstrated efficacy in better sensory properties such as appearance and flavour following F-T cycles. Therefore, our findings show that while reducing phosphate usage by 50%, LP-II achieved comparable or even superior performance to the full-phosphate formulation in key indicators such as water retention and texture.

Introduction

Pork patties are a popular convenience meat product worldwide, valued for their convenience, nutritional value and culinary versatility. However, their quality is highly susceptible to repeated freeze-thaw (F-T) cycles, which often occur during frozen storage, transport and retail handling. These cycles promote the formation and recrystallisation of ice crystals, damaging muscle cell structure. Consequently, protein denaturation, fluid loss and texture deterioration can occur, alongside accelerated lipid and protein oxidation. Such changes impair the sensory and nutritional characteristics of the product and can lead to significant economic losses (Liu et al., 2022; Wu, Li, Pan, Kong, & Xia, 2021).

Phosphate additives such as sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP), sodium pyrophosphate, sodium hexametaphosphate, and tetrasodium pyrophosphate are widely used in frozen meat products for their exceptional water-retention. However, excessive phosphate intake has been demonstrated to correlate with health risks including cardiovascular disease and renal impairment, prompting the search for alternatives (Pinton et al., 2022). Current strategies for phosphate reduction are broadly categorized into two groups: those employing processing technologies to modify the intrinsic structure of meat products, and those utilising phosphate-free substitutes as alternatives. Physical processing techniques (e.g., high pressure, ultrasonics, pulsed electric fields) can enhance water retention, yet their industrial application is constrained by high operational costs, safety concerns (Molina, Bohrer, & Mejia, 2023). Phosphorus-free substitutes, including sodium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate, and sodium citrate, fail to provide sufficient ionic strength at low incorporation levels, thus resulting in inadequate dissolution of salt-soluble meat proteins (Zhou et al., 2024). Dietary fibres and proteins primarily immobilise water through physical entrapment rather than by fundamentally enhancing the intrinsic water-holding capacity (WHC) of the myofibrillar proteins (Wang et al., 2025).

The primary mechanisms of traditional compound phosphates include: (1) increasing ionic strength to promote myofibrillar protein solubilisation; (2) adjusting pH to alter protein isoelectric points, thereby improving protein hydration and enhancing electrostatic repulsion; (3) chelating metal ions to reduce protein aggregation and denaturation (Thangavelu, Kerry, Tiwari, & McDonnell, 2019). In compound phosphates, STPP provides extremely high ionic strength, which forms the basis for water retention; sodium pyrophosphate exhibits the strongest alkalinity and rapidly elevates meat pH away from the isoelectric point, thereby opening the protein structure; while sodium hexametaphosphate ensures system stability by chelating metal ions (Balestra & Petracci, 2019). At an equivalent concentration, STPP delivers an ionic strength fifteen times that of sodium chloride, offering a stronger ionic strength. Alkaline amino acids such as L-arginine, L-histidine, and l-lysine possess side chains containing nitrogen atoms with lone pair electrons, enabling them to bind protons in aqueous solution and conferring alkaline properties. Among these, L-arginine is one of the strongest known organic bases and can effectively enhance the WHC of meat proteins while reducing thawing loss (Li, Fan, Gao, & Zhou, 2023). Currently, Li et al. (2022) have explored the effects of the combined use of trehalose, sodium bicarbonate, and chitosan to completely replace phosphates on the quality of frozen tilapia fillets. Su et al. (2025) on the other hand, analysed the cryoprotective mechanisms of lysine and sorbitol during the frozen storage of silver carp surimi. Although the above studies have confirmed the potential of basic amino acids and polyols to replace phosphates in fish products to maintain water retention, their research primarily focused on quality preservation during stable frozen storage. Under harsher repeated F-T conditions, the systematic correlation mechanism from protein structural changes and microstructural evolution to macroscopic quality deterioration still requires further investigation.

We put forward the hypothesis that an optimised formulation containing trace amounts of STPP, L-arginine, and sorbitol may provide pork patties with quality protection equivalent to that of traditional phosphate additives following multiple F-T cycles. Therefore, in this study, STPP, L-arginine, and sorbitol were selected to formulate an efficient, low-phosphate, composite water-retaining agent. Four treatment groups were established: a control group; an FP group (0.4% compound phosphates); an LP-I group (0.2% compound phosphates +0.2% sodium bicarbonate); and an LP-II group (0.2% STPP +0.7% L-arginine +0.6% sorbitol). The effects of these treatments on the water retention, physicochemical properties, oxidative stability, protein structure, and sensory quality of pork patties subjected to 0–7 F-T cycles were systematically evaluated. This study aims to elucidate the protective mechanisms of this composite formulation, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for developing high-performance, low-phosphate additives for meat products.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The pork loin and back fat were sourced from a Walmart supermarket (Chengdu, China). STPP and compound phosphate (food grade, ≥95% purity) were supplied by Xingfa Chemical Group Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). L-arginine (food grade, ≥95% purity) was obtained from Huayang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shijiazhuang, China), and sorbitol (food grade, ≥95% purity) was procured from Huakang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). All chemical reagents used were of at least analytical grade.

Preparation of meat patties

Pork patties were prepared according to the method of Zhang, Li, Kang, and Peng (2023) with slight modifications. Four treatment groups were established as detailed in Table S1, with the LP-II formulation determined via previous optimal mixture design experiments. Visible fat and connective tissue were removed from the lean meat, which was diced and blended with back fat at a 4:1 (w/w) ratio. The mixture was minced twice through a 6-mm plate using a mincer (Model HX-J358, Foshan Haixun Electrical Appliance Co., Ltd., Guangdong, China) to ensure homogeneity. The mince was formed into patties approximately 1.7 cm thick, 7 cm in diameter, and weighing 50 g. The weight of each raw patty was recorded. Patties were separated by silicone paper, vacuum-sealed in bags, and all procedures were conducted at 4 °C. Before the F-T cycles, the patties were equilibrated at 4 °C for 6 h. Each cycle consisted of freezing at −20 °C for 48 h, followed by thawing at 4 °C for 24 h, repeated for a total of 7 cycles. The freezing and thawing processes were carried out in a temperature-controlled freezer (model SC-390, produced by Qingdao Haier Company, China), with temperatures monitored regularly to ensure stability at −20 °C during freezing and 4 °C during thawing. Three independent batches were prepared, with each batch comprising 80 samples (20 per treatment group). Each group of samples underwent analysis of WHC, moisture distribution, fat oxidation, and protein characteristics after 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 F-T cycles; colour and texture were analysed after 0, 3, and 7 F-T cycles.

pH

The pH was determined using the method of Zahid et al. (2020) with slight modifications. Briefly, a 5.0 g sample was homogenised in 50 mL of distilled water using a homogeniser (IKA Ultra-Turrax T-18, Aika Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The pH of the supernatant was then measured using a calibrated digital pH meter (PHSJ-3F, Shanghai Yidian Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Water holding capacity (WHC)

Cooking loss

The thawed patty samples were first weighed and vacuum-sealed in polyethylene bags. All samples were then immersed in a water bath and heated at 75 °C for 15 min. After cooking, the samples were cooled to an internal temperature of 25 °C, and weighed by removing surface moisture. The cooking loss was calculated using the formula:(1)Cooking loss%=W1−W2/W1×100

Where W1 and W2 represent the weight (g) of the samples before and after cooking, respectively.

Thawing loss

Thawing loss was determined according to the method of Xia, Kong, Liu, and Liu (2009). The patty samples were thawed until their core temperature reached 4 °C. Thawing loss was then calculated using the following formula:(2)Thawing loss%=W0−W3/W0×100

where W0 and W3 represent the mass (g) of the samples before freezing and after thawing, respectively.

Moisture distribution

Moisture distribution was assessed following the method of Li, Zhu, Zhou, and Li (2025). with modifications. Samples (5.0 g) were wrapped in plastic film and placed into nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tubes for analysis using a low-field NMR analyser (MesoMR23-040 V-1, Suzhou Niumai Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The instrument was tested under a magnetic field strength of 0.5 T, a proton resonance frequency of 20 MHz, and a constant temperature of 32 °C. The transverse relaxation time (T₂) was measured using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill sequence. T2-weighted images were acquired with a spin-echo sequence under the following parameters: TD = 280, 086; SW = 200 kHz; TW = 2000.00 ms; TE = 0.40 ms; NECH = 3500; NS = 8.

Histological observation (H&E) and lipid distribution (ORO)

Referring to the method of Cao et al. (2022), and with modifications, structural changes and lipid profiles of different pork patties after 0, 3, and 7 F-T cycles were examined. Frozen sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and oleored O (ORO) according to standard procedures. H&E staining, sections fixed with acetone, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, dehydrated, transparent in xylene, and sealed with neutral gum. ORO staining, sectioning fixation, staining with freshly prepared ORO working solution (protected from light), differentiation in 60% isopropyl alcohol, hematoxylin counterstaining, glycerin gelatin sealing. Stained sections are viewed under a microscope and images are taken. All slides were stored in a dark environment at 4 °C prior to imaging.

Protein conformation

Protein extraction

Myofibrillar protein (MP) was extracted following a modified version of the method by Xia et al. (2009). Briefly, 20 g of thawed sample from each treatment group was homogenised in 80 mL of phosphate twice with 80 mL of 0.1 M NaCl solution under identical centrifugation conditions. The suspension was filtered through four layers of cheesecloth to remove connective tissue and subsequently centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 15 min. The final precipitate was collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Circular dichroism (CD)

The secondary structure of MP was analysed by CD spectroscopy following the method of Song et al. (2026). A 0.5 mg/mL MP solution was analysed at 25 °C using a Chirascan spectropolarimeter (Applied Photophysics Ltd., Surrey, UK). Measurements were acquired over a wavelength range of 180–260 nm using a 1.0 mm path length cuvette, with a scan speed of 120 nm/min and a response time of 1.0 s. The spectra were processed using CDNN software (Applied Photophysics Ltd., UK) to quantify secondary structure elements.

Surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of the MP was determined according to the method of Li et al. (2026). Briefly, 200 μL of bromophenol blue (BPB) solution (1.0 mg/mL) was added to 1.0 mL of MP solution (2.0 mg/mL) and mixed thoroughly. After shaking at room temperature for 10 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 ×g and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and diluted 10-fold. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Surface hydrophobicity was expressed as the amount of BPB bound, calculated using the following formula:(3)BPBboundμg=200μg×A0−A/A0

where A0 and A represent the absorbance of the blank and sample, respectively.

Protein oxidation

Protein carbonyl content was determined according to the method of Wang, Li, Pan, Kong, and Xia (2021) with minor modifications. Briefly, 1.0 mL of MP solution (5.0 mg/mL) was mixed with 1.0 mL of (10 mM) 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) and shaken continuously at 4 °C for 1 h in the dark. Then, 1.0 mL of 20% trichloroacetic acid was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 10 min to precipitate the protein. After centrifuging, the precipitate was washed three times with 1.0 mL of ethanol/ethyl acetate solution (1:1, v/v). The pellet was dried and dissolved in 0.8 mL of 6.0 M guanidine hydrochloride. The solution was incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 15 min. Then, the supernatant was measured at 370 nm. The carbonyl content was calculated using a molar absorption coefficient and expressed as carbonyl nmol/mg protein.

Lipid oxidation

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) content was determined according to the method of Fu et al. (2022). Briefly, a 5.0 g minced sample was thoroughly mixed with 50 mL of 7.5% trichloroacetic acid solution containing 0.1% EDTA-2Na. The homogenate was filtered through double-layer quantitative slow-speed filter paper. A 5.0 mL aliquot of the filtrate was combined with 5.0 mL of 0.02 M thiobarbituric acid solution, heated at 90 °C for 40 min. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1900, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). Results were expressed as malondialdehyde mg/kg sample, calculated using a standard curve prepared from 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane.

Colour

Colour measurements were measured using a portable colorimeter (WF32-16MM, Weifu Photoelectric Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) according to the method of Liang, Fan, Gao, Li, and Zhou (2024). The instrument was calibrated with a black and white reference tile (L* = 93.62, a* = −0.13, b* = 3.25), and all measurements were performed under the following settings: D65 light source, 10° standard observer, and 8 mm aperture. Before analysis, samples were equilibrated at 25 °C for 30 min. For each sample, L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) values were recorded at five random locations on the surface, and the mean values of these measurements were calculated.

Texture

Texture profile analysis was performed using a texture analyser (TA-XT plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., UK) equipped a P/36R probe. The cooked samples were cut into 1 cm cubes and positioned centrally on the compression plate. The test involved two consecutive compression cycles with the following parameters: pre-test speed of 3.0 mm/s, test speed of 5.0 mm/s, post-test speed of 5.0 mm/s, 60% compression, automatic trigger mode, and a trigger force of 10 g. Hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, chewiness, and resilience were derived from the resulting curves.

Visual and microstructure analysis

Images of the uncooked samples were first acquired, and then the samples were subsequently baked at 220 °C for 20 min, after which both their external appearance and internal texture were photographed. All images were captured under a fixed light source using a digital camera (XS20, Fujifilm Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) set to an aperture of f/4.4 and an ISO of 6400.

Microstructure characterisation was determined according to the method of Wang et al. (2021), with minor modifications. Briefly, uncooked samples were first fixed by immersion in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (pH 7.0) at 4 °C for 12 h prior to thawing. The samples were then rinsed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, and 90%). This was followed by immersion in chloroform for 1 h, and subsequently in a mixture of anhydrous ethanol/tert-butanol (1:1, v/v), and finally in pure tert-butanol, each for 15 min. The cooked samples were cooled to room temperature and sectioned into 3 mm slices, then fixed using the same protocol. After freeze-drying, all samples were sputter-coated with gold and examined using a low-vacuum tungsten scanning electron microscope (SEM-3200 A, Guoyi Quantum Technology Co., Ltd., Hefei, China) at 10 kV and a magnification of ×200.

Electronic nose analysis

Electronic nose analysis was performed using a PEN3 electronic olfactory system (Airsense Analytics, Germany) according to the method described by Yu et al. (2025). Briefly, 2 g of cooked meat (minced) was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial, which was sealed and then incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for 20 min. The sampling needle of the instrument was inserted into the vial headspace, and measurement was conducted for 120 s. Subsequently, the sensor array was purged with ambient air for 130 s to re-establish the baseline. Data from a stable and representative 3 s interval were used for subsequent analysis.

Electronic tongue analysis

Electronic tongue analysis was carried out using a C-Tongue system (Shanghai Baosheng Technology Co., Ltd., China) following the procedure reported by Wang et al. (2025). For sample preparation, 10 g of chopped cooked meat was homogenised with 100 mL of distilled water for 30 s. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was filtered prior to analysis. Signal acquisition commenced after all six sensors had reached a stable equilibrium. The acquisition time for each sample was set to 120 s, followed by a 30 s rinse cycle in distilled water to clean the sensors. Each formulation from every production batch was analysed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Three batches (replicate groups) of samples were prepared. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Data were presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Treatment groups (Control, FP, LP-I, LP-II) employed a completely randomised design. Variations in techno functional traits were analysed using a linear mixed-effects model. In this model, the number of F-T cycles (0, 1, 3, 5, 7) served as fixed effects, while the three replicate groups constituted random effects. One-way ANOVA assessed pork patty characteristics and F-T indices, with Duncan's multiple range test comparing significant mean differences at the 0.05 significance level. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results and discussion

pH

As shown in Fig. 1a, the type of water-holding agent significantly affected the pH of pork patties during the F-T cycling (P < 0.05). After seven cycles, the pH of all treatment groups exhibited an overall increasing trend. The final pH in the LP-II group was significantly higher than in all other groups, followed by the LP-I and FP groups, whereas the control group maintained the lowest pH and displayed pronounced fluctuations throughout the cycling period. The trend of changes varied considerably depending on the composition of additives. These marked differences in pH were primarily attributable to the inclusion of additives, particularly the combination of L-arginine and STPP in the LP-II treatment. As an effective buffering agent, L-arginine raises the pH of the meat matrix, shifting it away from the isoelectric point of myosin. This increase enhances electrostatic repulsion between myosin molecules, augments the number of bound-water sites, and thereby promotes hydration (Yu et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2022).Fig. 1Effects of different water-retaining agents on the pH (a), cooking loss (b) and thawing loss (c) of pork patties during the F-T cycles. WHC

The WHC of the patties, measured by thawing and cooking loss, showed a progressive deterioration with increasing numbers of F-T cycles. In the control, FP, and LP-I groups, cooking loss (Fig. 1b) initially increased and then decreased with successive cycles, whereas in the LP-II group, a significant rise (P < 0.05) was observed only after the 5th cycle. For thawing loss (Fig. 1c), the control, FP, and LP-I groups exhibited a gradual increase with increasing F-T cycles, while the LP-II group remained stable throughout. Notably, the LP-II treatment consistently showed the lowest values for both parameters across all cycles (P < 0.05). For instance, after the 7th cycle, cooking loss and thawing loss in the LP-II group were approximately 15% and 12% lower, respectively, than those in the control group. Repeated F-T cycling typically promotes ice crystal growth and recrystallisation, which damages ultrastructure and accelerates protein denaturation and aggregation, thereby impairing WHC (Leygonie, Britz, & Hoffman, 2012; Pan et al., 2021). The reduction in cooking loss during the late F-T stage may reflect the depletion of exudates rather than an improvement in WHC. The WHC observed in LP-II patties can be attributed to the action of L-arginine and sorbitol. In contrast, the LP-II formulation likely mitigates these defects. Both L-arginine and sorbitol may form hydrogen bonds with myosin and free water, converting free water into bound water. Concurrently, sorbitol can alter the non-polar environment around myosin, enhancing its thermodynamic stability (Xie et al., 2023). This synergy effectively reduces moisture migration and exudative loss during F-T cycling. These findings align with the report by Su et al. (2025), who noted that a lysine-sorbitol combination improved the gel properties and WHC of frozen silver carp surimi by promoting a denser myofibrillar protein network and maintaining protein integrity.

Moisture mobility and distribution

The distribution and mobility of moisture in pork patties were characterised using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) by measuring transverse (T2) relaxation times (Zheng et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 2a, three distinct water populations were identified: T2b (0–10 ms), T21 (10–300 ms) and T22 (300–1000 ms), corresponding to bound water, immobilised water and free water, respectively (Zhang, Zhao, Chen, Zhang, & Wei, 2019).Fig. 2Comparison diagrams of the T2 relaxation times (a) and the P2 (T2 peak ratio, b), and MRI images (c) of pork patty samples treated with different water-retaining agents under F-T cycles. Different lowercase letters (a-d) indicate significant differences between the (F-T) cycles within the same treatment groups (P < 0.05); Different uppercase letters (A-D) indicate significant differences among treatment groups within the same (F-T) cycle (P < 0.05).Fig. 2

Comparison diagrams of the T2 relaxation times (a) and the P2 (T2 peak ratio, b), and MRI images (c) of pork patty samples treated with different water-retaining agents under F-T cycles. Different lowercase letters (a-d) indicate significant differences between the (F-T) cycles within the same treatment groups (P < 0.05); Different uppercase letters (A-D) indicate significant differences among treatment groups within the same (F-T) cycle (P < 0.05).

As shown in Table S2, increasing F-T cycles had limited influence on T2b and T21 relaxation times but significantly increased T22 times (indicating enhanced water mobility) after the third cycle (P < 0.05). This increase was most pronounced in the control group. In contrast, all treatment groups (FP, LP-I, and LP-II) mitigated this change, with the LP-II group consistently exhibiting the lowest overall T₂ values (representing the overall relaxation time distribution) throughout the F-T process. The proportion of each water population (P₂) is shown in Fig. 2b, represented by the relative area under each peak. After multiple F-T cycles, significant changes in P22 and P21 occurred in the control, FP and LP-I groups (P < 0.05), indicating a conversion of immobilised water to free water. This conversion, however, was not significant in the LP-II group. The proportion of bound water (P2b) showed minor fluctuations around 2.5% in the three treatment groups containing water-retaining agents, whereas it increased significantly in the control group.

Collectively, the LF-NMR data indicate that the LP-II formulation most effectively restricted water migration and maintained water in a less mobile state during F-T cycling. The increased T22 times and the shift from P21 to P22 observed in the other groups can be attributed to physical damage caused by ice-crystal formation and growth, which impairs myocyte membranes and myofibrillar structure, thereby releasing confined water into a free state (Cheng et al., 2019). This trend is consistent with the thawing-loss results, in which extracellular ice formation and weakened protein-water interactions jointly altered water distribution (Zhang, Li, Diao, Kong, & Xia, 2017). Notably, the marked rise in P2b in the control group likely reflects an elevated relative proportion due to an overall reduction in total WHC and sample mass, rather than an absolute increase in bound-water content, as the latter is generally considered insensitive to F-T-induced changes (Zhang et al., 2023). The superior performance of LP-II resembles the reported synergy between konjac glucomannan and STPP (Jung et al., 2022), suggesting that the combination of L-arginine and sorbitol helps to immobilise water within the protein matrix, effectively reducing its conversion to a highly mobile state.

Magnetic resonance imaging was employed to monitor water migration throughout the F-T cycles (Fig. 2c). In all groups, high-signal (red) areas expanded from the interior toward the surface as cycling proceeded, reflecting water redistribution resulting from structural damage. While the control, FP and LP-I groups showed a progressive increase in the extent and intensity of these high-signal regions, the LP-II group displayed a distinct non-linear pattern: from cycles 1 to 3, its signal distribution remained similar to that of the other groups; however, a marked rise in surface water signal occurred at cycle 5, followed by a reduction at cycle 7, ultimately yielding the smallest high-signal area among all groups. This non-linear response suggests a dynamic competition between accumulating F-T damage and the ongoing stabilising effect of the water-retaining agent in LP-II, which helped to maintain the protein-water matrix and restrict water movement. Consequently, despite intermediate fluctuations, the LP-II treatment proved most effective in limiting excessive free-water accumulation.

Histological analysis of pork patties

The morphology of frozen pork patty tissues was analysed to better understand the changes in patty quality under F-T cycles. As shown in Fig. 3, H&E staining results indicated that F-T cycles gradually deteriorated the tissue structure of the patties. At F-T-0, the structure of each group was relatively intact and the structural continuity was good. By F-T-3, the control group showed widened inter fibre gaps and irregular vacuoles traces of ice crystal formation. These vacuoles are usually considered the structural traces of ice crystal formation and melting during F-T, suggesting tissue damage caused by mechanical compression and water migration (Cao et al., 2022). At F-T-7, control vacuoles enlarged with decreased tissue continuity. This deterioration compromises WHC: disrupted myofibrillar networks release immobilised water into extracellular space, converting it to free water lost during thawing/cooking (Pan et al., 2021). Thus, the gaps are directly related to thawing/cooking losses. In contrast, LP-II retains most of the continuous morphology with fewer cavities.Fig. 3Histological observation of pork patties subjected to repeated F-T cycles using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Oil Red O (ORO) staining. ORO staining showed that with increasing F-T cycles, lipid droplets in the control group were more prone to aggregation and uneven distribution, whereas LP-II exhibited relatively uniform lipid distribution with low levels of aggregation. F-T damage creates physical space for droplet migration, while membrane damage releases pro-oxidants accelerating oxidation (Li et al., 2022). Thus, LP-II mitigates damage by preserving microstructural integrity, preventing lipid migration and oxidation. Overall, histological observations structurally support the protective effect of LP-II under repeated F-T conditions.

CD spectroscopy

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra (Fig. 4a-d) revealed that F-T cycling disrupted the α-helical structure of myofibrillar proteins, as evidenced by the attenuation of the negative peaks at 208 and 222 nm and the deepening of the absorption band near 200 nm. This structural disruption was most severe in the control group. All treatments mitigated these changes, with LP-I and LP-II showing the best preservation of the 222 nm peak. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant shift from α-helix to β-sheet and random coil conformations with increasing cycles (Fig. 4e). The magnitude of change was greatest in the control. The F-T induced transition from ordered α-helix to more disordered conformations is a primary cause of functional loss and quality deterioration in meat proteins (Ali et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026). Our results demonstrate that the incorporation of water-retaining agents, particularly LP-II, effectively counteracts this process. The superior efficacy of LP-II in maintaining a near-native secondary structure provides a molecular-level explanation for its outstanding performance in mitigating water migration, discolouration, and textural deterioration, as it directly preserves the protein-water interactions and structural integrity crucial for quality.

Surface hydrophobicity

As shown in Fig. 4f, the surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar proteins in pork patties increased progressively with successive F-T cycles. This increase was most pronounced in the control group (P < 0.05). All treatments (FP, LP-I, LP-II) mitigated the rise, with the LP-II group showing the smallest overall increase (P < 0.05). During the early cycles (0–3), the rate of change increased gradually, but accelerated in the later stages (3–7), indicating the accumulation of damage. The rise in surface hydrophobicity reflects the unfolding of the tertiary protein structure and exposure of hydrophobic residues, resulting from ice-crystal recrystallisation and the associated cumulative oxidative stress (Zhu, Zhu, Shi, & Fan, 2025). This trend is consistent with the concurrent loss of ordered secondary structure and the increase in protein oxidation markers.

The mitigating effects of the three treatment groups supports their cryoprotective potential. The divergent trajectories of FP/LP-I and LP-II after mid-cycle stages suggest that phosphates and bicarbonates offer effective but ultimately limited protection under prolonged stress, in line with the findings of Li, Zou, Kang, and Ma (2022). In contrast, the effect of LP-II may be attributed to L-arginine, which potentially interacts with aromatic amino acid residues on the protein surface through cation-π interactions and salt-bridge formation, thereby protecting hydrophobic regions. (Shukla & Trout, 2010), while sorbitol stabilizes the hydrated microenvironment (Xiang et al., 2025), jointly reducing the exposure of hydrophobic residues induced by ice crystals.”

Lipid and protein oxidation

TBARS

As shown in Fig. S1a, TBARS values increased significantly in all groups throughout the progressive F-T cycles (P < 0.05). The control group exhibited the most pronounced rise, increasing from an initial 0.11 mg MDA/kg to approximately 0.37 mg MDA/kg after 7 cycles. Compared to the control, all treatments (FP, LP-I, LP-II) effectively suppressed lipid oxidation. Notably, from the third cycle onwards, the LP-I and LP-II groups showed significantly lower TBARS values than both the FP and control groups (P < 0.05). The acceleration of lipid oxidation during F-T cycling is well-established, being attributed to the release of pro-oxidants (e.g., heme iron, enzymes) from cellular compartments damaged by ice crystals (Leygonie et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2019). Oxidation proceeded despite vacuum packaging, likely due to residual oxygen and endogenous enzymatic activity (Domínguez et al., 2019). The superior inhibitory effect observed for LP-I and LP-II indicates enhanced antioxidant capacity. For LP-I, the pH elevation induced by sodium bicarbonate may have inhibited iron-mediated oxidation (Li et al., 2022b).

Protein oxidation

Protein oxidation, assessed via carbonyl content, showed a similar increasing trend to lipid oxidation (Fig. S1b). The carbonyl content in the control group rose significantly (P < 0.05) from 0.55 nmol/mg at cycle 0 to 3.04 nmol/mg by cycle 7. The progressive formation of protein carbonyls confirms cumulative oxidative damage to proteins, consistent with previous studies (Al-Dalali, Li, & Xu, 2022; Bao, Ertbjerg, Estévez, Yuan, & Gao, 2021). Notably, the LP-II group had the lowest carbonyl content after the first F-T cycle. However, as the F-T cycles progressed, the protective effect provided by the FP group became stronger, and by the seventh cycle, it had the lowest carbonyl content (P < 0.05). The LP-I group maintained higher carbonyl levels similar to LP-II in the later stages.

These results indicate that although LP-I and LP-II can effectively alleviate quality deterioration caused by F-T, such as water retention and microstructural integrity, their long-term inhibitory effect on cumulative protein oxidation is limited under severe repeated F-T conditions. This may be attributed to the fact that LP-I and LP-II primarily improve WHC and matrix formation by increasing the system pH and promoting the solubilisation/functionalisation of salt-soluble proteins (Cao et al., 2022c). During repeated F-T cycles, enhanced solubilisation may increase the exposure of reactive amino acid side chains (Chen et al., 2023), making proteins more susceptible to oxidative modification. By contrast, conventional blended phosphates may provide more sustained antioxidative protection in the later stages of repeated F-T, which may be related to their stronger buffering and ionic-strength effects, as well as their ability to chelate/inactivate pro-oxidative factors such as metal ions and haem-associated catalysts (Wu, Park, & Richards, 2022).

Visual and microstructure analysis

The visual impact of repeated F-T cycles on raw and cooked pork patties is presented in Fig. 5a. Initially, all patties displayed a uniform, bright-red and moist appearance. After seven cycles, raw patties exhibited noticeable surface dehydration and colour fading. The efficacy of the water-retaining agents was particularly evident in the cooked patties: the control group released substantial cooking exudate, forming a visible peripheral ring, which indicated severely impaired the WHC. In contrast, all groups (FP, LP-I and LP-II) retained internal moisture more effectively, with the LP-II group showing the least exudate and the fullest appearance.Fig. 5The effects of different water-retaining agents on the appearance (a) and microstructure (b) of raw and cooked pork patties after the 0th and 7th F-T cycles.Fig. 5

The effects of different water-retaining agents on the appearance (a) and microstructure (b) of raw and cooked pork patties after the 0th and 7th F-T cycles.

As shown in Fig. 5b, uncycled samples presented a continuous porous network. Following seven F-T cycles, the myofibrillar network in the control group became distinctly loosened, consistent with mechanical damage from ice-crystal growth (Liang et al., 2024). By comparison, the FP and LP-II groups maintained better structural integrity, exhibiting narrower pores and more tightly arranged fibres. Cooking further exacerbated microstructural damage. Heating induced protein denaturation and gelation, while the previously weakened myofibrillar network contracted, enlarging and stabilising pre-existing pores and cracks. This led to the formation of larger, irregular cavities accompanied by structural fragmentation and clear cracking. Although the FP and LP-I groups showed moderate disruption, their networks remained more continuous than those of the control. Most notably, the LP-II group displayed the best-preserved microstructure, retaining a relatively continuous and dense network with small, uniform pores that most closely resembled the intact structure of the uncycled samples.

Odour profile

The volatile odour profile of pork patties was analysed using an electronic nose, with results presented in Fig. S2a. All samples elicited responses from key meat-odour sensors, including W1W (sulphides), W5S (nitrogen oxides), W2W (aromatic compounds and organic sulphides), W1S (short-chain alkanes) and W2S (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones). Compared with non-cycled samples, the response values of sensors W1W, W2W and W5S decreased significantly in all groups after seven F-T cycles, indicating that repeated F-T generally suppressed the formation or release of sulphides, nitrogen oxides, and alcohol/aldehyde/ketone flavour compounds. Among non-cycled samples, the FP and LP-II groups showed similar response values, both higher than those of the control and LP-I groups, suggesting a richer initial volatile profile. Notably, after F-T cycling the response of sensor W1S increased in the LP-I and LP-II groups, implying that these formulations may promote the generation or retention of certain methyl-type volatile components.

As shown in Fig. S2b, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together explained 85% of the total variance (PC1: 69.4%; PC2: 16.6%). The score plot revealed a clear separation along PC1 between non-cycled samples and those subjected to seven F-T cycles, confirming that F-T cycling significantly altered the overall composition of volatile compounds. Loading analysis indicated that sensors W1S, W2S, W3S and W6S were located on the negative side of PC1, whereas sensors W1C, W5C, W3C, W5S and W1W contributed to the positive side. All non-cycled groups and the control group after seven cycles clustered in the positive PC1 region, associated with the latter sensor set, reflecting an odour profile more closely linked to traditional meat volatiles. In contrast, the FP, LP-I and LP-II groups after seven cycles were grouped on the negative side of PC1, their odour profiles being driven more strongly by sensors such as W1S and W6S. Furthermore, along PC2, the cycled LP-II group separated clearly from the other groups, suggesting that its specific formulation imparted a distinct influence on the odour characteristics of freeze-thawed patties.

Flavour profile

The flavour profile of cooked pork patties was evaluated using an electronic tongue, which assessed umami, richness, saltiness, bitterness, astringency and their corresponding aftertastes. As shown in the Fig. S2c, responses to saltiness and bitter aftertaste (Aftertaste-B) were similar across all groups and remained stable even after F-T cycling. In contrast, seven F-T cycles led to a reduction in signals for umami, richness and astringent aftertaste (Aftertaste-A), whereas bitterness and astringency signals increased. Among all cycled groups, the LP-II treatment exhibited relatively stronger signals for umami and richness.

Principal component analysis performed on the electronic-tongue data effectively discriminated the taste characteristics of the samples (Fig. S2d). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 80.7% of the total variance (63.8% and 17.9%, respectively). Non-cycled samples clustered on the positive side of PC1, which correlated positively with taste attributes such as umami, richness, saltiness and related aftertastes. After seven F-T cycles, all groups shifted toward the negative side of PC1, a region associated with bitterness and astringency. Furthermore, the LP-II group subjected to seven cycles showed separation along the positive direction of PC2, further distinguishing its flavour profile from those of the other groups.

Schematic mechanism

Based on the experimental results, we propose the possible following mechanisms for how different water-retaining agents may influence the quality attributes of pork patties during F-T cycling. A schematic summary of these proposed effects is presented in Fig. 6. Repeated F-T cycles may cause severe microstructural damage in muscle tissue and formation of water-migration channels. These changes are thought to result in juice loss and texture deterioration. The control sample exhibited the most pronounced damage. The results indicated that proteins may have undergone severe denaturation loss, resulting in the exposure of hydrophobic groups and subsequent aggregation through hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds. This may form an unstable, loosely structured network with poor WHC. In contrast, the LP-II group seems to follow the typical phosphate-mediated protective mechanism. The STPP in the LP-II group promotes the solubilisation of salt-soluble proteins by providing higher ionic strength, while arginine increases the electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules by maintaining an alkaline pH environment. Under the combined action of the two, a relatively strong water-retaining network structure may be formed. On this basis, sorbitol binds water molecules through hydrogen bonds, further inhibiting ice crystal growth. Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the low-phosphate formulation LP-II (0.2% STPP, 0.7% L-arginine, 0.6% sorbitol) effectively maintains the quality of pork patties during repeated F-T cycles. The effects of its components have reached levels comparable to traditional phosphate mixtures in some key indicators, such as water retention, textural properties, and core flavour characteristics. However, with an increasing number of F-T cycles, all samples exhibited significant protein oxidation, although the carbonyl content in the FP group was lower than that in the LP-II group. This suggests that under extreme F-T conditions, samples in the LP-II group may face a greater risk of severe protein oxidative deterioration. Therefore, even when using advanced protective formulations, minimising the frequency of F-T cycles throughout production, distribution, and storage remains essential. Future work should focus on optimising LP-II to further enhance oxidative stability of pork patties while maintaining its low-phosphate advantage. Overall, the LP-II formulation offers a promising strategy for reducing phosphate reliance while retaining the key quality attributes of frozen meat products. These findings provide theoretical insight for developing effective low-phosphate alternatives and underscore the importance of controlling F-T damage in maintaining meat product quality.
