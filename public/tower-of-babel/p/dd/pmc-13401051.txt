Robust and generalizable CNV detection for single-cell sequencing assays
Authors: Travis W Moore, Hisham Mohammed, Andrew C Adey, Galip Gürkan Yardımcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopy number variations (CNVs) are genomic structural variants that are strongly linked to cancer progression and genetic disorders. CNVs can be highly heterogeneous at population and tissue scale; thus, single-cell resolution detection holds great promise for studying clonal evolution and CNV-driven changes. Despite advanced sc-RNA-seq CNV detection methods, accurate methods for epigenomic single-cell modalities lag behind. We developed RIDDLER; a robust, unsupervised method that uses outlier-aware statistical modeling to detect CNVs across multiple single-cell modalities and assays. RIDDLER utilizes a robust regression framework to model the expected distribution of reads genome-wide by accounting for assay-specific biases, identifying CNVs as outliers from that distribution. This versatile framing allows deployment of RIDDLER in multiple modalities with appropriate bias features. We demonstrate the accuracy of RIDDLER in calling single-cell CNVs and dissecting clonal heterogeneity in sc-ATAC-seq and sc-methylation. RIDDLER is more accurate and more robust to data sparsity than competing methods. We illustrate useful applications of RIDDLER for dissection of clonal structure, identification of subclonal accessibility peaks, and multimodal integration from CNV structure. RIDDLER stands out as a scalable, generalizable multi-modal method for accurate CNV detection, empowering studies aiming to link CNV dynamics to epigenetic alterations within the same cell.

Introduction

Copy number variations (CNVs) correspond to the phenomenon where a particular region of a genome has fewer or more than two copies, in the case of a diploid genome. CNVs can dramatically vary in size, ranging from entire chromosomes to multiple kilobases [1]. CNVs can change gene dosage due to gain or loss of copies [2, 3], or have direct or indirect effects on gene regulation through disruption of non-coding regulatory regions [4, 5]. There are a variety of developmental disorders and diseases that have been linked to CNVs [6–8], and due to their potential for impacting gene regulation, they may alter the state of healthy cell populations in a subtle and heterogeneous manner. The prevalence and the heterogeneity of CNVs in cancerous cells have been deeply documented and are thought to play a critical role in cancer development and progression in many cancer types [9–15]. Thus, fast and accurate detection of CNVs is valuable for researchers studying cancer, development disorders, and links between gene regulation and genomic structural variations.

Contemporary approaches for CNV detection heavily utilize next-generation sequencing datasets, where CNV locations can manifest as clear outliers in signal coverage. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and exome sequencing naturally lend themselves to detection of CNVs through uniform coverage of the genome or exome. Additionally, bulk tissue RNA-seq [16–18] and Hi-C [19–21] have been utilized for CNV detection. It is challenging to identify heterogeneous CNV events from bulk genomics assays that average the signal across different single cells; however, algorithms that can identify CNVs from single-cell genomics datasets can solve this problem, enabling the study of clonal heterogeneity in cancerous cell populations [22–25], where “clones” define populations of cells with distinct signatures of inherited genetic structural variations. There are multiple algorithms to detect CNVs at single-cell resolution from sc-RNA-seq, such as inferCNV [26], HoneyBadger [27], CopyKAT [24], and SCEVAN [28]. sc-RNA-seq datasets have been utilized by many research groups and consortia to profile the transcriptome of many different heterogeneous cell populations, tissues, and even whole biological systems, making them a great resource for CNV detection [24, 25, 29]. However, sc-RNA-seq-based CNV callers are relatively challenged by the uneven coverage of coding sequences along the genome, and may require reference diploid or healthy cell populations to detect CNVs.

In this study, we present Robust Inference of Duplications and Deletions using LinEar Regression (RIDDLER): a novel CNV calling algorithm that utilizes multiple single-cell assays that measure the genome-wide state of chromatin and epigenome. Our algorithm utilizes robust statistics and a generalized linear model (GLM) to detect CNV gains and losses in an unsupervised fashion without the need for diploid reference cells. During the development of RIDDLER, a specialized version was deployed for sc-WGS to distinguish between diploid and aneuploid cells from sc-WGS data [30]. Here, we present RIDDLER as a comprehensive and multimodal method to identify genome-wide CNV landscape from sparse single-cell epigenomics assays, sc-ATAC-seq and sc-methylation. To achieve this generalization, we engineered modality-specific covariates, coupled with window and cell smoothing approaches to eliminate false discoveries. In sc-ATAC-seq, we thoroughly benchmarked our approach against recently published methods Copy-scAT [31] and EpiAneufinder [32] on public sc-ATAC-seq data from cancer cell lines; our approach consistently achieved a better balance between comprehensive detection of true CNV events and avoidance of false positives, and was able to detect subclonal CNV signatures and populations that better matched gold standard CNVs. Furthermore, RIDDLER is more robust to data sparsity than competing methods in sc-ATAC-seq, maintaining its ability to accurately call CNVs at decreasing levels of cell coverage. In multiomic data, RIDDLER has better agreement and integration with sc-RNA-derived CNVs, making it a better choice for multiomic and integrative analysis. For multimodal single-cell multiomics data, we present a novel CNV-based cell alignment strategy using RIDDLER’s highly accurate CNV calls, which can be expanded to different modality combinations. We further establish the flexibility and the accuracy of RIDDLER by deploying it on patient-derived sc-methylation data that results in accurate CNV detection and subclone identification. Together, these results establish the superior performance and versatility of our generalizable CNV detection algorithm for robust detection of CNVs at single-cell resolution in genomic assays, which can be broadly applied to existing and future sc-ATAC-seq, sc-methylation datasets to accurately dissect CNV dynamics in heteregeneous cell populations.

Materials and methods

RIDDLER CNV identification pipeline

Our RIDDLER pipeline for identifying CNVs involves four steps: binning and normalization, dropout filtering, regression model fitting, and copy calling. We start by binning the aligned sequencing reads into non-overlapping windows of uniform length across the genome for each single cell. To account for differences in read depth, each cell in a sample is normalized by its trimmed mean. Using the binned number of reads for all windows of a cell, we trim the top and bottom 15th percentile of values to remove outliers and compute the average of the remaining windows. All windows in the cell are divided by this trimmed mean value, and then multiplied by 100 and rounded to the nearest whole number. We perform this operation on each cell individually to normalize the coverage differences between cells.

Due to the sparse nature of the single-cell data, it is often difficult to determine whether a window in a cell with zero aligned reads is due to technical errors or a true biological loss of signal (such as a chromosome deletion). We use a probabilistic estimation to distinguish between zero values that are likely due to biological events and zeroes that are likely due to technical dropout. Intuitively, when zero values occur in typically high coverage windows for cells with many reads, we have a higher confidence that they are due to true biological events. Conversely, when they occur at low coverage sites in cells with few total reads, they are more likely due to sparsity. Genomic bias features

To model expected signal for a given assay for each window across the genome, we use features that reflect the properties of the target assay. GC content and the mappability of a given genomic interval window have well described universal effects on amplification of sequencing reads originating from that window and whether sequencing reads can align against the window, respectively [35, 36]. Thus, existing CNV algorithms for multiple modalities perform explicit correction for variations of GC content and mappability in order to account for their associated signal bias [31, 32, 37]. For both sc-ATAC-seq and sc-methylation, we use the genomic features of GC content, mappability, albeit we use different features to model mappability. For ATAC-seq, another highly relevant feature is peak coverage, calculated for every window; since ATAC-seq yields genome wide coverage that is higher at regions with greater chromatin accessibility.

To obtain GC content and mappability features, we used the bedtools nuc command for the given window resolution for GC content for a given window size. Mappability scores for a window were obtained from Umap [36] 50-kmer output. The resulting bigwig file was averaged over bin windows using the function bigWigAverageOverBed from kentUtils (https://www.encodeproject.org/software/bigwigaverageoverbed/).

Since methylation assays use a reduced alphabet, we used appropriate bisulfite mappability scores from Bismap [36] 50-kmer output, averaged over bin window size. Bismap specifically accounts for primary strand C \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\rightarrow$\end{document} T and compliment strand G \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\rightarrow$\end{document} A conversions that arise in bisulfite-treated genomes. All of these features were calculated for the hg38 genome assembly for all experiments, except for the sc-Trio-seq data, which uses hg19 assembly.

Peak coverage per window was computed with respect to an input set of cell type peaks, where each cell type has their own associated set of peak coordinates. For each window and a given cell type peak set, the peak coverage of the window for that cell type peak set is the number of basepairs that overlap with any peak in the given peak set divided by the number of base pairs in that window. Thus, this feature serves a proxy for the total accessible chromatin present in a given window, controlling the uneven coverage of ATAC signal across the genome. For each peak set, RIDDLER calculates the correlation of all peak features with the total reads across all cells at each window location, keeping only peak features with high correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $> $\end{document}0.5) to use as covariates. While we found this peak set filtering resulted in little change to CNV predictions, it simplifies model training and interpretation. RIDDLER can either utilize peaks called from the dataset that is input to the model, or a general purpose multi-tissue peak reference set of DNase I hypersensitive sites to avoid the peak calling operation [38]. In all cell line experiments, we used the full set of DNase I locations for hg38, downloaded from https://zenodo.org/record/3838751 and separated by tissue types (16 in total). This general purpose peak set works well for datasets with relatively few cell types. For the CD34+ positive data, we called peaks on each cell type using MACS2 (https://github.com/macs3-project/MACS/tree/macs_v2).

Comparisons to CNVs identified by other algorithms

To benchmark RIDDLER against competing methods, we evaluated their agreement to CNV calls from a separate modality, which we treat as a reference for comparison. We grouped CNV calls into the categories of gain, normal, and loss to simplify this comparison, and because not all algorithms can distinguish higher copy numbers or homozygous versus heterozygous loss. We report F1-score and recall for gain and loss events in each cell, measuring the agreement between the calls in each compared algorithm to the reference values. When calculating F1-score, we treat gain and loss events that match the reference as true positives, gains and losses that do not match the reference as false positives, and diploid regions that do not match the reference as false negatives.

Single-cell CNVs were evaluated against the consensus CNV profile from reference algorithms. Consensus calls were calculated across each window as all CNVs that appear in at least 35% of cells. For the 10x cell lines, which show no subclonal structure in the reference RNA modality, we computed a consensus over all the cells from each cell line and compared single-cell ATAC CNVs to the consensus RNA cell line profiles. In the SNU601 data, which presents subclones with distinct CNV profiles in the reference WGS modality, we clustered both modalities and obtained a matching between clusters based on maximum F1-score. Single-cell ATAC CNVs were then compared to the consensus profile of their matched WGS cluster.

In our sc-Trio-seq2 experiments on colorectal cancer samples, we compare RIDDLER directly to HMMcopy, as the paired RNA lacks sufficient coverage to be treated as a reference. We additionally compared bulk CNV profiles to published TCGA colorectal cancer samples [39], obtained from https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/docs/publications/coadread_2012/. To match the comparison made in the original paper [40], we filtered TCGA samples with >8.24 mutations per million, averaged fold changes for reported CNV regions across windows, and used average fold change cutoffs of 1.5 or less for loss and 2.5 or more for gain. We then computed the frequency of gains and losses across these samples for each genomic window.

Single-cell integration of ATAC and RNA CNVs

For integration of the MCF7 and T47D cell lines between ATAC and RNA called CNVs, we used the R function mnnCorrect in the package “batchelor,” which implements mutual nearest neighbor (MNN) batch correction [41]. MNN identifies anchor cells between the modalities that have common nearest neighbors, and uses them to compute locally weighted linear mappings from one modality to the other. We adjusted the outputs of all CNV algorithms to the values of −1,0,1, with −1 representing loss, 0 for normal, and 1 for gains. We used the integration procedure to correct the values from each ATAC-based CNV calling algorithm to align with the CNV call made by inferCNV method on RNA. Because these CNV values were in the set −1,0,1, we did not use the cosine normalization for inputs or outputs in mnnCorrect, and we did not perform variance adjustment. We set the number of nearest neighbors argument to 100 for all algorithms. All other parameters for the function were kept at default values.

To evaluate the agreement between the MNN corrected ATAC CNVs and RNA CNVs, we cluster the ATAC values, label the clusters by cell type, and compare the agreement between cluster labels and true cell types. Clustering was performed using kmeans with the number of clusters k set to 2. We seeded the cluster centers with the mean values of RNA CNV calls for each cell type, and used those seeds to label the cell type of each cluster. We evaluated the matching by comparing the seeded cluster cell type of each ATAC cell to their true cell type.

Phylogenetic tree generation

As part of evaluating the CNV calls for patient CRC01 of the sc-Trio-seq2 data, we constructed a phylogenetic tree of clones using a custom algorithm. Clones were identified through hierarchical clustering (Ward algorithm), selecting the number of clusters as the highest value that kept all non-cancer labeled cells contained in a single cluster. Average CNV profiles for each cluster were computed as detailed above, with this consensus profile defining the CNV state for the node in the tree representing that cluster.

For tree construction, our algorithm is designed with the assumption that CNV events (gains and losses) should only be acquired or become more extreme (defined as being further away from diploid) as the tree is traversed from root to leaf. Put another way, each node in the tree should inherit the CNVs from its parent, along with adding new gains or losses, or making inherited gains or losses more extreme. We start with a set of unconnect nodes representing each cluster, with the node that contains the diploid cells set aside to become the root node of the tree. The algorithm first identifies nodes that are direct parents of other nodes, by counting non-inherited events. Non-inherited events between node A (potential parent) and node B (potential child) are defined as CNV events in A that are more extreme than the copy numbers in B, i.e. events in A that B did not inherit. If the number of non-inherited events from A to B is <5% of the number of genomic windows, then we say A is a parent to B, and connect node A to node B. The 5% threshold allows for some error due to noise in the data. The algorithm adds parent connections within the threshold greedily, prioritizing the lowest number of non-inherited events and only allowing one parent per node.

Once all parent connections within the threshold are identified, the algorithm iteratively identifies the remaining two nodes without parents that have the fewest non-inherited events between them (in either direction). These nodes are connected as children of an intermediate node, with the intermediate node’s CNV profile defined as the intersection of the least extreme CNV events in the child nodes. This intermediate node is added to the pool of nodes without parents. The algorithm continues until only one node remains, which is connected as a child of the root normal node. Our approach is most similar to a weighted version of traditional distance-based phylogenetics [42], where the weights of evolutions that remove existing CNV events are very high.

This defines a P-value for the difference of peak \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} in cluster \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k$\end{document} versus all other clusters. Peaks with FDR corrected P-values of 0.05 or less and fold-change differences of at least 1.5 were labeled as DA for a cluster.

Gene activity scores were computed using the GeneActivity function of the Signac R package [43], with the peak matrix outlined above as input. Gene activity scores were normalized by total peak reads per cell. Significant subclone differences were identified via a Wilcoxon rank-sum test per gene, testing the one-versus-all difference for each clone. P-values were corrected using FDR across all experiments, and only genes with corrected P-values of 0.05 or less in at least one clone were considered for downstream evaluation. For visualization of gene activity values of genes of interest, we reduced the RIDDLER-derived CNV matrix of SNU601 data to two dimensions using the UMAP algorithm [44] using default parameters.

CNV detection from single-cell WGS and RNA-seq data

Aneufinder was used for CNV detection in scWGS. As parameter choices, we used the “edivisive” model, turned on GC correction, removed duplicate reads, set hotspot P-val threshold to 0.05, and used the default hg38 blacklisted regions. Subclones were identified via Louvain clustering of the CNV outputs from the software.

CNV detection in scRNA-seq was performed using InferCNV. We used the RNA portion of the 10x PBMC data as the reference population, and used the suggested 10x genomics cutoff value of 0.1 when running the software. We performed additional post processing of the output to identify gains and losses at genomic windows for each cell by aggregating the gene level outputs that overlapped with each window. Any window that contained at least 30% gains or losses across overlapping genes was labeled as a gain or loss, respectively.

Data processing

To create the dataset of CD34+ PBMS cells, single-cell ATAC-seq data of healthy peripheral blood and bone marrow mononuclear cells from Granja et al. [45] were re-aligned to hg38. The original BAM files aligned to hg19 for the three CD34+ samples were obtained from NCBI’s cloud data delivery service (SRP226885) and were used to regenerate FASTQ files using bamtofastq from cellranger-atac (version 2.1.0). Alignment to GRCh38 (2020-A-2.0.0) was performed with cellranger-atac count for each sample separately. Downstream processing was performed using ArchR (version 1.0.2). Cells with a TSS enrichment score below 10 and fewer than 1700 unique fragments were removed. Doublets were inferred with addDoubletScores and removed with filterDoublets using default parameters. Cells were labeled with cell type annotations from Granja et al. when available, while barcodes that passed these quality control filters and not those of the original publication were assigned NA values.

Raw fastq files for scNanoATAC were aligned to hg38 following the protocols outlined on the scNanoATAC github page. Bedtools bamtobed was used to convert aligned bam files into barcoded fragment files. These fragment files were then passed into the standard RIDDLER processing pipeline for ATAC-seq data.

In working with sciMETv2 human brain data, we noticed that cells with lower total reads had a high variance of coverage throughout the genome, indicating a higher presence of technical noise. To remove this coverage variance, we filtered cells for a total read count of 5 million or more, resulting in much more stable and consistent genome coverage.

Additional software used

ATAC peaks were called using the MACS2 algorithm (https://github.com/macs3-project/MACS/tree/macs_v2).

Heatmaps were generated using ComplexHeatmap [46].

Louvain clustering was performed using Rphenograph [47], with parameter k=50 for all datasets.

Gene set enrichment analysis of CRC01 was performed using the R package fgsea [48], using the two-sided enrichment option. As input, we used the combined difference in copy number values of clone 1 versus clone 9 and clone 1 versus clone 10, for genes located in chromosomes 15 and 17, respectively.

Results

Outlier-aware CNV detector for single-cell sequencing using robust statistics

RIDDLER detects CNVs by fitting a robust linear model to single-cell fragment counts for outlier detection. For each cell, we sort the fragments into a cell by window matrix, which is then normalized per-cell for read depth (Fig. 1a). These windows cover the entire genome in non-overlapping fashion and their sizes can be adjusted based on the average read depth; deeply sequenced experiments can support smaller windows for higher resolution calls. This matrix passes through a dropout filter to identify when missing signal windows are due to random technical dropout, as opposed to true genomic loss. After removing dropout windows, a Poisson robust GLM [33] is fit to all windows and cells simultaneously, using genomic features indicating the assay bias in each window as covariates. The residuals from this model are then smoothed, which removes spurious and isolated calls, using a sliding window test for statistically significant outliers. CNVs that are deemed statistically significant are then assigned a copy number based on the magnitude of their difference from the expected distribution. Figure 1b shows a simplified conceptual visualization of how the process of fitting residuals to the expected signal and then smoothing across cell windows using a sliding window mean (window size 5) can elucidate CNV patterns from otherwise noisy data, as we see with the spurious high residual windows in chromosome 4 removed while the loss regions become more consistent. Supplementary Fig. S2 additionally shows the benefits of performing window smoothing over multiple similar cells.

A robust Poisson regression model is fit across all cells along with genomic features for each window. A sliding window test is then applied across the model residuals to identify significant CNVs and assign copy numbers. (b) Example chromosome 4 reads from SNU601 cell line, ratios of values versus model expected value, and per-cell smoothing of ratios. Dots are colored red and blue at the thresholds for gain and loss events, respectively.

RIDDLER’s robust GLM model and fitting of assay bias features gives it a distinct advantage over competing methods. For example, while competing methods applicable to ATAC-seq, such as Copy-scAT and epiAneufinder, also normalize for GC content, they do not take into account the accessibility landscape of different cell types, which is the greatest source of bias of ATAC-seq read coverage. Accessible regions of the genome, often denoted by peaks of ATAC-seq reads, vary significantly by cell type and can be further disrupted in cancer [49, 50], leading to inherent differences in read coverage between genomic regions even in the absence of CNVs. By accounting for this and other signal biases within its assay-specific covariates, RIDDLER is better able to model the expected read distributions of diploid cells and distinguish outlier CNV regions. Using a robust GLM, optimized via Huber loss, further improves detection of these outliers by reducing their influence when fitting the model. Supplementary Fig. S2 shows that a non-robust version of our GLM has dramatically reduced performance. Finding deviations from an expected distribution is a common approach used in many CNV callers [24, 26, 31, 51], often with a reference dataset or additional inputs used to define the distribution corresponding to diploid cells. By utilizing robust GLMs and assay bias features, we are able to accurately model the diploid distribution within the target sample, eliminating the need for identifying or building a separate reference dataset. Another important advantage of RIDDLER’s GLM framing, which is not shared by other competitor algorithms, is the ability to assign P-values to each CNV call, therefore yielding an ability to rank discoveries and filter out potential false positives with adjustable thresholds.

The robust CNV calling framework used in RIDDLER allows it to be deployed on a variety of different sequencing assays simply by adjusting the assay bias features used in the regression fit. We demonstrate this versatility by evaluating RIDDLER on sc-ATAC-seq and sc-methylation data. Thanks to this framing, RIDDLER can be easily extended to additional current or future sequencing assays via inclusion of assay-specific features through its versatile framework of bias modeling and outlier detection, making it a uniquely useful CNV calling tool.

RIDDLER accurately detects single-cell CNVs across multiple clones

To evaluate the ability of RIDDLER to detect CNVs at single-cell resolution, we tested the algorithm on single-cell ATAC-seq data generated from the gastric cancer cell line SNU601 [51]. This cell line has previously been shown to contain both large scale gains and losses spanning several megabases in size, as well as subclonal groups with different CNV signatures [32]. To act as ground truth for comparison, we used sc-WGS of the same cell line [52]. The sc-WGS CNVs were computed using AneuFinder [53], producing a similar subclonal structure as reported in previous studies [32] (Supplementary Fig. S3). Using these WGS subclones as ground truth, we evaluated the sc-ATAC-seq CNV calls both for accuracy at single-cell level, and their ability to recapitulate the subclonal structure obtained from WGS calls.

We compared the performance of RIDDLER to other sc-ATAC-seq CNV callers Copy-scAT [31] and epiAneufinder [32]; we omitted scPloidy [54] in this comparison since it aims to identify aneuploid cells and is not sensitive to clonality. Both algorithms were run with the default suggested parameters. RIDDLER, Copy-scAT, epiAneufinder, and AneuFinder all used the same 1-Mb window size for binning data, though Copy-scAT can only report CNVs at chromosome arm resolution. To achieve the same resolution for all methods, we split the CNV calls of Copy-scAT to 1-Mb windows. We additionally only consider a three-state output for each algorithm of gain, loss, or normal, since epiAneufinder is restricted only to this resolution. CNV outputs were clustered to define the subclones called by each algorithm. Subclones from ATAC algorithms were matched via consensus CNV profiles to the closest WGS subclone, with the profile of the matched WGS subclone used as the ground truth CNV state for evaluation. Full heatmaps of the clustered CNVs for each algorithm can be found in Supplementary Fig. S3.

RIDDLER significantly outperforms both Copy-scAT and epiAneufinder in matching the clonal structure and single-cell CNV states identified from WGS. Of the seven WGS subclones matched by ATAC algorithms, RIDDLER identified six, while Copy-scAT and epiAneufinder only identified four and three, respectively (Fig. 2a). Within these subclones, RIDDLER also better matched the specific CNV differences and breakpoints that define them in WGS, including the chr11p and chr20p losses unique only to clone 5. Calculating the accuracy of CNV calls genome-wide, using the matched WGS clones as ground truth, reveals that RIDDLER has significantly higher F1 score, including improved recall for gains and losses (Fig. 2b). When the CNV status of cells is aggregated per cluster, reducing variations due to sparsity, RIDDLER still achieves the highest cluster match F1 score for the six subclones it matches (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the distribution of RIDDLER-defined clones most closely matches the distribution defined by WGS (Fig. 2d), as both identify clone 2 as the most prominent clone and clones 3 and 5 as less common. While neither competing algorithm is able to report copy numbers at each window, we did evaluate the matching of copy numbers reported by RIDDLER to the matched clones found in WGS observing that RIDDLER is able to achieve an 80% match rate or higher for each clone, both overall and for gain events (Supplementary Fig. S4). With its clear improvement in single-cell performance, RIDDLER more accurately captures the CNV structure of ground truth clones and approximates the clonal proportions better than other algorithms, despite the sc-ATAC-seq and sc-WGS experiments being performed separately.

Recent studies utilizing sc-multiomics assays to study subclonal structure in primary cancers have shown the existence of subclone-specific chromatin accessibility peaks, some of which are not explained by CNV dosage [49, 55]. We hypothesized that accurate dissection of subclonal structure should lead to identification of DA peaks across SNU601 subclones. Indeed, RIDDLER-defined subclones result in more than double the DA peaks found using Copy-scAT, even though Copy-scAT had more clusters overall (Fig. 2e). A significant number of the DA peaks found using RIDDLER are also present in diploid areas of the genome, indicating that it is able to identify downstream regulatory changes resulting from CNV disruptions as reported in other studies. Many of these DA peaks are also non-existent in other clones. To further explore clone-specific features in SNU601 cells, we looked at gene-activity scores; gene activity scores are a well-established proxy for gene expression that can be derived from sc-ATAC-seq data [56]. We performed a differential gene activity analysis, which yielded 1835 differentially active genes across clones, including 333 differentially active genes in clone 5. In Supplementary Fig. S5, we highlight two genes of note from this analysis, DHRS2 and ALPK2, which are highly clone dependent in their activity scores. Previous studies have indicated that DHRS2 plays a role in tumor suppression for gastric cancer [57], with downregulation leading to higher proliferation and invasion, while ALPK2 has been shown to be upregulated in gastric tumors versus non-malignant tissue [58], and acts as an oncogene in multiple types of cancers [59–61]. The activity scores of these two genes indicate that clone 5 may have a more aggressive phenotype than other clones in the sample. Taken together, these subclonal accessibility and gene activity differences further validate RIDDLER’s superior performance in subclone identification and demonstrate new clone-based avenues for studying CNV-driven changes of the epigenome and the transcriptome in cancer cells when utilizing sc-ATAC-seq.

We additionally evaluated each ATAC algorithm on sc-ATAC-seq data from CD34+ PBMC cells [45] as a non-cancer control study. We expected these cells to be predominately diploid, and indeed found that each algorithm predicted 89% or more of windows to be diploid (Supplementary Figs S6 and S7), with epiAneufinder having the highest diploid percentage at 94%. Notably, the spurious CNVs called by Copy-scAT showed the most structure, being consistently located in the same regions and presenting different rates for different cell types.

RIDDLER’s intuitive modeling framework allows it to predict CNVs from different sequencing technologies as well. We ran RIDDLER on K562 cell line data produced by scNanoATAC [62], a long read single-cell ATAC-seq assay, and compared them to standard scATACseq sequencing of the same cell line obtained from ENCODE [63] (experiment number ENCSR308ZGJ). These results, shown in Supplementary Fig. S8, show that RIDDLER is able to identify the same CNV patterns in both sequencing technologies, with strong consistency between cells. CNV calls in K562 are additionally consistent with those that have previously been identified by other methods in WGS [21].

We attribute much of the performance improvements in RIDDLER to its ability to robustly model the expected signal in genomic bins. Peak coverage in particular accounts for the majority of variation in ATAC-seq, making it a critical bias to model. If the peak covariate is removed from RIDDLER, it results in a large increase in false positives, as the natural variations in accessibility cause high and low signal regions to be mistaken for CNVs (Supplementary Fig. S1). Neither Copy-scAT or epiAneufinder account for the distribution of peaks in cells, necessitating the use of less direct models to identify outlier signal regions. Copy-scAT aggregates bins at chromosome arm level in order to define distributions that can be deconvolved into gain, loss, or diploid, sacrificing resolution in the process. EpiAneufinder segments chromosomes by breakpoints in ATAC coverage, which again can differ due to the natural accessibility landscape of a cell. It calls CNV status based on fold change differences in these segments, but requires whole number fold changes (equivalent to full loss and full gain events) in order to distinguish CNVs from diploid regions. This conservative approach is reflected in the extremely sparse CNV output from the algorithm, which produces clusters largely driven by sparsity with few DA peaks between them.

RIDDLER maintains high performance with reduced signal and increasing resolution

The linear framework and adjustable bin size of RIDDLER allows the resolution of CNV detection to be tuned to the coverage depth of the data; thus the algorithm can be adjusted to call CNVs on lower coverage cells using larger bins and be pushed to call finer scale CNVs on high coverage cells using smaller bins. To characterize the balance between coverage, bin size, and accuracy, we stress-tested the robustness of CNV calls from RIDDLER by downsampling the fragments from the SNU601 data to thresholds between 20 000 and 5000 fragments per cell (Supplementary Fig. S9), keeping the bin size fixed at 1 Mb. Downsampling of fragments was performed uniformly at random for each cell independently at the different total fragment thresholds; we generated three random downsamples for each threshold using three seeds. We additionally tested the performance of Copy-scAT and epiAneufinder on these downsampled datasets, to obtain performance benchmarks when working with sparser data. In these experiments, RIDDLER calls the same subclone identifying CNVs down to 15 000 fragments per cell, with consistent matching accuracy to WGS calls (Fig. 3). At 10 000 fragments, rarer CNV events, such as the clone 5 loss on chr11p, are lost, but broad CNVs are still accurately maintained. RIDDLER’s performance begins to suffer when working with 5000 reads per cell.

(a) Change in single-cell CNV calls on variable chromosomes for each ATAC algorithm as total reads per cell is reduced from 20 000 to 5000 for downsample replicate 1. (b) Per-cell F1 evaluation of ATAC CNVs compared to WGS CNVs, averaged over all three replicates, as total reads per cell is reduced.

Of the three algorithms tested, Copy-scAT is the most susceptible to changes in cell coverage. It loses the ability to detect the chr4 losses at 20 000 fragments, dramatically changes CNV profiles at 15 000, and substantially drops in WGS matching accuracy at 10 000. We additionally found that the performances of RIDDLER and epiAneufinder are largely robust to the random seed, while Copy-scAT’s performance varies between the downsampling replicates (Supplementary Fig. S10). Both RIDDLER and epiAneufinder exhibit a gradual transition of CNV calls becoming diploid predictions as signal is lost, while Copy-scAT is the only algorithm to switch between gain and loss states as coverage changes. EpiAneufinder is the most consistent algorithm in these experiments, maintaining similar CNV profiles and WGS matching, though RIDDLER has the highest matching accuracy at every coverage threshold.

To further test the robustness of RIDDLER to signal sparsity and assess its ability to call finer resolution CNVs, we ran the algorithm with smaller bin sizes, from 500 kb to 100 kb. We did not include a competitor comparison in these results, as Copy-scAT cannot identify higher resolution CNVs. RIDDLER results at 500 kb are consistent with 1 Mb (Supplementary Fig. S11). At 250 kb, CNVs in lower coverage cells are lost, but the majority of the cells are relatively unchanged. At 100 kb, the per-bin signal is too sparse, and most cells are called as diploid. Combined with the downsample results, these experiments indicate that the algorithm maintains good performance up to an average of approximately five fragments per bin, and can identify finer scale CNV events for deeply sequenced datasets, as sc-ATAC-seq assays continue to improve in the future.

While a decline in performance is unavoidable as the signal per window becomes more diluted, we emphasize that the calls RIDDLER does make are still largely corroborated in scWGS, they are just called with less frequency as signal diminishes. In particular, our dropout filter helps to ensure that spurious losses are not called when signal poor regions become more frequent.

RIDDLER reliably identifies CNVs in single-cell multiomics assays

The recent emergence and explosion of single-cell multiomics assays creates new opportunities for CNV identification that can be jointly analyzed with cell-level epigenetic and transcriptional changes. In this multiomic setting, it is not only important that algorithms demonstrate high performance on single modalities, but that they also integrate well-across modalities. To this end, we evaluated RIDDLER and other sc-ATAC callers ability to identify CNVs and achieve concordance with RNA readout on 10x Multiome data generated from breast cancer cell lines T47D and MCF7 [64]. The 10x Multiome yields both sc-RNA-seq and sc-ATAC-seq readout from the same cell. In the absence of matching WGS data from these cell lines, we instead used inferCNV [26] to compute CNVs from the sc-RNA-seq and aggregated the gene-level calls to the same genomic bins used in sc-ATAC-seq callers. RNA is a relatively difficult modality for CNV calling, due to its uneven coverage of the genome and non-linear dosage effects of CNVs on expression [65], but we would expect broad agreement of CNVs between the two assays. While previous studies have shown that inferCNV is capable of detecting subclonal populations [25, 29], it did not detect any in these cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S12). Given this, we computed consensus CNVs for the RNA modality to use as a comparison for sc-ATAC CNVs called made by each ATAC algorithm (Supplementary Figs S13 and S14).

RIDDLER achieves significantly better F1 scores in matching to RNA CNVs on T47D cells and comparable results to Copy-scAT on MCF7 (Supplementary Fig. S15). On T47D cells RIDDLER achieved higher recall on both gain and loss events, but only has higher recall on gains in MCF7. Notably, the MCF7 data has significantly lower coverage, with an average of 12 000 fragments per cell versus 17 000 for T47D, making losses more difficult to distinguish from technical dropout. In this scenario, when a sample has low coverage and large scale losses, Copy-scAT’s arm-level aggregation holds an advantage. EpiAneufinder calls far fewer CNVs than any other algorithm on both datasets, again proving to be highly conservative. It particularly struggles to call losses, having very low loss recall in both datasets.

In addition to evaluating direct CNV comparisons across modalities, we also demonstrate a new strategy to align cell populations based on CNV similarity, using the 10x multiome datasets as a proof of concept. We separated the ATAC and RNA reads from T47D and MCF7 10x multiome datasets, creating single-cell datasets comprised of both cell lines for each modality. We integrated the CNV calls from the two modalities using the MNN algorithm [41], and evaluated when cells from the same cell line matched together. Figure 4 shows the results of this CNV-based integration, illustrated using Principle Component Analysis (PCA) projections of the CNV matrices. PCA on the ATAC CNVs shows that RIDDLER most closely matches the distribution shape of RNA CNVs, with a distinct group for each cell line (Fig. 4b). In contrast, Copy-scAT projections indicate the prediction of subgroups for each cell line not found in RNA, while epiAneufinder lacks a strong separation between the cell lines. RIDDLER demonstrates the best integration between modality CNVs, with distribution centers aligned between RNA and ATAC (Fig. 4c), few outliers between the cell groups, and a 97.5% match between cell populations (Fig. 4f). EpiAneufinder maintains a separation of cell types after integration that also allows for a high matching rate of 96.8%, but has a distribution shift with the T47D ATAC cells not aligned with the RNA cluster. Copy-scAT has the worst integration, with a substantial population of T47D ATAC cells overlapping with MCF7 RNA cells. This is likely a result of the five distinct cell populations defined by its CNVs being integrated into two RNA cell groups.

Overall the matching results and integration figures show that RIDDLER has the highest concordance with inferCNV, with the two methods well aligned in identifying consensus cell groups. While previous algorithms have integrated single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq experiments using manifold alignment or ATAC estimated gene expression [66–68], these results illustrate the potential of RIDDLER in facilitating multi-modal analysis via CNV based integration in cell populations, which can be particularly useful when applied to primary cancer samples and populations with high clonality.

RIDDLER identifies accurate lesion-specific CNVs in single-cell methylation data

The flexibility of the RIDDLER framework enables CNV detection in single-cell modalities other than sc-ATAC-seq. Here, we demonstrate RIDDLER’s ability to detect CNVs in sc-methylation data at high resolution and clone-specific fashion. We used the methylation readout from the multiomic scTrio-seq2 assay from colorectal cancer patient samples as input [40]. RIDDLER covariates were adjusted to sc-methylation by removing ATAC-specific peak covariate and replacing mappability with bismap [36] computed estimates for bisulfite converted genome mappability. Using high resolution 250-kb windows to mimic the original study, we identified single-cell CNVs in cells from different tissues and lesions.

RIDDLER identified shared and subclone-specific CNV signatures that accurately match the CNVs and subclones from the original paper. We also ran HMMcopy [69]—a CNV caller for WGS data—on the same methylation data for each cell, and at the same window resolution (Supplementary Fig. S16), to compare CNV calls for individual cells. Figure 5a shows the CNVs called by RIDDLER for patient CRC01 from the scTrio-seq2 study, along with average CNV profiles for RIDDLER, HMMcopy, and other colorectal cancer patient samples reported in the TCGA Project. RIDDLER average CNV profiles are similar to CNV profiles obtained from HMMcopy and TCGA samples; however, RIDDLER shows better concordance to TCGA samples for chr13, chr15, and chr22. The single-cell agreement between RIDDLER and HMMcopy is also quite high, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S16, with a median total matching of 67.5% overall and high concordance between clone defining CNVs. The two algorithms differ most in terms of non-clonal losses, with HMMcopy calling losses at nearly twice the rate of RIDDLER (20.0% versus 11.9%); HMMcopy-specific losses further cover the genome in sporadic and unstructured fashion.

Each cell is also annotated by the lesion it originates from; the five different categories are: adjacent normal colon (NC) tissue, primary tumor (PT), lymph node metastasis (LN), liver metastasis (ML), and a post-treatment liver metastasis (MP). RIDDLER’s accurate methylation CNV calls again enable dissection of subclonal populations in each patient. Since this dataset is obtained from multiple biopsies from the primary tumor and metastatic lesions, we constructed a phylogenetic tree from these subclones, as shown in Fig. 5b and c. In the phylogenetic tree, we can see a clear differentiation between metastatic and primary-only clones, with clone 1 identified as a likely ancestor for all other metastatic clones. This same population of cells, defined by CNV state, was also identified in the original paper as a likely ancestor of other metastatic clones [40]. Using the clonal CNVs identified by RIDDLER, we compared clone 1, the metastatic ancestor, with clones 9 and 10, the primary only clones, and found that clone 1 and all its descendants possessed losses in the early segments of chromosomes 15 and 17 that are not present in the primary only clones. To nominate CNV-driven metastatic processes, we performed gene set enrichment analysis on the set of genes located within these metastasis-only CNV windows; this analysis yielded significant enrichment of GO pathways related to cell structure and motility (Fig. 5d), including myosin, intermediate, and keratin filaments [70, 71]. We additionally found unique amplification status of oncogene regions in many clones, along with near ubiquitous losses around tumor suppression genes (Supplementary Fig. S17). When ran on other CRC samples from the same dataset, RIDDLER consistently identified region-specific CNV events in both primary and metastatic lesions, as shown in Supplementary Figs S18 through S21.

We also evaluated RIDDLER on sciMETv2 human brain data [72] to illustrate its adaptability to a different sc-methylation assay. This dataset also acts as a non-cancer control, as we would expect these cells to be largely diploid. Supplementary Fig. S22 shows that this is indeed the case, as only a small number of cells are reported with CNVs, and only 1.4% of windows overall. Altogether these results clearly demonstrate that RIDDLER is a highly accurate CNV caller for emerging sc-methylation assays and can enable investigation of clonal evolution and putatively CNV-driven biological processes for future studies.

Discussion

RIDDLER is a versatile computational framework developed for high-resolution CNV detection in single-cell sequencing datasets of different modalities, particularly effective for assays targeting the epigenome. RIDDLER uses an outlier-aware GLM, dropout filtering, and a smoothing procedure to achieve high F1-scores, accurately identifying true CNVs while minimizing false discoveries. Thanks to its flexible design and GLM-based formulation, RIDDLER can be easily deployed on different sc-modalities by changing GLM covariates, can assign statistical significance to discovered CNVs independent of modality and be adjusted to strike a balance between resolution and sparsity. The flexibility of this framework is demonstrated through RIDDLER’s exceptional performance on both scATAC-seq and sc-methylation data, including applications to uni-modal and multi-modal sequencing assays for ATAC and methylation, and the high concordance of its output on long-read and short-read sequencing technologies. Benchmarking against competing algorithms in multiple datasets and assays, we conclusively showed that RIDDLER is a highly accurate and flexible method for calling CNVs in different modalities and studying clonal heterogeneity, while eliminating the need to identify reference diploid cell populations.

RIDDLER builds upon past outlier detection approaches for CNV detection, while utilizing robust statistics and signal filtering techniques to handle single-cell challenges. Central to RIDDLER is the Huber loss function, which down weights outliers and eliminates the need for diploid reference cells, a common requirement in sc-RNA-seq CNV calling. By changing the window size, RIDDLER can identify sc-CNVs at even 100-kb resolution; however, data sparsity presents a challenge. RIDDLER’s outlier filter prevents potential dropout events from being identified as losses, while its window smoothing approach further removes false gains and losses. Our thorough stress testing of per cell signal and changing window size for sc-ATAC-seq data shows that RIDDLER loses sensitivity but avoids false positives with increasing sparsity, likely due to the dropout filter. Notably, RIDDLER maintains higher accuracy than competing methods at every tested threshold of cell coverage. For sc-ATAC-seq data, RIDDLER retains most of its CNV calls at ten thousands fragments, which is the current standard yield for commercial sc-ATAC-seq assays. Datasets with lower coverage can still take advantage of RIDDLER by simply increasing the size of the genomic bins, as our stress testing indicates that it is the reads-per-bin that primarily drives algorithm performance. We anticipate that RIDDLER will be able to call finer scale CNVs as the sc-assay coverage increases; alternatively, meta-single-cells can be used to achieve necessary coverage for higher resolution CNV detection [73].

RIDDLER’s accurate CNV calls can unlock investigation of heterogeneous cell populations with distinct CNVs profiled with sc-ATAC-seq and sc-methylation assays. Previously, sc-WGS and sc-RNA-seq assays were used to study clonal heterogeneity of cancerous cells based on CNVs. Here, we validated that RIDDLER can accurately identify previously reported clonal structure in two separate datasets; (i) sc-ATAC-seq performed on a gastric cancer cell line SNU601 [32] and (ii) sc-methylation from colorectal cancer samples [40]. In the SNU601 dataset, utilizing the subclonal structure identified through RIDDLER’s CNV calls, we identified DA regions of chromatin, and putatively differentially activated genes of oncogenic relevance, demonstrating a clear cut paradigm on how RIDDLER can unlock dissection of shifts in multiple modalities of the genome. In the colorectal cancer study, RIDDLER accurately elucidates clonal heterogeneity similar to the original study, finding lesion-specific and shared clones. Of note, we observe some clones are only in primary tumors, whereas other clones are only seen at metastatic sites, across different patient samples. For patient CRC01, lesion-specific clones also show distinct CNV signatures on specific oncogenes, such as MYC [74], as well as clone-specific CNVs on gene related to filament-based motility in metastatic clones. Taken together, these findings highlight RIDDLER’s immense potential to study clonal heterogeneity in healthy and cancerous cells using sc-epigenome assays. RIDDLER-derived clonal structure can be utilized to study CNV-driven regulatory chromatin states, such as clone-specific accessible peaks, transcription factor motifs, or differential methylation states that may lie on aneuploid or diploid regions of the genome, as we demonstrated a proof-of-concept clone-specific differential peak discovery in the SNU601 cell line.

RIDDLER’s versatility extends to single-cell multiomic data integration by aligning cell populations based on CNV similarity. Our integration experiments on sc-RNA+ATAC multiomics data demonstrate that RIDDLER’s CNV calls can be utilized to accurately match MCF7 and T47D cells based on the similarity of ATAC and RNA-derived CNV calls from RIDDLER and inferCNV. In contrast, Copy-scAT does not align as well, due to identification of false CNV subpopulations. Despite the relative lower complexity of the integration task due to the presence of two distinct populations, this result demonstrates that RIDDLER can integrate cancerous and other cell populations that have been profiled by multiple single-cell modalities along a CNV axis when cells have variable but distinct CNV profiles, especially for future studies with higher clonal heterogeneity.

Looking forward, RIDDLER can be extended to other single-cell modalities due to its intuitive formulation through identification of assay-specific covariates. Single-cell Hi-C assay is an obvious candidate that exhibits assay-specific biases that are commonly corrected by matrix balancing algorithms [75, 76]. In future studies, we will adapt RIDDLER to other single-cell assay modalities and other sc-multiomics assays, such as sc-HiC+RNA-seq [77], and the trimodal sc-NMT-seq [78]. With these advancements, we can (i) identify CNVs from each modality to obtain higher confidence consensus CNVs across modalities, and (ii) identify even finer resolution CNVs than reported in this study thanks to deeper coverage from combining multiple modalities. With the recent emergence sc-multiomics assays that incorporate Hi-C modality, another exciting prospect is the identification of other structural variants from sc-Hi-C [79], such as translocations and inversions, which can be studied together with CNVs. Indeed, future applications of RIDDLER to such high confidence and high resolution CNV calls from sc-multiomics data holds great potential in linking CNV-driven phenotype changes at single-cell resolution, potentially enabling causal linking of CNVs to cell state.
