Prevalence and burden of coronary artery disease in young adults undergoing clinically indicated coronary CT angiography
Authors: Kevin J O’Gorman, Gil Metser, Sarah Mullen, Nicholas Ng, Jay Leb, Yiyi Zhang, Daichi Shimbo, Campbell Rogers, Ana Navas-Acien, Jonathon Leipsic, Andrew J Einstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The reported prevalence and burden of coronary artery disease in young adults varies markedly in published studies from the 1950s through 2000s but has not been evaluated in a large contemporary multicentre cohort of young adults referred for clinically indicated coronary CT angiography (CCTA).MethodsRetrospective, international, multicentre, cross-sectional study of consecutive patients aged 18–49 in 29 healthcare systems who underwent clinically indicated CCTA with artificial intelligence-enabled coronary plaque analysis (AI-CPA) between January 2021 and May 2022. The prevalence of coronary atherosclerosis and coronary plaque volume and characteristics were evaluated.Results1621 patients with mean age 41.9±6.6 years and 712 (43.9%) women were included. Among young adults referred for clinically indicated CCTA, 62.8% had coronary plaque identified by AI-CPA, including 34.7% of patients aged 18–29, 50.6% aged 30–39 and 68.8% aged 40–49 (p=<0.0001). In patients with coronary plaque, all had non-calcified plaque, 54.7% had calcified plaque and 63.4% had low-attenuation plaque. Median (IQR) total plaque volume was 36 (11–153) mm3, which increased with age and was higher in men than women (59 (17–214) mm3 vs 22 (9–70) mm3 (p≤0.0001)). In the entire cohort, 44% had a nadir CT fractional flow reserve (FFR-CT) ≤0.80, and this proportion increased with age.ConclusionsOver 62% of young adults referred for clinically indicated CCTA have coronary artery plaque detected by AI-CPA, and plaque prevalence and burden increase with age. These findings suggest that coronary artery plaque is present in most individuals referred for CCTA by early adulthood, warranting the development of strategies for risk stratification and prevention of coronary events early in life.Keywords: Coronary Artery Disease, Computed Tomography Angiography, Atherosclerosis, Epidemiology

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is commonly perceived as a disease of older adults. Indeed, the average age at the time of first myocardial infarction is 65.6 years in men and 72.0 in women.1 The reported prevalence and burden of CAD in asymptomatic young adults varies markedly in autopsy studies of soldiers killed in combat,2,4 young trauma victims5 and asymptomatic heart transplant recipients6; however, little is known about the prevalence and burden of CAD in young adults referred for clinically indicated non-invasive testing.

With the emergence of coronary CT angiography (CCTA), there now exists a non-invasive test that allows for in vivo assessment of non-calcified and calcified coronary artery plaque. More recently, advancements in CCTA combined with artificial intelligence offer a non-invasive method to identify coronary plaque and quantify its volume and composition in living hearts. Studies have demonstrated strong agreement between artificial intelligence-enabled coronary plaque analysis (AI-CPA) quantification from CCTA and plaque assessed by reference standard of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) during invasive angiography.7 8

In this study, we sought to characterise the prevalence, burden and composition of coronary atherosclerosis among young adults aged 18–49 years undergoing clinically indicated CCTA in 29 healthcare systems in the USA and Canada using AI-CPA.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an observational, retrospective, consecutive, multicentre cross-sectional study of all patients aged 18–49 who underwent clinically indicated CCTA between January 2021 and May 2022 in 29 healthcare systems in the USA and Canada. All sites obtained local institutional approval to analyse and publish metadata available from AI-CPA. CCTA was performed using a range of 64+ row scanners, and patients were pretreated with nitroglycerin to achieve coronary hyperaemia. Patients were included only if age/sex demographics were available and CCTA image quality was sufficient for quantitative plaque assessment. The AI-CPA software utilised in this study was developed and provided by Heartflow (San Francisco, California). Coauthors from Heartflow were actively involved in the concept and design of the study, performed statistical analysis, participated in the interpretation of the data and contributed to the critical revision of the manuscript. Nonetheless, all academic coauthors maintained complete control over the decision to submit the final data for publication.

Segmentation of cardiac structures

All CCTA datasets in each healthcare system were anonymised, except for patient age and sex, and images were transferred to Heartflow for analysis. AI-based algorithms were applied to each CCTA dataset for segmentation and plaque quantification. Using an automated algorithm, the phase of the cardiac cycle with best image quality was determined and then initial segmentation of the aorta, left ventricular myocardium and coronary artery tree was performed. Within the coronary tree, initial segmentation of the outer vessel wall, lumen and plaque for each vessel was obtained using deep learning-based regression models. Subsequently, a human quality-review process, performed by certified CT analysts blinded to patient age/sex and approved for clinical use by the Food and Drug Administration, was used to inspect and correct the initial segmentation and generate a final patient-specific three-dimensional model of the arterial tree and left ventricular myocardium. The need to correct initial lumen segmentation varied by patient, based on presence of coronary disease, image quality and results from the deep-learning algorithm.

AI plaque quantification

All CCTA studies were analysed for total plaque volume and for volumes of its components: calcified plaque, non-calcified plaque and low-attenuation plaque. AI-CPA was performed and measurements determined using a previously described,9 FDA-approved software approach based on the coronary segmentation performed above. No further manual adjustments are needed for determining AI-CPA measurements, which were automatically determined as follows. Between the lumen and the outer wall, plaque presence was detected using a deep learning algorithm, plaque volume quantified and plaque characterised using automatic thresholding. Calcified plaque was derived with adaptive thresholding on the basis of lumen contrast, non-calcified plaque as being less than the calcified plaque threshold and low-attenuation plaque, a high-risk phenotype10 associated with atheroma lipid-rich necrotic core that is more likely to rupture, defined as that portion of non-calcified plaque with <30 Hounsfield Units.11

Fractional flow reserve via CT

For each CCTA scan, using the AI-segmented and human edited model of the aorta, myocardium and coronary tree described above as inputs to a model of the microcirculation resistance and computational fluid dynamics simulation with a finite element model was performed to numerically solve the Navier-Stokes equations governing fluid flow. Here too, no further manual adjustments are needed, and analysis is performed automatically using the final segmentation. The ratio of pressure at each centreline point of the coronary arteries to pressure in the ascending aorta, termed the CT fractional flow reserve (FFR-CT), was determined throughout the coronary tree for all portions of the tree with diameter ≥1.8 mm.12

FFR-CT gradually decreases from more proximal to more distal portions of a vessel, corresponding to pressure dissipation down the vessel.13 14 In general, both an absolute FFR-CT value of ≤0.8 and also a discrete drop-off in FFR-CT across an anatomically-visualised stenosis are required for a plaque and its associated stenosis to be deemed haemodynamically significant. Here, given that patients had been referred for clinically-indicated CCTA due to symptoms such as chest pain or dyspnoea, which may bias estimates of prevalence of plaque due to oversampling of patients with obstructive CAD, we performed in addition to the primary analysis with all patients, a sensitivity analysis limited to the subgroup of patients who were FFR-CT-negative, defined (more stringently than haemodynamic significance) as having minimum FFR-CT value throughout the coronary tree (referred to as nadir FFR-CT) of >0.8, with or without a discrete drop-off in FFR-CT.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean±SD for normally distributed variables, and as median (IQR) or median (IQR) (5th–95th percentile) for non-normally distributed values, and compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and compared using Fisher’s exact tests. To evaluate the relationship between age and plaque volume, second-order polynomial curves were fitted to the median, upper end of the IQR (75th percentile) and 90th percentile of plaque volume as a function of age, using the least squares method. Statistical analysis was performed in Stata/SE V.16.1 and SAS V.9.4.

Results

From an initial cohort of 15 447 consecutive patients who underwent clinically indicated CCTA across the participating healthcare systems, standard registry-wide quality and clinical exclusion criteria were applied. A total of 1264 patients (8.2%) were excluded due to poor overall image quality, and 364 patients (2.4%) were excluded due to an inability to completely evaluate the right coronary artery. Additional exclusions included 2355 patients lacking baseline age or sex demographics. From the remaining age-eligible screening pool, 20 patients were excluded due to age <18, and 9565 were excluded due to age >50. Finally, 258 patients were excluded due to the presence of an existing coronary stent (comprising 242 individuals aged >50 and 16 individuals aged 18–49), leaving a final contemporary cohort of 1621 young adults who met all eligibility criteria for the primary analysis (online supplemental figure 1). Only 24 of the 29 healthcare systems had cases with patients aged 18–49 included for analysis, and 14 healthcare systems contributed >10 cases, which were included for site-specific analysis. Mean age was 41.9±6.6, and 712 (43.9%) were female. Proportions of patients with the various plaque characteristics, by age group, are reported in table 1. The majority of patients (62.8%) had plaque identified on CCTA. This proportion increased with age, and included 34.7% of patients aged 18–29, 50.6% aged 30–39 and 68.8% aged 40–49 (p=<0.0001). In patients with coronary plaque, all had non-calcified plaque, 54.7% had calcified plaque and 63.4% low-attenuation plaque (table 1). Prevalence of each plaque component increased with age. Of the 34.7% of patients within the youngest age group (18–29 years) who had plaque identified, all had non-calcified plaque, 29.4% had calcified plaque and 41.2% low-attenuation plaque (table 1).

Plaque burden

Median (IQR) total plaque volume was 9 (0–65) mm3 among all patients (table 2), and 36 (11–153) mm3 among patients with any plaque (table 2). Plaque volume increased with age (figure 1); among patients with any plaque, median total volume was 9.5 (5–37) mm3 for patients aged 18–29, 25 (10–62) mm3 aged 30–39 and 43 (14–187] mm3 aged 40–49 (p=<0.0001). Coronary plaque in these young patients was predominantly non-calcified. Even among patients aged 40–49, median non-calcified plaque volume was 41 (13–166) mm,3 compared with only 2 (0–22) mm3 for calcified plaque. Furthermore, only a small proportion of non-calcified plaque was low-attenuating, with a median volume of 2 (0–6) mm3.

Differences between young women and men

The prevalence of any plaque and plaque components by sex is summarised in figure 2. In patients with any coronary plaque, total plaque volume was higher in men than women (59 (17–214) mm3 vs 22 (9–70) mm3 (p=<0.0001)) and increased with age for each. In the youngest age group (18–29 years), higher proportions of women had calcified, non-calcified and low-attenuating coronary plaque than men; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p=0.29, 0.32 and 0.39, respectively) and women did not have a statistically significant higher total plaque volume (p=0.24). In patients older than 30, significantly higher proportions of male patients had any, non-calcified, calcified and low-attenuation coronary plaque (p<0.0001 for each). In patients with any coronary plaque, median low-attenuation plaque volume was higher in men (2 (0–7) mm3 vs 1 (0–3) mm3 (p=<0.0001)).

Findings based on FFR-CT results

In this cohort of young adults referred for clinically indicated CCTA, 44% had a nadir FFR-CT ≤0.80. Within both FFR-CT-negative and FFR-CT-positive subgroups, the prevalence of any plaque, calcified plaque, non-calcified plaque and low-attenuation plaque increased significantly with age (online supplemental table S1), with the highest burden observed in patients aged 40–49 years (all p<0.001). Compared with FFR-CT-negative patients, FFR-CT-positive patients had a significantly greater prevalence of all plaque phenotypes overall (all p<0.001) as shown in online supplemental table S2. These differences were most pronounced in the 30–39 and 40–49-year age groups, whereas plaque prevalence was generally similar between FFR-CT-positive and FFR-CT-negative patients aged 18–29 years. Sex-stratified analyses demonstrated similar findings, with age-related increases in plaque prevalence among both women and men and consistently higher plaque prevalence in FFR-CT-positive compared with FFR-CT-negative patients, particularly in older age groups (online supplemental table S1). Plaque volume increased with age for both FFR-CT-negative and FFR-CT-positive patients (online supplemental table S3), including total plaque volume, non-calcified plaque volume, calcified plaque volume and low-attenuation plaque volume (all p<0.001). Across all age groups, FFR-CT-positive patients demonstrated substantially greater plaque volumes than FFR-CT-negative patients (p<0.001 for all plaque volume measures), with the largest differences observed among patients aged 30–49 years (online supplemental table S4). Sex-stratified analyses showed comparable age-related increases in plaque volume among both women and men, with FFR-CT-positive patients consistently exhibiting greater plaque burden and lower nadir FFR-CT values than FFR-CT-negative patients.

Findings in different healthcare systems

Prevalence of coronary plaque and plaque volumes for the 1575 patients from 14 healthcare systems which contributed >10 cases for analysis are illustrated in online supplemental tables S5 and S6, respectively. Prevalence of coronary plaque and median plaque volume was 82.9% and 92 mm3, respectively, in the upper quartile, and 60.2% and 7.5 mm3 in the lower quartile of healthcare systems. Discussion

This is the first multicentre study to investigate the prevalence, burden and characteristics of coronary artery plaque in young adults referred for clinically indicated CCTA using AI-CPA (figure 4). The major findings were: (1) coronary artery plaque was identified in the majority (62.8%) of referred patients and in 34.7% of referred patients within the youngest age group (18–29 years); (2) among young adults with coronary artery plaque, all had non-calcified plaque and approximately half had calcified plaque; (3) plaque prevalence and burden increased throughout young adulthood; (4) total plaque volume was higher in men than women (p≤0.0001) and increased with age in both sexes; (5) 44% of young adults had an FFR-CT nadir of ≤0.80 and plaque burden was consistently higher among FFR-CT-positive than FFR-CT-negative patients in both women and men, with the greatest differences observed in the 30–39 and 40–49-year age groups and (6) there were differences in plaque prevalence and burden between sites, with prevalence of coronary plaque as high as 84.6% in one healthcare system.

Previous CCTA studies evaluating coronary plaque in young adults have generally been limited to single-centre cohorts. In a symptomatic Austrian cohort referred for CCTA, plaque prevalence ranged from 6.4% to 34.9% depending on age,15 while a single-centre AI-CPA study from the Bronx reported that 15% of subjects aged 18–45 years had coronary plaque.16 Although these studies used comparable imaging approaches, the prevalence observed in our multicentre cohort was substantially higher despite use of a similar AI-CPA approach, which has demonstrated strong agreement with plaque assessed by reference standard of IVUS during invasive angiography.7 8 The reasons for these differences cannot be determined from the present study and may reflect differences in referral patterns, patient characteristics, geographic variation or other unmeasured clinical factors.

In addition, the multicentre CAC Consortium observed calcified plaque in 13.1% of patients aged 20–30 years17, and 34.4% of patients aged 30–49 years.18 Furthermore, in patients aged 30–45 years, 21% (9% of women and 25% of men) had evidence of calcified plaque using three separate cohorts.19 These percentages are similar to the prevalence of calcified plaque we observed here, namely, 10.2% in patients aged 18–29 and 34.4% in the total cohort. However, our study identified considerably larger proportions of young people with non-calcified plaque, suggesting that CCTA may detect earlier stages of coronary atherosclerosis that would not be identified by calcium scoring alone.

Histopathologic studies have long demonstrated that coronary atherosclerosis may be present in young adults, although reported prevalences vary widely across military and trauma populations.2,5 Direct comparison with the present study should be made cautiously, as these cohorts differed substantially with respect to age distribution, sex composition, clinical status and methods of disease ascertainment. That being said, these studies support the concept that coronary atherosclerosis often begins decades before the onset of clinical cardiovascular events.

While women aged 18–29 had higher prevalence of plaque, this finding was not statistically significant and may reflect referral bias, wherein the youngest women, at higher risk for the long-term effects of radiation than their male counterparts,20 may have had higher risk factor burden and pretest likelihood. Overall, men had higher prevalence of all plaque subtypes; however, this must be interpreted cautiously as we were not able to adjust for differences in risk factor burden

Our study demonstrated differences in the prevalence and volume of coronary artery plaque between different regions. Given the absence of clinical covariates, referral indications, scanner-specific information and socioeconomic variables, the causes of these between-site differences cannot be determined.

CCTA with AI-CPA has demonstrated its powerful predictive value to identify patients at increased risk for cardiovascular events.7 10 21 Patients with non-obstructive CAD are at increased risk for major adverse cardiac events as evidenced by the fact that about half22 to two-thirds23 24 of lesions responsible for myocardial infarction are angiographically non-obstructive (ie, with <50% stenosis) prior to plaque rupture. Our study demonstrates that such non-obstructive CAD is frequently detected among young adults referred for clinically indicated CCTA. Future studies are needed to determine the prevalence, prognostic significance and clinical actionability of similar findings in asymptomatic screening populations before screening strategies can be recommended.

Limitations

This study represents a cohort of patients who underwent clinically indicated CCTA rather than screening CCTA, thus limiting the generalisability of this study to an asymptomatic patient population. Furthermore, patient demographic characteristics were limited to age and sex, since other characteristics are not provided to the AI-CPA vendor. Further investigation would be required to characterise differences in plaque prevalence and burden between, for example, race/ethnic groups, as was done for calcified plaque for adults aged 45–84 in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis.25 Similarly, cardiovascular risk factors, medications and presence of symptoms were not collected; while this is a virtue for clinically-performed studies, so that plaque volume and FFR-CT determinations are truly independent of other factors associated with CAD, it limits the study of their inter-relationships. Finally, concerns have been raised that AI-CPA may have limited ability to distinguish small regions of non-calcified plaque from image noise or motion artefact, potentially leading to overestimation of disease prevalence in low risk populations.26 27 However, the deep-learning AI-CPA algorithm employed in this study has been rigorously validated against three-vessel IVUS and shown excellent agreement and strong correlation with invasive measurements of total, non-calcified and calcified plaque volumes,7 8 and findings in this study suggest that this was not a low-risk population based on the fact that 44% of patients had a nadir FFR-CT of ≤0.80.

Conclusions

In a large cohort of young adults aged 18–49 undergoing clinically indicated CCTA, coronary plaque was identified in 62.8% of individuals overall (34.7% of those under age 30), and 44% had a nadir FFR-CT ≤0.80. These findings should be interpreted within the context of a referred patient population and should not be considered estimates of plaque prevalence in the general young adult population. Future studies incorporating clinical covariates and asymptomatic populations are needed to define the broader epidemiology and clinical implications of early coronary plaque.
