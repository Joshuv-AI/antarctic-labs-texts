Optimizing ADAMTS13 prophylaxis to reduce relapse and organ failure in congenital thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura∗

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Long-term organ complications are prevalent in cTTP and highlight the need for personalized follow-up and tailored prophylactic strategies.•rhADAMTS13 shows promising safety and efficacy in cTTP, potentially improving relapse-free survival and reducing treatment burden.

Introduction

Congenital thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (cTTP; Upshaw-Schulman syndrome; Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, 274150) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder, with a prevalence of 0.5 to 2.0 cases per million, caused by a severe hereditary deficiency (activity <10 IU/dL) in the specific von Willebrand factor (VWF)–cleaving protease ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloprotease with thrombospondin type 1 motif, member 13).1, 2, 3, 4 As a result, ultralarge VWF multimers accumulate in plasma and endothelial surface, with subsequently platelet aggregation, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, microvascular thrombosis, and organ damage. Acute episodes can be spontaneous or triggered by infections, vaccination, trauma, or pregnancy, whereas recurrent overt and subclinical TTP events contribute to life-threatening events or milder symptoms including headache, neurological and cardiovascular issues, kidney failure, and other ischemic long-term organ dysfunction.5, 6, 7, 8, 9

cTTP diagnosis relies on the identification of pathogenic homozygous or compound heterozygous ADAMTS13 mutations.10,11 To date, ∼200 distinct pathogenic ADAMTS13 variants have been reported.5,7,12,13 Notably, the c.4143_4144dupA mutation is common in Eastern European populations,5, 6, 7,13,14 whereas the p.Arg1060Trp mutation is frequently observed in pregnancy-onset cTTP, particularly in Europe, Scandinavia, Turkey, and North Americans of European ancestry.6,7,13,15

Three distinct clinical phenotypes of cTTP have been identified: (1) pediatric-onset cTTP occurs in the neonatal period or early childhood, and it is typically triggered by infections or vaccination5,7; (2) pregnancy-onset cTTP is observed during pregnancy and results in obstetric complications including fetal loss5, 6, 7,13,16; and (3) adult-onset cTTP, the rarest form, often presents with neurological symptoms, typically ischemic stroke.17,18 Accurate classification is crucial, because each form has distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications. Although pediatric-onset disease requires long-term prophylaxis, pregnancy-onset cTTP is managed with short-term prophylaxis during subsequent pregnancies and other potential risk periods with triggering events. Pregnancy-onset cTTP is typically associated with less deleterious ADAMTS13 mutations, and patients generally experience a normal life outside pregnancy, without the need for long-term prophylaxis. In contrast, the management of adult-onset cTTP remains less clear, and long-term prophylaxis depends on disease severity.

cTTP treatment has historically relied on prophylactic or on-demand ADAMTS13 replacement via plasma therapy, including fresh-frozen plasma (FFP), solvent detergent–treated plasma, or ADAMTS13-containing plasma-derived factor VIII–VWF concentrates.6,13,19,20 However, plasma therapy is limited by donor dependency, burdensome infusion schedules, allergic reactions, volume overload, and fluctuating low ADAMTS13 levels, which may contribute to cumulative organ damage.20,21 Since 2017, recombinant human ADAMTS13 (rhADAMTS13) has emerged as a promising alternative. A phase 3 randomized trial demonstrated that rhADAMTS13 better prevents acute episodes and may provide effective protection against long-term complications, such as recurrent ischemic events (cerebral, cardiac, kidney, and digestive) and pregnancy-related complications.22, 23, 24

So far, the rarity of cTTP has limited our understanding of its clinical course, including the full spectrum of long-term organ dysfunction. However, accumulating longitudinal data from registries have expanded our knowledge on the long-term outcome of the disease,5,7,13,17,25 reporting an increased long-term risk of cerebral and cardiovascular complications, which necessitates specific attention to cognitive evaluation and vascular risk prevention. These complications must be carefully considered to minimize their impact on patients’ health and quality of life (QoL).8,26,27

In France, patients with cTTP are usually treated with aggressive prophylactic strategy combining plasma therapy and, more recently, rhADAMTS13. However, the long-term impact of these strategies on ischemic organ dysfunction remains uncertain. In this study, based on our 25-year national experience, we aimed to assess long-term complication of cTTP, with an emphasis on neurological and cardiovascular dysfunction from a large, well-characterized cTTP cohort under treatment.

Patients and methods

Study population

Since 2000, all patients with a presumptive diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) have been prospectively enrolled in the national registry of the French Reference Center for TMA.5,13,16,28 ADAMTS13 analysis, including activity measurement, antigen levels, anti-ADAMTS13 immunoglobulin G (IgG) titration, and ADAMTS13 gene sequencing, were performed centrally in the national reference laboratory. Here, eligible patients had an ADAMTS13 activity <10 IU/dL, no detectable anti-ADAMTS13 IgG, and either biallelic deleterious ADAMTS13 sequence variations or a single deleterious ADAMTS13 sequence variation associated with a set of disease-linked Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNP).29 Pathogenicity and classification of ADAMTS13 variants was assessed using the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) scoring system, which ranges from 1 to 5, in which a score of 1 indicates a benign variant and a score of 5 represents a pathogenic variant.30 Clinical and biological data on acute phase and during follow-up were collected through hospital reports.

Definitions

Patients with cTTP were categorized into 3 groups based on age and clinical context at disease onset: pediatric onset (first episode before age <18 years), pregnancy onset (first episode during pregnancy or post partum), and adult onset (first episode in adulthood, excluding pregnancy). Age at first TTP episode was defined as the time of initial clinical features. The index episode was defined as the TTP episode leading to cTTP suspicion and inclusion in the national registry. Organ dysfunction is detailed in supplemental Material 1. Acute and subacute TTP events, as well as isolated TTP manifestations, have been defined elsewhere (detailed in supplemental Material 2).24

Blood collections and laboratory assays

Blood samples were prospectively collected and analyzed as part of routine care during acute phase, prior treatment, and during follow-up visits and stored in the Biobank of the French Reference Center for TMA (registration number AC-2023-6021; Lariboisière Hospital, AP-HP.Nord, Paris, France).5,13,16 ADAMTS13 activity was measured using our in-house FRETS-VWF73 assay (Peptide Institute Inc, Osaka, Japan),31 with a normal range of 50 to 150 IU/dL. Anti-ADAMTS13 IgG were detected and titrated using the Technozym ADAMTS13 INH ELISA (Technoclone, Vienna, Austria), with a positivity threshold of 15 U/mL according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. ADAMTS13 (NM_139025.5) genotype was determined through high-throughput sequencing of the coding sequence with Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) probes (Integrated DNA Technologies Inc, Switzerland), and raw data were analyzed using SOPHiA DDM (SOPHiA GENETICS). A pathogenicity scoring is done for every ADAMTS13 variants identified based on the ACMG and theAssociation for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines.30

Observational comparative analysis

This analysis comparing outcomes across different prophylactic treatment strategies (no treatment, FFP prophylaxis, and rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis) is observational and retrospective. Treatment allocation was not randomized but based on clinical decisions over time.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as medians ± interquartile range (IQR), whereas categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to assess relapse- and complication-free survival across the phenotypic groups (pediatric onset, pregnancy onset, and adult onset) and according to prophylactic treatment strategies, with comparisons performed using log-rank tests and time-dependent Cox models. Only acute events with a precisely documented date were included in the analysis. Additionally, a swimmer plot was generated to depict the different treatment phases in patients with pediatric-onset cTTP. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/).

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital (Paris, France). QoL was assessed using the Short Form-36 questionnaire at baseline and during follow-up.

Results

Classification of patients with cTTP based on age and context at onset

From 2000 to 2024, a total of 14 735 patients with TMA were prospectively enrolled in the French national TMA registry and investigated for ADAMTS13, leading to the diagnosis of TTP in 2046 patients (14%; Figure 1), including 1958 with acquired TTP (96%), mostly immune mediated,32 and 88 with cTTP (4%) from 83 families. Forty-two patients with cTTP experienced their first episode during childhood (48%; pediatric-onset group), with a confirmed diagnosis established at a median age of 2.3 years (IQR, 1.0-13.3). Among the remaining 46 patients (52%), cTTP occurred during adulthood; 33 (72%) had pregnancy-onset cTTP, and 13 (28%) had adult-onset disease unrelated to pregnancy.

Flowchart of the enrollment of patients. From 1 January 2000 to 1 October 2024, a total of 14 735 patients with TMA were enrolled and investigated for ADAMTS13. Among them, 2047 patients had a severe functional deficiency in ADAMTS13 (activity <10 IU/dL) and were subsequently diagnosed with TTP. Of those 2047 patients, 1958 had acquired TTP (anti-ADAMTS13 IgG ≥15 U/mL and/or detectable ADAMTS13 activity during remission), and 89 had cTTP. Among the 89 patients with cTTP, 42 patients had their first TTP episode during childhood, and 46 had them during adulthood, including 33 in obstetric context and 13 without any obstetric context.

Genotypic landscape of cTTP

We analyzed the distribution of 90 genetic variants based on age and disease onset, differentiating variants electively associated with pediatric-, pregnancy-, or adult-onset group from those shared across groups (supplemental Table 1; supplemental Figure 1). In the pediatric-onset group, we identified 55 distinct variants (21 homozygous) across 84 alleles, including 32 missense mutations and 23 variants of other types (10 splice site, 6 nonsense, 4 deletion, and 3 frameshift variants). Pregnancy- and adult-onset groups included 40 distinct variants (8 homozygous) across 92 alleles, comprising 21 missense mutations and 19 other types of variants (5 splice site, 2 deletion, 9 frameshift, and 3 nonsense variants).

Notably, 5 variants (c.988-2A>C, p.Arg916Cys, p.Cys977Arg, p.Arg1095Gln, and p.Asp1362Val) were common to both pediatric- and adult-onset groups: 1 was homozygous, whereas the others were associated with either 4 other missense mutations or 1 additional variant. Overall, our analysis identified 90 distinct variants (28 homozygous) across 176 alleles, including 49 missense mutations and 41 other types of variants (supplemental Table 2). The p.Arg1060Trp mutation was identified in 32 patients, predominantly in the pregnancy-onset group (n = 24 [73%], including 7 homozygous) and less frequently in the adult-onset group (n = 8 [62%], including 1 homozygous). Notably, it was not observed in pediatric-onset patients. Interestingly, all patients with pediatric-onset cTTP harbored biallelic deleterious mutations in ADAMTS13 gene (ACMG score of 4 or 5). In contrast, 10 patients with a late-onset disease (6 pregnancy onset and 4 adult onset) presented with an atypical genotypic profile, carrying 1 deleterious mutation in ADAMTS13 gene (ACMG score of 4 or 5, including 4 patients with the p.Arg1060Trp mutation), associated with a set of linked SNPs and/or several less deleterious mutations (ACMG scores 1-3).

Demographics, comorbidities, and clinical features at index episode

In the pediatric-onset cTTP group (n = 42), patients of African/West Indies ancestry were more prevalent, and consanguinity was reported in 14 patients (33%). The median age at diagnosis was 2 years (IQR, 0-13), although half of these children had a past history of TMA, suggestive of undiagnosed TTP. Only a small proportion of patients presented with comorbidities. In the pregnancy-onset cTTP group (n = 33), most patients were of Caucasian/North African ethnicity (97%) with minimal consanguinity (3%). Comorbidities were infrequent, with only 1 case (3%) of mental health disorder. Seven women (21%) had a documented history of TMA or TTP: 6 occurred in another obstetrical context, and 1 was associated with an infectious event during adulthood and carried a homozygous p.Arg1060Trp mutation. Patients with adult-onset cTTP unrelated to pregnancy (n = 13) were older (median age, 55 years; IQR, 35-65) and exclusively of Caucasian/North African ethnicity; consanguinity was rare (n = 1 [8%]). Comorbidities and cardiovascular risk factors were more prevalent in this group.

All patients of the 3 groups presented with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and severe thrombocytopenia, and an infectious triggering factor was a frequent event. Neurological involvement at presentation was observed in all 3 groups, but ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack and confusion were markedly prevalent in adult-onset cTTP unrelated to pregnancy; similarly, serum creatinine level was increased in this group (P = .005). In all 3 groups, emergency treatment consisted of plasma therapy, either through plasma infusion or therapeutic plasma exchange, and red blood cell transfusions; fetal extraction was reported in 19 patients (58%) with pregnancy-onset cTTP. Patients received various other therapies, mostly immunosuppressive agents, before the final diagnosis of cTTP. No fatalities occurred after the index episode (Tables 1 and 2).

Prophylactic treatment during follow-up

After the index episode, and according to the French guidelines,33 2 prophylactic strategies were used: regular FFP infusion since 2000; and more recently rhADAMTS13 (TAK755) perfusion starting in 2020 (supplemental Figure 2).

FFP-based prophylaxis

FFP prophylaxis was administered at regular intervals (weekly, every 2 weeks, or monthly) with the aim to prevent hemolysis, cytopenia, and overt TTP episodes. In the pediatric- and adult-onset groups, long-term FFP prophylaxis 10 to 15 mL/kg was generally given every 2 weeks (20/42 [49%] and 6/13 [46%], respectively). In the pediatric-onset group, vaccinations were managed with FFP infusions, and FFP prophylaxis was intensified to once or twice weekly during infections or pregnancy (5 patients and 8 pregnancies). In contrast, for patients with pregnancy-onset TTP, FFP prophylaxis was limited to midterm administration during subsequent pregnancies and consisted of FFP prophylaxis 20 mL/kg every 2 weeks during the first trimester, weekly during the second trimester, and 20 to 30 mL/kg or therapeutic plasma exchange once to twice weekly during the third trimester. In the postpartum period, FFP of 10 to 20 mL/kg weekly for 2 to 3 weeks was usually administered.19 Twenty patients carried 24 pregnancies to term with midterm FFP prophylaxis.

Plasma-related adverse events were reported in 31 patients (35%), including anaphylaxis or severe allergic reactions (n = 4), transfusion-related acute lung injury (n = 2), volume overload and respiratory complications (n = 3), cutaneous or minor allergic reactions (n = 19), and other general adverse effects, such as headache, nausea, or malaise (n = 6). No cases of alloimmunization against ADAMTS13 were observed.

rhADAMTS13-based prophylaxis

rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis 40 IU/kg was administered weekly or every 2 weeks. By the end of the recruitment period, 39 patients were treated with rhADAMTS13. Among them, 36 had transitioned to long-term rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis 40 IU/kg every 1 or 2 weeks: 28 with pediatric-onset cTTP and 8 with adult-onset cTTP, with a median follow-up period of 5 months (IQR, 6-17). On June 2025, the prophylaxis group comprised 4 patients aged <12 years, 3 patients aged 12 to 18, and 29 adult patients aged 18 to 85 years.

Adverse events associated with rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis were exclusively minor, the most frequently reported being headaches and mild gastrointestinal symptoms. No cases of alloimmunization against ADAMTS13 were observed.

We conducted serial measurements of ADAMTS13 activity during rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis to evaluate peak and residual activity, as well as the potential influence of the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) on residual levels. Administration of rhADAMTS13 40 IU/kg led to a rapid peak of plasma ADAMTS13 activity, typically observed 1 hour after injection (supplemental Results 1). In 1 patient monitored on 3 separate periods, ADAMTS13 activity was still detectable up to day 5 after injection (range, 8-13 IU/dL) but became undetectable by day 7. These results were consistent across the 3 monitored periods (supplemental Figure 3). Similarly, residual ADAMTS13 activity after rhADAMTS13 injections was consistently undetectable (<10 IU/dL) by day 14 (supplemental Figures 3 and 4).34 In 1 patient with cTTP with kidney failure treated with rhADAMTS13, the monitoring of ADAMTS13 activity reported peak and residual activity levels comparable to those observed in patients with normal GFR, suggesting that reduced GFR may not influence rhADAMTS13 clearance (not shown).

Relapse-free survival

The median follow-up was 17.3 years (IQR, 10.0-22.5) in the pediatric-onset group, 5.2 years (IQR, 3.5-9.1) in the pregnancy-onset group, and 2.3 years (IQR, 1.1 to not applicable) in the adult-onset group. A total of 76 acute events were reported (65 in the pediatric-onset group, 4 in the pregnancy-onset group during subsequent pregnancies only, and 7 in the adult-onset group), along with 9 subacute events (6, 0, and 3, respectively) and 62 isolated TTP manifestations (51, 5, and 6, respectively). Sixty-one events occurred in patients receiving no prophylaxis (48, 5, and 8, respectively, in the 3 groups), 80 in those with FFP prophylaxis (69, 4, and 7, respectively), and 6 in those on rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis (5, 0, and 1, respectively; supplemental Table 3). Clinical features associated with acute events were central nervous system involvement (headache, confusion, convulsion, focal deficiency, transient ischemic attack, or ischemic stroke), abdominal pain, nausea, diarrhea, and cardiac damage (ischemic, arrhythmic, or valvular cardiomyopathy, conduction disorders, cardiac hypertrophy, pericarditis, and myocardial fibrosis). Pediatric- and adult-onset groups were at higher risk of relapse during follow-up, with a risk persisting over time in the pediatric-onset group (Figure 2).

Among 20 women with pregnancy-onset cTTP, TTP-related events occurred only in the context of pregnancy (supplemental Table 5). A total of 26 subsequent pregnancies were reported. Of these, 24 were carried out under midterm FFP prophylaxis, and 22 resulted in live births. Other outcomes included 3 early spontaneous miscarriages, 1 voluntary termination, and 1 acute event resulting in miscarriage despite prophylaxis with high volumes of FFP. rhADAMTS13 was used as midterm prophylaxis in 2 patients. In the first, due to intolerance to FFP, the patient received rhADAMTS13 injections every 10 days during the first half of pregnancy and weekly thereafter. In the second, rhADAMTS13 was administered weekly throughout pregnancy as a first-line prophylactic strategy. This patient developed late-preterm preeclampsia at 35 weeks of gestation, with favorable outcome with continued rhADAMTS13 treatment and delivery induction.34 Both cases showed no adverse event and had favorable maternal and fetal outcomes. Prophylaxis was discontinued during the postpartum period. Only 2 patients underwent pregnancies without prophylaxis: one led to a voluntary termination, and the other experienced a cTTP relapse. During long-term follow-up, none of these patients experienced biological or clinical symptoms outside of pregnancy. One patient received short-term prophylaxis with rhADAMTS13 in the context of surgery.

In adult-onset cTTP, all acute events but 1 occurred in the absence of FFP prophylaxis (supplemental Table 6).

The median follow-up was 2.0 years (IQR, 0-5.4) for patients in the no treatment group, 6.9 years (IQR, 0-14) for those who received FFP prophylaxis, and 0.8 years (IQR, 0.1-1.5) for those on rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis. After a 15-year follow-up for FFP prophylaxis and a 4-year follow-up for rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis, patients in both pediatric- and adult-onset groups had comparable outcomes with the 2 therapeutic strategies regarding acute events (Figure 4).

Comorbidity-free survival

Despite prophylactic treatment, patients with cTTP experienced a range of complications and comorbidities with a variable distribution and severity among the 3 groups (Table 3; supplemental Figure 5).

Cardiac complications specifically occurred in the pediatric- and adult-onset groups outside pregnancy (Table 3; Figure 5A). The cumulative risk of ischemic stroke was highest in the adult-onset group (2/13 [15%]) but persisted in the pregnancy-onset group during short-term follow-up (1/33 [3%] within 5 years of the index episode) and in the pediatric-onset group during long-term follow-up (3/42 [7%] after 10 years; Table 3; Figure 5B). Mental health complications were reported in 21% to 46% of patients across all 3 groups. These ranged from depressive disorder to post-traumatic stress and neurocognitive disorders. Symptoms including depressive disorders, generalized anxiety disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, and neurocognitive disorders as memory loss appeared early in adult- and pregnancy-onset patients and later in pediatric-onset cases (Table 3; Figure 5C).

A trend was observed toward improved QoL under long-term rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis compared to FFP between baseline and month 1 or month 3, likely reflecting the small sample size and limited follow-up period (supplemental Figure 6).

One death was reported in the pregnancy-onset group, several years after the first TTP episode, unrelated to any obstetric event.

Discussion

Our 25-year study offers a comprehensive overview of cTTP based on a well-characterized cohort of 88 patients. By stratifying patients into pediatric-, pregnancy-, and adult-onset groups, our findings reveal the genetic heterogeneity of cTTP with key divergent clinical phenotypes, including age at onset and long-term outcomes: all pediatric-onset cases and most pregnancy- and adult-onset cases displayed biallelic deleterious mutations in the ADAMTS13 gene, with a high prevalence of p.Arg1060Trp variant or other less deleterious variants typically found in late-onset cases.35 Patients with pediatric-onset cTTP showed significant consanguinity5,7 and were prone to develop neurological symptoms, with infections often triggering TTP episodes. In contrast, pregnancy-onset patients presented primarily with neurological and abdominal symptoms without cardiac features, whereas adult-onset patients had more cardiovascular comorbidities and reduced GFR, likely reflecting delayed diagnosis and age-related risk factors. These differences underscore the need to stratify patients with cTTP but also the importance of long-term prophylaxis in both pediatric- and adult-onset groups. In contrast, based on our 25 years of experience, patients with pregnancy-onset cTTP, who do not experience TTP symptoms outside of specific conditions associated with increased VWF levels, may only require midterm prophylaxis during pregnancy or short-term prophylaxis in high-risk situations, rather than long-term prophylaxis.35

Although we observed no significant difference between FFP and rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis, our analysis demonstrates a clear improvement in the relapse-free survival with both prophylactic strategies. The comparable outcome between FFP and rhADAMTS13 strategies here could result from our historically aggressive FFP prophylaxis from the index event or upon clinical suspicion of cTTP in pediatric-onset patients36,37; moreover, rhADAMTS13 has only recently been introduced into clinical use, and therefore, follow-up duration is still limited. However, TTP-related manifestations in this therapeutic group remained rare and consistent with previous experience in which acute episodes could be observed in pediatric-onset cTTP despite rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis.24,38

Early reports of untreated pediatric patients demonstrated a high burden of organ dysfunction through follow-up, emphasizing the critical need for long-term ADAMTS13 prophylaxis from childhood.25 Nevertheless, despite FFP prophylaxis, a substantial number of patients in our cohort experienced organ dysfunction and mental health issues ranging from depressive disorder to post-traumatic stress and memory loss.8,27 Pediatric-onset patients remain at risk of relapse over time despite long-term FFP prophylaxis, and adult-onset patients showed a higher prevalence of cardiac comorbidities and ischemic events. In that regard, platelet count, anemia, and features of hemolysis were historically used as a basis to define the schedule of plasma therapy. However, whether their normalization reflects a sufficient protection to prevent long-term organ dysfunction remains unknown, and future studies should address surrogate markers in combination with cardiovascular risk factors and other comorbidities for a more optimized and personalized organ protection in these patients. Of note, however, the incidence of cardiovascular and neurological complications in our cohort appears lower than reported elsewhere,8,27 which may again reflect earlier therapeutic interventions and more intensive prophylactic modalities but also differences in genetic background.

Although FFP prophylaxis has historically been the mainstay of treatment, our results highlight that this strategy remains associated with substantial limitations, including allergic reactions, volume overload, and the logistical burden of regular infusions, particularly in children.22 In our study, the persistence of TTP-related manifestations despite long-term FFP prophylaxis suggests suboptimal disease control with FFP-based treatment. In that regard, the emergence of rhADAMTS13 offers a promising alternative.24 Notably, the use of rhADAMTS13, as well as FFP for a prolonged time, did not translate in alloimmunization against ADAMTS13, suggesting that the risk of developing alloantibodies with rhADAMTS13 is very low, probably due to structural homology between ADAMTS13 and other ADAMTS proteases; alternatively, the low residual ADAMTS13 levels associated with most genotypes may promote tolerance.22, 23, 24,39,40 In the continuation study, rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis demonstrated both efficacy and safety in pediatric patients with cTTP, consistent with findings in adults and adolescents, with dose adjustments only based on body weight for all patients.40 Adverse events observed during rhADAMTS13 prophylaxis were limited to minor side effects, in contrast to several cases of anaphylaxis and transfusion-related acute lung injury reported under FFP. Moreover, convenient home administration should enhance long-term compliance and thereby its ability to prevent relapses and long-term organ dysfunction.24,39

After the infusion of 1 or 2 units of FFP, ADAMTS13 activity typically increases to only 10% to 20% of normal levels, falling below 5 IU/dL within 3 to 8 days.41 Pharmacokinetic studies provided evidence that rhADAMTS13 offers superior enzyme replacement, enhancing clinical outcomes and significantly reducing treatment burden, with higher peak ADAMTS13 activity (101 IU/dL vs 19 IU/dL) and longer sustained enzymes levels, including in pregnant women and patients with kidney failure.24,39,42 Given previous pharmacokinetic studies and our observation that ADAMTS13 activity remains undetectable at day 14 after the last rhADAMTS13 injection in both pediatric- and adult-onset patients, a biweekly dosing regimen may be insufficient to maintain an optimal disease control, particularly during intercurrent events such as infections or pregnancy, in which individualized monitoring and therapeutic adjustments or intensification are warranted.33 In the setting of pregnancy, in which 2 of our patients experienced features of relapse despite prophylaxis, we suggest a more intensive prophylaxis consisting of weekly injections from diagnosis, followed by twice-weekly injections during the third trimester.

Our study has several limitations, including the retrospective nature of the data collection, which may have led to incomplete documentation of clinical or subclinical events and isolated manifestations. Although the registry spans >25 years, long-term follow-up data on end organ damage and QoL remain incomplete for many patients. These aspects now require systematic and prospective data documentation, in addition to ongoing retrospective collection, to give a more comprehensive understanding of the disease course and long-term outcomes.

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of cTTP and highlights the importance of tailored diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. It also provides important insights into the long-term management of cTTP and supports rhADAMTS13 as an effective prophylactic strategy. As the therapeutic landscape evolves, future efforts should focus on personalized management, earlier diagnosis, and long-term monitoring of cardiovascular, kidney, and neuropsychiatric outcomes. The integration of rhADAMTS13 into routine care should substantially improve QoL and long-term prognosis for patients with this rare and complex disorder.
