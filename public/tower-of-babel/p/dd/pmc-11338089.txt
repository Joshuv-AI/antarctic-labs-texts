A rare case of anterior sternal metastasis in ovarian cancer: A case report
Authors: Chaimae Lahlou, Badr Kabila, Ihssan Hadj Hssain, Mouna Khmou, Youssef Omor, Rachida Latib, Basma El Khannoussi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

They are usually a late manifestation that arises several years after the initial diagnosis and often serve as a poor prognostic indicator. Introduction

Subcutaneous metastases are uncommon locations for metastasis, occurring with an incidence of 5.3%. The most common primary tumor associated with subcutaneous metastases is breast cancer [1].

Common sites of metastasis in ovarian cancer include the pleura, liver, lungs, and lymph nodes However, ovarian cancers typically remain confined to the peritoneal cavity and rarely metastasize to the skin [2].

We present an unusual case in which a presternal lobulated mass was indicative of a serous epithelial adenocarcinoma of the ovary.

Case report

A 57-year-old patient with no prior medical history presented for a right pelvic pain associated with fatigue, anorexia, and weight loss. Gynecologic pelvic exam revealed a tender pelvic mass. Serum carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) was elevated at 650 U/mL (reference range: <35 U/mL), while the rest of the laboratory tests were within normal limits.

A pelvic MRI identified a rounded, right-lateralized ovarian mass with both solid and cystic components. The solid portion showed an intermediate T2 signal, restricted diffusion, and a type 2 enhancement curve. The patient underwent a hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, with histopathological analysis revealing a serous ovarian adenocarcinoma. The patient received 6 cycles of chemotherapy (Paclitaxel - Carboplatin).

The patient was lost to follow-up for 5 years, then presented with a slightly painful subcutaneous mass in the presternal region, which had been developing for 2 months. Over time, the mass gradually increased in size, becoming harder and more painful, prompting the patient to seek medical attention.

A thoraco-abdomino-pelvic CT scan was performed to characterize this mass and search for secondary sites. It showed an enhancing ovoid lobulated presternal soft tissue lesion with calcifications. Discussion

Ovarian cancer stands out as the most lethal gynecological malignancy due to the development of cancerous metastases in a majority of patients. These metastases often occur in the pleura, liver, lungs, and lymph nodes [1].

In men, the primary cancer that most commonly leads to subcutaneous metastases is melanoma, followed by lung cancer. However, in women, it ranks lower than breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and ovarian cancer in terms of frequency [2]. The chest is the most common site for subcutaneous metastases (28%), followed by the abdominal wall (20%), extremities (12%), neck (11%), back (11%), scalp (7%), pelvis (6%), and face (5%) [3].

Subcutaneous metastases originating from ovarian cancer are rare occurrences, typically emerging in the later stages of the disease. While they may occasionally present as the initial sign, they are found in only a small percentage of patients, ranging from 0.9% to 6% [1]. The most common type of subcutaneous metastatic carcinoma from ovarian cancer is serous epithelial adenocarcinoma [4].

These metastases are classified based on their location into Sister Joseph's nodules (SJN) when located at the umbilicus, and other subcutaneous metastases. They can also be categorized by their timing, either occurring at the initial diagnosis or as subsequent recurrences [5].

Most documented cases of subcutaneous metastases from ovarian cancer involve Sister Joseph's nodules (SJN), named after Sister Joseph, a nurse who collaborated with Dr. William Mayo at St. Mary's Hospital in Rochester. SJN typically appear in patients with gynecological and gastrointestinal cancers, often indicating an advanced stage of ovarian cancer and are usually discovered either during the initial diagnosis or as a sign of recurrence [6].

Subcutaneous metastases not classified as SJN commonly appear as recurrences. These can manifest in various locations, with the abdominal wall being the most common site [7]. Additionally, metastases may affect the groin, external genitalia, and thoracic wall, presenting as single or multiple nodules, cicatricial plaques, or inflammatory lesions [8].

Diagnosing non-SJN subcutaneous metastases before detecting ovarian cancer poses a challenge, especially when typical ovarian cancer symptoms are absent. These metastases often arise within surgical scars, necessitating a detailed history of past surgeries and chemotherapy. Confirming the diagnosis requires an ultrasound-guided biopsy of the skin lesion for histological confirmation [9].

For asymptomatic patients with subcutaneous metastases and an unknown primary tumor, The PET scan is the best method for making a definitive diagnosis for identifying the primary sites. In cases where ovarian cancer is diagnosed, imaging helps assess lesion size and explore additional locations [9].

Survival rates following the diagnosis of subcutaneous metastases from ovarian cancer vary between 3 to 18 months, influenced by factors including metastasis location, presence of additional metastases, time gap between diagnosis and recurrence, and overall patient health, In principle, the appearance of a secondary subcutaneous localization, regardless of its location, remains a poor prognostic factor [10].

Treatment options for subcutaneous metastases depend on factors such as patient fitness and tumor characteristics. Surgery is the preferred initial treatment for medically fit patients, while chemotherapy may be considered for those ineligible for surgery, with potential for later surgical intervention [11].

Patients identified with SJN should undergo standard treatment for advanced ovarian cancer, including cytoreductive surgery with SJN resection and adjuvant platinum/taxane chemotherapy, optionally with bevacizumab. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is considered for extensive abdominal metastases and ascites [7].

A personalized approach to management is crucial for patients with different types of subcutaneous metastases. Systemic chemotherapy is recommended for non-SJN metastases and recurrent cases [12].

Conclusion

In the past, skin metastases were often linked to a less positive outlook in medical terms. Yet, for individuals with ovarian cancer facing skin metastases, the situation is not always bleak. Subcutaneous metastases typically appear late, following a prolonged progression of the disease. When it comes to choosing the right treatment, it's important to consider the exact location of the skin metastasis and whether there are other metastases present or not.
