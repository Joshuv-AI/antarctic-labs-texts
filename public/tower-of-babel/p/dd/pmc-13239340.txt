Impact of Socioeconomic Disparities Among Pediatric Airway and Otology Patients on Access to Care
Authors: Lauren A Gardiner, Amber D Shaffer, Nanki Hura, Nicholas R Oberlies, Jennifer L McCoy, David H Chi, Jeffrey P Simons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveSocioeconomic disparities are ubiquitous in pediatric otolaryngology, yet there is no literature comparing barriers to care for patients who are referred for hearing and airway‐related complaints. This study investigates the differences in obstacles to care between the pediatric otology and airway patient cohorts and how they influence patient access and compliance.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary children's hospital. All new patients seen in the otology or airway specialty clinics in 2019 were included. Area deprivation index, number of adults and children in the residence, insurance status, guardianship, distance traveled to the specialty clinic, referral status, follow up recommendations, and adherence to treatment recommendations and follow up were collected.ResultsThree hundred and sixty three new patients were identified (160 [44.1%] otology, 203 [55.9%] airway). Otology patients were more likely to have a guardian other than the biological parent compared to airway patients (13.1% vs. 4.4%, p = 0.004). Airway patients were significantly younger at the time of evaluation compared to otology patients. Referral from primary care or other specialty clinics was more likely in otology patients (77.6% vs. 64.8%, p = 0.014). Airway patients were more likely to have at least one no‐show compared to otology patients (15.8% vs. 6.9%; p = 0.011).ConclusionsOtology patients were more likely to be referred to the specialty clinic, have fewer no‐shows, and attend a higher number of follow‐up visits despite having minimal differences in social demographics. Future study targeting follow‐up and retention for airway clinic patients is needed.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: area deprivation index, patient access, pediatric airway, pediatric otology, socioeconomic disparities

Introduction

Socioeconomic disparities can derive from a wide range of factors, including income, education level, health insurance status, geographic location, and cultural background. These variables often intersect and compound, resulting in complex and multifaceted healthcare disparities that are difficult to quantify and thus target for improvement. Low socioeconomic status (SES) has been associated with poorer health outcomes amongst pediatric patients with otolaryngology conditions [1]. Specifically, low SES has been associated with higher rates of prematurity, low birth weight, and congenital anomalies [2]. Within pediatric airway literature, there is also evidence to support that children with tracheostomies with more deprived Area Deprivation Index (ADI) have significantly longer hospital admissions and higher hospital‐associated costs [3, 4, 5].

Non‐Caucasian race is a well‐supported barrier to healthcare access. In a study by Bhattacharyya et al., Black and Hispanic patients with frequent ear infections were more likely to not be able to afford treatment and not seek specialist evaluation, as well as more likely to present to an emergency department [6]. Regarding health insurance status, in the early 2000s, the number of children with private insurance declined with a concomitant increase in children with public insurance. Although more children overall were insured, a gap remained in access and use of health care resources [1, 7].

Pediatric otologic diseases have garnered great attention on a national level, with health inequities well described for both medical and surgical pathologies [8]. In a scoping review by Urban et al., disparities in diagnosis and treatment of pediatric hearing loss represented the largest subset of publications within pediatric otolaryngology [9]. In contrast, there is much less attention on disparities within advanced pediatric airway disease. The reason for this underrepresentation in the literature is unclear. Furthermore, the airway and otology populations are impacted by unique risk factors, with patients with prematurity comprising a larger portion of the complex airway population while those seen in the otology clinic are more likely to be otherwise healthy and/or be impacted by a genetic etiology.

Our aim was to investigate differences in care access and utilization between the unique groups of pediatric otolaryngology patients, specifically those with primary hearing and airway complaints seen in our specialty clinics. Our objectives were to characterize medical characteristics of these two cohorts, investigate differences in SES as measured by the ADI between these specialty clinic populations and to evaluate referral patterns, treatment and follow up adherence, such that we may be able to identify modifiable factors to promote health equity and improve treatment outcomes.

Materials and Methods

A protocol was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Human Research Protection Office's Institutional Review Board (STUDY20060022). A waiver of informed consent was received for this chart review study. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Figure 1. Both the otology and airway center clinics were located at the main Children's Hospital campus, and the protocols for text/call appointment confirmations were identical for both clinics. The otology center at our tertiary care children's hospital had 570 encounters. After exclusion of encounters with return patients, patients over the age of 18, and new patients who were inadvertently scheduled in either specialty clinic, i.e., for an unrelated chief complaint, 211 patients remained. An additional 51 patients were excluded based on the above criteria and not residing at the primary address listed. The otology specialty clinic focuses on pediatric patients with complex otologic pathology utilizing a multidisciplinary approach between otolaryngologists and audiologists in a one‐day visit and includes the institution's cochlear implant program. The four most common International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐10 codes for the otology cohort were H90 (n = 100; 85 sensorineural, 12 conductive, and 3 mixed hearing loss), H65 (nonsuppurative otitis media, n = 8), H91 (other and unspecified hearing loss, n = 8), H93 (other disorders of ear, not elsewhere classified, including tinnitus, n = 8), and R42 (vertigo not otherwise specified, n = 8).

Flow‐chart of inclusion and exclusion criteria for airway and otology patients.

The airway center had 1495 encounters in 2019. The airway specialty clinic focuses on pediatric patients with complex airway pathology utilizing a multidisciplinary approach between otolaryngologists and speech language pathologists in a one‐day visit. After exclusion of encounters with return patients, patients over the age of 18, and new patients who were inadvertently scheduled in either specialty clinic, i.e., for an unrelated chief complaint, 508 patients remained. These patients were randomized using a random sequence generator, and 240 randomly selected charts were reviewed. This sample size was selected based on a power analysis with data from Powers et al. in which no‐shows occurred in 2.9% of the least‐deprived decile (ADI 1) and an ADI decile of 10 was associated with 3.17 odds of having at least one no show compared with an ADI of 1. Based on this effect size, 240 patients were required in each group for α = 0.05 and power = 0.80 [10]. An additional 37 patients were excluded based on the above criteria and not residing at the primary address listed. The airway specialty clinic serves patients with aerodigestive complaints which typically necessitate endoscopic evaluation, speech language pathology assessments, and potential interventions not offered by all otolaryngology providers, including endoscopic and open airway procedures. The most common ICD‐10 codes for the airway cohort were R13 (aphagia and dysphagia, n = 68), R06 (abnormalities of breathing, n = 57), Q31 (congenital malformations of larynx, n = 56), and K21 (gastro‐esophageal reflux disease, n = 41).

Demographics and clinical variables included age at appointment, biological sex, race, ethnicity, prenatal care, delivery complications, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay, congenital syndromes, cardiac and pulmonary history, newborn hearing screen (NBHS) results, evaluation by other subspecialties, otologic and airway surgeries, and follow‐up recommendations at the index visit. Potential barriers assessed included ADI (national percentiles), number of adults and children in the residence, insurance status (public only or private ± public), guardianship [biological parent or other (adopted, foster parent, state representative, or other relative)], distance traveled to the specialty clinic (shortest distance in Google maps), and referral status (self‐referred or physician referred). Days from referral to clinic visit, number of canceled and no‐show appointments, and adherence to treatment recommendations on follow‐up were collected as indicators of access challenges. This data was collected over a two‐year period from the initial date of service (DOS). Continuous data are reported as mean (95% confidence interval, CI). Categorical demographic characteristics, referral status, recommended follow‐up, and surgery completion of otology and airway patients were compared using Fisher's exact test. Continuous demographic characteristics were compared using Mann–Whitney U. Differences in no‐show, canceled, and completed appointments between otology and airway centers were assessed using logistic regression. The primary comparison was the Mann–Whitney U test assessing the difference in ADI between patients seen in otology and airway clinics. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY) and Stata/SE 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 363 patients were included, of whom 160 patients (44.1%) were from the otology center and 203 (55.9%) were from the airway center (Table 1). Mean age at clinic visit was 5.96 (95% CI: 5.11–6.82) years whereas airway patients were a mean of 1.54 (95% CI: 1.07–2.00) years of age (z = −9.32, p < 0.001). Overall, patients resided in areas with national ADI ranging from 7 to 100. Otology and airway patients spanned 120 and 132 zip codes, respectively. The mean distance from the hospital was 42.25 (95% CI: 35.77–48.73) miles and 43.81 (95% CI: 37.97–49.65) miles for airway and otology patients, respectively. No private insurance was reported for 101/203 (49.8%) airway patients compared to 69/160 (43.1%) otology patients (p = 0.244). Otology patients were significantly more likely to have a guardian other than the biological parent compared to airway patients (21/160, 13.1% vs. 9/203, 4.4%, p = 0.004). However, in multivariable logistic regression, younger age (OR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.76–0.85, p < 0.001) was significantly associated with otology patients, while the association with having a guardian other than the biological parent was no longer significant (OR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.18–1.12, p = 0.087). Amongst the subset of patients with referral information available (323/363, 89.0% of cohort), airway patients were less likely to be referred to a specialty care clinic than otology patients (114/176, 64.8% vs. 114/147, 77.6%, p = 0.014). Otology patients were recommended to follow up more than airway patients (115/160, 71.9% vs. 117/203, 57.6%, p = 0.006); however, airway patients were significantly more likely to have surgery recommended (45/206, 22.2% vs. 17/160, 10.6%, p = 0.005) and completed (68/203, 33.5% vs. 27/160, 16.9%, p < 0.001) within 2 years of initial evaluation (Figure 2).

Referral, follow‐up, and treatment patterns between airway and otology patients.

Fewer airway center patients compared with otology patients completed a subsequent visit with any specialty within the 2 years following presentation (177/203, 87.2% vs. 152/160, 95.0%, OR: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.16–0.81, p = 0.014) (Figure 3). However, the percentages of patients with at least one no‐show or cancellation to any specialty were comparable between the two centers. With regards to follow‐up specifically in otolaryngology, airway center patients were more likely to have at least one no‐show compared to otology center patients (32/203, 15.8% vs. 11/160, 6.9%; OR: 2.53, 95% CI: 1.23–5.20, p = 0.011) (Figure 4). There were no significant differences in the odds of cancellation or completion of otolaryngology follow‐up between the two centers.

National ADI and distance from the hospital were not significantly different between otology and airway clinics. However, in the total cohort, mean ADI was significantly greater for those with at least one no‐show to otolaryngology compared with those with none (70.67, 95% CI: 62.73–78.62 vs. 61.38, 95% CI: 68.81–6.96, p = 0.007). For otology patients (but not airway), patients with at least one canceled appointment came from lived farther from the hospital compared with those with no cancellations (mean 50.57, 95% CI: 38.75–62.40 miles vs. mean 38.35, 95% CI: 30.60–46.11 miles, p = 0.026). Meanwhile, mean age at clinic visit was younger in those with at least one canceled (2.55, 95% CI: 1.82–3.29 vs. 4.03, 95% CI: 3.36–4.71, p = 0.002) or completed follow‐up (2.98, 95% CI: 2.41–3.55 vs. 4.65, 95% CI: 3.63–5.68, p = 0.003) appointment with otolaryngology compared with those with none. Meanwhile, younger age, but not center, ADI, or distance from the hospital, was associated with canceled (OR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99, p = 0.030) and completed (OR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99, p = 0.013) follow‐up visits.

Discussion

Socioeconomic disparities in healthcare have been widely recognized as significant contributors to unequal access to medical services, differences in treatment outcomes, and variations in patient experiences. In the field of pediatric otolaryngology, socioeconomic factors play a crucial role in shaping the healthcare landscape for children with conditions related to otology and airway disorders—both unique populations with nuanced care needs and barriers.

Access to early and timely diagnostic services for pediatric hearing loss heavily depends on the primary care provider's (PCP) management of a referred NBHS, knowledge of early hearing detection and intervention programs (EHDI), and referral services. The EHDI recommends screening by 1 month of age, diagnostic audiological and medical evaluation by 3 months of age, and initiation of state provided early intervention developmental services by 6 months of age [11]. Despite no change in such recommendations since 2000 and overall excellent national compliance rates with NBHS (> 97%), the U.S. Department of Health cites that only 61% of infants who did not pass NBHS were evaluated for hearing loss by 3 months of age in 2020 [12, 13]. In a study by McInerney et al., negative predictive factors for follow up in babies at higher risk for hearing loss included admission to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) greater than 5 days, younger maternal age, and failing the NBHS [14]. Furthermore, younger age, race, and public insurance has been associated with lower appointment compliance. Multiple studies have shown patients residing in rural areas have poor follow‐up for referred NBHS in a setting where rural PCPs (46% of survey respondents) are less confident in managing NBHS follow up [15, 16]. With regard to hearing loss management, public insurance was associated with delays to cochlear implantation (CI) and lower rates of CI implantation in Black/Hispanic patients, while low SES was associated with delays in hearing amplification fitting and poor follow up [1]. In the present study, age at initial visit was significantly older for otology (mean 5.96 years) compared with airway (mean 1.54 years) patients. In the airway cohort, this reflects the preponderance of early airway pathologies such as laryngomalacia and care for tracheostomy‐dependent infants. In the otology cohort, this reflects the patients who presented with delayed‐onset hearing loss detected after school age. This is supported by the fact that 100/160 (62.5%) of patients presented with sensorineural hearing loss while only 51/160 (41.5%) referred on the NBHS. In addition, otology clinic treats patients with diagnoses such as tinnitus and vertigo, which present more commonly in older children and adolescents than in younger children. However, future studies should identify how many patients in this clinic have delayed referral following failed NBHS and assess our management against EHDI benchmarks. Mean age at initial visit for patients who referred on the NBHS was 2.06 years (95% CI 1.02–3.10 years). Assessing the timing and results of diagnostic audiograms and interventions such as hearing aid fittings that occurred prior to presentation to otology clinic was outside of the scope of this study, but future studies focusing on the care pathway following NBHS referral could guide quality improvement efforts.

There is little data relating to socioeconomic disparities amongst pediatric otolaryngology airway conditions. There is some information in the literature on pediatric sleep disordered breathing, tracheostomy, and laryngotracheoplasty outcomes. In a study by Kou et al., community disadvantage was associated with higher grades of airway stenosis in pediatric patients and longer times to successful decannulation in children requiring expansion airway surgery [17]. In addition, ADI has been shown to correlate with longer times to decannulation and increased hospital costs in children with tracheostomies [5]. For instance, Smith et al. found that time to decannulation was 10 months longer in children born into families of lower SES [18]. Interestingly, ADI has not been associated with tracheostomy‐related complications [5, 17].

Analysis of our data demonstrates that both the otology and airway center cohorts are relatively well matched in regards to patient demographics, with no significant difference in the number of parents or children in the household, distance from the specialty center, insurance status, ADI, or race/ethnicity between cohorts.

Our hospital provides care to individuals through age 26 years primarily in Western Pennsylvania but also in other regions of Pennsylvania, Maryland, and West Virginia. It is the only hospital with pediatric airway and otology specialty clinics within 160 miles. The patients in the present study traveled up to 60 miles. Although airway and otology patients traveled from comparable distances, living further away from the hospital was associated with visit cancellations only in otology patients. Qualitative approaches are well‐suited to examining reasons for cancellations in future studies and would illuminate whether the threshold for parents to cancel otology appointments due to logistical challenges such as travel is due to a perception of less urgency.

Despite comparable levels of deprivation as measured by ADI, airway patients were more likely to be self‐referred to the specialty clinic and 2.5 times more likely to have at least one no‐show appointment. Additional evidence revealed that otology patients were significantly older and more likely to be in the care of someone other than a biological parent at the time of visit compared to airway patients. The significant differences in barriers to care between subspecialty clinics are likely attributable to patient, provider, as well as systems‐based factors. For instance, while hearing‐related pathologies have garnered nationwide attention with significant progress on legislative regulations, early detection initiatives, and program funding, the same is not true for complex airway pathologies. This discrepancy suggests that significant progress can be made in improving access to care for airway patients through the implementation of initiatives, both at a national, local, and provider‐to‐patient level, beginning with increased education and awareness of these issues. Meanwhile, the propensity for airway patients to have earlier presentation and poorer follow‐up may reflect natural fluctuations in respiratory symptomatology such that follow‐up is not pursued when patients are healthy. There may also be barriers in coordination and transportation experienced by airway patients with oxygen, tracheostomy, and/or ventilator requirements typically not present in the otology cohort. Further investigation to elucidate the discrepancies seen between the cohorts could consist of a brief phone survey of patients who missed follow‐up appointments in hopes of identifying targets for future quality improvement efforts.

There are several limitations to this study. The nature of retrospective chart review introduces misclassification bias. There were 40 patients with missing referral information. We included all new patients in both clinics, which encompassed a wide range of otologic and airway conditions. This study is a cohort study and is not intended to draw conclusions or recommendations on specific disease states. All demographic data, with special attention to distance from hospital, census block group/ADI, and number of children/adults per household, were extrapolated at the time of initial visit. This design does not account for any change in such factors, as some studies have shown that children with high residential mobility have overall poorer physical health and more chronic conditions [19]. In this hypothesis‐generating study, many patient characteristics and outcome measures were assessed. The pre‐specified primary aim was to compare ADI between the two clinics. However, secondary comparisons described in the present study require replication in future work in order to differentiate between Type I errors and true differences. A strength of our study was that there were multiple subspecialty providers in both clinics. There may be inherent differences in practice patterns, though this factor may make our data regarding treatment/treatment adherence more generalizable. The airway center had one multi‐disciplinary aerodigestive clinic per month, which included providers from gastroenterology and pulmonology. This clinic introduces potential barriers such as a longer clinic visit but may also incentivize patient attendance given the convenience of seeing three providers in one visit, which may have confounded our comparisons. Finally, the prevalence of private insurance was not significantly different between specialty clinics. However, Pennsylvania provides full and free medical assistance to children with disabilities in households who would not otherwise meet income‐based eligibility for Medicaid coverage. The current findings may not be generalizable to other states without these programs and therefore with more non‐ or under‐insured children.

Understanding the extent and implications of socioeconomic disparities is crucial for developing targeted interventions and improving the overall quality of care for all pediatric patients. The selection of two distinct specialty clinics within otolaryngology afforded us the opportunity to compare barriers and how practice changes aimed at improving equity might be catered to the unique needs of different patient groups. For example, efforts at amplifying communication of the importance of timely evaluation for hearing concerns may improve completion of otology clinic appointments for patients who live further from the hospital and hasten the time to receipt of surgical interventions. Furthermore, we now have a designated nurse coordinator for airway patients. One purpose of this role is to reduce no‐shows and loss to follow‐up for this cohort. Future studies should also compare barriers to access to general pediatric otolaryngology clinic with those identified for specialty clinics to provide a more comprehensive view of the strengths and challenges of different care models. The field of otolaryngology can work towards ensuring equitable access to quality care, improving treatment outcomes, and ultimately enhancing the overall health and well‐being of all children with otologic and airway conditions.

Conclusion

Pediatric patients with hearing‐related conditions were more likely to be referred to the specialty clinic, have recommended follow‐up, and attend a higher number of follow‐up visits despite having minimal differences in social demographics compared to pediatric patients with advanced airway conditions. Future study targeting follow‐up and retention of airway clinic patients is needed.
