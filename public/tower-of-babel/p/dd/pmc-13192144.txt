Letrozole effectively treats peripheral precocious puberty in a girl with McCune-Albright syndrome: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Yutao Xie, Jinhua Huang, Zhixiang Zheng, Yanning Yang, Xiaoling Liang, Jieting Chen, Yina She, Yiwen Guo, Danling Guo, Meiqing Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS) is a rare disorder caused by somatic GNAS gene mutations, characterized by peripheral precocious puberty (PPP), café-au-lait spots, and fibrous dysplasia of bone (FD). This case report details a 3-year-and-10-month-old girl presenting with vaginal bleeding, scattered café-au-lait spots, elevated estradiol (E2: 57 pg/mL), and a large right ovarian cyst (72 × 44 × 71 mm). Diagnostic evaluations confirmed gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty without central endocrine abnormalities. The patient was diagnosed with MAS and treated with letrozole (2.5 mg/day), an aromatase inhibitor, under close monitoring. Over 13 months, letrozole therapy resulted in complete resolution of vaginal bleeding and ovarian cysts, suppression of estrogen levels (E2 < 15 pg/mL), and stabilization of bone age progression. Height and weight remained within normal percentiles, with no adverse effects observed. This case underscores the efficacy and safety of letrozole in managing MAS-associated PPP, highlighting its role in delaying skeletal maturation and improving clinical outcomes. Early intervention and multidisciplinary monitoring are critical to optimize long-term prognosis in MAS patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06800-y.

Introduction

McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS) is a rare somatic mosaic genetic disorder characterized by the classic triad of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, peripheral precocious puberty (PPP), and café-au-lait macules. Additional endocrine abnormalities, such as hyperthyroidism, Cushing’s syndrome, and growth hormone excess, may also occur in affected individuals [1, 2]. The estimated incidence of MAS is extremely low, approximately 1 in 100,000 to 1 in 1,000,000 individuals. Due to its heterogeneous clinical manifestations, early diagnosis remains challenging in many cases [3].

The pathogenesis of MAS is primarily attributed to post-zygotic activating mutations in the GNAS gene located on chromosome 20q13.3, which encodes the stimulatory G protein alpha-subunit (Gsα). The most common mutations involve the substitution of arginine at position 201 with either cysteine (Arg201Cys) or histidine (Arg201His) [4]. These mutations arise during early embryogenesis, resulting in somatic mosaicism in affected tissues. The constitutive activation of adenylyl cyclase leads to elevated cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels and persistent activation of protein kinase A (PKA), promoting autonomous hormone production and abnormal cellular proliferation [5]. In girls, the ovaries are frequently affected, forming autonomous functional ovarian cysts that secrete estrogen independent of pituitary gonadotropin stimulation, thus leading to peripheral precocious puberty [6, 7].

Although the molecular mechanism underlying MAS-associated PPP is well established, effective management remains challenging. Instead, aromatase inhibitors such as letrozole are preferred to suppress peripheral estrogen production, thereby slowing breast development and bone age advancement [8, 9]. However, due to the clinical heterogeneity of MAS and variable responses to therapy, long-term data regarding the efficacy and safety of aromatase inhibitors remain limited. This is especially true in pediatric populations, where standardized treatment protocols and adverse event monitoring guidelines are yet to be established.

Here, we report the case of a girl with MAS who was successfully treated with letrozole for PPP, with clinical follow-up over a 13-month period. Compared with previously published cases, this patient presented with an earlier onset of puberty, a large ovarian cyst initially considered for surgical removal, and no significant adverse effects during treatment. This case provides valuable insight into the clinical management of MAS-associated PPP and supports the potential role of letrozole in pediatric patients.

Case presentation

A 3-year-and-10-month-old girl presented to our hospital on March 16, 2023, with a 1-day history of vaginal bleeding. The patient was born full-term via spontaneous vaginal delivery, with a birth weight of 3.05 kg. Her mother’s height is 154 cm and her father’s height is 168 cm, yielding a mid-parental target height of 155 cm for the patient. Her mother denied any history of hormonal medication use during pregnancy. The patient was breastfed for approximately 7 months postnatally. Her developmental milestones and dietary habits were comparable to peers, with no history of trauma or estrogen medication exposure.

At admission, the vaginal bleeding was scant and bright red, without associated vulvar pain, abdominal discomfort, hematuria, or hematochezia.

On physical examination, her height was 105.2 cm (+ 1.0 standard deviation) and weight was 17.4 kg (+ 1.0 SD). Multiple café-au-lait spots were noted, measuring 1.2 × 0.8 cm on the left anterior thigh, 1.0 × 0.3 cm in the left lumbar region, and 0.5 × 0.2 cm on the left lateral thigh, all exhibiting light-brown pigmentation. Breast development was consistent with Tanner stage 2 and featured hyperpigmented areolae (Fig. 1). Pubic hair was at Tanner stage 1, and axillary hair was absent.

The diagnosis of peripheral precocious puberty secondary to McCune-Albright syndrome was ultimately established. This conclusion was based on the integration of key findings: characteristic clinical symptoms (vaginal bleeding and café-au-lait macules), physical signs (breast development at Tanner stage 2), and confirmatory diagnostic investigations. The latter included elevated estradiol levels in the context of gonadotropin suppression, the presence of ovarian cysts, and advanced bone age.

Treatment of diseases

After obtaining informed consent, treatment with letrozole (Zhejiang Hisun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) was initiated at a dose of 2.5 mg orally once daily. This dosage was selected based on previously published studies and tailored to the patient’s clinical condition [10] .Monitoring plan included: (1) Hormonal assays (E2, FSH, LH): monthly for the first 3 months, then every 3–6 months thereafter. (2) Liver and renal function tests: every 3 months. (3) Pelvic ultrasound: every 6 months to assess cyst size and uterine response. (4)Bone age assessment: annually. (5) Proactive adverse event assessment: at each visit, patients/caregivers were queried about bone or joint pain, hot flashes, headaches, and mood changes.

Throughout 13 months of follow-up, no laboratory or clinical evidence of hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or classical estrogen-suppression side effects (e.g., bone pain, arthralgia, vasomotor symptoms) was observed. At 6 months, minor labial adhesions developed; these were managed with topical steroid–antibiotic ointment and gentle manual separation, and were deemed unlikely to be drug-related given their localized nature and lack of systemic signs. The relatively short follow-up (13 months) is acknowledged as a limitation; longer observation is planned to assess final height, skeletal maturation, and pubertal progression.

Discussion

Diagnosis of MAS

McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS) is a rare disorder caused by somatic mutations in the GNAS gene during early embryonic development. It was first described by Dr. McCune and Dr. Albright in 1936 and 1937, respectively, and later named McCune-Albright syndrome. Although both males and females can be affected, the condition is more prevalent in females. MAS typically manifests during childhood, though neonatal onset has also been reported [11]. The classic triad of MAS includes peripheral precocious puberty (PPP), café-au-lait skin spots, and fibrous dysplasia of bone (FD) [12, 13]. However, only approximately 24% of patients present with all three features [14].

The clinical diagnosis of MAS is based on the presence of FD plus at least one additional major clinical feature or, in the absence of FD, at least two major clinical features [15, 16]. Major clinical features other than FD include: (1) Café-au-lait spots consistent with MAS, typically with irregular borders and unilateral distribution. (2) Non-GnRH-dependent precocious puberty in girls, often accompanied by recurrent ovarian cysts. In boys, it may present as autonomous testosterone production or testicular lesions detectable on ultrasound, with or without PPP. (3) Thyroid abnormalities on ultrasound, which may or may not be associated with non-autoimmune hyperthyroidism. (4) Growth hormone (GH) hypersecretion. (5) Neonatal Cushing’s syndrome due to ACTH-independent cortisol excess.

In patients with FD or the classic triad, the diagnosis is relatively straightforward, and genetic testing is not mandatory [1]. Notably, approximately 85% of female MAS patients develop functional ovarian cysts, leading to elevated estrogen levels and gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty [17].

In the present case, the diagnosis of MAS was established based on the presence of two major clinical features, specifically café-au-lait spots and gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty with recurrent ovarian cysts, which fulfills the current consensus diagnostic criteria [15, 16]. Although genetic confirmation through GNAS mutation analysis is a valuable tool, especially in atypical cases, it was not performed in this instance due to limitations in the availability of specialized genetic testing at our institution. It is important to note that the sensitivity of GNAS mutation detection is highly dependent on the tissue source and the extent of mosaicism, and a negative result from blood or leukocyte DNA does not exclude the diagnosis. Therefore, in classic presentations such as ours, a clinical diagnosis is considered sufficient to guide management [1, 15].

Current management of MAS

Fibrous Dysplasia (FD)

Patients with MAS often exhibit significantly elevated alkaline phosphatase levels, and those with phosphaturia may have reduced serum phosphate levels. Skeletal abnormalities typically emerge between 3 and 10 years of age, with the peak incidence of pathological fractures occurring between 6 and 10 years. Patients with phosphaturia tend to experience fractures earlier and more frequently [18]. Surgical intervention may be required for limb deformities or cranial lesions causing compressive symptoms [18]. Bisphosphonates, which inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, are effective in controlling the progression of fibrous dysplasia, alleviating bone pain, and reducing fracture rates.

Café-au-lait Spots

The characteristic skin lesions of MAS are café-au-lait spots, which typically appear on the back, waist, and buttocks. These spots often have irregular borders and usually do not cross the midline, although exceptions exist. The extent and location of pigmentation are not correlated with disease severity. Currently, there are no effective treatments for café-au-lait spots.

Precocious puberty

Precocious puberty in MAS exhibits significant gender differences, with girls being more commonly affected. Initial manifestations in girls often include breast development (Tanner stage B2–B3), vaginal bleeding, and ovarian cysts. Peripheral precocious puberty is confirmed by stimulation tests. Ovarian cysts are usually unilateral and may be misdiagnosed as malignancies, leading to unnecessary oophorectomy. If precocious puberty progresses, pharmacological intervention is necessary to prevent premature epiphyseal closure and compromised adult height. Currently, two classes of anti-estrogen drugs are used: (1) Estrogen receptor antagonists, such as tamoxifen, which compete with estradiol for estrogen receptors, thereby blocking estrogen action. However, these agents do not inhibit abnormal gonadal secretion. (2) Aromatase inhibitors (AIs), such as letrozole, which inhibit the conversion of androstenedione to estrone and testosterone to estradiol, thereby reducing systemic estrogen levels. By lowering estrogen concentrations in bone tissue, AIs delay epiphyseal closure [13].

Aromatase inhibitors are considered first-line therapy for MAS-associated precocious puberty due to their superior efficacy in improving final adult height compared to estrogen receptor antagonists [1]. Studies have shown that untreated girls with MAS often achieve adult heights below − 2 standard deviations (SD) of the normal population, whereas treated patients can reach heights within the normal range [19].

The therapeutic management of patients with MAS is only symptomatic and requires a multidisciplinary team with experience in pediatrics, endocrinology, orthopedics, pain management, and rehabilitation [13]. Girls with gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty are treated with aromatase inhibitors (letrozole), estrogen receptor modulators (tamoxifen), pure estrogen receptor antagonists (fulvestrant), and anti-androgens [13, 16, 20]. Tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator and has an effective anti-breast cancer effect. However, long-term use of tamoxifen can also lead to bone loss in premenopausal women and is associated with an increased risk of endometrial cancer in postmenopausal women [21]. Letrozole (a third-generation AI) directly inhibits aromatase, resulting in sustained reduction of estradiol, resolution of ovarian cysts, and consistent slowing of skeletal maturation [22]. Published series report minimal adverse events and superior height outcomes compared with tamoxifen [23]. Recent literature continues to support the use of letrozole, consistently demonstrating its effectiveness in slowing skeletal maturation and improving predicted adult height [24, 25]. A key long-term study by Estrada et al. on 28 girls with MAS showed that letrozole treatment significantly reduced the ratio of bone age advancement to chronological age advancement and improved predicted adult height Z-scores over a mean follow-up of six years [24]. Similarly, a retrospective study by Wang et al. on 23 girls confirmed these benefits, highlighting a significant reduction in skeletal maturation rate [25].

However, the therapeutic response to letrozole can be heterogeneous, particularly concerning its effect on vaginal bleeding and ovarian cysts. For instance, Wang et al. reported that while vaginal bleeding ceased in 40% of their cohort, it persisted or even worsened in others [25]. The impact on ovarian cysts is also variable, with some reports noting no significant change or even an increase in ovarian volume during treatment [24, 25]. In some cases, letrozole therapy has been associated with the development or enlargement of ovarian cysts, occasionally requiring clinical intervention [26, 27]. This variability underscores the complexity of managing MAS and the need for individualized treatment approaches.

The patient presented with vaginal bleeding, scattered café-au-lait spots on physical examination, and elevated estrogen levels accompanied by an ovarian cyst on diagnostic imaging. No evidence of central precocious puberty or other endocrine abnormalities was observed, confirming the diagnosis of McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS). Early intervention with letrozole was initiated to prevent further progression of precocious puberty, representing an off-label use of the drug. Throughout the treatment period, the patient underwent regular monitoring of liver and renal function, pelvic ultrasonography, and bone age assessments. Following letrozole administration, the patient experienced complete resolution of vaginal bleeding and ovarian cysts, along with a significant reduction in estrogen levels. No adverse effects, such as nausea, vomiting, headache, bone pain, or rash, were reported. Routine blood tests and liver/renal function tests remained within normal limits. Bone age progression aligned with chronological age, and height gain over one year of treatment was consistent with the median for her age group. The therapeutic outcomes were highly satisfactory.

This case report adds valuable insights to the limited body of literature on MAS treatment in very young children. A critical observation during the 13-month follow-up was the patient’s excellent and comprehensive response to letrozole. Unlike the mixed results reported in some larger series [25, 26], our patient demonstrated not only the expected stabilization of bone age but also a complete cessation of vaginal bleeding and resolution of the ovarian cyst. This is particularly noteworthy given that evidence for letrozole use in children under five is scarce and includes case reports with suboptimal outcomes, such as a 2-year-old who experienced persistent bleeding and enlarging ovarian cysts, leading to discontinuation of the therapy [28]. Our case, therefore, provides a positive example of successful and safe letrozole use in this challenging age group, highlighting its potential to prevent unnecessary surgical interventions for ovarian cysts, as was initially considered for our patient.

These findings suggest that early, targeted therapy with aromatase inhibitors can be an effective and safe approach in the individualized management of MAS. Regular and comprehensive monitoring—including pelvic imaging, hormone levels, liver/kidney function, and bone age—is essential to assess treatment response and optimize outcomes. This case reinforces letrozole as a viable therapeutic pathway for this challenging and rare condition, particularly demonstrating a favorable outcome in a very young patient.

In this case, although the clinical presentation, elevated estradiol, and the presence of a large ovarian cyst were highly suggestive of gonadotropin-independent peripheral precocious puberty, a GnRH stimulation test was performed to definitively exclude the possibility of concomitant or triggered central precocious puberty (CPP). This is considered in cases of peripheral precocity, as prolonged exposure to sex steroids can sometimes lead to the subsequent activation of the central hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, a phenomenon known as secondary central precocious puberty [29]. The results of the stimulation test, demonstrating a prepubertal and suppressed LH response, conclusively confirmed the peripheral nature of the condition and ruled out central activation at the time of diagnosis.

The GnRH stimulation test in our patient revealed an atypical FSH-predominant response, which merits discussion. In classic gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty, a flat response of both LH and FSH is typically anticipated. However, the observed progressive rise in FSH, alongside a blunted LH response (with an LH peak/FSH peak ratio of approximately 0.11), has been documented in a subset of MAS patients [30]. This pattern may be attributed to the hyperestrogenic state priming the pituitary gonadotrophs, leading to enhanced FSH synthesis and release upon GnRH stimulation, while the underlying Gsα activation in the ovary maintains its autonomy from central LH regulation. The critical diagnostic finding remains the suppressed basal LH and the low peak LH response, which definitively excludes central activation as the primary driver of puberty at the time of testing.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized. Second, the follow-up period of 13 months is relatively short. While intermediate outcomes are positive, longer-term follow-up is necessary to definitively assess the impact on final adult height, bone health, and future reproductive function. Large cohort studies have reported mean follow-up durations of up to six years, providing more robust data on long-term efficacy and safety (1). Lastly, without genetic analysis, a definitive link to a specific GNAS mutation could not be established, though the clinical presentation was classic for MAS.

Conclusions

McCune-Albright syndrome is a rare clinical disorder that poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. In this case, early intervention with the aromatase inhibitor letrozole was initiated following a confirmed diagnosis. The treatment effectively suppressed systemic estrogen levels by blocking the peripheral conversion of androgens. This mechanism led to the resolution of vaginal bleeding and ovarian cysts, stabilization of breast development, and a significant deceleration in bone age progression during the 13-month observation period. The patient tolerated the treatment well, with no adverse effects observed. This case suggests that letrozole may be an effective and well-tolerated option for managing MAS-associated peripheral precocious puberty in the short to medium term. However, the optimal treatment duration and long-term impact on final adult height require further investigation through longer-term studies. Clinical management should be highly individualized, with therapy choices and duration based on continuous assessment of treatment response, skeletal maturation, and pubertal progression. It is important to note that ovarian autonomous hyperfunction may persist into adulthood, necessitating ongoing monitoring for potential complications such as abnormal uterine bleeding. Therefore, a multidisciplinary and individualized approach is essential for managing MAS patients from childhood through adulthood.
