Psychometric validation of the student perception of virtual education quality (SPGP) questionnaire in medical education
Authors: Ghahraman Mahmoudi, Leila Jouybari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSimilar to global trends and recent findings, the rapid adoption of virtual education in Iran necessitates robust tools to evaluate its quality. This study aimed to validate the Student Perception of Virtual Education Quality (SPGP) questionnaire.MethodsData collection occurred from March–August 2022. Inclusion criteria: ≥ 2 virtual semesters; exclusion: incomplete responses (> 10%). This methodological study used a cross-sectional descriptive design to determine the SPGP questionnaire’s factor structure. The original questionnaire was translated following a structured framework, with validation adhering to WHO protocols. Cultural adaptation challenges included translating "immediate feedback" to reflect Persian academic norms; discrepancies were resolved via consensus with bilingual educators. After establishing validity, reliability, and validation, 400 students from University of Medical Sciences and the Faculty of Medical Sciences at Islamic Azad University were recruited using stratified cluster sampling. Data were analyzed via descriptive/inferential statistics using SPSS 26 and EQS 6.1.ResultsThe questionnaire's validity and reliability were confirmed, with all items achieving a Content Validity Ratio (CVR) above 0.56 and a Content Validity Index (CVI) above 0.86. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the three factors—interaction, learning, and teaching—confirmed the questionnaire's three-factor structure. Factor loadings > 0.7 confirm strong construct validity, consistent with psychometric standards. Pearson correlation analysis indicated a significant relationship between these dimensions (r = 0.95, p < 0.001). It is important to note that the critical Content Validity Ratio (CVR) value is dependent on the number of expert panel members. For a panel of 12 experts, as utilized in this study, the critical CVR value at a significance level of p < 0.05 is 0.56. All items in the questionnaire exceeded this critical threshold, indicating acceptable content validity based on the number of experts involved.ConclusionThe SPGP is a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating virtual education quality in Iranian medical schools. The findings provide a validated tool for institutional assessment and inform policymakers on prioritizing critical areas such as faculty development and technological infrastructure to enhance the quality of virtual medical education.

Background

Over the past decade, significant changes have occurred in the support of educational programs. One such change is the widespread use of the internet in education, which facilitates communication between learners, instructors, and the sharing of learning resources. With population growth significantly increasing the costs of conventional education, the use of the internet has reduced the costs of information storage and transmission [1, 2]. Despite the growing number of institutions offering virtual education, clarifying the process of virtual education remains essential [3]. Virtual education represents a transformative shift in educational institutions, effectively leveraging information technology to enhance resource accessibility, improve quality, and reduce costs [4]. Distance education can be both challenging and motivating. Instructors have developed innovative strategies, such as improving learner engagement and participation in synchronous and asynchronous classe [5]. Due to the appeal of new teaching methods, various global studies have focused on feedback quality, educational materials, and tasks [3]: motivating instructors through virtual support and counseling [6]; learners’ responsibility in their learning and virtual classroom management [7]; and methods of learner participation in virtual classes and their impact on class success [8].

Despite the growing prevalence and advantages of virtual education, it is also important to acknowledge its inherent limitations. These challenges can include technical barriers related to internet access and digital literacy [9], reduced opportunities for face-to-face interaction and the development of interpersonal skills [10], difficulties in assessing certain practical skills and competencies, the potential for increased feelings of isolation among learners, and the need for instructors to adapt their pedagogical approaches to the online environment [11]. The COVID-19 pandemic has disrupted medical education worldwide. Most medical schools quickly adapted to the shift from clinical exposure to virtual environments by transitioning to online classes [12]. Various forms of distance learning, such as e-learning, provide diverse opportunities that, when educational content is delivered through different devices and over varied timeframes, enhance the chances of effective learning. Medical learners require extensive clinical skills, which are difficult to replace with e-learning, but theoretical concepts can be easily conveyed through virtual education. Medical disciplines consist of several specialized components: the theoretical component (seminars and lectures), the practical component, and specialized clinical rotations. Therefore, some elements can be replaced with e-learning, enhancing traditional learning methods. The pandemic has brought about a transformation in medicine, with advancements in telemedicine, adaptive research protocols, and clinical trials employing flexible approaches to achieve solutions [12, 13].

Virtual education has been well-received due to its flexibility in terms of time, location, and accessibility. This form of education reduces learners' costs by approximately 25% [13]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, virtual courses were recognized by 82% of participants, who noted their positive impact [14]. Another study revealed that over 50% of learners believed in-person classes were better than online classes [12]. A study found that higher education institutions in Romania were not adequately prepared to adopt virtual teaching methods, while a study in Spain indicated general dissatisfaction among learners with many aspects of online teaching [15]. Another study in the United States in 2020 found that online education was becoming a new norm in universities, but this development could pose challenges for some instructors due to a lack of knowledge and expertise in creating engaging, positive, and supportive online environments for learners [16]. A 2020 study in Germany showed that both learners and instructors held positive views on the implementation of online learning. This could present an opportunity to incorporate online learning into future curricula even after the COVID-19 pandemic [17]. For learners, virtual education is welcomed due to its flexibility in time, location, and accessibility. These courses reduced learners' costs by about 25% [3]. While tools like the SPGP exist, their validation in non-Western medical education contexts remains limited. As highlighted by recent studies on student perceptions in similar settings [1]. there is a pressing need for culturally and contextually validated instruments, particularly in critical fields like Iranian medical education [3].

Therefore, given the widespread adoption of virtual education and online teaching during this period, further studies in this area are necessary. This research aimed to validate a tool for assessing students' perspectives on virtual classes (seven practices for effective performance in virtual classes), focusing on three core areas: interactions, learning, and teaching [18]. Virtual education limitations include technical barriers, reduced peer interaction, and challenges in clinical skill assessment [3]. This tool can be used to evaluate the quality of teaching in virtual classrooms.

Materials and methods

This study employed a methodological design, utilizing a cross-sectional approach to psychometrically validate the Student Perception of Virtual Education Quality (SPGP) questionnaire within the context of Iranian medical education.

Translation process

The study aimed to determine the factor structure of the Students' Perspectives on Virtual Classes Questionnaire (SPGP). The questionnaire employed in this study was an existing instrument that has been previously published in the literature. Written permission for translation/validation was obtained from Dr. Unal Cakiroglu via email correspondence [19]. The original 'Students’ Attitudes Toward Virtual Education Questionnaire' includes 40 items across seven dimensions (e.g., instructor interaction, active learning) and three domains (Interaction, Learning, Teaching), with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.85. Cultural adaptation challenges included translating 'immediate feedback' to reflect Persian academic norms. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus with bilingual educators. The translation process of the original questionnaire was carried out according to the following framework, followed by the validation process in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) protocol:

Step 1 (Forward Translation): In this step, the original English version of the questionnaire was translated into Persian by two official translators.Step 2 (Incorporation): In this stage, a meeting was held with the researchers to review the two translated versions of the questionnaire. After discussion and consensus, a preliminary unified Persian translation was produced.Step 3 (Backward Translation): The unified Persian translation obtained in the previous step was back-translated into English by two native individuals proficient in both Persian and English. This resulted in a single English version.Step 4 (Comparison):The back-translated English version obtained in the previous step was compared by the research team with the original English questionnaire to ensure conceptual consistency. After this comparison, the final Persian questionnaire was finalized. This structured approach ensured the accuracy and reliability of the translated questionnaire, aligning it with the original version while maintaining its conceptual integrity.

A key cultural adaptation challenge was translating the concept of 'immediate feedback.' In the Western context, this often implies instantaneous automated responses, whereas in the Persian academic context, it relates more to timely and personal feedback from instructors. Through consensus meetings with bilingual educators, the term was adapted to reflect this nuanced meaning, ensuring cultural and conceptual equivalence.

Validity assessment

The "Evaluation of Learners' Perspectives on the Quality of Virtual Classes" questionnaire includes seven assessment dimensions: student and instructor evaluation, collaboration among learners, evaluation of time management, assessment of immediate feedback, evaluation of high expectations, assessment of active learning, and evaluation of diverse talents and learning skills, along with the main dimension (interactions, learning, and teaching). To validate the questionnaire, the following steps were taken in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) protocol:

Determining Validation Characteristics (Face Validity, Content Validity, Construct Validity, and Reliability), The validation of the questionnaire was conducted using both quantitative and qualitative methods. Face Validity: To determine the face validity of the tool, the opinions of both learners and experts were utilized. The process involved: Requesting feedback from 10 learners and 10 experts on the appropriateness of the questionnaire's appearance, clarity of terms, absence of ambiguity, and logical sequence of items to achieve the tool's objectives. Calculating the importance score for each item using the formula: Importance Score = (Importance Rating × Frequency) Here, frequency refers to the number of individuals who rated each item with a score of 4 or 5.A score above 1.5 for each item was considered desirable. The criterion of 1.5 was based on an average score of 3 and a mean frequency of 50%.

Both qualitative and quantitative approaches were used to determine content validity. Qualitative Content Validity: The Persian version of the tool was provided to 12 experts, including faculty members in medical sciences and medical education specialists. They were asked to provide written feedback on grammar, appropriate word usage, proper placement of items, and scoring of the tool. Construct validity

Step 1: In this stage, construct validity of the scale for evaluating learners' perceptions of virtual education was assessed using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to extract the number of latent factors. The following steps were taken: Sample Adequacy and Bartlett's Test: The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity were calculated. A KMO value between 0.7 and 0.8 is considered good, and between 0.8 and 0.9 is considered excellent. Bartlett's Test was used to confirm the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Determining the Number of Latent Factors: The number of latent factors was determined using Parallel Analysis and the Scree Plot (Fig. 1) method. Factor Loading Criteria: The presence of an item in a factor was determined based on the formula: CV = 5.152 ÷ √(n—2), where CV ≈ 0.3.Fig. 1Determining the three-factor structure of the learners' evaluation questionnaire of the quality of education

Determining the three-factor structure of the learners' evaluation questionnaire of the quality of education

According to the Three Indicator Rule, at least three observed variables (items) are required for each latent variable. Communalities: Items with communalities less than 0.5 were removed from the EFA. This structured approach ensured the extraction of meaningful latent factors and the reliability of the scale for evaluating learners' perceptions of virtual education.

Step 2: Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA): In the second step, the most common Goodness of Fit Indices for the proposed model were evaluated using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). The following steps were taken: Assessment of Model Fit: The normality assumption was checked using Skewness (± 3) and Kurtosis (± 7) indices. Common fit indices, such as Chi-square (χ2), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), were evaluated based on acceptable thresholds.

Concurrent validity

Concurrent validity was assessed using the Learners' Perceptions of Virtual Classes Scale. After evaluating the model fit, the construct validity and reliability were examined. Since the correlation was high, there was no need to assess convergent validity, indicating that the indices were convergent.

Reliability assessment

The reliability of the SPGP questionnaire was assessed using multiple methods to ensure robustness. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's Alpha, which yielded a high coefficient of 0.91 for the overall scale, indicating excellent internal consistency. Additionally, test–retest reliability was assessed by administering the questionnaire to a subset of participants (n = 30) with a two-week interval, resulting in an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.89, demonstrating good stability over time. Furthermore, composite reliability (CR) was calculated for each of the three identified factors (Interaction, Learning, and Teaching), with all CR values exceeding 0.70 (range: 0.72–0.85), confirming adequate construct reliability.

Sampling

Study Population and Sampling: The study population included learners from the Islamic Azad University, Sari Branch (Faculty of Medical Sciences) and Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences (including faculties of Medicine, Health, Nursing, and Midwifery). A multistage cluster sampling method was used.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on established psychometric guidelines, which recommend a ratio of 5 to 10 participants per questionnaire item for factor analysis [20], Aligning with this principle for the 40-item questionnaire, we aimed for and recruited 400 participants to ensure adequate statistical power. Inclusion Criteria: Learners from medical, nursing, midwifery, health, and laboratory sciences fields. At least two semesters of experience with virtual education. Enrollment in Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences or Islamic Azad University, Sari Branch. Exclusion Criteria: Incomplete questionnaires (more than 10% of the main questions unanswered).

Data collection

After finalizing the questionnaire's validity, reliability, and validation, data collection was conducted using a multistage cluster sampling method:

Research instrument

The data collection tool was the "Students' Attitudes Toward Virtual Education Questionnaire", originally developed and used in Turkey by Unal Cakiroglu in 2014. The psychometric properties of the English version of this questionnaire were validated by Unal Cakiroglu and colleagues [19].

Validity and reliability of the original questionnaire

Content validity

Assessed by experts who evaluated the clarity, accuracy, and appropriateness of the questions using a three-part scale: "Not necessary – Sometimes necessary – Necessary." The Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was calculated as 0.91, representing the proportion of items rated as relevant by all experts.

Reliability

The overall reliability of the questionnaire, based on Cronbach's Alpha, was 0.85.Permission for validation of the tool in this study was obtained from the original designer, Unal Cakiroglu. The questionnaire consists of seven dimensions and three main domains (interactions, learning, and teaching). The structure is as follows:

Dimensions

Student-Instructor Interaction (Questions 1–6), Collaboration Among Learners (Questions 7–12), Evaluation of Time Management (Questions 13–18),Assessment of Immediate Feedback (Questions 19–23),Evaluation of High Expectations (Questions 24–29),Assessment of Active Learning (Questions 30–35),Evaluation of Diverse Talents (Questions 36–40).

Domains:1-interactions

Includes student-instructor interaction and collaboration among learners.2-Learning: Includes diverse talents and active learning.3-Teaching: Includes immediate feedback, time management, and high expectations. Scoring: The questionnaire uses a 5-point Likert scale ranging from "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree. "The minimum possible score is 1, and the maximum possible score is 200.Data Analysis: The data were analyzed using both descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive Statistics: Frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, and median. Inferential Statistics: Independent t-test, One-way ANOVA, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), Pearson Correlation. Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics with SPSS version 26 and EQS version 6.1 software.

Discussion

Fulfilling its primary aim to validate the Student Perception of Virtual Education Quality (SPGP) questionnaire, this study demonstrates robust psychometric properties for assessing virtual education in Iranian medical schools, evidenced by strong internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.91) and a three-factor structure (interaction, learning, teaching) confirmed through confirmatory factor analysis. The strong factor loadings (> 0.70) confirm robust construct validity, a finding that is consistent with the psychometric standards observed in similar validation studies in health sciences education [1, 17].

The results of the research showed that based on confirmatory factor analysis, the factor loadings and the adequacy of the fit indices of the three factors—interaction, learning, and teaching—constitute the structure of the online education quality model and are useful in measuring the quality of online education in medical sciences faculties.

Our findings on the importance of interaction challenges mirror those of Mahmoud et al. [1], who highlighted similar barriers in nursing education, underscoring the SPGP's potential cross-context applicability.. One of the most famous principles proposed for effective teaching is the seven principles by Tanis, which encourage the student–teacher relationship, promote mutual interaction and collaboration among learners, encourage active learning, provide quick feedback, focus on spending time on learning activities, set high expectations, and respect diverse talents and learning styles, along with the three factors of interaction, learning, and teaching [3].

Chakiroghlu showed in his study that while the virtual education system has its benefits, there are still challenges regarding collaboration among learners. Active learning, which is closely tied to learning itself, was considered a very important factor. The average evaluation scores indicated that the synchronous environment was rated very satisfactorily by learners, with respect to interaction (3.24), teaching (3.55), and learning (3.53) [19].

Jeniya Vilcha conducted a systematic study titled "The Effectiveness of Virtual Medical Education During the COVID-19 Crisis" in 2020 and identified weaknesses in virtual education as technical challenges, confidentiality issues, less interaction among learners, and loss of assessments [21].

The factor structure identified in this study, consisting of Interaction, Learning, and Teaching, is consistent with the theoretical framework of the SPGP questionnaire. Furthermore, our findings align with the original psychometric validation of the Turkish version of the instrument by Çakıroğlu (2014) [15], who also reported a three-factor structure and high internal consistency. Similarly, studies adapting and validating this questionnaire in other cultural contexts, such as the study conducted in Jordan by Mahmoud et al. (2023) [1] on nursing students' perceptions of online learning, have also supported its reliability and validity in diverse educational settings. These consistent findings across different populations and cultural contexts underscore the potential of the SPGP as a robust tool for evaluating the quality of virtual education in medical fields." . The Student Perception of Virtual Education Quality (SPGP) questionnaire, as validated in this study, demonstrates strong psychometric properties for assessing students' perceptions of the quality of theoretical aspects of virtual education within Iranian medical schools. While the SPGP effectively captures students' experiences regarding interaction, learning, and teaching within virtual didactic settings (e.g., online lectures, seminars, and discussions), its applicability to the comprehensive evaluation of practical and clinical components of nursing education, such as laboratory sessions and clinical internships, is acknowledged to be limited. These latter components necessitate assessment tools and methodologies that can directly evaluate the acquisition and application of practical skills and competencies in real-world or simulated clinical environments.

Limitations

While this study benefits from a rigorous translation process guided by WHO protocols and a diverse sample across various academic levels within medical sciences at two universities, several limitations should be considered. First, the study's focus on a single geographical region (Mazandaran province, Iran) may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or countries with different educational systems and technological infrastructures. Second, the exclusion of students in clinical training and internships restricts the applicability of the validated questionnaire to the evaluation of virtual aspects of clinical education. Future research should explore the validity of the SPGP in clinical settings or develop complementary tools for this purpose. Third, while our sample included students from various medical sciences, its generalizability to specific disciplines requires careful consideration. For instance, studies focusing exclusively on nursing students, such as Mahmoud et al. [1], provide a more targeted perspective. Future research should investigate the factor structure and reliability of the SPGP within distinct health disciplines to account for unique pedagogical needs. fourth, the aggregation of data from undergraduate, postgraduate, and professional doctorate students, while providing a broad perspective, may mask potential differences in perceptions across these diverse groups. Further studies could investigate the factor structure and reliability of the SPGP within specific academic levels. Finally, the reliance on self-report data may be subject to response biases, although efforts were made to ensure anonymity and confidentiality to mitigate this.

Strengths of the study

Despite these limitations, this study possesses several notable strengths. The rigorous methodology employed for translation and psychometric validation, following international guidelines, ensures the cultural appropriateness and scientific soundness of the Persian version of the SPGP questionnaire. The inclusion of a diverse sample of students from two major medical universities in the region enhances the representativeness of the findings within the Iranian context. The comprehensive assessment of validity (face, content, and construct) and reliability (internal consistency, test–retest, and composite reliability) provides strong evidence for the instrument's psychometric properties. Furthermore, this study contributes to the limited body of research on the evaluation of virtual education quality in the Middle Eastern region, providing a valuable tool for educators and policymakers in Iran.

Conclusion

The SPGP is valid for evaluating virtual education quality in Iranian medical schools. Policymakers should prioritize faculty training and infrastructure to enhance interaction and feedback (e.g., via blended learning).
