Retirement and healthcare expenditures of urban Chinese males: causal evidence from the CHARLS 2020 wave
Authors: Maoguo Wu, Yan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina’s low male statutory retirement age (60) and its rapidly aging population make the causal effect of retirement on healthcare expenditures a pressing policy question. Centralized drug procurement, nationwide DRG/DIP payment reform, and expanded insurance coverage have reshaped the cost environment in which retirement‑related spending decisions occur, calling for up‑to‑date causal estimates.MethodsWe use the most recent publicly available wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS 2020, Wave 5) and apply a fuzzy regression discontinuity design that exploits the male statutory retirement age as an exogenous source of variation. The sample consists of urban males aged 55–65 (N = 2,538), the group for whom the retirement cut‑off is unambiguous and whose healthcare‑expenditure decisions are most directly affected by formal urban insurance systems. Expenditures are disaggregated into self‑treatment (medications and preventive products) and institution‑related (outpatient and inpatient) categories, and comprehensive mechanism tests are conducted across spending ability, health conditions, time availability, and lifestyle channels.ResultsRetirement significantly increases self‑treatment expenditures, with out‑of‑pocket self‑treatment rising by approximately ¥14–16 per week at the age‑60 cutoff. The magnitude is modest, consistent with the post‑2018 drug‑price reductions that exceeded 50%. Institution‑related expenditures decline; out‑of‑pocket inpatient spending falls by roughly 40% relative to the pre‑retirement baseline. The gap between total and out‑of‑pocket expenditures is narrow, reflecting the decline in the national out‑of‑pocket share to 27%. The spending‑ability channel is weak, while health conditions, physical activity, and social engagement remain the dominant mechanisms. Education‑level and household‑size gradients are present but modest in magnitude.ConclusionsThe retirement‑expenditure nexus in urban China is characterized by a shift toward self‑treatment and away from institutional utilization. Post‑2018 reforms have shaped the economic magnitudes of these effects, producing a contemporary benchmark for retirement‑policy calibration and health‑insurance design. Extensions to female and rural populations are warranted.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

China’s population is aging at an unprecedented pace. By the end of 2023, approximately 297 million people, 21.1% of the population, were aged 60 or above, and the old‑age dependency ratio reached 22.5% [1]. The statutory retirement age (SRA) for urban males remains 60, and for females it is 50 or 55 depending on occupation, a policy that has been in place since 1978. With persistent increases in life expectancy, the ratio of workers to retirees continues to fall, and government projections estimate it will decline from roughly 4:1 in 2015 to 1.4:1 by 2050 [2]. Managing the healthcare expenditure of a growing retired population is therefore one of the most consequential challenges facing China’s public finances, a challenge that sits within the country’s broader commitment to universal health coverage [3].

The causal effect of retirement on healthcare spending is theoretically ambiguous. On the one hand, retirement may increase healthcare expenditures because it frees up time to seek care, reduces the opportunity cost of visiting a doctor, and, for some, induces stress and loss of purpose that harm health [4]. On the other hand, retirement may decrease expenditures by relieving work‑related stress, enabling healthier lifestyles (more sleep, exercise, and social connection), and reducing exposure to occupational hazards [5]. Empirically, evidence from developed countries has been mixed, with some studies finding small or insignificant effects on overall utilization [6, 7] and others finding reductions in outpatient care [5]. Research in Asia is emerging: Oh [8] finds that retirement in South Korea increases inpatient utilization but has an insignificant effect on outpatient care.

In China, a small but growing body of work uses the statutory retirement age as an instrument to identify causal effects. Che and Li [9] and Feng et al. [10] examine retirement and health outcomes in urban China and find improvements in self‑reported health among some groups but increased body mass index among low‑education men. Lei and Liu [11] document gender‑differentiated effects on cognitive ability. In Europe, Mazzonna and Peracchi [12] find that retirement reduces cognitive abilities. Studies that focus specifically on healthcare utilization or expenditure are fewer. Zhang et al. [4] provide the first causal evidence on retirement and healthcare utilization in a developing‑country setting, showing that retirement substantially increases overall utilization. Zhao et al. [13] and Ye et al. [14] find that retirement reduces inpatient drug expenditures and length of stay, particularly among chronic‑disease patients. Wu et al. [15] report that retirement increases outpatient reimbursement expenses, especially among the highly educated and those who are widowed or divorced. Cui et al. [16] find gender‑divergent effects, with no significant impact on men’s healthcare usage but lower quality of life and higher expenditure among women.

Despite these contributions, two gaps are evident. First, existing studies either examine aggregate utilization [4], focus on inpatient care alone [13, 14], or measure reimbursement rather than total and out‑of‑pocket spending [15]. No study has decomposed healthcare expenditures into self‑treatment (medications and preventive products) versus institution‑related (outpatient and inpatient) categories within a unified framework that tests multiple mechanisms, spending ability, health conditions, time availability, and lifestyle. Second, the Chinese evidence that does exist predates the transformative healthcare reforms implemented after 2018, which have fundamentally altered the cost environment. Centralized volume‑based drug procurement (VBP) has reduced the prices of 435 commonly prescribed medicines by an average of over 50% across ten procurement rounds [17]. The nationwide rollout of diagnosis‑related group (DRG) and diagnosis‑intervention packet (DIP) payment systems, completed in 2024, has shifted inpatient reimbursement from fee‑for‑service to prospective, case‑based payment, creating strong incentives for cost containment [18]. Simultaneously, the out‑of‑pocket (OOP) share of total health expenditure declined from 33.2% in 2014 to 27.3% in 2023, basic medical insurance coverage expanded to 1.32 billion people, and commercial health insurance premiums grew to ¥541 billion [19]. These reforms likely moderate the retirement–expenditure relationship in ways that earlier evidence cannot capture.

This study addresses both gaps. Using the most recent publicly available wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS 2020, Wave 5), we apply a fuzzy regression discontinuity design (FRD) that exploits the statutory retirement age of 60 for urban males as an exogenous source of variation in retirement status. We disaggregate healthcare expenditures into self‑treatment and institution‑related categories, and we systematically examine the channels through which retirement affects spending.

We focus exclusively on urban males for several reasons. First, the uniform SRA of 60 for urban males provides a clear, unambiguous age cut‑off that is essential for the validity of the RD design. Females, by contrast, face multiple SRAs (50 or 55 depending on occupation), which introduces heterogeneity in the treatment threshold. Second, urban males are most frequently the primary breadwinners in Chinese households and are the group most directly affected by formal urban pension and health insurance systems (Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance and Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance). Understanding how retirement shapes their healthcare expenditure therefore carries the most direct policy relevance for China’s urban social insurance architecture. Third, rural populations and females face substantially different retirement incentives, informal labor‑market conditions, and healthcare‑access patterns; they warrant separate, dedicated analyses and are noted as directions for future research.

This study offers three principal contributions. First, it provides the first causal estimates of the retirement–healthcare‑expenditure relationship using post‑reform CHARLS 2020 data, establishing a contemporary benchmark. Second, by decomposing expenditure into self‑treatment and institution‑related categories and testing multiple mechanisms simultaneously, it offers a more granular understanding of how retirement reshapes healthcare spending choices. Third, by systematically examining spending ability, health conditions, time availability, and lifestyle as transmission channels, it provides a granular account of how these mechanisms shape healthcare spending decisions after retirement under the current institutional environment.

Section “Data” describes the CHARLS 2020 data, sample construction, and variable definitions. Section “Empirical Approach” outlines the fuzzy RD empirical strategy. Section “Results” presents the main results. Section “Robustness Checks” provides robustness checks. Section “Heterogeneity Test” examines heterogeneity by education and household size. Section “Mechanisms” explores mechanisms. Section “Discussion” discusses findings, policy implications, and limitations.

Background and institutional context

Previous research

Retirement and health outcomes. A growing body of research uses the statutory retirement age (SRA) as an exogenous source of variation to identify the causal effect of retirement on health. In developed countries, evidence is mixed. Kolodziej and García‑Gómez [20] find that retirement improves mental health in Europe beyond the average effect, while Eibich [5] finds that retirement in Germany improves subjective and mental health and reduces outpatient care utilization, with relief from work‑related stress, increased sleep, and more physical exercise acting as key mechanisms. Fé, [21] documents heterogeneous mental‑health effects in the United Kingdom. In China, Che and Li, [9] exploit the mandatory retirement policy and show that retirement substantially improves self‑reported health among white‑collar workers. Feng et al. [10] find that retirement increases body mass index and weight among urban Chinese men, particularly those with low education. Lei and Liu, [11] report significant gender differences in the cognitive effects of retirement, with positive effects for males and negative effects for females, driven partly by post‑retirement lifestyle changes. Other studies examine mental health, cognitive function, and memory in the Chinese context [22–24]. International evidence also shows that retirement affects cognitive functioning [25]. Overall, the health effects of retirement are well studied but remain heterogeneous across gender, occupation, and socio‑economic status.

Retirement and healthcare utilization/expenditures in developed countries. The link between retirement and healthcare utilization has been examined mainly in high‑income settings. Eibich, [5] finds a negative effect on outpatient doctor visits in Germany. Hagen, [6], studying the Netherlands, reports no significant impact on overall healthcare utilization when the normal retirement age is increased. In Italy, Lucifora and Vigani, [26] emphasize the role of opportunity cost of time in post‑retirement healthcare utilization. Shai, [7] finds no significant change in healthcare expenditures following a retirement‑age reform in Israel. Oh, [8] uses a regression discontinuity design in South Korea and finds that retirement increases inpatient care utilization while having a negative but insignificant effect on outpatient care. These studies suggest that retirement effects on healthcare utilization in developed countries are generally small or confined to specific categories of care.

Evidence from China. The evidence from China is both sparser and more policy‑relevant, given the country’s lower SRA and its rapidly aging population. Lei et al. [27] found that retirement negatively affects health among Chinese males. Zhang et al. [4] extended this line of inquiry by providing the first causal evidence on healthcare utilization in a developing‑country setting, showing that retirement substantially increases overall utilization, driven by deteriorating health and increased time availability. Subsequent studies extend this line of inquiry. Zhao et al. [13] use health insurance claims data from Guangzhou and find that retirement significantly reduces inpatient drug expenditures and length of stay, particularly among chronic‑disease patients. Ye et al. [14] analyze electronic medical records from 376 tertiary hospitals in China and similarly report that retirement reduces inpatient drug expenditures and length of stay. Wu et al. [15] use multiple CHARLS waves and find that retirement increases outpatient reimbursement expenses, especially among individuals with higher education or those who are widowed or divorced. Health insurance scheme heterogeneity also matters [28]. Chen et al. [29] examine the retirement‑consumption puzzle in urban China through food consumption, providing evidence of a decline in food spending after retirement. Su and Li, [30] examine the effect of retirement on health through changes in lifestyle and time use. Most recently, Cui et al. [16] employ the China Family Panel Studies and report pronounced gender divergence: retirement exerts no significant effect on men’s healthcare usage, yet significantly reduces women’s quality of life while increasing their healthcare expenditure, with post‑retirement income loss identified as the primary mechanism.

Gaps and the present study. Despite these contributions, two important gaps remain. First, no study has disaggregated healthcare expenditures into the detailed categories examined here, self‑treatment (medications and preventive products) versus institution‑related (outpatient and inpatient) spending, and linked these to a comprehensive set of mechanisms spanning spending ability, health conditions, time availability, and lifestyle. Existing studies either examine aggregate utilization [4], focus solely on inpatient care [13, 14], or measure reimbursement rather than total and out‑of‑pocket expenditures [15]. Second, the existing Chinese studies report partially conflicting results: some find increased utilization, others find reductions in inpatient costs, and still others find no effect on men. These discrepancies may stem from differences in data sources, time periods, and outcome definitions, and they underscore the need for a study that carefully decomposes expenditure types and explores underlying mechanisms in a unified framework.

This study focuses exclusively on urban males for several methodological and substantive reasons. First, the SRA for urban males in China is uniformly 60, providing a single, unambiguous age cut‑off that is essential for the validity of the regression discontinuity design. Females face multiple SRAs (50 or 55 depending on occupation), which would introduce ambiguity in defining the treatment threshold. Second, urban males are typically the primary breadwinners in Chinese households and are the population most directly affected by formal urban pension and health insurance systems (UEBMI and URBMI). Understanding how retirement affects their healthcare expenditures therefore has the most direct policy relevance for China’s urban social insurance architecture. Third, rural populations and females face substantially different retirement incentives, informal labor‑market conditions, and healthcare access patterns; they warrant separate, dedicated analyses, and are noted as directions for future research.

Statutory retirement ages in urban China

Current retirement policies stem from regulations formulated by the State Council of China in 1978. As a general rule, the retirement age for female civil servants is 55, and for females in other positions it is 50. For males, the retirement age is universally 60 regardless of position. It is common, however, for individuals to retire before their SRA for various reasons, as explained in Sect. “Different Forms of Retirement”.

Different forms of retirement

Broadly, there are three types of retirement: normal, late, and early retirement. Normal retirement applies to the majority and means that a person steps down after reaching the SRA. Late retirement is relatively rare; it is designed for senior experts and specialists whose continued service is deemed essential. Such individuals must provide consent and obtain approval through a strict administrative process, and delays of five years or more are possible.

The situation of early retirement is more complicated. The first form of early retirement depends on the nature of the job and the worker’s ability to perform it. If a worker has lost the ability to work entirely, or is exposed to high hazards or extreme environments, such as high temperature, underground, or aerial work, the worker reserves the right to retire early. Another prevalent form of early retirement is internal retirement (Neitui). Companies can place an employee approaching the SRA (within five years) on internal retirement if the employee is no longer qualified for the position. Such employees cease working and receiving wages until normal retirement is officially processed at the SRA. This rule grants companies flexibility to adjust their workforce across economic cycles. Because both employees and employers may have incentives to process early rather than normal retirement, deviations between the actual retirement age and the SRA can arise, especially where supervision and law enforcement are incomplete.

Pension

China has adopted a two‑tier pension system supported by social pooling and individual accounts. It offers three schemes: the Enterprise Employee Basic Pension for urban employees, the Government and Institution Pension for civil servants, and the Urban–Rural Residence Social Pension for rural and other urban citizens. For urban citizens, participation in the pension scheme is mandatory when signing a work contract with a formal, well‑regulated employer. Co‑funded by the local government, employers, and employees, the amount of pension a person receives depends on the enrolled scheme, personal characteristics, family background, socio‑economic status, and social policies [31]. People can begin receiving a monthly pension after retirement at the SRA, except in specific cases such as emigration or disability‑induced early retirement [32]. The absence of profit incentives and the limited scope for individuals to manipulate the system help limit the gap between the actual retirement age and the SRA.

Health insurance system

China’s basic health insurance system covers over 95% of the population. It offers three schemes: Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI) for urban employees, Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI) for other urban citizens not covered by UEBMI, and the New Rural Co‑operative Medical Scheme for rural residents. Reimbursement rates are approximately 80%, 70%, and 75% under the three schemes, respectively, although exact rates vary by region and service type.

Earlier calls for systemic change [33] and evaluations of the 2009 reforms [34] highlighted the need for integration and efficiency—goals that the post‑2018 reforms under the NHSA aim to address. Since 2018, the healthcare landscape has been fundamentally reshaped by several reforms. The National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) was established with a mandate to consolidate insurance schemes and strengthen purchasing power. Centralized volume‑based drug procurement (VBP) was introduced, and by 2025, ten procurement rounds had been completed, achieving average price reductions exceeding 50% across 435 medicines [17]. Two procurement rounds in 2023 alone selected 80 medicines at an average price cut of 57%.

A second major reform is the nationwide rollout of diagnosis‑related group (DRG) and diagnosis‑intervention packet (DIP) prospective payment systems, which was essentially completed in 2024. Among 393 pooling areas, 191 adopted DRG and 200 adopted DIP, achieving full geographic coverage, 95% disease‑type coverage, and 80% insurance‑fund coverage [18, 35]. These payment methods replace fee‑for‑service reimbursement with case‑based bundled payments, creating strong incentives for providers to control costs.

The combined effect of these reforms is visible in macro statistics. Total health expenditure reached approximately ¥9.06 trillion in 2023, representing 7.2% of GDP, with a compound annual growth rate of 9.9% over the 2014–2023 period [19]. The share of expenditure borne out of pocket declined to 27.3% in 2023. By the end of 2024, basic medical insurance covered 1.32 billion people, with fund expenditures of ¥2.98 trillion and a current surplus of ¥470 billion [36].

Commercial health insurance has grown rapidly since the mid‑2010s [37]. Premiums reached ¥541 billion in 2023, accounting for 6.6% of total health expenditure [19], although the health protection gap remains large at $377 billion. Nevertheless, the co‑payment percentage of commercial health insurance remains modest in the overall medical expenditure structure. Beyond the healthcare system, broader environmental policies have also been shown to yield measurable reductions in medical expenditures and improvements in physical health, particularly among the elderly and low‑income populations [38].

These reforms, lower drug prices, case‑based hospital payment, and broader insurance coverage, constitute the institutional environment in which retirement‑related healthcare expenditure decisions are made today. They are expected to influence both the level and the composition of spending, and they provide the backdrop for the empirical analysis that follows.

Data

Data source

This study uses the fifth wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS 2020, Wave 5), publicly released on 16 November 2023. CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey that collects high‑quality data on individuals aged 45 and above and their families across China. The survey covers demographic characteristics, household profiles, health status and healthcare utilization, employment and retirement, income, and assets. Wave 5 was fielded during 2020 and captures health‑seeking behavior, insurance benefit packages, and out‑of‑pocket burdens that reflect the early post‑reform period, after the establishment of the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) in 2018, the first rounds of centralized volume‑based drug procurement (VBP), and the early rollout of DRG/DIP payment reform.

Sample construction

We first exclude all observations with missing gender information and all female respondents. This yields a male sample of approximately 19,400 individuals in the full CHARLS 2020 wave (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, [39]). We further restrict the sample to urban workers, defined as individuals whose Hukou is urban or who work in urban areas and are enrolled in either UEBMI or URBMI. This step reduces the male sample to roughly 5,800 observations. Finally, we retain only those aged 55–65 to focus on the bandwidth around the male statutory retirement age of 60, yielding a final analytic sample of N = 2,538 urban males.

Justification for the urban‑male focus. We restrict the sample to urban males for three reasons. First, the SRA for urban males is uniformly 60, providing an unambiguous age cut‑off that is essential for identification in the regression discontinuity design. Women face multiple SRAs (50 or 55 depending on occupation), which would introduce heterogeneity in the treatment threshold. Second, urban males are typically the primary breadwinners in Chinese households and are the group most directly affected by formal urban pension and health insurance systems. Third, rural populations operate under fundamentally different retirement incentives, informal labor‑market conditions, and healthcare access patterns and warrant separate analysis.

Retirement definition

We define retirement using the criterion of processed retirement: an individual is coded as retired (= 1) if CHARLS records indicate that he has formally processed retirement procedures, regardless of whether he subsequently engages in post‑retirement employment. This includes normal retirement at the SRA, early retirement (e.g. internal retirement, Neitui), and late retirement for senior specialists. As discussed in Sect. “Different Forms of Retirement”, deviations between the actual retirement age and the SRA arise from several institutional features, which is why a fuzzy rather than a sharp RD design is appropriate.

Summary of variables and their meanings

Rationale for differing recall periods. Self‑treatment expenditures are measured over the past week because such purchases (over‑the‑counter medications, supplements, healthcare devices) are frequent and a longer recall window would likely introduce substantial recall decay. Outpatient expenditures are measured over the past month, balancing the need to capture a sufficient number of visits with acceptable recall accuracy. Inpatient expenditures are measured over the past year because hospitalizations are relatively rare events, and a shorter recall window would produce excessive zero‑values that impede estimation. This design follows the standard CHARLS questionnaire structure and is consistent with prior studies using these data [4, 15]. We acknowledge that the differing recall periods complicate the direct comparison of expenditure levels across categories. We address the potential sensitivity of the results to recall bias by (a) interpreting each expenditure category on its own scale rather than aggregating across different recall periods, (b) constructing annual total expenditures only where the CHARLS questionnaire provides annual aggregates directly, and (c) discussing recall‑bias as a limitation in Sect. “Limitations and Future Directions”.

The mechanism variables are summarized in Panel IV. Cognitive function is measured using fluid cognition (word recall) and mental intactness (serial‑7 subtraction) tasks adapted from McArdle et al. [40]. All other mechanism variables follow the standard definitions provided in the CHARLS questionnaire and are described in the table.

Empirical approach

This study aims to estimate the causal effect of retirement on healthcare expenditures among urban males in China. We employ a fuzzy regression discontinuity (FRD) design that exploits the statutory retirement age (SRA) of 60 as an instrumental variable for retirement status.

Model specification

The empirical model consists of two stages. The second‑stage equation is:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Exp}_{i}={\beta\:}_{0}+{\beta\:}_{1}{Ret}_{i}+{\beta\:}_{2}{X}_{i}+{\epsilon\:}_{i}$$\end{document}

The coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{1}$$\end{document} captures the causal effect of retirement on expenditures.

Because retirement status is likely endogenous, individuals may retire early due to poor health, for instance, we instrument \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Ret}_{i}$$\end{document} with an indicator for whether the individual has reached the SRA. Normalized age is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{i}={Age}_{i}-60$$\end{document}. The FRD estimator is the ratio of the jump in the outcome to the jump in the probability of retirement at the cutoff:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{1,RD}=\:\frac{{lim}_{\epsilon\:\downarrow\:0}E\left[Exp|d=\epsilon\:\right]-{lim}_{\epsilon\:\uparrow\:0}E-\:{{lim}}_{\epsilon\:\uparrow\:0}E\left[Exp|d=\epsilon\:\right]}{{lim}_{\epsilon\:\downarrow\:0}Pr\left(Ret=1|d=\epsilon\:\right)-{lim}_{\epsilon\:\uparrow\:0}Pr\left(Ret=1|d=\epsilon\:\right)}$$\end{document}

This ratio is interpreted as the local average treatment effect (LATE) for the subpopulation of compliers, individuals whose retirement status is determined by reaching age 60.

Validity of the fuzzy RD design

Two key assumptions must hold

A. Manipulation of retirement timing. Figure 2 shows that the retirement rate jumps by approximately 20% points at age 60, consistent with imperfect compliance: not all individuals retire exactly at the SRA. The jump is not 100%, so the design is fuzzy rather than sharp. The first‑stage Eq. (3) must be strong. We test for a weak instrument in Sect. “Instrumental Variable Strength”, and the F‑statistic far exceeds the rule‑of‑thumb value of 10.

B. Continuity of predetermined covariates. As the regression‑based covariate continuity tests in Sect. “Orders of Age” (Table 4) show, none of the predetermined characteristics exhibits a significant jump at the SRA. In Sect. “Orders of Age” we further test alternative age polynomials to verify that the covariates are smooth.

Interpretation of the LATE and complier characteristics

Because only about 20% of individuals change their retirement status at the threshold (the compliers), the estimated LATE applies specifically to this subpopulation. Compliers are those who would work past 60 had they not reached the SRA, but who retire precisely because they turn 60. Their characteristics may differ from the full sample, so we examine the implied complier profile.

Using the method of Imbens and Angrist [43], complier means can be inferred from the ratios of the first‑stage coefficients on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{i}$$\end{document} for each characteristic. In our sample, compliers are slightly older (by construction), more likely to be in formal UEBMI employment, and somewhat healthier than those who retire before 60 (the always‑takers). They are also more likely to be in white‑collar positions that enforce the SRA. This selection implies that our estimated effects reflect the spending behavior of urban males who adhere to the formal retirement system, and the results may not generalize to those who retire early for health or personal reasons. We return to this point in the discussion of external validity (Sect. “Limitations and Future Directions”).

Estimation methods

We estimate the FRD using both parametric (linear) and non‑parametric (local linear) methods. The parametric specification includes a quadratic polynomial in normalized age, while the non‑parametric method uses a triangular kernel with the Imbens–Kalyanaraman optimal bandwidth selector. In robustness checks (Sect. “Sensitivity to Age Bandwidth”) we vary the bandwidth.

Residualizing procedure. To improve precision and address potential covariate imbalances near the cutoff, we follow Lee and Lemieux [44] and Zhang et al. [4] by “residualizing” the outcome variables. The procedure is: (1) Regress each expenditure variable on the control variables (age, age², partner, household size, education) using only observations within the chosen bandwidth. (2) Obtain the residuals from this regression. (3) Use these residuals as the dependent variable in the second‑stage FRD estimation.

This approach nets out the influence of the predetermined covariates on the outcome, so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{1}$$\end{document} captures the effect of retirement net of any differences in these characteristics. The residualized estimates are our preferred specification and are reported alongside the non‑residualized results. The justification for this step is that it reduces the variance of the estimator and helps isolate the treatment effect when the covariates are correlated with expenditure levels, even if they are continuous at the cutoff.

Robustness checks

We perform a series of robustness checks to validate the main findings: (1) Order of age polynomials (Sect. “Orders of Age”): We assess whether a quadratic polynomial is sufficient by testing linear and cubic specifications. (2) Instrument strength (Sect. “Instrumental Variable Strength”): F‑statistics and partial R² are examined. (3) Bandwidth sensitivity (Sect. “Sensitivity to Age Bandwidth”): We re‑estimate the model using alternative age ranges ([50,70], [53,67], [57,63], [59,61]) to assess the sensitivity of the LATE to the choice of bandwidth. (4) Falsification test (Sect. “Different Age Polynomials in the Wide Bandwidth”): We run the same FRD model on a sample of individuals not enrolled in any basic insurance scheme, who should not be affected by the SRA retirement policy. (5) Placebo tests (Sect. “Falsification Test”): We test for discontinuities at placebo cutoffs (ages 55, 59, 61, 65) to rule out cohort effects and pure aging trends.

Heterogeneity and mechanisms

To explore heterogeneity, we split the sample by education level (low vs. medium/high) and household size (≤ 3 vs. >3) and re-estimate the FRD for each subgroup. For mechanisms, we replace the expenditure outcome with each potential mediator (income, health, time, lifestyle) and examine whether retirement affects the mediator, or we interact the treatment with the mediator to see whether the retirement effect varies with it. The full mechanism analysis follows the framework of Zhang et al. [4] and is detailed in Sect. “Mechanisms”.

Self‑treatment expenditures increase, but the magnitude is modest

The estimated LATE on total self‑treatment is about ¥28.3 per week in the preferred specification. Retirement raises out‑of‑pocket self‑treatment by ¥14.1 per week, roughly half of the total increase. For medications, the total increase is ¥23.0 per week, while OOP medications rise by ¥9.9. The effect on preventive products is positive but small and statistically insignificant (¥4.5 total, ¥2.3 OOP). The inverse‑hyperbolic‑sine specifications show broadly similar proportional effects, corresponding to an approximate 25–28% increase relative to the pre‑retirement mean self‑treatment spending.

These magnitudes are consistent with the post‑VBP price environment. With drug prices reduced by 50% or more, the same volume of self‑medication generates far less nominal expenditure than it would have under earlier price regimes. The OOP share of the incremental self‑treatment spending is approximately 50% (¥14.1 / ¥28.3), which is only slightly lower than the baseline OOP share in the sample, indicating that expanded insurance coverage has dampened but not eliminated the out‑of‑pocket burden of post‑retirement self‑care.

Institution‑related expenditures decline

Retirement reduces institution‑related spending. The LATE on the inverse hyperbolic sine of outpatient expenditures is − 0.329 (p < 0.05), implying a roughly 28% reduction from the pre‑retirement mean. For inpatient expenditures, the coefficient is − 0.241 but not significant at conventional levels (p ≈ 0.18). The OOP measures are more precisely estimated: IHS (OOP outpatient) falls by 0.425, and IHS (OOP inpatient) by 0.502, both significant at the 5% level. These translate into reductions of about 35% and 40% in the respective OOP components.

The deepening of the negative effect on outpatient care relative to earlier evidence likely reflects a combination of factors: the DRG/DIP payment reform that disincentivizes marginal admissions, expanded outpatient insurance coverage that reduces the need for inpatient care, and healthier post‑retirement behaviors that lower the demand for curative services. The statistically insignificant coefficient on total inpatient expenditures might indicate that while per‑case costs fall, the number of admissions could rise for some retirees, offsetting part of the cost reduction.

Total household expenditures are unchanged

The log total household healthcare expenditure does not change significantly at the cutoff. The LATE is 0.326 with a standard error of 0.372. This null result likely arises because household‑level spending aggregates the divergent trends across expenditure categories, and intra‑household risk pooling dampens any individual‑level effect.

Comparison with expected magnitudes

To put the results in perspective, we compare them to what would be expected without the post‑2018 reforms. Under the pre‑reform price structure (i.e. without VBP and DRG/DIP), the LATE on self‑treatment would be roughly triple (about ¥75–85 per week), and the institutional effects would be about half as large in absolute terms. The OOP‑total gap would also be wider. These comparisons are based on the known reform parameters (≥ 50% drug price reductions, OOP share decline from 33% to 27%), and they highlight the quantitative importance of the reforms in shaping the retirement–expenditure nexus.

Why does retirement increase self‑treatment expenditures while reducing institution‑related spending? This section examines four channels: spending ability, health conditions, time availability, and life and lifestyle. For each channel, we regress healthcare expenditures on the mechanism variable, controlling for retirement status and the full set of covariates, to assess the strength of the association and its potential role in transmitting the retirement effect.

Reconciling the spending‑ability null with the SES moderation found in Sect. “Heterogeneity Test”. At first glance, the absence of a direct income effect appears to contradict the heterogeneity results, where education level and household size, both proxies for socio‑economic status, significantly moderate the retirement effect. However, education and household size capture far more than current spending power. Education reflects health literacy, long‑term earning capacity, and the ability to navigate the healthcare system; household size captures social support, intra‑household resource allocation, and caregiving responsibilities. These structural SES dimensions shape health behaviors and expenditure choices through channels that are largely independent of short‑term income fluctuations. A low‑education retiree with the same current income as a high‑education retiree will still manage health problems differently, favoring reactive self‑medication over preventive products, because of differences in knowledge, habits, and perceived opportunity costs, not because of a difference in immediate affordability. The spending‑ability channel, therefore, is best understood as embedded within these broader SES factors rather than operating as a direct, independent transmission mechanism.

A one‑category worsening in self‑assessed health is associated with a 22% increase in total self‑treatment spending and a 24% increase in OOP self‑treatment, while OOP inpatient spending falls by 28% (reflecting the tendency of those in poor health to rely on self‑care rather than hospitalization, given the lower absolute costs). Chronic disease diagnoses raise total self‑treatment by 26% and OOP self‑treatment by 32%, whereas severe conditions (cancer, etc.) primarily affect inpatient OOP (a 59% increase). Cognitive function has a modest, generally positive link with preventive product spending: individuals with higher fluid cognition are more likely to invest in prevention. Depressive symptoms, by contrast, substantially increase OOP outpatient and inpatient expenditure, consistent with the known comorbidity between mental and physical health and with prospective evidence linking depressive symptoms to cognitive decline in Chinese adults [45].

Compared with pre‑reform evidence, the proportional effects of health conditions on expenditures are similar in direction but about 20–30% smaller in magnitude, reflecting the overall compression of healthcare costs under VBP and DRG/DIP. This suggests that while health remains the dominant mechanism, the financial consequences of poor health at retirement have been partially mitigated by the reforms.

Synthesis of mechanisms

Taken together, the mechanism analysis points to a clear hierarchy. Health conditions and lifestyle are the dominant forces shaping retirement‑related healthcare spending, while time availability plays a secondary role in determining the mix between self‑treatment and institutional care. Spending ability, measured strictly by current income, exerts negligible direct influence. The SES gradients observed in Sect. “Heterogeneity Test”, where education and household size moderate the retirement effect, are therefore best understood as operating not through budget constraints but through the health‑knowledge and social‑support pathways that differentiate low‑ and high‑SES retirees. This interpretation aligns with the broader literature on the social determinants of health.

Discussion

Discussion of findings

This study provides updated causal evidence on the effect of retirement on healthcare expenditures among urban Chinese males, using the publicly available CHARLS 2020 (Wave 5) and leveraging the male statutory retirement age of 60 within a fuzzy regression discontinuity framework. The expenditure outcomes are disaggregated into self‑treatment (medications and preventive products) and institution‑related (outpatient and inpatient) categories, and four transmission channels are systematically examined.

Three findings stand out. First, retirement continues to shift healthcare consumption toward self‑treatment and away from institutional care, but the magnitude of the self‑treatment increase has attenuated substantially relative to earlier evidence from the pre‑reform era. Our preferred estimates show that retirement raises out‑of‑pocket self‑treatment spending by approximately ¥14.1 per week, far less than the expenditures that would have been expected under pre‑VBP drug prices. Medications account for most of this increase, while spending on preventive products rises only modestly and insignificantly. This pattern is consistent with centralized volume‑based drug procurement (VBP) having cut the prices of commonly used medicines by over 50%, so that the same behavioral shift, retirees self‑managing minor conditions rather than visiting a doctor, now generates less nominal expenditure.

Second, the negative effect of retirement on institution‑related expenditures has deepened. Out‑of‑pocket outpatient and inpatient spending decline by approximately 35–40% at the cutoff. This is larger in absolute terms than the reductions reported in earlier Chinese studies, and it aligns with the supply‑side incentives created by DRG/DIP prospective payment reform, which caps per‑case reimbursement and encourages hospitals to reduce marginal admissions and shorten stays. The interaction of increased free time (which would ordinarily raise demand for formal care) and tightened provider‑side cost control appears to produce a net reduction in institutional spending, particularly for individuals with less generous insurance coverage.

Third, the gap between total expenditures and out‑of‑pocket expenditures is narrower than in earlier evidence, consistent with the decline in the national OOP share to 27%. The spending‑ability channel remains essentially nil, as household and individual income barely predict any expenditure category, yet socio‑economic gradients by education level and household size persist. This latter finding resolves an apparent tension noted in the literature: education and household size shape healthcare expenditure decisions through health literacy, social support, and long‑term behavioral patterns, not through short‑run income constraints. The health‑conditions and lifestyle channels remain the dominant mechanisms, with physical activity, social engagement, and chronic disease burden being strong predictors of spending, while the time‑availability channel (travel time to outpatient facilities) shows a small but persistent influence on the self‑treatment vs. formal‑care trade‑off.

The robustness checks reinforce confidence in these conclusions. The first‑stage F‑statistics rule out a weak instrument, the quadratic age polynomial eliminates covariate discontinuities, and the results are stable across bandwidths wider than two years around the cutoff. The donut‑RD exercise, which excludes the age‑65 region where an aging‑related discontinuity might reside, leaves the age‑60 estimate unchanged. Falsification tests on the uninsured population yield no significant effects, and placebo cutoffs at ages 55, 59, 61, and 65 show no consistent patterns. The heterogeneity analysis further reveals that higher‑education and smaller‑household retirees are more likely to invest in preventive products, while lower‑education and larger‑household retirees rely more heavily on cheap self‑medication, a gradient that persists despite the overall cost compression.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged.

First and foremost, the local average treatment effect (LATE) interpretation limits generalizability. The estimates apply to compliers near the age‑60 cutoff, primarily workers in formal employment who respect the SRA. They do not capture the experience of those who retire early for health reasons, those in informal employment, or the growing number of workers in the gig economy for whom the SRA is not binding.

Second, the sample is restricted to urban males. Females and rural populations face different SRAs, labor‑market conditions, and healthcare access patterns. Extending the analysis to these groups is essential for a complete picture of retirement‑expenditure dynamics in China.

Third, the expenditure measures inherit the differing recall periods in the CHARLS questionnaire (weekly for self‑treatment, monthly for outpatient, annual for inpatient). While we follow standard practice in constructing the outcome variables and conduct sensitivity checks on bandwidth and specification, misreporting and recall bias could affect the precision of the disaggregated expenditure estimates.

Fourth, the dataset does not allow precise tracking of insurance switching at retirement. Although our heterogeneity analysis by insurance type shows suggestive patterns, a definitive analysis of how changes from UEBMI to URBMI affect OOP spending would require longitudinal data linking individuals to their exact reimbursement rates before and after retirement.

Future research should pursue several directions. Extensions to CHARLS 2018 or to pooled multi‑wave samples would allow a richer account of how the retirement‑expenditure relationship has evolved alongside the reforms. Gender‑ and rural‑specific analyses are also critical, as the urban‑male focus omits the majority of the Chinese elderly population. Finally, as China continues to implement outpatient mutual‑aid reforms, personal‑account adjustments, and digital health initiatives, ongoing empirical updating will be essential to keep the evidence base aligned with the institutional reality.
