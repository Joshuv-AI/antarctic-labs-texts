Tobacco Rattle Virus Coat Protein Targets Ferredoxin 1 for Degradation
Authors: Shaorui Tian, Changyun Liu, Jie Dong, Xin Zhu, Xingyi Luo, Weina Liu, Haoran Peng, Lin Cai, Xianchao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant virus infections commonly inhibit leaf photosynthesis, leading to characteristic symptoms such as chlorosis. However, whether a conserved mechanism underpins this phenomenon remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that the coat protein (CP) of tobacco rattle virus (TRV) interacts with Nicotiana benthamiana ferredoxin (NbFd1) in chloroplasts, recruiting the 26S proteasome to promote NbFd1 degradation. This degradation reduces the net photosynthetic rate facilitated by NbFd1, ultimately causing leaf chlorosis. Notably, this interaction is not unique to TRV CP, as other viral proteins also recognise Fd1, suggesting a conserved mechanism among plant viruses. Evolutionary analyses indicate that Fd1 originated from prokaryotic photosynthetic bacteria and was maintained in plants through endosymbiosis under strong selective pressure. Notably, Fd1 from Selaginella moellendorffii, an early‐diverging vascular plant, is also recognised by TRV CP, suggesting an ancient origin of this interaction. In addition, Solanum lycopersicum Fd1 interacts with TRV CP, and its overexpression suppresses TRV‐GFP infection, supporting a defensive role. Together, these findings show that TRV CP targets Fd1 to impair host photosynthesis and promote symptom development, whereas highlighting the evolutionary significance of this interaction.Keywords: coat proteins, conservation, ferredoxin 1, interaction, tobacco rattle virus

Photosynthesis plays a pivotal role in the vital life processes of green plants. Following virus infection, photosynthesis is typically the first process to be impacted, resulting in typical symptoms such as mottling, a greenish appearance, stunted growth and mosaic patterns. In case of virus infection in the host plant, alteration in chloroplast photosynthesis primarily manifests through changes in chloroplast physiological morphology, which encompasses the induction of a hypersensitive reaction (HR) triggered by the virus, modulation of chloroplast gene expression (Seo et al. 2000), and perturbation of chloroplast electron transfer activity (Naidu et al. 1984; Liu et al. 2021).

Ferredoxin (Fd), an acidic and low molecular weight protein, is widely present in animals and plants, serving as a versatile electronic carrier in various redox systems. Fd plays a pivotal role in electron transfer and maintenance of the oxidoreduction state within the matrix (Arnon 1988). Within the chloroplast, Fd receives electrons from photosystem I (PSI) and transfers them to Fd:NADP+ oxidoreductase (FNR), which reduces NADP+ to NADPH (Arnon 1988). The resulting NADPH is involved in the reduction of 1,3‐bisphosphoglycerate by NAD(P)‐dependent glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase during CO2 assimilation. Fd contains a ubiquitous [2Fe‐2S] domain located at the C‐terminal, which plays a crucial role in Arabidopsis' resistance to bacterial infection (Su et al. 2014; Lin et al. 2011). The phosphorylation of plant ferredoxin‐like protein (pflp) is significant for NADPH oxidation and HR activation when comparing the pflp gene with or without CDK2 locus (Lin et al. 2011). Fd1 is one member of the Fd family, accounting for only 5% of Fd protein (Holtgrefe et al. 2003). However, in the absence of Ferredoxin 2 (Fd2) in plants, Fd1 alone can still ensure the smooth growth and carbon assimilation process (Voss et al. 2008, 2011). Reduction in the Fd1 content leads to photoinhibition in plants (Voss et al. 2011), whereas cyanobacterial flavodoxin complements Fd1 deficiency in knocked‐down transgenic tobacco plants (Blanco et al. 2013).

A large number of studies have consistently demonstrated the wide‐ranging implications of Fd in conferring broad‐spectrum resistance to various plants including rice, tobacco and Arabidopsis (Huang et al. 2006, 2004; Ger et al. 2014). Upon pathogens' invasion, plants promptly and transiently generate a substantial quantity of reactive oxygen species (ROS) to combat and hinder pathogens' invasion. The NADPH oxidase system, constituting a fundamental component of the oxygen reduction system, represents a key source of ROS (Wojtaszek 1997). Fd assumes a critical role in modulating intracellular ROS levels in NADP regeneration (Blanco et al. 2011). Introduction of the pepper ( Capsicum annuum ) pflp gene into Oncidium orchid protocorm‐like bodies has been shown to confer resistance against Erwinia carotovora (You et al. 2003) while transfer of pflp into Arabidopsis triggers harpin recognition, leading to HR activation and other resistance mechanisms (Ger et al. 2014). Similarly, Fd1 facilitates host defence against a diverse range of plant viruses. Fd1 physically interacts with tomato mosaic virus (ToMV, genus Tobamovirus, family Virgaviridae) coat protein (CP) (Sun et al. 2013), and reduced Fd1 expression during tobacco mosaic virus (TMV, genus Tobamovirus, family Virgaviridae) infection is associated with mosaic symptoms (Ma et al. 2008). The CP of cucumber mosaic virus (CMV, genus Cucumovirus, family Bromoviridae)‐M strain associates with the Fd1 precursor in the cytoplasm, obstructing Fd1 transport into chloroplasts and impairing Fd1 functions during viral infection (Qiu et al. 2018). Fd1 also interacts with potato virus X (PVX, genus Potexvirus, family Alphaflexiviridae) triple‐gene‐block 1 (TGB1, p25), and overexpression of Fd1 hampers the RNA silencing inhibition mediated by p25 (Yang et al. 2020). Rice stripe virus (RSV, genus Tenuivirus, family Phenuiviridae)‐derived virus‐derived small interfering RNA (vsiRNA) also targets Fd1 to inhibit viral resistance (Cui et al. 2021).

Tobacco rattle virus (TRV, genus Tobravirus, family Virgaviridae) stands out among plant viruses due to its extensive host range, capable of infecting nearly 500 kinds of plants (MacFarlane 2008). In tobacco, TRV induces necrotic regions on stems and leaf veins, whereas the leaves exhibit a crumpled appearance. In potatoes, TRV causes necrotic areas on the leaves and stem (Agrios 2005). TRV is widely recognised as the most utilised virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) vector (Burch‐Smith et al. 2006; Foureau et al. 2016). Nevertheless, limited research has been conducted thus far regarding its interaction with host plants.

In this study, we investigated the interactions between various viral coat proteins (CPs) and Nicotiana benthamiana Fd1 (NbFd1) using yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) analysis, and found a strong interaction between the CP of TRV and NbFd1 in yeast (Figure 1A). However, CMV CP and PVX CP did not interact with Fd1, which may be because only CP from the chlorosis‐inducing CMV‐M strain and p25 from PVX interact with Fd1 (Qiu et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2020). Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays further demonstrated that the interaction occurs within chloroplasts (Figure 1B, Figure S1). However, colocalisation experiments with mCherry:TRV CP and NbFd1:GFP are challenging because the cytoplasmic distribution of TRV CP often masks chloroplast fluorescence (Figure S2). This overlap complicates the visualisation of their interaction and limits the reliability of co‐immunoprecipitation assays for confirmation. Nonetheless, based on prior studies, it is reasonable to hypothesise that TRV CP interacts with NbFd1 within chloroplasts (Qiu et al. 2018). To test the interaction, we performed a glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down assay, and the results confirmed that GST‐tagged TRV CP physically directs interacted with SUMO‐HIS‐NbFd1 (Figure 1C) (materials and methods are provided in File S1).

Subsequently, we conducted evaluations to confirm the antiviral function of NbFd1 against TRV. Initial attempts to perform CRISPR/Cas9 on NbFd1 were unsuccessful maybe due to the essential role of NbFd1 in the electron transport chain. Therefore, we employed PVX‐mediated gene‐silencing technology to down‐regulate NbFd1 expression, which was designed as PVX:NbFd1. Empty vector PVX:00 served as the control. After confirming that NbFd1 was successfully silenced in PVX:NbFd1 by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis (Figure S3), we examined the resistance phenotype of the silenced plants. Notably, early accumulation of TRV CP content in the inoculated leaves showed no significant change following NbFd1 silencing (Figure 1D,F). However, during the later stages of TRV‐GFP infection, the accumulation of TRV CP protein and nucleic acids in systemic leaves was significantly accelerated compared to the control (Figure 1D–F), indicating that suppression of NbFd1 facilitates TRV infection. In further studies, the infection rate of TRV‐GFP in the transgenic N. benthamiana plants overexpressing NbFd1 under the CaMV 35S promoter was significantly reduced, with TRV CP RNA levels in the leaves of lines 9 and 13 markedly lower than those in the control group (Figure 1G,H). These findings strongly suggest that NbFd1 plays a positive regulatory role in the antiviral response of plants.

Although not entirely novel, as Fd1 has previously been reported to inhibit infections by several viruses (Cui et al. 2021), this observation prompted us to examine NbFd1 expression during viral infection. Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis revealed that NbFd1 mRNA was significantly downregulated after TRV infection (Figure S4), consistent with earlier reports of Fd1 suppression upon viral attack (Cui et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2008; Qiu et al. 2018). The current vsiRNA theory provides a plausible explanation for transcript‐level inhibition (Cui et al. 2021). At the protein level, overexpression assays showed that NbFd1 suppressed TRV CP accumulation in inoculated leaves (Figure 2A), supporting its antiviral function. At the same time, 35S promoter‐driven NbFd1:GFP was directly suppressed by TRV CP:mCherry, but not by mCherry empty (Figure 2B). We then investigated how this suppression was achieved. Co‐expression experiments revealed that 35S promoter‐driven NbFd1:GFP levels were reduced in the presence of TRV CP:mCherry, and this reduction was alleviated upon MG132 treatment (Figure 2B), indicating that TRV CP promotes NbFd1 degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway.

A critical question remains: does the degradation of NbFd1 induced by TRV CP contribute to chlorosis in plant leaves? To address this, we first observed symptoms of TRV infection in N. benthamiana, which invariably led to chlorosis (Figure S5). We then measured various photosynthesis‐related parameters in TRV‐infected plants and found significant reductions in both net photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance (Figure 2C, Figure S6). Furthermore, we assessed photosynthetic parameters in NbFd1‐silenced plants and observed a marked decrease in the net photosynthetic rate (Figure 2D, Figure S7). These findings underscore the critical role of NbFd1 expression in regulating plant photosynthesis, particularly regarding the net photosynthetic rate. We hypothesise that upon TRV infection, the CP interacts with NbFd1, promoting its degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway. This disruption of the host's photosynthetic machinery, specifically the inhibition of the photosynthetic electron transfer rate, impairs ATP and NADPH production. Consequently, this limitation in the dark reaction reduces the net photosynthetic rate, potentially exacerbating TRV infection and contributing to the chlorosis observed in the host plant.

Although these findings may not be entirely novel, they raise important questions about why many plant viruses target Fd1. As a key electron carrier in the chloroplast photosynthetic electron transport chain, Fd1 has a remarkable evolutionary history. According to the endosymbiotic theory, chloroplasts originated from ancient photosynthetic bacteria, and Fd proteins can be traced back to these ancestors, with homologues identified in Synechocystis sp. and Gloeobacter violaceus (Figure 3A). Over ~1.6 billion years of evolution, Fd diversified from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii to Klebsormidium nitens, and eventually into higher angiosperms (Figure S8). Notably, plant viruses are thought to have originated 350–400 million years ago (Lefeuvre et al. 2019), coinciding with the emergence of gymnosperms and lycophytes such as Selaginella moellendorffii. This raises the intriguing question: could plant viruses have been capable of recognising Fd1 from their inception? To test this hypothesis, we analysed the conserved sequences of Fd1 across evolutionary processes and found that these proteins share over 50% sequence similarity, with the C‐terminal [2Fe‐2S] domain undergoing selective pressure, maintaining a conservation level exceeding 80% in the tested species (Figure 3B). Interestingly, TRV CP was found to interact with SmFd1, suggesting that this recognition mechanism may have originated early in the evolution of plant viruses (Figure 4A). Although Y2H assays indicated a weak interaction between TRV CP and SlFd1, a homologue of NbFd1, transgenic overexpression of SlFd1 in Solanum lycopersicum plants resulted in strong inhibition of TRV‐GFP infection (Figure 4B,C, Figure S9), highlighting the importance of the TRV CP–Fd1 interaction in S. lycopersicum resistance to TRV. These findings suggest that plant viruses may have developed the capability to recognise Fd early in their evolutionary history, a recognition mechanism that has persisted and may be a primary reason why plant viruses disrupt photosynthesis in host plants.

Viral CPs are central regulators of plant–virus interactions and symptom development. Among the various symptoms induced by viral infections, chlorosis in mature leaves is a hallmark that has been closely linked to CP function. CPs actively participate in altering chloroplast structure and disrupting photosynthesis in infected tissues, as evidenced in studies of multiple plant–virus systems (Culver 2002; Reinero and Beachy 1989). For instance, in TMV infections, the severity of chlorosis correlates with CP accumulation within chloroplasts, which compromises the activity of photosystem II (PSII) at the thylakoid membrane (Blanco et al. 2013). Excessive CP accumulation, particularly in TMV mutants, exacerbates symptom severity and further inhibits PSII function (Reinero and Beachy 1989).

The connection between CP accumulation and chlorosis symptoms has been further substantiated by studies demonstrating reduced expression of chloroplast‐related proteins during viral infection (Lehto et al. 2003). For example, TMV‐induced yellowing of tobacco leaves has been attributed to decreased expression of Fd1 (Ma et al. 2008). Consistently, CP exchange experiments between chlorosis‐ and green mosaic‐inducing CMV strains revealed that CP acts as a determinant of chlorosis symptoms, primarily through its interaction with Fd1 (Qiu et al. 2018). Similar findings were observed in our study that in infections caused by TRV, where CP directly interacted with Fd1, suppressing its expression and disrupting chloroplast function. Interestingly, this suppression bypasses the typical vsiRNA‐mediated silencing mechanism, suggesting an alternative post‐translational regulation of Fd1. Mechanistically, CPs interfere with Fd1 incorporation into chloroplasts by targeting its conserved C‐terminal domain, an interaction critical for maintaining electron transport chain integrity (Lin et al. 2011). The loss of Fd1 function leads to disruptions in PSII, degradation of the core D1 protein, and subsequent inhibition of photosynthesis and chlorophyll biosynthesis (Qiu et al. 2018; Lehto et al. 2003). Furthermore, Fd1 enhances ROS production under stress conditions, exacerbating chloroplast damage and contributing to symptom development including yellowing and chlorosis (Tognetti et al. 2006; Ma et al. 2008; Qiu et al. 2021).

Our study also provides insights into the co‐evolutionary dynamics between plant viruses and their hosts. Conservation analysis revealed that Fd1 probably originated from the ferredoxin proteins of ancestral photosynthetic bacteria and has since been highly conserved across the plant phylogeny (Ku et al. 2015). Importantly, the emergence of FdI predates that of plant viruses, suggesting that its interaction with viral components may have roots in earlier biological processes. Although the possibility remains that some unknown biological or non‐biological entity interacted with early plant Fd1 proteins before the advent of plant viruses, the conserved nature of this interaction supports the notion that FdI was recognised during the initial stages of plant virus evolution. Further evidence supporting this hypothesis comes from the FdI protein of S. moellendorffii, an early‐diverging vascular plant (Lefeuvre et al. 2019). Notably, emerging evidence suggests that conserved virus–host recognition mechanisms may also operate in non‐vascular plants. A recent study in Marchantia polymorpha demonstrated that MpNAC7, a liverwort NAC transcription factor, is a conserved interactor of the TMV silencing suppressor p126, and that this interaction enhances antiviral defence when MpNAC7 is ectopically expressed in tobacco (Ros‐Moner et al. 2024). In this context, our observation that TRV CP recognises Fd1 from the early‐diverging vascular plant S. moellendorffii, together with the high conservation of the Fd C‐terminal [2Fe‐2S] domain across plant lineages, raises the possibility that ferredoxin proteins from non‐vascular plants may also represent conserved viral targets.

In conclusion, our results reveal a mechanism through which plant viruses recognise Fd1, a process likely established early in the evolutionary history of plant viruses. This recognition results in the degradation of Fd1, disrupting the photosynthetic electron transport chain and leading to photosynthetic defects in plants. Future studies will aim to assess the strength of this recognition throughout evolutionary processes and investigate how plants adaptively regulate this mechanism to mitigate photosynthetic impairments induced by viral infection.
