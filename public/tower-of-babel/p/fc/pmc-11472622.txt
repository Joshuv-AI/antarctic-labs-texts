Protocol for analyzing functional gene module perturbation during the progression of diseases using a single-cell Bayesian biclustering framework
Authors: Kunyue Wang, Yuqiao Gong, Zixin Yan, Zhiyuan Dang, Junhao Wang, Maoying Wu, Yue Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe pathogenesis of complex diseases involves intricate gene regulation across cell types, necessitating a comprehensive analysis approach. Here, we present a protocol for analyzing functional gene module (FGM) perturbation during the progression of diseases using a single-cell Bayesian biclustering (scBC) framework. We describe steps for setting up the scBC workspace, preparing and exploring input data, training the model, and reconstructing the data matrix. We then detail procedures for Bayesian biclustering, exploring biclustering results, and uncovering pathway perturbations.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Gong et al.1Subject areas: Bioinformatics, Genetics, Neuroscience

Before you begin

In recent years, the emergence of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our ability to sequence cells that were previously challenging to isolate or retrieve.2 This breakthrough in resolution offers unprecedented insights into the functionality and dysregulation of cells.3 Recently, there has been a notable increase in single-cell studies aiming to unravel the mechanisms underlying complex diseases based on transcriptional profiles.4,5,6 However, many of these studies lack an integrated analysis of functional gene modules (FGMs). The FGMs can reveal how genes collaborate to regulate biological processes. Given the analysis of functional gene modules plays such an important role in underlying mechanisms of complex diseases, there is a pressing need to identify these modules and their corresponding functional cell groups simultaneously.

In our previous study,1 we propose a novel single-cell biclustering method (scBC) that utilizes the Bayesian biclustering method to fill this gap and to resolve the issues of batch effects and high dropout rates. Moreover, we integrate existing biological knowledge, such as gene interactions and regulations, into the biclustering process using a Bayesian framework. This incorporation guides variable selection to better capture pathway information and biological signals.7 Our algorithm aims to have better performance in functionally related cell group identification, FGM finding, and cell-gene correlation pattern detection, which is urgently needed to assist the research of complicated diseases (Figure 1).

Flowchart of scBC procedure

The data input to scBC is first fed into a variational autoencoder (VAE). The xng refers to the gth gene in cell n, and sn is an extra dimension added for each cell to denote the batch annotation. After training, we can get a low dimensional approximate posterior distribution q(zn|xn,sn) conditional on sn. During the inference stage, we utilize non-linear mapping and the low-dimensional representation of each cell to reconstruct the expression data. We use the following likelihood function: π(xji|ρj,μji)=ρj1/22πexp{−ρj(xji−μji)2/2}. To reduce randomness, we decompose only the parameter matrix μ of the reconstructed data matrix. At the same time, in this process, we add a gene correlation prior for the gene-level result matrix W.

The following protocol outlines the specific steps of the scBC workflow to analyze the datasets of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) study, to showcase the robustness of our tool.

Prepare input data

Timing: 7 min

3.Data format transform.

Data format transform.

>>>import pandas as pd

>>>from scipy.io import mmread

>>>from anndata import AnnData

>>>from scipy.sparse import csr_matrix

>>>column_metadata = pd.read_csv('[column_metadata_file]', sep='∖t')

>>>gene_names = pd.read_csv('[gene_name_file]', names=['gene_name'])

>>>gene_names.set_index('gene_name', inplace=True)

>>>count_matrix = mmread('[count_matrix_file]')

>>>adata = AnnData(X=count_matrix.T, obs=column_metadata, var=gene_names)

>>>adata.X=csr_matrix(adata.X)

>>>adata.write_h5ad('[output].h5ad', compression='gzip')

Note: If your data consists of a read count matrix along with row and column names in txt files, you can run the above code to convert your data into standard h5ad format. If your data is already in h5ad format, you can skip this step. If you fail to transform, see troubleshooting 2.

4.Subsampling.

Subsampling.

We use stratified sampling to draw one out of ten cells and 2,000 highly variable genes for later analysis.

>>>import scanpy as sc

>>>adata = sc.read('[output].h5ad')

>>>adata.layers['counts'] = adata.X.copy()

>>>sc.pp.normalize_total(adata, layer='counts')

>>>sc.pp.filter_genes(adata, min_counts=1)

>>>sc.pp.log1p(adata, layer="counts")

>>>sc.pp.subsample(adata, fraction=0.1)

>>>sc.pp.highly_variable_genes(adata, n_top_genes=2000, layer="counts")

>>>adata = adata[:, adata.var['highly_variable']]

>>>adata.write("[processed_output].h5ad")

CRITICAL: Since scBC requires a raw count matrix as input, it's necessary to create a layer to store the raw, unnormalized count matrix. The layer should be specified during the normalization, log transformation, and highly variable gene (HVG) calculation steps.

CRITICAL: This step is crucial for extracting prior information for subsequent analysis. Skipping this step may result in accessing a large number of genes from the database, leading to errors due to excessive querying.

5.Partition data.

Partition data.

Note: Since our example AD dataset involves comparisons between patients at three different stages: no-pathology, early-pathology, and late-pathology, we need to first partition the data into three separate datasets based on these stages. If your data does not require partitioning, you can skip this step.

>>>import pandas as pd

>>>import numpy as np

>>>import scanpy as sc

>>>adata = sc.read_h5ad("[your_h5ad_file].h5ad")

>>>clinico_pathology_df = pd.read_csv("[your_meta_data]")

>>>adata.obs['Sample_Number'] = adata.obs['TAG'].str.split('.').str[-1].astype(int)

>>>clinico_pathology_df['Sample_Number'] = clinico_pathology_df['Subject'].str.extract(r'ROS(∖d+)').astype(int)

>>>merged_df = pd.merge(adata.obs, clinico_pathology_df[['Sample_Number', 'msex', 'pathology.group']], on='Sample_Number')

>>>for group in merged_df['pathology.group'].unique():

>>> subset_df = merged_df[merged_df['pathology.group'] == group]

>>> sample_numbers = subset_df['Sample_Number'].unique()

>>> subset_adata = adata[adata.obs['Sample_Number'].isin(sample_numbers)].copy()

>>> subset_adata.obs['sex'] = subset_df.loc[subset_df['pathology.group'] == group, 'msex'].values

>>> subset_adata.obs = subset_adata.obs[subset_adata.obs['Sample_Number'].isin(sample_numbers)]

>>> subset_adata.write_h5ad(f"{group}_adata.h5ad")

Optional: Our model also has optional parameters as follows:

adata: AnnData object with an observed n∗p matrix, p is the number of measurements and n is the number of subjects.

layer: if not None, uses this as the key in adata.layers for raw count data.

labels_key: key in adata.obs for label information. Categories will automatically be converted into integer categories and saved in adata.obs['_scvi_labels']. If None, assigns the same label to all the data.

size_factor_key: key in adata.obs for size factor information. Instead of using library size as a size factor, the provided size factor column will be used as offset in the mean of the likelihood. Assumed to be on linear scale.

categorical_covariate_keys: keys in adata.obs that correspond to categorical data. These covariates can be added in addition to the batch covariate and are also treated as nuisance factors (i.e., the model tries to minimize their effects on the latent space). Thus, these should not be used for biologically-relevant factors that you do not want to correct for.

continuous_covariate_keys: keys in adata.obs that correspond to continuous data. These covariates can be added in addition to the batch covariate and are also treated as nuisance factors (i.e., the model tries to minimize their effects on the latent space). scBC uncovers the pathway perturbation in AD progression

(A–C) In the merging of the six FGMs generated by biclustering, we selected three of them and conducted GO enrichment analysis. We selected several pathways with larger GeneRatio values for display. The color of the dots represents the adjusted p value, as indicated by the color bar on the right. The size of the dots represents the count of corresponding genes, as shown in the legend on the right.

(D and E) Taking the change of Astrocytes from the no pathology stage to the early pathology stage as an example, we calculate the significant genes in FGMs with increased and decreased proportions, and conduct GO enrichment analysis. We select several pathways with larger GeneRatio values for display.

(F and G) Perform GO enrichment analysis separately for the increase and decrease groups, selecting three statistically significant and biologically relevant pathways for each. Present the results in dot plots and bar plots, with orange indicating pathways with increased proportions and blue indicating pathways with decreased proportions.

(H) Taking excitatory neurons as an example, use pie charts to display the proportion of each FGM of this cell type in each stage of disease progression, thus detecting the changes in FGM proportion at different stages of disease progression.

Expected outcomes

Using our scBC program, we obtained several key results. The model’s S attribute allows us to extract biclusters, along with the genes and cells in each bicluster. Alternatively, the strong_class() function provides strong classification results, assigning each cell exclusively to a bicluster. Further analysis of these biclusters enables the aggregation into FGMs, identifying the cell populations where FGMs are active. Additionally, the W and Z matrices generated offer detailed information on biclustering at the gene and cell levels, respectively. For instance, the Z matrix can be used for strong classification by analyzing each cell’s loading on various biclusters. These FGMs can be utilized for downstream analyses, such as enrichment analysis and tracking changes in FGM proportions at different disease stages, as demonstrated in our workflow.

Limitations

While the intricate co-expression or co-regulation patterns within network biomarkers remain elusive in their precise and quantitative regulatory relationships, future investigations may delve into elucidating these relationships within the context of cell-specific network structures. This endeavor would facilitate more accurate inferences regarding the principles underlying perturbations in Functional Genomics Maps (FGMs) during disease progression. Additionally, given the time-consuming nature of matrix-factorization-based Bayesian optimization procedures, particularly when handling extensive datasets, the implementation of an unbiased subsampling procedure becomes imperative. Addressing the challenge of accelerating computations while maintaining algorithmic accuracy emerges as a key area of interest for future research.
