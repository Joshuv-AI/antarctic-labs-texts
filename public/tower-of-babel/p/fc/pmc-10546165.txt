Early use of oral semaglutide in the UK: A cost-effectiveness analysis versus continuing metformin and SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy
Authors: Hongye Ren, Sasha Berry, Samuel Joseph Paul Malkin, Barnaby Hunt, Stephen Bain
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMany people with type 2 diabetes experience clinical inertia, remaining in poor glycaemic control on oral glucose-lowering medications rather than intensifying treatment with a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, despite an efficacious, orally administered option, oral semaglutide, being available. The present study evaluated the long-term cost-effectiveness of initiating oral semaglutide versus continuing metformin plus sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor therapy in the UK.DesignOutcomes were projected over patients’ lifetimes using the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model (V.9.0). Clinical data were taken from the oral semaglutide and placebo arms of the patient subgroup receiving metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor in PIONEER 4. Costs, expressed in 2021 Pounds sterling (GBP), were accounted from a healthcare payer perspective.InterventionsModelled patients received oral semaglutide immediately (in the first year of the analysis) or after a 2-year delay, after which all physiological parameters were brought to values observed in the immediate therapy arm. During the simulation, patients intensified with the addition of basal insulin and, subsequently, by switching to basal-bolus insulin.ResultsImmediate oral semaglutide therapy was associated with improvements in life expectancy of 0.17 (95% CIs 0.16 to 0.19) years, and quality-adjusted life expectancy of 0.15 (0.14 to 0.16) quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), versus a 2-year delay. Benefits were due to a reduced incidence of diabetes-related complications. Direct costs were estimated to be GBP 1423 (1349 to 1496) higher with immediate oral semaglutide therapy versus a 2-year delay, with higher treatment costs partially offset by cost savings from avoidance of diabetes-related complications. Immediate oral semaglutide therapy was therefore associated with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of GBP 9404 (8380 to 10 538) per QALY gained versus a 2-year delay.ConclusionsImmediate oral semaglutide is likely to represent a cost-effective treatment in people with type 2 diabetes with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor in the UK.Trial registration numberNCT02863419.Keywords: HEALTH ECONOMICS, DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, ORAL MEDICINE

Introduction

Diabetes affects more than 4.9 million people in the UK, with type 2 diabetes accounting for around 90% of cases and with more than GBP 19 billion spent on treating the disease in 2021.1 2 Approximately 80% of National Health Service (NHS) diabetes-related expenditure is associated with the treatment of diabetes-related complications, the long-term incidence of which have been associated with short-term changes in physiological parameters such as glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), blood pressure and body weight.1 3–11 Treatment guidelines for type 2 diabetes published by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) therefore aim to lower HbA1c and control associated risk factors, and recommend metformin as a first-line pharmacological therapy followed by addition of other oral glucose-lowering, antidiabetic medications (OADs) as second-line and third-line therapies, including sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors.12 The recent update of these guidelines also indicated SGLT-2 inhibitors as an add-on to metformin in patients with high risk of or existing cardiovascular disease.12 Indeed, real-world evidence has shown that metformin is widely used as a first-line medication in the UK, with SGLT-2 and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors being increasingly used as second-line therapies.13 14

However, a number of people with type 2 diabetes are subjected to clinical inertia, defined as the reluctance to intensify treatment despite poor glycaemic control. This can manifest due to fear of medication side effects (eg, hypoglycaemia), concerns around weight gain, or the increasing complexity of treatment regimens that require familiarisation with new administration methods (particularly injectable medications).15–18 In the UK, this is evident in the number of people remaining on OADs despite other efficacious medication classes, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, being available.19 20 NICE guidelines recommend GLP-1 receptor agonists only if triple therapy with metformin and two other OADs is ineffective, not tolerated or contraindicated and only in people with a body mass index (BMI)≥35 kg/m2 and psychological or medical problems associated with obesity, or people with a BMI<35 kg/m2 for whom insulin therapy would have significant occupational implications or where weight loss would benefit obesity-related comorbidities.12

Previously, GLP-1 receptor agonists were only available as injectable formulations, a possible factor limiting uptake.15 18 However, oral semaglutide is a novel GLP-1 receptor agonist developed for one time per day oral administration, which has demonstrated improvements in HbA1c and BMI versus other OADs, including the DPP-4 inhibitor sitagliptin and the SGLT-2 inhibitor empagliflozin in the PIONEER clinical trial programme.21 22 These clinical benefits translated to oral semaglutide being indicated as a cost-effective medication in the UK in a previous long-term analysis, which aimed to elucidate the cost-effectiveness of oral semaglutide in people with type 2 diabetes with inadequate glycaemic control on one to two OADs.23 Similar analyses have evaluated the long-term cost-effectiveness of oral semaglutide versus empagliflozin and dulaglutide in Portugal, and one time per week injectable semaglutide versus high-dose dulaglutide and liraglutide in the UK.24–26 However, questions remain over the impact of introducing oral semaglutide versus continuing therapy with the oral agents recommended by NICE, specifically metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor, and the present study aimed to build on these previous analyses to address this pertinent research question using similar methodology.

Two trials in the PIONEER programme have assessed oral semaglutide in populations with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin with or without an SGLT-2 inhibitor: PIONEER 4 and PIONEER 7.27 28 A substantially greater number of patients were receiving metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor at baseline in PIONEER 4, which compared oral semaglutide 14 mg with liraglutide 1.8 mg and placebo, with oral semaglutide associated with significantly improved HbA1c and BMI versus placebo, factors that could influence the incidence of long-term diabetes-related complications.3–8 10 29

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the long-term cost-effectiveness of early use of oral semaglutide in people with type 2 diabetes with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor in the UK, compared with remaining on their current therapies and delaying oral semaglutide initiation for a further 2 years, based on subgroup data from PIONEER 4.

Materials and methods

Choice of model and approach

Long-term clinical and cost outcomes were evaluated using the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model (V.9.0). The structure, functions, assumptions and capabilities of the model, as well as two validation studies, have been previously described.30–32 Outcomes were projected over patients’ lifetimes (up to 50 years), as per the guidance on evaluating the cost-effectiveness of diabetes interventions, and discounted at 3.5% per annum.33 34 The base case analysis used the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) 68 risk equations to predict outcomes. Background mortality was captured from UK-specific life tables published by the WHO.35 The base case and deterministic sensitivity analyses used a first-order Monte Carlo approach, with second-order uncertainty captured in a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA).

Modelled treatment arms and long-term parameter progression

Two treatment arms were evaluated: immediate treatment with oral semaglutide 14 mg (with an uptitration through the 3 and 7 mg doses across a 2-month period, as per PIONEER 4), and a 2-year delay in receiving oral semaglutide 14 mg (during which time patients continued metformin plus SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy). ‘Immediate’ treatment was defined as patients receiving oral semaglutide from the beginning of the analysis, versus the 2-year delay in the comparator arm.

In the immediate oral semaglutide arm, after application of treatment effects in the first year of the analysis, HbA1c was modelled to follow the UKPDS 68 progression equation, with patients assumed to receive oral semaglutide until HbA1c exceeded 8.2% (66 mmol/mol, based on data from the initial therapy cohort from The Health Improvement Network (THIN)), after which patients treatment-intensified with the addition of basal insulin (online supplemental figure S1).36 HbA1c was then brought to and held at 8.2% for the remainder of the analysis. After 4 years of oral semaglutide plus basal insulin therapy (based on data published by Khunti et al, which indicated patients receiving basal insulin are treated for a median of 3.7 years before intensifying treatment), patients were assumed to discontinue oral semaglutide and SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy and intensify to basal-bolus insulin (alongside concomitant metformin), which they received for the remainder of their lifetimes.37 BMI was modelled to remain constant following application of treatment effects in the first year of the analysis, revert to baseline on initiation of basal insulin, and increase on initiation of bolus insulin based on the ‘insulin-experienced’ multivariate prediction equations published by Willis et al (online supplemental figure S2).38 Other physiological parameters, including blood pressure and serum lipids, were modelled to follow the progression equations built into the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model, based on the UKPDS and Framingham data, respectively (as described by Palmer et al).30

In the 2-year delay arm, patients continued to receive metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor for the first 2 years of the analysis, with HbA1c following the UKPDS 68 progression equation, before intensifying with the addition of oral semaglutide. HbA1c, BMI and all other physiological parameters were brought to the levels observed in the immediate oral semaglutide arm (thereby abolishing physiological differences between the arms when there was no difference in the received treatments), and further treatment intensifications and changes in physiological parameters were matched (online supplemental figures S1 and S2). The 2-year intensification delay was based on data published by Boye et al, which indicated that time on OADs before GLP-1 initiation was approximately 19 months (rounded to 2 years, as treatment switching in the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model can only occur at the end of an annual cycle).39

Clinical input data

Baseline cohort characteristics were sourced from the subgroup of patients with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor in the PIONEER 4 clinical trial (online supplemental table S1). Alcohol and tobacco consumption were not collected in PIONEER 4 and these were therefore assumed to be the same as the general UK population.40 41

Changes in physiological parameters were also sourced from the subgroup of patients with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor in PIONEER 4, with the immediate oral semaglutide arm based on the oral semaglutide 14 mg arm of the trial and the 2-year delay arm (ie, remaining on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor, reflecting clinical inertia) based on the placebo arm of the trial (table 1). Non-severe and severe hypoglycaemic event rates were based on data from the PIONEER 4 subgroup for each arm while patients received initial therapies, with these increasing to 4.08 and 0.10 events per person per year, respectively, when patients initiated basal insulin and to 10.20 and 0.70 events per person per year, respectively, when patients initiated bolus insulin (based on data from the UK Hypoglycaemia Study Group).42

Cost and utility input data

Costs were accounted from an NHS perspective in the UK and expressed in 2021 Pounds sterling (GBP). Direct costs captured pharmacy and management costs, based on published list prices in the Monthly Index of Medical Specialities database, and the costs of treating diabetes-related complications, based on methodology from a published literature review (online supplemental tables S2 and S3).43 44 Costs from the literature review were updated where possible by identifying the most recent publications (and thereby the most up-to-date costs) of annually updated sources, and inflated where necessary to 2021 GBP using the Health and Community Health Services Index published by the Personal Social Services Research Unit.43 45–52

Annual treatment costs were calculated based on resource use of the PIONEER 4 subgroup, with 100% of patients receiving metformin and an SGLT-2 inhibitor in both treatment arms. No needle or self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) use was assumed to be required while patients received OADs (metformin, an SGLT-2 inhibitor and oral semaglutide), but patients were assumed to require one needle and one SMBG test per day following initiation of basal insulin and four needles and four SMBG tests per day following initiation of bolus insulin.

A 2014 systematic review by Beaudet et al was used to inform health-state utilities and event-based disutilities, with hypoglycaemia disutilities sourced from a 2013 publication by Evans et al (published after the literature searches by Beaudet et al had been completed; online supplemental table S4).53 54 Beaudet et al reviewed the methods of the identified publications to ensure that they met the criteria of the NICE reference case, and this study informs the default utility set in the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model.

Key drivers of clinical benefits

The key drivers of clinical outcomes were evaluated through a series of separate analyses, which applied the differences in HbA1c, blood pressure, serum lipids, BMI and hypoglycaemic event rates in the immediate oral semaglutide arm in turn, with the other parameters set to the values observed in the comparator arm.

Sensitivity analyses

Uncertainty is inherent when extrapolating short-term clinical data to long-term model outcomes. Sensitivity analyses were therefore performed to assess the robustness of the base case results. These included: applying only differences between the treatment arms that were statistically significant (as shown in table 1); shortening the time horizon to 30, 20 and 10 years; applying clinical and cost discount rates of 0% and 6% in separate analyses; applying a UK-specific baseline cohort sourced from THIN; shortening and increasing the delay in receiving oral semaglutide in the comparator arm to 1 and 3 years, respectively; increasing and decreasing the length of time patients received basal insulin before intensifying to basal-bolus insulin to 3 and 5 years, respectively; applying the UKPDS 82 risk equations to predict model outcomes; increasing and decreasing the direct costs of treating and utilities associated with diabetes-related complications by 10% in separate analyses; applying an alternative BMI disutility, giving greater weight to changes in BMI; and PSA.55 56

Guideline scenario analyses

Further analyses were performed to evaluate the impact of modelling different treatment algorithms and intensification thresholds on cost-effectiveness outcomes. These aimed to capture further clinically realistic treatment algorithms and intensification patterns, in line with NICE guidelines, but were associated with significant uncertainty regarding changes in risk factors at each intensification step.12 These analyses included:

No oral semaglutide use in the comparator arm, in which patients were modelled to receive either immediate oral semaglutide (with all assumptions and effects in line with the base case analysis) or continue metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor until HbA1c exceeded 8.2% (66 mmol/mol). At this stage, patients in the comparator arm were assumed to treatment-intensify with the addition of basal insulin, and were then assumed to receive metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor plus basal insulin for 4 years. After this time, patients discontinued SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy and intensified to basal-bolus insulin, which they received for the remainder of their lifetimes. Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of the present study.

Results

Base case analysis

Long-term projections in patients with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor indicated that immediate treatment with oral semaglutide was associated with improvements in life expectancy of 0.17 years, and quality-adjusted life expectancy of 0.15 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), versus a 2-year delay (table 2). Immediate oral semaglutide use was associated with improved clinical outcomes due to a reduced incidence and delayed time to onset of diabetes-related complications versus a 2-year delay, with the mean time to onset of any diabetes-related complication estimated to be 0.4 years longer with immediate oral semaglutide.

Projections of long-term outcomes indicated that both life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy were improved with immediate oral semaglutide therapy versus a 2-year delay, at an increased cost from an NHS perspective (table 2). Immediate oral semaglutide use was therefore associated with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of GBP 9404 per QALY gained versus continuing metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor for a further 2 years for the treatment of type 2 diabetes in the UK.

Key drivers of clinical benefits

Applying the differences in each of the physiological parameters in turn showed that the key driver of clinical benefits with immediate oral semaglutide use was a greater difference in HbA1c versus continuing metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor, with quality-adjusted life expectancy improvements of 0.10 QALYs when only this difference between the treatment arms was applied. Greater reductions in blood pressure and BMI made smaller contributions to the clinical benefits with immediate oral semaglutide treatment, with observed improvements in quality-adjusted life expectancy of 0.02 and 0.03 QALYs, respectively, versus a 2-year delay in the analyses when only these differences were applied. Differences in serum lipid levels and hypoglycaemia rates made a negligible impact on incremental outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses

Wide-ranging sensitivity analyses showed that the results of the base case analysis were robust to changes in the input parameters and assumptions used (table 3). Immediate oral semaglutide use remained associated with ICERs below GBP 20 000 per QALY gained in all-but-one analysis, and ICERs below GBP 25 000 per QALY gained in all analyses. The largest increase in the ICER was observed when shortening the time horizon of the analysis to 10 years, with cost differences similar to the base case analysis but incremental clinical benefits with immediate oral semaglutide reduced, indicating the majority of benefits were observed over the long term. In this analysis, immediate oral semaglutide use was associated with an ICER of GBP 23 350 per QALY gained.

Guideline scenario analyses

Analyses evaluating different treatment algorithms and intensification thresholds (in line with NICE guidelines) showed that immediate oral semaglutide treatment remained cost-effective versus a treatment pathway without a GLP-1 receptor agonist and with different HbA1c intensification thresholds applied (table 3).

Modelling the comparator arm with no addition of oral semaglutide (instead intensifying from metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor first with addition of basal insulin and subsequently discontinuing SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy and initiating basal-bolus insulin) led to reduced costs, but also substantially reduced life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy. Immediate oral semaglutide therapy was associated with improvements in quality-adjusted life expectancy of 0.53 QALYs, increased costs of GBP 4218 and a consequent ICER of GBP 7962 per QALY gained versus a treatment algorithm without a GLP-1 receptor agonist.

Applying HbA1c thresholds of 7.5% (58 mmol/mol) and 8.0% (64 mmol/mol) for treatment intensification with the addition of basal insulin also did not change the conclusions of the analysis. Both modelled treatment arms were associated with reduced quality-adjusted life expectancy, but also reduced costs, with incremental outcomes remaining comparable to the base case analysis. Immediate oral semaglutide therapy was therefore associated with ICERs of GBP 10 608 per QALY gained in the analysis using a 7.5% HbA1c threshold, and GBP 9859 per QALY gained in the analysis using an 8.0% HbA1c threshold.

Discussion

The present analysis has projected that, while differences in quality-adjusted life expectancy and costs were small, immediate oral semaglutide therapy is likely to be a cost-effective treatment option for people with type 2 diabetes with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor, compared with remaining on their current therapies and delaying oral semaglutide initiation for a further 2 years. Projected clinical benefits were due to greater short-term improvements in HbA1c, blood pressure and BMI with oral semaglutide translating to a reduced cumulative incidence and delayed time to onset of diabetes-related complications over the long-term. These findings should help to drive policy recommendations by NICE, and help guide treatment decisions made by physicians for people with type 2 diabetes in the UK.

An original aspect of the present study was the evaluation of a novel research question for the cost-effectiveness of diabetes interventions. Previous analyses have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of oral semaglutide, including a study in the UK versus SGLT-2 inhibitor empagliflozin, DPP-4 inhibitor sitagliptin and injectable GLP-1 receptor agonist liraglutide.23 24 57 However, these studies aimed to answer a different research question to the present study, namely the cost-effectiveness of oral semaglutide versus a comparator initiated at the same time point. As demonstrated by published real-world evidence and current NICE guidance, it is rare for GLP-1 receptor agonists to be indicated or prescribed as second-line or even third-line treatment options in the UK, despite European-wide guidelines published by the European Association for the Study of Diabetes indicating SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists as equal second-line options, particularly for people with established or high risk of cardiovascular disease.12–14 58 The present study was therefore designed to answer a different research question for the UK setting, specifically whether immediate oral semaglutide initiation could improve health outcomes for people with type 2 diabetes while providing value for money for the NHS. The guideline scenario analyses therefore represent another key strength of the present study, as these analyses were designed to follow the intensification thresholds defined by NICE to the letter, rather than the real-world threshold used in the base case analysis, and immediate oral semaglutide use was shown to be cost-effective in all of these scenarios.12 Moreover, based on the projected reduced incidence of diabetes-related complications with immediate oral semaglutide and a recently published analysis by Fordham et al, which replaced the costs of diabetes-related complications in the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model with carbon dioxide equivalent emissions for each complication, early oral semaglutide use would likely lead to reduced carbon emissions versus a 2-year delay.59

Use of a more clinically realistic treatment algorithm and intensification approach required modification of key modelling assumptions that were not in line with any previous cost-effectiveness analysis of oral semaglutide, and use of these assumptions could be seen as a limitation.23 24 57 For example, the duration of poor glycaemic control in patients receiving metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor prior to enrolment in PIONEER 4 was unclear, with an assumption that these patients reflected those experiencing clinical inertia in the UK. However, there is growing clinical interest in modelling more realistic treatment algorithms for type 2 diabetes, and all assumptions were informed by published data in lieu of long-term studies evaluating changes in surrogate physiological parameters (or even direct health outcome measures) with modern diabetes interventions, which are currently lacking.37–39 These aspects of the analysis were also chosen to adequately represent real-world clinical practice while preserving modelling fairness, with differences in clinical and cost parameters only maintained while there was a difference in received treatments. Moreover, all key assumptions in the analysis were tested in wide-ranging sensitivity analyses, in which immediate oral semaglutide use remained cost-effective.

A limitation inherent to all long-term modelling studies was the extrapolation of outcomes from short-term clinical data. However, in the absence of long-term clinical trial data, modelling studies represent arguably the best option for informing decision-making for treatments of type 2 diabetes. Indeed, computer modelling guidance for diabetes interventions recommends projecting outcomes over patients’ lifetimes, and every effort was made to minimise uncertainty, through the extensive sensitivity analyses and the use of a published and validated type 2 diabetes model.20

While the present study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of immediate oral semaglutide use versus continuing metformin plus SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy, unanswered questions remain over the cost-effectiveness of immediate oral semaglutide use versus continuing DPP-4 inhibitor therapy. However, no clinical trial data currently exist assessing oral semaglutide in a population with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus a DPP-4 inhibitor versus placebo, meaning a vast number of assumptions would be required to prepare the analysis. However, given the improvements in physiological parameters demonstrated with oral semaglutide versus DPP-4 inhibitor sitagliptin in the PIONEER 3 trial, and the subsequent findings of cost-effectiveness analyses, it could be inferred that immediate oral semaglutide use is likely to represent a cost-effective treatment option in this population.21 23 57 Nonetheless, future clinical (and, subsequently, cost-effectiveness) studies should focus on elucidating the effects of initiating oral semaglutide in this population.

Conclusions

Based on clinical trial data, immediate treatment with oral semaglutide is likely to represent a cost-effective treatment option for people with type 2 diabetes with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor in the UK, compared with remaining on their current therapies and delaying oral semaglutide initiation for a further 2 years.
