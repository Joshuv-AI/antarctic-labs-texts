Developing a Silver Nanocomposite With Quorum-Quenching Enzyme Ag-Est816 to Prevent Periodontitis
Authors: Zelda Ziyi Zhao, Xinjiong Fan, Chun Hung Chu, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to synthesise a novel silver nanocomposite incorporating quorum-quenching enzyme N-acyl-homoserine lactone-lactonase Est816 (Ag-Est816) to inhibit periodontal biofilm formation.MethodsA one-pot reaction was employed to synthesise Ag-Est816 using silver nitrate, branched polyethyleneimine, and Est816. Ag-Est816 was characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Material stability was assessed by inductively coupled plasma spectrometry to determine the cumulative release rate of Ag⁺ and by a colorimetric enzyme activity assay to determine the retention rate of enzyme activity. Cytotoxicity toward human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and immunofluorescent staining. Biomass of periodontal biofilm (Porphyromonas gingivalis and Streptococcus sanguinis) was analyzed using a crystal violet staining assay; viability and thickness were examined with confocal laser scanning microscopy. The biofilm and bacterial cells were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and TEM. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) served as the control.ResultsDLS and TEM revealed spherical particles with a diameter of 98 ± 5 nm. XPS confirmed silver coordination bonds formation. The cumulative release rate of Ag⁺ was 0.19 ± 0.01%. The retention rate of enzyme activity was 93.3 ± 0.4% at 48 hours. Ag-Est816 was non-cytotoxic to HGFs, as evidenced by normal cytoskeletal morphology and no difference in cell proliferation compared to the PBS group (P = .36). The biomass in the Ag-Est816 and PBS groups was 0.82 ± 0.05 and 2.77 ± 0.16 (P< .001), and their live/dead ratios were 46 ± 6% and 80 ± 5%, respectively (P< .001). SEM revealed dispersed biofilms, and TEM showed bacterial cells with disrupted membranes in the Ag-Est816 group.ConclusionThis study developed a novel Ag-Est816 antibacterial nanocomposite effective against periodontal biofilm.Clinical significanceAg-Est816 warrants future investigation for the localized control of periodontal biofilms.Keywords: Quorum sensing, N-acyl-homoserine lactones, Biofilms, Periodontitis, Oral microbiota, Silver nanoparticles

Introduction

Periodontal diseases, including gingivitis and periodontitis, are among the most prevalent oral health issues globally, significantly impacting populations by contributing to tooth loss and systemic health problems.1 A critical factor in the persistence and progression of these diseases is the formation of resilient biofilms on periodontal tissues, composed primarily of a complex community of bacterial species.2 Within these complex communities, key pathogens like Porphyromonas gingivalis (P gingivalis) interact with early colonisers such as Streptococcus sanguinis (S sanguinis) to establish and stabilise the biofilm architecture. This synergistic relationship, wherein initial colonisers facilitate the attachment and integration of later pathogens, contributes to enhanced biofilm resilience and heightened resistance to conventional antimicrobial treatments.3, 4, 5, 6

The ability of bacteria to communicate through quorum sensing (QS) plays a crucial role in biofilm formation and virulence.7 QS relies on the production and detection of signalling molecules, such as N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs), to coordinate gene expression and collective behaviour within the biofilm. Notably, while AHLs are primarily produced by Gram-negative bacteria, they can be sensed and responded to by a broader range of organisms within a polymicrobial community, including Gram-positive species. This cross-talk can significantly modulate the structure and virulence of the biofilm consortium, as observed in both subgingival and supragingival plaques. The development of innovative strategies to disrupt biofilm formation and enhance the efficacy of antimicrobial agents is of utmost importance. One such approach involves the use of quorum quenching enzymes, which are capable of degrading or modifying QS signalling molecules, thereby interfering with bacterial communication and biofilm formation.8 Among these enzymes, AHL-lactonase Est816 has demonstrated potent quorum quenching activity against a wide range of AHLs.9

The limitations of current periodontal treatments highlight the need for advanced materials capable of targeting and disrupting multispecies biofilms. Currently used antimicrobial agents in clinical practice, such as chlorhexidine or topical antibiotics, are often limited in efficacy due to biofilm barriers, the development of drug resistance, or potential cytotoxicity to host cells.10 In recent years, nanomaterials have garnered significant attention for their antimicrobial properties and ability to enhance the effectiveness of traditional antibiotics.11 The development of sophisticated nanocomposites offers a promising solution. Nanocomposites can integrate multiple functional components to exert multifaceted antimicrobial activities, potentially overcoming the defensive mechanisms of biofilms.12

This research focuses on the synthesis and characterisation of a novel nanocomposite material, comprising AHL-lactonase (Est816), silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), and branched polyethyleneimine (BPEI). Each component is selected for its unique biofilm-disruptive capability, with the combination anticipated to produce an additive effect. (1) Est816 is a thermostable AHL-lactonase with catalytic efficiency against medium- and long-chain AHLs, including those produced by periodontal pathogens.13,14 It plays a pivotal role in this nanocomposite due to its ability to interfere with QS. By enzymatically degrading these signalling molecules, Est816 disrupts bacterial communication, effectively preventing the establishment and proliferation of biofilms. (2) AgNPs have been known for their broad-spectrum activity and ability to target multiple bacterial structures. The inclusion of AgNPs in the nanocomposite contributes instant bactericidal effects, working primarily by releasing silver ions that penetrate and destabilise bacterial cell membranes, leading to cell death. (3) BPEI serves as both a structural and functional component in the nanocomposite. Its high cationic charge density promotes interaction with the negatively charged bacterial cell membranes and biofilm matrix, disrupting cohesion and integrity. Moreover, BPEI provides a versatile platform for functionalization, offering numerous active sites for anchoring the enzyme and silver, thus enhancing the overall stability and functionality of the nanocomposite. Thus, the synthesis of the Ag-Est816 nanocomposite involves optimising the ratios of each component to maximise antimicrobial and biofilm-disruptive effects.

Given that current therapies remain insufficient to effectively control pathogenic biofilms causing periodontitis, this study aimed to synthesise a novel silver nanocomposite with the quorum-quenching enzyme Est816 (Ag-Est816) for the inhibition of periodontal biofilm.

Materials and methods

Materials preparation

P gingivalis (ATCC 33277) was cultured anaerobically in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (3.6% BHI broth, 0.5% yeast extract, 5.0 μg mL−1 hemin, and 1.0 μg mL−1 vitamin K1). S sanguinis (ATCC 10556) was cultured anaerobically in BHI broth (3.6% BHI broth, 0.5% yeast extract). The mid-log phase cultures of P gingivalis and S sanguinis (1 × 107 CFU mL−1) were used in the subsequent experiments. The Est816 was prepared and purified according to our established protocols, and its molecular mass was confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis.9,15,16 Human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) (HGF-1, ATCC CRL-2014) were used in the following experiments.

Synthesis process

The Ag-Est816 was synthesised in the absence of light. A total of 25.5 mg of BPEI (Mw 40k) was dissolved in 16.25 mL of deionised water and added to a 100 mL flask. An 8.75 mL of Est816 solution (30 U mL−1) was then added to the flask. After stirring for 30 minutes, a 12.5 mL silver nitrate solution (0.68 mg mL−1) was added dropwise to the mixture. Then the mixture was reacted for 3 hours at 25 °C under magnetic stirring. The nanocomposite was stored at 4 °C in a light-proof bottle for further use. The ratios of BPEI, Est816, and AgNO₃ were optimised based on the principles established for enzyme-Ag-polymer nanocomposites.17 The molar ratio of BPEI to Ag⁺ (1:78) was selected to ensure sufficient Ag-N coordination for nanoparticle stability and controlled ion release. The enzyme-to-silver ratio (31 U/mg Ag) was determined through preliminary antibacterial assays to balance the quorum-quenching function of Est816 with the bactericidal activity of silver, aiming for a dual effect against biofilms. The final working concentrations (5 µg mL−1 of silver and 6 U mL−1 of enzyme activity) were chosen to maximise antimicrobial efficacy while maintaining cytocompatibility with host cells.

Physicochemical characterization

The range of particle size of Ag-Est816 was examined by a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyser (NanoBrook 90Plus PALS, Brookhaven Instruments Corporation). The morphology of Ag-Est816 was evaluated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-F200, JEOL Instruments Corporation). The coordination of silver in Ag-Est816 was analysed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific Incorporation). All characterisations were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Material stability

The yellowness index of Ag-Est816 after storage at 4, 25, and 37 °C was determined by measuring the absorbance at 400 nm using a UV-Vis spectroscope (UV3600, Shimadzu Corporation). The release concentration of silver ions from Ag-Est816 was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP) (SPECTRO ARCOS ICP-OES analyser, SPECTRO Analytical Instruments Corporation, Kleve, Germany). The enzyme activity of Ag-Est816 was determined by measuring the hydrolysis of ρ-nitrophenylthiol acetate to ρ-nitrophenol at 405 nm using a UV-Vis spectroscope (UV1800, Shimadzu Corporation). Stability assays were conducted with three independent samples (n = 3).

Material cytotoxicity

HGFs were used to evaluate the cell toxicity of Ag-Est816. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used as a control, and added at the same proportion as Ag-Est816. The final working concentrations of silver reached 5 μg mL−1 with enzyme activity of 6 U mL−1 in Ag-Est816. These concentrations were selected based on previous studies showing biocompatible Ag⁺ limits (≤ 10 µg mL−1) and enzyme activity levels (≤ 12 U mL−1) applied on HGFs.16,18 The CCK-8 reagent was used, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. CCK-8 assays were performed with six replicates per group (n = 6). HGFs were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well, treated with Ag-Est816 at the same working concentration, to further evaluate the effects on cell morphology. F‑actin (filamentous actin) and nuclei were stained with fluorescent dye Tetramethylrhodamine (TRITC)‑phalloidin and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich Corporation), respectively. Cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope (DM300, Leica Camera AG). Fluorescence staining was carried out in triplicate (n = 3).

Antibiofilm property

Bacterial suspensions of P gingivalis and S sanguinis were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and coinoculated into 24-well plates, each well containing a sterile glass coverslip. Subsequently, the plates were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 hours. Then the dual-species biofilms were treated with 0.5 mL of Ag-Est816 (final working concentrations of silver reached 5 μg mL−1 with enzyme activity of 6 U mL−1), Est816 (6 U mL−1), AgNPs suspension (5 μg mL−1) and PBS (control) for 15 minutes, respectively. After treatment, planktonic cells and residual agents were removed by gently washing the biofilms three times with sterile PBS. This exposure time was chosen to simulate a clinically feasible topical application, such as in mouth rinses or local gels, and aligns with previous reports demonstrating rapid bacterial membrane disruption by AgNPs and the enzymatic quenching efficacy of Est816 on AHL signals.14,18

Following the treatments described above, biofilms were prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis. Samples were fixed with glutaraldehyde and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Morphological examination was then performed using a scanning electron microscope (GeminiSEM 300, Carl Zeiss AG). SEM imaging was conducted on three independent biofilm samples (n = 3).

The biofilm biomass was quantified by crystal violet staining. After treatment, samples were incubated in 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 minutes. Unbound dye was removed by rinsing with PBS, and the samples were air-dried completely. Then the crystal violet bound to the biofilms was eluted with 200 μL of 95% ethanol for 30 minutes. The optical density of the eluate was finally determined at 590 nm. Crystal violet staining analysis was performed with six biological replicates (n = 6).

Biofilm viability and thickness were evaluated by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The dead and live bacterial cells were stained by SYTO 9 (3.34 mM) and PI (20 mM) (LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific Incorporation). The images were obtained by a confocal microscope (LSM880, Carl Zeiss AG). CLSM analysis was performed with six biological replicates (n = 6). The acquired Z‑stack images were analysed using two software tools to extract quantitative parameters of biofilm thickness and bacterial viability. Biofilm thickness was determined from the Z‑stack images using the ZEN (Blue edition, Carl Zeiss AG) software. The vertical distance from the substrate surface (first slice with detectable bacterial signal) to the topmost slice where bacterial signal was observed was measured as the local thickness. The live/dead ratio was calculated based on the fluorescence intensity of the two viability stains using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). A maximum intensity projection was generated from the Z-stack for both the green (SYTO 9, live cells) and red (PI, dead cells) channels. The integrated fluorescence intensity of each channel within a defined region of interest (ROI) covering the biofilm area was measured. The live/dead ratio was then expressed as the ratio of the green fluorescence intensity (live) to the red fluorescence intensity.

Biofilm exopolysaccharide (EPS) was evaluated by phenol-sulfuric acid assay. The biofilms were washed three times with PBS and dried for 20 minutes at room temperature. Then 80 μL of ddH2O, 80 μL of phenol (6%) and 400 µL of sulfuric acid (95%∼97%) were added into each well. The coverslips were covered with a lid mixed by shaking (5 minutes at 900 rpm, room temperature) and incubated for 30 minutes for the colour reaction. Then the solution was transferred into a new 96-well plate and measured at 450 nm. EPS analysis was performed with six biological replicates (n = 6).

Antibacterial property

To elucidate the direct antibacterial effect of Ag-Est816 at the ultrastructural level, morphological changes in P gingivalis and S. sanguinis were assessed by TEM. Following treatment with Ag-Est816 or PBS, bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation. Observations were conducted on a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 20, Thermo Fisher Scientific Incorporation). TEM observations were made on three separately prepared bacterial samples per group (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0, GraphPad Software Company). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for a normality test to check the normal distribution of the data collected. Student t test was used for comparisons between two groups. One‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for comparisons among more than two groups. The significance level was set at 0.05. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates (n ≥ 3).

Results

Synthesis and characterisation of Ag-Est816 nanocomposite

DLS analysis indicated a homogeneous size distribution of Ag-Est816 in aqueous solution with an average diameter of 98 ± 5 nm. TEM imaging confirmed the formation of well-dispersed, spherical nanoparticles of Ag-Est816, which was consistent with the DLS result (Figure 1A). XPS analysis provided conclusive evidence for the formation of Ag-Est816. The appearance of N-Ag characteristic peaks at 398.3 eV in the N 1s spectrum proved the coordination between Ag⁺ ions and the nitrogen atoms, confirming the formation of the N-Ag coordinate bond. The spin-orbit splitting of the 3d orbital of Ag indicated that silver did not exist in a single chemical state but coexisted as zero-valent metallic silver (Ag⁰) and monovalent silver ions (Ag⁺) in Ag-Est816 (Figure 1B).

Stability of Ag-Est816 nanocomposite

The yellowness index of Ag-Est816 following 48 hours of storage at 4, 25, and 37 °C was 0.99 ± 0.01, 1.01 ± 0.01 and 1.06 ± 0.08, respectively (P = .08). The yellowness index remained stable, demonstrating robust colloidal stability across refrigerated, ambient, and physiological temperatures. It indicated that almost no Ag+ was reduced to AgNPs, nor Ag0 was oxidised to Ag2O in Ag-Est816. ICP analysis revealed a sustained but exceptionally slow release of Ag⁺ ions, with a cumulative relative release concentration of less than 0.2% (0.19% ± 0.01%) over 48 hours (Figure 1C). This controlled ion release profile is crucial for long-term efficacy and reduced toxicity. Concurrently, the enzyme activity assay showed that Ag-Est816 retained about 90% (93.3% ± 0.4%) of its initial lactonase activity after synthesis and over 48 hours. This indicates that neither the conjugation process nor the nanocomposite environment significantly compromised the quorum-quenching function of Est816 (Figure 1D).

Cytocompatibility evaluation

The CCK-8 assay showed that Ag-Est816 exhibited no significant inhibitory effect on HGF proliferation at the working concentration at Day 7 compared to the PBS group (1.96 ± 0.16 vs control 2.07 ± 0.10, P = .36) (Figure 2). This was further corroborated by immunofluorescence staining. The morphology of HGFs treated with Ag-Est816 remained normal, with well-organised actin cytoskeletons and prominent stress fibres, indistinguishable from the healthy, spindle-shaped morphology observed in the PBS group. These results confirmed the excellent cytocompatibility of Ag-Est816 nanocomposite (Figure 3).

Antibacterial mechanism

Ultrastructural changes in individual bacteria were examined with TEM to elucidate the antibacterial mechanism. Ag-Est816-treated P gingivalis and S sanguinis cells exhibited severe morphological damage, including cell wall rupture, membrane detachment, and leakage of intracellular contents. While bacteria in the PBS group displayed intact cell envelopes and homogeneous cytoplasm. This suggested that Ag-Est816 could penetrate and physically disrupt bacterial cells (Figure 5).

Discussion

This study successfully developed and characterised a novel silver nanocomposite, Ag-Est816, which integrates the bactericidal activity of silver nanoparticles with the quorum-quenching capability of the AHL-lactonase Est816. It investigated the potential of conjugates comprising Est816 and AgNPs as an advanced composite material for biofilm interference. The conjugates were characterised for their physicochemical properties, such as stability and enzyme activity. Subsequent biological evaluation involved assessing the nanocomposite's effects against periodontal dual-species (P gingivalis and S sanguinis) biofilms under in vitro conditions. This involved quantifying biofilm inhibition and examining changes in biofilm architecture. Our findings demonstrated that Ag-Est816 exhibited stability and biocompatibility, coupled with effects against periodontal biofilms, positioning it as a promising candidate for periodontal therapy.

A pivotal aspect of this combination is the preserved functionality of both components within Ag-Est816. The stability data is particularly illuminating. The controlled and slow release of Ag⁺ ions, as quantified by ICP, is crucial for maintaining a sustained antibacterial effect while minimising the rapid depletion of the active agent. Concurrently, the retention of 90% of Est816′s enzyme activity postsynthesis and under physiological temperatures confirms that the conjugation chemistry and the local nano-environment do not denature the enzyme. This retention of activity is nontrivial, as immobilisation processes can often lead to significant loss of function.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 The stability across a range of temperatures (4-37 °C) further suggests a robust formulation, enhancing its potential for storage and clinical handling.

A critical finding that significantly bolsters the translational potential of Ag-Est816 is its excellent cytocompatibility with HGFs. The historical concern regarding the cytotoxicity of AgNPs is effectively mitigated in our design. This can be attributed to the stabilising BPEI matrix, which likely modulates the release kinetics of Ag⁺ ions, preventing the cytotoxic “burst release” often associated with unprotected nanoparticles.17,24 The CCK-8 assay and normal cell morphology collectively demonstrate that the nanocomposite achieves a therapeutic window where antimicrobial concentrations are effective against pathogens yet harmless to host cells. This selective toxicity is a cornerstone of successful antimicrobial therapeutics.

The superior antibiofilm efficacy of Ag-Est816, as evidenced by the most significant reduction in biomass, architectural collapse, and bacterial viability, stems from a dual-mechanism action. These findings address the inherent limitations of single-mode therapies. While AgNPs exhibit potent bactericidal activity against planktonic cells, their efficacy against resilient, mature biofilms is severely limited by a narrow therapeutic window. The silver ion concentration required to disrupt dense biofilms often exceeds biocompatible levels, leading to unacceptable host cytotoxicity.25,26 In contrast, Est816 alone can disrupt quorum sensing and attenuate biofilm virulence but lacks direct bactericidal activity to eradicate established biofilms.15,16 The mechanism in Ag-Est816 is likely two-fold, operating through a ‘disarm and destroy’ strategy. First, the lactonase activity of Est816 ‘disarms’ the bacterial community by degrading AHL signalling molecules. This quorum quenching impairs the coordinated behaviour essential for biofilm maturation and stability, potentially leading to a looser, more permeable biofilm structure that is primed for invasion. Second, the silver component ‘destroys’ the compromised community. The destabilised biofilm matrix allows for enhanced penetration of the nanocomposite, enabling Ag⁺ ions to exert their bactericidal effect more effectively on the now-vulnerable bacterial cells. The observed significant reduction in EPS content upon Ag-Est816 treatment provides direct biochemical evidence for this matrix-destabilising phase. The degradation of EPS, a key structural component of the biofilm, corroborates the architectural collapse seen by SEM and facilitates the subsequent penetration and bactericidal action of the silver component. While the present study employed a quantitative phenol-sulfuric acid assay to measure total carbohydrate content, future studies utilising spatial imaging techniques, such as Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated lectin staining, would offer valuable insights into the precise distribution and disruption of specific EPS constituents within the biofilm. The physical disruption of bacterial cell integrity, as vividly captured by TEM, confirms the potent direct antibacterial action of the nanocomposite, leading to irreversible cell damage and death.

While the present study did not employ direct transcriptional profiling of QS-related genes, the observed correlation between Est816 and two-component system (TCS) pathways predicted by PICRUSt2 analysis in our previous study provided functional evidence.27 TCSs are the primary signal transduction machinery that bacteria use to detect and respond to environmental cues, including QS autoinducers such as AHLs. The inhibition of TCSs impaired the bacteria's ability to coordinate population-wide behaviours such as biofilm maturation. These findings provided molecular evidence that Est816 disrupted the QS regulatory network by interfering with its core signal transduction. Future studies incorporating transcriptomics or targeted qRT-PCR could further delineate the specific QS regulons affected by Ag-Est816.

Nevertheless, the limitations of this study should be acknowledged. While the present study confirms cytocompatibility and direct antibiofilm activity, its potential interactions with host tissues and inflammatory pathways (eg, effects on cytokine expression or reactive oxygen species production) remain to be elucidated. Investigating these aspects will be crucial for fully defining its therapeutic profile and potential to disrupt the pathogenic cycle of infection and inflammation in periodontitis.28, 29, 30 The use of a dual-species biofilm model, while providing valuable insights into interspecies interactions, does not fully recapitulate the immense complexity of the subgingival microbiome. Future investigations should employ more complex, multispecies consortia or in situ models to validate efficacy. Furthermore, long-term toxicity studies and in vivo animal models of periodontitis are essential next steps to comprehensively assess the safety and therapeutic efficacy of Ag-Est816 before clinical translation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a stable and biocompatible Ag-Est816 nanocomposite that effectively combated periodontal pathogens. It combined the biofilm-dispersing effects of quorum-quenching enzyme Est816 with the potent bactericidal activity of silver nanoparticles. The findings from this study underscore the great potential of Ag-Est816 as a novel and effective adjuvant strategy for the control and treatment of periodontitis, addressing the critical challenges of biofilm resistance and host tissue damage simultaneously.
