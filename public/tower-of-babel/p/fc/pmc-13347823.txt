Unbiased avidity-based isolation of antigen-specific T cells
Authors: Amanda Montoya, Meredith L Frank, Peixin Jiang, Hui Nie, Minying Zhang, Emily Bontekoe, Jared K Slone, Ludovica La Posta, Sofia Rosy Caterina Sorice, Tina Cascone, Maura Gillison, Don L Gibbons, Jianjun Zhang, Eleonora Dondossola, Lydia Kavraki, Pamela L Wenzel, John V Heymach, Alexandre Reuben
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer immunotherapies have significantly improved treatment efficacy and patient survival by exploiting antigen-specific T cells to eliminate cancer cells. However, current approaches for identifying and isolating antigen-specific T cells typically require prior knowledge of target antigens, limiting discovery, and reducing the ability to consistently detect rare tumor-reactive T cells. We therefore sought to develop an unbiased platform for the identification and enrichment of antigen-specific T cells using naturally processed and presented tumor antigens.MethodsWe developed ATTACH (Assessment of T cells Tethered to Antigen Class I Histocompatibility), a microfluidic platform that applies controlled shear stress and leverages tumor cells as a natural source of endogenous major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-peptide complexes to isolate antigen-specific T cells based on MHC/peptide binding avidity. ATTACH was evaluated in both human and mouse systems for its ability to enrich rare tumor-reactive T-cell populations and deplete bystander virus-specific T cells.ResultsATTACH resulted in up to a 10-fold enrichment of antigen-specific T cells across both human and mouse systems, enabling the isolation of clonotypes present at frequencies as low as 0.1%. In addition to enriching rare tumor-reactive T cells, ATTACH efficiently depleted virus-specific bystander T cells.ConclusionsATTACH provides a streamlined and unbiased approach for the rapid identification and isolation of antigen-specific T cells, and may facilitate the optimization of cellular therapies for the treatment of solid tumors.Keywords: Tumor infiltrating lymphocyte - TIL, Tumor microenvironment - TME, T cell

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL)-based therapies have demonstrated clinical benefit in solid tumors, but their efficacy remains limited by the high proportion of non-tumor-reactive T cells often present within tumors. Isolating these tumor-reactive T cells remains a major challenge. Existing strategies to identify antigen-specific T cells require prior knowledge of target antigens or rely on complex and resource-intensive workflows, limiting their scalability and discovery potential. Methods leveraging T-cell receptor avidity via microfluidic selection have been explored but remain largely incapable of rapidly enriching functional antigen-specific T cells for downstream applications.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study presents Assessment of T cells Tethered to Antigen Class I Histocompatibility (ATTACH), a microfluidic avidity-based platform that uses controlled shear stress and naturally presented tumor antigens to enrich antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. ATTACH enabled enrichment of rare tumor-reactive T-cell populations across both human and murine systems, including clonotypes present at very low frequencies, while simultaneously reducing non-specific bystander T cells. The platform also preserved T-cell functionality after isolation and achieved enrichment without requiring predefined antigens or highly specialized instrumentation.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings support the use of ATTACH as a rapid and accessible approach for isolating tumor-reactive T cells for research and cellular immunotherapy applications. By simplifying enrichment of rare antigen-specific T cells from heterogeneous populations, ATTACH could improve workflows for TIL-based therapies and facilitate development of personalized cancer immunotherapies. The platform also provides a useful framework for future avidity-based immune cell selection technologies.

Introduction

In the field of adoptive cell therapy (ACT), tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) have gained considerable attention for their ability to leverage the host’s immune system against cancer.1 Numerous studies have suggested that increased TIL density in solid tumors contributes to prolonged recurrence-free survival and lowers the risk of systemic recurrence.2–8 Additionally, in previous phase I and II clinical trials using TILs for the treatment of metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), tumor regression was achieved.2 9 However, only a small subset of patients experienced benefit, and durable responses were limited.2 9

One of the main challenges facing ACT using TILs is the limited frequency of antigen-specific T cells. Prior studies have demonstrated that within populations of TILs, only a minority of T cells exhibit tumor reactivity, with a significant presence of bystander T cells recognizing other non-tumor antigens, including those associated with viruses and/or bacteria.10–13 Notably, there is evidence supporting a correlation between clinical responses following TIL therapy and the reactivity of infused TILs toward mutated antigens.14 Typically, patients who achieve complete responses present higher amounts of neoantigen-specific CD8+ TILs in their infusion product.15 16 While TIL therapy has demonstrated efficacy in inducing tumor regression across various solid tumors, drawbacks such as diminished CD8+ T cell counts and the loss of tumor-specific clonotypes in the expanded TIL product have posed challenges in achieving success across broad patient populations. Thus, there has been a concerted effort to identify and isolate antigen-specific CD8+ TILs from solid tumors.

The leading strategy for identifying and isolating antigen-specific T cells involves the use of MHC/peptide multimers, which exploit the antigen-binding avidity of T cell receptors (TCRs).10 17 18 However, the use of multimers demands prior knowledge of the epitope of interest and the MHC presenting it.10 17 18 Therefore, inherent biases exist toward pre-selected or “known” antigens when utilizing multimers for the identification and isolation of antigen-specific T cells.19 20 Moreover, custom multimer synthesis can be both time-consuming and costly.21 Furthermore, functional assays have emerged that leverage systematic peptide screens, T cell trogocytosis,22 bifunctional receptors on antigen-presenting cells,23 tandem minigenes,24 and yeast antigen display libraries,25 among others. These methods have overcome many hurdles associated with antigen discovery and T cell isolation but remain restricted by the need for prior knowledge of which antigens or TCRs to further investigate.

Significant efforts have been invested to identify phenotypic attributes associated with antigen specificity, such as increased clonotype frequency and expression of markers including 4–1BB, PD-1, CD39, CD103, OX40, and TOX,26–32 among others. However, many of these markers delineate activated, exhausted, or tissue-resident T cells, meaning they can be expressed by both anti-tumor and bystander T cells. In silico-based approaches such as netMHC predict potential antigens based on epitope length, sequence, and MHC-binding affinity. However, these algorithms rely heavily on MHC-binding affinity and are not yet capable of consistently distinguishing whether an epitope will be immunogenic, thereby requiring substantial subsequent in vitro screening.33–36 Databases cataloging TCR sequences and their cognate antigens have also been developed. However, these fail to predict novel TCR binding because of their polymorphisms and promiscuity in TCR/epitope binding interactions.13 37 38 All in all, current methods for identifying antigen-specific T cells exhibit clear limitations.

Here, we describe a novel versatile platform called ATTACH (Assessment of T cells Tethered to Antigen Class I Histocompatibility), which enriches for antigen-specific T cells using tumor cells as a natural source of endogenously processed and presented MHC-peptide complexes. The process relies on the ability of antigen-specific T cells to recognize and bind to antigens presented on MHC molecules from target cells under a sustained, constant, low level of shear stress. Importantly, we show ATTACH can be used in both mouse and human systems and highlight its potential for future applications.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Lewis Lung Carcinoma (LLC-JSP) cells were transduced to endogenously express the H-2Kb-restricted OVA257-264 SIINFEKL antigen. Selection was maintained in transduced LLC-JSP-OVA cells by adding 0.05 mg/mL of Hygromycin B (Corning, #30-240-CR) to cell culture media. The NSCLC cell line H1975 was obtained from ATCC (RRID:CVCL_1511) and was transduced to express the HLA-A*03:01 (Vector Builder, VB241121-1833ydj), HLA-A*02:01 (Vector Builder, VB250619-2298uvq), and the FOXM1 genes (cloned with pENTER vector by GenScript USA, Inc). The K562 cell line was obtained from ATCC and was transduced to express HLA-A*02:01 and mutant HER2. All cell lines were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 in complete RPMI 1640 media (Corning, #10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GenDEPOT, F601-050) (referred to as “complete RPMI media” hereafter). Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using the Lonza MycoAlert Detection Kit (#LT07-218).

Murine CD8+ T cell isolation and expansion

Isolated spleens were disrupted in complete RPMI media, and cell suspensions were passed through a 40 µm nylon mesh strainer (Fisher Scientific, #22363547) to remove debris and aggregates. Strained cell suspensions were pelleted by centrifugation at 600×g for 6 min. All centrifugation steps were performed at room temperature. Red blood cell lysis buffer (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-296258) was prepared at 1× concentration in sterile Millipore water and added to the pelleted cell suspension at a volume 10× that of the cell pellet. After incubating for 12 min at room temperature, complete RPMI media was added at 10× the volume of red cell lysis buffer to quench the lytic reaction. Cells were centrifuged at 600×g for 6 min and resuspended at ≤1×108 nucleated cells/mL in Ca2+ and Mg2+ free 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA (hereafter referred to as the “recommended media”). 50 µL/mL of normal rat serum (Sigma-Aldrich, #R9759) was added to samples before transferring them to 5 mL polystyrene round-bottom tubes. Negative CD8+ selection was used to isolate CD8+ splenocytes via magnetic sorting under sterile conditions using the STEMCELL Technologies EasySep mouse CD8+ isolation kit (#19853) per manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated CD8+ T cells were counted using VitaStain AOPI staining solution (Nexcelom Biosciences, #CS2-0106) on a Nexcelom Cellometer K2 fluorescent viability cell counter.

An expansion protocol was adapted from Lewis et al.39 All cells were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2. Freshly isolated CD8+ splenocytes from OT-I and C57BL/6 mice were resuspended in complete RPMI media supplemented with 50 uM 2-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #21985023) and 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium-Sodium Pyruvate (ITS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #51300044) and plated at 5×106 cells/well in 6-well Nunc plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #140675) precoated with 0.5 µg/mL anti-mouse CD3 (clone 2C11, Bio X Cell, #BE0001-1) and 5 µg/mL anti-mouse CD28 (clone 37.51, Bio X Cell, #BE0015-1). Cells were cultured without cytokine stimulation for 24 hours. Recombinant mouse IL-7 (R & D Systems, 407 ML-005) and IL-2 (R&D Systems, #402 ML-020) were added to final concentrations of 0.5 ng/mL and 0.1–0.4 ng/mL, respectively. Cells were cultured for another 24 hours before subculturing cells at 1×106 cells/well in non-coated Nunc 6-well plates in freshly made complete RPMI media supplemented with 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1% ITS, 0.5 ng/mL recombinant murine IL-7, and 0.1–0.4 ng/mL recombinant murine IL-2. Cells were subcultured every 24 hours for 8–10 days in freshly made complete RPMI media containing all supplements and cytokines. Cells were frozen at 80%–90% viability in FBS supplemented with 10% DMSO and stored in liquid nitrogen for later use.

Generation of TCR-T cells

Fresh leukapheresis products from healthy adult donors were purchased from Charles River and were obtained at U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-registered collection centers under written informed consent. Healthy donor PBMCs were processed within 48 hours of collection, preserved, and stored in liquid nitrogen. TCR-T cells targeting EGFR, FOXM1, E7, and HER2 were generated as follows. For retroviral production, 293GP packaging cells were seeded overnight on poly-D-lysine-coated 100 mm plates at 7×10⁶ cells per plate in complete DMEM. Cells were transfected with 6 µg of the pMSGV1-TCR construct and 4.5 µg of the RD114 envelope plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #L00015). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 hours post-transfection, pooled, and added to Retronectin-coated (20 µg/mL, Takara Bio, #T100B) non-tissue-culture-treated 24-well plates. Plates were centrifuged at 2000×g for 2 hours at 32°C to facilitate viral loading. CD8+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs by negative selection using the Human CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, #17953). Purified cells were activated with ImmunoCult Human CD3/CD28/CD2 T Cell Activator (STEMCELL Technologies, #10970) in the presence of 200 IU/mL recombinant human IL-2 and cultured for 48 hours in a 1:1 mixture of RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS and AIM V medium. Activated T cells were transferred onto the virally loaded plates and spinoculated at 800×g for 20 min at 32°C. This transduction step was repeated on the next day to enhance efficiency. Transduced T cells were assessed 3–4 days later by flow cytometry for surface expression of cognate tetramer. Cells were subsequently expanded for 1 week in 24-well plates and G-Rex culture vessels (Wilson Wolf, #80 660M) and cryopreserved in FBS containing 10% DMSO.

Flow cytometry

To measure H-2Kb presentation of the SIINFEKL antigen on target cells, an APC anti-H-2Kb bound to SIINFEKL antibody (clone 25-D1.16, BioLegend, #141606) was added to target cells at 0.2 mg/mL and incubated on ice for 30 min. Non-transduced target cells served as a negative control.

For CD8 and CD4 surface staining, anti-mouse CD4-FITC (clone RM4-5, BioLegend, #100509), and anti-mouse CD8-FITC (clone 53–6.7, BD Pharmingen, #553030), or anti-mouse CD8-PE (clone 100708, BioLegend, #100708) antibody was used, and immediately prior to flow cytometric analysis, 7AAD viability staining solution (BioLegend, #420404) was added to cells.

Tetramers were provided by the Baylor College of Medicine MHC Tetramer Production Core. Staining was performed for 30 min on ice prior to any other antibody staining.

Samples were acquired by the University of Texas MD Anderson Flow Cytometry & Cellular Imaging Facility and collected on BD FACSCanto, Beckman Coulter Gallios and Gallios 561 cytometers. Acquired data were analyzed using FlowJo V.10.8.0 software (BD Biosciences), gating on live single cells.

Immunofluorescence staining

Adherent LLC-JSP and LLC-JSP-OVA cells were detached and resuspended to ≤5×105 cells/mL in 1× PBS and stained with 6 µM of CellTracker blue CMAC dye (Invitrogen, #C2110) following manufacturer’s instructions. Then, cells were resuspended in complete RPMI media and immediately used for ATTACH. OT-I CD8+ T cells were thawed and washed prior to staining. Cells were resuspended to ≤3 ×105 cells/mL in 1× PBS+0.1% FBS and stained with 0.7 µM of CFSE dye (Invitrogen, #65-0850-85) for 8 min at room temperature, obscured from light. An equal volume of 100% FBS was added to quench staining before transferring cells to a 37°C water bath and incubating for 10 min. After staining, cells were washed with 1× PBS+2% FBS and resuspended to 1×106 cells/mL in complete RPMI media for immediate use in ATTACH. C57BL/6 CD8+ T cells were resuspended to ≤1 × 106 cells/mL in 1× PBS followed by staining with 500 nM of CellTracker Deep Red dye (Invitrogen, #C34565) or 250 nM MitoTracker Deep Red dye (Invitrogen, #M22426) for 30 min at 37°C, 5% CO2, washed, and resuspended in complete RPMI media for immediate use in ATTACH. For human experiments, T cells were resuspended to ≤1×106 cells/mL in PBS followed by staining with 250 nM of CellTracker Deep Red dye (Invitrogen, #C34565) or 10 µM of CellTracker Blue CMAC dye (Invitrogen, #C2110) for 30 min at 37°C, 5% CO2, washed, and resuspended in complete RPMI media for immediate use in ATTACH.

MIP-1β ELISA

Human MIP-1β quantification was performed using Invitrogen’s Human MIP-1β (CCL4) Instant ELISA Kit (#BMS2030INST) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance values were obtained using an Accuris SmartReader 96 microplate absorbance reader, and concentrations of unknown samples were interpolated using the standard curve’s equation of the line and absorbance values.40

ELISPOT

Following recovery from ATTACH, T cells from each fraction were pulsed with either 1 µg/mL of HER2 peptide (GenScript) or CEF peptide pool (AnaSpec CEF Control Peptide Pool). Peptide-pulsed T cells were then subjected to IFN-γ ELISpot analysis using the Human IFN-γ ELISpot kit (CTL, catalog # hIFNg-2M/2), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Spot-forming units (SFUs) were quantified and compared between fractions and stimulation conditions using a CTL S6 Entry M2.

Isolation of antigen-specific T cells in T-25

LLC-JSP cells were seeded in tissue culture-treated vented T25 flasks and placed at 37°C, 5% CO2 overnight to adhere. Cells were seeded at 80% confluency so that they would be at 95% confluency for ATTACH the following day. The day of ATTACH, adhered cells were pulsed with 10 µg/mL of SIINFEKL peptide by adding stock aliquots directly to T25 flasks containing adhered cells + 3 mL complete RPMI media. Cells were incubated for 1.5 hours at 37°C, 5% CO2. Meanwhile, expanded CD8+ T cells were thawed and resuspended to 1–2×106 cells/mL in complete RPMI media and placed in 6 well plates at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 1 hour. Following rest, T cell populations were stained as described above. Media containing SIINFEKL peptide was removed from flasks and flasks were washed with 1× PBS by gentle swirling. 1× PBS was removed and replaced with complete RPMI media containing the input population (either bulk OT-I splenocytes or a 1:1 mixture of OT-I: B6 CD8+ T cells). Part of this “input” subset was set aside at 37°C, 5% CO2 for downstream flow cytometric analysis. After a 90-minute incubation, media in flasks was carefully removed by pipette and placed on ice in a labeled tube (“aspirate” subset). Flasks were washed with 6 mL of 1× PBS, which was removed and placed on ice in labeled 15 mL tubes (“wash” subset). Finally, bound cells were detached with 0.25% Trypsin + 2.21 mM EDTA and transferred to labeled tubes (“detach” subset). Entire collections from the flask were stained and analyzed by flow cytometry as described above.

Microfluidic isolation of antigen-specific T cells

Sterilized ibiTreat: #1.5 polymer coverslip μ-Slide I0.4 luer tissue culture-treated channel slides (#80176), luer lock female connectors (#10825), elbow luer male connectors (#10802), and 1.6 mm silicone tubing (catalog #10842) were obtained directly from ibidi. Target cells were seeded in separate channel slides with complete RPMI and were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 overnight to allow target cells to adhere to the channel. The following day, slides were flushed by pipetting 125 µL of complete RPMI through channels to remove dead and detached cells prior to loading T cells. After fluorescently staining, CD8+ effector cells were loaded into channels at a 1:1 ratio of effector to target cells, followed by a 15-minute incubation.

Shear stress was applied to channels by infusion via 140 mL syringes filled with prewarmed complete RPMI media connected to a Harvard Apparatus PHD ULTRA automated syringe pump. For ramped “enrichment” experiments, applied shear stress increased linearly with time y=1.56x+0.5, where y represents shear stress and x represents time, and ranged from 0 to 20 dyne/cm2 (0–21.1 mL/min) over the course of 12.5 min. Images were taken at 2.06, 8.3, 16.1, and 20 dyne/cm2. For de-enrichment experiments where a low shear stress was constantly applied to wash off non-specific CD8+ T cells, a shear stress of 1 dyne/cm2 (1.05 mL/min) was selected based on the point at which OT-I CD8+ became detached from negative control slides containing LLC-JSP cells (no antigen) during ramped shear experiments. Shear stress of 1, 2, and 3 dyne/cm2 was used for the EGFR-specific T cells and 3 dyne/cm2 for FOXM1-specific T cells and HER2-specific T cells. Slides were visualized under Nikon A1RSi (Center for Advanced Microscopy, Department of Integrative Biology & Pharmacology at McGovern Medical School, UTHealth) or Nikon ECLIPSE Ti confocal microscopes using Nikon NIS-Elements AR 5.21.03 and Zeiss Axio Observer 7. Fluorescent images were captured within 1 region of interest (ROI) in the middle of the channel. The ROI was consistent across all slides within an experiment. For experiments using 1:1000 ratios, images were taken at three randomly selected ROIs both before and after ATTACH. The average counts across these ROIs were calculated and used for subsequent analysis.

Rapid immobilization of tumor cell lines

Ibidi µ-Slide VI0.4 channels were coated overnight at room temperature with 1 mg/mL lyophilized streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 430431). The following day, channels were washed with PBS and blocked with 2.5% BSA (MilliporeSigma, #9048-46-8) for 30–60 min at room temperature, followed by a wash with RPMI 1640. Tumor cell biotinylation was performed.41 Briefly, tumor cell lines were washed with PBS and resuspended at 0.5×10⁶ cells/mL. Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A39257) was added at a final concentration of 1 mM, and cells were incubated at 4°C for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed with RPMI and seeded into coated channels at 1×10⁶ cells per channel. Cells were allowed to adhere for at least 2 hours at room temperature, after which channels were gently washed with 1 mL of RPMI.

Live cell imaging

To assess cell death following ATTACH, 0.15 µM propidium iodide was introduced into the channels. Live time-lapse imaging was conducted using an EVOS microscope equipped with a heated environmental control chamber (37°C, 5% CO₂). Fluorescent and bright-field images were captured every 3 min for 24 hours using a 10× objective lens. The images were then stitched into a time-lapse sequence using ImageJ software (RRID:SCR_003070). For analysis, bounding boxes were drawn around cells using the OpenCV2 Python package (RRID:SCR_024202),42 and cell tracking across frames was performed using the OpenCV2 implementation of the CSRT tracking algorithm.43

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10 (GraphPad Software; RRID:SCR_002798). Ordinary one-way and two-way analysis of variance tests (with multiple comparisons) and paired t-tests were performed when appropriate. Quantification of fluorescent images captured during live imaging was performed using ImageJ Fiji software. Experiments were performed with independent biological replicates ranging from n=2 to n=4, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Manual recovery of antigen-specific T cells with ATTACH

In order to leverage the avidity of TCRs for their antigens, we envisioned ATTACH to include the following steps. First, a population of target cells would be seeded and allowed to adhere overnight. Second, a population of T cells would be added and allowed to co-incubate and engage the target cells through their TCR based on their cognate MHC-presented epitopes. Third, culture media would be aspirated, resulting in the removal of any debris and dead cells. Fourth, cells would be washed gently, with T cells lacking the ability to bind to their target cells through MHC I being removed. And lastly, remaining cells (target cells and antigen-specific T cells) would be detached using trypsin for subsequent downstream analysis and quantification (figures 1 and 2A). To investigate the feasibility of this process, in the first generation of ATTACH, we first used OT-I T cells which recognize the MHC-I (H-2Kb)-restricted OVA257-264 (SIINFEKL) epitope. Briefly, LLC-JSP cells transduced with an OVA257-264 minigene (LLC-JSP-OVA) to endogenously express and present the H-2Kb/OVA257-264 epitope were seeded in T-25 flasks overnight to be used as target cells (online supplemental figure S1A). The next day, bulk splenocytes from OT-I mice were added (Subset 1, S1) followed by a 90-minute co-incubation with tumor cells. Cells were then recovered in three subsets: aspirated culture media, debris and dead cells (S2), PBS used to wash cells and non-specific/unbound cells (S3), and antigen-specific T cells and their targets detached by trypsinization (S4), to assess the proportion of antigen-specific T cells within each. Interestingly and as expected, a gradual loss in CD4+OT I splenocytes and gradual increase in CD8+OT I splenocytes was observed with a fourfold enrichment for CD8+ in presence versus absence of H-2Kb/OVA257-264 (figure 2B–F). Furthermore, OT-I CD8+ enrichment was most significant in the final (S4) subset compared with earlier fractions, confirming differential binding avidities between antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and non-CD8+ T cells when exposed to OVA257-264-presenting cells.

Microfluidic recovery of antigen-specific T cells using ATTACH

We next sought to increase reproducibility of ATTACH by developing a microfluidic pipeline leveraging laminar flow. To do so, LLC-JSP-OVA cells were seeded overnight in chamber slides (online supplemental figure S1B). Subsequently, a 1:1 ratio of fluorescently labeled OT-I and C57BL/6 CD8+ T splenocytes was added to the channel followed by a 15-minute incubation. The reduced volume and height of the microfluidic system enabled rapid T cell settling and interaction with tumor cells, making this shorter incubation sufficient under these conditions. Next, a ramped pattern of shear stress, which increased linearly with time, was applied to channels via infusion by a programmable syringe pump to determine the level of shear stress needed to separate antigen-specific from non-specific CD8+ T cells. Shear stress values ranging from 0 to 20 dyne/cm2 were applied to channels over 12.5 min. Fluorescent images were taken at timepoints corresponding to shear stress of 2.06, 8.3, 16.1, and 20 dyne/cm2. Results showed a fourfold enrichment of OT-I CD8+ cells and a corresponding de-enrichment of C57BL/6 CD8+ splenocytes in the final recovered fraction, with the most pronounced enrichment occurring at low shear stress (<8.3 dyne/cm²) (figure 3A–D). However, a low viability of recovered T cells was observed at high levels of shear (online supplemental figure S1C,D).

Low shear microfluidic de-enrichment of non-specific T cells using ATTACH

To increase the versatility of ATTACH and enhance viability of recovered cells, we hypothesized that applying a constant low level of shear stress sufficient to remove non-specific T cells but low enough to retain antigen-specific T cells regardless of their avidity would be preferable (i.e., de-enrichment, figure 4A). Fluorescent imaging was used to assess T cell populations in real-time, and a constant rate of 1 dyne/cm2 of shear stress was applied via infusion for 5 min. Images were taken within a single ROI in the center of the channel before and after the assay (figure 4B,C). Quantification of CD8+ T cell proportions pre-ATTACH and post-ATTACH confirmed a fivefold antigen-dependent enrichment of OT-I CD8+ T cells in LLC-JSP-OVA slides compared with LLC-JSP slides (figure 4D, p=0.004).

To further elucidate the specificity of T cell enrichment, a heterogeneous CD8+ T cell population was next used for de-enrichment experiments. A 1:1 ratio of OT-I to C57BL/6 CD8+ T splenocytes was added to channels containing LLC-JSP-OVA cells, and 1 dyne/cm2 of shear stress was applied via infusion for 5 min. Quantification of fluorescent images pre-ATTACH and post-ATTACH revealed a twofold enrichment of OT-I CD8+ T cells (p=0.0151, figure 4D-F) with 15% recovery of input OT-I CD8+ T cells. Conversely, the average retention of C57BL/6 CD8+ T splenocytes was 8% and no different between channels seeded with either LLC-JSP or LLC-JSP-OVA (p=0.7561).

ATTACH allows recovery of human antigen-specific T cells

We next sought to evaluate the feasibility of ATTACH using primary human T cells. To do so, we used previously validated TCR-engineered T cells (TCR-T) recognizing an HLA-A*03:01-restricted epitope derived from EGFR.44 H1975 cells, which endogenously express the EGFR mutation, were transduced to express HLA-A*03:01 and seeded into microfluidic channels overnight. The next day, antigen-specific CD8+ T cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with bulk CD8+ T cells isolated from healthy donor PBMCs and added to the channels at a 1:1 ratio of effector to target cell (figure 5A). To de-enrich for non-specific T cells, we tested three different constant shear levels for 5 min to determine the amount of shear stress needed to enrich for EGFR-specific CD8+ T cells. We observed a progressive enrichment of EGFR-specific T cells as shear stress increased from 1 to 3 dyne/cm2. At 3 dyne/cm2, the proportion of antigen-specific T cells surpassed that of non-specific T cells, suggesting that this level of shear stress may be sufficient for the enrichment of EGFR-specific T cells (figure 5A–C).

To further evaluate the versatility of ATTACH, we tested these conditions using a different TCR-T population targeting an HLA-A*02:01 epitope derived from FOXM1 (figure 5D).45 Using CD8+ T cells isolated from HLA-A*02:01 healthy donor PBMCs (non-specific), we tested two different cell ratios (5:1 and 1:1). As shown in figure 5E, FOXM1-specific TCR-T cells were 2.6-fold (p=0.019) enriched compared with non-specific T cells in channels with a 1:1 (non-specific: FOXM1) ratio and by 4.4-fold (p=0.0084) in channels with a 5:1 ratio (figure 5E). Moreover, FOXM1-specific TCR-T cells were enriched by 22.4% (p=0.034) following ATTACH with an initial 1:1 input ratio, and by 35.2% (p=0.027) with an initial 5:1 input ratio (figure 5F,G). To confirm recovered T cells maintained their functional potential, channels were incubated overnight, and MIP-1β secretion and cytotoxicity were quantified, confirming functional potential following ATTACH (figure 5H, online supplemental figure S2A,B).

Selective depletion of bystander T cells using ATTACH

Lastly, we evaluated ATTACH’s ability to selectively enrich rare, antigen-specific T cells while simultaneously depleting bystander populations. Using a 1:1 mixture of HER2-specific TCR-T cells and non-specific CD8+ T cells, we collected the washed and retained fractions and stimulated them with either the HER2 peptide or a CEF peptide pool, a well-characterized mix of immunodominant viral epitopes derived from cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, and influenza virus, commonly used as a control for detecting memory T cell responses.46 ATTACH preferentially retained HER2-reactive (anti-tumor) T cells while depleting CEF-reactive (anti-viral) bystanders, as assessed by ELISpot (figure 6A,B). While anti-tumor responses were largely preserved (~100%), anti-viral responses were largely lost (~4%–22%).

To assess sensitivity, we next diluted antigen-specific populations to a 1:1000 ratio (0.1%). Even at this low frequency, ATTACH achieved a significant 10-fold enrichment of HER2-specific T cells along with a corresponding depletion of non-specific CD8+ T cells (figure 6C–E). These results highlight ATTACH’s robust ability to isolate rare, antigen-specific T cells from complex mixtures by enriching for desired clonotypes while depleting non-specific and bystander T cells.

Discussion

ACT using TILs has shown promise for the treatment of solid tumors.1–9 However, a key limitation is the considerable proportion of T cells present in the tumor microenvironment lacking specificity toward tumor antigens, limiting its effectiveness in targeting and eliminating cancer cells.10–13 Several methods have been developed to identify tumor antigen-specific T cells; however, these methods have proven time-consuming and costly. While automated approaches to assess TCR avidity have been previously described,47–49 these methods are primarily limited to measurement, whereas ATTACH functions as a platform for the isolation of T cells based on their avidity, with the added advantage of recovering viable, functional cells for downstream applications. Herein, we present a method which leverages antigen-specific T cell avidity, using tumor cells as a natural source of endogenously-processed and -presented peptide-MHC complexes to rapidly enrich antigen-specific T cells within a matter of minutes. In the first generation of ATTACH, we show the feasibility to enrich for antigen-specific T cells based on binding avidity to cognate antigens without any need for specialized microfluidics slides or equipment. Given that our assay focuses on MHC I antigens, the first generation of ATTACH demonstrated specific MHC I/CD8 interactions by enriching for CD8+ T cells in the final fraction with a fourfold greater retention of OT-I CD8+ T cells in flasks where OVA257-264 was presented, therefore, confirming antigen-specific CD8+ enrichment. Furthermore, microfluidic experiments demonstrated that shear stress can be effectively utilized to isolate antigen-specific T cell populations across different HLAs with low, constant shear stress proving sufficient to selectively deplete non-specific T cell clones. To our knowledge, these studies are the first to demonstrate the ability to isolate neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells via microfluidic shear stress.

Ashby and colleagues recently described an approach leveraging custom microfluidic chips for the isolation of tumor-associated antigen-specific CD8+ T cells,50 although the studies do exhibit numerous key technical and conceptual distinctions. First, ATTACH can be performed manually and without any specialized equipment. Next, through ATTACH, we demonstrate the efficacy of applying 10–100 fold lower levels of shear stress, while still achieving almost 10-fold enrichment, which may present key advantages. Increasing shear stress during microfluidic selection has been shown to enhance the purity of enriched high-avidity T cells; however, we have observed that higher shear stress levels were associated with an increase in cell debris in our hands, potentially due to mechanical damage or increased cell lysis caused by excessive force.51–53 Furthermore, the antigens and reactive clonotypes present in a tumor are seldom known beforehand, and antigen-specific T cells span a wide range of affinities, making it difficult to fine-tune shear stress to achieve pure enrichment without case-by-case optimization and limiting translational potential. Moreover, recovering a mix of low, intermediate, and high affinity T cells, rather than only high affinity cells, may be more favorable, as high affinity T cells are prone to overstimulation and activation-induced cell death,54–56 while low and intermediate affinity T cells can maintain functionality over extended periods and support robust immune responses.57 58 By focusing on constant low-level shear stress de-enrichment, we believe ATTACH is highly versatile and could increase the potential of this approach for downstream applications.

To broaden the applicability of ATTACH, we demonstrate its capacity to selectively deplete bystander T cells while enriching for rare, antigen-specific populations. This capability addresses a critical challenge in ACT, particularly in the context of TILs, where a substantial proportion of recovered T cells are often reactive to irrelevant antigens. For instance, T cells specific for common viral epitopes such as those found in the CEF peptide pool are frequently present among TILs and can dominate the expansion process during ex vivo culture, ultimately diluting the therapeutic impact of truly tumor-specific clonotypes.10 Our results show that ATTACH can effectively eliminate these non-specific bystanders from mixed populations, achieving robust depletion of CEF-reactive T cells while preserving antigen-specific reactivity toward neoantigens. This is notable given studies suggesting that only ~10% of TILs are truly tumor-specific.10 Accordingly, we tested ATTACH under increasingly stringent conditions, testing its ability to recover antigen-specific T cells representing just 0.1% of the total population. This is especially important for improving the precision and efficacy of TIL therapy, where conventional bulk expansion often fails to discriminate between functional and irrelevant subsets. Notably, the T cells recovered post-ATTACH remain functionally competent, retaining their ability to recognize and respond to cognate antigen stimulation. Although the recovered fraction contains both T cells and tumor cells, ATTACH effectively increases the frequency of antigen-specific T cells within the recovered population. Together, these findings underscore the potential of ATTACH for numerous downstream and therapeutic applications.

Furthermore, the antigen-specific nature of ATTACH is demonstrated by the low retention of C57BL/6 CD8+ T cells after the assay. The comparable retention of C57BL/6 CD8+ T cells on both LLC-JSP and LLC-JSP-OVA slides suggests that their retention was not driven by antigen-specific interactions. This observation aligns with previous studies in tumor microenvironments, where T cell interactions can be influenced by factors beyond antigen recognition.55–57 Moreover, a major advantage of ATTACH is the ability to purify antigen-specific T cells with a low amount of shear stress. In addition, it can enrich for antigen-specific T cells when these are at a lower frequency than non-specific T cells, closer in line with real-world circumstances. These findings suggest that ATTACH may have significant translational potential.

ATTACH does exhibit certain limitations. First, T cell attachment is governed by stochastic cell–cell encounters and limited interaction time, such that not all antigen-specific T cells establish stable contacts. Consequently, shear stress may also remove a fraction of antigen-specific cells, reducing overall recovery. This effect may be further exacerbated when targeting endogenous neoantigen-specific T cells, which occur at very low precursor frequencies and may require additional expansion or stimulation to enable reliable detection after the assay. In addition, assay performance depends on target cell quality and antigen presentation, as reduced tumor cell viability or insufficient HLA/antigen expression can impair stable T cell–tumor interactions and limit enrichment efficiency, particularly in patient-derived samples. These challenges are compounded in clinical settings by the limited availability of autologous tumor cell lines; while patient-derived xenograft models may provide an alternative source of tumor material, their use introduces additional logistical and translational constraints.

In conclusion, we demonstrate the feasibility of isolating tumor antigen-specific T cells based on binding affinity using a microfluidic platform via ATTACH. By using tumor cells as a natural source of endogenously-processed and -presented MHC-peptide complexes, ATTACH can rapidly enrich for human and murine antigen-specific CD8+ T cells based on TCR avidity and circumvent many of the challenges associated with current antigen identification methodologies in a rapid and cost-effective manner. All in all, ATTACH allows simple yet effective enrichment of functionally intact antigen-specific T cells, which can be used for multiple downstream applications.
