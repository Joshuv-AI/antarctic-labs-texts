VOC source apportionment in Shanghai based on the identification of two-stage photochemical ages
Authors: Leifeng Yang, Hui He, Zhonghui Huang, Yusen Duan, Qingyan Fu, Huxiong Cui, Juntao Huo, Xiong Wang, Zibing Yuan, Danping Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe positive matrix factorization (PMF) model is widely used for volatile organic compound (VOC) source apportionment, yet its assumption of chemical stability conflicts with reactive transport processes. To address this, we developed a two-stage approach that quantifies photochemical aging by separately identifying distinct atmospheric processing phases. Analyzing one year of VOC measurements in Shanghai, we resolved five factors, including diesel exhaust, industry/solvents, and aged VOCs. By correlating factor contributions with OH radical reaction rate constants (k_OH), we determined transport times for primary emissions (stage I) and the ultimate aging state (stage II). Correcting for these stages revealed that conventional ozone formation potential (OFP) estimates account for only 15% and 42% of the actual potential at QP and PD, respectively, when accounting for photochemical loss. This framework provides a more accurate representation of initial emission profiles, demonstrating that pre-receptor processes are critical for characterizing the true impact of VOC sources on urban air quality.Subject areas: atmospheric science, environmental chemistry, pollution

Introduction

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are defined as organic compounds with boiling points ranging from 50°C to 260°C and a saturated vapor pressure greater than 133.32 Pa at 20°C, as per the World Health Organization (WHO). Exposure to specific VOCs, such as benzene, can result in irreversible harm to human health.1,2 Furthermore, VOCs are the precursors of ozone and secondary organic aerosols (SOAs).3,4 In view of their great environmental and human health implications, VOCs should be prioritized in air pollution control. Effective VOC control relies on the accurate identification of VOC sources, and source apportionment is a useful technique to quantify VOC contributions from different sources in a particular region.

The positive matrix factorization (PMF) receptor model has been widely applied in VOC source apportionment over the past decades.1,5,6 An inherent assumption of PMF is that no chemical reactions occur during pollutant transport from emission sources to receptors. This assumption contradicts the atmospheric behavior of VOCs, which undergo continuous chemical reactions that lead to the formation of ozone and SOA. Different samples and even different species in a particular sample may be associated with different aging processes, making the assumption of unchanged VOC source profiles in the atmosphere implausible. Therefore, without considering the aging process and quantifying its extent, VOC source apportionment is fundamentally incorrect.

Unfortunately, only a small number of studies have taken photochemical aging into account in VOC source apportionment. Previous attempts to incorporate photochemical aging into VOC source apportionment have followed two main approaches. Some studies estimated photochemical ages based on the relative abundances of VOC pairs and retrieved initial mixing ratios for all species.7,8,9 However, this approach assumes uniform aging for all primary hydrocarbons from a common source, which oversimplifies the reality where ambient air masses contain emissions with different photochemical ages and source origins. Other studies identified “aged” factors in PMF runs by examining correlations between factor contributions and kOH values (the OH radical reaction rate constant).10,11,12 While these approaches recognize aging effects among PMF factors, they neglect the photochemical depletion that occurs before VOCs reach the receptor site.

More specifically, in the actual atmosphere, the photochemical processing of VOCs includes two stages. In the first stage (stage I), freshly emitted VOCs undergo photochemical aging, but their source profiles and characteristic fingerprint species are preserved, allowing PMF to distinguish and assign them to specific source categories. This implies that all factors identified by PMF undergo photochemical aging to varying extents, as is necessarily the case in the atmosphere. In the second stage (stage II), VOCs are further processed and depleted in the atmosphere, and eventually lose their general patterns of source profiles. In this stage, VOCs emitted from different sources tend to converge to source profiles enriched in unreactive species. PMF is therefore unable to distinguish their original emission source categories, and very often a factor referred to as “aged VOCs” is separated. Thus, to provide a comprehensive VOC source apportionment, it is essential to estimate photochemical ages in both stages to fully characterize VOC photochemical processing and accurately retrieve the initial VOC emissions.

To address these challenges, in this study, we propose a novel two-stage photochemical aging approach and apply it to VOC source apportionment in Shanghai. This method accounts for photochemical aging in both stages to better retrieve the original emission characteristics before atmospheric transformation, thereby providing a more accurate representation of actual emission sources. We used hourly speciated VOC data continuously monitored from July 2016 to June 2017. Such a rich and robust VOC speciation dataset is rare in China and can provide a comprehensive understanding of VOC emissions and their seasonal and diurnal variabilities in Shanghai and the surrounding Yangtze River Delta (YRD). This two-stage photochemical aging approach is expected to provide more reliable information for VOC emission control strategies that may alleviate secondary pollution and protect public health in Shanghai.

Results

Estimation of photochemical ages in both stages

The first step in photochemical age estimation was to perform conventional VOC source apportionment. Five factors were resolved by PMF at the two sites, including diesel exhaust, combustion, LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions, industry and solvent usage, and aged VOCs. At QP, LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions were combined in a single factor, while industry and solvent usage were separated. At PD, LPG was separated from gasoline and ship emissions, while industry and solvent usage were combined. Detailed information on source identification and error estimation of the PMF run is provided in the Supplement, with source profiles illustrated in the Figure S1. These conventional VOC source apportionment results provided the basis for the subsequent photochemical age estimation of the factors.

As discussed in the STAR Methods section, Spearman correlation analysis was used to identify the “aged VOCs” factor. Table 1 shows correlation coefficients between relative contributions and kOH values of VOC species in the five factors at QP and PD, respectively. The factor showing a statistically significant negative correlation, with a significance level of 0.05 at QP and of 0.001 at PD, was identified as “aged VOCs.” It was noted that a significantly negative correlation was also found for the combustion factor at QP. This may be attributable to the relatively longer photochemical age of this factor at QP, as evidenced in Table 2. The notable presence of some reactive species (e.g., isoprene) highlighted that this profile had great potential to be processed further; therefore, it was classified as a stage I factor. There was no significant correlation between factor contributions and kOH values of VOC species for the other factors at both sites; therefore, they were classified as stage I factors.

Based on similar emission source characteristics, we combined industry and solvent sources at QP, LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions at PD. In the stage I output, two fresh factors in the preliminary PMF run (factors 3 and 4, representing the combined LPG-gasoline-ship source at PD) showed highly similar chemical compositions (C2–C5 alkanes and aromatics) and nearly identical diurnal/seasonal patterns, indicating they were likely rotationally split representations of the same source type. To avoid over-splitting and preserve physical coherence, these two factors were merged into a single source profile before photochemical age correction. Factor assignments were further validated through PSCF back-trajectory analysis, which identified source regions consistent with factor chemistry, and by comparison with local source profiles from field sampling and previous VOC source apportionment studies in Shanghai and the YRD region.

We further calculated photochemical ages of Δt1 and Δt2 using Equations 4 and 5, as listed in Table 2. The unique fitting equations for the four factors at QP and PD are provided in Tables S3A and S3B, respectively. Δt1 ranged between 0.57 and 5.4 h at QP and 1.3–3.5 h at PD, comparable to photochemical ages reported in Beijing (1.7 ± 0.9 h).13 The results demonstrate that diesel exhaust showed the lowest photochemical ages at both sites (0.57 h at QP and 0.10 h at PD), while industry and solvent usage also showed relatively short photochemical ages (3.7 h at QP and 1.3 h at PD) compared to other factors. In general, Δt1 of PMF-resolved factors was inversely correlated with the distances from emission sources to the monitoring station. In particular, diesel exhaust emissions from diesel-fueled trucks were banned from traveling in the urban area, leading to higher Δt1 at QP than at the urban PD. For LPG, on the other hand, Δt2 was generally lower at QP than its counterparts at PD, suggesting the air mass at QP was more photochemically processed and may be associated with a wider range of transport, as evidenced by backward-trajectory cluster analysis in Figure S3. A significant discrepancy in Δt2 was discovered at the two stations because 75% of air masses at QP come from the inland, covering a larger area than PD and traveling a longer distance overall.

Retrieval of initial VOC source profiles

As demonstrated in the results section, VOCs from different emission sources underwent different degrees of photochemical aging before reaching the receptors. Therefore, PMF-resolved factors could not reflect the actual VOC characteristics from emission sources. In order to achieve a rigorous VOCs “source” apportionment, it is essential to retrieve the initial VOC emissions from sources. Figure 1 shows the initial source profiles retrieved by Equation 6 and a comparison with PMF-resolved factor profiles. As the “aged VOCs” factor is the final aging state of stage I factors, and it is impossible to explicitly identify its initial emission sources, only profiles for the stage I factors before and after retrieval were compared.

In comparison with the PMF-resolved profiles, the retrieved initial source profiles preserved the general patterns and fingerprint species, with total concentrations varying significantly. One notable change in the initial VOC source profiles was the significant presence of isoprene in the combustion factor at QP, and isoprene was almost completely depleted before reaching the QP station. The explained variation of isoprene increased from 43% in the PMF-resolved combustion factor profile to 80% in the retrieved initial source profile. As isoprene is a typical tracer of biogenic emissions, it is more plausible to name this factor as combustion/biogenic emissions at QP, as shown in Figure 1. Mixing with biogenic emissions was not observed at the PD station, largely due to its urban background.

We further compared the PMF-resolved factor contributions with the retrieved initial source contributions. The pie chart of factor contribution percentages before and after retrieval is shown in Figure 2. At QP, contribution percentages of combustion/biogenic emissions, LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions increased by 15% and 1% in the retrieved initial source profiles, while those of diesel exhaust and industry and solvent usage decreased by 5% and 11%, respectively. At PD, the contribution percentage of industry and solvent usage increased by 1% in the retrieved initial source profiles, while that of diesel exhaust decreased by 1%. Generally, contributions of factors with a higher Δt1 increased after retrieval, while those with a lower Δt1 decreased. The only exception was LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions at PD, with a relatively longer Δt1 of 3.5 h hours, but its contribution did not increase. This is because the source profile of LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions is dominated by low-carbon-chain species with relatively low reactivity. Although it had a relatively longer photochemical age in the air, its concentration increased by 24%, much less than other factors even with a shorter photochemical age, e.g., industry and solvent usage, with a Δt1 of 1.3 h, showed a concentration increase of 29%. This agreement between the retrieved initial source profiles and known source characteristics provides additional confidence in the robustness of the two-stage photochemical age correction, even in light of uncertainties in the assumed OH concentration.

Estimation of source-specific ozone-forming potential

With the initial source contributions of different factors characterized, we further estimated the initial ozone formation potentials (OFPs) of different VOC emission sources. VOC source apportionment followed by OFP analysis is a widely used approach to shed light on prioritizing VOC source controls with the aim of reducing ozone and other secondary pollutants effectively.14,15,16,17,18 However, most OFP calculations were directly based on the PMF-resolved factors, which have the fundamental problem that such estimated OFPs only reflect ozone formed by VOCs after bypassing the receptor (post-receptor), while ozone formed by VOC depletion during transport from emission sources to the receptor (pre-receptor) is neglected. In this regard, the post-receptor OFPs are decoupled from ambient ozone levels, which are usually linked to pre-receptor VOC depletion, and therefore cannot reflect the effectiveness of ozone reduction when certain VOC sources are controlled.

In this study, we corroborated this notion by comparing OFPs of PMF-resolved factors and those of the retrieved initial source emissions, as depicted in Figures 3A and 3B. Substantial enhancement of OFPs was discovered after retrieval, especially at the QP station. Total OFP increased from 41 ppbv to 328 ppbv at QP and from 23 ppbv to 55 ppbv at PD. This highlights that conventional OFP estimation only accounted for 13% and 42% of total OFP at QP and PD, respectively, and was therefore associated with significant limitations in accurately characterizing contributions of VOC factors to ambient ozone. Total OFP was much higher at QP, due much to the longer photochemical age and higher abundances of TEX species and isoprene emitted from the solvent-using industry and dense vegetation, respectively. This may partly explain why there is very often an ozone pollution hotspot around the QP area.

Additionally, the OFP ranking of different factors also varied greatly after retrieval. At QP, industry and solvent usage and LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions accounted for 61% and 26% of the total OFP in the conventional source apportionment, followed by diesel exhaust and combustion/biogenic emissions. However, combustion/biogenic emissions rose to become the second largest OFP contributor after retrieval, accounting for 36% of the total OFP. At PD, the contribution of LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions increased from 37% to 64% of the total OFP, while that of industry and solvent usage decreased from 47% to 28%. Such changes were essentially driven by the differences in photochemical ages and abundances of reactive species in the factor profiles. This also underscores the importance of taking retrieval into consideration in VOC source apportionment to prioritize VOC control when formulating an ozone pollution abatement strategy. The consistency between corrected OFP patterns and expected source reactivity offers further indirect evidence that the retrieved initial profiles are chemically reasonable despite uncertainties in the OH concentration.

Variation of corrected source profiles in different scales

Since factor concentrations were influenced by both local emissions and regional transport, PSCF analysis was conducted to identify the geographical source regions of the aging-corrected factor contributions. PSCF is based on meteorological transport patterns and does not directly account for photochemical transformations during transport. In this study, it was applied to factor contributions that had already been corrected for photochemical aging using our two-stage PMF framework, thereby providing spatial context for the improved source estimates while minimizing biases from chemical loss during transport. Within this context, PSCF serves as a complementary spatial analysis tool to distinguish local versus regional contributions and was also used to assist in interpreting the diurnal variation patterns of source factors. The darker areas in PSCF maps correspond to larger PSCF values, indicating greater potential influence on VOC concentrations at the sampling sites.

Figure 4 shows the diurnal variation of four corrected actual factors at QP and PD. For vehicle-related factors, the diurnal variations were mainly affected by changes in planetary boundary layer (PBL) height and the occurrence of traffic rush hours when local emissions were dominant. Daytime PBL height is much higher than that at nighttime in Shanghai.19 A more stable curve without significant peaks and diurnal variation often reflects a stronger impact of regional transport since regional transport delivers pollutants to a site at a more consistent rate. In Figure 4A, the diurnal variation of diesel exhaust at PD was more stable than that at QP, which may be related to the prominent contribution from areas outside the PD site. According to the PSCF maps of this factor in Figure 5B, the external contributors probably originated from southern Jiangsu Province, in addition to the surrounding areas of PD. Comparing Figure 5A with Figure 5B, the impact of regional transport was more significant at PD than at QP.

On the contrary, other vehicle-related factors (PD in Figure 4A and both sites in Figure 4D) were mainly affected by local emissions. During midnight and early morning hours, the relatively low PBL height facilitates the accumulation of VOCs. Therefore, with the increasing use of vehicles during morning rush hours, the concentrations of vehicle-related factors rose sharply and reached a peak value shortly after. In the meantime, pollutants were subject to photochemical aging after sunrise. Gradually, the PBL height rose and the vertical mixing enhanced, causing the concentration to be diluted after the peak. In addition, the reduced traffic emission intensity and increased photochemical aging intensity promoted the dilution of vehicle-emitted VOCs. During evening rush hours, the traffic pollutants increased and started to accumulate slowly. For LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions in Figure 4D, general patterns and corrected abundances of the two sites were similar, while the peak at PD was delayed for about an hour. The delayed peak time at PD may result from the stronger average wind speed (Figure S4), which impeded the accumulation of pollutants. According to Figures 5G and 5H, local areas and the northwest of Shanghai were the main contributors to this factor at both sites, especially the Suzhou-Wuxi-Changzhou city clusters.19 In addition, potential origins of this factor also included Jiaxing and Hangzhou Bay at QP, and the Yangtze River estuary at PD, which verified contributions of ship emissions in this factor.

However, the patterns of combustion, industry, and solvent usage factors were not the same. For the combustion factor in Figure 4B, a moderate peak appeared in the early morning at QP, while there was no significant peak at PD. It was consistent with the diurnal pattern of plumes of biomass burning at a site in the PRD, which had a moderate peak at approximately 8:00.9,20 In addition, the concentration at QP was almost twice as high as that at PD, which indicated combustion-related emissions, especially biomass burning, were more significant at the suburban site QP than at the urban site PD. According to Figures 5C and 5D, the higher average concentration of combustion at QP may result from regionally transported air mass from the north of Shanghai, especially the Suzhou-Wuxi-Changzhou city clusters.

In Figure 4C, no peak appeared at either site, probably because this factor was irrelevant to vehicles during rush hours. A distinct afternoon minimum and the absence of significant peaks were observed at QP, which were probably caused by the variation of PBL height and photochemical aging loss. In Figure 5E, the dominant contribution at QP was from local emissions. A more stable curve and larger darker areas in PSCF maps at PD (Figures 5E and 5F) implied there was a large amount of industry and solvent-related emissions transported from the northwest, west, and south of the PD site. There were many industrial parks located in these directions, such as Qingpu Industrial Park and Suzhou Industrial Park.

Uncertainty

Accurately quantifying VOC sources at a sampling site is challenging due to their high photochemical reactivity. However, uncertainties remain in the photochemical aging estimation method developed in this study.

As previously discussed, the corrected PMF profiles were derived using local source profiles from Shanghai to estimate photochemical ages. This step introduces inherent uncertainties. In stage I of the analysis, it was difficult to obtain source profiles that perfectly matched the PMF-resolved factors. Due to the lack of comprehensive data, the specific abundances of LPG, gasoline exhaust, and ship emission markers in the Shanghai source profiles were not fully determined. Furthermore, the precise fractions of fresh factors converted to aged VOCs were unknown, contributing to estimation errors in the Δt1 and Δt2 values.

Additionally, the OH radical concentration ([OH] in Equations 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8) exhibits significant temporal and spatial variability, fluctuating across seasons, sampling sites, and times of day. Thus, the calculated Δt value represents an effective timescale of the photochemical aging process rather than a precise transport duration. In this study, Δt serves as a proxy for the transport time from the emission source to the receptor, reflecting the extent of VOC depletion due to photochemical reactions. Therefore, while the absolute values of Δt may be sensitive to OH concentration variability, the relative differences between factors—calculated under consistent OH assumptions—remain robust. Theoretically, factors associated with varying transport times should exhibit distinct diurnal patterns. However, in practice, the observed diurnal variations did not strictly follow this rule, likely because the differences in photochemical ages between the two sites were minor, and local emissions are not the sole determinant of VOC concentrations; regional transport also plays a critical role. Currently, it remains challenging to fully quantify these separate impacts and generalize the governing rules of diurnal variations. Consequently, the “corrected” PMF profiles in this study should be interpreted as refined approximations, acknowledging that a portion of the VOC concentration remains unaccounted for.

More specifically, in Equations 4 and 5, the fitted slope constrains the product [OH]×Δt from observational data, but the decomposition into separate [OH] and Δt values depends on the assumed OH concentration. When [OH] varies, Δt changes inversely, which affects the dilution coefficients d1 and d2 in Equations 6 and 7, and consequently the retrieved initial concentrations and OFP estimates. A lower OH would yield larger Δt, smaller dilution coefficients, and higher corrected concentrations; a higher OH would reduce the correction magnitude. Nevertheless, since all factors are evaluated under a consistent OH assumption, the relative differences among factors and the OFP ranking changes after correction remain robust.

To advance the understanding of VOC photochemical aging, it is imperative to develop comprehensive local source profiles with higher temporal and chemical resolution. Incorporating these refined profiles will significantly improve the robustness and accuracy of VOC source apportionment in future studies.

Discussion

To address the conflicts between PMF assumptions and the photochemical aging of VOCs during transport, this study developed a novel VOC source apportionment based on the identification of photochemical aging degrees of different factors. Based on this method, four fresh factors (diesel exhaust, combustion, industry and solvent usage, and LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions) and one aged VOC factor were identified. The photochemical ages in stage I were 0.6 h, 5.4 h, 3.7 h, and 4.8 h for diesel exhaust, combustion, industry and solvent usage, and LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions at QP, and 0.1 h, 1.8 h, 1.3 h, and 3.5 h for the corresponding four factors at PD, respectively. The photochemical age in stage II was within 16.6 h at QP and less than 58 h at PD, indicating that the four fresh factors at PD had greater potential for further photochemical processing.

Based on the corrected profiles, the factor concentrations and OFPs exhibited substantial discrepancies compared to those from conventional PMF results at both sites. The conventionally calculated OFPs accounted for only 15% and 42% of the corrected OFPs at QP and PD, respectively. Moreover, the ranking of factors in terms of OFP contribution shifted at both sites. This highlighted that pre-receptor OFP, rather than the conventionally calculated post-receptor OFP, constitutes the dominant component of total OFP. The corrected profiles revealed that diurnal variations of factors were more pronounced at QP than at PD, with the exception of the LPG, gasoline, and ship emissions factors. Integrating diurnal variation patterns with PSCF analysis indicated that the Suzhou-Wuxi-Changzhou city clusters were principal contributors to both sites, in addition to local emissions from Shanghai. Furthermore, regional transport exerted a stronger influence on PD than on QP.

The corrected OFP results suggest that emission control strategies in Shanghai should prioritize combustion/biogenic sources in suburban areas and vehicle/shipping emissions in urban areas, as their OFP contributions were substantially underestimated by conventional methods. This two-stage photochemical aging framework is also applicable to other urban environments where PMF-based VOC source apportionment is conducted. While the proposed photochemical aging method warrants further refinement, it provides a valuable framework for the accurate quantification of pollution source contributions—a prerequisite for formulating effective emission control strategies.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the absence of local OH radical measurements in Shanghai, a fixed OH concentration of 6×106 molecules cm−3 was adopted, which does not capture seasonal and diurnal variations in OH levels and introduces uncertainty in photochemical age estimation. Second, the atmospheric dilution coefficients (d1 and d2) were estimated under simplified assumptions, whereas actual dilution during transport varies with meteorological conditions. Third, the “aged VOCs” factor, having undergone complete two-stage photochemical aging, cannot be traced back to its original emission sources, limiting the ability to fully characterize all source contributions in the corrected profiles.

Method details

Sampling sites and VOCs measurements

Online VOCs and meteorological data were measured continuously at Qingpu (QP) and Pudong (PD) sites in Shanghai from July 2016 to June 2017. QP site is located near Dianshan Lake (31.094°N, 120.978°E) and surrounded by residential and rural areas. It is approximately 45 km from the city centers of Shanghai, Suzhou and Jiaxing cities. PD site is located in central Shanghai (31.228°N, 121.533°E), close to the Lujiazui central business district.

23 VOC species, including 13 alkanes, 3 alkenes, 6 aromatics and 1 alkyne, were measured hourly by automated GC-FID systems (Chromatotec GC866 at QP and PE Clarus580 at PD). Online calibrations were conducted every day, and on-site calibrations were performed weekly to check for standard gases and blanks. The method detection limit (MDL) of all VOC species was calculated as 3.14 times the standard deviations of the seven replicate measurements of the lowest concentration (i.e., Student's t value for 99 percent confidence for seven values). The MDL values of 90% of the species must be less than 0.15 ppb. To confirm the precision of the data, the same concentration of n-pentane and toluene were selected as the diluted standard gas. Seven replicate analyses were performed on two diluted species, with the relative standard deviations of the seven replicates being less than ±15% for both species. For quality control, the relative deviation between the measured and nominal concentrations of each compound must be within 30%.

Positive matrix factorization (PMF) model

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s receptor model PMF version 5.0 was used for VOC source apportionment in this study. The required input data of the PMF model are VOC species concentration and the corresponding uncertainties. We also included CO data in the PMF analysis as it is a relatively stable tracer of aged VOCs. The basic assumptions of PMF and detailed instructions are provided in the PMF User Guide.21

Data uncertainty is an important input in PMF runs. In VOC source apportionment, variability in source profiles is a major source of uncertainties that may directly influence the source apportionment results. Conventional uncertainty estimation usually applied a uniform empirical coefficient to all species.12 Without considering differences in their chemical activity and the changing source profiles in the atmosphere, such an estimation approach may not be appropriate for VOC source apportionment. In the ambient air, species with higher reactivity would change more rapidly, and the degree of violation of the constant source profile assumption is higher. Thus, in this study we propose an uncertainty estimation approach bounded by species reactivity for reliable VOC source apportionment.

Generally, uncertainty includes two parts: sampling uncertainty and analytical uncertainty. Xij multiplied by Error Fraction implies the sampling uncertainty, and MDL as the minimum concentration of a substance with a 99-percent confidence that the analyte concentration is greater than zero implies the analytical uncertainty.22 Previous research usually determined the Error Fraction empirically, which cannot reflect the variability of VOCs source profiles in the atmosphere.23,24,25,26 To address this limitation, we proposed a new method to calculate the Error Fraction based on Maximum Incremental Reactivity (MIR) values of each VOC.27 MIR takes into account the reaction of VOCs with OH radicals and the subsequent ozone formation potential, which comprehensively quantifies the reactivity of all VOC species. The Error Fraction of a VOC species was calculated as one third of its MIR value. The factor of 1/3 was selected to ensure that calculated Error Fraction values fall within the typical range used in PMF studies while preserving relative differences among species based on their chemical reactivity.

Equations 1 and 2 were applied to the concentrations greater than MDL. For the data below MDL, uncertainties were set as 5/6 of the MDL.12

Previous studies have identified five to seven primary VOC emission sources in Shanghai.28,29 In this study, the number of PMF factors was set to six, which enabled both sampling sites to clearly resolve five fresh emission factors and one aged VOCs factor. The robustness of the PMF solution was evaluated using Bootstrap resampling and Displacement (DISP) analysis. Model performance metrics including Q-values, Bootstrap mapping results, and DISP stability tests are detailed in the supplemental information (Tables S1 and S2).

Two-stage photochemical age estimation

The identification of photochemically aged factors is based on the principle that aged air masses show systematic relationships between VOC species contributions and their atmospheric reactivity (k_OH values), as highly reactive compounds are preferentially consumed during transport.11,12 In this study, a two-stage photochemical age method was developed to characterize the photochemical aging processes of VOCs in the atmosphere and to optimize VOC source apportionment. As introduced in the introduction section, ambient VOCs are the mixture of those emitted from different sources with different photochemical aging processes. Therefore, it is a rational assumption that all PMF-resolved factors were photochemically aged, differing in stages and degrees.

The figure below provides a conceptual illustration of the two photochemical ages of VOCs in the atmosphere, and their reflection in PMF analysis. Stage-I represents photochemical aging of PMF-resolved factors transported from emission sources to the receptor, with the aging time represented by Δt1; Stage-II represents further aging beyond Stage-I, in which profiles of different factors converge toward the final ‘aged VOCs’ profile, with the aging time represented by Δt2. In other words, the ‘aged VOCs’ factor undergoes both stages, whereas the other PMF-resolved factors undergo Stage-I only. The factors in Stage-I are referred to as ‘fresh VOCs’ as they retain source-specific characteristics, while the factor in Stage-II is termed ‘aged VOCs’ due to the loss of original source signatures through extensive photochemical processing.

Conceptual illustration of the photochemical aging stages

The first step of the two-stage photochemical age estimation is to identify the ‘aged VOCs’ factor, as it is the only factor assumed to experience two-stage photochemical processes while all the other PMF-resolved factors only experience Stage-I. The identification of the ‘aged VOCs’ factor was based on multiple criteria: (1) Spearman correlation analysis showing negative correlation between factor contributions and k_OH values, (2) chemical composition dominated by relatively inert species (CO, benzene, short-chain alkanes), and (3) absence of source-specific fingerprints. Factors retaining characteristic source markers and reactive species were classified as Stage-I factors regardless of correlation coefficients, while those lacking source signatures and enriched in long-lived gases were identified as ‘aged VOCs’.11
