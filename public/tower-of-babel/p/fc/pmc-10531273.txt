Graft-versus-host disease following liver transplantation: A systematic review of literature
Authors: Felicia Kneifel, Thomas Vogel, Eike Bormann, Felix Becker, Philipp Houben, Isabelle Flammang, Patrycja Slepecka, Ann-Kathrin Eichelmann, Jens G. Brockmann, Andreas Pascher
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Graft-versus-host disease following liver transplantation is a serious and usually fatal complication. Data identifying the risk factors and specifying the diagnosis and treatment options of the disease are scarce and contentious. Moreover, recommendations for therapeutic approaches are similarly sparse.Methods:A systematic review of the literature from 1988 to 2020 on graft-versus-host disease following liver transplantation was performed using the PubMed and MEDLINE databases. Medical subject headings, such as graft-versus-host disease and GvHD were used in combination with solid organ transplant, transplantation, or liver transplant. Following duplicate removal, 9298 articles were screened for suitability. A total of 238 full-text articles were analyzed for eligibility, resulting in 130 eligible articles for meta-analysis. Two hundred twenty-five patients developing graft-versus-host disease following liver transplantation reported herein were mainly published in case reports and case series.Results:Graft-versus-host disease occurred with an incidence of 1.2%. 85% developed following deceased donor liver transplant and 15% following living-related donor liver transplantation. The median follow-up period following liver transplantation was 84 days (interquartile range, 45–180). The median time from liver transplantation to graft-versus-host disease onset was 30 days (interquartile range, 21–42). The main clinical features included skin rash (59%), fever (43%), diarrhea (36%), and pancytopenia (30%). The overall mortality rate was 71%. Neither univariate (HR = 0.999; 95% CI, 0.493–2.023; p = 1.0) nor multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between adaptation of immunosuppression and survival probability (HR = 1.475; 95% CI, 0.659–3.303; p = 0.3).Conclusions:This systematic review suggests that an increase in immunosuppressive regimen does not yield any survival benefit in patients suffering from graft-versus-host disease following liver transplantation.

INTRODUCTION

The clinical presentation of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) is a well-known complication of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).1 GvHD is also known as a severe complication of solid organ transplantation (SOT).2–6 Although reports of SOT-GvHD after lung, pancreas, or kidney transplantation are scarce,7–10 several studies have reported on GvHD following liver transplantation (LT).

Acute GvHD following LT was first described by Burdick et al2 in 1988. It is a life-threatening complication with an overall mortality rate of up to 87% in adult recipients in 0.1%–2% of LT. Typical symptoms include fever, skin rash, and diarrhea appearing 1–8 weeks following LT, often accompanied by pancytopenia or septicemia.11–13

A recent Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network/United Network for Organ Sharing (OPTN/UNOS) database for retrospective data analysis surveying 77,416 LT recipients in the 2003–2018 period identified basiliximab induction and organs arising from younger donors (>20 y age mismatch) as independent risk factors for fatal outcomes in GvHD. However, maintenance therapy with mycophenolate mofetil was associated with decreased GvHD mortality.14 Additionally, the amount of lymphoid tissue in the donor organ and a greater human leukocyte antigen (HLA) match between donor and recipient has received adverse attention.5,15 Moreover, the utilization of HLA-homozygous donors resulting in 1-way HLA matching significantly increased the risk of GvHD following living donor liver transplantation (LDLT).16,17

The diagnosis of GvHD is based on clinical presentation, histological proof of targeted tissue, and the molecular manifestation of the recipient target tissue invasion by donor lymphoid cells. Secondary to uncharacteristic symptoms, such as rejection, infection, or drug side effects, a delay in diagnosis is likely. The diagnosis and management of GvHD following LT remain challenging, and there is no consensus on the best treatment algorithm once diagnosed. Due to the absence of therapy recommendations, the current treatment of GvHD following LT is still inherited from the HSCT experience, often comprising an increase in immunosuppression utilizing high doses of corticosteroids and/or calcineurin inhibitors.18,19 Nevertheless, increased immunosuppression frequently results in toxicity and infectious complications leading to sepsis.19 Due to the unknown risk of impairing engraftment, eliminating T lymphocytes using anti–T-lymphocyte globulin or irradiation has not been proven worthwhile.18,20 Reduction or discontinuation of immunosuppression following LT is presumably associated with a higher risk of graft rejection but allows host lymphocytes to regain control over donor lymphocytes responsible for GvHD.21,22 Due to disease scarcity and the very limited number of published cases worldwide, no clear evidence-based consensus on therapy is available.

Herein, we hypothesize that the reduction of immunosuppression might improve outcomes by strengthening the host immune system to overcome GvHD and lower the risk of infectious complications.

METHODS

A systematic review of the literature on GvHD following LT was performed using PubMed and MEDLINE databases. The medical subject headings, such as graft-versus-host disease and GvHD were used in combination with solid organ transplant, transplantation, or liver transplant.

Titles and abstracts were screened to determine whether the publications met the inclusion criteria, that is, reporting 1 or more cases of GvHD following LT and comprising details on the clinical course, diagnosis, therapy, and outcome. Additionally, the references of the selected articles were searched for relevant publications. The initial search yielded 9264 publications: 238 publications were considered relevant, but 108 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria or were duplicate reports.

Finally, 130 articles reporting 225 patients with GvHD following LT were included. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram23 are presented in Figure 1.

Statistics

The results are presented as absolute or relative frequencies for categorical variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean and SD or median with interquartile range (IQR, Q0.25–Q0.75). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate median survival time, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival times between groups. The follow-up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. A generalized linear mixed model with binary response variable and logit link function—including a random intercept corresponding to the study and the fixed factor age group, sex, adaptation of immunosuppression, and time to the onset of GvHD, fever, diarrhea, skin rash, leukopenia, and pancytopenia—was adopted. The model was fitted using maximum likelihood estimation with a Gauss-Hermite quadrature likelihood approximation. Cox regression, including a random intercept corresponding to the study, was used to evaluate the influence of time on the onset of GvHD, fever, diarrhea, skin rash, leukopenia, and pancytopenia and the adaptation of immunosuppression.

RESULTS

A total of 130 articles reporting 225 patients and their survival following diagnosis and therapy for GvHD following LT were included. While 45 publications refer to a GvHD incidence rate of 0.1%–2%,11–13,15,18,19,22,24–61 a precise incidence rate was provided in 19 articles: GvHd following LT occurred in 90 out of 7786 patients, resulting in a pooled incidence rate of 1.2%.11,13,21,29,32,33,36,39,55,57,60,62–69

Most articles were retrospective reports. One hundred nine patients (48%) were documented in case reports, 90 patients (40%) within case series, and 26 patients (12%) in published reviews. The average recipient age at LT was 51.5 (±18.9) years, and the majority of patients were males (75%). GvHD after LT occurred significantly more frequently in adults (90%). Frequent indications for LT included, among others, HCC (22%), alcohol-associated liver disease (15%), HBV (10%), and HCV virus–related liver cirrhosis (8%). Immunosuppressive therapy after liver transplant

There were different therapeutic approaches in the treatment regimen following the diagnosis of GvHD, such as increasing the dose of initial immunosuppression and decreasing/discontinuing immunosuppression, corticosteroid pulse, monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies, immunoglobulins, or plasmapheresis. Immunosuppressive therapy was categorized into 2 subgroups to evaluate the optimal therapy regimen: increasing immunosuppression, including steroid pulse therapy with high doses of corticosteroids, and decreasing immunosuppression. Most patients were treated with an increase in immunosuppression (n = 178, 85%), and treatment based on decreasing immunosuppression was rarely undertaken (n = 23, 11%). Immunosuppression was not adapted after GvHD diagnosis in 8 patients (4%).

Decreasing immunosuppression was more frequently used in children (28%) than in adults (9%), whereas almost the entirety of the adult patients (91%) were treated by increasing immunosuppression (vs. 72% of children).

Adaptation of immunosuppression after diagnosis of GvHD

Comparing therapy regimens after diagnosis of GvHD, the Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that the overall survival probability was higher in the subgroup with decreasing immunosuppression (Figure 2D). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.8). Neither univariate (HR = 0.999; 95% CI, 0.493–2.023; p = 1.0) nor multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between adaptation of immunosuppression and survival probability (HR = 1.475; 95% CI, 0.659–3.303; p = 0.3) (Table 6). Additionally, there was no significant difference in overall survival according to the different regimens of initial immunosuppressive therapy (p = 0.1).

In subgroup analyses, age-related differences in overall survival were analyzed alongside the adaptation of immunosuppressive regimes. Cause of death

The cause of death was reported in 116 patients (52%). Infection was the most frequent cause of death, accounting for 88%. Among these, sepsis (61%), multiorgan failure (17%), and pneumonia (10%) were the leading causes. Sepsis was caused by invasive aspergillosis in 9 cases.

DISCUSSION

GvHD after LT is a rare but fearsome complication with a poor prognosis. To date, <300 cases have been reported. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, the mortality rate remains high up to 87%.11,14,28

The diagnosis of GvHD is prevalently made on clinical grounds in patients presenting with skin rash, fever, diarrhea, or pancytopenia. However, it remains a diagnosis of exclusion. One major problem is that diagnosis is often not straightforward. The occurrence of skin rash following LT, especially when accompanied by fever, diarrhea, and pancytopenia, should always be considered a cardinal symptom. Thus, suspicious symptoms should lead to prompt diagnostic investigations, such as biopsies of the affected organs or proof of chimerism.135

Risk factors for GvHD after LT include young donor age, senior recipient age, donor-recipient age difference >20 years, greater HLA-match, and LT for HCC.11,14,62,63 Furthermore, using HLA-homozygous donors resulting in donor-dominant 1-way HLA matching is associated with a significantly increased risk of GvHD development.16,17,73 Considering that HLA-homozygous donors pose a higher risk to recipients who share these alleles, Perri et al19 suggested a reduction in immunosuppression in recipients of grafts from HLA-homozygous donors, for both living, and deceased donors. Even though risk factors for the development of GvHD following LT have been found to include host immunosuppressive therapy,94 other publications do not endorse the idea that pre-existing immunosuppressive therapy is a prerequisite for the occurrence of GvHD post-LT.55 The impact of other factors, such as CMV and Epstein-Barr virus status, ABO disparity, or acute rejection, which are established treatment modalities in HSCT GvHD,136,137 remains unclear. However, reports on recipients of allogeneic stem cell transplantation suggest an increased risk of acute GvHD due to HLA-DPB1 mismatch and CMV reactivation.137 Whether this is evident in patients with GvHD after SOT has not been evaluated yet.

Few data refer to the therapeutic regime of GvHD following SOT in the current literature. Furthermore, the majority of cases have been published as case reports;2–4,16–18,22,25,28,30,31,34–38,42–46,48–54,56,58,60,61,65,67,69–77,80–90,92,95–103,105–109,111–120,122–128,130,132–134,138–141 thus, obtaining an overview of this sophisticated topic is aggravated. Treatment options for SOT-GvHD are also controversial as there is limited evidence given the scarcity and unaltered high mortality of cases, foreclosing a conclusive evidence-based therapy recommendation. Hence, there is an unmet and pivotal need for an evidence-based therapy consensus.

This literature review investigated the incidence and outcome of patients with GvHD following LT to identify the best therapeutic approach. Based on our local experience, we hypothesized that reducing the immunosuppressive burden is associated with better outcomes, allowing host immunocompetent cells to counteract GvHD.

Several case reports and series have described therapeutic approaches involving the administration of alefacept,22,32,119 etanercept,56,125,131 activated host lymphocytes,99 and monoclonal antibody11,12,81,99 treatment. These attempts must be determined as guided by experiences predominantly inherited from GvHD management in HSCT recipients. However, the high mortality rate of GvHD following LT indicates that none of these approaches has been proven to be uniformly effective in treating SOT-GvHD.

Increasing immunosuppressive therapy is frequently used in the therapy for GvHD following LT.2,3,11–13,15,16,18,19,21,22,24,25,28–30,33,35,37,40–45,48,49,51–55,57,58,60,61,64,65,68,69,71,74,75,77,78,80–83,86–88,90,94,95,98–103,106–108,111,112,114,116–119,122–127,130,132,134,142 Undoubtedly, increased immunosuppression frequently leads to toxicity and infection, resulting in septic shock.19 The treatment of GvHD with corticosteroids4,11,13,19,21,33,36,38,39,46,56,64,73,78,79,84,85,89,93,96,97,109,113,115,120,121,131,135 is derived from the experience of HSCT. The exact mechanisms by which steroids ameliorate acute GvHD are not entirely understood but are presumably associated with the lymphocytic activity of activated and inactivated T cells. However, GvHD therapy following SOT aims to eliminate adverse donor lymphocytes from the host immune system. Although transient symptom control may be reasonably achieved by the administration of glucocorticoids, resolution of GvHD cannot be expected. Reports on the decrease or discontinuation of immunosuppression in SOT-GvHD are extremely rare in the literature.4,15,33,34,39,50,55,70,72,74,78,92,93,99,104,122,129,135,143. Efforts to implement this approach were associated with a primary aggravation of symptoms122 and the risk of graft rejection.21,22 However, reducing immunosuppression may prevent the incidence of the most prevalent cause of death, that is, infections, within this particular group of patients.

In our opinion, inheriting therapeutic approaches from HSCT to SOT-GvHD is inappropriate. Hence, the rationale for treatment should aim at different underlying principles. In HSCT, the host immune system is modulated and immune-ablated before administering donor immune cells. Thus, GvHD can be forecasted to occur exceptionally frequently. The recipient is recognized as foreign by donor immune cells; therefore, the therapeutic targets of GvHD treatment are managing symptoms and enabling the tolerance of the host tissues to develop in the donor immune cells. The treatment of GvHD after HSCT focuses on agents aiming at extensive suppression of the immune system to mute the attack on recipient tissues.137,144–146 In contrast, the primary objective of immunosuppressive therapy after LT is to avoid allograft rejection by an unimpaired host immune system. Therefore, the occurrence of GvHD in this context must be considered an unwanted expansion of donor immune cells transferred along with the donor organ, subsequently targeting the recipient. In this instance, treatment with increased immunosuppression might mitigate the force of donor immune cells although this attempt should not be presumed to encourage the rejection of donor immune cells by the recipient.19,147,148

The timing of therapy applied during the clinical course of GvHD is likely to be important in mediating outcomes although the optimal timing of therapy has not yet been determined.19 Surprisingly, our analysis did not reveal a significant correlation between the timepoint of GvHD diagnosis and overall survival. This finding could be due to the fact that the time of diagnosis was only given in 61 cases. Nevertheless, early diagnosis will enable adjusting therapy and be beneficial for a favorable outcome.

Interestingly, according to the present data, most reported patients with acute cellular rejection experienced allograft rejection before the onset of GvHD (55%). The entirety of the patients were treated with pulse corticosteroid therapy. However, the underlying cause of GvHD development, even after acute cellular rejection, remains uncertain. Nonetheless, augmented immunosuppressive therapy as a treatment for liver graft rejection should be suspected as a predisposition to preferential inhibition of the host immune system, giving rise to donor immunocompetent cells.

The data showed an overall survival of 29% after GvHD diagnosis. With an overall mortality rate of 71%, the results correspond with the data described in recent studies.11–14 Early GvHD onset and adult age were identified as independent risk factors for increased mortality.

The median follow-up period following GvHD onset was 32 days, whereas the median follow-up period after LT was 84 days. As is known from a large OPTN/UNOS database registry study, the majority of GvHD mortality (87%) occurs within 180 days after LT.14 Differences in later survival in this review (Figure 2A–D) are neither significant and may be due to many confounding factors.

Concerning the different therapy regimens, our analysis indicates that the overall survival probability is higher in the subgroup with decreased immunosuppression. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.8). The overall survival rate was higher in children (p = 0.01). Though, comparing overall survival with respect to the adaptation of immunosuppressive regimens, the Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated no significant difference for both adults (p = 0.6) (Figure 3A) and children (p = 0.2) (Figure 3B).

This review illustrates sepsis (61%) as the leading cause of death, followed by multiorgan failure (17%) and pneumonia (10%). Although there is no consensus on managing immunosuppressive therapies during severe sepsis, dose reduction or withdrawal of immunosuppression is suggested to strengthen the recipient’s immunological response.149

Given the scarcity and difficulty in diagnosing GvHD post-LT, prospective and randomized studies of potential therapies are highly improbable. Accordingly, detailed and precise case descriptions enable advances in diagnosis and treatment. Thus, reporting relevant details disclosing case reports of GvHD following LT should be made mandatory.

CONCLUSIONS

This literature review suggests that an increase in immunosuppressive regimen does not yield any survival benefit in patients suffering from GvHD following LT.

Multicenter participation in a network prospectively studying GvHD following LT, along with obligatory reporting of GvHD cases comprising detailed and relevant information, needs to be addressed.

LIMITATIONS

The present study has several limitations as it was based on retrospective reviews, case series, and case reports. First, a strong publication bias must be considered. Accordingly, our review merely comprises diagnosed and published cases of GvHD following LT. Indubitably, there will be several other cases as GvHD is extremely rare and often underdiagnosed, and physicians are unlikely to report eventful courses.

Moreover, most published articles do not report relevant data regarding risk factors, occurrence, or therapy of GvHD. Therefore, for most investigations, only fragmentary data are available.
