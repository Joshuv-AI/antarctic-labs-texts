Paravalvular leak (PVL) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) remains a clinically significant complication, associated with progressive heart failure, adverse ventricular remodeling, and increased morbidity.1 Percutaneous closure of PVL after self-expanding valve implantation can be technically demanding owing to frame geometry and misaligned commissures, necessitating innovative approaches.

We describe a novel and pragmatic approach to PVL closure by leveraging the misaligned diamond-shaped cell of an Evolut FX+ valve (Medtronic), highlighting procedural planning, technical execution, and key learning points.

Case Summary

An 85-year-old man with a history of severe aortic stenosis and moderate aortic regurgitation underwent TAVR with a 34-mm Evolut FX+ valve (Figures 1 and 2). Postprocedural echocardiography demonstrated moderate PVL. Given preserved hemodynamics and absence of heart failure symptoms, a conservative approach was initially adopted.

Over the ensuing 3 months, the patient developed progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA functional class III), lower extremity edema, and intermittent chest discomfort. His medical history included atrial fibrillation and flutter after having undergone ablation, permanent pacemaker implantation, and hypertension. Despite medical optimization with intravenous diuretics, the symptoms persisted, prompting further investigation.

Procedural Steps

Repeat transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated worsening PVL with a high-velocity paravalvular jet originating from the noncoronary cusp region (Video 1). There was evidence of progressive left ventricular dilation compared with post-TAVR baseline, elevated filling pressures, and grade III diastolic dysfunction. Right ventricular systolic function was mildly reduced.

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) revealed dense circumferential annular calcification, most pronounced along the noncoronary sinus, which appeared to prevent complete prosthesis apposition despite appropriate implantation depth. The Evolut frame was rotated relative to the native commissures, resulting in coronary misalignment. Importantly, this orientation positioned a large diamond-shaped cell of the valve frame directly over the PVL tract, without compromising coronary artery flow (Figures 3 and 4, Video 2).

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a moderate to severe regurgitant volume with evidence of adverse left ventricular remodeling (Video 3). After multidisciplinary team discussion, transcatheter PVL closure was recommended in light of progressive symptoms, ventricular remodeling, and failure of prior post-TAVR balloon dilation.

The procedure was performed under fluoroscopic and transesophageal echocardiographic guidance (Video 4). Vascular access was obtained via the left common femoral artery (6-F), left common femoral vein (7-F), and right common femoral artery (14-F) using a Gore DrySeal sheath (W. L. Gore & Associates). Initial attempts to cross the PVL from the aortic side using conventional catheter techniques along the outer frame of the self-expanding valve were unsuccessful owing to the tall nitinol frame and obstructive annular calcification.

Based on preprocedural CT findings, the leak was accessed through the misaligned diamond-shaped cell of the Evolut frame, which was directly aligned with the PVL jet. A 6-F FR4 catheter and CXI angled catheter (Cook Medical) were advanced through the diamond cell. The PVL tract was crossed using a hydrophilic guidewire, which was subsequently exchanged for a stiff angled Glidewire (Terumo Medical Corporation) and supported by Amplatz 0.032-inch wires (Boston Scientific Corporation).

After confirmation of appropriate positioning by angiography and transesophageal echocardiography, a 6 × 110 cm Cook delivery sheath (Cook Medical) was advanced through the diamond cell and into the paravalvular defect. The PVL was closed using Amplatzer Vascular Plug II and IV devices (Abbott) with excellent stability. Immediate angiographic and echocardiographic assessment demonstrated near-complete resolution of the leak, with no interference with transcatheter valve leaflet motion. The procedure was completed without complications (Videos 5 and 6, Figure 5).

Potential Pitfalls

PVL closure after self-expanding TAVR presents several technical challenges. The height and rigidity of the nitinol frame may limit catheter maneuverability and restrict access to the paravalvular space. Dense annular calcification can further impede sheath advancement and device positioning.

Commissural malalignment, although generally undesirable given implications for coronary access, may occasionally provide an opportunity for direct frame-based access to a PVL tract. Careful preprocedural CT assessment is essential to ensure coronary safety and to identify favorable access windows. Operators must also consider the risk of device instability, interference with valve leaflets, and embolization when deploying closure devices through valve frame cells.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient demonstrated immediate hemodynamic improvement after PVL closure and was weaned off intravenous diuretics within 24 hours. He was discharged home without procedural complications (Video 7).

At the 1-month follow-up, he reported marked improvement in functional capacity, with resolution of orthopnea and peripheral edema. Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated only trivial residual PVL, improved diastolic filling patterns, and stabilization of left ventricular dimensions. The closure device remained well positioned, with normal motion of the transcatheter valve leaflets (Video 8).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents one of the first documented cases of PVL closure performed through a diamond-shaped cell of a self-expanding Evolut valve frame. PVL remains a recognized complication after TAVR, with moderate to severe leaks occurring in approximately 5% to 20% of cases.2 Its development is multifactorial and reflects the interaction between patient-specific anatomy, valve design, and procedural factors. Established predictors include severe annular or leaflet calcification, asymmetric bulky calcium, bicuspid aortic valve morphology, elliptical annular geometry, valve undersizing, and suboptimal implantation depth.3 Although self-expanding valves provide enhanced conformability, extensive circumferential calcification may prevent complete frame expansion and effective sealing, predisposing to residual PVL.4 In the present case, dense noncoronary annular calcification was the dominant mechanism underlying residual PVL despite appropriate valve sizing and postdilation.

The clinical significance of PVL is determined by both severity and chronicity. While mild PVL is often well tolerated, moderate to severe PVL is associated with progressive left ventricular volume overload, worsening heart failure, adverse remodeling, arrhythmias, hemolysis, and increased long-term mortality.5 Reintervention should therefore be considered in symptomatic patients or in those demonstrating objective evidence of adverse remodeling.6 In this case, progressive symptoms, worsening diastolic dysfunction, and left ventricular dilation despite optimized medical therapy supported timely intervention before irreversible myocardial remodeling occurred.

Management strategies for post-TAVR PVL must be individualized according to leak mechanism, anatomical characteristics, and patient risk profile. Balloon postdilation is frequently the first-line approach early after TAVR when underexpansion or malapposition is suspected; however, its effectiveness is limited in the presence of rigid annular calcification and may increase the risk of annular injury or stroke or conduction disturbances. Redo TAVR can address diffuse or circumferential PVL but it carries potential drawbacks, including higher transvalvular gradients, increased risk of coronary obstruction, and compromised future coronary access. Surgical correction remains definitive but is often prohibitive in elderly or high-risk patients.7 In this context, transcatheter plug-based PVL closure offers a targeted and less invasive alternative for focal leaks, particularly when prior postdilatation has failed and surgical risk is prohibitive.

A key instructive feature of this case was the intentional use of a misaligned Evolut FX+ diamond cell as an access route for PVL closure. Commissural malalignment is generally considered undesirable because of its implications for coronary access;8 however, in this instance, it created a favorable and reproducible access window. Preprocedural CT demonstrated that a large diamond-shaped cell of the Evolut frame was directly aligned with the PVL tract without compromising coronary flow. This configuration allowed a coaxial trajectory into the defect, avoiding the need to navigate around the tall nitinol frame or beneath the inflow skirt—common technical challenges when treating PVL after self-expanding valve implantation. Leveraging this frame-based access facilitated stable sheath positioning and precise device deployment, resulting in immediate and durable PVL reduction.

This case highlights the central role of meticulous preprocedural CT planning in PVL closure, which is critical for defining leak location, understanding frame geometry, identifying potential access routes, and minimizing procedural time, contrast use, and radiation exposure. As TAVR expands to younger and lower risk populations, durable and anatomically tailored solutions for PVL will become increasingly important, and frame-based strategies may play a growing role in the management of complex PVL anatomies.

Conclusions

Transcatheter PVL closure is a safe and effective treatment for symptomatic PVL after TAVR in high-risk patients. Comprehensive multimodality imaging is essential to identify the mechanism of PVL and guide procedural strategy. In select cases, frame-based access through an Evolut diamond cell may provide a feasible and effective route for targeted PVL closure.
