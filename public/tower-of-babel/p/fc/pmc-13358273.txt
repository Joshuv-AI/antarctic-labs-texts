Prevalence of suspected scoliosis based on angle of torso rotation in children and adolescents in Heqing County at different periods: a large-sample mixed-methods study
Authors: Li Zhang, Zhibo Song, Yanrui Yang, Yingsong Wang, Zhi Zhao, Xiaochen Yang, Genghao Qian, Weijie Xie, Zhiyue Shi, Tao Li, Ni Bi, Zhaoquan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo analyse changes in the prevalence of suspected scoliosis among children and adolescents in Heqing County, South-West China, over different periods and to identify risk factors for its progression.DesignA mixed-methods study, including a cross-sectional survey and longitudinal follow-up.SettingSingle-centre study. An angle of torso rotation (ATR) ≥5° was defined as a positive screening result and progression was defined as an increase in ATR ≥2°.ParticipantsIn November 2021, 22 740 students aged 6–18 years were screened; in May 2023, 28 009 students aged 6–18 years were screened. A total of 350 students with suspected scoliosis identified in 2021 were enrolled for longitudinal follow-up.ResultsThe prevalence of suspected scoliosis was 2.47% in 2021 (22 740 students) and 3.14% in 2023 (28 009 students). Among 4468 students screened in both years, 109 (2.44%) tested positive in 2021 and 124 (2.78%) in 2023. Prevalence was significantly higher in girls (2.92% in 2021; 4.36% in 2023) than in boys (2.01% in 2021; 1.94% in 2023) and higher in the 10–18 years age group (3.38% and 4.11%) than in the 6–10 years age group (0.59% and 0.83%; both p<0.001). From 2021 to 2023, overall prevalence, as well as prevalence among girls and the 10–18 years age group, increased significantly (all p<0.001), whereas no significant changes were observed in boys (p=0.71) or the 6–10 years age group (p=0.07). Of 329 students with suspected scoliosis in 2021 who completed follow-up in 2023, 35 (10.6%) showed progression. Progression was significantly associated with lower body mass index (BMI) and higher baseline ATR, with correlation coefficients of −0.112 and 0.127, respectively (both p<0.01). Binary logistic regression showed that higher BMI was associated with reduced progression risk (OR=0.827, 95% CI 0.700 to 0.978, p<0.05).ConclusionThe prevalence of suspected scoliosis in children and adolescents in Heqing County was higher in 2023 than in 2021. Approximately 10.6% of individuals with suspected scoliosis progressed over 1.5 years. BMI may serve as a protective factor against scoliosis progression.Keywords: Scoliosis, Epidemiology, Risk Factors, Bone diseases, Musculoskeletal disorders, Spine

Introduction

Scoliosis is a common spinal deformity in children and adolescents, with reported prevalence ranging from 0.47% to 5.2%1 2 Onset is insidious and progressive; in addition to cosmetic impairment, severe cases (Cobb angle >100°) may lead to cardiopulmonary dysfunction, chronic low back pain and even life-threatening complications.3,5

Severe scoliosis often arises from the gradual progression of mild curves.5 Thus, early detection, active monitoring and timely intervention are critical to preventing severe deformity. For children with mild scoliosis, bracing or surgery is indicated only for progressive curves, whereas non-progressive or spontaneously improving cases require no active treatment. However, epidemiological data on scoliosis progression remain limited, and relevant risk factors are incompletely understood. The lack of reliable predictive tools necessitates prolonged monitoring and frequent radiographic follow-up for all affected children, increasing both economic burden and radiation exposure.

Angle of torso rotation (ATR) is easy to measure, radiation-free and cost-neutral. As a sensitive and specific screening marker, ATR can indirectly reflect changes in the Cobb angle.6 7 Recent studies also suggest that ATR may predict pain intensity in adults with idiopathic scoliosis and quality of life in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis (AIS).8 9 Accordingly, ATR represents a promising tool for monitoring scoliosis progression, particularly in large-scale population-based surveillance.

To clarify the epidemiological characteristics and progression risk factors of suspected scoliosis, we conducted school-based screenings in Heqing County, south-western China, in 2021 and 2023, and followed 329 children with suspected scoliosis identified in 2021. This study compares the prevalence and progression of suspected scoliosis across periods and identifies predictive factors for progression.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from students, teachers and legal guardians.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Research subjects

Screening participants were primary and secondary school students aged 6–18 years in Heqing County, Yunnan, screened in November 2021 and May 2023. Inclusion criteria: (1) All primary and secondary school students in November 2021 and June 2023 in Heqing; (2) Students aged 6–18 years old. Exclusion criteria: (1) Students for whom Adams flexion test cannot be performed for various reasons; (2) Students unable to participate in scoliosis screening on time; (3) Students unwillingness to participate.

A total of 350 students with suspected scoliosis in 2021 were recruited for follow-up to assess ATR changes after 1.5 years. Inclusion criteria: (1) Students with suspected scoliosis in November 2021 in Heqing; (2) Students aged 6–18 years old. Exclusion criteria: (1) Students for whom Adams flexion test cannot be performed during follow-up for various reasons; (2) Students unable to attend follow-up on time; (3) Students unwilling to participate.

All examiners were specialised spine surgeons or nurses who completed standardised training and proficiency assessments before data collection.

Screening test

Visual examination: Students stood upright with arms relaxed at their sides. Shoulder asymmetry, scapular asymmetry, waistline asymmetry, uneven iliac crests or a deviated spinous process line were considered positive findings.

Adam’s forward bending test (FBT): Students bent forward approximately 90° with feet together and palms joined. Back asymmetry was recorded as a positive FBT.

ATR measurement: During FBT, ATR was measured from the spinous processes to the sacrum using a scoliometer. The maximum angle was recorded as ATR. ATR ≥5° was defined as a positive screening result.10 The screening process is summarised in figure 1.

Scoliosis school screening process. ATR, angle of torso rotation.

According to Chinese clinical guidelines, ATR severity was graded as: Grade I (5° ≤ ATR <7°), Grade II (7° ≤ ATR <10°) and Grade III (ATR ≥10°). Progression was defined as an increase in ATR ≥2°.

Data analysis

Data included sex, age, height, weight, curve location and ATR. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26.0. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean±SD; non-normally distributed data as median (IQR). Categorical data are reported as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used χ2 test, independent samples t-test or non-parametrical tests, as appropriate. Correlation and regression analyses were used to identify risk factors. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence of suspected scoliosis

In 2021, 22 740 students were screened, with a suspected scoliosis prevalence of 2.47%. In 2023, 28 009 students were screened, with a prevalence of 3.14%. Among 4468 students screened in both years, 109 (2.44%) were positive in 2021 and 124 (2.78%) in 2023. In both years, prevalence was significantly higher in girls, students aged 10–18 years and middle school students (p<0.001). The overall prevalence, female prevalence, 10–18 years age group prevalence and Bai ethnic group prevalence were all significantly higher in 2023 than in 2021 (p<0.001). No significant differences were observed in boys or the 6–10 years age group (p=0.722 and p=0.074, respectively; table 1).

The prevalence of suspected scoliosis increased slowly from ages 6 years to 10 years, rose rapidly after age 10 years, peaked in mid-adolescence and declined slightly thereafter. This pattern was consistent across sex and study years. Peak prevalence occurred at age 16 years in boys, 17–18 years in girls and 17 years overall (figure 2).

Characteristics of positive cases

There were 552 positive cases in 2021 (225 boys, 327 girls) and 877 in 2023 (275 boys, 602 girls), with a significant between-year difference in sex distribution (p<0.001). No significant differences were observed in age, ethnicity, height, weight, body mass index (BMI) or ATR between 2021 and 2023 (p=0.062–0.890; table 2). The most common curve location was lumbar in both 2021 (46.9%) and 2023 (36.4%), although curve distribution differed significantly between years (p<0.001; figure 3). Mean ATR was 6.9±2.0° in 2021 and 6.7±1.7° in 2023. Most cases were mild or moderate: mild 53.3% vs 55.0%, moderate 35.9% vs 38.7%, severe 10.8% vs 6.3% (2021 vs 2023). Severity distribution differed significantly between years (p<0.05).

Follow-up and progression

We followed up with 350 students suspected of scoliosis in 2021; 21 were lost to follow-up (dropout rate 6.0%). The final analysis included 329 patients (110 boys, 219 girls). At baseline, the median age was 13.0 years, height 160 cm, weight 45 kg, BMI 17.7 kg/m²; 62.0% were of Bai ethnicity, and 47.7% had lumbar curves (table 3). During follow-up, 35 students (10.6%) showed progression: 10 boys (9.1%) and 25 girls (10.9%). Progression rates did not differ significantly by sex, age, ethnicity or curve location (p=0.274–0.807; table 4).

Risk factors for progression

Progression was significantly correlated with baseline BMI (r=−0.112) and baseline ATR (r=0.127; both p<0.01). No significant correlations were found for sex, age, ethnicity, height or weight (p=0.218–0.990; table 5). Binary logistic regression confirmed that higher BMI was independently associated with lower progression risk (OR=0.827, 95% CI 0.700 to 0.978, p<0.05; table 6).

Discussion

Global prevalence estimates for paediatric and adolescent scoliosis vary widely, ranging from 0.47% to 5.2%.1 11 12 13 A recent meta-analysis of over 55 million children and adolescents reported a pooled prevalence of approximately 3.1%14, with some regions reporting rates as high as 24.3%.15 A systematic review estimated the prevalence among Chinese adolescents aged 10–18 years at 1.2%.16 Owing to China’s large geographical and socioeconomic diversity, regional prevalence varies: 5.14% in Guangdong17, 2.5% in Hebei Province, 18 1.3% in northern China, 1.2% in southern China16 and 2.4% in eastern China.19

Studies from western China remain scarce, likely due to mountainous terrain, limited transportation, inadequate medical resources and relatively low scoliosis awareness. Most available reports from this region use school-based screening with a cut-off of ATR ≥5°. 20 A systematic review reported a mean prevalence of suspected scoliosis of 4.40±2.81% in Mainland China.21 Our previous study in Dali Bai Autonomous Prefecture found an overall prevalence of 2.37%20, similar to the 2.47% (2021) and 3.14% (2023) rates in the present study but lower than the national average. This discrepancy may reflect our broad age range (6–18 years), whereas many studies focus on the high-risk 10–18 years age group. In our study, the prevalence in adolescents age 10–18 years (3.41% in 2021; 4.11% in 2023) is closer to that of national estimates. Nevertheless, our rates remain much lower than those reported in Prishtina (26.1%).22

Most studies report a significantly higher prevalence in girls11 12 14 16 23, consistent with our findings. Although some studies found no sex difference13 or even male predominance, our results align with the majority of evidence. Previous research also indicates increasing prevalence with age14 16 24, although small sample sizes may account for null findings in some studies.13 Our cohort of 6–18 years showed slow increases from 6 years to 10 years, rapid acceleration after age 10 years, and a slight decline after peak adolescence, consistent with typical pubertal growth patterns.25,30

The higher prevalence of suspected scoliosis in 2023 rather than 2021 may reflect several factors: (1) Cohort differences: The 2023 screening included 5381 more students than 2021, of whom 4807 (89.3%) were aged 10–18 years, a high-risk group, inherently increasing the potential prevalence. (2) Increased awareness: The 2021 screening was the first of its kind in Heqing. Initial understanding of scoliosis was limited, with misconceptions about treatment being common. Although professional personnel have popularised and promoted knowledge related to scoliosis in the later stages of screening, a considerable number of people were unwilling to actively participate in scoliosis screening due to concerns or anxiety, especially in the early stages of screening when there is no popularisation of scoliosis knowledge, which undoubtedly leads to a low prevalence of screening in 2021. (3) Rising trend: Recent studies suggest a rising global prevalence of scoliosis, which may have also contributed to the higher rate observed in 2023.16

Screened patients with suspected or confirmed scoliosis are typically mild to moderate in severity.12 16 19 23 While some studies report thoracic scoliosis as predominant31, others, like ours, find lumbar curves most common.12 Although curve distribution differed between 2021 and 2023, lumbar dominance persisted.

The Scoliosis Research Society defines early onset scoliosis (EOS) as onset before age 10 years. Previous studies report an annual EOS incidence of 0.019% and prevalence of 0.077%.31 Our positive rates in children age 6–10 years (0.59% in 2021; 0.83% in 2023) were higher, likely because we did not include children under 6 years, the age at which EOS is most often diagnosed.31

We observed a 10.6% progression rate over 1.5 years based on ATR. A 10-year follow-up study reported progression in 35.8% of patients with baseline Cobb angle <10° and 48.3% with Cobb angle 10°−20°. 32 AlNouri et al conducted a follow-up study on patients with EOS, with male patients followed up until the age of 14 years and female patients followed up until the age of 12 years.31 Among them, 38% of the patients experienced progression, while 44.33% did not. A 40–50 years natural history study found a progression rate of approximately 68% in AIS.5 The shorter follow-up duration in our study is the most likely explanation for our lower progression rate.

In addition, we selected ATR to assess the progression in our study, and the reasons why we selected ATR rather than Cobb angle were as follows: first, as we said before, the ATR can indirectly reflect changes in the Cobb angle.6 7 Second, ATR was often used to assess the effectiveness of exercise therapy in patients with scoliosis. 33 Third, ATR can be a predictor of pain intensity and quality of life in patients with AIS. 8 9 Finally, ATR has high importance value in large-scale scoliosis screening and monitoring because it can be easily obtained and is non-radiative.

We found a weak negative correlation between BMI and progression, and multivariate analysis identified BMI as a protective factor. Several studies have linked low BMI to higher scoliosis risk19 25 34, although Ciaccia et al15 reported that obesity may also increase prevalence. The relationship between BMI and scoliosis development and progression appears complex, and the underlying mechanism requires further investigation.

Limitations

First, not all screen-positive students underwent confirmatory imaging; thus, true Cobb angles and definitive scoliosis prevalence could not be determined. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short, which may have underestimated progression. We plan to continue follow-up until skeletal maturity. Third, loss to follow-up (6.0%) may have introduced bias. Fourth, we did not assess skeletal maturity directly, although age served as a partial proxy. Future studies should include skeletal maturity assessment.
