Unraveling the interplay between a small RNA and RNase E in bacteria
Authors: Meshi Barsheshet Vigoda, Liron Argaman, Mark Kournos, Hanah Margalit
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall RNAs (sRNAs) are major regulators of gene expression in bacteria, exerting their regulation primarily via base pairing with their target transcripts and modulating translation. Accumulating evidence suggest that sRNAs can also affect the stability of their target transcripts by altering their accessibility to endoribonucleases. Yet, the effects of sRNAs on transcript stability and the mechanisms underlying them have not been studied in wide scale. Here we employ large-scale RNA-seq-based methodologies in the model bacterium Escherichia coli to quantitatively study the functional interaction between a sRNA and an endoribonuclease in regulating gene expression, using the well-established sRNA, GcvB, and the major endoribonuclease, RNase E. Studying single and double mutants of gcvB and rne and analysing their RNA-seq results by the Double Mutant Cycle approach, we infer distinct modes of the interplay between GcvB and RNase E. Transcriptome-wide mapping of RNase E cleavage sites provides further support to the results of the RNA-seq analysis, identifying cleavage sites in targets in which the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E is evident. Together, our results indicate that the most dominant mode of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction is GcvB enhancement of RNase E cleavage, which varies in its magnitude between different targets.

Introduction

The levels of gene transcripts in a bacterial cell are adjusted by various factors that act at the transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels. First and for most are transcription factors that regulate transcription initiation rates. At the posttranscriptional level, endoribonucleases play major roles in degradation and processing of RNA transcripts. Among these stands out RNase E, which makes up part of the RNA degradosome and is involved in RNA degradation of bulk mRNA in the cell and processing of rRNA, tRNA and mRNA in many bacteria (1–3). RNase E is a large multidomain endoribonuclease with a highly conserved N-terminal catalytic domain, which cleaves single stranded RNA in A/U-rich regions (4,5). RNase E enzymatic activity was shown to be greatest for RNA substrates with a 5′ monophosphate group (6). In addition to endoribonucleases, other major posttranscriptional regulators that shape the bacterial transcriptome are small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs). These 50–400 nucleotide-long RNA molecules are encoded in the genomes of Gram-negative bacteria and play central roles in various cellular processes, especially under specific growth or stress conditions (reviewed in (7)). Most sRNAs exert their regulatory function in trans by base pairing with the RNA transcript of their target genes, while both the sRNA and the target RNA are bound to the protein chaperone Hfq (8). Trans-encoded base-pairing sRNAs act mostly as negative regulators of their targets, but can also function as positive regulators (7). In fact, a sRNA can be a negative regulator of some targets and a positive regulator of others. sRNAs have been mainly acknowledged as regulating the translation initiation of their targets, but there is growing understanding that they also affect the stability of their target transcripts (e.g. (9–12)) (Figure 1).

The target mRNA has a sRNA binding site adjacent to the ribosome binding site; when the sRNA is not present (-sRNA), ribosomes can bind the RBS and translate the mRNA into a protein. The RBS is occluded in a stem-loop structure and is inaccessible to the ribosome (-sRNA). When the sRNA is present (+sRNA), its base pairs with the region of the stem-loop structure of the mRNA and exposes the RBS, enabling ribosome binding and initiation of translation. As a result, the mRNA is covered with ribosomes that protect it from degradation by RNase E, increasing its stability. (C, D). Translation-independent effects of the sRNA on RNase E cleavage. (C) Negative regulation. A RNase E cleavage site is occluded in a secondary structure within the target RNA molecule. Without the sRNA (-sRNA), the cleavage site is inaccessible to RNase E and the target RNA is not degraded. When the sRNA is present (+sRNA), it base pairs with the target RNA, opens the secondary structure and exposes the RNase E cleavage site, enabling degradation of the target RNA. (D) Positive regulation. A RNase E cleavage site overlaps the sRNA binding site on the RNA target. Without the sRNA (-sRNA), the target RNA is cleaved by RNase E. When the sRNA is expressed (+sRNA), it base pairs with the target RNA at the cleavage site and prevents cleavage by RNase E, stabilizing the target RNA.

sRNAs influence the stability of their target mRNAs mainly by affecting their exposure to RNase E cleavage (13), either by their effect on translation initiation or by inducing structural changes. As regulators of translation initiation, sRNAs influence the density of the ribosomes covering the mRNA, and, hence, its exposure to RNase E binding and cleavage (Figure 1A, B). In this mechanism, the binding of the sRNA to the target mRNA can be distal or proximal to the RNase E cleavage site. sRNAs can also affect RNase E cleavage in a translation-independent manner (e.g. (14)). They can impose a structural change on the target mRNA, which affects the accessibility of the RNase E cleavage site (Figure 1C, e.g. (15)), demonstrating possible cleavage enhancement or reduction that are translation independent. Here also, the base pairing of the sRNA with the target may be distal to the cleavage site, as demonstrated, for example, for sodB mRNA, a target of RyhB (16), or close to the cleavage site, as demonstrated in Figure 1C (e.g. (15)). In addition, by base pairing with their target transcripts, sRNAs can mask a cleavage site and prevent cleavage by RNase E, leading to stabilization of the transcript (Figure 1D), as shown, for example, in the case of cfa mRNA stabilization by the sRNA RydC (17). It was also suggested that by binding to their target RNA, sRNAs can provide the 5′P that plays a role in enhancing RNase E contact with the target (18,19), although a recent study has challenged this mechanism and suggested that sRNAs are involved in mRNA decay through Hfq-RNase E interaction (20). Taken together, in all of these mechanisms there is a functional interaction between the sRNA and RNase E, which affects the target transcript stability. This functional interaction, which does not imply physical interaction, can be translation-dependent or independent.

While translation-independent effects of sRNAs on RNase E cleavage have been demonstrated through a few individual examples (e.g. (16,17)) and the concept of translation-dependent effects of sRNAs on endoribonuclease cleavage has been widely acknowledged, there has not been a systematic study assessing quantitatively the functional interaction between a sRNA and RNase E in the regulation of different targets. Here, we address this challenge by the Double Mutant Cycle approach, presented originally to study the functional interaction between two transcription factors in regulation of gene expression (21). We use this approach to study the functional interaction between a sRNA and an endoribonuclease, using the well-established sRNA, GcvB, and RNase E in E. coli. GcvB is a highly conserved sRNA (22,23), known to regulate genes involved in amino acid metabolism and transport (10,22,24). Fifty-one targets of GcvB were previously reported based on experimental evidence (24–30) (Supplementary Table S1). By measuring the effect of RNase E on gene transcript levels in strains expressing GcvB and in gcvB-deleted strains, we disentangle quantitatively the individual and combined effects of GcvB and RNase E in regulation of gene expression. Furthermore, mapping RNase E cleavage sites at a transcriptome-wide scale in each of those strains further supports the RNA-seq analysis results and conclusions. Our results suggest that for most targets affected by GcvB at their transcript level, the sRNA enhances the cleavage by RNase E. Yet, the magnitude of the effect of GcvB on RNase E cleavage differs among different GcvB targets.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

Escherichia coli K-12 MG1655Z1 was used for the construction of the different strains in this study. The rne3071:Tn10 mutation was transduced by P1 transduction from strain EM1277, followed by sequence verification. The gltIAAA (Supplementary Material) and gltI5UTR mutations were introduced using the Scarless Recombineering method (31). To construct a mutated insH3-gltI fragment, the insH3-gltI locus was amplified by two PCR reactions, using two oligonucleotide pairs: For gltIAAA, oligonucleotide pairs gltI_AAA_F/gltI_R and insH3_F/gltI_AAA_R were used. For gltI5UTR, oligonucleotide pairs gltI_5UTR_F /gltI_R and insH3_F/gltI_5UTR_R were used. The two PCR products were then cleaned-up, mixed in equal volumes, and used as template for a third PCR reaction using primers insH3_F/gltI_R, giving rise to the mutated insH3-gltI fragments. Each of the mutated fragments was used for the recombineering procedure as described (31). To create the p-gcvB_mut plasmid, the p-gcvB plasmid was mutated using the QuickChange Lightning kit (Agilent technologies), with oligonucleotides gcvB_mut_F/gcvB_mut_R. The comprehensive list of strains and plasmids used in this study can be found in Table 1, and the oligonucleotides employed are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

RNA extraction

Single colonies were cultivated in LB-Amp medium overnight at 30°C with shaking. The following day, each culture was diluted 1:100 into flasks containing fresh LB-Amp medium and grown at 30°C while shaken at 200 rpm until reaching an OD600 of 0.3. Half of each culture was transferred to a new flask and grown at 44°C, while the other half remained at 30°C. After 20 min, both cultures were treated with IPTG at a final concentration of 1 mM and incubated for an additional 10 min at their respective temperatures (Figure 2A). The experiments were done with three biological replicates per each combination of strain and temperature.

Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation for 5 min at 4°C, 4696g, re-suspended in 50 μl TE (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA) and mixed with 5 μl of 9 mg/ml lysozyme prior to freezing the sample in liquid nitrogen and storing it at –80°C. Frozen samples were subjected to two cycles of thawing at 37°C and refreezing in liquid nitrogen. Next, the samples were resuspended thoroughly to homogenization with 1 ml TriReagent (pre-warmed to room temperature) and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Two hundred microliters of chloroform were added, and the tubes content was mixed by inverting the tubes for 15 s. The samples were incubated for 10 min at room temperature, centrifuged (25 000g, 10 min at 4°C) and the upper phase was collected and transferred into new Eppendorf tubes. For RNA precipitation, 500 μl isopropanol were added, the tube contents were mixed thoroughly by inversion of the tubes and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The tubes were centrifuged (25 000g,15 min at 4°C) and the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were washed twice by addition of 1 ml of freshly made 75% (vol/vol) ethanol, followed by centrifugation (25 000g, 5 min at 4°C) and removal of the supernatant. Pellets were air dried by at room temperature, and then resuspended in 300 μl nuclease free water and stored at –20°C. The RNA concentration was measured using Nanodrop (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Construction of RNA-seq libraries

Whole transcriptome libraries were prepared and sequenced at Vertis Biotechnologie AG (Munich, Germany), applying a standard protocol described previously (34,35). In brief, ribosomal RNA molecules were depleted using an in-house protocol. The depleted RNA samples were first fragmented using ultrasound (1 pulse of 30 s each at 4°C). Then, an oligonucleotide adapter was ligated to the 3′ end of the RNA molecules. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using M-MLV reverse transcriptase and a primer complementary to the 3′ adapter. The first strand cDNA was purified and the 5′ Illumina TruSeq sequencing adapter was ligated to the 3′ end of the antisense cDNA. The resulting cDNA was PCR-amplified to about 10–20 ng/μl using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase. The TruSeq barcode sequences, which are part of the 5′ and 3′ TruSeq sequencing adapters, are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The cDNA was purified using the Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics) and was analyzed by capillary electrophoresis. The cDNA libraries have a size of about 200–600 bp. The 3′ and 5′ adapters and the primers used for PCR amplification were designed for TruSeq sequencing according to the instructions of Illumina. Adapter sequences flanking the cDNA inserts are noted in Supplementary Table S2. The cDNA library was single-end sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 system using 75 bp read length.

Construction of TIER-seq libraries

To identify cleavage sites created by RNase E, we leveraged the characteristic that it leaves fragments with 5′P end after cleavage. We employed the TIER-seq protocol, which enables to construct libraries of 5′P RNA fragments. Libraries were prepared and sequenced at Vertis Biotechnologie AG (Munich, Germany) following the previously described TIER-seq protocol (36). In brief, the total RNA samples were poly(A)-tailed using poly(A) polymerase. Then, the 5′PPP was changed to 5′P using RNA 5′ Polyphosphatase (Lucigen). Next, an RNA adapter was ligated to the 5′-monophosphate of the RNA. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using an oligo(dT)- adapter primer and the M-MLV reverse transcriptase. The resulting cDNAs were PCR-amplified to about 10–20 ng/μl using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase.

The cDNAs were purified using the Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics) and were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis. The cDNA libraries have a size of about 200–600 bp. The 3′ and 5′ adapters and the primers used for PCR amplification were designed for TruSeq sequencing according to the instructions of Illumina. Barcode sequences, which are part of the 5′ and 3′ sequencing adapters, are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The cDNA library was sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 system using 75 bp read length, single-end sequencing.

Processing of sequencing data

For both whole transcriptome and TIER-seq sequencing we processed and mapped the sequenced reads as follows. Reads were split into their library of origin using the barcode sequences at the beginning of the read by an in-house script. The reads were then processed by cutadapt (https://journal.embnet.org/index.php/embnetjournal/article/view/200) (37) to remove low-quality ends and adapter sequences (reads ≤ 15 nucleotides were excluded from the analysis). Reads were mapped to the genome of E. coli K-12 MG1655 (RefSeq accession number NC_000913.3) using bwa aln followed by bwa samse for single-end sequencing (38). For the whole transcriptome sequencing data, bwa samse version 0.7.17-r1188 with default parameters was run.

For the TIER-seq sequencing data, the parameters used in bwa aln run were -t 8 -R 200 -l 4 -k 0. Default parameters were used in bwa samse run. Note that the choice of parameters for the processing and mapping software packages was aimed at maximizing the accuracy of the detection of the original 5′ end of the transcripts. Thus, there was no removal of 5′ end adapters (even at the cost of reduced mapping), reads that were mapped with soft-clipping were excluded, and no mismatches were allowed at the first four positions of the mapped read (-l 4 -k 0). A custom script was used to retrieve the number of reads that start at each position, representing the fragments with 5′P. Importantly, only the first read position was considered where the other read positions were ignored. For both types of sequencing data, a custom script was used to retrieve the gene/position count files from the bwa files. The Deeptools heatmap tool (version 3.5.2) (39) was utilized to evaluate the consistency among the replicates of the same strain.

Northern blot analysis

Total RNA (30 μg) was denatured for 10 min at 65°C in RNA loading buffer containing 40% formamide and 1.3 M formaldehyde, and then separated on 1.4% agarose gel containing 1.17 M formaldehyde in 20 mM MOPS, 2 mM Na-citrate and 1 mM EDTA pH 8.0. RNA was transferred to Zeta-Probe membrane (Bio Rad) by capillary transfer in 10X SSC (1.5 M NaCl and 150 mM Na-citrate). The membranes were hybridized with specific [32P] end labeled DNA probes in Ultrahyb-Oligo Hybridization Buffer (Invitrogen) at 45°C overnight, washed 2–3 times for 10 min in 2× SSC 0.1% SDS at 45°C and visualized by Phosphorimager (Typhoon FLA 7000, GE Life Sciences). For each tested RNA, the northern blot was repeated at least twice, with a different replicate of total RNA. The probe sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Annotating GcvB target genes

Genes were marked as GcvB targets if they were either previously recorded as known targets or identified by RIL-seq as GcvB targets (25) (Supplementary Table S1). Based on the annotation in RegulonDB (40), genes residing in an operon that includes a reported target of GcvB were marked as ‘in an operon with GcvB target’.

Annotation of genomic elements

For the analyses of the RNA-seq and the TIER-seq data, the genomic annotation of MG1655 was used and extended by custom annotations of sRNAs. In addition, UTRs were added for the analysis of TIER-seq data. The annotations of mRNA, tRNA, rRNA and other non-coding RNAs in E. coli K-12 MG1655 genome database (NC_000913.3.gff) was based on EcoCyc version 22.0 (http://ecocyc.org) (41). If the 5′ and 3′ ends of a protein-coding gene were annotated in EcoCyc, we used these annotations to determine the 5′UTR and 3′UTR (termed 5UTR and 3UTR in figures and tables). If the transcription start site or transcription termination site of a mRNA were unknown, the UTRs were considered the regions 100 nt upstream the ATG and downstream the stop codon, respectively (or shorter if these regions spanned another transcript). In such cases, the UTRs were termed EST3UTR/EST5UTR for estimated UTRs. A coding region within a mRNA was termed CDS. Intergenic regions were termed IGR if their flanking genes were not in the same transcription unit or IGT if the two flanking genes shared a transcript. In addition, we termed regions encoding non-coding RNAs as following: tRNA, rRNA, sRNA - a sRNA being one that exerts its regulation via base-pairing and has at least one known trans-encoded target. Regions antisense to genes or to IGT were termed AS and AS_IGT, respectively.

Extracting the contributions of GcvB, RNase E and their functional interaction to the change in gene expression levels

In order to extract the relative contribution of GcvB, RNase E and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction, the double mutant cycle approach was used (21). To compare gene expression levels between strain combinations, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (42) (version 1.28.1, package in R version 4.2.2). The read counts were normalized across all libraries. DESeq2 was applied using the default run parameters with the addition of the pre-filtering parameter set to 1, which only retains genes with at least two reads in at least one library. For comparing the normalized counts between the tested libraries, two types of tests implemented by DESeq2 were conducted: (i) Wald test was used to determine statistically significantly changed genes between two condition/strain combinations. (ii) The likelihood ratio test was applied to determine genes with statistically significant GcvB-RNase E functional interaction contribution to the change in gene expression. We applied the DESeq2 ‘interaction’ design (∼ genotype.1 + genotype.2 + genotype.1:: genotype.2), where ‘genotype.1’ refers to the rne gene and ‘genotype.2’ to the expression of gcvB from a plasmid. Information on this design can be found in the vignette of DESeq2 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/devel/bioc/vignettes/DESeq2/inst/doc/DESeq2.html#interactions). For both statistical tests, the P-value was corrected for multiple hypothesis testing by Benjamini-Hochberg method (termed hereinafter Padj, for adjusted P-value) (43), and results with Padj ≤ 0.01 were considered statistically significant.

On the right there are, the design matrix, the vector of contributions of the three components (GcvB (G), RNase E (R) and their functional interaction (Int)), and a matrix representing the noise for each comparison (ϵ). For each gene, a linear fit to the data was conducted (employed by DESeq2) to infer the contribution of each component to the gene expression change, minimizing the errors ϵ across all genotypes and replicates (21).

Gene classification by the contributions of RNase E and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction to the change in gene expression

Each of the genes with a statistically significant functional interaction component (Padj ≤ 0.01) was assigned a vector of size 3 with the values of the contributions to the change in expression level of (i) GcvB independent of RNase E (G), (ii) RNase E independent of GcvB (R) and (iii) GcvB-RNase E functional interaction (Int), as described above. Since the independent contribution of GcvB was minimal and was not statistically significant for all but six genes, we divided the genes to four clusters based on the contributions of RNase E and the functional interaction term to the change in gene expression: Cluster I: log2 fold change of RNase E contribution <0 and log2 fold change of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction <0. Cluster II: log2 fold change of RNase E contribution >0 and log2 fold change of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction >0. Cluster III: log2 fold change of RNase E contribution >0 and log2 fold change of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction <0. Cluster IV: log2 fold change of RNase E contribution <0 and log2 fold change of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction >0. The values of G, R and Int for each gene in the four clusters are presented by a heatmap (generated by pheatmap package, version 1.0.12).

Identification of RNase E cleavage sites in TIER-seq sequencing data

Various cellular processes generate 5′P fragments. To specifically identify those that are produced by RNase E cleavage, we prepared 5′P libraries from wild type and from a rne3071 mutant (rnets), in which RNase E is temporarily inactive at 44°C (three biological replicates each). We then counted for each library, as described above, the number of read starts that mapped to each position along each of the genome strands. The read start counts were normalized by DESeq2 using all 12 libraries in our study (3 X gcvB+/rnewt, gcvB−/rnewt, gcvB+/rnets, gcvB−/rnets). Since by the TIER-seq protocol the 5′PPP of a transcript is converted by Tap treatment to 5′P, transcription start sites (TSSs) could be falsely interpreted as RNase E cleavage sites. To minimize such potential false-positive detections, we excluded positions of known TSSs (44) from the analysis. Finally, we applied DESeq2 analysis (42) version 1.28.1 under R version 4.2.2 to identify positions that differed statistically significantly in read start counts between the wild type and mutant libraries. At the end of this process each position was assigned two values: (i) log2 fold change, which is the log2 of the ratio of DESeq2-normalized read start counts between two strains. (ii) Padj, which is the statistical significance of the log2 fold change value (corrected for multiple hypothesis testing by False Discovery Rate). DESeq2 was applied using the default run parameters with the addition of the pre-filtering parameter set to 1, which only retains positions with at least two reads in at least one library. A position was considered as a putative RNase E cleavage site if it passed the following criteria: for rnewt versus rnets at 44°C, log2 fold change ≥ 1, Padj ≤ 0.01 and average normalized counts >50 for the three libraries of rnewt strain (with or without GcvB expression).

Comparing RNase E cleavage sites between different datasets

We compared the RNase E cleavage sites determined in our study to experimentally-determined RNase E cleavage sites in Salmonella (36). For this we first extracted sequences of 15 nucleotides upstream and downstream the identified cleavage sites in Salmonella Typhimurium SL1344 and determined their homologous regions in E. coli K-12 MG1655 by blast-short using BIOpython package (version 1.76), employing blastn (version 2.8.1) with e-value ≤0.05. The word size parameter was set to 17 (ensuring that the cleavage site and the U located two positions downstream are included). For consistency, in comparisons to other datasets of RNase E cleavage sites (E. coli O157:H7 str. Sakai (45) and E. coli BW25113 (46)), only E. coli genes homologous to Salmonella Typhimurium SL1344 genes were considered. Comparison of cleavage sites was made at the gene level, and the results were presented as an UpSet plot using R (version 4.2.2) library ‘UpSetR’.

Analysis of sequence features of cleavage sites

To identify the sequence and structural features of RNase E cleavage sites, sequences of five nucleotides upstream and downstream of the identified cleavage sites were extracted and aligned at the positions flanking the cleavage site. To assess sequence conservation, the information content of each position was computed (47). The logo of the RNase E-detected motif was generated using all the sequences derived from the 7490 cleavage sites by using library ‘ggseqlogo’ in R (version 4.2.2).

Identification of stable RNA regions

Positive log2 fold change values between rnewt strains and rnets strains in the total RNA experiments suggest that RNase E increases the expression levels of these genes. As this result might be due to RNA regions that were stabilized following RNase E cleavage we developed a score for the detection of stable regions based on read coverage data. To identify putatively stabilized regions, we conducted the following analysis: (i) we calculated for each gene the average positional read count and its standard deviation (along all the gene's positions); (ii) using these values, we computed a z-score of read counts for each position in a given gene; (iii) we then defined a stable region as a region of at least 50 consecutive positions with z-score ≥2. Regions that were determined as stable in all three libraries of the gcvB+/ rnewt strain, but not in the rnets strain, were recorded.

Secondary structure prediction

To assess changes in RNA secondary structure in GcvB targets, we analyzed the sequence ranging from the annotated transcription start site (TSS) to the end of the coding sequence. To this end, we employed ViennaRNA tools version 2.3.6. (48) Specifically, RNAfold was utilized to predict the secondary structure without GcvB binding, while RNAcofold was employed to predict the secondary structure when GcvB is bound. We compared the accessibility of the RNase E cleavage site in the structures obtained with and without GcvB.

Results

Experimental design using the double mutant cycle approach

The experimental design includes measuring of gene expression by RNA-seq in four strains derived from E. coli K-12 MG1655, presenting different combinations of RNase E and GcvB activities (Figure 2A). In all strains the gcvB gene is deleted and they contain either a control plasmid, i.e. GcvB is not expressed (hereinafter, gcvB−), or an inducible plasmid expressing gcvB (hereinafter, gcvB+). Each of these strains carries either the wild type rne gene (hereinafter, rnewt), or a temperature sensitive mutation, rne-3071 (hereinafter, rnets). In the wild type rne strains, RNase E is active when the bacteria are grown at either 30°C or 44°C. In the rnets strains, RNase E is active at 30°C but is inactive at 44°C. We term the four strains as follows: (i) gcvB+/rnewt: GcvB is expressed and RNase E is active at 44°C; (ii) gcvB+/rnets: GcvB is expressed and RNase E is inactive at 44°C; (iii) gcvB−/rnewt: GcvB is not expressed and RNase E is active at 44°C; (iv) gcvB−/rnets: GcvB is not expressed and RNase E is inactive at 44°C. The RNA-seq results of the strains are then incorporated into the double mutant cycle analysis scheme, represented schematically as a diamond in Figure 2B. Each of the arrows in the diamond describes a comparison of RNA-seq results between the two strains it connects. For example, in comparison A we compare gene expression between the strains gcvB+/rnewt and gcvB−/rnewt. The change in gene expression between these strains is due to any effect GcvB may have on the transcript level of the gene, independent of RNase E, and to the effect of GcvB on RNase E cleavage of the gene transcript (referred here as the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E, Figure 2B). Accordingly, the other comparisons manifest different combinations of the effects of GcvB, RNase E and the functional interaction between them on the change in gene expression between the various strains, as shown in Figure 2B. By fitting a linear model to the data of each gene in the different comparisons (21,49) (Materials and methods), we can quantitatively infer the contributions of GcvB, RNase E and the functional interaction between them to the change in gene expression and understand the relative contribution of each component to the change in transcript level of the various genes. Hereinafter, we use interchangeably the terms ‘gene expression’ and ‘transcript level’.

Confirming the differences between the strains

We constructed the above described four strains (Figure 2A). RNA-seq was applied to each of the four strains grown to exponential phase at 30°C, and then either kept at 30°C for 30 min or moved to 44°C for 30 min. GcvB expression was induced in the last 10 min of growth (Materials and methods). The libraries of strains that were grown at 44°C and 30°C (three libraries for each strain per temperature, a total of 12 libraries for each temperature), successfully passed the quality and consistency assessments, and they comprised the set of libraries analyzed in our study (Supplementary Table S3). The read counts were normalized by DESeq2 (42) across all libraries of a given temperature, and gene expression levels were compared between the pairs of strains, as indicated in Figure 2B (Materials and methods).

First, to validate the inactivity of RNase E in the mutant strain grown at 44°C, we examined in the RNA-seq results the changes in expression level of rne itself between the strains rnewt and rnets, as it was previously shown that RNase E cleaves its own mRNA to regulate its level (50). We observed equivalent counts of reads corresponding to rne in libraries of the wild type strain grown at both temperatures and in the rnets strains grown at 30°C (Supplementary Figure S1A, B), while at 44°C, the read counts corresponding to rne in the mutant strains were substantially higher than in the wild type strains (Padj≤ 3.5e-23 for gcvB+/rnewt versus gcvB+/rnets strains and Padj≤ 9.4e-12 for gcvB−/rnewt versus gcvB−/rnets strain; Padj is the P-value corrected for testing of multiple hypotheses, computed by DESeq2), suggesting there is no autoregulation of rne in the mutant strain at 44°C, consistent with inactivity of the enzyme at this temperature.

In addition, the inactivity of the mutant RNase E was reflected in the general results of the differential gene expression analysis between the libraries of strains rnewt and rnets grown at 30°C or 44°C, in both gcvB+ and gcvB− backgrounds. As expected, at 30°C less than 0.0001% of the genes showed a statistically significant change in gene expression in both comparisons, gcvB+/rnewt versus gcvB+/rnets and gcvB−/rnewt versus gcvB−/rnets (Padj ≤ 0.01, Figure 3A, B). Upon shift to 44°C, >40% of the genes showed a statistically significant change (Padj≤ 0.01) in transcript level between rnewt and rnets in the two gcvB backgrounds (Figure 3C, D). These results, once again, confirm the inactivity of RNase E in the mutant strain grown at 44°C.

The distinction between the strains grown at 44°C is also evident in the results of the principal component analysis (PCA) applied to the 12 libraries, where libraries of biological replicates of a strain were clustered close together (Supplementary Figure S1C). While there was clear separation both between strains of rnewt and rnets and between gvcB+ and gcvB− strains, most of the variance, as expected, could be explained by RNase E inactivation at 44°C. Such a distinction was not apparent for the strains grown at 30°C (Supplementary Figure S1D), as at 30°C the RNase E encoded by the mutant is active. Still, there was some distinction between strains expressing GcvB and those that are not, possibly due to effects of GcvB on gene expression that are independent of RNase E. The latter may be achieved by GcvB effect on other endoribonucleases or by its effect on premature transcription termination.

Finally, <10 reads corresponding to gcvB were identified in libraries of the strain harboring the control plasmid grown at both temperatures, confirming the absence of GcvB in the control plasmid strains (Supplementary Table S5, sheet of normalized counts). We also verified the absence of GcvB in the control strains by northern blot analysis (Supplementary Figure S1E). As shown in Supplementary Figure S1E, GcvB is highly expressed in the strains overexpressing it from an inducible plasmid. Consistent with a previous study (30), we observed some decrease in the levels of GcvB in rnets as compared to rnewt.

Extracting the contribution of GcvB, RNase E and the functional interaction between them to the changes in gene transcript levels

We compared gene expression levels by DESeq2 (42) between the pairs of strains connected by arrows in Figure 2B (grown at 44°C), and report genes for which the expression change (log2 fold change) was statistically significant (Padj ≤ 0.01). As expected, since RNase E is the major endoribonuclease in E. coli and cleaves many transcripts, many genes showed expression changes in the comparison between a strain in which RNase E is active (rnewt) and a strain in which RNase E is inactive (rnets) (Figure 3C, D and Supplementary Tables S4, S5). As is clearly seen in Figure 3, this result was obtained for strains in which GcvB is either active (Figure 3C, comparison B in Figure 2B) or inactive (Figure 3D, comparison C in Figure 2B). About half of the genes with a statistically significant expression change showed a decrease in their transcript level, conceivably due to the transcript destabilization following RNase E cleavage. Interestingly, the other half of the genes that changed statistically significantly showed an increase in their transcript level due to RNase E activity (higher normalized read counts in the rnewt strain compared to rnets strain). This may be either due to secondary effects or to stabilization of transcripts by RNase E cleavage ((51), and as demonstrated previously for gadE stabilization following processing by RNase III (52), see below).

Most interesting is the comparison of transcript levels between a strain expressing GcvB and one that is not expressing GcvB, when either RNase E is active (comparison A in Figure 2B) or is inactive (comparison D in Figure 2B). In strains in which RNase E is active, 297 genes changed their expression level due to GcvB activity (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, Figure 3E and Supplementary Figure S2A; 222 genes were downregulated, and 75 genes were upregulated). Out of these 297 genes, 23 were previously reported as direct GcvB targets (Supplementary Tables S1, S4 and S5), a statistically significant enrichment by hypergeometric test (P ≤ 1.4e-14, where N = 4454, m = 297, l = 51, k = 23). This fraction of targets affected by GcvB in a rne wild type strain (23 out of a total of 51 GcvB targets, Supplementary Table S1) is consistent with previous RNA-seq large-scale studies (11). In contrast, in strains where RNase E is inactive, only 20 genes in total showed a statistically significant change in transcript level between gcvB+ and gcvB−, out of which only one (glpC) is a previously reported target gene of GcvB (Supplementary Tables S1, S4 and S5, Figure 3F and Supplementary Figure S2B). In the case of glpC, it is possible that in the absence of RNase E, GcvB induces a structural change that exposes the glpC mRNA to another endoribonuclease, causing its destabilization. Overall, our results imply that most of the effects of GcvB on gene transcript levels are associated with RNase E cleavage, suggesting functional interaction between RNase E and GcvB in regulation of gene expression.

Taking advantage of the double mutant cycle design, we inferred for each gene the relative contribution of GcvB, RNase E and the functional interaction between them to the change in transcript level by fitting a linear model to the log2 fold change values of the various gene expression comparisons (Figure 2B, Materials and methods). Two hundred seventy-six genes had a statistically significant functional interaction term (Padj ≤ 0.01), out of which 21 genes are previously documented GcvB targets (Supplementary Tables S1, S4 and S5), an enrichment that is highly statistically significant by hypergeometric test (P ≤ 4.3e-13; where N = 4454, m = 276, l = 51, k = 21). Also, 16 additional genes with statistically significant functional interaction terms reside in operons with annotated GcvB targets (Supplementary Table S5), an enrichment that is also highly statistically significant by hypergeometric test (P ≤ 2.6e-5, where N = 4454, m = 276, l = 81, k = 16; 81 is the number of genes in operons that include GcvB targets). Additional six genes that showed statistically significant functional interaction terms are indirect targets of GcvB, as they are known targets of the Lrp transcription factor (44,53), encoded by the lrp gene, which is a known GcvB target and by itself also obtained a statistically significant functional interaction term (Supplementary Table S5). The other 232 genes that had statistically significant functional interaction terms could potentially be either yet unknown direct targets of GcvB or genes affected by the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E in an indirect manner. Finally, out of the 297 genes that were found to be affected by GcvB at their transcript levels (Padj ≤ 0.01 in the gcvB+/rnewt versus gcvB−/rnewt comparison), 186 genes (63%) obtained statistically significant functional interaction terms, an enrichment that is highly statistically significant by hypergeometric test (P ≤ 1.5e-177, where N = 4454, m = 276, l = 297, k = 186; Supplementary Figure S2C). Among the 23 GcvB targets that were found to have a statistically significant change in their transcript level in the gcvB+/rnewt versus gcvB−/rnewt comparison (Padj≤ 0.01), 19 were found to have a statistically significant GcvB-RNase E functional interaction component. This enrichment is also highly statistically significant by a hypergeometric test (P ≤ 2.97e-8, where N = 51, m = 23, l = 21, k = 19; Supplementary Figure S2D). It is of note that the fraction 19/23 (0.83) of targets with statistically significant GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term, is statistically significantly higher than the overall fraction of genes with statistically significant GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term, which was 186/297 (0.63) (P ≤ 0.05 by a binomial test). The determination of a substantial group of GcvB targets with statistically significant functional interaction terms further supports the conjecture that the effect of GcvB on the transcript levels of its target genes is mostly mediated by RNase E and that GcvB has an effect on RNase E cleavage.

Possible modes of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction in regulating gene transcript levels

To further understand the different co-regulation modes in which GcvB and RNase E control gene expression, we turned to divide the set of 276 genes that obtained a statistically significant GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term (Figure 3G) by the relative contributions of GcvB, RNase E and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction to the gene expression change. Examination of these contributions revealed a negligible independent contribution of GcvB, which was not statistically significant for most genes (Figure 4A). Therefore, we divided the genes to four clusters according to the contribution of RNase E and of the functional interaction component to the change in gene expression (in log2 fold change values, see Materials and methods and Figure 4A): (I) Contribution of RNase E < 0 and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term < 0. In cluster I, RNase E decreases gene transcript levels, as is implied by the negative RNase E term, and the effect of GcvB on RNase E cleavage further enhances the decrease of gene transcript level, as is implied by the negative functional interaction term. Notably, for a subset of genes in this cluster, GcvB is required for the decrease in transcript level by RNase E cleavage (substantial negative functional interaction term), and RNase E by itself has hardly any effect (small negative value of RNase E). This is clearly seen, for example, for lrp and was further confirmed by northern analysis (Figure 4B cluster I and Supplementary Figure S3). (II) Contribution of RNase E > 0 and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term > 0 (Figure 4A). In cluster II, RNase E independently of GcvB increases the gene transcript levels (positive RNase E term). GcvB, by affecting RNase E cleavage, further enhances the increase exerted by RNase E (positive functional interaction term). (III) Contribution of RNase E > 0 and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term < 0. (IV) Contribution of RNase E < 0 and GcvB-RNase E functional interaction term > 0. In clusters III and IV (Figure 4A), GcvB-RNase E functional interaction and RNase E affect gene expression in opposite directions. In cluster III, RNase E by itself increases the gene transcript level but the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E leads to a decrease in transcript level (Figure 4B, cluster III), while in cluster IV RNase E decreases the transcript level and the functional interaction with GcvB increases it (Figure 4B, cluster IV). There are several putative scenarios that can explain such observations. For example, a scenario relevant to cluster III is that cleavage at certain sites by RNase E generates stable fragments, but when GcvB is expressed it can expose other RNase E cleavage sites, which may lead to a decrease in gene expression. The simplest scenario for cluster IV is that GcvB binding masks an RNase E binding site, partially making it inaccessible to RNase E (and thus the functional interaction term is positive). It is of note that the above regulatory modes are plausible for genes that are known GcvB targets. For other genes, the effects of GcvB-RNase E interaction are more speculative and may be indirect.

In-vivo transcriptome-wide mapping of RNase E cleavage sites

To further assess the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E, we turned to map RNase E cleavage sites at a transcriptome-wide scale. We carried out TIER-seq (Transiently Inactivating an Endoribonuclease followed by RNA-seq) (36) for in-vivo transcriptome-wide mapping of RNase E cleavage sites (Supplementary Figure S4A, Materials and methods). In brief, TIER-seq utilizes the generation of RNA fragments with 5′P ends by RNase E cleavage. It captures by specific ligation 5′P transcripts, which are then sequenced and mapped to the genome. The 5′P positions correspond to the positions where read starts are mapped and can thus be identified. TIER-seq is applied to the wild type strain (rnewt) and the rne mutant strain (rnets), both transiently grown at 44°C. Differential RNA-seq analysis between libraries of the two strains, focused at the positions with mapped read starts of the related 5′P fragments, can identify putative RNase E cleavage sites. Such experiments were previously conducted in E. coli using one library for each strain (46), and in Salmonella enterica using two libraries for each strain (36), revealing comprehensive cleavage maps. Here, we applied this methodology to map RNase E cleavage sites in both gcvB+ and gcvB− strains, attempting to unravel the effect of GcvB on RNase E cleavage.

To construct the TIER-seq libraries, we used the same RNA samples used for RNA-seq (gcvB+/rnewt, gcvB+/rnets, gcvB−/rnewt, gcvB−/rnets) grown to exponential phase (OD600 of 0.3) at 30ºC and subjected to transient inactivation at 44ºC. Following library preparation and sequencing (three libraries per strain), we conducted 5′P mapping and compared the results of rnewt and rnets strains grown at 44ºC by DESeq2 to determine RNase E cleavage sites (Supplementary Figure S4A, Materials and methods). A position with statistically significant higher read start count in rnewt compared to rnets was considered a putative RNase E cleavage site. Of note, comparison of 5′P mapping results between gcvB+/rnewt and gcvB+/rnets determines the RNase E cleavage sites in a strain that resembles the wild type strain, providing an additional comprehensive RNase E cleavage map in E. coli that can accompany and complement previously published data (36,46). As a control we also generated 5′P libraries where all four strains were grown at 30°C. Since in all the strains grown at 30°C RNase E is presumably functional, we do not expect the DESeq2 analysis to identify multiple positions which differ statistically significantly in their read start counts in the comparison of rnewt and rnets strains. Notably, the ability to detect RNase E cleavage sites by TIER-seq depends on the stability of the 5′P fragments generated by the cleavage. Cleavage sites generating stable 5′P fragments can be easily detected by the TIER-seq protocol. However, cleavage sites that produce unstable 5′P fragments, which may have been degraded, can be missed in the sequencing and in the following analysis.

Aligning all reads per library to the E. coli genome, we mapped an average ∼13 000 000 reads per library (Supplementary Table S3). Comparative analysis of read start counts between the rnewt and the rnets strains of bacteria grown at 30°C and carrying a GcvB inducible plasmid, has revealed, as expected, only a few positions that differed statistically significantly in their read start counts (Figure 5A, left panel). Most positions with high read start counts observed in the wild type strain grown at 30°C showed also high read start counts in the mutant strain grown at 30°C, confirming once again that the mutant RNase E is fully functional at the permissive temperature. Similar results were obtained in the comparison made between the rnewt and the rnets strains harboring the control plasmid and grown at 30°C (Supplementary Figure S4B, top panel). In contrast, the comparison of read start counts between the rnewt and the rnets strains grown at 44°C has revealed multiple positions with statistically significant differences in the read start counts (Figure 5A, right panel and Supplementary Figure S4B, bottom panel). Hereinafter, we focus on positions with statistically significantly higher read start counts in the rnewt strain compared to the rnets strain grown at 44ºC, inferred as putative direct RNase E cleavage sites. Setting thresholds of average read start counts ≥ 50 for libraries of the rnewt strains grown at 44°C (with or without GcvB), log2 fold change of read start counts between rnewt and rnets ≥ 1 and Padj ≤ 0.01, we detected a total of 7490 putative RNase E cleavage positions in the E. coli transcriptome (Supplementary Table S6). This catalogue of RNase E cleavage sites is supported by several lines of evidence: (i) consistently with previous studies (4,5), we found that the cleavage sites reside in A/U-rich regions (Supplementary Figure S5A). Furthermore, a motif that is very similar to the previously identified weak sequence motif around the cleavage site (36,54,55) has been identified in our data as well, i.e. RN|WU, where R is G/A, N is any nucleotide, | is the cleavage site, and W is A/U (Supplementary Figure S5B). (ii) We conducted a literature search and compiled a list of 28 genes with previously determined RNase E cleavage sites in individual studies. In 17 out of these 28 genes cleavage sites were re-identified in our study (Supplementary Table S7). These include, for example, aceK (generating the sRNA AceK-int (56–58)), rpsO (59) and glmU (60) RNAs in E. coli, and ArcZ and flgL RNAs in Salmonella enterica (36) (Figure 5B, Supplementary Table S7). Other RNase E cleavage sites were detected in tRNA, rRNA and sRNA molecules, already known to be processed by RNase E (e.g. (36,61–63)) (Supplementary Table S7). Notably, some of the cleavage sites missed by TIER-seq were discovered in the original studies only in vitro or under specific conditions. (iii) There is high agreement between our results and results of previous transcriptome-wide mapping studies of RNase E cleavage sites (36,45,46) (Figure 5C).

The 7490 identified RNase E cleavage sites reside in 1239 transcripts. About 64% of the cleavage sites were identified in mRNAs, primarily in the coding sequence (CDS) (Figure 5D), emphasizing the role of RNase E in mRNA degradation in the cell. Altogether, we detected a total of 1125 mRNAs cleaved by RNase E (25% of 4390 E. coli mRNAs), showing variation in the number of cleavage sites per transcript, from one cleavage site up to 22 cleavage sites per transcript (Supplementary Figure S5C). Finally, 25% of successive cleavage sites were found to be in close proximity, mostly less than five nucleotides apart (Supplementary Figure S5D).

Assessing GcvB effect on the cleavage of targets

While it looked plausible that by applying the double mutant cycle to the read start counts, we could get a quantitative assessment of the effect of GcvB at the different cleavage positions, such an analysis did not yield consistent results due to the noisiness of this type of data. Therefore, we turned to simply conduct comparative analysis between the cleavage maps of a strain expressing GcvB and a strain carrying the control plasmid. Comparative analysis of gcvB−/rnewt and gcvB−/rnets provides the RNase E cleavage map in a strain lacking GcvB. Comparative analysis of gcvB+/rnewt and gcvB+/rnets provides the RNase E cleavage map in a strain expressing GcvB. Possible changes between these two cleavage maps should provide clues to RNase E cleavage sites that are affected by GcvB activity. Examination of the cleavage maps of gcvB+ and gcvB− strains has revealed 1100 cleaved transcripts identified in both gcvB+ and gcvB− strains, whereas 53 and 86 cleaved transcripts were identified only in gcvB+ or gcvB−, respectively. This implies that, as expected, the cleavage of most genes is unaffected by GcvB. Still, some genes that were found to be cleaved in both gcvB+ and gcvB− strains show differences in the magnitude of cleavage (differences between the TIER-seq log2 fold change values of gcvB+/rnewt versus gcvB+/rnets and the log2 fold change values of gcvB−/rnewt versus gcvB−/rnets), suggesting possible positive or negative effects of GcvB on the cleavage (Supplementary Table S6). Among these are GcvB targets that obtained a statistically significant functional interaction term in the double mutant cycle analysis of the total RNA-seq data described above, indicating co-regulation by GcvB and RNase E and implying possible (indirect or direct) effects of GcvB on RNase E cleavage. In addition, comparative analysis of read start counts of gcvB+/rnewt versus gcvB−/rnewt and of gcvB+/rnets versus gcvB−/rnets grown at 44°C further supports the impact of GcvB on RNase E cleavage (Supplementary Figure S6). Given that most GcvB binding sites are at the 5′ UTR of the targets (Supplementary Table S8), a translation-dependent effect of GcvB on RNase E cleavage seems a plausible mechanism for most of the targets. Still, a mechanism by which GcvB affects the cleavage in a translation-independent manner, as observed, for example, for RyhB–sodB (16) is possible.

Examples of genes showing different modes of GcvB-RNase E functional interaction

Examples representing cluster I

The GcvB target sstT encodes a serine/threonine symporter. It was first shown to be regulated by GcvB in a study of Pulvermacher et al. (24), and was identified in RIL-seq experiments as one of the most dominant targets of GcvB (25,64). In strains harboring the control plasmid there is some downregulation of sstT by RNase E itself, based on the comparison of read coverage between gcvB−/rnewt and gcvB−/rnets strains at 44°C (log2 fold change of –0.66). However, this downregulation is substantially enhanced by GcvB, as implied by the comparison of read coverage between gcvB+/rnewt and gcvB+/rnets strains (log2 fold change of –1.86) (Figure 6A). This enhancement in downregulation is due to the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E, contributing log2 fold change of –1.2 to the decrease in sstT transcript level (Figure 4B, Supplementary Table S5). We further confirmed the impact of GcvB on RNase E cleavage by northern blot analysis (Figure 6B), clearly showing that in a rnewt strain expressing GcvB, the downregulation is enhanced. The TIER-seq analysis revealed three RNase E cleavage sites within the coding sequence of sstT, while the GcvB binding site on sstT mRNA is at the 5′UTR (Figure 6C, Supplementary Table S8). This might imply that GcvB binding makes the mRNA more accessible to RNase E, possibly either by inhibiting ribosome binding and/or by inducing a structural change that unmasks an occluded RNase E binding site.

The GcvB target gltI encodes the periplasmic binding component of the glutamate/aspartate ABC transporter. It was previously shown in Salmonella that gltI is downregulated by GcvB’s binding to a CA-rich region in its 5′ UTR through GcvB’s R1 binding site, and that GcvB inhibits gltI’s translation by occluding a translation enhancer overlapping its binding site and reducing the binding of 30S ribosomal subunit (10,28). The results of the double mutant cycle analysis showed that the decrease in gltI level due to RNase E cleavage was observed only in the gcvB+ strains, emphasizing the importance of the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction for RNase E cleavage of this transcript (Figures 4B and 7A–B, Supplementary Table S5). These results were further confirmed by northern blot analysis (Figure 7C). Consistent with the double mutant cycle results and northern analysis, the TIER-seq experiment indicated substantial cleavage in the gcvB+/rnewt strain, but not in the gcvB−/rnewt strain (Figure 7D, E). Most pronounced RNase E cleavage sites were revealed in the 3′ UTR of gltI, while the GcvB binding site is at the 5′ UTR (Figure 7E and Supplementary Table S8). However, experiments using compensatory mutations, intended to further support the effect of GcvB binding at the 5′ UTR on gltI transcript level, were inconclusive. Substitution of the gltI initiation codon to AAA, in an attempt to test if the mechanism underlying the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction is associated with translation inhibition, also yielded inconclusive data (Supplementary Figure S7). Finally, there was one cleavage site at the 5′ UTR of gltI, adjacent to GcvB binding site (Figure 7E), which was observed only in gcvB−/rnewt, suggesting that the binding of GcvB possibly prevents this cleavage.

Examples representing cluster II

The sRNA SroC (65) is processed from the 3′ UTR of the above described gltI by RNase E (25,63). It was shown to function as a sponge of GcvB, triggering its degradation mainly in stationary phase (30,63). The double mutant cycle analysis shows that the contribution of the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction component to the increase in SroC transcript level in gcvB+/rnewt compared to gcvB−/rnets is substantial (Figures 4B and 7A–B). Consistently, as mentioned above, the TIER-seq results suggest that GcvB has a major effect on the cleavage of gltI, as in gcvB−/rnewt the cleavage is much weaker than in the gcvB+/rnewt strain (Figure 7D and E). These results were also confirmed by the northern blot analysis of gltI (Figure 7C). Very intriguingly, the most prominent RNase E cleavage site revealed by TIER-seq in the 3′ UTR of gltI (Figure 7E and Supplementary Table S6), is located exactly where SroC is processed from the gltI transcript. Thus, our results imply that GcvB itself plays an important role in the generation of its own sponge, SroC. Experiments in which GcvB or its R1-mutated version was overexpressed suggested that the R1 site of GcvB is important for the effect on SroC level, independent of the ability of GcvB to base pair with gltI at the known binding site (Supplementary Figure S7A, B). Using a non-translatable gltI mutant, we showed that the effect of GcvB on SroC generation was only partially dependent on translation inhibition (Supplementary Figure S7C).

Our results complement those of Miyakoshi et al. (63), who focused mainly on the effect of SroC on GcvB, while here, by the double mutant cycle analysis and TIER-seq mapping, we could appreciate the effect of GcvB on the generation of SroC. Also, since our experiments were done with E. coli cells grown to exponential phase, our results suggest that SroC starts being generated in exponential phase. It reaches higher levels in stationary phase, as shown by Lalaouna et al. (30). It is quite fascinating that GcvB, while regulating its targets directly, also fine tunes their expression indirectly by playing a role in generating its sponge SroC, which maintains the level of GcvB as needed.

An example of an indirect target of GcvB in cluster II is gltW, a target of Lrp (66) that is increased in its transcript level by RNase E-GcvB functional interaction (Figure 4B, cluster II). The functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E decreases lrp expression level, and as a result, the transcript level of gltW, which is transcriptionally repressed by Lrp, increases.

An example representing Cluster IV

The GcvB target ivbL (25,64) is a leader peptide that controls the expression of the ivbL-ilvBN operon by attenuation (67). The double mutant cycle analysis revealed a notable downregulation of ivbL by RNase E itself in strains carrying the control plasmid, as indicated by the reduction in read coverage between gcvB−/rnewt and gcvB−/rnets strains at 44°C (with a log2 fold change of -1.56). GcvB appears to counteract this downregulation, at least partially (Supplementary Table S5). This conjecture was also confirmed by the comparison of read coverage between gcvB+/rnewt and gcvB−/rnewt strains (with a log2 fold change of 1.22) (Supplementary Table S5). Thus, the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction is substantially reducing the cleavage and leads to an increase in ivbL’s transcript level (log2 fold change of 1.6, Supplementary Table S5, Figure 4B cluster IV). These results were supported by northern blot analysis (Supplementary Figure S8). Since TIER-seq identified the RNase E cleavage sites in the intergenic region between ivbL and ilvB (Supplementary Table S6), and the binding site of GcvB is predicted to be located proximal to the AUG start codon in the 5′UTR (Supplementary Tables S8), it is possible that GcvB binding opens a secondary structure, uncovering the ribosome binding site and allowing translation, thus protecting ivbL from RNase E cleavage. This mechanism needs further corroboration.

Gene transcripts stabilized by RNase E cleavage

While not the main focus of our study, we also examined globally the genes that exhibited statistically significant increase in their RNA levels as a result of RNase E activity, amounting to ∼1500 genes. It is possible that many of these genes have been influenced indirectly by RNase E, as mentioned above for the targets of Lrp, a transcriptional dual regulator that regulates many genes (44) (Supplementary Table S5) while being regulated by GcvB at the transcript level. Indeed, out of 209 known transcription factors (44), 160 showed statistically significant changes in their expression level upon RNase E inactivation. Among the 1596 genes exhibiting an elevation in gene expression in response to RNase E activity, 832 genes were identified as targets of affected TFs (many of which are dual regulators), suggesting that alterations in transcription factor activity could account for the observed changes in expression levels of multiple genes.

In addition to the possible indirect effects, ∼500 genes showing increase in transcript level in the comparison between rnewt and rnets were found in the TIER-seq experiment to contain RNase E cleavage sites (in the 5′UTR, and/or CDS and/or 3′UTR). We explored the possibility that the alluded upregulation of some genes in this group could be attributed to the generation of stable RNA regions formed by RNase E cleavage, as shown for SroC. To investigate this suggestion, we developed a ‘stabilization score’, based on positional read coverage derived from our total expression RNA-seq data (Materials and methods). Using this score, we identified 66 regions meeting this criterion, located adjacent to RNase E cleavage sites (Supplementary Table S9). Among our findings, we confirmed that RNase E is responsible for the processing of four previously identified sRNA genes, namely ftsO, aceK-int, rbsZ and xylZ (56,58,64) (listed in Supplementary Tables S6 and S9). In Supplementary Figure S9 we show the cleavage pattern obtained by TIER-seq for ftsO and its further validation by northern blot analysis. Our results further support the role of RNase E in processing of stable short fragments from their host mRNA.

Assessing the potential impact of other sRNAs on RNase E cleavage

It is conceivable that other sRNAs may impact RNase E-mediated RNA cleavage. To assess the feasibility of this conjecture we examined if RNase E cleavage sites are mapped to targets of other sRNAs (Supplementary Table S10). The list of sRNA targets was based on Melamed et al. (25). The analysis revealed that more than half of the targets of half of the sRNAs contain at least one RNase E cleavage site. Out of 1564 genes with RNase E cleavage sites and 1339 genes that are known targets of sRNAs, 529 are predicted to be regulated by both RNase E and at least one sRNA, an enrichment that is highly statistically significant (P ≤ 3.5e-5 by hypergeometric test, where N = 4454, m = 1564, l = 1339, k = 529). Hence, it is conceivable that our conclusions on the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E in control of gene expression may be applicable to other sRNAs as well. It will be interesting to see if the enhancement of RNase E cleavage by sRNA binding, revealed as dominant for GcvB, will be found to be dominant for the other sRNAs, especially those with many binding sites within the CDS.

Discussion

Bacteria exploit various mechanisms to regulate gene expression levels, including transcription regulation, RNA degradation, and translation control. While transcriptional regulation has traditionally been considered the primary level of regulation, accumulating evidence indicates that posttranscriptional regulation plays a role in determining transcript levels. One significant group of posttranscriptional regulators are sRNAs, which modulate gene expression by influencing translation initiation and RNA stability. While it has been widely acknowledged that sRNAs affect the target RNA stability through regulation of translation initiation, and there have been a few reports showing translation-independent effects of sRNAs on the cleavage by endoribonucleases (e.g. (30,68)), these mechanisms have not been assessed comprehensively at a transcriptome-wide scale. In this study, we employed a special design of RNA-seq experiments along with global mapping of RNase E cleavage sites to expand our understanding of the interplay between one sRNA, GcvB, and RNase E in gene expression regulation in E. coli. To this end, we measured gene expression by RNA-seq in single and double mutant strains of gcvB and rne, the comparison of which has allowed us to quantitatively estimate the effect of the functional interaction between GcvB and RNase E on gene expression. The rne mutant strain used was a temperature-sensitive mutant strain that allowed temporary inactivation of endogenous RNase E. This approach overcomes the inability to use the lethal rne deletion mutation. The strains that we used were also deleted of gcvB, and gcvB was expressed from a plasmid for a brief period, reducing the potential of secondary effects that may result from prolonged overexpression of the sRNA. Through this experimental setup, we determined the relative contributions of GcvB, RNase E, and their functional interaction in driving changes in expression levels of different genes, demonstrating variability in the magnitude of the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction for different GcvB targets.

RNase E-mediated downregulation of GcvB targets: insights into GcvB-RNase E functional interaction

GcvB has been recognized as a negative regulator of its target genes, downregulating their expression. In this study, we observed minimal changes in the transcript levels of GcvB targets when RNase E was inactive, implying that RNase E plays a major role in the effect GcvB has on gene transcript levels. In parallel, our results indicate that GcvB plays a role in RNase E cleavage magnitude, as for a substantial fraction of GcvB targets there were differences in RNase E cleavage in corresponding positions between strains expressing gcvB and strains harboring the control plasmid. We found that for most affected targets, the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction leads to a decrease in transcript level. These results, based on the double mutant cycle analysis, were supported by our northern blot experiments (Figures 6, 7 and Supplementary Figure S3), as well as by a previous study (30). For certain genes, the degradation of target RNA by RNase E is largely dependent on GcvB binding, while for other targets, GcvB only enhances RNase E activity. Interestingly, for no target, we can explain the increase in gene expression due to the masking of RNase E cleavage site by GcvB binding. Our results indicate that for most GcvB targets, GcvB binds near the ribosome binding site and enhances RNase E cleavage. Two major mechanisms can potentially explain the observed GcvB-RNase E functional interaction: (1) GcvB-dependent blocking of translation initiation, which in turn reduces the ribosome occupancy on the transcript, exposing it to cleavage by RNase E. (2) GcvB-induced structural change that exposes an otherwise occluded RNase E binding site. The latter applies not only to mRNAs but to all regulated transcripts. To assess whether GcvB affects the accessibility of RNase E cleavage sites by structural changes, we predicted the structure of the target RNA with and without bound GcvB (Materials and methods). These predictions have not revealed higher accessibility of the cleavage site in the presence of GcvB (data not shown). While additional experiments are needed to further validate our findings, these results suggest that the main mechanism by which GcvB affects RNase E cleavage is by inhibition of translation initiation, which makes the RNA more accessible to RNase E. This is supported by previous studies showing that GcvB inhibits the binding of the 30S ribosomal subunit and initiation of translation (28), as well as by our results showing that the effect of GcvB on gltI’s transcript level is associated with translation inhibition (Supplementary Figure S7B). Future investigations of the functional relationship between RNase E and other sRNAs, in particular sRNAs with reported binding sites within their CDS, are expected to shed more light and further expand our understanding of the mechanisms underlying sRNA-RNase E functional interaction.

TIER-seq results: insights and caveats

To further support the conclusions of the RNA-seq analysis, we conducted TIER-seq for mapping RNase E cleavage sites at a transcriptome-wide scale. Our results demonstrate that RNase E has a substantial impact on gene expression, affecting nearly half of the genes encoded in E. coli genome either directly or indirectly, independent of the GcvB sRNA. This is expected, as RNase E is part of the degradosome and plays a major role in degradation of RNA in the cell (1–3). One surprising observation was the increase in transcript levels of many genes in the RNase E wild type strain (rnewt) compared to the mutant strain (rnets). While the majority of these results can be explained by secondary effects, where RNase E directly regulates gene regulators of the genes showing an increase in transcript levels, we identified a subset of ∼60 genomic regions where RNase E seems to directly lead to upregulation of the RNA levels. In these cases, we observed at least one RNase E cleavage site that has led to processing of transcripts of protein-encoding genes, followed by the generation and accumulation of stable short RNA transcripts (Figure 7 and Supplementary Figure S9, Supplementary Table S9). Our findings align with the growing understanding of the role of RNase E in processing and generation of functional short RNAs, as highlighted previously (19,36). Still, it should be kept in mind that the cleavage positions are determined as those positions where in the TIER-seq experiment there are statistically significantly more mapped read starts in the libraries of the rnewt strain than in the rnets strain. Some of these positions may be falsely interpreted as RNase E cleavage sites in cases of genes whose transcription is upregulated in the rnewt strain, while their transcripts are cleaved by other RNases. It might be possible to distinguish between these possibilities by disentangling the effects of transcription and degradation on the transcript levels (e.g. (69)). Also, there are positions where we observed higher read start counts in the rnets strain compared to the rnewt strain, possibly implying putative cleavage by other endoribonucleases, such as RNase G (70,71), at positions that became accessible in the mutant strain.

Integrating the TIER-seq results with information about GcvB binding sites on the targets, we observe that in many cases GcvB binding site is located at the 5′ UTR while TIER-seq reports the cleavage site downstream, often at a distance of many nucleotides. As mentioned above, the effect of GcvB binding on RNase E cleavage sites that are remote from its binding site might occur due to the effect of the sRNA binding on translation or on transcript structure, which alter the accessibility of the transcript to RNase E at a distant site. However, since the identification of RNase E cleavage sites by TIER-seq relies on the stability of the cleavage products, it is also possible that there was an initial cleavage nearby the GcvB binding site, directed by GcvB binding, which led to downstream cleavage events, the products of which are more stable than those of the initial cleavage and therefore are detected in the TIER-seq experiment. Therefore, concluding from the TIER-seq results about the mechanism by which GcvB affects RNase E cleavage should be done with caution and accompanied by additional data, as we demonstrated for gltI (Supplementary Figure S7B).

In conclusion, we applied a systematic approach that has allowed us to study the interplay between GcvB and RNase E in regulation of gene expression at a transcriptome-wide scale. Indeed, we found that (except for one target) RNase E is required for GcvB to have an effect on the target transcript level, and that GcvB enhances the cleavage by RNase E for a statistically significant fraction of its targets, mostly destabilizing the target transcripts. Using the double mutant cycle approach, we were able to quantitatively derive the GcvB-RNase E functional interaction values. We observed that it varies in its magnitude between different targets, and that there are targets that are hardly cleaved by RNase E without the effect of GcvB. It is of note that the double mutant cycle approach can be applied to study quantitatively the functional interaction of a sRNA with any other endoribonuclease (72) or any other regulator, including various transcription factors (73) and the transcription termination factor Rho (74). Finally, our study provides rich resources of data, of gene expression patterns as well as of RNase E cleavage maps in various genomic contexts of E. coli, holding useful biological information still to be revealed.
