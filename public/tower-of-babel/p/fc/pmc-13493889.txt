Intermetallic Pt3In concave tetrahedra for oxygen reduction electrocatalysis in proton exchange membrane fuel cells
Authors: Wenhe Yu, Menggang Li, Lu Li, Xiaowen Wu, Wenqian Zhu, Zehong Yin, Hongyu Guo, Nan-Nan Liang, Quanquan Pang, Biao Li, Xin Gao, Jing Xia, Jin-Song Hu, Mingchuan Luo, Shaojun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scaling proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) demands efficient and durable electrocatalysts for the cathodic oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). Concave surfaces and intermetallic phases have been proven to promote the activity and stability of Pt-based catalysts, yet integrating these two structural features in one catalyst remains challenging. Herein, we achieve this in a class of intermetallic, concave tetrahedral Pt3In (i-ct-Pt3In), through a sequential Pt(111)-selective wet-chemical etching and indium-enabled morphology-preservable annealing. The i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst delivers a mass activity of 2.49 A mgPt−1 in acidic media, preserving 97.8% of its activity after 30,000 cycles. In phosphate-containing electrolytes, it achieves a mass activity of 0.3 A mgPt−1, representing a 7.5-fold improvement over commercial Pt/C and translating to a peak power density of 1.0 W cm−2 in a high-temperature PEMFC at 160 °C. Theoretical calculations verify weakened adsorption of oxygenates and phosphate anions on concave Pt3In(111) relative to flat Pt(111), accounting for enhanced ORR kinetics and phosphate tolerance. This work highlights the potential of ordering morphological nanocrystals for energy electrocatalysis.

Introduction

Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are appealing devices for directly converting the chemical energy of fuels into electricity and are meanwhile attractive power sources for a wide range of applications1–3. However, the stringent requirement of low-temperature PEMFCs (LT-PEMFCs) for high-purity hydrogen severely limits the flexibility of hydrogen sources4,5. In contrast, high-temperature PEMFCs (HT-PEMFCs) can directly utilize impure hydrogen derived from industrial byproducts, methanol, or ammonia reforming, thereby reducing the dependence on high-purity hydrogen and improving fuel adaptability6,7. Nevertheless, the performance advancement of HT-PEMFCs has been persistently hindered by a fundamental challenge: phosphoric acid, as an indispensable proton carrier in the system, exhibits strong specific adsorption on the Pt surface of the cathode, leading to severe activity degradation and a high demand for Pt loading8–10.

Current strategies to alleviate phosphate poisoning primarily focus on regulating the electronic structure of Pt atoms and tailoring the chemical microenvironment at the catalyst-electrolyte interface11,12. Nevertheless, such microenvironmental modulation is often insufficient under complex operating conditions, prompting the exploration of alloying or dealloying strategies that can intrinsically tune the Pt electronic structure13. Unfortunately, the catalytic performance of these alloys is highly dependent on the modulation capability of the secondary metal, while their structural stability under harsh conditions remains poor due to the dissolution or migration of active sites14–16. As a result, achieving both high activity and long-term durability for interfacial Pt catalysis under phosphate-dominated conditions remains an unsolved issue.

A demonstrated strategy to stabilize alloy catalysts is to arrange different types of atoms in an ordered manner to form an intermetallic phase17,18. Meanwhile, the introduction of concave surfaces with high generalized coordination numbers can further optimize the surface electronic states, thereby enhancing ORR activity19,20. In this context, it is appealing to expose intermetallic Pt-based nanocrystals with concave surfaces, which simultaneously promote activity and stability21,22. However, realizing this is experimentally challenging because thermal annealing is generally required to overcome atomic diffusion barriers to yield intermetallics, but high-temperature treatment inevitably flattens a concave surface with high surface energy23.

Here, aiming to integrate a concave surface and intermetallic phase, we alloyed Pt with low-melting-point In to enable the conversion of the solid-solution, concave tetrahedral Pt3In into an intermetallic phase at such a low temperature that the tailored morphology could be well preserved. X-ray diffraction, atomic-resolution transmission electron microscopy, and X-ray absorption spectra collectively verified the intermetallic morphology. Applied for ORR electrocatalysis, the intermetallic, concave tetrahedral Pt3In (i-ct-Pt3In) showed considerably enhanced activity and stability in half-cell and PEMFC tests. Density functional theory (DFT) revealed that the i-ct-Pt3In weakens oxygen and phosphate adsorption relative to Pt, consistent with operando X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) showing suppressed Pt oxidation during potential cycling. A weakened adsorption of phosphate anions was further found on i-ct-Pt3In relative to the pure Pt catalyst, which motivated us to explore its application for a phosphoric acid-based HT-PEMFC. These synergistic effects lead to markedly improved tolerance to phosphate poisoning, with i-ct-Pt3In/C delivering 0.3 A mgPt−1 and 0.63 mA cm−2 in phosphate-containing electrolyte, 7.5- and 10.5-fold improvements over commercial Pt/C, respectively. In an HT-PEMFC, the i-ct-Pt3In/C delivered a peak power density of 1.0 W cm−2 at 160 °C under H2-O2 conditions, 1.67 times that of the Pt/C benchmark.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structural characterizations

Aiming to balance catalytic activity and stability, we rationally devised an engineering route (Fig. 1a), in which pure concave Pt (Ⅰ) was first alloyed with a secondary metal (PtM) (Ⅱ)24,25, followed by the formation of ordered intermetallic PtM phases (Ⅲ) to improve structural stability26. Experimentally, the i-ct-Pt3In catalyst was synthesized via a sequential wet-chemical approach, followed by moderate-temperature annealing (Fig. 1b). The solid-solution, concave tetrahedral Pt3In (ct-Pt3In) nanocrystals were synthesized using platinum acetylacetonate (Pt(acac)2) and indium (III) acetylacetonate (In(acac)3) as precursor salts, hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (CTAC) as the tetrahedron-directing agent, ferric (III) acetylacetonate (Fe(acac)3) as the facet-selective etching agent, glucose as a moderate reductant, and oleylamine (OAm) as the solvent. The role of each chemical in the morphology-controlled synthesis was discussed in detail in the Supplementary notes (SN 1 and SN 2) and Figures (Figs. S1–S5). A typical high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image of i-ct-Pt3In is shown in Fig. 1c, confirming a highly concave surface after thermal annealing. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. S6) confirms a face-centered cubic (fcc) phase. Figures 1d and S7 show the aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) along [110], where periodically alternating bright and dim dots correspond to Pt and In atoms, respectively, suggesting the formation of an ordered intermetallic structure. The atomic model of the i-ct-Pt3In along this zone axis is displayed in Fig. S8, matching the electron microscope result. Since In has an atomic radius greater than Pt, the d-spacing of the (111) plane increases from 0.226 to 0.235 nm, and that of the (002) plane expands from 0.196 to 0.208 nm. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern of i-ct-Pt3In along the [110] zone axis is shown in Fig. S9, indicating the alternating stacking of Pt and In atoms in i-ct-Pt3In/C. Additionally, the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping of a typical i-ct-Pt3In particle, presented in Fig. 1e, shows that Pt and In are uniformly distributed throughout the concave tetrahedron. The atomic percentages were measured to be 22% for In and 78% for Pt, matching the theoretical composition of an ideal L12 intermetallic compound. This result is also in agreement with the result determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES).

To further evaluate the chemical states and coordination environments of the i-ct-Pt3In intermetallics, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) were conducted. The Pt 4f XPS spectra of commercial Pt/C and i‑ct‑Pt3In (Fig. 2b) exhibit Pt 4f7/2 binding energies at around 71 eV, indicating that Pt in both samples predominantly exists in the metallic state. Consistently, the XPS spectrum of In 3d for i‑ct‑Pt3In displays In0 signals at 443.8 eV (Fig. S10), confirming that metallic In also dominates. Notably, the Pt 4f binding energy of i‑ct‑Pt3In is shifted by 0.5 eV to lower values compared with that of Pt/C, implying electron transfer from In to Pt within the intermetallic structure.

The white line intensities of i-ct-Pt3In/C, ct-Pt3In/C, and Pt foil in Pt L3 X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra show the trend of Pt foil > ct-Pt3In > i-ct-Pt3In, indicating a slightly higher filling of the d-orbital electrons of Pt (Fig. 2c). This trend also agrees with the gradual binding energy shift towards negative values in XPS. The Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) of i-ct-Pt3In/C, ct-Pt3In/C, and Pt foil elucidates the coordination bond of Pt. In FT-EXAFS, the first coordination peak was found to slightly shift from the position of the Pt foil, indicating that Pt has formed a new coordination environment with In, resulting in a change in bond length (Fig. 2d). XAFS analysis reveals two dominant scattering paths at approximately 2.27 and 2.85 Å, corresponding to Pt-In and Pt-Pt coordination, respectively. However, XAFS fitting reveals that the average Pt-In bond length (2.5–2.6 Å) is shorter than the Pt-Pt bond, suggesting strengthened orbital hybridization between Pt and In. While the bulk lattice expands upon In incorporation, surface Pt atoms experience a stronger Pt-In interaction at subsurface sites, leading to locally shorter Pt-In bonds and electron transfer to Pt, as corroborated by the Pt 4f binding energy shift. To visualize the coordination environment, the wavelet transform EXAFS (WT-EXAFS) analysis was adopted (Figs. 2e, f, and S11, S12). The maximum intensity near 8.6 Å−1 corresponds to the Pt-Pt/In coordination path at approximately 2.6 Å in i-ct-Pt3In/C. Moreover, the scattering signal of i-ct-Pt3In is relatively concentrated, indicating an ordered structure.

Half-cell evaluations

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) of the Pt3In catalysts were collected in 0.1 M HClO4, with a commercial Pt/C catalyst (20 wt%) as a benchmark. The i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst possesses a lower electrochemical surface area (ECSA) of 33.6 m2 g−1 compared to that of commercial Pt/C (57.0 m2 g−1) due to large size (Fig. 3a). Pre-conditioning CVs suggest In dissolution during activation, which is in line with the Pourbaix diagram (Fig. S13)27. A noticeable feature of the i-ct-Pt3In/C CV is the shift of the Pt oxidation/reduction redox to a higher potential compared to that of Pt/C, indicating weakened adsorption energies of hydroxyls or oxygenates on the former. This voltammetric observation predicts higher ORR activity, as indicated by established energetic descriptors28,29, which is indeed what we observed experimentally (Fig. 3b). The half-wave potential (E1/2) of i-ct-Pt3In/C is measured to be 0.919 V, 73 mV higher than that of commercial Pt/C. Anion adsorption is also important for practical electrocatalysis, as exemplified by the phosphoric acid-based HT-PEMFCs30. A major challenge for HT-PEMFCs is the strong adsorption of phosphate anions from the electrolyte on Pt(111), which severely inhibits ORR. We then postulate that our concave surface might mitigate the phosphate adsorption and enable a higher ORR activity in phosphate-containing electrolytes. To this end, we recorded CVs of i-ct-Pt3In/C in 0.05 M H3PO4, and found minimal adsorption of phosphate in the double-layer region (Fig. 3c). In contrast, Pt/C shows a notable phosphate adsorption between 0.4 and 0.6 V31,32. As a result of this strong phosphate adsorption, the oxidation peak is markedly suppressed.

This weakened adsorption of phosphate renders the i-ct-Pt3In/C a higher half-wave potential (E1/2) than commercial Pt/C in the HClO4/H3PO4 mixed electrolyte (Fig. 3d). Comparing the E1/2 attenuation in the solution containing 0.1 M H3PO4, the Pt/C shows a decrease in E1/2 of 96 mV, while the i-ct-Pt3In/C shows a decrease in E1/2 of only 69 mV. The i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst exhibits a 7.5-fold increase in MA (0.3 A mg−1) and a 10.5-fold improvement in SA (0.63 mA cm−2) over commercial Pt/C at 0.9 VRHE. In addition, ORR measurements in pure 0.05 M H3PO4 showed a similar trend (Fig. S18), further confirming the superior phosphate tolerance of i-ct-Pt3In/C relative to commercial Pt/C. This smaller activity decay under identical phosphate concentrations directly indicates that phosphate adsorption is suppressed, thereby shielding active sites from H3PO4 poisoning. This phosphate tolerance observed in the RDE measurements also suggests its potential to improve HT-PEMFC performances, where the catalyst operates in a phosphoric-acid-rich environment.

The energy difference between the ordered and disordered configurations of Pt3In is calculated to be −19.88 kJ mol−1, providing a thermodynamic basis for atomic ordering (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 1). Operando XAFS was employed to track the potential-dependent evolution of Pt valence over the ORR-relevant region (0.6, 0.85, and 1.2 V vs. RHE) and at OCP for i-ct-Pt3In/C and Pt/C (Figs. 4c and S21). The i-ct-Pt3In/C exhibits a slight increase in the white-line intensity (Δμnorm) over the entire potential range and nearly recovers to its OCP state, indicating suppressed Pt oxidation (Fig. 4d). In contrast, commercial Pt/C shows a pronounced increase in white-line intensity under ORR potentials.

DFT calculations were further performed to elucidate the origin of enhanced ORR activity and stability. Figure 4e shows the ORR energetics on different catalysts. At 1.23 V, on Pt(111), the potential-determining step (PDS) is demonstrated to be the third reaction step from *O to *OH with an energy barrier of 0.78 eV. On the ct-Pt(111), this PDS is calculated to be the first reaction step from O2 to *OOH with the free energy uphill of 0.50 eV. More importantly, i-ct-Pt3In(111) further reduces the maximum free energy uphill to 0.40 eV, with the PDS shifted to the final *OH to H2O conversion step, which explains the enhanced ORR activity on i-ct-Pt3In/C compared to Pt/C.

To rationalize our experimental trend on phosphate adsorption, we further investigated the adsorption of H2PO4− (PA) on ct-Pt(111) and i-ct-Pt3In(111). The binding energy of H2PO4− on i-ct-Pt3In(111) was calculated to be −3.21 eV, higher than that on ct-Pt(111) (−3.68 eV, Figs. 4f and S22). This result elucidates a weaker adsorption of dihydrogen phosphates on the i-ct-Pt3In(111) surface relative to the pure Pt surface. Geometrically, fewer 3-fold surface sites on our i-ct-Pt3In also account for the weaker adsorption of phosphate anions and oxygen atoms compared to flat Pt(111), as adsorption of phosphate anions and oxygen atoms prefers these sites33. It is worth noting that these DFT calculations are based on simplified models and should be interpreted mainly as qualitative support for the experimental trends.

PEMFC performances

We finally strive to transfer our material design and fundamental understanding into a practical HT-PEMFC. To this end, the MEA was assembled with i-ct-Pt3In/C as the cathode (0.6 mg cm−2), commercial Pt/C (0.3 mg cm−2) as the anode, and a phosphoric acid-doped polybenzimidazole (PBI) membrane as the proton-conducting electrolyte membrane (Fig. S23). As shown in Fig. 5a, the peak power density (PPD) of the i-ct-Pt3In/C reaches 1.0 W cm−2 under the H2/O2 feeds at ambient pressure, markedly outperforming that of Pt/C (0.6 W cm−2) and compares favorably with reported Pt-based catalysts under comparable testing conditions (Table S2). Under the more practical H2/air feeds, i-ct-Pt3In/C delivers a PPD of 585 mW cm−2 at ambient pressure, exceeding that of the Pt/C cathode (382 mW cm−2, Fig. 5b). Aiming to further boost the cell power output, the HT-PEMFC was operated at an elevated backpressure of 150 kPa. This condition enabled a high PPD of 0.74 W cm−2 under H2/air feeding (Fig. S24). Motivated by the results in HT-PEMFCs, we further extended our i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst into the typical Nafion membrane-based PEMFC, with MEA assembling with i-ct-Pt3In/C as the cathode (0.1 mg cm−2) and commercial Pt/C (0.05 mg cm−2) as the anode. At 0.9 V under the H2-O2 feeding, we measured a mass activity of 0.80 A mgPt−1, more than 3 times higher than that of commercial Pt/C (0.24 A mgPt−1), along with enhanced PEMFC performances (Figs. S25 and S26).

In summary, we have devised a synthetic approach that integrates a concave surface with an intermetallic phase in tetrahedral Pt3In nanocrystals (i-ct-Pt3In). The resulting i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst retains 97.8% of its mass activity after 30,000 accelerated potential cycles in 0.1 M HClO4. Moreover, it delivered a mass activity of 0.3 A mgPt−1 at 0.9 V in H3PO4-containing electrolyte, representing a 7.5-fold enhancement over commercial Pt/C. The enhanced half-cell activities of i-ct-Pt3In/C have been well translated into practical fuel cells, delivering a peak power density of 1.0 W cm−2 at 160 °C, exceeding the Pt/C benchmark by 1.67-fold and showing competitive performance among reported Pt-based intermetallic catalysts under comparable conditions. Our findings showcase ordering morphological nanocrystals as a viable route toward high-performance fuel cell electrocatalysts.

Methods

Materials

Platinum(II) acetylacetonate (Pt(acac)2, 97%), indium(III) acetylacetonate (In(acac)3), iron(III) acetylacetonate (Fe(acac)3), and oleylamine (OAm, >70.0%) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Perchloric acid (HClO4, 70.0%) was obtained from Aladdin Reagent Inc. Isopropanol (AR, ≥99.7%), ethanol (AR, ≥99.7%), glucose, and cyclohexane (AR, ≥99.7%) were acquired from Beijing Tongguang Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd. Hexadecyltrimethyl ammonium chloride (CTAC) was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai). The commercial Pt catalysts (Pt/C, 20 wt%, Premetek) were obtained from the Premetek Corp. Vulcan XC-72R was acquired from Cabot Corp. Nafion solution (5 wt%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Gas diffusion layer (280 μm, Toray), Nafion membrane (NR211, 25.4 μm, DuPont), and high-temperature proton exchange membrane (polybenzimidazole, PBIM40, 40 μm) were purchased from Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd. All the chemicals were used as received without further purification. The water (18 MΩ cm−1) used in all experiments was obtained by passing through an ultra-pure purification system.

Synthesis of concave tetrahedral i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst

(a) Synthesis of concaved tetrahedral Pt3In (ct-Pt3In/C) catalyst. Ten milligrams of Pt(acac)2, 5 mg of In(acac)3, 6 mg of Fe(acac)3, 60 mg of glucose, 80 mg of CTAC, and 5 mL of oleylamine were subjected to sonication at 20 °C for 1 h to achieve a homogeneous solution. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to an oil bath and maintained at 230 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature (25 °C), the product was obtained by centrifugation and washed with a mixture of ethanol and cyclohexane (v:v, 1:4) to remove any excess surfactants. Finally, the resulting product was dispersed in cyclohexane for future use.

(b) Following the synthesis of the ct-Pt3In/C catalyst, Vulcan XC-72R carbon was mixed with cyclohexane and subjected to sonication at 20 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, a precise quantity of ct-Pt3In was added to the suspension and sonicated for an additional 3 h to achieve a ct-Pt3In/C catalyst. The product was then washed with ethanol once and dried under vacuum. In this process, the loading of Pt was maintained within the range of 18–20 wt%.

(c) The ct-Pt3In/C underwent annealing in a mixture of 5% H2 and 95% Ar at a temperature of 450 °C for 2 h with a ramp rate of 5 °C min−1 and a gas flow rate of 100 mL min−1 to obtain the i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst. Throughout the entire heating procedure, the ct-Pt3In/C was kept in the center and allowed to cool naturally to 25 °C in the furnace after the heat treatment.

Materials characterizations

TEM was conducted on a HITACHI HT7700 transmission electron microscope at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. HRTEM images were obtained using JEM 2100F instruments operating at 200 kV. HAADF-STEM and EDS were conducted using a JEM-ARM300F TEM instrument operating at 200 kV. PXRD patterns were collected using a Shimadzu XRD-6000 X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα (λ = 1.5406 Å) radiation. The Pt loading of catalysts prior to electrochemical measurement was determined using ICP-MS (Agilent 7700). XPS analyses were conducted using a Thermo Scientific Escalab 250Xi instrument. XAFS spectra were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. A Pt foil was measured in the transmission mode for energy calibration and comparison. Potential-dependent in situ XAFS spectra at the Pt L3-edge were acquired in a homemade electrochemical cell with the catalysts loaded on a 4 cm2 carbon paper in N2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4. A photograph of the operando XAFS electrochemical-cell setup is shown in Fig. S27. The electrodes were first polarized at the set potential for 5 min to reach a steady state, and then the operando X-ray absorption signals were collected. Athena and Artemis codes were used to extract data. For the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) part. The parameters describing the electronic properties and local structure environment, including the distance to the scattering atom (R) and the mean-squared displacement (σ2), were allowed to vary during the fit process.

Electrochemical measurements

The ORR activity of the catalysts was assessed using RDE techniques with a CHI electrochemical workstation (CHI760e) from Shanghai Chenhua Instruments Co., Ltd. A three-electrode cell was used for the electrochemical measurements. A glassy-carbon electrode (GCE) (diameter: 5 mm, area: 0.196 cm2) from Pine Instruments was used as the working electrode, a saturated silver chloride electrode (Ag/AgCl) was used as the reference electrode, and a Pt foil was used as the counter electrode. Prior to the electrochemical measurement, the catalyst ink was prepared by sonicating a mixture of 1.5 mg catalyst, 5 μL Nafion (5 wt%), 0.75 mL isopropanol, and 0.25 mL water in an ice-water bath for 1 h. The ink was then deposited on the glassy carbon electrode with a Pt loading of 15 μgPt cm−2 and naturally dried under ambient conditions to obtain a uniform coating film. After activating the catalyst film for about 100 cycles under Ar-saturated electrolyte conditions at a scan rate of 500 mV s−1 in the range of 0.05–1.05 V vs. RHE, static cyclic voltammograms (CVs) (Ar-saturated electrolyte, 50 mV s−1, 0.05–1.05 V vs. RHE) and linear sweep voltammograms (LSVs) (O2-saturated electrolyte, 20 mV s−1, 0.05–1.05 V vs. RHE, 1600 rpm) were recorded. MEA performance measurements

The MEA activity and stability of these catalysts were evaluated using the SF100U fuel cell test system provided by Scitech Korea Inc. The catalyst inks were first prepared by mixing 5 mg of catalyst, 1 mL of isopropanol, and 3 mL of deionized water through sonication to achieve a homogeneous dispersion. Additionally, the ratio of ionomer to carbon was precisely controlled at 0.8. The as-synthesized catalysts and commercial Pt/C catalyst (Premetek, 20 wt%) were used to prepare the cathodic layer, while another commercial Pt/C catalyst (Premetek, 30 wt%) was used to prepare the anodic layer. In the MEA of LT-PEMFC, the cathode loading was set at 0.1 mg cm−2, and the anode loading was set at 0.05 mg cm−2, respectively. A commercial Nafion 211 membrane with a thickness of ca. 25 μm was sequentially pretreated by boiling in 5 wt% H2O2, deionized water, 0.5 M H2SO4, and deionized water for 1 h each, and then used as the proton exchange membrane.

The cathode and anode catalyst inks were ultrasonically sprayed onto the two sides of the pretreated NR 211 membrane using an ultrasonic spray coater to fabricate a catalyst-coated membrane (CCM) with an active geometric area of 4 cm2, which was then sandwiched between two pieces of carbon paper. To ensure comparability, the cathode and anode catalyst loadings in the reference sample (commercial Pt/C, 20 wt%, Premetek) were maintained at the same level as the i-ct-Pt3In/C catalyst. Before testing, the electrode catalyst underwent a 6 h activation process to achieve a stable performance. This activation procedure involved a steady current of 4 A for 1 h, a constant voltage of 0.65 V for 0.5 h, a constant voltage of 0.45 V for 0.5 h, and was repeated for a total of 3 cycles. After activation, polarization current experiments were carried out under controlled conditions of 80 °C, 100% relative humidity, a pressure of 0.15 MPa, and H2 flow of 0.4 L min−1, O2 flow rate of 0.8 L min−1 (or an air flow rate of 1.2 L min−1). Meanwhile, ADT was conducted, adhering to the U.S. DOE recommended square-wave protocol, involving cycling between 0.6 and 0.95 V with a 3 s hold at each voltage step at a temperature of 80 °C, relative humidity of 100%, a pressure of 0.15 MPa, H2 flow rate of 0.4 L min−1, and air flow rate of 1.2 L min−1.

Uniform catalyst ink was pneumatically sprayed onto carbon paper placed on a heating plate. Using the weighing method, the Pt loading of the anode and cathode was determined to be 0.3 and 0.6 mgPt cm−2, respectively. The dried PBI film (40 μm) was doped with phosphoric acid at 150 °C to make the doping level reach 400%. To clarify the membrane conditioning process, the dependence of membrane weight on phosphoric acid treatment time and the corresponding HT-PEMFC performance at different membrane weight percentages are shown in Fig. S29. Here, the membrane weight percentage is defined as the final membrane weight normalized to the initial dry membrane weight; therefore, a membrane weight of 500% corresponds to a weight gain of 400% relative to the dry membrane. The MEA was obtained by stacking electrodes on both sides of the phosphoric acid-doped film. The obtained MEA was then hot-pressed at 160 °C for 6 min to reduce the contact resistance between the electrodes and the film. The active area of the MEA was 4 cm2. The polarization curves are presented as measured cell voltages without iR correction.

DFT calculations

Spin-polarized calculations were performed using DFT by the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)34–36. The projector augmented wave37 method and revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (RPBE) functionals38 were applied. The dispersion correction was described in Grimme’s D3 scheme with Becke–Johnson damping DFT-D3/BJ method39. The plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 450 eV and the convergence criteria for energy and forces were set to 10−5 eV and 0.01 eV/Å, respectively. A Monkhorst–Pack k-mesh was used for k-points sampling with the interval less than 0.035 Å−1.

In this study, Pt and PtIn alloy surfaces were simulated using a slab model with a vacuum region more than 20 Å in the z direction. Pt surfaces were modeled (2 × 2) by a surface slab of four atomic layers with the bottom two layers fixed in position. For PtIn alloy surfaces, platinum atoms on the second layer were replaced with indium atoms to emulate the alloy composition. Here, only the d-band center of the first Pt layer was calculated with code VASPKIT40.
