Safety and efficacy of HLBLS-200 (BleeFix) as a novel hemostatic agent in hepatic surgery: a multicenter, randomized, active-controlled clinical trial
Authors: Junsun Lee, Hee-Hoon Yoon, Sang-Eun Cho, Yun Kyung Jung, Kyeong Sik Kim, Kyeong Geun Lee, Han Joon Kim, Hwon Kyum Park, Gyu-Seong Choi, Dongho Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of HLBLS-200 (BleeFix, HLB Cell Co., Ltd.) in patients requiring hemostasis for exudative bleeding following primary hemostasis during hepatic resection.MethodsThis prospective, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted at three hospitals in South Korea, with Arista AH (Becton Dickinson) serving as the active comparator. A total of 156 patients who underwent elective liver resection with exudative bleeding were randomized. The primary endpoint was the hemostasis success rate within 5 minutes of application. Secondary endpoint included hemostatic success rates at 3, 4, 7, and 10 minutes. Safety was assessed by monitoring adverse events over a 12-week follow-up period.ResultsThe hemostatic success rate within 5 minutes was 98.5% (66/67) in the BleeFix group and 97.0% (65/67) in the control group. The between-group difference was 1.49% (95% confidence interval [CI], −5.72% to 9.07%). The lower bound of the CI (−5.72%) exceeded the predefined non-inferiority margin of −15.00%, confirming the non-inferiority of BleeFix. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were more frequent in the BleeFix group (97.44% vs. 87.18%, P = 0.016), with most events classified as mild (grade 1). This difference may be partially attributed to a statistically significant variation in sex distribution between groups. Serious TEAEs occurred in four participants, with no statistically significant differences between groups.ConclusionBleeFix demonstrated non-inferior hemostatic efficacy compared with Arista AH during hepatectomy. These findings support the clinical use of BleeFix as a safe and effective hemostatic agent for managing exudative bleeding during hepatic resection.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatic resection is the surgical approach of choice for patients requiring curative treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma, metastatic liver malignancies, and other hepatic neoplasms [1]. It is a technically complex procedure in hepatobiliary surgery, with effective hemostasis serving as the cornerstone of successful outcomes [23]. The liver receives blood from 2 main sources, the hepatic artery and the portal vein, which together form a dense network of small vessels. This vascular complexity makes bleeding control during liver surgery particularly challenging [4]. Primary hemostatic methods such as suture ligation, vascular clipping, electrocautery, and argon beam coagulation are effective for controlling major vessel bleeding but are often insufficient for managing diffuse parenchymal oozing from the transection plane [56]. Consequently, surgeons frequently encounter difficulties in achieving complete hemostasis when exudative bleeding persists despite standard interventions [47].

Hemostatic agents represent a major advancement in surgical bleeding control, providing surgeons with additional tools to manage bleeding effectively [89]. These agents act through various mechanisms, including mechanical tamponade, enhancement of platelet aggregation, acceleration of the coagulation cascade, and promotion of fibrin clot formation [8101112]. They are available in several formulations, such as liquid agents (e.g., fibrin glue), patches containing clotting proteins like fibrinogen and thrombin, absorbable patches made from collagen or cellulose, and powder-based products derived from starch [131415].

Arista AH (Becton Dickinson) is a well-established microporous polysaccharide hemostatic powder derived from potato starch, proven effective across multiple surgical specialties [16171819]. It acts by rapidly extracting plasma fluid, concentrating platelets and coagulation factors at bleeding sites, and enhancing the natural clotting process [20]. Clinical studies have demonstrated its safety and efficacy, making it an appropriate active comparator for evaluating new hemostatic agents.

HLBLS-200 (BleeFix, HLB Cell Co., Ltd.) is a novel powder-type hemostatic agent developed using advanced biomaterial engineering principles. It contains 3 main components—hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, and polylysine. When exposed to moisture, this material exhibits rapid gel formation—occurring within approximately 10 seconds—through a mechanism that operates independently from the body’s intrinsic coagulation pathways. The resulting gel demonstrates notable cohesive properties and establishes robust adhesion to adjacent tissues. Practical advantages of HLBLS-200 include ambient temperature storage requirements, elimination of thawing procedures before use, and versatility in application to challenging anatomical sites, including irregular or confined bleeding surfaces.

The 2 major constituents of HLBLS-200, hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate, belong to the glycosaminoglycan family of molecules found throughout the human extracellular matrix. Clinical applications of hyaluronic acid have been extensive, particularly in wound management and osteoarthritis therapy, owing to its excellent biocompatibility profile. Chondroitin sulfate contributes multiple beneficial properties: it serves as an antioxidant, provides protective effects for articular tissues, demonstrates antimicrobial activity, and promotes coagulation. These characteristics have established chondroitin sulfate as a preferred material in cartilage regeneration strategies. The third component, polylysine, consists of repeated L-lysine units—an essential amino acid—and finds primary application in wound coverage materials.

The hemostatic action of HLBLS-200 initiates immediately upon exposure to blood or tissue fluids through a chemical crosslinking process. This reaction involves aldehyde functional groups present on oxidized forms of both hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate, which undergo condensation with amine groups on polylysine molecules. The resulting imine bonds (also termed Schiff bases) drive gel network formation within a 10-second timeframe. The gel matrix serves dual functions: it establishes a physical barrier at the bleeding site while simultaneously adhering strongly to surrounding tissue, thereby achieving hemostasis via localized compression. Following its hemostatic function, the material undergoes enzymatic degradation. The polysaccharide components experience breakdown through the action of hyaluronidase, hydroxyl radicals, and various endoglycosidases, with subsequent hepatic metabolism and elimination through respiratory and renal routes. Meanwhile, proteolytic enzymes hydrolyze polylysine, which is then cleared by the kidneys [2122].

Nexpowder (NEXTBIOMEDICAL Co., Ltd.), which has received Korean regulatory approval (Medical Device Approval No. 18-14), operates through comparable mechanisms. This absorbable synthetic wound dressing combines dextran with polylysine in powder form and has established clinical utility within Korea.

A clinical trial was therefore warranted to confirm the safety and efficacy of HLBLS-200. Arista AH was selected as the active control to provide comparative data against a clinically validated hemostatic standard, as the use of a placebo would be ethically inappropriate in this surgical context. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of HLBLS-200 in patients requiring hemostasis for exudative bleeding following primary hemostasis during hepatic resection, by demonstrating non-inferiority in hemostatic success rate compared with Arista AH, an approved powder-type hemostatic agent.

METHODS

This multicenter, single-blind, randomized clinical trial was conducted at 3 medical centers: Hanyang University Hospital (primary coordinating center), Samsung Medical Center, and Hanyang University Guri Hospital. The study spanned 31 months, with patient enrollment beginning on July 7, 2022, and the final follow-up completed on February 18, 2025.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Hanyang University Hospital (No. HYUH 2022-02-016), Samsung Medical Center (No. SMC 2022-02-051), and Hanyang University Guri Hospital (No. GURI 2022-04-008). The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Korean Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants provided voluntary written informed consent.

The study aimed to show that HLBLS-200 works just as well as Arista AH in controlling bleeding during liver surgery. Both products were tested on patients who still had minor bleeding even after doctors used standard methods to stop it initially.

The non-inferiority margin was determined using the fixed-margin method as recommended in the U.S. Food and Drug Administration guidance for industry: non-inferiority clinical trials [25].

Determination of the statistical margin (M1)

In general, the statistical margin (M1) is established based on the effect size between an active control and a placebo. However, because the present trial involved patients requiring active hemostasis, a placebo-controlled comparison was not ethically feasible. Therefore, the statistical margin was derived using a standard treatment comparator, namely oxidized regenerative cellulose, to estimate the treatment effect of the active control. As direct data on the 5-minute hemostatic success rate of standard treatment were unavailable, the success rate was estimated using published results from Koea et al. [26], Koea et al. [27], and Bektas et al. [24]. The hemostatic success rates at 4 and 10 minutes reported in these studies demonstrated similar incremental patterns across references. In the study by Bektas et al. [24], the hemostatic success rate increased rapidly during the early postoperative minutes and then exhibited a more gradual increase over time. Although Koea et al. [26] and Koea et al. [27] reported only 4- and 10-minute outcomes, it was assumed that their time-dependent success rates followed a similar trend. Accordingly, linear interpolation was applied using the slope between 4 and 6 minutes (0.1) derived from Bektas et al. [24] to estimate the 5-minute hemostatic success rates for the studies by Koea et al. [26], Koea et al. [27] (Supplementary Table 1). Using the pooled results from the Arista AH trial [23] and the studies by Koea et al. [26], Koea et al. [27], and Bektas et al. [24], the estimated difference in 5-minute hemostatic success rates between Arista AH and standard treatment was 0.407 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.309–0.505) as summarized in Table 1. According to the fixed-margin method, the lower bound of the 95% CI (0.309) was selected as the statistical margin (M1).

Determination of the clinical margin (M2)

The clinical margin (M2) should be established based on clinical judgment and is typically defined as a prespecified proportion of M1. In the present study, M2 was set at 0.15, corresponding to approximately 50% preservation of the treatment effect (0.309 × 0.5 ≈ 0.15). Furthermore, recent non-inferiority clinical trials evaluating topical hemostatic agents have reported non-inferiority margins ranging from 0.10 to 0.239 [28293031323334], as summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The margin of 0.15 selected in this trial corresponds to the median value of these previously published margins, supporting its clinical and methodological appropriateness.

Inclusion criteria

Data were prospectively collected from patients undergoing hepatic resection at Hanyang University Hospital, Hanyang University Guri Hospital, and Samsung Medical Center who met the following inclusion criteria: Screening phase: (a) age ≥19 years; (b) scheduled for open or minimally invasive elective liver resection; (c) provision of voluntary written informed consent; and (d) ability to comply with study procedures and follow-up requirements. Intraoperative phase: (a) presence of oozing hemorrhage at the hepatic resection site after primary hemostatic interventions (including suture ligation, vascular clipping, electrocautery, or argon beam coagulation).

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: Screening phase: (a) history of inherited or acquired bleeding disorders; (b) platelet disorders or bone marrow dysfunction; (c) previous liver transplantation; (d) coagulopathy (platelet count <50 × 109/L or international normalized ratio >2.0); (e) severe renal dysfunction (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min); (f) hepatic dysfunction (ALT or AST >3 × the upper limit of normal); (g) disseminated intravascular coagulation or other fibrinolytic disorders; (h) use of anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, or thrombolytics within 7 days before surgery; (i) known hypersensitivity to any component of the study device; (j) substance abuse or dependence; (k) pregnancy or lactation; (l) women of childbearing potential or men planning pregnancy without effective contraception; (m) participation in another investigational study within 4 weeks; and (n) any condition deemed inappropriate for study participation by the investigator. Intraoperative phase: (a) major bleeding (arterial spurting or significant venous hemorrhage) persisting after primary hemostasis; (b) anticipated need for anticoagulation within 48 hours postoperatively; and (c) any intraoperative condition precluding safe study participation.

Patients who met all inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled in the clinical trial. Randomization numbers were generated using SAS software (ver. 9.4 or higher, SAS Institute) with a stratified block randomization method to ensure a 1:1 allocation between the test and control groups based on clinical site and surgical method. After screening, participants were assigned sequential randomization numbers, and the corresponding study device (test or control) was applied according to group allocation.

A single-blind design was employed because complete blinding of the operating surgeon was impractical because of visible differences between HLBLS-200 and Arista AH. To minimize bias, all efficacy endpoints were assessed by independently trained reviewers blinded to treatment allocation, using standardized criteria and video documentation. Liver resections were performed using standard surgical techniques. Primary hemostasis was achieved using conventional methods (suturing, ligation, vascular clips, argon beam coagulation, or electrocauterization). When the investigator determined that exudative bleeding persisted after primary hemostasis, intraoperative eligibility was confirmed, and randomization was performed. The assigned hemostatic agent was then applied to the bleeding site according to group allocation. All procedures were video-recorded for independent efficacy evaluation.

Efficacy and safety outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to demonstrate the non-inferiority of HLBLS-200 to Arista AH in achieving hemostasis within 5 minutes of application in patients with oozing hemorrhage following primary hemostatic interventions during liver resection. Secondary objectives included the evaluation of safety and assessment of hemostatic success at additional time points. The primary efficacy endpoint was defined as hemostatic success within 5 minutes of application. The secondary efficacy endpoints included hemostatic success rates at 3, 4, 7, and 10 minutes after application. The time from application to complete hemostasis was recorded on the hemostasis time evaluation form. To ensure consistency, hemostatic success within 5 minutes was analyzed according to bleeding severity at randomization (mild, moderate, or severe) and by surgical approach (open vs. laparoscopic).

Safety was evaluated based on the incidence, severity, and relationship of adverse events (AEs). AEs were defined as any unintended signs, symptoms, or diseases occurring during the trial. Adverse device effects (ADEs) were defined as harmful or unintended reactions with a possible causal relationship to the investigational device. Serious AEs or ADEs included events resulting in death, life-threatening situations, hospitalization or its prolongation, significant disability or impairment, or congenital anomalies or birth defects.

Patients underwent follow-up assessments at the following time points: immediate postoperative period, 12 hours (±6 hours), 36 hours (±6 hours), 4 weeks (±7 days), and 12 weeks (±7 days) after application.

Postoperative rebleeding was classified based on the investigator’s comprehensive clinical judgment in cases showing signs suggestive of bleeding, such as the presence of fresh blood in the surgical drain or a decrease in hemoglobin level of ≥2 g/dL.

Statistical analysis

Efficacy was analyzed in the per-protocol set (PPS), with supplementary analyses using the full analysis set (FAS). Safety analyses were conducted on the safety set. The FAS included all randomized participants, while the PPS encompassed participants from the FAS who completed efficacy evaluations without major protocol violations (i.e., with complete bleeding site imaging on the day of surgery). The safety set included participants who underwent at least 1 safety evaluation during follow-up.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations and compared using two-sample t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for non-normal data. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact tests. Within-group changes were analyzed using the McNemar test. All statistical analyses, except for the non-inferiority assessment, used a significance level of 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient selection

A total of 163 participants from 3 clinical sites who provided written informed consent were recruited for this study. After screening, 156 eligible participants were randomized—78 each to the BleeFix and Arista AH groups. Of these, 22 participants (13 in the test group and 9 in the control group) discontinued the study prematurely, while 134 completed the clinical trial.

Reasons for discontinuation included withdrawal of consent by the participant or legal guardian (n = 9), administration of prohibited medications or the need for additional surgical procedures that could affect safety or efficacy evaluation (n = 5), investigator’s judgment that continued participation was inappropriate (n = 4), failure to meet inclusion or exclusion criteria discovered post-randomization (n = 3), and loss to follow-up (n = 1). Participant enrollment and completion are summarized in Fig. 1.

Patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the 156 participants included in the FAS are presented in Table 2. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) age was 47.29 ± 15.64 years. Of the participants, 93 (59.62%) were male and 63 (40.38%) were female; 35 (55.56%) women were of childbearing potential, and all pregnancy tests were negative. The mean ± SD height, weight, and body mass index were 166.77 ± 8.76 cm, 68.21 ± 13.48 kg, and 24.40 ± 3.64 kg/m2, respectively. The mean ± SD surgical duration was 3.67 ± 2.00 hours. Surgical approaches included open surgery in 19 participants (12.18%) and laparoscopic surgery in 137 participants (87.82%). At randomization, bleeding severity was classified as moderate in 133 participants (85.26%) and mild in 23 participants (14.74%); no cases of severe bleeding were observed. Hepatic resection procedures included lobectomy in 96 participants (61.54%), segmentectomy in 38 (24.36%), wedge resection in 21 (13.46%), and trisegmentectomy in 1 (0.64%). Primary hemostatic methods employed included electrocautery in 155 participants (99.36%), vascular clips in 120 (76.92%), conventional suturing in 111 (71.15%), and ligation in 7 (4.49%). At the time of study device application, all participants presented with grade 1 (mild) bleeding.

Baseline demographics and characteristics were generally well balanced between groups, except for sex distribution, which differed significantly (P = 0.006). The control group had a higher proportion of male participants (70.51%), whereas the test group had a higher proportion of female participants (51.28%). No other baseline differences were statistically significant.

The surgical indications for liver resection, standardized using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) ver. 28.0, are presented in Table 3.

Efficacy outcomes

Hemostatic success within 5 minutes at the bleeding site was analyzed in the PPS. The success rate within 5 minutes was 98.51% (66/67) in the BleeFix group and 97.01% (65/67) in the Arista group. The between-group difference was 1.49% (95% CI, −5.72% to 9.07%). The lower bound (−5.72%) exceeded the predefined non-inferiority margin of −15.00%, demonstrating the non-inferiority of BleeFix compared with Arista AH. Because sex distribution differed significantly between groups, a sensitivity analysis adjusting for sex as a covariate was conducted. The sex-adjusted between-group difference in the PPS was 0.74% (95% CI, −7.84 to 9.58). The lower bound (−7.84%) also exceeded the non-inferiority margin of −15.00%, confirming non-inferiority and consistency with the unadjusted analysis.

Hemostatic success rates at 3, 4, 7, and 10 minutes after application are summarized in Table 4. No statistically significant differences were observed between the BleeFix and Arista groups at any time point. At the final 10-minute assessment, complete hemostatic success (100%) was achieved in both groups.

The median hemostasis times were 1.45 minutes in the BleeFix group and 1.48 minutes in the Arista group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.936).

Safety analysis

Safety analysis was performed on the safety set, which included all 156 participants (78 per group). Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and ADEs are summarized in Tables 7 and 8.

A total of 470 TEAEs occurred in 144 participants (92.31%). These included 258 events in 76 participants (97.44%) in the BleeFix group and 212 events in 68 participants (87.18%) in the Arista group. The incidence of TEAEs was significantly higher in the BleeFix group (P = 0.016). Of the 470 reported events, 292 were grade 1 (mild), 166 were grade 2 (moderate), and 12 were grade 3 (severe). All 12 grade 3 events were deemed unrelated to the investigational medical devices.

Two ADEs occurred in 2 participants—one (1.28%) in the BleeFix group and another (1.28%) in the Arista group—with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.999). The ADEs were classified as one grade 1 (mild) and one grade 2 (moderate) event, neither considered a serious ADE. Both were reported as “not applicable” and confirmed as “recovered/resolved.”

Serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred in 4 participants (2.56%)—1 (1.28%) in the BleeFix group and 3 (3.85%) in the Arista group (P = 0.620). Reported SAEs included biloma (2 events), increased blood bilirubin (1 event), and pelvic fluid collection (1 event). No statistically significant differences were observed between groups.

All TEAEs were recorded as “not applicable.” Final outcomes were: “recovered/resolved” (430 events), “recovering/resolving” (35 events), “not recovered/not resolved” (4 events), and “unknown” (1 event). No AEs resulted in discontinuation of study treatment or death.

Postoperative rebleeding rate

Postoperative rebleeding rates among the 156 participants in the safety set were analyzed. In the BleeFix group, rebleeding occurred in 1 patient immediately postoperatively and in 2 patients 12 hours after application. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups (immediate postoperative, P = 0.497; 12 hours after application, P = 0.497).

DISCUSSION

This clinical trial aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of BleeFix in patients undergoing liver resection surgery and to demonstrate its non-inferiority in hemostatic success rate within 5 minutes compared with Arista AH, a previously approved powder-type hemostatic agent of the same form. The primary efficacy endpoint—hemostatic success rate within 5 minutes at the bleeding site—showed that BleeFix was non-inferior to Arista AH. The secondary efficacy endpoints assessing hemostatic success rates within 3, 4, 7, and 10 minutes also showed no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups.

The safety analysis revealed that a total of 470 TEAEs were reported in 144 of 156 participants (92.31%) in the safety analysis set. Although the frequency of AEs was significantly higher in the BleeFix group, all events were evaluated as unrelated to the medical device and were not considered safety concerns. Despite randomization, the proportion of female participants was significantly higher in the BleeFix group, and this sex imbalance is considered a potential contributing factor to the observed difference in TEAE incidence. Previous studies have reported that postoperative nausea and vomiting occur more frequently in female patients than in male patients [35].

TEAE incidence was analyzed stratified by sex, as shown in Table 9. Among male participants, a total of 128 TEAEs were reported in 36 of 38 patients (94.74%) in the BleeFix group, whereas 125 TEAEs were reported in 46 of 55 patients (83.64%) in the Arista group. The difference in TEAE incidence between the 2 groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.190). Among female participants, 130 TEAEs were reported in all 40 patients (100%) in the BleeFix group, while 87 TEAEs were reported in 22 of 23 patients (95.65%) in the Arista group. Similarly, no statistically significant difference in TEAE incidence was observed between the 2 groups (P = 0.365). These findings suggest that the apparent difference in overall TEAE incidence between the BleeFix and Arista groups may be partly attributable to differences in sex distribution rather than to device-related effects. SAEs occurred in 4 participants (2.56%) across both groups.

In the BleeFix group, 1 patient developed pelvic fluid collection; percutaneous catheter drainage and antibiotic therapy were performed, leading to recovery. In the Arista AH group, 1 patient developed increased serum bilirubin levels, and 2 patients experienced biloma. To determine the cause, computed tomography and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography were performed, which confirmed that biliary obstruction-related complications were not involved. A 5-French 15-cm single pigtail stent was inserted in 1 patient, and follow-up visits showed that serum bilirubin levels decreased. The other 2 patients with biloma underwent percutaneous catheter drainage and antibiotic therapy, with outpatient follow-up confirming recovery. These were expected postoperative complications following liver resection and were determined to be unrelated to the investigational medical devices.

Standard hemostatic methods, such as suture ligation, vascular clipping, and electrocautery, are effective for controlling major vessel bleeding but are often insufficient for managing diffuse parenchymal oozing at the liver resection site [36]. This limitation has led to the development of topical hemostatic agents. Modern hemostatic agents can be broadly categorized into several classes: fibrin sealants (e.g., Tisseel), gelatin-based products (e.g., Surgiflo), thrombin-containing products, and newer synthetic polymers [3738]. Each category acts through distinct mechanisms—fibrin sealants replicate the final steps of the coagulation cascade, gelatin-based products provide a scaffold for clot formation, and thrombin products directly activate fibrinogen conversion to fibrin [3439].

Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have highlighted the importance of topical hemostatic agents in liver surgery. A meta-analysis of randomized trials demonstrated that while many agents effectively shorten the time to hemostasis, their main benefit lies in achieving rapid bleeding control rather than significantly reducing transfusion requirements or major postoperative complications [40]. The unique formulation of BleeFix, comprising hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, and polylysine, enables rapid polymer crosslinking to form a physical barrier independent of the coagulation cascade [41]. This mechanism offers several clinical advantages. Unlike fibrin-based sealants that depend on intact coagulation pathways, BleeFix achieves hemostasis through physical barrier formation, which is particularly beneficial for patients with coagulopathies or those receiving anticoagulant therapy. Additionally, its powder formulation allows easy application to irregular surfaces and confined spaces, which is advantageous during laparoscopic procedures. In this study, 98.5% of patients achieved hemostasis within 5 minutes, with many achieving control even sooner. The powder’s ability to conform to tissue contours and fill small crevices ensures superior coverage compared to patch-based systems. Moreover, the naturally occurring glycosaminoglycans (hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate) minimize the risk of foreign body reactions and promote tissue integration. These components are naturally present in human tissues and are gradually resorbed without leaving permanent residues. Unlike some hemostatic agents requiring reconstitution or cold storage, BleeFix is ready-to-use and storable at room temperature, reducing preparation time and minimizing application errors during critical bleeding situations.

The high hemostatic success rate observed in this study (98.5%) compares favorably with that of other hemostatic agents tested in similar clinical settings. For instance, recent trials involving gelatin-based matrices have reported success rates ranging from 85% to 95% within similar time frames [2042].

Despite its advantages, BleeFix demonstrated certain limitations that warrant further consideration. The most notable finding was a statistically significant increase in TEAEs compared with the control group (97.44% vs. 87.18%, P = 0.016). However, detailed evaluation indicated that most of these events were unrelated to device use and should be interpreted within the appropriate clinical context. Additionally, the single ADE in the BleeFix group, a mild bloody discharge, resolved without intervention. Postoperative rebleeding classification cases were observed in 1 BleeFix patient immediately after surgery (1.32%) and in 2 patients (2.67%) at 12 hours, while no postoperative rebleeding classification was observed in the Arista AH group. A decrease in hemoglobin level of ≥2 g/dL and signs of hemodynamic instability are clinical findings that may occur following hepatic resection. All 3 cases that were classified as postoperative rebleeding were assessed as not being related to the investigational device and consistent with clinical events that can commonly occur after hepatic resection. In 1 case, bleeding was identified at the lower abdominal incision site created for specimen extraction during laparoscopic surgery, and this event was therefore considered a rebleeding case unrelated to the investigational device. None of the 3 cases required reoperation.

This study has some limitations. First, while the trial was adequately powered for the primary non-inferiority endpoint, the sample size (156 patients; 78 per group) may have been insufficient to detect smaller differences in secondary endpoints or rare AEs. Larger trials would strengthen safety assessments and allow for more meaningful subgroup analyses. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in South Korea across 3 hospitals. This geographic and institutional homogeneity may limit the generalizability of our results to other populations, surgical techniques, or healthcare settings. Cultural factors, genetic variations, and differences in perioperative care protocols could potentially influence both efficacy and safety outcomes. Third, the 12-week follow-up period, although suitable for assessing short-term safety and efficacy, may not capture long-term effects or complications such as adhesion formation or chronic inflammatory responses. Additionally, although blinding of investigators and patients was implemented, differences in the physical properties of the powders may have compromised blinding in some cases, potentially introducing bias in outcome assessment. Finally, while Arista AH was an appropriate comparator, comparisons with other classes of hemostatic products (e.g., fibrin sealants or gelatin-based agents) would provide a broader context for evaluating BleeFix’s relative performance.

In conclusion, this multicenter, randomized, active-controlled clinical trial demonstrated that HLBLS-200 (BleeFix) is a safe and effective hemostatic agent for managing exudative bleeding during liver resection procedures. Its favorable efficacy, ease of use, and biocompatible composition suggest that BleeFix can positively influence patient outcomes in hepatic surgery.
