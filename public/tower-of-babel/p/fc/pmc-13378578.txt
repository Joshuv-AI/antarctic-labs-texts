Disease landscape–guided design of neutrophil-specific reporters for early and accurate pneumonia detection in vivo and in urine
Authors: Zhen Li, Tong Li, Keyang Li, Maochao Zheng, Lili Chen, Jialong Sun, Yanhui Li, Shasha He, Huayu Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate and early detection of pneumonia is crucial for effective treatment; however, current diagnostic methods often lack the necessary specificity and sensitivity. Herein, we begin with a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, identifying neutrophil elastase (NE) as a critical biomarker associated with pneumonia progression. We subsequently develop an NE-responsive probe (NERP), composed of a hemicyanine fluorophore linked to an NE-sensitive peptide, specifically designed for activation in inflamed tissues. To facilitate urinalysis, NERP is further refined into a hydrophilic active targeting responsive probe (ATRPH). ATRPH demonstrates exceptional sensitivity in both in vivo imaging and urine-based detection, with urine analysis offering a noninvasive, early-stage diagnostic option that overcomes the limitations of tissue penetration and low accuracy. In addition, ATRPH is highly effective in drug screening, dosage optimization, and exploring mechanisms of NE production. This biomarker screening and design strategy not only enhances pneumonia diagnosis and treatment via different routes of administration but also has broader potential for other inflammatory lung diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Pneumonia is one of the most prevalent infectious diseases globally (1). Its acute onset and complex causes particularly threaten immunocompromised groups, such as children and the elderly (2–4). Early and accurate diagnosis of pneumonia is imperative to reducing mortality and improving clinical outcomes. Current diagnostic tools, including chest x-ray radiography (5), computed tomography (CT) (6), lung ultrasound (7), and magnetic resonance imaging (8, 9), are routinely used to evaluate pulmonary conditions. However, these techniques are hampered by limitations such as insufficient spatial resolution, exposure to ionizing radiation, and a general inability to detect early-stage disease with high accuracy (10, 11). The global shortage of skilled radiologists further complicates timely diagnosis, leading to delayed or incorrect treatment. Therefore, developing advanced imaging technologies that offer precise, radiation-free, noninvasive, and straightforward diagnostic methods to facilitate the early detection of pneumonia is crucial for improving therapeutic effectiveness and patient outcomes (12, 13).

Molecular imaging has revolutionized diagnostic medicine by enabling noninvasive visualization of biological processes at the molecular and cellular levels (14–16). Unlike traditional imaging modalities that primarily depict structural changes, molecular imaging allows for the precise detection of disease-specific biomarkers, thus facilitating early diagnosis, detailed disease characterization, and real-time monitoring of treatment (17–19). This approach has gained widespread use in fields such as oncology (20), neurology (21), and infectious diseases (22) because of its ability to provide precise insights into pathophysiological mechanisms. However, its clinical application in pneumonia diagnosis has been limited by several challenges, particularly with regard to reporter specificity and tissue penetration. Many existing probes lack the required specificity to reliably differentiate between pneumonia and other tissue pathologies, leading to false positives and reducing their clinical utility (23–26). In addition, their limited tissue penetration restricts the detection of subtle molecular changes in situ (27, 28). In addition, extensive studies have demonstrated that neutrophils are key orchestrators in pneumonia progression, rapidly accumulating in the infected lung and driving tissue injury through the release of inflammatory mediators, proteases, and reactive species (29–33). Thus, the development of molecular probes capable of detecting neutrophil-associated biological events with multimodal detection capabilities is essential for more accurate and timely pneumonia diagnosis.

In this study, we designed an activatable fluorescent probe for noninvasive early detection of pneumonia via in situ imaging and urine. Through a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of gene expression profiles from human blood samples and a murine lung injury model, we have confirmed neutrophils, particularly neutrophil elastase (NE), as key roles in pneumonia pathogenesis. Although a variety of NE-responsive probes (NERPs) have been developed, including those based on boron-dipyrromethene (34), hemianthocyanin derivatives (35, 36), quantum dots (37), nitrobenzoxadiazole (38), and phenoxazine (39), these systems generally offer only a single imaging modality and exhibit limited fluorescence penetration depth, which restricts their ability to detect early-stage pneumonia. Herein, we develop a NERP that can be activated specifically in inflamed lung tissue. To enhance its hydrophilicity, we introduce a hydrophilic polymer onto its side links to obtain a hydrophilic active targeting responsive probe (ATRPH). ATRPH enables real-time in situ imaging of pneumonia and noninvasive urinalysis, detecting the disease at earlier stages than conventional clinical methods and providing valuable insights into neutrophil-mediated tissue damage. In addition, ATRPH is applied for drug screening, dosage optimization, and monitoring of NE as the end point for in vivo mechanism evaluation, demonstrating its utility in assessing therapeutic efficacy and refining treatment strategies. Moreover, a NERP with a DSPE-PEG [1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-poly(ethylene glycol)] side chain (NERPD) can be prepared into liposomes [liposome active targeting responsive probe (ATRPL)] and membrane-doped [membrane active targeting responsive probe (ATRPM)] nanoparticles through formulation optimization, extending the administration route of ATRPs, which are expected to be used for the detection of systemic inflammation (table S1). This approach not only offers a novel tool for early pneumonia diagnosis and monitoring but also advances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying pneumonia, laying the foundation for more precise and effective therapies.

RESULTS

Analysis of signatures in pneumonia

Bioinformatics is an important part of biomedical research and development and plays a key role in drug discovery and structural design (40). To identify key biomarkers of pneumonia progression, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of gene expression profiles from patients with pneumonia and healthy controls of the GSE42830 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Using the Limma package in R language, we identified 120 notably up-regulated and 44 down-regulated genes in the blood of patients with pneumonia compared to healthy individuals (fig. S1). Among the up-regulated genes, the top 10 hub genes including OLFM4, RETN, LTF, CEACAM6, CEACAM8, OLR1, MMP8, ELANE, BPI, and ARG1 were identified as critical for neutrophil activation, modulating processes such as antimicrobial activity, migration, signaling, and the regulation of inflammatory responses (Fig. 1A) (41). In particular, CEACAM6 and CEACAM8 are involved in cell adhesion and migration, which are critical for neutrophil activation, while MMP8 and ELANE encode enzymes that are essential for the degradation of bacterial proteins and extracellular matrix components. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis linked these up-regulated genes to immune responses, including functions related to azurophil granules, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) binding (42), and the Wingless-type MMTV integration site family (Wnt) signaling pathway (fig. S2A). Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis linked these genes to the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) (fig. S2D). In addition, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis revealed that BPI, LTF, AZU1, PGLYRP1, CAMP, MPO, DEFA1, DEFA4, CTSG, and ELANE have high interaction degrees, underscoring their critical role in neutrophil function and innate immune response to infection (Fig. 1B and fig. S3). Collectively, these findings suggest the central role of neutrophil activation in the pathogenesis of pneumonia.

We then performed an in-depth analysis of cellular profiles and intercellular interactions by using the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of LPS-inflamed mouse lungs from GEO (GSE159161). Post–LPS challenge, neutrophils dominated the bronchoalveolar lavage cell population, comprising 77% of all cells, while alveolar macrophages accounted for 18% (Fig. 1, C and D). CellChat ligand-receptor interaction analysis revealed intercellular communication between neutrophils and other immune populations within the inflamed lung microenvironment (Fig. 1, E and F). By integrating the proportion of genes expressed in at least a defined percentage of each cell population, we observed that the top-expressed genes in neutrophils were distinctly segregated from other cell types (fig. S4). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these up-regulated genes were notably enriched in molecular functions related to LPS response and innate immune processes (fig. S5A). Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that these genes are critically involved in inflammatory pathways, notably the nuclear factor κB signaling pathway (fig. S5B), indicating that neutrophils play an important role in pneumonia pathogenesis.

To further delineate the molecular mechanisms underlying pneumonia, we used a model of acute lung injury (ALI) that induces pneumonia (43). ALI was induced by intratracheal injection of LPS (44), and lung tissues were harvested 12 hours postinstillation for transcriptome profiling. Differential expression analysis using the Limma package identified 1020 notably up-regulated and 1150 notably down-regulated genes in the lungs of ALI compared to healthy controls (fig. S6). The most up-regulated genes included CXCL5, SAA1, IL-6, CSF3, SAA2, CXCL2, CXCL3, SAA3, and CCL20 (fig. S7A). Although these up-regulated genes are not identical from those identified in whole blood from patients, they are recognized as key regulators of neutrophil biology, particularly in modulating neutrophil production, recruitment, activation, and chemotaxis (45). Furthermore, the neutrophil-specific gene ELANE exhibited a 14-fold up-regulation in injured lungs compared to controls, while MPO showed a 24-fold up-regulation (fig. S7B). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these up-regulated genes are notably associated with biological processes including neutrophil activation, neutrophil chemotaxis, and response to LPS (Fig. 1G). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that these genes play a crucial role in inflammatory pathways such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling (Fig. 1H). PPI network analysis highlighted the centrality of up-regulated neutrophil-associated genes, including CXCL3, Ly6G, ELANE, CCL20, MPO, MMP8, CXCL5, CXCL2, IL-6, and TNF-α, all of which exhibited notable interaction hub status within the network (Fig. 1, I and J). Collectively, these transcriptomic findings suggest neutrophils as key effectors in pneumonia pathogenesis and NE as a critical biomarker associated with the progression of the disease, a conclusion that is consistent with the previous literature (29, 31, 32, 46).

Involvement of NE in the pathogenesis of pneumonia

The involvement of NE in lung pathogenesis was validated against a model of ALI in living mice induced by intratracheal injection of LPS. CT imaging of mice was performed at 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours postinjection of LPS, and mice were subsequently euthanized and lungs were harvested for biological analyzes (Fig. 2A). CT images (Fig. 2B) showed increased lung density, reduced air volume, and the replacement of air by exudates, with the development of lung inflammation at 12 and 24 hours postinjection of LPS (6). After the establishment of ALI, mice had a notably higher expression level of MPO associated with neutrophil activation (47) and a storm of cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-2 compared to healthy mice (figs. S8 and S9). For example, the MPO expression level was 2.5-, 4.1-, 6.0-, and 4.3-fold higher in the lung of ALI mice at 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours postinjection of LPS than that of the control, respectively, indicating the successful establishment of ALI (Fig. 2C). Accordingly, the NE expression level in the lungs of ALI mice was 1.1-, 1.2-, 1.3-, and 1.2-fold higher than that of the control group at 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours postinjection of LPS, respectively, indicating the involvement of NE in the pathogenesis of ALI (Fig. 2D).

To further investigate the role of NE in the progression of ALI, quantification of neutrophils and their corresponding NE levels in neutrophils was performed. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the highest levels of CD11b+Ly6G+ cells (neutrophils) (48) and NE+ cells were observed at 12 hours postinjection of LPS (Fig. 2E and fig. S11A). Notably, increased neutrophil and NE expression was observed as early as 3 hours postinjection of LPS (Fig. 2F and fig. S11B), which was further confirmed by immunofluorescence (IF) staining of lung sections using an anti-Ly6G antibody (Fig. 2G and fig. S11). In addition, the extent of pulmonary edema, exudation/infiltration of alveolar inflammatory cells, and alveolar damage (4), as demonstrated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of lung sections, increased with a longer duration of injury (Fig. 2H and fig. S11A). The apoptotic areas in the injured lung also expanded over time (Fig. 2I). Linear regression analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between the population of Ly6G+NE+ cells in the lung and lung injury scores (R2 = 0.86, ρ = 0.93) and lung TNF-α levels (R2 = 0.92, ρ = 0.96), indicating the potential of NE as a biomarker for lung injury detection (Fig. 2, J and K). These data suggest that NE, a neutrophil-specific biomarker, can be used as a hallmark for the detection of pneumonia.

Synthesis of NERP and in vitro detection

To specifically detect NE, an activatable NERP (Fig. 3A) was designed using the fluorescent hemicyanine probe CyOH (49, 50), with the synthetic routes illustrated in fig. S12. The synthesis of NERP involved two key steps. First, CyOH was synthesized through the reaction of CyCl with resorcinol (51). Next, the NE-cleavable peptide substrate N-acetyl-Ala-Ala-Pro-Val-OH (AAPV) was synthesized using standard solid-phase synthesis and coupled with 4-aminobenzyl alcohol (PABA) to obtain AAPV-PABA. Bromination of AAPV-PABA with PBr3 (phosphorus tribromide) generated the intermediate AAPV-PABA-Br, which was then subjected to nucleophilic substitution with CyOH to obtain NERP. The successful synthesis of the above compounds was confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry analyses.

The optical properties of NERP were then validated. NERP exhibited two characteristic ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption peaks at 608 and 655 nm and was initially nonfluorescent. Upon addition of NE, a new peak at 680 nm emerged, corresponding to the uncaged CyOH (Fig. 3B and fig. S13A) (52). Concurrently, the fluorescence signal at 710 nm increased by 10-fold (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S13B), indicating the successful activation of NERP by NE. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis further verified the release of CyOH (tR = 8.5 min) from NERP (tR = 12.9 min) in the presence of NE (Fig. 3E). The enzymatic Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) of NE toward NERP was calculated to be 167.1 μM, with a catalytic efficiency (Kcat/Km) of 1.67 × 10−4 μM−1 s−1, where Kcat represents the catalytic rate constant (Fig. 3F). Besides, NERP showed negligible fluorescence increases upon incubation with other enzymes or reactive oxygen species (ROS) potentially present in the pneumonia microenvironment (Fig. 3G and fig. S14). In neutrophils extracted from bone marrow, the fluorescence activation of NERP was comparable to that of uncaged CyOH, with a three- to sevenfold increase in signal compared to nonspecific cells, such as bone marrow–derived macrophage (BMDM), bone marrow–derived cells (BMDCs), mouse leukemia cells of monocyte macrophage (RAW 264.7), or cluster of differentiation 3–positive (CD3+) T and mouse lung epithelial cells (MLE-12) or in the presence of an NE inhibitor, sivelestat (Fig. 3, H and I, and figs. S15 and S16) (34). These data indicate that NERP is specifically responsive to NE and is fully activated in neutrophils.

Comprehensive assessment of ATRPH for ALI detection

To facilitate in vivo application, ATRPH was synthesized using a copper(I)-catalyzed alkyne-azide cycloaddition (CuAAC) reaction between NERP and PEG2K (polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 2000)–alkyne (Fig. 4A and figs. S17 and S18) (53). ATRPH had a zeta potential of 1.6 mV (table S2). As shown in figs. S19 and S20, the introduction of PEG did not affect the NE responsiveness or safety of NERP but notably enhanced its aqueous solubility, facilitating in situ intratracheal injection and rapid renal excretion from the lungs.

The pharmacokinetics of ATRPH were first explored in healthy mice. To accurately quantify the concentration inhaled by the mice, a passive delivery strategy of a microsprayer aerosolizer was used for intratracheal administration, where the ATRPH solution was passed through the aerosolizer to form an aerosol (fig. S21), which could mimic active inhalation by the patient using a nebulizer in the clinic (54). After intratracheal injection, ATRPH plasma levels returned to the baseline at 180 min postinjection, with an elimination half-life (T1/2) of 90 min (fig. S22). The renal clearance efficiency (RCE) of ATRPH was determined to be 60% at 24 hours postinjection, as measured by HPLC analysis (Fig. 4B). This clearance efficiency is attributed to the hydrophilic PEG side chain introduced to NERP (51). In addition, the excreted ATRPH in urine maintained the same retention time in the HPLC spectra as in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Fig. 4C) and exhibited an average molecular weight of ∼2872 Da in the matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight mass analysis, confirming its stability during renal clearance for ALI urinalysis (fig. S23). Furthermore, no organ damage was observed in mice following intratracheal injection of ATRPH (fig. S24). In addition, ATRPH was administered systematically to rats, and rat plasma was collected at 3, 5, and 8 days postinjection for safety assessment. As shown in fig. S25, liver and kidney function in rats at 3, 5, and 8 days showed no differences compared to healthy rats.

To visualize ALI progression, an LPS-induced ALI model with varying infection times (1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours) was established, and ATRPH was administered intratracheally into the lungs of mice for real-time in vivo imaging (Fig. 4D and fig. S26). Intensified near-infrared fluorescent (NIRF) signals were observed within 2 min postinjection of ATRPH in the lungs (both ventral and dorsal views) of mice infected with LPS for 12 and 24 hours (ALI 12 hours and ALI 24 hours, respectively) (Fig. 4, E to I), reaching their maxima at 45 to 60 min postinjection. At 2 min postinjection, ATRPH exhibited 4.7- and 8.5-fold higher NIRF signals in the ventral lung (Fig. 4F) and 8.3- and 5.9-fold higher NIRF signals in the dorsal lung of ALI 12 hours and ALI 24 hours (Fig. 4G), respectively, compared to that of healthy controls. The bladder was delineated in mice 10 min postinjection of ATRPH, with its brightness gradually increasing until the 60-min time point (Fig. 4E). At the 10-min postinjection time point, ATRPH showed a detectable NIRF signal in the bladder of ALI 12 hours mice, indicating the lung injury. By 60 min, the bladder signals in all ALI mice were notably brighter than those in healthy mice. ATRPH enabled detection of ALI as early as 3 hours, with a 1.6-fold higher signal in the bladder (ventral view) compared to healthy controls (Fig. 4H). To validate in vivo specificity, a negative probe (NGRPH) was synthesized. NGRPH shared a similar structure to ATRPH but lacked the NE-responsive peptide segment (fig. S17, C and D). The UV-vis absorption peaks of NGRPH remained unchanged, and no increase in fluorescence intensity was observed upon addition of NE or other analytes, indicating that NGRPH lacks NE responsiveness (fig. S27). Following NGRPH injection, no obvious NIRF signals were observed in the lung or bladder of ALI 12 hours or healthy mice (fig. S28). Moreover, lung and bladder NIRF signals in ALI 12 hours mice preinjected with a high concentration of NE inhibitor (sivelestat) were consistent with the healthy control group (fig. S29). These data indicate that ATRPH responds specifically to NE activity within the pneumonia microenvironment and is capable of detecting lung injury at very early stages.

Ex vivo optical urinalysis of ALI progression using ATRPH was then evaluated. To further evaluate the feasibility of earlier urine-based detection, we stimulated micturition and successfully collected urine 2 hours after probe administration. As shown in fig. S30, the urine fluorescence signal in the ALI 12 hours group was more than 2.0-fold higher than that of the healthy group. However, because of the low success rate of stimulated urine collection, we adopted the 12-hour metabolic cage method for consistency in subsequent experiments. Notably, the urine collected after 12 hours exhibited stronger fluorescence intensity than that obtained at 2 hours, although the signal-to-background ratio between ALI and healthy groups was comparable for both time points. To reduce variability in urinary fluorescence, we used IR820 as an internal reference to account for differences in renal excretion. This approach supports more accurate signal interpretation, and efforts are underway to identify clinically applicable normalization strategies. To rule out the possibility that the disease model affects probe excretion, we coadministered IR820 conjugated with PEG, a hydrophilic and “always-on” fluorescent probe, together with ATRPH as an internal reference. The results showed no notable differences in IR820 fluorescence intensity in either the lungs (fig. S31, A and B) or urine (fig. S30, C to E). Urine samples were collected from mice 12 and 24 hours postinjection of ATRPH. As shown in Fig. 4J and fig. S32, urine collected 12 hours postinjection of ATRPH from mice pretreated with LPS for 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours exhibited 0.8-, 6.5-, 6.9-, 15.6-, and 5.5-fold higher NIRF signals, respectively, compared to healthy mice. Similarly, urine collected 24 hours postinjection of ATRPH showed 1.1-, 5.0-, 6.7-, 25.2-, and 8.4-fold higher signals for the same LPS-treated 1.5-, 3-, 6-, 12-, and 24-hour groups, respectively, compared to healthy mice. Urinary ATRPH enabled the detection of lung injury as early as 3 hours postinfection, outperforming CT imaging, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) cytokine assays, and H&E staining in early diagnostic sensitivity (Fig. 2, fig. S9, and Fig. 4J).

To further validate the accuracy of urine-based ATRPH detection for pneumonia and to exclude potential interference from inflammation in other organs, models of cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) and alcohol-induced acute liver injury (ALIi) were established (55, 56). The AKI group exhibited abnormal kidney function indicators (fig. S33, A to C) represented by plasma levels of creatinine, uric acid, and urea nitrogen. Compared to the healthy group, AKI mice showed elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) cytokines in renal tissues. Mice with combined renal-lung injury (AKI + ALI) demonstrated increased levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ cytokines in both lung and kidney tissues (fig. S33, D and E). Real-time imaging revealed that the AKI group showed no increase in NIRF signals in the lung, bladder, and kidney compared to the healthy group. In the AKI + ALI group, NIRF signals in the lungs, bladder, and urine increased relative to the healthy group, while kidney fluorescence signals remained unchanged (figs. S34 to S36). In the ALIi group, liver function indicators represented by alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase exhibited abnormalities (fig. S37, A to C). In the damaged organs of mice with combined acute liver-lung injury (ALIi + ALI) and ALIi mice, cytokine levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ were notably elevated (fig. S37, D and E). Similar to the AKI group, the liver, bladder, and isolated urine NIRF signals in the ALIi group showed no notable differences compared to healthy mice (figs. S38 to S40). Although ALIi mice exhibited increased hepatic NE and MPO levels (fig. S40, C and D), the majority of the intratracheally administered ATRPH bypassed hepatic metabolism. Consequently, hepatitis would not affect the detection of pneumonia using ATRPH. These results demonstrate that ATRPH urinalysis remains highly specific to lung inflammation, enabling early, sensitive, and accurate detection of ALI without interference from nonpulmonary inflammatory conditions.

The accuracy of ATRPH for ALI detection was further evaluated. Simple linear regression modeling confirmed a positive correlation (R2 = 0.88, ρ = 0.94) between NE+ cell populations and NIRF signals in the lungs (Fig. 4K), as well as between NE+ cell populations and urinary NIRF signals (R2 = 0.84, ρ = 0.92; fig. S41). Principal components analysis (PCA) clearly distinguished between healthy and ALI mice following ATRPH injection (Fig. 4L). Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed that the areas under the curve for the NIRF signal in the lungs, bladder, and 12-hour urine samples were 0.87, 0.69, and 0.99, respectively (Fig. 4M and fig. S42). Note that the accuracy of detecting lung injury using in vivo bladder imaging data was lower because of the variability in murine voluntary excretion. In addition, the sensitivity of ATRPH for ex vivo fluid detection was also evaluated. To simulate NE detection in clinical human samples, the limit of detection of ATRPH for NE was determined in artificial human plasma and urine. Artificial human plasma contained albumin, glucose, cholesterol, K+, Na+, and other components, with pH 7.4. Artificial human urine contained urea, creatinine, uric acid, K+, Na+, and other components, with pH 5.6. The limits of detection were calculated to be 107 mU/liter (26.7 μg/liter) in plasma and 148 mU/liter (37.0 μg/liter) in urine (fig. S43, A and B), which were notably lower than the NE levels reported for patients with pneumonia in plasma (220 μg/liter) (29, 32) and urine (215 μg/liter) (31). Subsequently, ATRPH was incubated with plasma and urine from healthy and ALI 12 hours mice. The fluorescence signals in the ALI group were elevated by 1.3-fold in plasma and 1.2-fold in urine compared to the healthy group (fig. S43, C and D). These findings support the potential of ATRPH for noninvasive detection using clinically accessible body fluids. Overall, these findings confirm the high accuracy of ATRPH in detecting early lung injury by in situ and urine, as well as the potential for noninvasive detection using clinically accessible body fluids.

Drug screening and dosage optimization in ALI treatment

After validating the early and sensitive diagnostic capability of ATRPH for ALI, its efficacy was further evaluated for therapeutic drug screening and dosage optimization in ALI treatment. To mitigate ALI, mice were treated with various clinical drugs (Fig. 5A), including ulinastatin, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), Xuebijing, dexameth, and sivelestat, 1 hour after ALI induction by LPS. Ulinastatin (57), a nonpeptide protease inhibitor, is known for its enzyme inhibitory, anti-inflammatory, and organ-protective effects and is commonly used in treating critical diseases such as pancreatitis and sepsis. LMWH (58) has been investigated for its anti-inflammatory effects, particularly its ability to inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis. Xuebijing (59), a proprietary Chinese medicine, has been shown to reduce inflammation by antagonizing LPS and is approved for use in treating COVID-19. Dexameth (60), an adrenocorticotropic hormone, is commonly used as an anti-inflammatory agent in clinical practice. Sivelestat (34), an NE inhibitor, has been shown in clinical studies to improve lung function in patients with lung injury.

After 24 hours of mitigation, ATRPH was administered intratracheally into the mice. Real-time imaging revealed that lungs treated with ulinastatin, LMWH, Xuebijing, dexameth, and sivelestat had 38, 59, 83, 93, and 89% reduction in lung NIRF signals, respectively, at the 45-min postinjection time point (Fig. 5, B and C, and fig. S44) compared to untreated ALI mice. Similarly, ex vivo imaging of isolated lungs treated with ulinastatin, LMWH, Xuebijing, dexameth, and sivelestat showed 41, 72, 79, 87, and 97% reduction in NIRF signals (fig. S45), respectively, compared to untreated ALI mice. Furthermore, urine samples from mice treated with ulinastatin, LMWH, Xuebijing, dexameth, and sivelestat showed 6, 22, 43, 8, and 62% reduction in NIRF signals (Fig. 5D and fig. S45D), respectively, compared to untreated ALI mice. These data suggested the potential of ATRPH for screening ALI treatments.

The efficacy of ATRPH for treatment screening was further confirmed by flow cytometry analysis. As shown in fig. S46, neutrophil populations and NE content in the lungs of ALI mice returned to near-healthy levels after treatment with Xuebijing, dexameth, and sivelestat, while ulinastatin and LMWH were less effective. Similar trends were observed for MPO levels in the lungs (fig. S47A). The levels of NE, TNF-α, IL-6, granzyme B (GZMB), and IL-2 obtained by ELISA tests did not accurately reflect the effectiveness of treatments (fig. S47, B and C). PCA showed that ATRPH-based lung imaging effectively differentiated between healthy, ALI, and treated mice (Fig. 5E). Both in vivo and ex vivo NIRF signals (Fig. 5, B and C, and figs. S44 and S45) correlated well with flow cytometry and MPO results (figs. S46 and S47), indicating that ATRPH could be used for screening therapeutic drugs through in situ imaging and urinalysis.

The application of ATRPH in adjuvant drug dosage selection was further validated using low, medium, and high concentrations of ulinastatin (ulinastatin1, ulinastatin2, and ulinastatin3) for the treatment of ALI mice. As shown in Fig. 5 (F and G) and fig. S48, higher concentrations of ulinastatin resulted in lower NIRF signals in the lung. For example, NIRF signals of the lung treated with ulinastatin1, ulinastatin2, and ulinastatin3 were 1.4-, 1.7-, and 3.8-fold lower than that of untreated ALI mice, respectively. NIRF signals from isolated lungs and urine samples were consistent with flow cytometry, indicating that the highest concentration (ulinastatin3) was most effective in treating ALI (Fig. 5H and figs. S49 to S51). In addition, ATRPH provided a more accurate assessment of drug dosage compared to NE and cytokine levels measured by ELISA. PCA showed that ATRPH effectively differentiated between the different ulinastatin treatment concentrations, with the ulinastatin3 group showing a return to healthy status (Fig. 5I). These data confirm that ATRPH has a high discriminatory ability for ALI, can accurately assess drug therapeutic effects, assists in dosage selection, and has the potential to predict ALI prognosis.

Exploring mechanisms of NE production in LPS-induced ALI

It is well established that NE serves as a biomarker during LPS-induced ALI. In addition, there is a correlation between LPS and the binding of its primary receptor Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) (61), the protein kinase C (PKC) pathway (62), ROS generation (61), peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 enzyme (PAD4) activation (63), citrullination of histone H3 (CitH3) (58), and the production of NE (63). However, the precise pathways through which LPS induces NE production and the interdependence of these pathways remain unclear. Given the strong correlation between ATRPH activation and NE content in the lungs, ATRPH was used to investigate the processes and underlying mechanisms of NE production.

Mice were intratracheally treated with resatorvid [TLR4 inhibitor (I-TLR4)], ruboxistaurin [PKC inhibitor (I-PKC)], acetylcysteine [ROS inhibitor (I-ROS)], and simvastatin [PAD4 inhibitor (I-PAD4)] 1 hour after LPS-induced ALI. Whole-body fluorescence imaging, urinalysis, and lung expression of TLR4, PKC, ROS, PAD4, CitH3, and NE pathways were conducted 24 hours posttreatment. NIRF signal reductions of 7.8-, 6.7-, 5.8-, and 4.5-fold were observed in the lungs of the I-TLR4–, I-PKC–, I-ROS–, and I-PAD4–treated groups compared to the untreated ALI group, respectively (Fig. 6, A and B, and fig. S52). Similarly, urine NIRF signals were reduced by 87, 75, 73, and 45% in the I-TLR4–, I-PKC–, I-ROS–, and I-PAD4–treated groups compared to the untreated ALI group, respectively (Fig. 6, C and D), indicating that inhibition of TLR4, PKC, ROS, and PAD4 pathways resulted in reduced NE production.

Flow cytometry analysis provided further insights into these effects (Fig. 6, E to K). TLR4 expression in the lungs of ALI mice showed a 31% reduction upon treatment with I-TLR4 but was minimally affected by I-PKC, I-ROS, and I-PAD4 (Fig. 6F). PKC expression decreased 1.4- and 1.6-fold following I-TLR4 and I-PKC treatments, respectively, but remained unchanged with I-ROS and I-PAD4 (Fig. 6G). ROS levels were reduced by 1.2-fold with I-ROS and moderately decreased with I-TLR4 and I-PKC treatments (Fig. 6H). PAD4 expression was reduced by 1.3-, 1.4-, 1.1-, and 1.2-fold following I-TLR4, I-PKC, I-ROS, and I-PAD4 treatments, respectively (Fig. 6I). All inhibitors markedly affected the expression of CitH3 and NE (Fig. 6, J and K). These findings suggest that during neutrophil infiltration, TLR4 acts upstream of the PKC, ROS, and PAD4 pathways, with PKC downstream of TLR4 and upstream of ROS and PAD4, while ROS and PAD4 are downstream of PKC. A strong correlation was observed between NIRF signals from the bladder, lung, urine, and flow cytometry results (Fig. 6L). Combining these results, the proposed signaling cascade for LPS-induced neutrophil infiltration and function in ALI is illustrated in Fig. 6M. Overall, as a tool for rapid and accurate detection of NE, ATRPH visualized NE production and provided a convenient method for validating the mechanism of protein production in complex pathways. In addition, this integrative approach, combining ATRPH real-time imaging and flow cytometry, provided detailed insights into the underlying mechanisms of NE production in the LPS-induced ALI model.

Imaging of other pulmonary diseases

The pathological mechanisms of pneumonia are diverse, arising from bacterial, viral, or fungal infections, as well as from inhalation injury or dysregulated immune responses. To explore the applicability of ATRPH in different pulmonary diseases, the bacterial infection model using methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (64), a viral pneumonia infection model using influenza virus A/PR/8/34 (PR8) (65), and a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis (BLM) model (66) that captures features of chronic lung injury were established to more closely mimic the microenvironmental characteristics of clinical pneumonia. Following successful model establishment, ATRPH was administered intratracheally for in vivo real-time imaging. Sixty minutes postinjection of ATRPH, lung NIRF signals in the MRSA and PR8 groups increased by 4.1- and 2.1-fold, respectively, while bladder NIRF signals increased by 5.15- and 8.5-fold, respectively, compared to the healthy group (Fig. 7, A to C). No notable fluorescence signal was observed in the lungs of BLM mice compared to healthy mice (Fig. 7, A to C). Similarly, compared with the healthy group, ex vivo imaging of isolated lungs in the MRSA and PR8 groups showed 3.8- and 2.2-fold increases in NIRF signals, while no increase was observed in the BLM group (Fig. 7D and fig. S53). Urine NIRF signals increased by 6.4- and 2.3-fold in the MRSA and PR8 groups, respectively, compared to healthy mice, while no notable change was observed in the BLM group (Fig. 7E and fig. S53D). Consistent with flow cytometry analysis, bacterial and viral infections notably increased neutrophil and NE+ cell populations in lung tissue. In BLM-induced fibrotic mouse lungs, neutrophils showed a slight increase, while NE+ cell populations remained indistinguishable from healthy mice (Fig. 7, E and G, and fig. S54). Levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-2, and NE, detected by ELISA, along with pulmonary MPO levels, exhibited similar trends (Fig. 7, H and I). Because of lower NE expression and/or limited probe sensitivity in chronic inflammation (fibrosis), ATRPH currently only enables detection of pulmonary infections or acute inflammation.

Development of ATRPs for real-time imaging of ALI

To better meet various in vivo administration requirements for real-time detection of ALI, we conjugated DSPE-PEG2K-alkyne to the NERP side chain, yielding NERPD (fig. S55A), and optimized its injectable formulation to develop ATRPs that can be administered intravenously to detect inflammation.

First, the potential application of NERPD for in vivo real-time imaging of ALI via intravenous administration routes was evaluated. NERPD (5 μmol/kg) was administered intravenously into healthy and ALI mice induced by LPS for 12 hours. Imaging results (fig. S56) showed no notable NIRF signal enhancement in the lungs of ALI mice, which may be due to less NERPD reaching the lungs (fig. S57C).

According to the literature (15, 20), the introduction of a permanently cationic lipid enhances the accumulation of cargoes in target organs in vivo. On the basis of this, we designed ATRPL (with cationic lipid introduction) using the thin-film hydration method (fig. S55B), which consisted of 1,2-dioctadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, dimethyl dioctadecylammonium bromide (DDAB), and cholesterol (67). By adjusting the percentage of the permanently cationic lipid DDAB, which contains quaternary amino groups and serves as the lung-targeting functional segment, three ATRPL variants (ATRPL1 to ATRPL3; DDAB addition percentage at 55, 71, and 80%) were prepared and compared (68). As shown in table S2, the diameter of ATRPL increased with the DDAB doping percentage. Although the variant with 81% DDAB exhibited the best lung-targeting capability, it also resulted in higher toxicity (fig. S58). Therefore, the ATRPL variant with a DDAB content of 71% was chosen as the optimal balance between targeting efficiency and safety. As shown in fig. S59, ATRPL exhibited a spherical morphology and was NE-responsive. Then, the potential application of ATRPL (5 μmol/kg) for imaging of ALI via intravenous administration was evaluated. ATRPL exhibited intensified NIRF signals in the lungs of ALI mice within 5 min. At the 45-min postinjection time point, ATRPL showed 1.7-fold higher NIRF signals in the lungs and a 1.4-fold increase in the bladders compared to healthy controls (figs. S60 and S61A). Ex vivo urinalysis of ATRPL showed 3.3-fold higher activation in ALI mice than in healthy mice (fig. S62), suggesting the potential of ATRPL as convenient renal reporters for ALI. In addition, flow cytometry analysis revealed a notable increase in Ly6G+CyOH+ cell populations in the lungs of ALI mice (fig. S61B), indicating the activation of ATRPL alongside neutrophils. These data suggest that in vivo and in vitro urinary fluorescence signal changes in combination with ATRPL enable monitoring of the severity of inflammation in target organs.

Given the high adjustability of NERPD, ATRPM (with membrane decoration) was prepared using the membrane-insertion method (69), incorporating LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 membranes and NERPD (fig. S55C). It was expected that ATRPM could achieve targeted detection of inflammation through homologous targeting of inflammatory cell membranes. ATRPM displayed similar optical properties and spherical morphologies to ATRPH, with 50-fold fluorescence enhancement upon NE addition (fig. S63). Following systemic administration, ATRPM demonstrated lung-targeting capabilities compared to single-component NERPD, showing a 1.3-fold signal enhancement upon activation and accumulation in the lungs (figs. S64 and S65). Notably, ATRPM exhibited stronger fluorescence in the liver (fig. S64A), which can be attributed to both the varying tissue depths of lungs and livers during in vivo imaging and due to the influence of LPS-induced inflammation affecting both the lung and liver (fig. S66). In addition to in situ imaging, 3.6-fold higher activated NIRF signals were observed in the urine of ALI mice than healthy mice (fig. S67), suggesting the potential of ATRPM as convenient renal reporters for systemic inflammation. These data suggest the potential of ATRPM for detecting inflammation in various in vivo organs.

In summary, because of the different targeting mechanisms of cationic lipids and membrane-based formulations, ATRPL showed a greater lung targeting capacity and was more suitable for intravenous administration for ALI detection compared to ATRPM. ATRPM showed better inflammation targeting ability and was more suitable for intravenous administration to detect the severity of systemic organ inflammation.

Overall, ATRPs were designed for pneumonia detection, starting from pneumonia biomarkers and combining different routes of administration (Fig. 8, A and B). When injected into mice, ATRPs are cleaved by NE in the inflammatory lung microenvironment, resulting in fluorescence activation and the release of fluorescent fragments (Fig. 8C). The hydrophilic PEG moiety in ATRPs facilitates renal clearance, allowing for both in situ molecular imaging and urinalysis of ALI. Experimental results demonstrate that ATRPs enable accurate detection of lung injury. Compared with clinical lung injury detection methods (ELISA, CT, and H&E staining) and other papers, ATRPs had the advantages of being noninvasive, radioactive-free, and versatile to detect lung injury earlier (table S3). In addition, it can also be used to evaluate the therapeutic effect of LMWH, Xuebijing, dexameth, sivelestat, and ulinastatin in treating ALI, as well as to study the mechanisms of NE production in ALI (Fig. 8D).

DISCUSSION

The detection of pneumonia remains notably challenging because of the disease’s multifaceted nature and the inherent limitations of current diagnostic modalities, such as radiological imaging and microbial cultures, which often lack the requisite specificity and sensitivity for early detection. To address these limitations, we conducted an in-depth bioinformatics analysis and identified NE as a key biomarker in pneumonia pathogenesis. Leveraging this discovery, we designed and synthesized a NERP, which was subsequently developed into ATRPs for diverse diagnostic applications. NERP exhibited a 10-fold increase in fluorescence upon NE activation, with a sevenfold signal enhancement in neutrophils relative to nontarget cells, achieving an overall detection accuracy of nearly 95%. This suggests its strong potential as a highly specific and sensitive tool for detecting NE activity and monitoring neutrophil dynamics in pneumonia.

Following in vivo administration, ATRPs reliably detected NE activity with notable signal amplification and minimal background interference, resulting in an up to 8.5-fold increase in fluorescence intensity within inflamed lung tissues. This confirms their reliability as real-time imaging agents. The NERP platform may also be applicable to other acute inflammatory conditions characterized by neutrophil activation, such as bacterial and viral pneumonia. With further tuning of probe sensitivity, it holds promise for detecting neutrophil-driven inflammation in selected chronic lung diseases.

During drug screening and dosage optimization, treated models showed an 80% reduction in lung fluorescence, correlating with therapeutic efficacy. ATRPs further facilitated mechanistic studies, revealing a strong correlation (R2 = 0.92) between NE activity and neutrophil infiltration, with a sevenfold increase in fluorescence, specifically within neutrophils, suggesting their accuracy in monitoring disease progression at the molecular level.

Beyond in vivo imaging, ATRPs excelled in urine-based diagnostics. ATRP-based urinalysis exhibited a 6.5-fold increase in fluorescence, enabling the detection of lung injury as early as 3 hours postinsult, notably earlier than traditional methods, which typically detect changes after 12 to 24 hours. The correlation between urinary fluorescence and NE activity in the lungs was strong (R2 = 0.84), with an early detection efficacy of 95%, surpassing other techniques that often suffer from reduced sensitivity in the initial stages. The urine-based approach also mitigates the tissue penetration limitations of near-infrared imaging, ensuring reliable detection regardless of lung injury depth or location. Compared with clinical lung injury detection methods (ELISA, CT, and H&E staining) and other papers, ATRPs had the advantages of being noninvasive, radioactive-free, and versatile to detect lung injury earlier (table S3).

In summary, this study successfully integrates bioinformatics-driven biomarker identification with the design and optimization of molecular imaging probes to address pneumonia diagnostic challenges. By identifying NE as a critical biomarker, we developed NERPs and refined them into ATRPs, which demonstrated outstanding performance in both in vivo imaging and urine-based detection. These probes offer a highly sensitive, specific, and noninvasive solution for early diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring of lung injury. In addition, ATRPs have shown the potential to enhance therapeutic precision and advance our understanding of underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. This integrative approach represents a notable advancement in precision medicine, offering powerful tools for the clinical management of pneumonia and extending their applicability to other inflammatory lung diseases.

However, several issues remain to be addressed for future clinical applications. There is a need to optimize particle size and formulation for effective pulmonary targeting by making pulmonary drug delivery more closely resemble the clinical approach of nebulization or dry powder inhalation. To achieve the feasibility of probe synthesis at scale and human applicability of the probe, it is necessary to identify clinically approved NE-responsive groups, fluorescence quenching strategies, and fluorescent probes and conduct biocompatibility evaluations in larger animal models. In this study, an attempt was made to optimize the formulation for intravenous delivery, which is closer to clinical administration; however, optimized organ targeting materials are desired for improving lung accumulation of probes. On the other hand, to facilitate the clinical translation of the probe, we tried to use the probe for body fluid detection, and how to quantify and facilitate the results of body fluid detection and establish the correlation with inflammation is still a direction we need to work on. Regarding the diagnosis of pneumonia, the study tried to use the ALI, MRSA, PR8, and BLM models as representatives of acute and chronic inflammation. Although this probe can distinguish between infectious and chronic inflammation in mouse lungs, clinical human samples are still lacking to validate its imaging utility for human NE. Establishing high-throughput workflow for drug screening requires increasing the number of samples tested, developing criteria for evaluating drug efficacy, and constructing image-to-digital signal conversion techniques to enable big data-assisted high-throughput drug screening. However, more large animal models and lung disease models are needed to confirm the feasibility of the probe. As for the effect of excretion differences on the accuracy of urine testing, an uncaged fluorescent probe was introduced as an internal reference in this study, and it is necessary to find more accurate urine internal reference to calibrate the urine test results. In addition, the relatively high signal-to-noise ratio remains a limitation of the current imaging system. Developing activatable NIR-II, magnetic resonance imaging, and CT probes for NE detection could potentially enhance both the accuracy and sensitivity of pneumonia detection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance to the Xiamen University Laboratory Animal Management Ethics Committee (approval nos. XMULAC20230020 and XMULAC20220025).

Establishment and characterization of ALI model

ALI was established in mice by intratracheal injection of LPS (8 mg/kg) according to the previous method. The healthy group was injected with the same volume of PBS intratracheally, and the modeling group was euthanized after intratracheal administration for 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours. Afterward, a portion of lungs from the healthy and ALI 12 hours groups was collected for gene sequencing analysis. One part was homogenized with nylon gauze to obtain a single-cell suspension, which was stained using anti–CD45-PE, anti–CD11b-BV650, anti–Ly6G-APC-Cy7, primary NE antibody, and secondary Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G antibody and analyzed with a BD FACS Celesta multicolor cell analyzer. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software X. One part was made into lung tissue homogenate to test NE, TNF-α, IL-2, IL-6, and MPO content in mouse lungs using kits. One part was given to Nanjing Freethinking Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China), to make sections and then underwent H&E, TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick end labeling), and IF staining. The lung injury score is a double-blind method based on alveolar capillary congestion, hemorrhage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and alveolar wall thickness [0, no injury; 1, mild injury (<25% of the lesion range); 2, moderate injury (25 to 50% of the lesion range); 3, severe injury (51 to 75% of the lesion range); 4, very severe injury (>75% of the lesion range)], and the lung injury was scored as the sum of the scores of the four indicators. The score is the sum of the four index scores. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified using ImageJ 1.52a.

In vivo real-time imaging and ex vivo organ imaging

The hair was removed from the chest and hindlegs of the mice using depilatory cream, and then the healthy, ALI, drug treatment, and pathway validation groups were established according to the corresponding methods. ATRPH (2 μmol/kg) was intratracheally injected 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours after ALI modeling (ALI 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours group) and into healthy mice, and real-time NIRF imaging was performed 2, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min postinjection. ALI modeling was performed 12 hours later and in the healthy group by intratracheal injection of NGRPH (2 μmol/kg) or IR820 (1 μmol/kg), and real-time NIRF imaging was performed 2, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min postinjection. The ALI + sivelestat group received intratracheal injection of sivelestat (20 mg/kg) before ALI 12 hours modeling for 1 hour, followed by intratracheal injections of ATRPH (2 μmol/kg). ALI modeling was performed 12 hours later and in the healthy group by intravenous injection of NERPD (4 μmol/kg), ATRPL (4 μmol/kg), or ATRPM (4 μmol/kg), and real-time NIRF imaging was performed 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 45, and 60 min postinjection.

The treatment group received intratracheal injections of ulinastatin (2 mg/kg), LMWH (300 U/kg), Xuebijing (1.25 ml/kg), dexameth (10 mg/kg), and sivelestat (10 mg/kg) 1 hour after ALI modeling. The dose screening group was injected with ulinastatin (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg; 1, 2, and 3) intratracheally after 1 hour of ALI modeling. The pathway validation group had intratracheal injections of resatorvid (I-TLR4; 3 mg/kg), ruboxistaurin (I-PKC; 5 mg/kg), acetylcysteine (I-ROS; 100 mg/kg), and simvastatin (I-PAD4; 5 mg/kg) after 1 hour of ALI modeling. Twenty-four hours later, real-time NIRF imaging was performed by intratracheal injection of ATRPH (2 μmol/kg) in the treatment, dose screening, and mechanism validation groups at 2, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min. ATRPH (2 μmol/kg) was intratracheally injected 12 hours after MRSA, PR8, BLM, ALIi, AKI, ALIi-ALI, and AKI-ALI modeling and into healthy mice, and real-time NIRF imaging was performed 2, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min postinjection.

After imaging, live mice were euthanized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were collected for imaging. Fluorescence images were all acquired using the IVIS spectral imaging system, and NIRF fluorescence signals from the lung and bladder in living mice were analyzed by the region-of-interest analysis using Living Image 4.3 software.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical calculations were performed using Origin2017 and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1, including model fitting and assumptions of tests used. Quantification of fluorescence signals in all in vivo and ex vivo region-of-interest regions was accomplished by Living Image 4.3 software. The average fluorescence intensity of confocal and IF images was determined using ImageJ 1.52a. For all data, the means ± standard deviation (SD) were expressed. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were tested by a two-tailed Student’s t test. For all tests, P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
