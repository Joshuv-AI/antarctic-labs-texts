An integrative defense mechanism mediated by GhBsr-d1L7 and GhERD10 confers resistance to Verticillium dahliae in cotton
Authors: Guoshuai Zhang, Chuanzong Li, Youzhong Li, Zongming Xie, Yanqing Bi, Xinyu Zhu, Jianfeng Lei, Peihong Dai, Xiaofeng Su, Yue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVerticillium wilt (VW), a devastating disease caused by the soil-inhabiting fungus Verticillium dahliae, poses a substantial threat to cotton cultivation worldwide by drastically impairing both yield and fiber quality. Although C₂H₂ zinc finger proteins (ZFPs) are known regulators of plant stress responses, their role in cotton immunity against V. dahliae remains largely unexplored.ResultsHere, we identified the C₂H₂ ZFP gene GhBsr-d1L7 through homology-based approaches, and investigated its function in disease resistance in upland cotton. Silencing GhBsr-d1L7 using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) rendered cotton plants more susceptible to V. dahliae, as evidenced by reduced levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), jasmonic acid (JA), lignin, and decreasing Catalase (CAT) activity. Conversely, heterologous overexpression of GhBsr-d1L7 in Arabidopsis enhanced resistance and activated associated defense responses. Transcriptome analysis indicated that silencing GhBsr-d1L7 altered the expression profiles of genes associated with hormone signaling, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and MAPK cascades. In addition, yeast two-hybrid screening identified GhERD10, a dehydrin-family protein, as a nuclear interactor of GhBsr-d1L7, with this interaction validated by luciferase complementation imaging and bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays. Notably, silencing GhERD10 significantly enhanced cotton resistance to V. dahliae, implicating its role as a negative regulator of plant immunity.ConclusionsTogether, these findings reveal a novel C₂H₂ ZFP-chaperone module (GhBsr-d1L7-GhERD10) that integrates physiological and transcriptional defenses to bolster cotton immunity. This study deepens our understanding of plant-pathogen interactions and identifies promising molecular targets for developing disease-resistant cotton varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02563-1.

Background

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) represents a cornerstone of the international textile industry, however, its production faces severe threats from Verticillium wilt (VW), a vascular disease caused by the soil-dwelling fungus Verticillium dahliae [1]. This pathogen produces melanized microsclerotia capable of persisting in soil for over two decades, thereby serving as long-term inoculum sources. After invading roots, V. dahliae colonizes root xylem vessels and secretes effector proteins that disrupt water transport, causing systemic wilting, yield losses of up to 80%, and considerable declines in fiber quality [2]. Conventional fungicides are largely ineffective due to their inability to penetrate lignified vascular tissues [3]. Similarly, agronomic practices such as crop rotation fail to mitigate infections due to the pathogen’s broad host range and persistent microsclerotia [4]. These challenges underscore the critical need to develop genetically resistant cotton cultivars, thereby prompting ongoing efforts to identify and characterize VW resistance genes [5].

Cotton’s defense against V. dahliae involves complex physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses. A critical early response is the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), primarily generated by NADPH oxidases such as GbRboh5 and GbRboh18. This activity leads to increased hydrogen peroxide in the apoplast, serving to activate systemic acquired resistance (SAR) throughout the plant [6, 7]. Lignin deposition, mediated by enzymes such as laccases (e.g., GhLac1) and peroxidases (e.g., GhDIR1), reinforces vascular tissues and physically limits pathogen spread [8, 9]. Hormonal signaling pathways further modulate defense responses. Salicylic acid (SA) signaling upregulates pathogenesis-related (PR) genes via ribosomal signaling pathways involving GaRPL18 [10]. Concurrently, JA, often acting synergistically with ethylene, activates defense-related transcription factors (TFs) such as GhWRKY70, which enhance JA biosynthesis through the regulation of GhAOS1 [11]. Although traditionally viewed as antagonistic, the roles of SA and JA pathways in vascular wilt diseases appear more complex, with evidence indicating context-dependent synergy [12, 13]. Additionally, TF families such as MYBs [14] and NACs [15, 16] have been implicated in VW resistance by modulating lignification and hormonal crosstalk. While individual pathway components have been increasingly characterized, how these pathways integrate and their regulation through post-translational mechanisms remain poorly understood.

C₂H₂-type zinc finger proteins (ZFPs) represent a prominent class of TFs defined by the conserved CX2–4CX3FX5LX2HX3–5H sequence motif. Members of this family are implicated in a wide array of plant responses to abiotic stresses. For example, PhZFP1 in petunia enhances freezing tolerance by modulating the expression of raffinose synthase [17], whereas Osbsr-d1 in rice stabilizes ROS levels by repressing MYB TFs associated with peroxidase activity [18]. Notably, some C₂H₂ ZFPs coordinate both abiotic and biotic stress responses. For instance, TaZFP1B in wheat enhances drought tolerance while simultaneously activating PR genes [19]. Although numerous studies have explored gene expression and functional responses during cotton-V. dahliae interactions, the roles of C₂H₂ ZFPs in this pathosystem remain uncharacterized, and the molecular mechanisms by which they influence defense signaling are not thoroughly understood.

Protein stability and nuclear localization, both critical for transcription factors to access their target genes, can be modulated by molecular chaperones. For example, in Arabidopsis, the autophagy receptor NBR1 recruits HSP90.1 and ROF1 to mediate the degradation of heat shock TFs, thereby modulating heat stress memory [20]. Conversely, in rice, OsDjA6 stabilizes MNAC3, a phosphatase that negatively regulates immunity-related TFs such as OsINO80, thus attenuating disease resistance [21]. These examples highlight the dual roles of chaperones, which can either facilitate or repress plant stress responses depending on the cellular context. However, the specific mechanisms of C₂H₂ ZFPs in cotton resistance to VW remain to be further explored.

In this study, we identified and functionally analyzed GhBsr-d1L7, a C₂H₂ ZFP gene in G. hirsutum, by leveraging sequence homology with OsBsr-d1-a rice gene recently linked to blast disease resistance [18, 22]. The role of GhBsr-d1L7 in cotton’s defense against V. dahliae was further explored. Silencing GhBsr-d1L7 via VIGS drastically impaired plant defense as evidenced by reduced ROS production, JA accumulation, lignin deposition, and CAT activity-collectively rendering plants hypersusceptible to V. dahliae infection. On the contrary, the exogenous overproduction of GhBsr-d1L7 in Arabidopsis resulted in increased tolerance, indicating functional conservation across species. Transcriptomic profiling implicated GhBsr-d1L7 as a central modulator integrating multiple defense pathways, including argets hormonal signaling, phenylpropanoid metabolism and MAPK signaling, with downstream targets such as PR1, PAL, and FLS2 identified. Further molecular assays, including bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening, established that GhBsr-d1L7 interacts in the nucleus with GhERD10, a dehydrin-family chaperone protein. Interestingly, GhERD10 negatively regulated disease resistance, potentially by destabilizing GhBsr-d1L7 or sequestering it away from its target promoters. These findings reveal a novel regulatory mechanism involving a ZFP-chaperone interaction in cotton immune signaling and significantly expand our understanding of transcriptional and post-translational control of VW resistance.

Results

GhBsr-d1L7 encodes a nuclear-localized protein with two C2H2 zinc finger domains

To characterize the potential function of GhBsr-d1L7, which was identified through homologous alignment with the rice blast resistance regulator OsBsr-d1, we performed systematic bioinformatic analyses. This gene, positioned on chromosome D12 of upland cotton, lacks introns and encodes a protein comprising 233 amino acids. Sequence analysis revealed that GhBsr-d1L7 contains two C2H2 zinc finger domains, which are predicted to adopt the classical β-β-α fold (Fig. 1A-B). Phylogenetic analysis grouped the protein into one of seven distinct evolutionary subgroups, with the tobacco protein AAQ54303.1 showing the closest evolutionary relationship to GhBsr-d1L7. Notably, AAQ54303.1 is known to enhance plant immunity in tobacco by mediating responses to SsCut, a cutinase secreted by Sclerotinia sclerotiorum [23]. To determine the subcellular localization of GhBsr-d1L7, we transiently co-expressed GhBsr-d1L7 fused to GFP alongside various organelle markers in Agrobacterium-infiltrated tobacco mesophyll cells. Confocal microscopy showed that GhBsr-d1L7 localized exclusively to the nucleus, as confirmed by co-localization with the nuclear marker H2B-mCherry. In contrast, the control GFP signal was distributed across multiple organelles (Fig. 1D). These results confirm that GhBsr-d1L7 is a nuclear-localized protein.

GhBsr-d1L7 mediates cotton’s defense against V. dahliae

To elucidate the function of GhBsr-d1L7 in cotton’s resistance to VW, we analyzed its expression in roots, stems, and leaves over a 48-h period following inoculation with V. dahliae. GhBsr-d1L7 was expressed across all sampled tissues, with the highest levels observed in roots (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). In roots, GhBsr-d1L7 expression peaked at 1 hpi, reaching a 75-fold increase compared with the control. In stems, expression significantly increased, peaking at 46-fold at 12 hpi. Leaves exhibited 54- and 46-fold increases in GhBsr-d1L7 expression at 1 and 12 hpi, respectively (Fig. 2A). We next utilized VIGS in G. hirsutum cv. Xinluzao 27 to evaluate the involvement of GhBsr-d1L7 in conferring resistance to VW. Two weeks after infecting plants with our control vector, pTRV2::GhCLA1, seedlings exhibited intense bleaching in their new leaves, signifying the successful implementation of the VIGS system under our experimental conditions (Fig. 2B). GhBsr-d1L7 expression was significantly downregulated in pTRV2::GhBsr-d1L7 plants, exhibiting a 61% silencing efficiency compared to seedlings inoculated with the empty vector control, pTRV2::00 (Fig. 2C). After a two-week treatment period with V. dahliae, the pTRV2::GhBsr-d1L7 cotton displayed exacerbated symptoms, including leaf wilting and vascular bundle browning, compared to control plants (Fig. 2D). Disease severity, as measured by the disease index, was significantly higher in the GhBsr-d1L7 knockdown group (Fig. 2E), and fungal quantification revealed a fivefold increase in pathogen biomass in these plants relative to controls (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that suppressing GhBsr-d1L7 expression compromises cotton’s defense capacity against V. dahliae.

Silencing of GhBsr-d1L7 disrupts ROS accumulation, lignin deposition, and JA signaling in cotton

To investigate how GhBsr-d1L7 contributes to resistance against V. dahliae, we examined ROS production, CAT activity, lignin accumulation, and JA content in GhBsr-d1L7-silenced cotton plants. Under both experimental conditions-treatment with sterile water or inoculation with Vd991-GhBsr-d1L7-silenced plants consistently exhibited significantly lower ROS accumulation compared to control plants (Fig. 3A). Similarly, both CAT activity and lignin levels were significantly diminished in the silenced plants (Fig. 3B-D). Moreover, silencing GhBsr-d1L7 led to a significant decrease in JA levels (Fig. 3E). Overexpression of GhBsr-d1L7 in arabidopsis enhances resistance and defense-related physiological responses

We next generated Arabidopsis thaliana transgenic lines expressing GhBsr-d1L7 under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter to explore the contribution of GhBsr-d1L7 to disease resistance mechanisms (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). RT-PCR analysis confirmed the successful expression of GhBsr-d1L7 in transgenic lines, and two independent events, OE-GhBsr-d1L7−1 and OE-GhBsr-d1L7−2, were selected for further analysis (Fig. 4A). Seven days post-inoculation (dpi) with V. dahliae, WT plants displayed pronounced symptoms of leaf wilting, whereas OE-GhBsr-d1L7−1 and OE-GhBsr-d1L7−2 lines exhibited milder symptoms and retained a greater proportion of healthy foliage (Fig. 4B-C). Fungal biomass was substantially reduced in OE-GhBsr-d1L7−1 and OE-GhBsr-d1L7−2 lines compared to WT (Fig. 4D), confirming that GhBsr-d1L7 positively regulates VW resistance in Arabidopsis. Physiological and biochemical tests demonstrated that treatment with water and Vd991 resulted in significantly higher levels of lignin, JA, CAT enzyme activity, and ROS in OE-GhBsr-d1L7−1 and OE-GhBsr-d1L7−2 Arabidopsis compared to WT (Fig. 4E-H). Multi-omics characterization of GhBsr-d1L7-mediated defense mechanisms

To delineate the molecular basis of GhBsr-d1L7-mediated VW resistance, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on VW-infected roots from pTRV2::00 and pTRV2::GhBsr-d1L7 plants. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between transcriptomes of control (pTRV2::00) and GhBsr-d1L7-silenced plants, indicating distinct global gene expression profiles induced by GhBsr-d1L7 knockdown. Additionally, correlation analysis demonstrated high reproducibility among biological replicates within each group (Additional file 1: Fig. S3), supporting the reliability of the RNA-seq data. Analysis of the transcriptome revealed 2,008 genes displaying differential expression, including 896 upregulated and 1,112 downregulated transcripts (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Validation by RT-qPCR confirmed that the expression patterns of representative genes were consistent with the RNA-seq results (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily localized to the membrane and cell pcriphcry, involved in catalytic activity and oxidoreductase activity, and significantly enriched in biological processes such as response to stimulus and response to chemical (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signaling, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and MAPK cascades (Fig. 5B). Transcriptome analysis of GhBsr-d1L7-silenced cotton plants following V. dahliae inoculation revealed systemic disruption of their multidimensional defense network. Within phytohormone pathways, auxin and gibberellin signaling exhibited gene expression divergence (bidirectional regulation of AUC and DELLA genes), while cytokinin response factors B-ARR, the abscisic acid (ABA) transcriptional activator ABF, the JA pathway suppressor JAZ, and the TF MYC2 were significantly upregulated. In contrast, the ethylene signaling negative regulators EBF1/2 were sharply downregulated, and the brassinosteroid (BR) receptor BAK1 along with its downstream cell wall modification gene TCH4 were markedly suppressed, indicating disruption of hormonal crosstalk balance (Fig. 5C, Additional file 1: Fig. S7A). The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway displayed a characteristic "midstream activation and terminal obstruction" pattern: expression of upstream synthesis genes phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), and coumaroyl ester 3-hydroxylase (C3H) was generally downregulated. Key midstream enzymes for lignin monomer synthesis-caffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT), cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), and ferulate 5-hydroxylase (F5H)-showed enhanced expression. However, terminal peroxidases (PRX), responsible for lignin polymerization, exhibited significantly reduced gene expression (Fig. 5D, Additional file 1: Fig. S7B). This led to metabolic flux accumulation towards G/S-type lignin monomers with blocked final polymerization, further exacerbated by the differential expression of hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (HCT). The MAPK signaling cascade was severely compromised. Pathogen recognition-associated protein FLS2, BAK1, kinase cascade components WRKY22, MPK1/2, MAPKKK17/18, and the ethylene signaling node EBF1/2 were significantly downregulated. Although ethylene synthase ACS6 and the JA regulator MYC2 were upregulated, downstream defense effector genes-pathogenesis-related protein PR1, chitinase ChiB, reactive oxygen species-generating enzyme RbohD, and the ABA signaling negative regulator PP2C-exhibited contradictory fluctuations (Fig. 5E, Additional file 1: Fig. S7C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that loss of GhBsr-d1L7 culminates in the collapse of cotton resistance to VW through a triple mechanism, disrupting hormonal coordination, blocking terminal phenylpropanoid polymerization (notably via PRX downregulation), and dismantling MAPK defense signaling transduction.

Nuclear interaction of GhBsr-d1L7 and GhERD10

To explore the regulatory mechanism of GhBsr-d1L7 in VW resistance, we performed a Y2H screen and identified a dehydrin family protein, GhERD10, as an interacting partner of GhBsr-d1L7. This physical interaction was confirmed independently with additional assays. An interaction between these two proteins was also observed in a targeted Y2H assay (Fig. 6A), while firefly Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI) detected fluorescence only when GhBsr-d1L7 and GhERD10 were co-expressed in tobacco leaf cells (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, BiFC assays revealed yellow fluorescence localized in the nucleus, confirming that GhERD10 interacts with GhBsr-d1L7 in this compartment (Fig. 6C). Silencing of GhERD10 enhances cotton resistance to VW

Bioinformatic analysis revealed that GhERD10 (XP_016702923.2) encodes a dehydrin-family chaperone protein consisting of 199 amino acids, with a predicted molecular weight of 19.8 kDa and isoelectric point (pI) of 5.42. Multiple sequence alignment with orthologs from Arabidopsis (AtDEH1, AtDEH2), and Oryza sativa (OsSK3, OsDNH1) demonstrated high conservation of the motif (EKKGFLVKIKEKLPG), a characteristic feature of dehydrins involved in stress protection (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). Phylogenetic analysis showing closest evolutionary relationship with its G.hirsutum ortholog (KAK8369423.1) (Additional file 1: Fig. S9). Discussion

C₂H₂ ZFPs are widely recognized for their involvement in abiotic stress responses, however, their contribution to immunity against vascular pathogens has remained largely unexplored [24]. Here, we identify GhBsr-d1L7, a previously uncharacterized C₂H₂ ZFP in upland cotton, as a key positive regulator of resistance against V. dahliae. Consistent with the known roles of ZFPs-such as ZFP36 in wheat, which enhances antioxidant defenses through superoxide dismutase (SOD) activation [25–27], and NbcZF1 in tobacco, which mediates immune responses to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum cutinase [23, 28, 29]-silencing GhBsr-d1L7 led to reduced ROS accumulation, JA accumulation, and lignin biosynthesis (Fig. 3), highlighting its role in maintaining redox homeostasis and strengthening physical barriers against pathogen spread. Conversely, heterologous overexpression of GhBsr-d1L7 in Arabidopsis enhanced resistance to V. dahliae, with transgenic lines showing less wilting, lower fungal biomass, and elevated ROS, JA, CAT activity, and lignin levels compared to WT plants (Fig. 4B-H). These cross-species results underscore a conserved role for GhBsr-d1L7 in activating multiple layers of plant immunity, from early signaling to structural and metabolic defense responses.

Transcriptomic analysis of GhBsr-d1L7-silenced cotton provides a systems-level view of its central role as a defense coordinator. The identification of 2,008 DEGs (Additional file 1: Fig. S4) revealed profound disruptions across three critical defense axes, hormone signaling networks, phenylpropanoid metabolism, and MAPK cascades, GO and KEGG analyses confirmed enrichment in these pathways (Fig. 5A-B). Crucially, silencing GhBsr-d1L7 led to dysregulation of hormone signaling (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A), impaired phenylpropanoid flux characterized by midstream activation but terminal obstruction of lignin polymerization (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B), and compromised MAPK signaling, including downregulation of key receptors (FLS2, BAK1) and cascade components (WRKY22, MPK1/2) (Additional file 1: Fig. S7C). Notably, the downregulation of FLS2 and BAK1 likely cripples pattern recognition [29, 30] and initiation of downstream immune signaling cascades, including MAPK activation and ROS production [31]. Given that ROS not only function as antimicrobial agents but also act as key signaling molecules involved in maintaining cellular homeostasis [32, 33], the reduced ROS accumulation observed in GhBsr-d1L7-silenced plants (Fig. 3A) may reflect both weakened immune activation and dysregulation of redox balance. The impact of GhBsr-d1L7 silencing extended to transcriptional suppression of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, including genes involved in lignin production (Fig. 5C). As lignin reinforces cell walls and contributes to pathogen containment [34, 35], this aligns with the reduced lignin content observed in silenced plants (Fig. 3B) and further supports a role for GhBsr-d1L7 in structural defense reinforcement. Together, these findings suggest that loss of GhBsr-d1L7 dismantles cotton's defense through a triple mechanism by disrupting hormonal coordination, blocking terminal phenylpropanoid polymerization, and impairing MAPK defense signaling transduction.

Overexpression of GhBsr-d1L7 significantly increased JA accumulation (Fig. 4F), whereas silencing induced the expression of JAZ repressors and altered expression of genes related to auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin, ABA, ethylene, and brassinosteroid signaling (Fig. 5C). This complex modulation of JAZ and diverse hormone pathways reflects the intricate and context-dependent hormonal crosstalk critical for vascular defense against V. dahliae [36, 37]. Similar hormone integration has been observed with GhTLP1, which coordinates JA, ABA, and MAPK signaling to enhance resistance [38], supporting a model where C₂H₂ ZFPs like GhBsr-d1L7 function as upstream integrators orchestrating complex defense networks encompassing hormonal, metabolic (phenylpropanoid), and signaling (MAPK) pathways. These comprehensive transcriptional changes, spanning the three key axes identified, align with physiological results and underscore the role of GhBsr-d1L7 in coordinating both active immune signaling and passive defense fortification.

A particularly novel finding of this study is the identification of GhERD10, a dehydrin family chaperone, as a nuclear interactor of GhBsr-d1L7, with protein–protein interaction assays including Y2H, LCI, and BiFC confirming this interaction (Fig. 6). Unexpectedly, GhERD10 functions as a negative regulator of disease resistance, as its silencing resulted in significantly reduced symptoms, diminished vascular colonization, and lower fungal biomass compared to control plants (Fig. 7). These findings contrast with commonly reported protective functions of LEA proteins and chaperones in stress tolerance, such as Arabidopsis ERD10, which preserves oxidoreductase activity during stress [39], and wheat Group 1 LEAs, which stabilize citrate synthase to maintain metabolic homeostasis [40], this suggests lineage-specific neofunctionalization of chaperones in immune regulation, possibly as an evolutionary adaptation to balance defense and growth. Furthermore, our transcriptomic analysis of GhBsr-d1L7-silenced plants revealed a significant upregulation (1.6-fold) of GhERD10, suggesting that GhERD10 expression may be negatively regulated by GhBsr-d1L7 at the transcriptional level. Conversely, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that silencing GhERD10 did not alter GhBsr-d1L7 transcript levels (Additional file 1: Fig. S9). This asymmetric regulatory relationship indicates that while GhBsr-d1L7 may suppress GhERD10 expression, GhERD10 likely exerts its negative effect on immunity through post-translational modulation of GhBsr-d1L7 activity, rather than by affecting its transcription. Given the nuclear co-localization of GhBsr-d1L7 and GhERD10 (Fig. 6C) and the contrasting resistance phenotypes upon their silencing, we propose that GhERD10 likely sequesters GhBsr-d1L7 from target promoters or facilitates its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. This model is reminiscent of the OsDjA6-MNAC3 complex in rice, which attenuates the expression of immunity-related genes [41] and raises the possibility that GhERD10 fine-tunes immune responses to prevent excessive or harmful activation. In summary, our findings position GhBsr-d1L7 as a pivotal transcriptional hub in cotton immunity. It integrates diverse physiological and molecular responses critical for defense, including ROS dynamics, JA biosynthesis and signaling, phenylpropanoid-derived lignin fortification, and MAPK cascade activation. The transcriptome analysis reveals its essential role in coordinating three core defense modules, hormonal networks, phenylpropanoid metabolism, and MAPK signaling. Its functional conservation in Arabidopsis underscores its significance in basal immunity. The discovery of its interaction with the negative regulator GhERD10 adds a crucial layer of post-translational control, potentially fine-tuning the activity of this central hub to balance defense efficacy with fitness costs. These findings underscore the intricate nature of immune regulation in vascular tissues and provide promising molecular targets for breeding cotton cultivars with enhanced disease resistance.

Conclusions

This study identifies the C₂H₂ ZFP gene GhBsr-d1L7 as a pivotal transcriptional hub regulating cotton’s defense against V. dahliae. Pathogen infection triggers its expression, while silencing GhBsr-d1L7 reduces ROS accumulation, JA levels, and lignin content, resulting in increased susceptibility. Heterologous overexpression of GhBsr-d1L7 in Arabidopsis enhances disease resistance, demonstrating its functional conservation across species. Transcriptome profiling reveals that GhBsr-d1L7 coordinates defense responses by modulating hormone signaling, phenylpropanoid metabolism, and MAPK pathways.

We further identified GhERD10, a dehydrin family chaperone, as a novel nuclear interactor of GhBsr-d1L7. Intriguingly, while silencing GhBsr-d1L7 led to an upregulation of GhERD10 expression, silencing GhERD10 did not significantly affect GhBsr-d1L7 transcript levels, revealing an asymmetric regulatory relationship. Given that GhERD10 itself functions as a negative regulator of resistance, its interaction with GhBsr-d1L7, coupled with this one-sided transcriptional influence, suggests a potential feedback loop or post-translational checkpoint that may fine-tune the activity of the GhBsr-d1L7 defense hub. The functional conservation of GhBsr-d1L7 in Arabidopsis further supports its role as a core component of basal immune signaling and highlights the potential for translating this mechanism across plant species.

Together, these results introduce a novel conceptual model for the role of ZFP-chaperone interactions in plant immune responses and offer a theoretical foundation for breeding cotton varieties with durable disease resistance. Further studies are warranted to clarify the molecular mechanisms through which GhERD10 exerts its inhibitory effects, as well as to evaluate the conservation and biological significance of this regulatory network in different cotton germplasm resources.

Methods

Plant and fungal cultivation conditions

Seeds of the G. hirsutum cultivar Xinluzao 27 were sown in a growth medium consisting of one part vermiculite to two parts nutrient soil. After planting, seedlings were grown in a growth chamber set to 25 °C, with a photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark, and maintained at 60% relative humidity [42].

The ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) of Arabidopsis thaliana was used as the wild-type (WT) control. Seeds were germinated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar containing 1% sucrose and 0.8% agar, and grown in a tissue culture facility under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod at a constant temperature of 22 °C. Once primary roots had developed, seedlings were transferred to pots filled with a substrate of one part vermiculite to two parts soil. Plants were subsequently cultivated in a controlled growth chamber under the same environmental conditions as described above [43].

The fungal pathogen V. dahliae strain Vd991 was first propagated on potato dextrose agar (PDA), prepared with 200 g/L potato, 20 g/L dextrose, and 20 g/L agar, and supplemented with kanamycin and ampicillin at concentrations of 100 µg/mL each. Cultures were maintained at 25 °C for 7 days. Subsequently, spores were aseptically harvested and transferred to a complete medium containing 6 g/L yeast extract, 6 g/L casein hydrolysate, and 10 g/L sucrose. This suspension was cultured at 25 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 72 h, yielding a final spore concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL [44].

Bioinformatics analysis, RNA extraction, and gene cloning

Protein sequences of GhBsr-d1L7 (Accession: XP_040964147.1) and a selection of C2H2 ZFPs representing broad phylogenetic diversity were retrieved from the NCBI database. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using MEGA11 (version 11.0), while multiple sequence alignments were carried out with ESPript3 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/). Homology-based three-dimensional structure predictions were subsequently performed utilizing the Swiss-Model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/).

Total RNA was isolated from cotton tissues using the RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 ug of total RNA using the HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with oligo (dT)18 primers.

The full-length coding sequence of each target gene was amplified from cDNA using gene-specific primers (Additional file 1: Table 1). PCR reactions were carried out using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) under standard amplification conditions. The resulting PCR products were purified with a gel extraction kit and subsequently cloned into a blunt-ended cloning vector for sequence verification. All constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing provided by Youkang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xinjiang, China).

Analysis of GhBsr-d1L7 gene expression

To examine the expression of GhBsr-d1L7 following V. dahliae infection, cotton seedling roots were submerged in a spore suspension (Vd991) for five minutes. Samples from roots, stems, and leaves were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 hpi for RNA extraction. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the RT Easy™ II Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol [45].

Gene-specific primers for GhBsr-d1L7 quantification were designed in parallel with primers for the endogenous control GhUBQ7 (LOC107912293), with oligonucleotide sequences documented in Additional file 1: Table 1. Amplification reactions (20 µL total volume) employed Real Time PCR Easy™-SYBR Green I (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China), according to established fluorescence detection protocols, performed on an ABI 7500 Fast platform. Relative transcript levels were calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method, and the results were visualized using TBtools software (2.007, Chengjie Chen, Guangzhou, China).

Subcellular localization analysis

The full-length coding region of GhBsr-d1L7 was amplified from cDNA of G. hirsutum cv. Xinluzao 27 cDNA using primers detailed in Additional file 1: Table 1. The amplified product was directionally inserted into the pYBA1132-GFP vector using the ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), resulting in a C-terminal GFP fusion construct (pYBA1132-GhBsr-d1L7-GFP). Both the recombinant construct and the empty vector control (pYBA1132-GFP) were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 for subsequent analysis.

For subcellular localization studies, transformed agrobacteria (OD600 = 1.5) suspended in infiltration buffer (200 μM acetosyringone, 100 μM MES, 100 μM MgCl2) were co-infiltrated with organelle markers into N. benthamiana leaves. The nuclear marker H2B-mCherry and plasma membrane marker PIP2A-mCherry served as positional references. After 72-h incubation, dual-channel fluorescence signals were observed using a laser-scanning confocal microscopy (Leica TCS SP5) under standardized settings: GFP was detected at 488 nm excitation/525 nm emission, and mCherry at 561 nm excitation/595 nm emission [46].

VIGS of GhBsr-d1L7

For targeted silencing of GhBsr-d1L7, a 300-bp silencing fragment was computationally identified and validated through the SGN-VIGS (https://vigs.solgenomics.net/) bioinformatics pipeline. The pTRV2::GhBsr-d1L7 plasmid was constructed and introduced into Agrobacterium, which was delivered into cotyledons of cotton seedlings at the unifoliate stage. The empty vector, pTRV2::00, and a vector targeting chlorophyll regulation, pTRV2::GhCLA1, were used as controls. Following infiltration, plants were incubated in darkness for 24 h and then transferred to a greenhouse. Silencing efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR after newly emerged leaves exhibited bleaching. Roots of silenced plants were washed and challenged with V. dahliae spore preparation (1 × 107 CFU/mL) during 5-min immersion. Mock-treated control groups were hydrated with filter-sterilized water. After disease symptoms appeared, disease severity were rated on a 0–4 rating scale, and with the disease index was determined based on previously published methods. Samples were collected for fungal biomass quantification by RT-qPCR with GhUBQ7 and VdEF1-α (VDAG_10074) as described by Li [47, 48]. Refer to Additional file 1: Table 11 for complete primer repertoire employed [49].

Arabidopsis transformation and disease resistance assays

The CDS of GhBsr-d1L7 was amplified from cDNA derived from G. hirsutum cv. Xinluzao 27, utilizing primer pairs specified in Additional file 1: Table 1. The resultant amplicon was directionally cloned into the pYBA1132 linearized vector via the ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the ligation protocol detailed in Section 2.4. The recombinant plasmid pYBA1132-GhBsr-d1L7 was introduced into Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Col-0 via floral dip transformation. Homozygous T₃ transgenic lines were screened on kanamycin-supplemented medium (50 mg/L) followed by transgene verification through RT-qPCR with AtActin2 (AT3G18780) serving as the reference gene [50].

Transgenic plants were further confirmed by RT-PCR using primers spanning a specific exon region of GhBsr-d1L7 (Additional file 1: Table 1). This region was selected for its high specificity to distinguish the cotton transgene from any homologous sequences in Arabidopsis, as verified by NCBI Primer-BLAST. PCR amplification was performed under the standard conditions described in Section 2.2.

Two events of Arabidopsis overexpressing GhBsr-d1L7 were selected and compared with Col-0. Plants were washed and soaked in V. dahliae spore suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL) for 5 min, while controls were soaked in sterile water. Following infection, seedlings were replanted, maintained in complete darkness for 24 h, and subsequently cultivated under regular conditions.

Pathogen biomass quantification employed RT-qPCR as detailed in Section 2.5. The VdEF1-α gene served as the fungal detection target, while Arabidopsis thaliana AtActin2 provided endogenous normalization for plant samples. Disease severity was assessed using a standardized 0–4 rating scale, with indices calculated per established protocols (Section 2.5) [51].

Assessment of physiological and biochemical responses

The plant tissue ROS detection kit (DAB method) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used to quantify ROS content. CAT activity was measured using the CAT activity detection kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) with a microplate reader. The JA enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Spbio, Wuhan, China) was used to measure JA content with a microplate reader. Lignin distribution was observed using the lignin staining solution kit (Phygene, Fuzhou, China) and quantified with the lignin detection kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Approximately twenty days after VIGS treatment (following the procedure in Section 2.5), once silencing symptoms became visible, roots of pTRV2::00 and pTRV2::GhBsr-d1L7-silenced seedlings were inoculated with V. dahliae for 8 h, after which root samples from three biological replicates per treatment were collected for High-throughput RNA-seq [52]. RNA sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA) using S4 flow cells. Raw reads underwent quality control via Fastp (v0.19.7), yielding adapter-trimmed clean reads. These clean reads were aligned to the G. hirsutum TM-1 reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). Transcript quantification was conducted with Subread (v2.0.1), and DEGs were identified. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses of DEGs, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI), were performed using ClusterProfiler (v4.0). Selected DEGs were further validated experimentally via RT-qPCR [53].

Screening of potential interacting proteins

The bait plasmid pGBKT7-GhBsr-d1L7 was constructed and introduced into the yeast strain Y2HGold. Proteotoxicity and self-activation assays were conducted on SD/-Trp/-Leu and SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade media. Y2H analysis implemented was conducted using a custom cDNA library independently constructed from V. dahliae-infected cotton plants [54]. The Y2H library plasmid and the bait vector were cotransfected into Y2HGold cells, with interacting transformants selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade quadruple dropout medium. Yeast clones were analyzed by PCR and sequencing [55].

To validate protein interactions, the candidate gene GhERD10 coding sequence was cloned into pGADT7, generating the recombinant plasmid pGADT7-GhERD10. This recombinant plasmid was co-transformed with the pGBKT7-GhBsr-d1L7 plasmid into the Y2HGold yeast strain. The transformants were plated on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium. After colonies reached a diameter of 2–3 mm, they were selected, resuspended in 10 µL of ddH₂O, and serially diluted. Diluted suspensions were plated on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade medium and cultured at 30 °C for 3–5 days to assess colony formation. The pGBKT7-53 and pGADT7-SV40-T plasmid pair served as mating efficiency controls.

Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI) and Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) assays

For the LCI assay, recombinant plasmids cLCI-GhBsr-d1L7 and nLCI-GhERD10 were generated and transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. Bacterial suspensions were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves, followed by application of 1 mM D-luciferin (HY-12591B; 10 min). Luminescence signals were captured instantaneously with a Berthold NightSHADE LB 985 system (Germany).

BiFC assays were employed to further confirm protein–protein interactions. Recombinant plasmids cYFP-GhBsr-d1L7 and nYFP-GhERD10 were engineered and transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. After infiltration into N. benthamiana leaves, plants were incubated for 48 h, after which reconstituted YFP fluorescence was visualized using a Zeiss LSM confocal microscope (Jena, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All experimental procedures included at least three biological replicates. Quantitative results are presented as mean values ± standard error (SE) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was evaluated using Student’s t-test (*P < 0.05, and **P < 0.01) or by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, with significant differences indicated by different superscript letters at P < 0.05.
