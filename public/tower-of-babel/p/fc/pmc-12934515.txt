Coaxial Electrospray of Nanodrug-Loaded Porous Polylactic Acid/Poly(Ethylene Oxide) Core–Shell Microparticles for Intrapulmonary Drug Delivery
Authors: Chi Wang, Dandan Guo, Juntao Luo, Yingge Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Biocompatible nano-to-microscale particles offer significant advantages for therapeutic applications, particularly in targeted and sustained drug delivery for lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). This study focuses on the fabrication of porous core–shell microparticles encapsulating bioactive telodendrimer (TD) nanodrug carriers using electrospray technology. The microparticles were designed to enhance pulmonary drug delivery by optimizing particle size (1–5 μm) and morphology for deep lung deposition and controlled drug release. The effects of solution viscosity and surface tension on microparticle formation were systematically investigated. Results demonstrated that higher polymer concentration and controlled electrospray parameters yielded spherical microparticles with uniform porosity, essential for sustained drug release. Surfactant addition reduced particle size and enhanced pore formation but introduced challenges such as morphological variability. In vitro cytotoxicity, hemolysis, and drug release studies confirmed the biocompatibility and therapeutic potential of the fabricated microparticles. The findings highlight the promise of electrospray-derived core–shell microparticles for non-invasive COPD treatment, warranting further exploration into polymer-solvent interactions and formulation refinements for optimized drug delivery.

Introduction

Biocompatible nano-to-microscale particles are desirable in therapeutics given their enhanced drug stability, targeted and sustained delivery, and minimized toxicity [1–3]. The versatile particulate systems can deliver a wide range of compounds from small molecules to proteins and genetic materials that provide flexibility to the treatment of lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [4–6]. We have developed a series of novel bioactive immune modulating telodendrimer (TD) nanodrug carriers which could be used for the inhibition of toll-like receptors (TLRs), i.e., TLR2 and TLR4, therefore suppressing the excessive inflammatory response in COPD. In addition, the TD nanodrug carriers can encapsulate antibiotics and biotherapeutics such as enzymes and growth factors, thus synergizing with the infection control and lung repair in COPD. To treat COPD, inhalation drug delivery enables direct local administration to disease sites. However, overcoming mucociliary clearance and achieving deeper lung delivery remain challenging [7, 8]. Effective pulmonary delivery and prolonged drug release for COPD require micro-scale particles with an ideal median mass aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) of 1–5 μm, allowing encapsulation of therapeutic nanodrugs for effective deposition and sustained release [9–11].

Electrostatic spraying has been a promising method for synthesizing microparticles for offering advantages such as ease of operation, versatility in material selection and quantity, and flexible control over particle size and structure [12, 13]. This technique enables the fabrication of advanced particle forms, including core–shell and porous structures, making it particularly suitable for drug delivery and biomaterial applications [14, 15]. In electrospray, a proper solvent with polymer/drug and surfactant or additives is pumped through a capillary needle to which a high voltage is applied. The strong electrical field imposed onto the liquid leads to the formation of a Taylor cone at the end of the needle, generating highly charged monodispersed droplets when the balance breaks down between surface tension and the electrostatic force [16].

Engineering microparticles with diverse morphologies and sizes is critical, as studies have shown that particle shape and size play a significant role in determining drug release profiles. For instance, porous particles increase surface area, enhancing drug release efficiency and permeability, while particle size and shape will influence the drug dissolution rate and phagocytic clearance in the lung [17, 18]. Furthermore, controlled drug release can be achieved by tailoring polymer characteristics, such as molecular weight, concentration, hydrophobicity, and permeability, as well as particle structural features, including surface pore size, pore distribution, and shell thickness. The formation of electrospray particles involves several interconnected processes, including solvent evaporation, solute diffusion, Coulombic fission, and the collision and coagulation of particles, as charged droplets are atomized, shrunk, and solidified during their flight through the air [16]. The competition and interaction among these processes ultimately influence the final particle size and morphology [19]. For instance, Almería’s study suggests that spherical particles form when polymer entanglements in evaporating droplets set before Coulomb fission, which occurs when the surface charge density exceeds the Rayleigh limit, causing the droplet to split into smaller droplets. In contrast, tailed and elongated particles form if Coulomb fission occurs first [20].

Electrospray particle morphology and size can be controlled by material properties (e.g., viscosity, conductivity, surface tension), process parameters (e.g., voltage, flow rate, tip-to-ground distance), and ambient conditions (e.g., temperature, humidity). While particle size can be tuned by flow rate and polymer concentration [21], shape control is more complicated and relies on the interplay of processes like solvent evaporation and Coulombic fission [22]. Surface tension and viscosity are key factors that will influence the electrospray mode and the formed particles. Increased viscosity typically stabilizes the jet and makes jet duration longer, forming larger particles, while low viscosity promotes smaller particles [23, 24]. Lower surface tension in electrospray facilitates smaller particle sizes and a more uniform size distribution by promoting easier droplet breakup. However, excessively low surface tension can destabilize the spray, leading to irregular particle morphologies. Few studies explored the viscosity and surface tension as well as their interactions on the electrospray particle size and morphology. Accordingly, the correlation between viscosity, surface tension, and particle morphology remains unclear, and there is a need to verify their effect on the core–shell electrospray particles.

In this study, we explored the effects of solution viscosity and surface tension on particle size and morphology to optimize the fabrication of porous core–shell microparticles with diameters ranging from 1 to 5 μm using electrospray. These particles were designed for non-invasive pulmonary delivery of TD nanodrug carriers, specifically targeting COPD treatment through core-drug encapsulation. Application tests such as cell viability, in vitro cytotoxicity, and drug release studies were conducted to evaluate their biomedical potential. We hypothesize that surface tension, viscosity, and their interaction significantly influence particle size and morphology. Accordingly, various parameter combinations were investigated, and we concluded the optimal formula to achieve spherical particles with controlled diameters and surface pores. The drug release profile was also investigated to show the disease treatment potential.

Materials and Methods

Electrospray Solution Preparation

Polylactic acid (shell layer solution solute, PLA, density = 1.24 g/cm3) pallets were sourced from Avantor Performance Materials (Radnor, PA, USA). Polyethylene oxide (core layer solution solute, PEO, molecular weight = 100,000) powder and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) powder were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MI, USA). Chloroform was purchased from Avantor Performance Materials (Radnor, PA, USA). Deionized water (DI water) was obtained from a Millipore Milli-Q system.

Polylactic Acid (PLA)-based microparticles have gained significant attention for drug delivery applications as they can be naturally decomposed through enzymatic or nonenzymatic processes and result in safe and biocompatible human byproducts, which are then eliminated by the normal metabolic systems [25]. Polyethylene oxide (PEO) is widely used in drug delivery and biofabrication due to its biocompatibility and ease of dissolution in water, making it a suitable core material [26, 27]. Chloroform is widely used in electrospray given its low surface tension and high volatility, which promote rapid droplet evaporation and facilitate the formation of spherical particles with smooth surfaces. Additionally, its good solubility for many polymers ensures homogeneous solutions, crucial for uniform particle morphology [28]. The surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) powder was added to both PLA and PEO solutions for lower surface tension. The SDS surfactant reduced the surface tension without significantly affecting the viscosity. The addition of surfactants to polymer solutions aims to reduce surface tension, thereby enhancing droplet atomization and resulting in the formation of smaller particles during the electrospraying process [29]. The presence of SDS may also stabilize the electrospray, leading to more uniform particle production [30]. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated that SDS significantly influences particle porosity formation. Therefore, it is worthwhile to investigate its effect on the porosity of microparticles [31].

Shell solutions were prepared by dissolving PLA pellets in chloroform for 12 h at room temperature. The 5% ethanol with or without SDS was added to chloroform to serve as a cosolvent and to produce porous surfaces. PEO powder with or without SDS was dissolved in chloroform until fully dissolved at room temperature to make the core solution.

Electrospray Process of Core–Shell Microparticles

The electrospray process was conducted using the TL-Pro robotic electrospinning platform (Tong Li Tech, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a high-voltage power supply up to 50 kV. A coaxial stainless-steel spinneret was used, with two micro pumps delivering the core and shell solutions through the spinneret, as depicted in Figure 1. In this setup, two syringe pumps independently infused the core solution (PEO with/without the drug) and the shell solution (PLA) through the inner and outer sections of the coaxial spinneret. A high voltage (13–20 kV) was applied between the spinneret and a grounded aluminum foil collector, forming a double-layered Taylor cone at the needle tip that overcame surface tension to spray microparticles. The tip-to-collector distance (TTD) was set at 26 cm based on our preliminary studies. Unless otherwise specified, gauge 22 and gauge 16 needles were used for the core and shell solutions, respectively, and the core–shell flow rate was set at 1:1 mL/h.

Characterization of Microparticles and Solution Properties

The microparticles collected on the aluminum foil were coated with sputter coating and imaged using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Figure 2 presents the measurements of particle circularity, degradation area, diameter, and surface pore size. In Figure 2A, the red circle represents the circularity path, while the yellow area indicates the degradation region. The degradation area percentage is calculated as the ratio of the degradation area (yellow) to the total particle area (red). Figure 2B,C illustrate the methods used to determine particle diameter and surface pore sizes, respectively. Each group includes three replicate samples, and 20 particles were randomly selected from each sample and measured using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The viscosities of the polymer solutions were measured by a digital rotational viscometer (IKA, Wilmington, NC, USA). The surface tension of the polymer solutions was measured by the capillary rise method and calculated using the following equation: γ=2h⋅ρ⋅g⋅r2, where h is the liquid column height, ρ is the density, g is the acceleration due to gravity, and r is the capillary radius.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity and Drug Release Studies

Cell Culture

Murine macrophage cell line Raw 264.7 and human monocyte cell line THP-1 were purchased from American Type Culture Collections (Manassas, VA, USA). Raw 264.7 cells and THP-1 cells were cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640 medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin G, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C using a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

Cell Viability Study

Raw 264.7 cells and THP-1 were seeded in a 96-well plate with a cell density of 4 × 103 cells and 8 × 103 per well, respectively. Raw 264.7 cells and THP-1 cells were treated with different concentrations of microparticles after overnight and 30 min incubation, respectively. The microparticles used for the following in vitro and drug release studies were from the high ST–3%–16–18 kVgroup. After 72 h incubation, Cell Titer 96 Aqueous Cell Proliferation Reagent, including 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5- (3-carboxy methoxyphenyl)-2- (4-sulfophenyl)- 2H tetrazolium (MTS) and an electron-coupling reagent phenazine methosulphate (PMS), was added to each well according to the manufacturer’s instructions and further incubated for 2 h at 37°C. The cell viability was determined by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). Untreated cells are considered a control. Results were shown as the average cell viability of triplicate wells by the following equation: Cell viability % = [(ODtreat − ODblank)/(ODcontrol − ODblank) × 100%].

Hemolytic Assays

Fresh blood from a healthy human volunteer was collected into a 20 mM EDTA PBS solution. Red blood cells (RBCs) were separated via centrifugation for 10 min at 1000 rpm. After three PBS washes, the RBCs were resuspended in PBS. Microparticles were added to 100 μL RBC solution at the concentrations of 2, 20, and 200 μg/mL and incubated for 0.5 h, 4 h, and 24 h at 37°C. PBS and 2% Triton X-100 were incubated with RBCs as negative and positive controls, respectively. The hemoglobin in the supernatant was measured by UV absorbance at 540 nm via NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). The hemolysis was calculated through the following equation: Hemolysis% = [(ODsample − ODPBS)/(ODtriton − ODPBS)] × 100%.

In Vitro Drug Release

The drug release profiles of doxorubicin (DOX) formulations were measured by a dialysis method. The 300 μL of free DOX, DOX-TD, nanoparticles-embedded core–shell particle (NECS)- DOX, and DOX-MP formulations were loaded into dialysis cartridges with 3.5 kDa MWCO dialysis membranes (Thermo Scientific, Rochford, IL). The drug release profiles of doxorubicin (DOX) formulations were measured by a dialysis method. The 300 μL of free DOX, DOX-TD, NECS (DOX), and DOX-MP formulations were loaded into dialysis cartridges with 3.5 kDa MWCO dialysis membranes (Thermo Scientific, Rochford, IL). The cartridge was dialyzed against 40 mL PBS and gently shaken at 37°C in a water bath. The PBS solution was changed every 4 h. The 5 μL of DOX solution within the dialysis cartridge was withdrawn at different time points and further diluted in 10 μL DMSO, and then fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em 470/560 nm) was measured by a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). Results were reported as the average percentage of DOX release for triplicate samples.

Antibacterial Susceptibility Testing

Antibacterial susceptibility tests for gentamicin (GM) formulations were performed using the broth microdilution method according to the study. The GM formulations were prepared as stock solutions at a 500 μg/mL concentration of GM in lysogeny broth (LB). A total volume of 100 μL of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) in LB broth with a 20 μg/mL GM concentration of different GM formulations was conducted in a 96-well plate at 37°C. The bacteria growth was monitored at an OD of 600 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1).

Turbidimetric Lysozyme Assay

The 1000 U/mL stock solution of lysozyme (Lyso) in different Lyso formulations was prepared and stored at 4°C overnight. The 0.2 mg/mL suspension of Micrococcus lysodeikticus lyophilized cells (M3770, Sigma Aldrich) was prepared in buffer with a pH of 6.24. The solution of substrate and Lyso was mixed in a cuvette with the final concentration of Lyso at 400 U/mL. The absorption was read at a wavelength of 450 nm every minute at room temperature for 5 min via NanoDrop.

Statistical Analysis

Manufacturing Parameter Studies

Data were analyzed using the Minitab Statistical Software. A t-test was applied for two group comparisons, and a two-way ANOVA was applied to examine two independent variables at a time to assess the main effects of polymer concentration, solution surface tension, and applied voltage, along with their pairwise interactions on the outcome measures. Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with n ≥ 30 for each group. Exact sample sizes are provided in the figure captions. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with figures indicating * for p < 0.05 and ** for p < 0.001.

In Vitro Studies

Data are presented as mean ± SD or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, applying Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA for multiple groups. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****). The number of replicates and data presentation format are indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analysis for Figure 8A is included in the Figure S1.

Results and Discussion

The core–shell electrospray mechanism ensures the encapsulation of nanodrugs in the particle core. The charged liquid at the needle tip forms a double-layered Taylor cone [32]. As the polymer solution passes through a long capillary and forms rapidly expanding fluid jets, the dispersed phase tends to accumulate at the center of the liquid due to the elongation effect along the jet’s direction during its flight through the air. This phenomenon aids in the incorporation of the nanodrug encapsulated within the particle core instead of surface accumulation, promoting the formation of core–shell structures [33].

Electrospray adopts a high-voltage electric field to initiate positive and negative electrolyte ion separation in polymeric solution as it penetrates the liquid surface at the capillary tip [34]. A key factor in this process is the surface tension of the liquid that might cause electric corona discharge to occur before electrospray [35]. Reducing the surface tension of the polymer solution during electrospray may enhance atomization, reduce particle size, and influence porosity, as atomization occurs when the balance between surface tension and electrical force is disrupted [36]. Incorporating the nanodrug into the core solution alters the surface tension, making it important to investigate the impact of surface tension on particle formation. Here, we used SDS to adjust the surface tension of the solution, creating two levels of surface tension. The low surface tension group included 0.05% SDS, resulting in a surface tension of approximately 22.4 mN/m, while the high surface tension group had a value of 28 mN/m. In addition, solution viscosity can also influence particle formation. Generally, higher polymer concentrations increase viscosity, which affects droplet formation and stability, ultimately leading to variations in particle size and morphology [37]. In this study, viscosity was controlled by varying the polymer concentration at 1%, 2%, and 3% w/v. For PLA solutions, the viscosities were 7.12, 10.65, and 13.5 mPa·s, respectively, while for PEO solutions, the viscosities were 6, 11.3, and 14.5 mPa·s. Thus, two levels of surface tension and three levels of viscosity (defined by polymer concentration) were examined. The core and shell concentrations were kept at the same level for each experimental group, as we hypothesized that similar core–shell viscosities would result in stable electrospray and encapsulation based on previous research [38]. The electrospray jet may form a stream before breaking up into droplets with significantly different core–shell viscosities, which could affect the stability and uniformity of particle formation.

Particle Morphology

The voltage range applied was between 13 and 20 kV: at 13–15 kV, a long Taylor cone was observed; at 16–18 kV, a shorter cone was formed; and at 19–20 kV, no cone was present, but a skewed stream was observed. Figure 3 illustrates how particle morphology changes with varying surface tension, concentration levels, and applied voltage. It can be observed that particles are more spherical when the solution concentration/viscosity increases. Previous research has shown that when polymer concentration falls below a critical level, particles tend to collapse and lose their spherical shape due to insufficient polymer entanglement [39]. Figure 3A,B show that the contribution of surfactant on spherical particles was not positive, and it is obvious that lower surface tension with 3% concentration facilitated the formation of electrospinning fibers with electrospray particles. The 1% and 2% concentrations with lower surface tension resulted in smaller but deformed particles. This can be explained by the increased ability of the jetting Taylor cone to retain its shape with higher viscosity and the ease of electrospinning when the electric force tends to exceed the lowered surface tension in the Taylor cone for jetting. Additionally, the addition of surfactants accelerates Coulombic fission, causing it to occur earlier in the process. Studies have demonstrated that if polymer entanglement occurs after Coulombic fission, particle morphology is deformed from a spherical shape [20]. Our results from Figure 3A,B confirm that achieving spherical particles requires a polymer concentration above a certain threshold (≥ 3%) to ensure sufficient polymer entanglement, and both concentration level and surfactant level are significant on circularity with p-values less than 0.0001. Moreover, adding surfactants does not produce smaller spherical particles when using low-viscosity solutions, as Coulombic fission will always precede polymer entanglement under these conditions, leading to collapsed, non-spherical particles.

To achieve spherical particles, the applied voltage needs to be controlled within a stable range. Excessively low or high voltages resulted in particle deformation. Low voltage led to dripping or collapsed particles due to insufficient electrical force for proper jet formation. In contrast, excessively high voltage increased the velocity of electrospray particles and resulted in flat particles due to insufficient solvent evaporation and possibly high contacting speed on the collector. In Figure 3C, whereas the circularity data did not reveal any significant impact of applied voltage on particle morphology, voltages exceeding 19 kV notably reduced surface porosity. Overall, voltages in the range of 15–18 kV, combined with a 3% polymer concentration, produced optimally spherical particles with well-defined surface pores, as highlighted by the red box in Figure 3A.

Although spherical particles have more predictable aerodynamic behavior, better dispersion, and higher disintegration rates, which can improve deposition efficiency in certain regions of the lung [40], elongated particles favor adhesion to cell membranes yet are internalized only at their round ends, a behavior that can improve pulmonary deposition and help evade macrophage clearance [41]. In this study, spherical particles were favored to ensure complete drug encapsulation compared with fractured or irregular particles; however, alternative morphologies, such as elongated, donut-shaped particles, will be explored in future research.

Particle Size

Based on statistical analysis, surface tension, viscosity, voltage, and the interaction among them have significant effects on particle size. Figure 4A illustrates that increased viscosity has a higher chance of producing larger particles with increased standard deviation. To reduce particle sizes, the polymer concentration can be decreased, but it must remain above the critical entanglement concentration. Falling below this threshold could result in unstable jets and collapsed particles [42]. Figure 4B demonstrates the result that lower surface tension did help reduce the particle sizes, which aligns with Shakri’s study as lower surface tension facilitates the Coulomb fission [43]. The lower surface tension (ST) groups with added SDS generally produced particles approximately 2 μm smaller in size compared to the non-SDS groups. However, the morphology was suboptimal, as the addition of surfactants also promoted the formation of electrospinning fibers. A potential solution to address this issue could be to further reduce the surfactant concentration or replace surfactants with TD nanodrugs, which may slightly reduce surface tension without inducing fiber production. Figure 4C shows that the effect of applied voltage on particle size is less obvious in our case compared to surface tension and polymer concentration, as the range of 13–20 kV falls within the stable spray region. Based on the SEM images, voltages between 16 and 18 kV had the highest likelihood of producing spherical particles. In contrast, voltages below or above this range caused slight particle collapse, leading to a minor increase in particle diameter.

Figure 4D presents the overall particle size distribution in a boxplot format, incorporating all variables. Generally, polymer concentration and particle size are positively correlated, with higher concentrations leading to larger particles. Lower surface tension consistently reduces particle sizes with lower standard deviations. In contrast, the high surface tension (ST) groups exhibit a broader range of particle sizes because high ST resists the breakup of the liquid jet into uniform droplets. This leads to less controlled atomization, producing larger particles during Coulomb fission and fewer subsequent fission events compared to low ST groups. As a result, the particle size distribution becomes more spread out, resulting in a larger standard deviation. The particle size in the high ST-2%−19–20 kV group is exceptionally large because the high voltage (19–20 kV), combined with 2% polymer concentration, causes most particles to collapse or elongate. In the current optimal morphology group (high ST-3%−16–18 kV), 75% of the particles are smaller than 7 μm. This suggests the potential to further reduce the particle diameter to below 5 μm by optimizing the surfactant concentration, reducing flow rate, or selectively filtering particles within the desired size range [43]. To avoid mucociliary clearance with particle diameters larger than 10 μm and exhaled particles with nanoscale particles when administrated through inhalation, particles with an ideal median mass aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) of 1–5 μm are desired to deliver the drug deep into the alveolar epithelium [9–11]. Therefore, the optimization of experimental parameters such as surfactant concentration and flow rate is necessary in the future to further reduce the particle diameter under 5 μm [43]. Notably, studies have shown that particles exceeding 7 μm can still reach deep lung tissue when their porosity lowers the MMAD to under 5 μm [44]. In addition, larger microparticles can evade phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages, therefore prolonging drug residence and enhancing therapeutic efficacy [45, 46].

Surface Pores

The addition of surfactants plays a crucial role in surface pore formation in electrospray particles by modifying the interactions between the solvent and polymer during solidification. Surfactant–polymer interactions can alter the molecular structure and dynamics of the polymer, thereby influencing the rheological and interfacial properties of the polymer dispersion, which in turn affect the surface pore formation [47, 48]. In Figure 5A, in low concentrations (1% and 2%) and lower ST groups, smaller, finer, and more uniform surface pores were observed. The role of surfactants in promoting porosity during electrospinning or electrospray has been demonstrated by other groups as well, who utilized various surfactants to produce porous fibers or membranes [49, 50]. However, at higher polymer concentrations (3%), surfactants can destabilize polymer distribution during solidification, leading to varied pore sizes and the formation of fibers. Additionally, under high-voltage conditions (> 16 kV), particles in non-surfactant groups lose porosity due to accelerated solvent evaporation and rapid solidification, while surfactant-containing groups maintain surface porosity regardless of voltage. Overall, surfactants enhance pore formation and stability but can introduce variability in pore size at high polymer concentrations due to phase separation during solidification.

Degradation Analysis

This degradation test aims to simulate the in vivo environment and investigate how core–shell microparticles degrade over time, providing insights into the potential drug release profile. Hydrolysis biodegradation of PLA involves the breakdown of the polymer by water molecules, leading to the cleavage of ester bonds in the PLA chains. This process results in the production of smaller lactic acid molecules, which can be further metabolized or eliminated from the body. Numerous studies have examined the degradation rate of PLA, which varies significantly depending on factors such as pH, temperature, and molecular weight. For instance, braided PLA fibers with an 18.6 μm diameter degraded over 12 months, while PLA fibers around 10–15 μm in diameter degraded within 25 days under pH conditions of 2, 3, 7.4, and 10 [51, 52]. In our experiment, PLA microparticles were immersed in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM) at 37°C in the sterilized incubator for specific durations. At each time point, samples were removed from the liquid, air-dried, and analyzed using SEM to observe the degradation. The degradation area was measured, as controlling the particle weight proved challenging due to potential particle loss in the liquid.

Figure 5B illustrates the degradation of the microparticles over 3 weeks. On day 0, the samples were immersed in water for 2 h and showed no surface breakage, indicating the integrity of the PLA shells and ruling out potential PEO leakage during the electrospray process because PEO could be easily dissolved in water. By day 3, voids began to appear on the surface, and by week 3, debris had accumulated on the ground. The surface degradation area increased significantly, with nearly 50% of the PLA particles exhibiting surface breakdown by week 3. This relatively rapid degradation can be attributed to the low PLA concentration in electrospray (3%). Additionally, the surface porosity facilitates liquid permeability, allowing the dissolution of the PEO core, while the thin wall thickness and small particle size further enhance the degradation rate.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Analysis

The in vitro cytotoxicity of the designed blank microparticles (MP) and TD-encapsulated microparticles was evaluated in RAW 264.7 murine macrophage and THP-1 human monocyte cultures. The blank microparticles and TD-encapsulated microparticles at the concentration range of 0.2 ng/mL to 200 μg/mL were incubated with RAW 264.7 and THP-1 cells for 72 h.

Both RAW 264.7 and THP-1 cells exhibited a non-statistically significant decrease in the viability of cells after incubation with blank microparticles or TD-encapsulated microparticles (Figure 6A,B). It can be concluded that both blank microparticles and TD-encapsulated microparticles are non-cytotoxic up to 200 μg/mL. In addition, no hemolytic toxicity was observed in blank microparticles and TD-encapsulated microparticles with concentration up to 200 μg/mL after overnight incubation (Figure 6C).

In Vitro Drug Release, Antibacterial, and Enzymatic Activity Analysis

Figure 7A,B illustrate different particles with and without a TD nanodrug in the core. In Figure 7A, where the core included PEO only, a smooth particle surface and homogeneously distributed pores were observed. In contrast, Figure 7B shows particles with incorporated TD nanodrug, which exhibited a wrinkled particle surface and unevenly distributed surface pore sizes. Figure 7C illustrates that the surface pore size increased, leading to the shell opening after 8 h of water immersion, highlighting its potential for sustained drug release.

In Vitro Drug Release

The in vitro drug release profiles of DOX formulations were evaluated by dialysis method in PBS at 37°C under a sink condition, i.e., frequently refreshed PBS. After 2 h dialysis (Figure 8A), free DOX exhibited complete release, and less than 20% of DOX was released from nanocarrier-embedded core–shell microparticles (NECS (DOX)), which were significantly slower than the DOX release (~30%) of DOX-TD and DOX-MP. A linear DOX release profile was observed in DOX-TD and DOX-MP formulations for the remaining 70% of the drug within 20 h. In comparison, NECS(TD) exhibited a slow and sustained DOX release of 50% of the drug within 24 h. The sustained release profiles without a burst release from NECS (DOX) formulations can be explained by the hydrophobic PLGA porous shell of NECS that can reduce burst release.

In Vitro Antibacterial Activity

To evaluate the in vitro antibacterial activity of GM formulations, the commonly susceptible P. aeruginosa was applied in culture for a drug test. No antibacterial activity was observed for NECS (TD only) in P. aeruginosa culture. As shown in Figure 8B, all the GM formulations at the concentration of GM 20 μg/mL show significant inhibition of P. aeruginosa growth after overnight incubation, indicating GM can be effectively released from GM-TD and NECS (GM) formulations.

Turbidimetric Lysozyme Assay

To evaluate the enzymatic activity of Lyso formulations, the turbidimetric assay was used for the activity test. The stock solution of Lyso-TD and NECS (Lyso) was prepared and stored at 4°C overnight to allow Lyso release from the nano/micro-carriers. No enzymatic activity was observed for TD and NECS (TD only). As shown in Figure 8c, free Lyso exhibited the most effective lysis activity, while the enzymatic activity of Lyso-TD and NECS (Lyso) formulations was slightly reduced, indicating the majority of the Lyso can be effectively released from GM-TD and NECS (GM) formulations after overnight incubation.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

In this study, PLA microparticles loaded with TD nano-drug with a porous surface were successfully fabricated via core–shell electrospray. The effect of surface tension, polymer concentration, and applied voltage on the morphology and size of the microparticles was studied. The experimental results showed that the 3% PLA with the high surface tension group at 16–18 kV has the optimal spherical morphology. The surfactant SDS plays a significant role in reducing particle size and promoting pore formation, but may also introduce challenges such as pore size variability and fiber production issues during electrospray. The resulting microparticles have shown the potential in intrapulmonary drug delivery with minimal toxicity and extended drug release. The shape of particles is known to influence all phases of pulmonary drug delivery, including aerodynamic deposition, biodistribution, and macrophage uptake [53]. Thus, microparticles with various shapes, such as rods and donut shapes, will be generated and compared with the spherical microparticle for their aerosol properties and cellular uptake in the future. In addition, future research could focus on optimizing the surfactant type and concentration, as well as tailoring polymer properties to better control particle morphology and size. Studies have shown that different polymers interact uniquely with solvents, influencing polymer chain entanglements and the resulting particle morphology [54]. These interactions are strongly dictated by both polymer concentration and molecular weight. To further refine core–shell microparticles, future work could explore variations in the molecular weight of PLA, alternative polymers, or the use of different solvents.
