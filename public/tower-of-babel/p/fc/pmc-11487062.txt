Cu/N-doped carbon spheres derived from soybean flour as an active green nanocomposite for the synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazole derivatives
Authors: Mahya Kohansal Moghadam, Hossein Eshghi, Sara S E Ghodsinia, Ali Shiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCu immobilized onto N-doped carbon spheres (Cu/N-doped CS) derived from soybean flour was synthesized via the hydrothermal method and certified as a green high-efficiency catalyst for the regioselective synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles. The obtained N-doped carbon spheres from a combination of glucose and soy flour have a larger size and suitable affinity for load copper species. The morphology and structure of the as-prepared catalyst have been confirmed based on FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, EDX, BET, and ICP-OES characterizations. The catalytic performance of the Cu/N-doped carbon spheroids was investigated experimentally. It was found that the Cu/N-doped CS nanocomposite has a high efficiency and recyclability for 5 cycles without any appreciable loss in its catalytic activity in the synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles via the three-component condensation reaction of various epoxide/alkyl or aryl halides, sodium azide, and phenylacetylene in a water medium. Furthermore, significant advantages such as stability, ease of preparation, low cost of the carbon resource, high regioselectivity, and good product yield have been achieved in the heterogeneous catalyst system attractive for large-scale in many industrial products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75803-5.Keywords: Cu/N-doped carbon spheres; Soybean flour; Nanocomposite; 1,4-Disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazole derivativesSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering

Introduction

1,2,3-Triazoles and their derivatives are significant heterocyclic scaffolds that are extensively applied in organic synthesis1, chemical sensors2, coordination chemistry as potent ligands3, agrochemicals agents4, dyes and corrosion inhibitors5–7. Triazoles are found to be potent biological and pharmacological moieties such as antiviral8, antiallergic9, anti-HIV, anticancer10, antibacterial11, antitubercular12, anti-inflammatory13, antimalarial14, and antifungal agents8,15–18. Two principal synthetic methods have been developed for the synthesis of triazoles, including cycloaddition of organic azides and alkynes as Huisgen’s 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction19 and the one-pot multicomponent reaction of epoxides, alkynes, and azides20. Among these two methods, in situ click reaction of azides, epoxides and alkynes are the most direct and convenient pathway because of Huisgen’s reaction drawbacks such as time consuming, high reaction temperature, and low yield and selectivity, which cause its limited use21,22. The multicomponent one-pot reaction of epoxides, alkynes, and azides has been carried out in the presence of copper catalysts [16b]. For this purpose, direct utilization of Cu (I) as a homogeneous catalyst is not a convenient method because of their instability and difficulty in product separation, catalyst recovery, and the leaching of metal ions, which resulted in the impurity of the product23. Since then, reusable and recoverable heterogeneous copper catalysts have been employed in syntheses of triazoles, such as copper(I)-zeolite24, imidazole–Cu(II), copper(I)@phosphorated SiO225, copper-polymer nanocomposite26, polymer-modified Cu/Cu2O, CuSO4.5H2O/sodium ascorbate27, etc.

Carbon is one of the most significant and abundant elements in nature, which is bioavailable, low-cost, and environmentally friendly. Carbonaceous materials as catalysts can be synthesized via efficient and economical hydrothermal carbonization from different widely-used commercial chemical precursors such as starch28, glucose29, cellulose30, and xylose31, which exhibit a variety of morphologies, including carbon fiber32, carbon sphere (CS)33, graphene34,35, and carbon nanotube36. Many studies demonstrate that the physical and electrochemical characteristics of carbon materials are enhanced by doping with heteroatoms, especially nitrogen37,38. In recent years, nitrogen-doped carbon materials have garnered much interest because of convincing advantages, such as porous shell structure which can facilitate the diffusion of reactants and products39,40. Some bio-based precursors with high protein content, like cocoon silk, can be applied for effective nitrogen doping41. Soybean is an inexpensive and abundant substance that is sustainable and contains high nitrogen content42.

Soy flour, as a nitrogen source, is used in N-doping in activated carbons synthesized by glucose43. Different techniques can be applied to produce N-doped carbon spheres, such as thermal activation, chemical vapor deposition, pyrolysis, and solvothermal44–47. Based on these considerations, by our interest in developing efficient, green, and stable supported catalysts, soybean as a renewable carbon resource is used to load copper species onto nitrogen-doped carbon materials as a novel heterogeneous catalyst.

The obtained N-doped carbon spheres from a combination of glucose and soy flour are synthesized via the hydrothermal method at a low temperature and have a good yield, larger size, and suitable affinity for load copper species (Fig. 1).

Schematic process of the synthesis of Cu immobilized onto N-doped carbon spheres derived from soybean.

Concerning the significant features of click reaction, we have used the Cu immobilized onto N-doped carbon spheres derived from soybean as a highly efficient and recoverable catalyst for the synthesis of 1,2,3-triazoles from phenylacetylene, sodium azide, and epoxide/alkyl or aryl halides in water medium. Operational simplicity, higher product yield, catalyst reusability, and economic viability are advantages of this method.

Experimental section

Materials

All materials used in the research without any further purification were obtained from Merck company. In the present study, soybeans were collected from the farms in Golestan province located in northeast of Iran which is the most important center of soybean production in Iran48. Thermo Scientific IA9200, melting point apparatus, was applied to determine the melting point of the synthesized compounds. The FT-IR spectra were recorded on KBr-pressed disks using a Nicolet Fourier spectrophotometer (USA). 1HNMR and 13CNMR spectra were obtained in a Bruker DRX-300 AVANCE spectrometer at 300 and 75 MHz in CDCl3 as solvents. The product ions data were taken on a 5973 Network Mass Selective Detector. The surface morphology of the catalyst was studied using FE-SEM and EDX analysis on TESCAN BRNO-Mira3 LMU to specify the elements. The XRD patterns of the catalyst were recorded on a Bruker D4 Endeavor with a Ni-filtered using monochromatized Cu Kα radiation in the angle 2θ = 20–80 °C (40 kV, 30 mA). The element analysis was performed by a Varian Vista-PRO CCD (Australia). The BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) surface area and pore size distribution was measured on a Belsorp-mini II system at − 196 °C using N2 as the adsorbate.

Preparation of N-doped carbon spheres (N-doped CS) derived from soybean flour by hydrothermal process

In order to prepare N-doped carbon spheres derived from soybean flour in the laboratory, soya flour, which is a fine powder obtained from grinding soybeans, was dispersed along with glucose (1:0.3 w/w ratio) in 15 mL of deionized water by magnetic stirring. To increase the hydrochar yield and fixation of nitrogen, the addition of glucose is necessary. Afterward, to hydrothermal carbonization, the solution was transferred to a stainless-steel autoclave, heated at 180 °C, and maintained for 12 h. The resulting precipitates were recovered by centrifuge, washed with distilled water, filtrated, and dried at 80 °C for 24 h49.

Synthesis of Cu immobilized onto N-doped carbon spheres (N-doped CS)

In the next step, 0.5 g N-doped CS in 50 mL absolute EtOH was dispersed for 15 min, and then a solution of Cu(OAc)2.H2O (1.5 mmol) in absolute EtOH (10 mL) was added to it. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 5 h under an argon atmosphere. After this time, a suspension of 0.5 g NaBH4 in ethanol (10 mL) was injected at a rate of 3 mL/min into the flask content and kept stirring for 48 h. Finally, the obtained Cu/N-doped CS was centrifuged and washed with ethanol several times and dried in an oven under vacuum at 80 °C overnight.

Synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles derivatives in the presence of Cu/N-doped CS

To a solution of phenylacetylene/propargyl esters (1mmol), alkyl halide (1.0 mmol), and sodium azide (1.1 mmol) in water (2 mL), as-synthesis catalyst (5 mol%) was added. The reaction mixture was stirred at 80 °C and monitored by TLC for the appropriate time. After reaction completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the catalyst was separated by simple filtration, washed with water and ethanol, and then dried at 60 °C for the next run. The crude product was diluted with water (10 mL) and extracted with EtOAc (3 × 10 mL), and then the organic phase was washed with water and dried with Na2SO4. The crude product was purified via crystallization from ethanol to provide the corresponding triazole. The synthesized derivatives were identified using melting point, FT-IR, 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR spectroscopy, and mass spectrometry.

Results and discussion

Catalyst characterizations

In the present study, the functional groups present on the Cu/NCS were studied by FT-IR (Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrophotometer). The morphology and structure of the practical synthesis of N-doped CS and Cu/N-doped CS catalyst were confirmed using FE-SEM (field emission scanning electron microscopy). The crystal structure of the as-prepared catalyst was investigated by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). Also, the elemental composition of the catalyst was determined by EDX and EDX-mapping (Energy Disperse X-ray Spectroscopy). The specific surface areas and pore size distributions of carbon spheres were confirmed using Emmett and Teller (BET) surface area analysis by the physical adsorption of nitrogen gas on the solid surface of the sample.

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy of N-doped CS (Fig. 2a), and Cu/N-doped CS catalyst (Fig. 2b) was used to identify different functional groups on the surface of samples and their chemical structure. The appearance of the characteristic band around 3413 cm-1 was related to the stretching vibrations of the O-H (Fig. 2a). The absorption bands at 2935 cm-1 and 2856 cm-1 correspond to the stretching vibration of C-H (Fig. 2a). Bands centered at 1710 cm-1 was C = O stretching vibration from N-doped CS. Moreover, weak intensity absorption bands at 1533 and 1203 cm−1 depicted the presence of ring stretching of conjugated double bond C = N and C–N bond, confirming the presence of nitrogen in the sample (Fig. 2a)50. In addition, FTIR analysis was used to confirm the presence of the Cu immobilized onto N-doped carbon spheres (Fig. 2b). The vibration of Cu-N interaction has appeared at 469 cm-1, while emerging of Cu-O bands were detected at 595 cm-1, which is consistent with previous reports51. Also, the shifting of the O-H, C = N, and C = O bands to lower frequency regions are due to a decrease in electron density due to the successful coordination of the Cu-N and Cu-O bonds.

FT-IR spectra of N-doped CS (a), Cu/N-doped CS catalyst (b), and 5th Cu/N-doped CS catalyst (c).

XRD analysis

The crystalline structure of the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst was recorded by XRD patterns to investigate Cu-oxidation state changes (Fig. 3). The advent of the peaks situated at 2θ = 43.3 (1 1 1), 50.9 (2 0 0), and 74.5 (2 2 0) confirmed the presence of FCC lattice of copper nanoparticles (Cu0) on the surface of the catalyst. The Cu NPs are partially oxidized in atmospheric oxygen, which leads to their oxidation to the copper(I) oxide (Cu2O). The weak diffraction peaks position recorded at 2θ = 36.6 (1 1 1), 42.8 (2 0 0), 61.4 (2 2 0), 73.4 (3 1 1) could be assigned to copper (I) oxide (JCPDS card No. 04-0836)52. The appearance of Cu NPs and Cu2O in the XRD pattern of the Cu/N-doped CS, successfully confirms the immobilization of Cu onto N-doped carbon spheres in the final step of catalyst synthesis.

XRD pattern of the crystalline structure of the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst.

FE-SEM and EDX analyses

To study the microscopic morphologies and structural features of N-doped CS, and the synthesized Cu/N-doped CS catalyst, FE-SEM analysis was utilized (Fig. 4a & b). It is evident from Fig. 3a that the prepared N-doped CS exhibited spherical morphology (diameter of 200–500 nm), which had an excellent dispersity. Interestingly, the microspheres were well preserved after Cu immobilizing onto their surface (Fig. 4b).

FE-SEM images of N-doped CS (a), and the synthesized Cu/N-doped CS (b).

The EDX spectrum can be considered a valuable tool in the elements determination of solid materials that determined by an EDX detector equipped with SEM instrument. The elemental composition spectrum of the as-prepared Cu/N-doped CS catalyst detected the presence of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and copper (as major elements) (Fig. 5). Also, the table of quantitative analysis results of the catalyst can be seen in Fig. 5.

EDX spectrum of the as-prepared Cu/N-doped CS catalyst.

Furthermore, EDX-mapping data of Cu/N-doped CS suggested the uniform doping of copper, nitrogen, oxygen and carbon species, which correctly implied that the Cu immobilized onto N-doped carbon spheres were achieved successfully and complete agreement with the EDX data (Fig. 6).

Catalytic performance of Cu/N-doped CS catalyst in synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles

After confirming the successful synthesis of the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst, the performance of its catalytic activity in the synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles was investigated experimentally. To obtain information on the optimum reaction conditions and evaluate the catalytic efficiency of Cu/ N-doped CS, the reaction between sodium azide, benzyl bromide, and phenylacetylene was selected as a reaction model to optimize the different reaction conditions (Fig. 8).

The evaluation of the catalytic efficiency of Cu/ N-doped CS toward optimization of the synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles.

To optimize the reaction conditions, several parameters were studied, including solvent effect, catalyst amount, molar ratio of NaN3, and reaction temperature. The results are depicted in Table 2 (entries 1–16). In early evaluation, the catalytic role of Cu/N-doped CS in water medium at moderate temperatures (25–90 °C) in the presence of 0.01 g of catalyst (3 mol%), was evaluated (Table 2, entries 1–5). The best yield of the desired product outcome was achieved at 70 °C (Table 2, entry 4). The amount of catalyst loading plays an essential role in optimizing product yields. The best yield of the desired product was found in the 3 mol% of the catalyst, nevertheless, the raising of the catalyst amount didn’t lead to an increase in the yield of the product (Table 2, entry 7). In the next experiment, the influence of several solvents was investigated on the related product formation (Table 2, entries 8–12), in which the desired product was obtained with an appreciable yield in water as an ideal green solvent (Table 2, entry 4). Finally, the effect of the molar ratio of NaN3 was tested in the model reaction. As can be seen, with increasing the amount of NaN3 to 1.2 mmol, the yields of the product significantly did not change (Table 2, entry 13). However, decreasing the amount of NaN3 (1 mmol) led to a decrease in the product yield to 70% (Table 2, entry 14). As a result, the best yield of the desired product was achieved in the presence of 3 mol% of Cu/N-doped CS catalyst in the green solvent at 70 °C (Table 2, entry 4).

By knowing the optimal reaction conditions, the formation of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-tria-zoles derivatives via the reaction between epoxides/halides having electron-donating and -withdrawing groups with phenylacetylene, and sodium azide was expanded. It is evident from Table 2 that the desired 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles were formed as only one regioisomer possible by the azidolysis–cycloaddition reaction, in short times with good to excellent isolated yields (Table 3., entries 1–14). It was found that alkyl- and aryl-substituted epoxides afforded the reactions effectively. Also, dichloroethane and 1,4-bis(chloromethyl) benzene were studied as alkyl dihalide sources (Table 3., entries 9 & 10). It was found that 1,4-bis (chloromethyl) benzene in the presence of phenylacetylene (2 mol) and NaN3 (2 mol), led to the product (4 g) (Table 3., entry 9). Increasing the charge at the benzylic carbon leads to displacing azide with benzylic chlorine via the nucleophilic attachment. Whereas, dichloroethane underwent selective mono-azidation, and resulted in the product (4 h) (Table 3., entry 10). An interesting observation is that in the case of aryl substituted epoxide, a preferential attack on the more hindered carbon atom occurred (Table 3., entry 12). While, alkyl substituted epoxides attacked the sterically less hindered carbon atom (Table 3., entries 11, 13 and 14).

High regioselectivity of the Cu/N-doped CS in the detection of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles as the only product of the click reaction was confirmed by simple one-dimensional 13C NMR spectroscopy with the appearance of a 13C signal of at δ ≈ 120 ppm for compound 4a (Fig. S2), which is due to C5 of the 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles54.

According to the valuable information obtained from the mechanistic evaluation of this reaction by one-dimensional 13CNMR spectroscopy, a proposed pathway for one-pot synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles catalyzed using Cu/N-doped CS is observed in Fig. 9.

The proposed reaction mechanism for the regioselective formation of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles catalyzed by Cu/N-doped CS.

In the first step, a terminal alkyne binds to a copperI center as a π-ligand (I). The formation of stable σ, π-(copper) acetylide complex led to an increase the acidity of the alkyne terminal proton, providing deprotonation in the aqueous system without the use of the strong base55. Concurrent, in situ generated alkyl azide intermediate (II) coordinated to the copper center of acetylide via the nitrogen proximal to carbon (III)56. Subsequently, by nucleophilic attack nitrogen of the azide to the C-2 carbon of the acetylide, an intermediate six-membered cupracycle was formed (IV)57. The next step involves by ring contraction, leading to a triazolide intermediate (V). Ultimately, through a rapid protonation of the copperI triazolide, the triazole product is released (VI), and the active copper species catalyst is regenerated, thereby completing the catalytic cycle.

The approach does not lead to the formation of 1,5-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles (VII), due to stronger nucleophilicity of the alkylated amine in comparison to the terminal nitrogen of alkyl azide, at moderate temperature in an aqueous medium.

The efficient reusability of the heterogeneous catalyst is considered a severe requirement environmentally and economically. In this aspect, to test the reusability, and renewability of the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst, the model reaction was carried out under optimized conditions (Table 2, entry 4). In brief, upon completion of the reaction in each run, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature. Then, ethyl acetate was added to the mixture, and the catalyst was separated using a simple filtration from the reaction mixture, washed with diluted ethyl acetate (2 × 5 mL), and dried under vacuum at 80 °C overnight for use in the subsequent execution. It was found that over five consecutive cycles of uses of the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst, no noticeable decrease was observed in catalytic activity and selectivity, indicating reasonably good catalyst durability in the reaction (Fig. 10).

Recycle of Cu/N-doped CS catalyst in synthesis of the 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles for compound 4a.

To confirm the catalyst stability in the synthesis of the 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles after five runs in the model reaction, structural changes of the catalyst during the reaction were evaluated by FT-IR spectroscopy and ICP-OES analysis. The FT-IR spectrum of Cu/N-doped CS after five recycling runs, obviously shows that frequencies, intensities, and types of the absorption bands are well-preserved (Fig. 2c). Besides, according to the ICP-OES analysis, the amount of Cu immobilized onto N-doped CS shows 19.2 wt% Cu NPs that after the fifth run, a slight decrease has been observed (copper content after 5th run = 18.9 wt%). Based on the obtained results, no significant leaching of Cu NPs was observed from the surface of the support.

In addition to the stability of the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst throughout five consecutive cycles of reaction and its easy recovery, it is also very beneficial if the reactions are carried out on a gram-scale. For this purpose, straightforward gram-scale reactions for the formation of desired 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazole 4a were performed using sodium azide, benzyl bromide, phenylacetylene in the presence of Cu/N-doped CS catalyst under optimized reaction conditions (Table 1, entry 4). The reactions were found to proceed successfully to give the corresponding product 4a in excellent yields and the Cu/N-doped CS catalyst could be preserved its great performance. To demonstrate additional benefits of the present study, the efficiency of the as-prepared catalyst has been compared to previously researched ones using copper-based catalysts in the reaction of benzyl bromide, sodium azide, and phenylacetylene under different catalytic conditions (Table 5). It is reputed that maintained catalysts show good catalytic performance. However, it is seen that Cu/N-doped CS as a green, low-cost, environmentally friendly, and impressive catalyst is more efficient in one-pot synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazoles than almost all the catalytic systems reported in Table 4. It is evident that different homogeneous and heterogeneous Cu catalysts show excellent catalytic activity in azide-alkyne cycloaddition reactions (Table 5, entries 1–10). However, some of them suffer from the un-reusability of the catalyst, prolonged reaction time (Table 5, entries 1–5, 7, 9 & 10), use of hazardous solvents (Table 5, entry 7), and the expensive catalytic systems (Table 5, entries 1, 2, 4, 6 & 10). Although, some catalytic systems like CuIL1PPh3, were prepared using readily available reagents having significant advantages including low catalyst loading, environmental friendliness, high yield, shorter reaction time, but using an ultrasound irradiation is one of the limitations of this method (Table 5, entry 8). Likewise, some copper-based heterogeneous catalysts are more active at room temperature in the presence of water than the other copper‐based heterogeneous catalysts. However, the presence of expensive reagents or the lengthy manufacturing process of the catalyst, the long reaction time, and not achievement of the gram-scale synthesis of product, are the disadvantages of these methods (Table 5, entries 1,2 & 10). It is observed that Cu/N-doped CS as a green, low-cost, available, reusable, environmentally friendly, and easy gram-scale supply of catalyst, has been used for high exclusive regioselectivity one-pot synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1,2,3-triazoles.

Conclusions

In this work, N-doped carbon spheroids were produced from soy flour, as a nitrogen source assisted by glucose via the hydrothermal method. This green, stable, and renewable carbon resource having a larger size, and suitable affinity was used to load copper species onto nitrogen-doped carbon as a novel heterogeneous catalyst for synthesizing 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazole derivatives. The characterization results of the as-prepared catalyst have been explained based on morphological, structural, and chemical characterizations (FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, EDX, BET, and ICP-OES). Cu/N-doped CS nanocomposite was certified to be a highly efficient and recoverable catalyst for the synthesis of 1,2,3-triazoles via the three-component condensation reaction of various epoxide/alkyl or aryl halides, sodium azide, and phenylacetylene in a water medium. The availability and low cost of the carbon resource, followed by its synthesis at low temperature, reusability (for at least five repeated cycles without appreciable loss of activity without appreciable loss of activity), ease of handling, high regioselectivity, and higher product yield, make this as-prepared catalyst very suitable candidate for the synthesis of 1,4-disubstituted 1H-1,2,3-triazole derivatives.
