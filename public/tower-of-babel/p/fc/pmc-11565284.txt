Electrochemical Reduction of Nitrogen to Ammonia Using Zinc Telluride
Authors: Sham J. Mane, Nesta B. Joseph, Rekha Kumari, Awadhesh Narayan, Aninda J. Bhattacharyya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Electrosynthesis of ammonia (NH3), an important constituent molecule of various commercial fertilizers, is a promising and sustainable alternative strategy compared with the century-old Haber-Bosch process. Herein, zinc telluride (ZnTe) is demonstrated as an efficient electrocatalyst for reducing nitrogen (N2) under ambient conditions to NH3. In this simple chemical strategy, Zn preferentially binds N2 over hydrogen (H2), and Te, by virtue of its superior electronic properties, enhances the electrocatalytic activity of ZnTe. The analysis of the X-ray diffraction data using the Bravais-Friedel-Donnay-Harker (BFDH) theory predicted a crystal geometry with the active electrocatalytic sites predominantly confined to the (111) planes of ZnTe. The preferential binding of nitrogen (N2; adsorption energy = −0.043 eV) over hydrogen (H2, adsorption energy = −0.028 eV) to Zn on the (111) plane of ZnTe is further confirmed by density functional theory. The ZnTe catalyst is observed to be stable in the acidic medium and delivers a very high yield of NH3 (19.85 μg/h–1 mgcat–1) and a Faradaic efficiency of 6.24% at −0.6 V (versus RHE). Additional verification experiments do not reveal the formation of side products (such as NH2–NH2) during N2 reduction by ZnTe. Further, density functional theory calculations strongly predict that the electrocatalytic reduction of N2 to NH3 by ZnTe preferentially occurs via the alternate pathway.

Introduction

Ammonia (NH3) inherently contributes to the diverse needs of mankind, ranging from food production to pharmaceuticals and lifestyle.1 Recently, NH3 has been considered a potential source of renewable energy. It has a high hydrogen content (≈17.6 wt %) and high energy density (≈4.3 kW h–1).2,3 The production of NH3 takes place via the conventional century-old Haber-Bosch process, which involves high temperatures (400–500 °C) and pressures (20–40 MPa) and consumes more than 1% of global energy.4 Moreover, this method generates significant greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide (CO2), contributing significantly to global environmental pollution.5 To address the challenges of conventional NH3 synthesis, NH3 synthesis under ambient conditions will be an alternative, energy-efficient, and sustainable strategy. The electrochemical nitrogen reduction reaction (ENRR) is a promising approach that enables NH3 synthesis at room temperature using renewable electricity.6 This will drastically reduce CO2 emissions and energy consumption, leading to the minimization of environmental damage.7 The efficiency of the NRR depends on the electrocatalyst function immobilized at the gas diffusion layer of the porous cathode. Thus, developing effective and durable electrocatalysts for ENRR is key to enabling NH3 synthesis from N2 at room temperature and pressure using an electrochemical (reduction) process.

The N≡N triple bond has a high bond energy (941.0 kJ mol–1), thus making it difficult to activate the inert nitrogen (N2).8 NRR occurs at ≈0.092 V (versus RHE), close to the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER ≈ 0.000 V versus RHE). The latter, however, exhibits much faster reaction kinetics. This poses substantial challenges in selectively and efficiently reducing N2, leading to high NH3 yields.9,10 Ideally, electrocatalysts with a weak affinity for hydrogen binding would be a favorable choice for ENRR. Transition metals are effective catalysts for ENRR due to their ability to facilitate electron transfer. Among them, zinc (Zn) exhibits weak hydrogen binding and demonstrates limited promotion of the HER, thereby making it a promising candidate for ENRR. Additionally, zinc (Zn) is stable, abundant, and demonstrates excellent catalytic activity, making it a desirable choice for ENRR.11,12 Recently, transition metal chalcogenides have gained remarkable attention as alternative materials to diverse applications primarily due to their versatile covalent bonding and structural properties.13−16 Tellurides have garnered significant interest due to their unique properties,17 leading to their extensive exploration in diverse electrochemical applications, including batteries,18 supercapacitors,19 and catalysis.20 Combining tellurium with zinc, as in zinc telluride (ZnTe), can exhibit synergy, leading to improved electrocatalytic activity depending on the type of reaction and conditions.21−24 Recently, ZnTe has been demonstrated to facilitate the CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR)25 and oxygen evolution reaction (OER),26 which exhibit a similar underlying electrochemical redox process.27,28 Here, ZnTe is demonstrated as an alternative, efficient electrocatalyst for ENRR. The interaction between telluride and zinc is expected to generate new active sites, alter the reaction pathway, facilitate fast electron transfer, and improve catalytic activity.

Crystal morphology, e.g., macroscopic crystallite/particle shape, plays a major role in various fields, such as pharmaceuticals,29 organic semiconductors,30 catalysis,31,32 and energy.33−35 A specific shape (and size) crystal exhibits beneficial crystal facets and sites, significantly influencing the performance of the materials. Apart from the specific choice of chemical compositions, the modulation of the electrocatalyst crystal morphology employed in ENRR provides additional ways to augment the electrocatalytic activity, increasing the yield of the value-added product. Thus, an accurate fundamental understanding of the composite crystal morphology will enhance our understanding of the electrocatalytic process and boost performance. Several first-principles theories, e.g., BFDH (Bravais-Friedel-Donnay-Harker) law,36 HP (Hartman–Perdok),37,38 and HNB (Harsh-Nidhi-Binay)39 have been proposed to predict the macroscopic shape of the crystals using the internal crystal structures. These models have successfully predicted the external morphology of various organic40 and inorganic41 crystals. The BFDH model, which predicts crystal geometry by considering the lattice and symmetry, can illuminate the effect of the crystal planes and facets on the electrocatalytic function of ZnTe. BFDH yields a list of potential growth faces and their growth rates, often providing an accurate description that helps fully understand their morphology. The analysis also identifies the active sites for the effective interaction between their surfaces and reactive species. The delicate balance between the macroscopic shape of the crystal and its internal arrangement of atoms will augment the electrocatalytic applications of ZnTe in the ENRR.

Based on the above chemical design and methodologies, we investigated the ENRR to NH3 under ambient conditions using ZnTe via experiment and theory. The N2 reduction to NH3 by ZnTe is accounted for based on the synergy arising from the preferential adsorption of N2 on Zn and the beneficial electronic characteristics of Te. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations provide insights into the probable binding sites and reaction pathways for converting N2 to NH3.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Chemicals used for this study including Zn metal powder (99.99%), tellurium metal powder (99.99%), sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), sodium nitroferricyanide-(III) dehydrate (C5FeCN6Na2O·2H2O), hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O), and Nafion solution (5%) are all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, whereas Nafion membrane 211 and carbon cloth (CC) are purchased from fuel cell store. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), salicylic acid (C7H6O3), sodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7), hydrochloric acid (HCl), acetic acid (CH3COOH), N-(1-naphthyl)-ethylenediamine dihydrochloride (C12H16Cl2N2), and sulfanilic acid (C6H7NO3S) are all purchased from SDFCL and used without further purification.

Synthesis of Catalyst

ZnTe is synthesized using a solid-state reaction comprising an optimized heating protocol.42 The equimolar amount of Zn and Te is transferred into a quartz tube and vacuum-sealed at a pressure of 4 × 10–6 mbar. The sealed tube is kept in an electric resistive rotary furnace and heated to 450 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the temperature is further increased to 850 °C, and the tube is rotated at 24 h intervals for a total rotation time of 48 h to achieve uniform homogenization of the melt. After 48 h, the loaded quartz tube is rapidly quenched in ice water. The obtained bulk sample is ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle, and the resulting powder is characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern is recorded using a PANalytical Empyrean X-ray diffractometer embedded Cu Kα radiation with a wavelength of 1.5418 Å in the 2θ range of 20–80° with step size 0.020°. X’Pert high score plus (version 4.8)43 is used to analyze the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern. Profile-fitting refinement is conducted with JANA 2006 Software, utilizing the cell parameters to enhance the accuracy and precision of the analysis. This approach aids in fine-tuning and improving the fit between the observed X-ray pattern and the theoretical profile generated from the given cell parameters.44 Profile parameters such as GU, GV, GW, LX, and LY are refined by using the Pseudo-Voigt function to model diffraction peak shapes accurately. Powder X-ray diffraction pattern is refined and analyzed through a Rietveld refinement method using the FULLPROF suite program.45 The background, including a pronounced “amorphous hump”, is subtracted before the refinement. The profile is fitted with Pseudo-Voigt functions. Crystal representations are obtained using VESTA software. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Thermo Fisher K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometry system. The sample’s morphology is studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Ultra-55FESEM Karl Zeiss). Chemical composition and elemental mapping of the sample are performed using the EDS facility of the FESEM. UV–vis spectrophotometry (Lambda 750, PerkinElmer) is used to collect UV–vis absorption spectra.

Bravais-Friedel-Donnay-Harker (BFDH) Analysis

Analyzing the .cif file from the PXRD through Rietveld refinement, Bravais-Friedel-Donnay-Harker (BFDH) analysis is conducted to estimate the faces that are supposed to appear in the crystal morphology.46 In this approach, the growth rate of a particular plane is assumed to be inversely proportional to the interplanar distance (dhkl). Consequently, growth in a grown crystal is faster in a direction with a lower “dhkl” value. The crystal pattern is dominated by faces, namely, (111), (200), (020), (002), (1̅11̅), and (111̅) (Figure 1c, as revealed by the BFDH morphological prediction [summary shown in Table S2)]. According to the BFDH law, the growth rate of a crystal plane is inversely proportional to its interplanar distance, corresponding to a lower growth rate for the plane, indicating greater significance for the growth of that particular crystal plane in the crystal structure. The interplanar distance of the (111) plane is the largest, which suggests that this plane has a lower growth rate compared to others. This implies greater significance for the growth and interaction sites of the (111) plane in the crystal structure. The obtained results, derived from geometrical inferences, are supported by density function theoretical (DFT) calculations. This combination of experimental and theoretical approaches adds credibility to the findings by aligning geometric predictions with the results obtained through quantum mechanical calculations.

Electrochemical Measurement

All measurements are performed on an electrochemical workstation (CHI645D) with a standard three-electrode system in an in-house developed “H-Type” cell comprising a proton exchange membrane. A Pt wire and a Ag/AgCl electrode are used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively, and ZnTe is used as the working electrode. For the preparation of the ZnTe working electrode, typically 5 mg of catalyst was dispersed in a 450 μL mixture of water/ethanol (1:2 v/v) and 50 μL of Nafion (5%) and kept for ultrasonication for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous ink. The prepared 40 μL ink is drop-cast on carbon cloth (1 × 1 cm2) and dried overnight at 60 °C in the oven. The proton exchange membrane (Nafion 211) is pretreated first in H2O2 (5 wt %) for 1 h, 0.5 M H2SO4, and then pure water for another 1 h at 80 °C. Then, the pretreated membrane is immersed in pure water for subsequent use. Before the NRR test, 55 mL of the 0.1 M HCl electrolyte is purged first with Ar, followed by N2 for 30 min in the cathodic compartment. The NRR test is conducted with the continuous N2 supply for 2 h. All of the potentials versus Ag/AgCl are scaled to the reversible hydrogen electrode RHE: (ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.197+ 0.0591 × pH)

Computational Details

The first-principles calculations are carried out based on the density functional theory (DFT) framework as implemented in the Quantum Espresso code.47,48 A 2 × 2 × 2 supercell of ZnTe is considered with the (111) plane as the catalytic surface. The surface is modeled by relaxing only the top few layers of ZnTe, keeping the rest fixed, and creating a vacuum of ∼15 Å along the surface normal to avoid periodic interactions. Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) form of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) for the exchange-correlation functional49 with a kinetic energy cutoff of 55 Ry, is used, along with the ultrasoft pseudopotentials50 to describe the core electrons. The Brillouin zone is sampled over a uniform Γ-centered k-mesh of 3 × 3 × 1. The geometries of all of the intermediates are relaxed until forces on each atom are less than 0.03 eV/Å. The adsorption energy (Eads) for N2 and H2 molecules on the ZnTe surface is calculated by subtracting the energies for catalyst (Ecat) and molecule (Emol) from the total energy (Etotal): Eads = Etotal – Ecat – Emol

The computational hydrogen electrode (CHE)51 framework is used to calculate the Gibbs free energy associated with each elementary step, with the zero-point energies and entropies calculated using normal modes associated with each intermediate. The experimental standard enthalpy and entropy of gaseous species are obtained from the NIST-JANAF thermochemical tables.52

Determination of Hydrazine Hydrate (N2H4)

One of the side products that is formed in the ENRR is determined quantitively using the Watt and Chrisp method54 spectrophotometrically. The colored solution is prepared by taking 5.99 g of p-(diethylamino) benzaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) and is added to 30 mL of HCl and 300 mL of absolute ethanol. Then, after the ENRR experiment, 5 mL of the electrolyte is taken and added to the colored reagent, mixed well, and kept under undisturbed conditions for 15 min. The absorbance spectra are recorded at λmax = 455 nm using a UV–vis spectrometer. The calibration plot is plotted with different concentrations (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 μg/mL) of hydrazine hydrate standard solution. The good linear regression curves obtained with varying concentrations of NH2–NH2, represented by the equation: y = 0.4232x + 0.024 and R2 = 0.996.

Determination of NOx Contaminants

The presence of NO2 species in the gas source is determined quantitatively using Griess reagents55 by spectroscopic technique. The Griess reagent is prepared by dissolution of 0.5 g of sulfanilic acid in 90 mL of Millipore water and 5 mL of acetic acid, followed by the addition of 5 mg of N-(1-naphthyl)-ethylenediamine dihydrochloride, and the solution is adjusted to 100 mL. Then, 1 mL of electrolyte is added to 4 mL of Griess reagent. Further, the solution is kept at rest for 15 min, and then absorption spectra are recorded at λmax = 540 nm using a UV–vis spectrometer. The calibration plot is plotted with different concentrations of sodium nitrite (Merck) solution with different concentrations in 0.1 M HCl. The absorption versus concentration plot shows a good linear relationship between the absorption and concentration of NO2.

NO3 shows typical absorption to ultraviolet light at the wavelength of λmax = 220 nm. In a typical procedure, 5.0 mL of standard solutions is added to the test tubes, followed by 0.1 mL of 1.0 M HCl. After shaking up and standing for 5 min, the concentration of NO3 is measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength range from 200 to 300 nm. The calibration plot is plotted with different concentrations of lithium nitrate solution with a series of different concentrations in water. The absorption versus concentration plot shows a good linear relationship between the absorption and concentration of NO3.

Results and Discussion

The Rietveld refinement (Figure 1a) of powder X-ray diffraction data confirms that ZnTe crystallizes in the space group F-43m (JCPDS #00-015-0746), where both Zn and Te constitute an asymmetric cell (Figure 1b).56

The obtained refinement parameters (cf. Supporting Information) for ZnTe are as follows: Rwp = 8.0900, Rexp = 6.8801, Rp = 6.1813, and Chi2 = 1.38. These values are in accordance with standard data as per JCPDS #00-015-0746. All powder X-ray diffraction data details are shown in Table S1. dhkl values are highest (3.51 Å) for the (111) plane, which refers to the large distance between atoms significantly increasing the availability of a large surface area. Furthermore, the illustrative representations of the ZnTe crystal structure are shown in Figure 1b. The Zn–Te bond length is found to be 2.650 Å. The cell parameters are as follows: a = b = c = 6.12 A°, α = β = γ = 90°, volume = 229.25 cm3. This describes the overall shape and size of the catalyst, which will significantly impact the efficiency of the N2 gas adsorption. Each Zn atom is bonded with four equivalent Te atoms in the crystal structure to form corner-sharing ZnTe4 tetrahedra. Similarly, each Te atom is bonded to four equivalent Zn atoms to form corner-sharing TeZn4 tetrahedra. A greater number of prominent peaks are observed in the (111) plane, consistent with findings in other studies, suggesting that the (111) plane is more efficient for binding to N2 gas.

We further quantified the crystal morphology using the BFDH analysis shown in Figure 1c. The BFDH will expectedly illuminate the crystal morphology, especially active facets/sites of ZnTe, which will contribute significantly to the catalytic activity of NRR. The crystallographic details obtained from BFDH analysis are shown in Table S2. Notably, the (111) crystallographic plane exhibits the largest facet area. This indicates that the (111) face is the most morphologically significant and highlights the concurrent activity of the Zn and Te atoms in the electrocatalyst. Telluride plays an important role in the electrocatalytic N2 reduction reaction by enhancing the conversion of N2 into NH3. The telluride in the ZnTe electrocatalyst activates the basal plane (111) and the under-coordinated Zn sites for superior utilization in the electrocatalytic process. Applying the BFDH model for surface analysis revealed a minimal rugosity value of 1.139 for the (111) plane, which indicates a surface with exceptional smoothness. This further signifies that N2 adsorption will preferentially occur on the (111) plane. Skewness, a metric for symmetry and its deviation, provides insights into the direction and degree of asymmetry within a data set. The highest positive skewness value of 0.411 suggests a prevalence of peaks over valleys on the (111) plane, thereby confirming the enhanced efficiency in N2 adsorption. Additionally, positive kurtosis values indicate sharp peaks with long and thick tails, reflecting an increased surface area in the (111) plane. This heightened surface complexity contributes to the overall efficiency of nitrogen gas adsorption in the (111) plane. This implies greater significance for the growth and interaction sites of the (111) plane in the ZnTe crystal structure.

Scanning electron micrograph (Figure S1) reveals the agglomeration of ZnTe particles, forming relatively large aggregates (≈200 nm). Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis (Figure S2) confirms the homogeneous distribution of Zn and Te throughout the material, suggesting the successful formation of ZnTe, as described earlier in the context of the XRD findings. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of ZnTe is performed and included in Figure S3. TEM revealed the presence of both spherical and block-like nanoparticles with diameters of approximately 186 nm. The particles are observed to be in an aggregated state. These findings align with the results obtained from SEM (Figure S1). Additionally, the interplanar distance between the two planes is found to be 4.18 nm, as illustrated in Figure S3. The existence of Zn, Te, and C is confirmed through the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum in the energy range 0–1200 eV, as shown in Figure S4. The characteristic peaks of Zn2+Figure 1d are situated at 1044.8 eV (2p1/2) and 1021.8 eV (2p3/2). XPS of Te shows doublet peaks of Te2– for Te 3d3/2 and 3d5/2 situated at 583.07 and 572.68 eV, respectively Figure 1e. Te peaks, 3d3/2 and 3d5/2, observed at 586.98 and 576.78 eV, are assigned to TeO2. The observance of TeO2 is due to the possible oxidation of surface-bound telluride ions.

Electrocatalytic NRR is carried out in an in-house assembled H-type cell, as shown in Figure S5. N2 purity is quantitively ascertained before the electrochemical studies. This exercise is essential to rule out interference from possible impurities, e.g., traces of NH3 and NOx, which may otherwise severely infringe on the key findings of the work.57−59 The UV–vis absorption spectra (Figure S6) of the 0.1 M HCl electrolyte (black line) in the absence and presence of 14N2 (ultrahigh-purity grade, 99.999%, red line) are recorded. The absence of NH4Cl formation indicated the absence of NH3/NOx in the 14N2 feed gas. Subsequently, additional verification testing confirmed the absence of nitrite (NO2–, Figure S7) and nitrate (NO3–, Figure S8) in nitrogen gas (N2). The systematic calibrations substantiate the purity of the N2 gas used for probing the electrochemical NRR by using ZnTe.

The catalytic activity of the prepared catalyst is evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) within a potential window −0.2 to −1.0 V (versus. RHE) under N2- and Ar-saturated atmospheres. The deviation in the cathode current density is observed at −0.4 V when the electrolyte is purged with N2 and Ar (red and black lines, respectively, as in Figure 2a). Notably, the ZnTe catalyst enhanced the current density for the N2-saturated electrolyte compared to the Ar-saturated electrolyte in the potential range of −0.5 to −0.9 V versus RHE. The observed difference can be attributed to the interaction between protons, and N2 molecules adsorbed on the surface of ZnTe. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (Figure S9) indicates that the charge transfer resistance, Rct, of bare carbon cloth (CC) is 1193 Ω. The ZnTe catalyst supported on the CC, however, exhibited a lower charge transfer resistance of 839 Ω. The reduction in resistance suggests that the CC-ZnTe is more conducting due to exposed active metal sites. This enhanced charge transfer capability highlights the superior electrochemical performance of the ZnTe/CC catalyst.

This is also supported via controlled experiments, as demonstrated in Figure S10. UV–vis spectra reveal a clear absorbance band at 660 nm when the solution is purged with N2. This indicates that specific active sites and facets of ZnTe facilitate electrochemical NRR. ENRR is also conducted with Zn and Te (Figure S11). Though the ENRR is higher in the case of Zn compared with Te, it is still significantly lower than that of ZnTe. The enhanced performance of Zn vis a vis Te confirms our design strategy that Zn is the active site for N2 adsorption and is preferred over H2. This is also confirmed by DFT calculations, which predict Zn to be the preferred site for N2 adsorption over Te. Based on LSV results, the approximate potential range of −0.5 to −0.9 V is chosen for the subsequent NRR studies. The catalytic stability is confirmed using continuous chronoamperometry testing over 2 to 12 h at −0.6 V. At shorter time scales, the current density increases rapidly and settles to a constant saturation value. No decline in the current density is observed thereafter, indicating the excellent electrochemical stability of ZnTe for NRR (Figure 2b). Figure 2b (inset) shows the chronoamperometry profiles at other potential values, in the range of −0.5 to −0.8 V (versus RHE) for the N2-saturated electrolyte. The quantitative determination of NH3 is obtained using the Indophenol Blue method.60 The calibration curve for the estimation of NH4Cl is shown in Figure S12 and is used to calculate the generated NH3 concentration following electrolysis. The NH3 yield and Faradaic efficiency (FE) at different potentials are calculated using eqs 1 and 2 and are shown in Figure 2c,d. As evident from Figure 2d and Table S3, the NH3 yield (and Faradaic efficiency) shows a nonmonotonic variation with sweep potential. The NH3 yield (and Faradaic efficiency) increases from −0.5 V to a maximum at −0.6 V. The ZnTe catalyst achieves the highest yield of 19.85 μg h–1 mgcat–1 and Faradaic efficiency of 6.24% at −0.6 V in 0.1 M HCl electrolyte (comparative performance data are compiled in Table S4). As the potential becomes more negative, both the NH3 yield and Faradaic efficiency NH3 yield decrease slightly, indicating a dominance of the HER over the ENRR. A possible byproduct NH2–NH2 is detected using the Watt and Chrisp method.54 The absence of absorption at λmax = 455 nm strongly confirms that (Figure S13) NH2–NH2 does not form during the electrocatalysis process. Several controlled experiments are performed to confirm the origin of the generated NH3. Specifically, at open-circuit potential (OCP = −0.3 V) using carbon cloth as the substrate, the absorption band at λmax = 660 nm occurred solely in the presence of N2, indicating the conversion of N2 to NH3. The lower-intensity absorbance band (Figure S10) under an Ar atmosphere is likely attributed to trace amounts of NH3 contamination from the surrounding air.

Additional investigations via X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) are performed to study the catalyst’s structure, morphology, and valence states following prolonged electrocatalytic activity. The powder XRD patterns of ZnTe, CC, and CC-ZnTe after 12 h of chronoamperometry measurement are analyzed (Figure S14). The ZnTe sample exhibited agglomeration of the nanoparticles, similar to the situation before the start of electrocatalysis (SEM: Figure S1, TEM: Figure S3). The results demonstrated that the catalyst maintains its structure even after long cycling. The XPS data revealed an increase in the intensity of the Te-peak and a decrease in the oxide peak intensity after long cycling, which is attributed to the reduction of TeO2 to Te (from the Te4+ to Te0 state). This reduction process indicates that impurities such as TeO2 are removed from the electrode and converted to pure ZnTe, thereby enhancing the activity of the ZnTe catalyst.

Density functional calculations are carried out to gain atomistic insight into the experimental results. For the first-principles calculations, the (111) surface of ZnTe is considered (Figure 3) since experiments indicate the (111) plane to be one of the catalytically active planes. The Zn site is considered the adsorption site (marked in red square in Figure 3a) since the transition metals, in general, have a higher affinity toward N2 adsorption61,62 compared to H2.

In electrochemical NRR, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) is the competing reaction. The N2 and H2 adsorption energies are calculated per the formalism stated in the Supporting Information. The adsorption energies on the Zn surface are −0.043 eV and −0.028 eV for N2 and H2 molecules, respectively. Additionally, the adsorption energies on the Te surface for N2 and H2 are obtained to be −0.0103 eV and −0.0125 eV, respectively (Figure S15), indicating that the Zn surface has a higher affinity toward the adsorption of N2 compared to the Te surface.

The density of states (DOS) associated with the two molecules adsorbed on the catalytic surface is shown in Figure 3b,c. ZnTe is a semiconductor with a bandgap of around 2.2 eV. From Figure 3b,c, it is seen that the contribution at the Fermi level predominantly arises from the surface ZnTe states, while the bulk states remain unclosed. Comparing the DOS of N2 and H2 adsorption, it is observed that the N2 states exist within the bulk bandgap, while the H2 states are buried deep inside the valence band (ca. −6 eV below the Fermi level). (a) Optimized geometries and free energy diagram. The alternate pathway (red) is found to be a slightly more favorable path. The potential determining step is the formation of *NH3 from *NH2 prior to the release of the second NH3 molecule.

DFT calculations strongly suggest that ZnTe reduces N2 to NH3 via the alternate pathway, as shown in Figure 4. Also, in both cases, the highest free energy barrier is the transition from *NH2 to *NH3 to form the second NH3 molecule. Hence, this chemical formation can be considered as the potential determining step during the NRR process catalyzed by ZnTe. The DOS associated with each reaction intermediate in the reaction pathway is plotted. As previously mentioned, the metallic states near the Fermi level originate from the ZnTe surface (shown in red). The states associated with the activated intermediate species remain close to the Fermi level. When the first hydrogen atom is added to *N2, the DOS within the bulk gap undergoes a splitting, with nearly equal contributions from both N atoms. On the addition of a second hydrogen atom, the peak below the Fermi level shifts further down into the valence band in both the distal and alternate pathways. It arises from the N atom bonded to the Zn site in the former, while the major contributor is the N atom away from the Zn site in the latter. In the distal pathway (Figure S16), on the addition of a third hydrogen atom, the *NH3 states spread out into the conduction band. A similar spread is also observed just before the release of the second *NH3 in the distal pathway. In the alternate pathway, a similar spread in the conduction band is a mixture of contributions from both N atoms and associated hydrogen atoms originating from the intermediate (Figure S17).

Conclusions

ZnTe has been demonstrated here as an efficient electrocatalyst for reducing N2 to NH3 under ambient conditions. The crystal structure of ZnTe is shown to play a major role in the NRR process. Through systematic experimental and theoretical investigations, Zn localized at specific planes/facets exhibits preferential adsorption of N2 over H2, leading to high selectivity and efficient conversion of N2 → NH3. The yield of the NH3 versus potential, specifically the potential range pertaining to NRR, is observed to be nonmonotonic. At a potential of −0.6 V (versus RHE), the highest NH3 production rate at 19.85 μg h–1 mgcat–1 is achieved at a faradic efficiency of 6.24%. Theoretical investigations convincingly reveal an alternate pathway for reducing N2 → NH3. In addition to designing novel electrocatalysts solely based on chemical composition, the present work highlights the critical role of the electrocatalyst crystal structure. Thorough know-how of the crystal structure inherently connects with the optimal reaction pathway for the electrochemical synthesis using NRR. The approach adopted here has multiple benefits, mainly providing invaluable chemical intuitions for improved structurally engineered electrocatalysts leading to a high yield of NH3 at ambient conditions.
