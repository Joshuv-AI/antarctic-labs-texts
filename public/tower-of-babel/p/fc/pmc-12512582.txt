Effects of high temperature with pressure polymerization on the physical and mechanical properties and dimensional changes of 3D-printed denture teeth resin
Authors: Shanglin Wu, Yuriko Komagamine, Tamaki Hada, Keyu Qi, Pong Pongprueksa, Manabu Kanazawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal post-treatment methods for improving the mechanical properties and degree of conversion of three-dimensional (3D)-printed denture teeth resin remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the impact of high temperature with pressure polymerization on the degree of conversion, Vickers hardness, flexural strength and modulus, and dimensional changes of 3D-printed denture teeth resin.MethodsIn total, 180 specimens (Dima Print Denture Teeth A3; Kulzer) fabricated from the denture teeth resin using a digital light processing 3D printer (Cara printer 4.0; Kulzer) were randomly divided into five groups: no post-treatment (control) and post-treatment at 121 °C for 4, 30, 60, or 90 min. The air pressure applied during the treatments was set at 2 bar and was maintained throughout the post-polymerization process. Furthermore, 30 rectangular-shaped specimens (22 × 4 × 2 mm) were used for the degree of conversion test (6 specimens/group), 50 disk-shaped specimens (10 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness) were used for the Vickers hardness test (10 specimens/group), and 50 bar-shaped specimens (25 × 2 × 2 mm) each were used for the flexural strength, flexural modulus, and dimensional change tests (10 specimens/group). The degree of conversion, flexural modulus, and dimensional change of width were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference post hoc test. The flexural strength, Vickers hardness, and dimensional change of length were analyzed by Welch’s ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons post hoc tests. The dimensional change of thickness was analyzed with a Kruskal–Wallis analysis and a post hoc comparison using the Mann–Whitney U test (α = 0.05).ResultsThe degree of conversion and Vickers hardness values were the lowest in the control group and the highest in the 121 °C/90 min group. The flexural properties and error rates for length, width, and thickness were also the highest in the 121 °C/90 min group.ConclusionsPost-treatment at 121 °C for 90 min increases the degree of conversion and surface hardness of 3D-printed denture teeth resin, without damaging the resin’s flexural properties, and maintains dimensional changes within acceptable limits.

Background

Computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing technology (CAD/CAM) has transformed complete denture fabrication through digital workflows, offering superior precision and efficiency over conventional methods [1–6]. CAD/CAM methods include subtractive manufacturing, which cuts preformed blocks. They also encompass additive manufacturing, such as three-dimensional (3D) printing, which builds layers without material waste. Additive manufacturing excels in complex shapes and customization, with stereolithography and digital light processing (DLP) as the prominent techniques [7–9]. DLP offers better resolution and faster production, making it the preferred method for denture fabrication [10–12].

Artificial denture teeth must have sufficient Vickers hardness (VHN), flexural strength (FS), and flexural modulus (FM) to withstand chewing forces [13]. While digital workflows offer advantages, 3D-printed denture teeth often exhibit inferior mechanical properties compared with milled ones [14, 15]. The rapid layer-by-layer printing reduces the curing intensity, limiting polymer chain crosslinking [16]. Shin et al. [17] reported that the relatively low degree of matrix crosslinking and insufficient polymerization in methyl methacrylate-based denture teeth resin yielded the lowest wear performance among all tested 3D-printed dental materials.

Previous studies have explored various methods to enhance the mechanical properties of 3D-printed materials. The incorporation of nanofillers has improved the mechanical properties of denture resins; however, the degree of enhancement is dependent on the nanoparticle characteristics, such as structure, surface chemistry, type, concentration, aggregation rate, and surface treatment [18]. Nevertheless, this approach is encumbered by several challenges, including difficulties in achieving uniform nanoparticle dispersion, a lack of clinical standardization, potential health risks, and increased production costs [18]. Compared with nanotechnology-based approaches, heat polymerization offers a simpler and more convenient alternative for enhancing mechanical properties.

Studies have demonstrated that increasing the post-curing time and temperature enhances the degree of conversion (DC), as higher temperatures promote the diffusion of free radicals, thereby increasing their reactivity, facilitating further polymerization, and improving the mechanical properties [19–22]. Kim et al. [16] found that post-curing (> 90 min at 60 °C) improved the mechanical properties of poly methyl methacrylate-based (PMMA) resin but caused unacceptable color changes. Nam et al. [23] demonstrated that 5-min water treatment at 100 °C enhanced the cytocompatibility and DC of PMMA resin, albeit without improving microhardness and FS. Other studies have suggested that heating polymerized resin induces material expansion and internal stresses during manufacturing [24–28]. Moreover, Mohammed et al. [29] reported that elevated temperatures combined with pressure enhanced cross-linking in PMMA resin, thereby significantly improving the flexural properties. Murakami et al. [30] found that polymerizing PMMA under 500 MPa pressure at 70 °C significantly enhanced fracture resistance—an effect primarily attributed to pressure-induced increases in molecular weight. Tangpothitham et al. [26] demonstrated that exposing urethane dimethacrylate-based resin to autoclave steam at elevated temperatures (121–132 °C) and pressure significantly reduced residual monomer release while increasing surface microhardness.

Although some studies have reported the effects of different post-treatment heating methods on the mechanical, biological properties and monomer elution of printed resins, none has specifically focused on 3D-printed denture teeth resin. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the combined impact of time, high temperature, and pressure polymerization on 3D-printed denture teeth resin. Specifically, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of high temperature with pressure polymerization on the degree of conversion, Vickers hardness, flexural properties, and dimensional changes of 3D-printed denture teeth resin. The null hypothesis was that high temperature with pressure polymerization would have no effect on the degree of conversion, Vickers hardness, flexural properties, or dimensional changes of the 3D-printed denture teeth resin.

Methods

Resin material

This study used methyl methacrylate-based 3D-printable denture teeth material (Dima Print Denture Teeth A3; Kulzer, GbmH, Hanau, Germany).

Specimen preparation

In total, 180 3D-printed specimens were fabricated from the denture teeth resin. Based on a power analysis using G*Power 10.09.05 (Dusseldorf, Germany), a total sample size of 50 specimens (10 specimens per group) was determined to provide 80% power at a 5% alpha level. For the degree of conversion experiment, a total of 30 specimens were prepared (6 specimens per group). Post hoc power analysis (G*Power 10.09.05; Dusseldorf, Germany) and sensitivity analysis indicated that the acquired sample size provided 80% power to detect large effect sizes. Rectangular specimens (22 × 4 × 2 mm; length × width × thickness [L × W × T]) were fabricated for the DC test (n = 30). Disk-shaped specimens (10 × 2 mm; diameter × T) were fabricated for the VHN test (n = 50). Bar-shaped specimens were fabricated for the FS and FM experiments (n = 50; 25 × 2 × 2 mm; L × W × T) and the dimensional change test (n = 50; 25 × 2 × 2 mm ± 0.5 mm; L × W × T).

3D CAD design and printing

The 3D-printed specimens were designed using a CAD software program (Geomagic Freeform; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). The specimen designs were saved as standard tessellation language files and exported into 3D-printing software (Cara Print CAM2.0; Kulzer, GmbH). The 3D-printed resin material was homogenized using a tilting mixer and poured into a tank. The bar and disk specimens were printed in a 0° orientation with a 50-µm layer thickness, without support structures, on a DLP printer (Cara Print 4.0 Pro; Kulzer GmbH) featuring a light-emitting diode light source with a wavelength of 385 nm.

Cleaning and post-processing

After printing, the specimens were removed from the build plate with a metal scrapper then underwent post-rinsing and post-curing. In the post-rinsing process, the printed samples were cleaned with 99% isopropyl alcohol in a cleaning machine (Cara Print Clean; Kulzer, GmbH) to remove the remaining uncured resin from the specimen surface. The first cleaning round (3 min) was followed by another round (2 min) per the manufacturer’s recommendation. After air drying, the printed specimens were immersed in a glycerol bath, and the upper and lower sides of each specimen were post-cured for 10 min using a light curing unit at 200 W (wavelength range: 390–540 nm; HiLite Power 3D; Kulzer, GmbH). The specimens were washed with water in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min.

Specimen grouping

The completed printed specimens were randomly divided into five post-treatment groups: I) No post-treatment; the specimens were kept dry in the dark (control), and Ⅱ–V) Specimens underwent post-treatment in a drying oven (OFW-300V; AS ONE) and pressure pot (TAF-SR-300; TAIATSU, Osaka, Japan) at 121 °C under 2 bar air pressure for 4, 30, 60, and 90 min, respectively (Fig. 1). After completing the post-treatment processes, all printed samples were kept in a dry and dark environment at room temperature (23 °C) for 24 h.

Degree of conversion test

Bar-shaped specimens (n = 6 per group) were polished with 800, 1000, and 1500-grit wet silicon papers for 30 s each, cleaned ultrasonically in distilled water for 5 min, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Vickers hardness test

Disk-shaped specimens (n = 10 per group) were polished with 800, 1000, and 1500-grit wet silicon papers for 30 s each, cleaned ultrasonically in distilled water for 5 min, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A VHN tester (MVK-H2; Akashi, Osaka, Japan) was used to measure the hardness under a 50-g indentation force and a 15-s dwell period. Ten specimens per group were indented five times at various sites, and the mean value was calculated for analysis.

Flexural strength and flexural modulus tests

Bar-shaped specimens (n = 10 per group) were prepared and polished with 800, 1000, and 1500-grit wet silicon papers for 30 s each, and cleaned ultrasonically in distilled water for 5 min for the three-point bending test. The samples were placed in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h before the experiment. Before flexural testing, the specimen dimensions were measured with a digital micrometer (minimum reading: 0.001 mm) to achieve the required dimensions of 25 × 2 × 2 mm, according to ISO 4049:2019 for 3D-printed resin-based restorative materials [31]. A universal testing machine (AG-Xplus; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a 20-mm span distance and 1 mm/min crosshead speed was used to measure the FS and FM. FS was calculated using Eq. (2):

FS = 3FL/2bh2 (2).

Where F, L, b, and h denote the maximum applied load (N), support span distance (20 mm), specimen width (mm), and specimen height (mm), respectively, prior to testing.

FM was calculated using Eq. (3):

FM = F1L3/4bh3d (3).

Where F1 and d denote the point load (N) applied on the straight-line portion of the flexural load-deflection curve and the deflection (mm) at load F1, respectively.

Dimensional change test

Bar-shaped specimens (n = 10 per group) were prepared for the dimensional change test, which was determined by comparing the dimensions of the printed bars between the specimens pre- and post-treatment. The L, W, and T of all 3D-printed samples were measured at the mid-point and both endpoints with a digital micrometer (MDC-25 M, Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan; minimum reading of 0.001 mm). Each specimen was measured three times, and the mean value was calculated for analysis. Then, the dimensional mean error percentage between the pre- and post-treatment measurements was calculated.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The DC, FS, and dimensional change of width were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference post hoc test. The FS, VHN, and dimensional change in length were analyzed using Welch’s ANOVA due to unequal variance, followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons post hoc test. The dimensional change in thickness was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc comparison using the Mann–Whitney U test (α = 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we found that a high temperature with pressure treatment is an effective and convenient method to improve the mechanical and physical properties of 3D-printed denture teeth resin. Post-treatment significantly increased the DC and VHN, induced dimensional changes, and influenced the FS and FM; these findings reject our null hypothesis.

We found that additional heat treatment affected the DC, even after post-curing was complete, demonstrating that post-treatment with high temperature and pressure improved the DC. Added pressure diminishes the space between monomer molecules while contracting their free volume [32], and elevated temperatures facilitate enhanced movement of the free radicals and polymer chain formation, consequently optimizing the monomer conversion rate and further polymerization [33–35]. Previous studies have indicated that high temperature and pressure polymerization yields higher molecular weight polymers by accelerating chain propagation while suppressing termination [36]. Consequently, the observed mechanical improvements could also originate from higher crosslinking degrees attained under the specified polymerization conditions.

The VHN of a material is an essential physical property that can be utilized to assess its resistance to indentation. The 121 °C/90 min group had the highest VHN value at 24.16 (23.99–24.33), exceeding that of conventional denture teeth resin [37]. These findings align with those of Tangpothitham et al. [26], who reported that autoclaving after the 3D-printing process significantly enhanced the surface hardness of 3D-printed acrylic resin. This can be attributed to the fact that extending the post-treatment time results in a higher DC, thus reducing the residual monomer content and increasing the hardness value [20].

In the present study, the FS value ranged from 104.74 (101.22–107.52) to 115.44 (113.53–117.4) MPa, which aligns with the findings of Abdelfattah et al., who reported FS values ranging approximately from 83± 7.12 to 117 ± 4.98 MPa for 3D‑printed and milled denture tooth materials [38]. Among all experimental groups evaluated herein, the 121 °C/60 min group yielded the lowest FS. These findings align with those of Kim et al. [16], who reported that NextDent C&B flexural strength dropped to its lowest value with 60 min of post-curing, rising again after 90 and 120 min of curing. The lowest FS observed in the 60-min group in the current study is likely owing to uneven polymerization and increased heterogeneity among specimens. Increasing the post‑treatment time to 90 min reduced these discrepancies, resulting in more consistent flexural performance. Importantly, all experimental groups exceeded the ISO 4049:2019 minimum flexural strength requirement of 80 MPa for resin-based restorative materials [39]; therefore, the lower value observed at 60 min does not affect its clinical applicability.

Regarding FM, the 121 °C/60 min group showed a significantly lower value than the 121 °C/90 min and control groups, while the value in the 121 °C/90 min group did not differ significantly from that in the control group. The lowest FM value observed in the 60 min group is likely due to incomplete polymerization and greater specimen heterogeneity at that time point [16]. Hwangbo et al. reported that FM values for 3D‑printed dental restorative resins range from 1.01 ± 0.2 to 1.3 ± 0.28 GPa [40]. All post-treatment groups in the present study exhibited FM values above this range, with even the 121 °C/60 min group exceeding 1.3 GPa, indicating consistently higher stiffness across all treatments.

The current study also demonstrated that high temperature with pressure post-treatment influenced the dimensional changes of the 3D-printed dental teeth resin. The highest error rate was observed in the 121 °C/90 min group, with an L error rate of 0.11% (0.08–0.11%), a W error rate of 0.86% (0.76–1.05%), and a T error rate of 0.47% (0.41–0.7%). These results are consistent with those of Tangpothitham et al. [24], Arora et al. [41], and Savabi et al. [42], showcasing the dimensional changes of resin during the polymerization process. According to ISO 22112:2017 [43], polymer denture teeth must remain within ± 2% of their original dimensions. In the present study, the dimensional changes in the 121 °C/90 min group, which were the largest among all groups, remained well within this threshold. Therefore, all observed deviations comply with ISO tolerance and can be considered acceptable limits. Post‑treatment temperature exposure may have elevated internal stress, resulting in plastic deformation, or alternatively may have relieved internal stresses formed during processing; if the post‑treatment temperature exceeded the resin’s glass transition temperature, it may have caused plastic deformation owing to the increased chain mobility [24, 26, 44]. Dimensional changes in denture teeth have been investigated in specimens of varying shapes and using various methodologies. Rectangular specimens were employed in the present study, as this standardized approach is widely used in dimensional stability research [45–48] and has demonstrated reliability [45, 46]. However, linear measurements alone may inadequately capture the complexity of dimensional alterations, potentially limiting the clinical applicability of the findings.

This study has some limitations. First, only a single type of 3D-printed denture teeth resin was evaluated; different materials may exhibit diverse mechanical and physical properties. Second, the polymerization method (high temperature with pressure) differed from the manufacturer’s recommended protocol (curing light equipment), which could influence material performance. Third, dimensional changes were assessed without 3D analysis, potentially overlooking subtle deformations. Finally, this study did not directly quantify the residual monomer content. Instead, we relied on measurements of the DC and surface hardness. Sarcev et al. [49] demonstrated that the DC and Vickers microhardness concurrently increase as the monomer elution decreases, indicating that surface hardness is a reliable indirect measure of polymerization completeness and the low residual monomer content. Ayman et al. [50] showed that PMMA denture-base resins processed under high temperature and high pressure demonstrate decreased residual monomer levels and increased hardness. Thus, the elevated DC and Vickers hardness observed in the present study indirectly indicate a reduced residual monomer content. Future studies should employ anatomically accurate 3D-printed denture teeth specimens, compare multiple resin materials using precise 3D measurement techniques, and measure the residual monomer content.

Conclusion

High temperature with pressure polymerization at 121 °C for 90 min is a suitable method to increase the degree of conversion and surface hardness without damaging the flexural properties of 3D-printed denture teeth resin; it also maintains dimensional changes within acceptable limits.
