Revisiting potential role of metformin in the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA axis: translational medicine perspectives in gastrointestinal cancer
Authors: Juan A Godoy, Juvenal A Ríos, Tomás de Mayo, Jenny F Henríquez, Cristopher San Martin Abello, María E Avendaño, Marcelo Garrido Villanueva, Fernando Sigler Chávez, Marcelo Garrido
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetformin, a widely prescribed antidiabetic drug with a well-established safety profile, has attracted increasing interest as a candidate for drug repurposing in oncology. Epidemiological, preclinical, and translational studies suggest that metformin exerts antitumor effects through both systemic metabolic regulation and direct modulation of intracellular signaling pathways, particularly the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis. In parallel, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have emerged as key regulators of cancer biology, acting as microRNA sponges, transcriptional regulators, and, in some cases, templates for peptide translation. Increasing evidence implicates circRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks in gastrointestinal cancer initiation, progression, therapy resistance, and immune evasion. This review critically examines the role of metformin as a metabolic modulator of the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory axis in gastrointestinal cancers, focusing on gastric, colorectal, and hepatocellular carcinomas. We distinguish experimentally supported mechanisms from theoretical pathway-based intersections, discuss the translational potential of circRNAs as biomarkers, and highlight the current limitations and future research priorities. Collectively, the available data support a conceptual framework in which metformin indirectly reshapes oncogenic RNA networks via AMPK/mTOR signaling, providing a rationale for biomarker-driven clinical studies in precision gastrointestinal oncology.Keywords: Metformin, Cancer, Diabetes mellitus, miRNA, circRNA, Translational oncology

Introduction

Oncogenesis is a multistep process driven by growth factors, hormones, and cell cycle regulators, along with genetic alterations that disrupt key signaling pathways, ultimately enabling uncontrolled proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [1]. Recent evidence has established RNA biology as a critical regulatory layer in tumorigenesis, where aberrations in RNA splicing, transport, editing, and decay generate molecular vulnerabilities that may be exploited therapeutically [2].

Only ~ 2% of the human genome encodes proteins, nearly 98% is transcribed into non-coding RNAs, including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) [3]. Among these, miRNAs (~ 22 nucleotides) are well-established post-transcriptional regulators that modulate oncogenes and tumor suppressors, playing essential roles in carcinogenesis [4].

Although historically less explored, circRNAs are now recognized as important regulators of cancer biology. Many circRNAs function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or “sponges” for microRNAs (miRNAs). For example, circRS-7 antagonizes miR-7 to promote hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), whereas circHIPK3 modulates cell growth by targeting miR-124, miR-1, and miR-802, thereby influencing Wnt/β-catenin signaling [5, 6]. In addition to miRNA sponging, several circRNAs are associated with polysomes and encode bioactive peptides with tumor-suppressive or oncogenic properties [7]. circSHPRH, derived from the SHPRH gene, produces the SHPRH-146aa peptide, which prevents the proteasomal degradation of the SHPRH protein and exerts tumor-suppressive effects [8]. Likewise, circLINC-PINT, generated from the p53-induced long intergenic non-coding RNA LINC-PINT, encodes an 87-amino-acid peptide (PINT87aa) that functions as a nuclear suppressor of proliferation and tumorigenesis in colorectal cancer (CRC) [9]. In HCC, circβ-catenin encodes β-catenin-370aa, activating Wnt signaling and promoting tumor growth [10]. Metformin, a widely prescribed antidiabetic drug with a well-established safety profile, has emerged as a promising candidate for drug repurposing in oncology. Beyond its systemic metabolic effects, metformin modulates key oncogenic signaling pathways, most notably the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, which plays a central role in gastrointestinal tumorigenesis. In parallel, circRNAs have been recognized as critical regulators of gene expression through their interactions with miRNAs and mRNAs, forming complex regulatory networks that influence cancer progression, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion. This review critically examines whether metformin can be conceptualized as a metabolic modulator of the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory axis in gastrointestinal cancers and explores how this interaction can be leveraged for biomarker development and translational precision oncology.

Metformin as a modulator of cancer hallmarks: implications for therapy and disease progression

Metformin, a biguanide introduced more than 60 years ago, remains one of the most widely prescribed medications for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Beyond its glucose-lowering properties, growing evidence highlights the broader health benefits, including their protective effects against obesity, cardiovascular and liver diseases, chronic kidney disease, and aging [11]. These pleiotropic actions have stimulated considerable interest in their potential application as anticancer agents.

Pharmacokinetics and cellular transport mechanisms

Metformin is a hydrophilic, positively charged compound with low lipophilicity, making passive membrane diffusion inefficient. Its oral bioavailability ranges from 40% to 60%, with most of the absorption occurring within six hours. Absorption is dose-dependent and saturable, indicating a reliance on active transport mechanisms. After absorption, metformin is rapidly distributed, does not bind to plasma proteins, and exhibits an elimination half-life of approximately five hours in individuals with normal renal function. No metabolites or conjugates have been identified, underscoring renal clearance as the primary elimination route [12, 13]. Transport is mainly mediated by organic cation transporters (OCTs), OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3 (encoded by SLC22A1-SLC22A3). OCT1 is highly expressed in the liver, where metformin exerts most of its metabolic actions, whereas OCT2 predominates in the kidney and facilitates renal excretion. OCT3, a high-capacity transporter expressed in skeletal muscle and other tissues, accounts for nearly 76% of the systemic distribution of metformin [14, 15].

Metabolic disease, diabetes, obesity, and cancer risk

The association between diabetes and cancer was first proposed in 1910 [16, 17]. More than a century later, consensus statements from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) formally recognized this relationship [18]. T2DM and obesity share multiple biological features that promote oncogenesis, including hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, elevated insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), dyslipidemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, increased leptin levels, and reduced adiponectin levels [19]. Together, these factors create a permissive environment for tumor initiation and progression. T2DM and obesity promote a tumor-permissive metabolic state characterized by hyperinsulinemia, chronic inflammation, and aberrant activation of insulin/IGF-1-dependent PI3K/AKT signaling. Metformin partially reverses these oncogenic metabolic drivers by suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis and reducing circulating insulin levels. (Fig. 1)

Epidemiological evidence for anticancer effects

In 2005, Evans et al. reported that metformin use in patients with T2DM was associated with a reduced incidence of cancer, prompting a surge in interest in its oncologic potential [20]. Subsequent epidemiological and preclinical studies have identified protective associations across numerous malignancies, including breast, liver, endometrial, colorectal, cholangiocarcinoma, lung, pancreatic, prostate, thyroid, head and neck, melanoma, and esophageal squamous cell carcinomas [21]. Clinical studies have also reported improved overall and progression-free survival in patients with lung, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers treated with metformin [22–24]. Nonetheless, the findings remain heterogeneous, underscoring the need for well-controlled prospective trials. (Fig. 1)

Preclinical and translational studies

In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that metformin suppresses tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis in multiple cancer models, including hepatocellular carcinoma, ocular melanoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and breast cancer models. Metformin also enhances sensitivity to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, while reducing toxicity when combined with lower doses of standard anticancer regimens [25–28]. These encouraging findings have prompted numerous clinical trials investigating metformin as a repurposed anticancer agent [29–31].

Mechanistic insights into cancer hallmarks

Metformin exerts antitumor effects through diverse mechanisms aligned with the hallmarks of cancer

Metformin inhibits mitochondrial complex I, leading to reduced ATP production, energetic stress, and alterations in glucose metabolism through the TCA cycle [32]. These changes are particularly detrimental to cancer cells thriving in hypoxic microenvironments. Metformin reduces Cancer Stem Cells (CSCs) populations and CSC-mediated chemoresistance by targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Combined treatment with the Wnt inhibitor ICG-001 enhances autophagy, apoptosis, and chemotherapeutic sensitivity in multidrug-resistant models [33, 34]. Metformin inhibits epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastatic progression through epigenetic regulation of E-cadherin (CDH1). AMPK phosphorylates PHF2, increasing its demethylase activity at H3K9me2 and reactivating epithelial gene expression [35]. Metformin also phosphorylates EZH2 at Thr311 via AMPK, disrupting PRC2 (EED-EZH2) activity and reducing oncogenic gene silencing [36, 37], positioning metformin as a potential epigenetic modulator. Into the Tumor Microenvironment (TME), metformin reduces cancer-associated mesenchymal stem cells (CA-MSCs), leading to decreased recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs). Although metformin may not fully reverse the CA-MSC phenotype, it attenuates TME-driven tumor progression [38].

Metformin suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion and fibroblast-driven tumor proliferation. By inhibiting HIF-1α and VEGF expression in cancer-associated fibroblasts, metformin interferes with angiogenesis, adaptation to hypoxia, and metastatic potential. Additionally, inhibition of HIF-1α reduces CXCR4-driven stromal-tumor signaling and breast cancer progression [39]. Metformin enhances cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity by promoting PD-L1 degradation. AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of PD-L1 at Ser195 results in its endoplasmic reticulum (ER) retention and subsequent ER-associated degradation suggesting a potential role for metformin as an adjuvant to immune checkpoint blockade therapy [40].

Metformin has emerged as a versatile candidate for cancer therapy, acting on multiple hallmarks of cancer, including metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic remodeling, microenvironmental regulation, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and CSC dynamics. Its favorable safety profile, affordability, and broad tissue distribution support its repurposing in oncology. However, inconsistent epidemiological findings, pharmacogenomic variability driven by OCT polymorphisms, and the lack of robust randomized clinical trials underscore the need for caution. Future research should prioritize biomarker-driven patient stratification, evaluate synergy with targeted agents and immunotherapies, and undertake large, well-controlled trials to establish its definitive therapeutic value. Metformin exemplifies the translational potential of drug repurposing at the interface of metabolic disease and oncology.

The interplay between circular RNAs, microRNAs, and PI3K/AKT signaling in human cancer

Metformin operates through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Its indirect effects arise from AMPK activation, inhibition of hepatic gluconeogenesis, and stimulation of glucose uptake in the skeletal muscle. These actions reduce circulating insulin levels, thereby diminishing the insulin-dependent activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in cancer cells. The direct insulin-independent effects of metformin involve AMPK and LKB1 activation, inhibition of TSC2 and mTOR signaling, and consequent suppression of protein synthesis (Fig. 2). Notably, metformin can inhibit mTOR independently of LKB1, AMPK, and TSC2 [41].

Gastric cancer, metformin, circRNAs, and miRNAs

In gastric cancer (GC), circRNA-miRNA interactions critically modulate PI3K/AKT signaling by regulating the activity or expression of key genes, such as PTEN, p21, and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1). These genes influence major oncogenic pathways, contribute to cisplatin resistance, and have potential value as biomarkers for gastrointestinal malignancies [42–46].

The tumor-suppressive miR-375 decreases PDK1 activity in the PI3K/AKT pathway [47]. Multiple circRNAs, including circ0008035, circSERPINE2, and circUBA1, regulate miR-375 circ0008035, along with the transcription factor YBX1, promotes GC progression. circSERPINE2 enhances tumor growth by regulating YWHAZ, leading to altered cell cycle control [48, 49]. circUBA1 promotes proliferation and metastasis by modulating TEAD4 [50].

Another key regulatory loop in GC is the circGRAMD1B/miR-130a-3p axis, which suppresses PTEN and p21 (CDKN1A), resulting in increased cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. p21 is a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor that is directly integrated into PI3K/AKT signaling and is an essential mediator linking DNA damage responses to cell cycle arrest [51, 52]. Collectively, these circRNAs converge on the miR-375/PDK1/AKT axis, indicating that circRNA-mediated suppression of miR-375 may enhance GC cell growth, survival, and motility [53]. Thus, circRNAs can contribute to gastric carcinogenesis by primarily activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [54]. Targeting this regulatory axis may represent a therapeutic opportunity to limit tumor progression [55].

In contrast to tumor-suppressive networks, circNRIP1 (nuclear receptor-interacting protein 1) functions as an oncogenic circRNA in GC. circNRIP1 sponges miR-149-5p, increasing AKT1 expression and promoting tumorigenesis via the AKT1/mTOR axis. circNRIP1 is also secreted through exosomes, facilitating communication between tumor cells and distant metastatic sites. Through this mechanism, circNRIP1 enhances metabolic reprogramming, autophagy, EMT, and tumor metastasis [56]. Moreover, circNRIP1 sponges miR-138-5p, sustaining hypoxia-induced 5-fluorouracil resistance via HIF-1α-dependent metabolic rewiring [57].

Given the need for reliable GC biomarkers, circulating circRNAs show considerable diagnostic promise. A panel of eight circRNAs, initially identified in tumor tissues and validated in patient serum samples, demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, effectively distinguishing GC patients, including those with early-stage disease from healthy controls. Together, these findings illustrate how circRNAs and miRNAs integrate into cancer-associated signaling pathways. Metformin may exert antitumor effects in GC, at least in part, by modulating the miR-375/PDK1/PI3K/AKT/circRNA axis, which regulates multiple circRNAs implicated in gastric carcinogenesis (Fig. 3).

Metformin in colorectal cancer: mechanisms, evidence and therapeutic potential

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health burden in terms of morbidity and mortality despite notable progress in treatment strategies. Advances in surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have improved patient outcomes; however, early detection remains one of the most critical factors for improving survival [58]. In this context, the study of circRNAs and their interactions with the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway has emerged as a promising area for CRC diagnosis and therapy [59]. Among these, exosome-derived circRNAs (Exo-circRNAs) have attracted substantial attention as non-invasive biomarkers for early cancer detection. Exo-circRNAs influence the surrounding microenvironment and contribute to tumor progression through competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory networks [60]. The PI3K/AKT pathway regulates essential cellular functions, including proliferation, survival, and migration, and is frequently dysregulated in cancers. Therefore, the intersection of these two fields is of significant translational medicine interest.

Using gene expression data from public repositories and machine-learning methods, researchers developed a diagnostic model based on six exo-circRNAs, which was able to distinguish CRC patients from healthy controls with high accuracy. Complementary bioinformatic analyses identified a network of exosomal circRNAs implicated in CRC progression. Several mechanistic studies have provided additional insights. Intergenic circ0007379 suppresses CRC progression by regulating the biogenesis of miR-320a in a KSRP-dependent manner, highlighting the role of RNA-binding proteins in circRNA-miRNA interactions [61]. circRHOBTB3 alleviates metastasis by stabilizing PTBP1 mRNA via a HuR-mediated mechanism [62]. circ0001666 inhibits CRC progression by modulating the miR-576-5p/PCDH10 axis [63] circRNA101277 promotes cisplatin resistance by sponging miR-370, resulting in increased IL-6 expression. This circ101277/miR-370/IL-6 regulatory loop is a key mechanism underlying chemoresistance [64].

Together, these studies reveal a complex interplay between exo-circRNAs, PI3K/AKT signaling, and the immune and stromal microenvironments in CRC. Emerging evidence underscores the potential of exo-circRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers, prognostic indicators, and therapeutic targets, offering new avenues for the precision management of colorectal cancer [65] (Fig. 4).

Collectively, these exo-circRNA-miRNA networks converge on PI3K/AKT-driven pathways, highlighting their roles in colorectal cancer progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance

Metformin and hepatocellular carcinoma

circRNAs have gained considerable attention as regulators of gene expression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where they modulate key oncogenic pathways, including the PI3K/AKT signaling axis, a central regulatory node in liver tumorigenesis. Distinct circRNA expression profiles have been observed between tumor and non-tumor tissues in HCC. Several circRNAs exhibit altered expression patterns, circ100381 and circ103489 are upregulated, whereas circ101764 is downregulated. These circRNAs interact with members of the miR-181 family, influencing genes involved in proliferation, apoptosis, and invasion. In addition, a peptide encoded by circZKSCAN1 has been shown to inhibit HCC development by promoting degradation of mTOR, revealing a novel layer of post-transcriptional regulation [66].

Exosomal circRNAs also contribute to HCC progression. Exosomal circCCAR1 acts as a sponge for miR-127-5p, upregulating its target WTAP and establishing a regulatory loop within the circCCAR1/miR-127-5p/WTAP axis. circCCAR1 secretion relies on hnRNPA2B1, and exosomal circCCAR1 is taken up by CD8+ T cells, where it stabilizes PD1 and induces T-cell dysfunction, thereby contributing to resistance to anti-PD1 immunotherapy [67]. These discoveries highlight the potential of circRNAs as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in HCC [68] (Fig. 5).

Metformin may further intersect with HCC biology through its effects on viral hepatitis. Gene expression profiling of HBV-infected livers identified upregulation of transcription factors such as HNF4α, PPARα, and LRH1 in HBcrAg-positive samples. In HepAD38 cells, LRH1 overexpression enhanced HBV replication, as indicated by increased levels of viral DNA and pregenomic RNA. Overexpression of the HBV precore/core protein in HepG2 cells similarly increased these transcription factors. Importantly, metformin suppresses HBV replication in primary human hepatocyte cultures, suggesting its potential utility in HCC prevention among HBV-infected individuals [69].

CircRNAs can act as either oncogenic drivers or tumor suppressors in HCC. circ0001459 accelerates HCC progression through the miR-6165/IGF1R axis [70]. circ104348 promotes tumor growth by regulating the miR-187-3p/RTKN2 pathway and activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [71]. circ0051040 enhances HCC progression by sponging miR-569 and upregulating ITGAV, thereby promoting EMT [72]. circRHOT1 (hsa_circRNA_102034) is markedly overexpressed in HCC tissues and associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, circRHOT1 recruits the histone acetyltransferase TIP60 to the promoter of NR2F6, leading to transcriptional activation. NR2F6 knockdown suppresses HCC cell growth and invasion, while its re-expression rescues the malignant phenotype in circRHOT1-silenced cells, confirming the circRHOT1-NR2F6 oncogenic axis [73].

Conversely, some circRNAs suppress HCC progression, circITCH (hsa_circ_0001141) inhibits tumorigenesis by sponging miR-184, reducing proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo [74].

These findings underscore the dual, context-dependent roles of circRNAs in liver cancer biology. Given the central importance of the PI3K/AKT pathway in regulating cellular growth, survival, and apoptosis, dysregulated circRNAs in HCC may exert their oncogenic or tumor-suppressive effects through modulation of this signaling axis. Accumulating evidence highlights the crucial interplay between circRNAs and PI3K/AKT signaling in HCC, with significant implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy. Further research is needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions and to explore their potential for targeted therapeutic intervention [75] (Fig. 5).

Understanding metformin as a therapeutic modulator of the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network in cancer

Available data suggest that metformin affects circRNA networks mainly through indirect mechanisms, including AMPK activation, mTOR inhibition, metabolic stress, and the regulation of RNA-binding proteins associated with circRNA biogenesis and translation. Evidence supporting the direct circRNA-specific regulatory effects of metformin is currently limited and warrants further investigation. Metformin, widely used for the treatment of T2DM, has also been proposed as a potential therapeutic agent in neurobiology. Epidemiological and experimental evidence links type 2 diabetes-associated insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress to cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease, suggesting that metformin may exert neuroprotective effects beyond its glucose-lowering activity [76] (Fig. 1).

Recent studies in aged monkeys have shown that metformin delays brain aging and confers neuroprotection by restoring Nrf2-mediated antioxidant signaling and reducing oxidative stress. Metformin restored the active form of the Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-related factor 2 (phosphorylated Nrf2), a critical regulator of the cellular antioxidant response. By lowering reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation, these findings highlight metformin’s capacity to modulate cellular redox balance and metabolic stress, mechanisms that are also central to cancer prevention, tumor metabolism and resistance to therapy [77].

The mechanism behind the anticancer or growth-inhibitory effect of metformin in cancer cells can be ascribed to AMPK activation, leading to different downstream effects that work together to restrain the growth of tumors. Epidemiological evidence has indicated that metformin lowers the risk of cancer and reduces cancer-related deaths in patients with T2DM. However, the precise mechanisms underlying its action remain to be investigated in both preclinical and clinical trials. The effects of metformin on energy metabolism at both the cellular and organismal levels can be categorized into two types of mechanisms: "direct effects," which impact cancer cells independently of blood glucose and insulin levels, and "indirect effects," which involve systemic metabolic changes that regulate blood glucose and insulin levels [1, 78] (Fig.79).

Various mechanisms of its antitumor action have been suggested by both in vitro and in vivo studies, including (i) activation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK),(ii)modulation of the adenosine A1 receptor (ADORA), (iii) reduction in insulin and insulin-like growth factors, and (iv) inhibition of endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS). AMPK activity results in the inactivation of mTOR, a central regulator of cellular responses to mitogenic stimuli and environmental signals, as demonstrated by Dilman and Anisimov (1980) [80, 81].

Metformin has been extensively studied in clinical trials across various medical and pharmacological fields, including reproductive medicine, cancer chemotherapy, metabolic diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases[27, 82].Once the primary mechanisms of action and their corresponding messengers have been identified at the molecular level, metformin may potentially be used in a targeted fashion, integrating patient conditions such as and tumor types, as shown by Dowling et al. in 2012 [83]. It is crucial to fully elucidate these mechanisms to develop a more successful use of metformin for other health conditions, such as cancer. If indirect effects are more prominent, it is recommended to focus on obese patients with elevated insulin levels, germline OCT1 expression, IR/IGF1R expression, and activated PI3K pathways.

However, if direct mechanisms are prevalent, tumor characteristics, such as LKB1 and TSC2 expression, OCT1 presence, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation should be considered. CircRNAs play vital roles in various biological processes owing to their unique structures and properties. One of the primary functions of circRNAs is to function as miRNA sponges. Although several circRNAs have been identified as miRNA sponges in recent years, it is essential to note that they represent only a small fraction of the thousands of human circRNAs. Informatics tools have identified multiple binding sites for individual miRNAs within circRNAs, which bind directly to miRNAs and inhibit their activity, thus, regulating the expression of target genes [84].

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which includes phosphoinositide 3-kinase, has important functions in various cellular and biological processes such as proliferation, growth, invasion, migration, and angiogenesis. It also plays a significant role in cancer development. These data indicate that the circRNA/PI3K/AKT axis affects the expression of genes associated with cancer and regulates tumor progression. Deregulation of circRNA expression in this axis is significantly associated with important clinicopathological characteristics of cancer. Research suggests that metformin may have a regulatory role in this critical cellular signaling pathway [85].

Peptide-encoding circRNAs represent an emerging frontier in the field of cancer biology. Given that metformin suppresses mTOR-dependent translation, it may influence the functional output of circRNA-derived peptides, linking metabolic regulation to antigen presentation, immune surveillance, and tumor suppression pathways. For instance, circFAM53B is an example of a circRNA that translates antigenic peptides with a strong affinity for HLA-I and HLA-II molecules. These peptides specifically target naïve CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, inducing antitumor immunity. The expression of circFAM53B and its encoded peptides correlated with substantial infiltration of antigen-specific CD8 + T cells and improved patient survival in both breast cancer and melanoma. This discovery suggests the potential use of vaccines consisting of tumor-specific circRNAs or their encoded peptides, which can induce greater infiltration of tumor antigen-specific cytotoxic T cells, ultimately resulting in effective cancer control in humans [86].

The PI3K/AKT pathway plays a central role in several cellular signaling processes, and its deregulation is associated with the development and progression of various cancers. A growing body of evidence suggests that targeting or modulating the components and kinases of this pathway may lead to the discovery and development of novel anticancer drugs. Although some inhibitors targeting the PI3K pathway have been studied, their clinical effectiveness is limited because of the diverse mechanisms through which this pathway is activated and its relationship with oncogenesis. Limitations and challenges

Despite encouraging preclinical and epidemiological data, several limitations warrant careful consideration of this approach. Reported clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous, reflecting the variability in metabolic status, treatment duration, cancer subtype, and study design. Pharmacogenomic differences, particularly polymorphisms in OCTs, may substantially influence metformin uptake, tissue distribution, and therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, the antitumor concentrations employed in experimental models often exceed those achieved with standard antidiabetic dosing, raising important translational and dosing considerations for clinical application. At the mechanistic level, most available evidence supports the indirect modulation of circRNA networks by metformin rather than direct circRNA-specific regulation, and tumor heterogeneity further complicates causal interpretation. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for biomarker-driven prospective clinical studies integrating metabolic, molecular, and RNA-based profiling to define the patient populations most likely to benefit from metformin-based therapeutic strategies.

Final remarks

In this review, we synthesize the current evidence supporting metformin as a metabolic regulator of oncogenic signaling and RNA-based regulatory networks in gastrointestinal cancer. Accumulating data indicate that metformin predominantly modulates the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory axis indirectly, primarily through AMPK activation and inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, thereby influencing tumor metabolism, stemness, immune evasion, and resistance to therapy.

From a translational perspective, circRNAs, particularly those associated with PI3K/AKT pathway activation and detectable in circulation or exosomes, emerge as promising candidate biomarkers for risk stratification, early detection, and potential prediction of sensitivity to metabolic interventions, such as metformin. Accordingly, future clinical studies should prioritize biomarker-guided patient selection, integrate metabolic and molecular profiling, and evaluate rational combination strategies involving metformin with targeted therapies and immunotherapy. Collectively, these approaches may help realize the full potential of metformin as a cost-effective adjunct in precision gastrointestinal oncology [88–96].

It is important to emphasize that this review does not aim to provide an exhaustive catalog of all known circRNAs. Rather, it focuses on representative gastrointestinal cancer contexts to illustrate the functional roles of circRNAs in tumor biology. Although substantial progress has been made in understanding circRNA biogenesis, expression patterns, and regulatory mechanisms, further studies are required to elucidate their precise functional roles and clinical applicability as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Importantly, the available evidence indicates that metformin primarily influences circRNA networks indirectly through AMPK activation, mTOR inhibition, and metabolic stress. While these pathways are known to regulate circRNA biogenesis, stability, and translational output, direct circRNA-specific regulation by metformin has not yet been conclusively demonstrated. Recognizing this distinction is essential to avoid overinterpretation and to clearly separate experimentally validated mechanisms from theoretical and inferential models.
