Artificial intelligence and electronic health records: a narrative review of current applications and challenges in pediatric surgery
Authors: Javeria Javed, Saleem Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractApplication of artificial intelligence (AI) within electronic health record (EHR) systems is influencing clinical care, research, education, and quality improvement in pediatric surgical practice. In clinical settings, these technologies organize and structure patient information, enable predictive analytics, and support decision-making tools that enhance awareness and facilitate interventions.Longitudinal datasets in EHR create opportunities for data-driven research ranging from modeling and outcome prediction to clinical trial recruitment. Educational applications of AI-enabled EHR extend to healthcare professionals, trainees, patients, and caregivers. For professionals, case-based learning and simulation resources strengthen decision-making skills and clinical judgment. For patients and caregivers, personalized guidance and accessible health records foster understanding of disease, encourage active participation in care, and improve adherence to management plans. Quality improvement initiatives benefit from AI-assisted audit systems, automated tracking of outcomes, and identification of workflow inefficiencies. These benefits are accompanied by challenges, including the risk of overreliance on automated alerts, limited representativeness of training data, lack of external validation, workflow misalignment, and concerns about privacy and transparency. Addressing these issues through rigorous validation, inclusive datasets, user-focused design, and informatics education will be essential to ensure that AI-enabled EHR systems enhance rather than replace clinician expertise, while promoting safe, equitable, and patient-centered care.Keywords: Education, Medical

Introduction

From the papyrus scrolls of ancient Egypt to the sophisticated electronic platforms of the 21st century, the history of medical record keeping reflects the broader evolution of medicine. In its earliest form, documentation served not only as a record of cases but also as a means of preserving and transmitting clinical knowledge across generations. Egyptian physicians inscribed symptoms, treatments, and outcomes on papyrus, establishing a foundation for systematic medical documentation.1 Over time, as medical knowledge and clinical practice expanded, these records evolved from personal collections to organized repositories housed in libraries, becoming indispensable tools for education, reference, and accountability. This progression has been particularly significant in fields that require long-term continuity of care, such as pediatric surgery, where accurate and comprehensive records support management across the dynamic stages of growth and development.

By the 19th century, formalized approaches to medical record keeping had been firmly established. The 1860s saw the widespread adoption of handwritten charts that recorded vital signs, fever trends, and urinary outputs.2 Standardized disease histories, charts, and forms became common in the early 20th century. Medical records gained increasing importance in legal and insurance contexts, expanding their value beyond the clinical domain. The American College of Surgeons has led efforts to register every patient to monitor therapeutic interventions and compare outcomes in a variety of surgical conditions.3 This period also marked the formalization of medical record keeping, prompted by the growing healthcare workforce and increasing complexity of diagnostic and therapeutic processes.4

The advent of electronic health records (EHRs) has revolutionized this long-standing practice through digitization. An EHR represents a comprehensive, longitudinal digital record of a patient’s healthcare story that is accessible to authorized providers (and patients) across various clinical settings.5 A digital format facilitates the integration of extensive information, including physician notes, laboratory values, and diagnostic imaging, thereby enhancing the quality of documentation, continuity of care, and accessibility of information. However, these advancements have also introduced new challenges. Much of the data in EHR remains unstructured, limiting its direct clinical utility. Problems such as ‘swivel chair”’ interoperability, where clinicians manually transfer information between incompatible systems, along with increased provider fatigue and burnout, have emerged as significant barriers to the optimal utilization of this tool.6

The astonishing development and rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) offers multiple new horizons of potential solutions to these challenges. Natural language processing (NLP), machine learning (ML), and deep learning (DL) techniques have enabled EHR systems to transform unstructured data into clinically meaningful insights by analyzing and understanding numerical data, images, and words at levels that are not humanly possible.

Despite the rapid expansion of AI research in surgery, there has been limited attention to EHR-integrated applications. Existing reviews rarely examine how EHR based AI tools influence pediatric surgical practice. These tools extend the utility of EHRs across four critical domains: improving clinical care, enabling high-quality research, advancing education, and driving quality improvement. This review examines how AI-enabled EHRs are shaping contemporary healthcare, with a focus on their potential in pediatric surgical care.

Clinical care

EHRs consolidate patient information (notes, labs, radiology, and others) across multiple interactions with hospitals, physicians, and subspecialties. Over time, this has generated massive amounts of longitudinal data, representing one of the most important achievements made possible through advancements in healthcare technology. In pediatric practices, where patients pass through distinct developmental stages with corresponding physiological changes, such continuity of information enables optimal coordination of care.7 EHRs also supports a smooth transition to adult services by preserving the full scope of the patient’s clinical story.

Historically, the vast data stored within EHR systems have been dispersed across domains, such as laboratory results, radiology images, physician notes, and vital sign reports. Clinicians often face the challenge of locating relevant information within large volumes of unstructured data. In time-pressured clinical settings, essential details could potentially be overlooked, increasing cognitive workload and affecting decision-making.5 NLP algorithms address this challenge by extracting and organizing unstructured text into structured, navigable formats. Through this process, disparate clinical details from multiple domains are integrated. This enhances accessibility and enables a comprehensive view of the patient’s status.8

Once information is accessible and organized, a next step is its application to improve clinical care. AI methods, particularly ML, offer potential advanced analytical capabilities for this purpose. In supervised learning, algorithms are trained on labeled datasets to identify associations between variables and are then tested on separate datasets to validate performance. In unsupervised learning, algorithms can detect complex, previously unrecognized associations within large datasets.9 Leveraging longitudinal records in EHR repositories can strengthen the predictive performance of these approaches.10 A single hospital admission provides only a partial view of a patient’s health, whereas children with congenital or genetic disorders who require long-term management benefit greatly from the integrated perspective offered by comprehensive EHR data. Children who require complex intensive care stays with multiple services involved also require parsing of information that ML algorithms can accomplish with accuracy and ease. This broader view enables clinicians to make more informed and contextually grounded decisions.

One of the most transformative applications of these analytical capabilities is in predictive analytics. Traditional tools such as the Pediatric Early Warning Score rely on a limited set of variables to assess the risk of clinical deterioration and require nurses or physicians to manually evaluate this scoring. ML and DL methods expand this capacity by analyzing hundreds of variables simultaneously and incorporating real-time updates from the EHR. Studies have shown that these models outperform conventional scoring systems, improving patient care through earlier recognition of postoperative complications, reducing mortality, and enabling timely interventions.11,13

Another impactful application of AI in healthcare is the development of clinical decision support systems (CDSS). These systems transform the outputs of predictive models into actionable insights for clinicians. In busy pediatric intensive care units, where physicians manage multiple critically ill patients and monitor extensive data streams, subtle deviations from baseline physiology often go unnoticed. CDSS generates timely alerts, drawing attention to patients at highest risk and providing evidence-based recommendations for intervention.14 For example, in a child recovering from complex cardiac surgery, continuous monitoring of the data may reveal a gradual drop in oxygen saturation along with small changes in heart rate variability. These changes may be too minor to trigger standard alarms but become significant when analyzed together. A CDSS can detect this pattern early and prompt the care team to assess the patient before overt signs of deterioration appear.15 Similarly, automatic and consistent calculation of the oxygenation index in critically ill children or neonates triggers early warning to consider extracorporeal life support. In doing so, these systems reduce cognitive load, support decision-making during periods of clinician fatigue, and enhance the safety and quality of care for vulnerable children.

As AI applications continue to optimize the use of existing clinical data, further advancements are expanding the scope of information available for decision-making. Wearable technologies (such as smart watches) are one such development that enables continuous, objective monitoring of vital signs and other physiological parameters.16 These devices offer a more comfortable alternative to traditional wired monitors, which can be cumbersome for active young children and require frequent adjustments. Wearable devices not only improve patient comfort but also facilitate earlier hospital discharge by enabling remote monitoring.17 The result is a reduction in the length of hospital stay, healthcare costs, and risk of hospital-acquired infections, while maintaining standards of patient safety and continuity of care.

Collectively, these developments illustrate a stepwise integration of AI within pediatric surgical workflows: first through data consolidation and structuring, then through analytic modeling and decision support, and finally through continuous postoperative monitoring. Together, these innovations are transforming pediatric surgical care into a more precise, responsive, and patient-centered practice that holds promise for improving outcomes across diverse pediatric populations.

Research

The transformation of clinical care through EHR and AI extends to the generation of new knowledge. Advances in patient care depend on evidence-based medicine, which must adapt to the evolving demands of healthcare systems and changing needs of patient populations. At the foundation of robust evidence lies high-quality data. The EHR surpasses many existing registries and data repositories in both scale and scope.18 The use of EHR data for research offers the potential to reduce the costs and inefficiencies often associated with clinical studies. Research in pediatric surgical care has been limited by small sample sizes and underpowered studies. These limitations arise due to the rarity of many clinical conditions and fragmented nature of available documentation.19 The breadth of both cross-sectional and longitudinal data within EHR systems is drawn from diverse populations. This presents new opportunities for large-scale observational studies and rapid assembly of cohorts in genomic medicine trials and investigations of rare diseases. We are starting to see these potential advances become reality as large EHR systems in the USA have created deidentified datasets that transcend physicians, hospitals, and states—with large numbers of patients and granular data.

Realizing the full research potential of these datasets requires analytical approaches capable of managing their size and complexity. DL, a class of ML algorithms that employs artificial neural networks, can model complex relationships between variables and outcomes.20 These methods are particularly well-suited to mining the large, heterogeneous datasets contained within EHR systems. In research, applications range from predictive modeling to automated data extraction, enabling studies that were previously limited by the labor-intensive nature of data curation.21 NLP has enhanced the accessibility and structure of information stored within EHR systems. With a growing number of clinical variables now organized into formats that can be analyzed, predictive models are being developed to identify patients likely to require surgical intervention. For example, researchers have developed predictive algorithms that can estimate the likelihood of single or multiple procedures in children with acute hematogenous osteomyelitis.22 Furthermore, in conditions with high mortality and an insidious course, such as necrotizing enterocolitis, predictive modeling supports physicians in assessing disease severity and determining the appropriate timing for surgical management.23 24 In pediatric oncology, these models assist with prognostication.25 This allows physicians to counsel patients and caregivers effectively and to design individualized treatment strategies. Research employing such approaches has introduced a new dimension to precision medicine, revealing latent patterns and relationships within data that were not identifiable through conventional statistical methods. EHR-derived datasets are also increasingly being transformed into structured registries and databases dedicated to specific conditions or specialties, creating enduring resources for longitudinal follow-up and multi-institutional collaboration.26

Beyond data analysis and registry creation, EHR systems have practical applications in the operational aspects of research, particularly in identifying and enrolling eligible participants. One of the most immediate and widely adopted uses is clinical trial feasibility assessment and patient recruitment. Automated functions within EHR systems can generate lists of patients who meet predefined eligibility criteria, allowing recruitment processes to proceed more efficiently, particularly for trials targeting rare or geographically dispersed populations.27 These functions are fully approved by institutional review boards. In some settings, integrated alerts notify physicians when a patient in their care matches the inclusion criteria of an active study.28 Such capabilities accelerate recruitment, broaden access to participation, and improve the generalizability of the study findings.

As research demands grow in complexity, extracting relevant information from the vast repositories of EHR data becomes increasingly important. Large language models are emerging as powerful tools for navigating unstructured clinical narratives with speed and precision.29 They can identify key variables for research, synthesize complex case details, and streamline data preparation for analysis. At the same time, efforts to standardize data formats across institutions are improving interoperability, enabling datasets from different centers to be combined for large-scale studies.30 These developments expand the scope of EHR-enabled research and create new opportunities for collaborative, multi-institutional investigations that can address questions not answerable within a single institution.

Education

The expansion of AI capabilities within EHR systems has extended their value beyond documentation and clinical decision-making to the creation of meaningful educational opportunities for physicians, trainees, patients, and caregivers. For healthcare professionals, the extensive data stored in EHRs can be used to support multidisciplinary case discussions, particularly for complex or rare conditions.31 AI-enabled tools can synthesize patient information, generate concise case summaries, and highlight key learning points. For example, in a surgical morbidity and mortality meeting, an AI system could compile the complete perioperative course of a child with a rare congenital anomaly, highlight deviations from standard protocols, and present relevant literature.32 In fact, with the rapidity of ML functions in different AI applications, we are already noting the ability to quickly point out where possible errors may have occurred and remove any personal contexts with neutral, factual reporting. This structured resource could then be incorporated into simulation exercises that replicate similar clinical scenarios, allowing trainees to apply theoretical knowledge, refine decision-making skills, and prepare for the management of high-acuity situations.33 Integrated alert systems within EHR platforms also have an educational role by reinforcing best clinical practices in real time, thereby strengthening diagnostic reasoning and clinical judgment.

Educational applications also extend directly to patients and caregivers. AI-driven chatbots embedded within EHR systems can answer caregiver questions, provide personalized guidance on postoperative care, outline medication regimens, and recommend dietary or lifestyle modifications.34 These tools, informed by patient-specific data, ensure that educational content is accurate, relevant, and tailored to the individual needs. Furthermore, access to patient portals now allows families to review their medical records, including edited notes and test results. This enhances their understanding of their condition, treatment plan, and expected outcomes, in addition to putting control into the hands of the patient.35 This transparency supports shared decision-making, patient autonomy, improves adherence to care recommendations, and can foster a stronger partnership between healthcare teams and families.

Through these combined applications, AI-enabled EHR systems strengthen the knowledge and skills of both healthcare providers and recipients of care. This educational foundation not only benefits individual patient encounters but also contributes to a culture of continuous improvement within surgical practice.

Quality improvement

The integration of AI tools and EHR systems aims to strengthen the quality of care through greater efficiency, consistent adherence to best practices, expanded research capacity, and enhanced patient engagement. Their use in understanding medical errors has also been mentioned above as well.

Reducing the administrative workload and cognitive fatigue experienced by physicians is one important contribution. Automating routine documentation and streamlining information retrieval allow clinicians to dedicate more time to patient assessment, communication, and follow-up.36 37 This shift in focus supports accuracy, attentiveness, and continuity in care across the patient’s clinical course.

Structured documentation within EHR systems ensures that critical information is organized and accessible, eliminating the need to search through large volumes of unstructured data. CDSS tools reinforce evidence-based guidelines and promote consistent practice.12 38 For example, automated reminders for periodic glucose monitoring in postoperative patients can reduce the risk of hypoglycemia, while streamlined workflows improve reliability and reduce the likelihood of errors.39

The same infrastructure also enables research that feeds directly into quality improvement.40 Access to diverse patient populations, including those with rare conditions, supports the development of new management strategies and the refinement of existing protocols. Such evidence-based refinements promote continuous innovation in pediatric surgical care.

Patient engagement is another critical pillar. Providing families with transparent access to their health information encourages adherence to treatment and follow-up plans, supports shared decision-making, and improves long-term outcomes.41

Resource optimization further strengthens quality initiatives. In the healthcare industry, substantial funds are spent on manual data abstraction for audits, registries, and research.42 Automating these processes through AI-enabled EHR systems reduces these expenditures. Combined with the prevention of medical errors and improved workflow efficiency, such savings create capacity for reinvestment into targeted quality improvement programs. This reinvestment creates a sustainable cycle where data-informed processes continue to improve patient care.

Through these combined mechanisms, AI-enabled EHR systems have the capacity to deliver sustained and measurable improvements in healthcare, ensuring that advances in technology translate into tangible benefits for patients and providers alike.

Limitations and challenges

While AI tools within EHR systems are designed to support physicians and enhance the quality of care, several limitations and challenges must be addressed to ensure their safe and equitable use. These systems are not infallible, and when errors occur, questions arise regarding responsibility, whether with the physician, the healthcare institution, or the EHR vendor. Overreliance on CDSS tools poses additional risks.43 If clinicians defer to automated alerts and delay direct patient assessment, opportunities to identify early signs of deterioration may be missed, potentially compromising patient safety. Equally problematic would be the decisions to order potentially harmful and unnecessary tests or procedures based on the alerts generated by AI-enhanced EHRs.

Existing disparities in healthcare, both local and global in nature, could be worsened if AI models are trained on unrepresentative data. Models are only as reliable as the information they learn from, and when training datasets lack diversity, the generalizability of outputs is reduced.13 Many current models are developed using single-center datasets and have not undergone external validation This limits their applicability across different clinical environments.44 45 This narrow scope risks embedding and amplifying systemic biases within clinical workflows—a phenomenon observed to a concerning degree in many AI applications.46

The challenge of interoperability remains a major barrier to the effective implementation of AI within EHR systems. Medical centers often use different EHR vendors and data storage architectures.47 Variations in documentation practices introduce differences in structure and format of data within these systems. Such inconsistencies limit the seamless exchange of information across multiple healthcare centers and impede continuity of care. Achieving true interoperability requires that patient data be accessible to healthcare providers across institutions during transitions of care, while maintaining stringent standards of privacy and security.48 However, expanding data sharing inevitably increases the risk of breaches and unauthorized access to sensitive patient information. Future research focused on the development of secure frameworks will be essential to enable safe data exchange and achieve semantic interoperability across EHR systems.

Another challenge arises from the lack of communication between those who design CDSS tools and the clinicians who use them. Systems developed without adequate input from surgeons and other clinical staff can fail to align with workflow requirements. Limited training in informatics among surgical teams can also hinder effective communication of needs, resulting in tools that are less intuitive and more disruptive to daily practice.49 Usability depends on close collaboration between developers and clinicians to create systems that are efficient and attuned to real-world clinical demands.

Privacy and trust remain unresolved concerns. The ‘black box’ nature of many AI models can undermine confidence among physicians, as the reasoning behind recommendations is often unclear.43 50 Questions persist over how sensitive patient information is stored, analyzed, and shared. Without transparent governance, strong data protection measures, and clear ethical guidelines, adoption of AI-enabled EHR systems may meet resistance from healthcare providers and patients alike.

Addressing these limitations through rigorous validation, inclusive dataset development, transparent model design, and sustained collaboration between clinicians and developers will be essential to advance the in silico successes of AI applications towards meaningful clinical integration. Regulation of AI applications and models remains in its infancy and will require thoughtful interpretation within the healthcare arena.

Conclusion

The incorporation of AI within EHR systems represents more than a technological upgrade; it signals a shift in how healthcare knowledge is generated, applied, and sustained. Predictive analytics and decision support tools do more than just alert clinicians; they reshape the rhythm of care by enabling earlier recognition of clinical deterioration and guiding timely intervention. Expanding datasets not only allow research on rare and diverse populations but also create a foundation where education, quality improvement, and innovation reinforce one another. The greatest benefit will be when these systems function as partners to clinical expertise, strengthening judgment rather than substituting for it, and when they operate with transparency, privacy safeguards, and equitable representation in their design. Realizing this vision will require ongoing commitment to rigorous validation, thoughtful data stewardship, and investment in informatics education. In doing so, AI-enabled EHR systems can become enduring instruments of safer, more responsive, and more equitable care.
