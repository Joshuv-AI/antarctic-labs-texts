Implementing integrated genomic risk assessments for breast cancer: lessons learned from the Electronic Medical Records and Genomics study

Background and significance

Polygenic risk scores (PRS), summarizing the effect of many common variants from GWAS, have emerged as tools for estimating disease risk. While not yet recommended for clinical use,1 multiple studies show PRS can predict breast cancer risk in cohorts.2–5 Consequently, models like BOADICEA6,7 (implemented in the CanRisk tool8) and Tyrer-Cuzick9,10 have incorporated PRS into their risk calculation. Integrated risk models combining PRS and other factors have shown clinical validity,11–17 yet its real-world uptake is hindered by fragmented data sources: genomic/genetic laboratories, Electronic health records (EHRs) pedigree tools, and survey platforms rarely share a vocabulary, let alone an application programming interface (API). Pipelines to collect and automate risk calculation from data gathered across various sources are lacking.

The Electronic Medical Records and Genomics (eMERGE) Network, spanning ten U.S. academic health systems, offers an unprecedented testbed to operationalize integrated risk prediction at scale and evaluate downstream.18 eMERGE’s genome-informed risk assessment (GIRA) reports required a pipeline capable of ingesting REDCap surveys, Broad-generated PRS, Invitae monogenic results, and MeTree pedigrees, normalizing these inputs, invoking the BOADICEA web service, and returning results under a harmonized governance framework. Here we report the implementation process, emphasizing the operationalized obstacles and mitigation strategies that allowed >10 000 women of diverse ancestry and their healthcare providers to receive actionable breast-cancer risk information.

Materials and methods

Stakeholders and implementation overview

Key stakeholders and their roles in implementing are outlined in Figure 1. The eMERGE Steering Committee (PISC) created the Breast Cancer Working Group (BCWG) to lead and coordinate the pipeline development. To generate risk scores, the CanRisk Tool (CanRisk.org) was selected for its ability to handle multiple data types and tolerate missing inputs. The BCWG and CanRisk team adapted the tool to fit eMERGE protocols. Input data were collected from multiple sources, including participant surveys (via REDCap) capturing menarche, parity, first live birth, oral contraceptives, menopausal hormone therapy, BMI, alcohol intake, and menopause; in addition, PRS reports (Broad Institute), monogenic testing results (Invitae), clinical records, and family pedigrees (MeTree) were also integrated. These were centralized in the REDCap-based R4 platform, maintained by the Coordinating Center (CC). The REDCap tech team implemented a rule-based mapping defined by BCWG, and a plugin to standardize data and automate calls to the CanRisk API. Site staff could also manually run or edit scores during testing. Bugs were identified collaboratively and addressed in iterative cycles. Finally, BCWG issued guidelines to support consistent return of GIRA reports to participants across the ten clinical sites.

Five-stage implementation framework

Stage 1—Design: eMERGE was originally designed to evaluate the clinical impact of PRS across ten conditions.18 The BCWG proposed using the BOADICEA algorithm to estimate lifetime breast cancer risk instead of PRS alone because it integrates monogenic factors (eg, BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2), family history, and PRS, which are already used in clinical practice. BOADICEA v5 was selected to align with a 313-SNP PRS15,19 extensively validated during earlier pilots.14,16,20 Three genes (ie, BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2) integral to the BOADICEA were included in a gene panel that mainly includes the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Tier 1 conditions. A 25% lifetime risk threshold (from age 18 to 80) was selected to define high vs not high risk, aligning with MRI screening guidelines21 and categorical risk design used for other conditions. This cutoff was expected to classify 2.5%-5% of participants as high risk based on previous study2 (∼50 to 100 females per site), which is a manageable number given study resources and timelines.

Stage 2—Customization: Two customizations were essential. First, the eMERGE genotyping array lacked six PRS markers in 313 SNP PRS; one surrogate in high linkage disequilibrium was substituted and five markers were dropped, resulting in a 308-SNP model. The BOADICEA team then recalibrated the parameter alpha, an effective size estimation proportional to the odds ratio (OR), based on the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC) cohorts.22 Second, to overcome the challenge that BOADICEA is developed primarily based on individuals of European ancestry, the orginal PRS (z-score) were recalibrated using All of Us Research Program (AoURP) samples with genetic ancestry-specific adjustments,23 and consequently, both the SNP-size adjusted alpha (effective-size) and ancestry-adjusted z-score were documented in the header of the CanRisk input file, available for API invocation or manual adjustments within the user interface.

Stage 3—Technical Build: Vanderbilt’s REDCap development team fabricated a plug-in (open-source) for a custermized REDCap (R4) that (1) ingests PRS score (Broard), monogenic HL7-based message (Invitae), and pedigree JSON files (MeTree) via various sources; (2) maps all-condition based survey responses to BOADICEA specific variables (eg, converting height ft/in to cm), (3) imputes missing relative ages using ±25 years per generation distance or diagnosis age ±5 years, (4) check eligbility (ie, 18+ females) and data readyness (eg, BMI available) for risk calculation, (5) posts a JSON payload to the CanRisk API endpoint, (6) writes results back to REDCap and flags cases crossing risk thresholds for human review, and (7) generate a Genome Informed Risk Assessment report with explanatory language and recommendations.

Stage 4—Testing and Refinement: A two-month alpha version test was then conducted at two clinical sites to ensure the correctness of the risk calculation in production by comparing the API-returned risk assessments (“API”) with results manually inputted by the clinical research staff using the CanRisk user interface (“UI”). To ensure consistency between API input and UI input, multiple QC training sessions were held to instruct site research staff on how to use the R4 data and apply the same imputation strategies if needed. During this process, mapping bugs, logic errors, and API failures were identified and resolved. In particular, high missingness were observed in completing the MeTree pedigree due to the complexities of data entry. To address this issue, a secondary survey were implemented to collect minimal family history information to construct the CanRisk pedigree. Beta testing extended to eight additional sites with a risk-focused sampling scheme. Cases with API-generated lifetime risk exceeded 20% were reviewed. To minimize discrepancies caused by individual interpretations of clinical variables or pedigree differences, the CC re-ran the UI risk assessments. If discrepancies persisted, the BCWG further investigated these cases, leading to further identification and resolution of additional issues during this phase.

Stage 5—Deployment: After the beta test, the BCWG established guidelines to address risks at the border of the threshold for high-risk and non-high-risk categorization (eg, 25%). Site research staff were instructed to manually review cases when the breast cancer risk was between 20%-25%, based on automated CanRisk API calculations, while an independent score was generated using the CanRisk UI. Cases above 25% were excluded from manual comparison, as our review of the alpha and beta test results indicated that these cases are highly likely to be high risk. Subsequently, we applied a set of rules to borderline cases (20%-25%) determine the final status: (1) If the UI score was below 25%, the case was classified as not at study threshold for high risk; (2) If the UI score is equal to or greater than 25%, BCWG reviewed the case together with the site staff to make the final determination. Finally, the entire risk generation, review, validation and return process (Figure 2) is composed of multiple phases and includes five checkpoints.

Results

High risks in different categories

By October 2024, integrated BOADICEA scores were returned for 10 928 eligible women (aged 18+, with consent and BMI data). Of these, 398 (3.6%) had a lifetime breast cancer risk ≥25%, classifying them as high risk. Using PRS alone, 608 women (5.6%) exceeded the top 5% threshold, aligning with the AoURP reference panel. Among them, 207 (34%) also had high integrated risk, which is 9.4 times higher than the non-high PRS groups. Their mean integrated risk was 23.4% vs 11.8% in others. Although monogenic results were returned separately, retrospective analysis showed 94 women (0.9%) carried pathogenic variants (BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, PTEN). UI vs API generated results

At one clinical site, a quality control project was undertaken to compare risk scores generated by the API with those produced through its web UI. Risk scores for 733 participants, initially calculated using R4 via the API, were manually recalculated through the web UI. This process involved thoroughly reviewing, interpreting, converting, and inputting all available patient data, including proband pedigrees and survey responses from multiple instruments, into the UI. The average lifetime risk scores from the UI closely aligned with those from the API, showing an average difference of just 0.13%. Most discrepancies were attributed to variations in the number of data points entered and potential imputation inconsistencies. Overall, the API and UI scores exhibited strong concordance within this subset of eMERGE participants.

Issues encountered during multiple testing phases

During testing, site coordinators logged 174 issues, which the coordinating center triaged with input from the BCWG. Issues were grouped into technical, input, interpretation inconsistencies, and edge cases (Table 1). A key lesson was the need for a standardized pedigree format to make MeTree data compatible with tools like CanRisk. Many issues stemmed from incompatibilities between MeTree and CanRisk formats (Table 2). To address this, a complex mapping schema was built into the REDCap plugin to resolve discrepancies and, when needed, perform imputations.

Discussion

This study is the first to operationalize BOADICEA-based breast cancer risk assessment—including PRS and monogenic variants—across multiple institutions. Unlike prior studies, it includes a racially diverse population (over 50% non-European), requiring PRS adjustments, and integrates gene panel testing, which is more common in clinical care. The PRS-based risks were generated for multiple diseases, not just breast cancer, necessitating customization of the BOADICEA-based workflow. A standardized report returned individualized risk, enabling follow-up to assess clinical impact.

Workflows were iterative and often overlapped. Some decisions, like using a binary (≥25%) threshold, were made to align with other eMERGE conditions but introduced limitations. Small differences in continuous risk (eg, 24.5% vs 25.5%) could change a participant’s classification and potentially affect clinical decisions. Unlike monogenic results, integrated risk is continuous, and presenting both categorical and quantitative scores may offer clearer context.

Most other eMERGE conditions lacked robust models like breast cancer; their risk classification relied solely on PRS. When multiple reports (monogenic, PRS, integrated) were returned, this sometimes caused confusion. PRS alone misclassified over 60% of “high PRS” participants as not high life-time risk when all factors were considered. Until more comprehensive models exist, PRS only based risk must be interpreted cautiously. Other conditions such as coronary heart disease24 and type 2 diabetes25 have also seen the development of integrated risk scores. However, a key lesson from the breast cancer implementation is the need to ensure variable compatibility during real-world application, where data often originate from heterogeneous sources. This contrasts with model development in controlled cohorts, where all variables are collected in a standardized format. In addition, different clinical scenarios may require varying risk thresholds (eg, >20% vs 25%), and establishing stakeholder consensus around these thresholds and intended use contexts remains a shared challenge across conditions.

The study also revealed challenges coordinating across modular systems (MeTree, REDCap, CanRisk). For example, differing data completeness requirements prompted imputation strategies. System updates added complexity (eg, CanRisk’s UI moved to version 6,20 while the API remained on version 5), raising questions about reanalysis timing. One limitation we encountered was that the BOADICEA model underwent updates during the middle of our recruitment-to-return process, which prevented us from conducting large-scale external validation. We anticipate that such evolving model scenarios will become increasingly common in clinical risk modeling applications. While our modular design allows for the flexible integration of updated models, it is equally important to maintain thorough documentation, including traceable metadata and provenance tracking, to ensure reproducibility and support future adaptability across deployments.

Unlike monogenic variant reclassification, updates to risk models can have variable impacts across populations, particularly by ancestry, and may require tailored responses. As with monogenic updates, changes to risk models should be verified through rigorous data science methodologies and confirmed by domain experts. These updates must be communicated transparently to both clinicians and patients to ensure trust and understanding. While monogenic reclassification typically involves updating a curated variant database, risk model updates may necessitate changes to input variables, parameters, and even the underlying algorithm. Therefore, formal documentation of software versions, including parameters and evidence sources, is essential for each deployment. To support safe and consistent clinical use, model registries should be maintained to track version changes and integrate with EHR systems and other digital platforms, enabling timely updates to downstream clinical decision support tools.

Lastly, while we focused on lifetime risk, some women may need age-specific evolve with age.26 Knowing when and how to rerun risk models and re-communicate results will require collaboration among clinicians, patients, and ELSI experts to ensure recommendations remain ethical, relevant, and actionable.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of a coordinated, open-source approach to deliver integrated BOADICEA-based breast cancer risk including PRS, monogenic variants, and clinical data, and delive the risk assessment to over 10 000 women across ten health systems. It highlights key lessons for scaling genomic risk assessment, including the need for standardized pedigree formats, ancestry-informed calibration, and policies for model updates. These insights can guide the design of future precision prevention strategies that are equitable, clinically actionable, and adaptable to evolving genomic science.
