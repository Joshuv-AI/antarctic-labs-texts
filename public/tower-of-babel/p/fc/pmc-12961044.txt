Abnormal signal transmission in white matter revealed by resting-state communication connectivity in Alzheimer’s disease: A comprehensive cross-sectional and longitudinal study
Authors: Yihao Guo, Weiyuan Huang, Xiaoli Xiong, Huijuan Chen, Chaoqi Lv, Tao Liu, Feng Chen, Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional functional connectivity of blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals varies with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression. However, it is unable to describe how white matter (WM) is engaged in brain networks. In this study, we introduced a novel communication connectivity metric, which was defined as the triple-wise correlation coefficient between BOLD signals from pairs of gray matter volume and white matter bundles, to investigate the change of signal transition through WM bundles. A total of 169 participants with longitudinal resting-state fMRI data from the ADNI dataset were included, which consisted of 44 cognitively normal (CN), 58 early mild cognitive impairment (EMCI), 45 late MCI (LMCI), and 22 AD. Cross-sectional analyses at baseline and longitudinal within-group comparisons were conducted to examine changes in pattern correlation coefficients (CC) between 2D graphs across the AD continuum. In the cross-sectional study, the 2D graph of the CN group showed moderate correlation with those of the EMCI and LMCI groups, whereas these associations generally declined in the AD dementia group. Bootstrapping test showed that the pattern CC for the right retrolenticular part of internal capsule (RLIC.R) and posterior corona radiata (PCR.R) significantly declined in the EMCI, LMCI, and AD groups for both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies. These results demonstrated that signal transmission in RLIC.R and PCR.R has great potential to be markers in the early diagnosis of AD and tracking the progression of AD. Communication connectivity based on rs-fMRI is a promising tool for investigating WM signal transmission alterations in AD.Subject terms: Long-term memory, Diagnostic markers

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) stands as the leading cause of dementia in elderly adults [1]. In patients with AD, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) typically manifests as the initial symptomatic stage [2], preceding a steady decline in cognitive function [3]. Furthermore, decades before the manifestation of clinical symptoms, recent neuroimaging-based [4, 5] and fluid-related [6, 7] studies have detected alterations in biomarkers that precede the development of AD. To elucidate the pathologic nature of the disorder and facilitate the development of therapeutic interventions, it is crucial to investigate more early biomarkers in individuals with a potential risk of developing AD.

During progression from early-stage AD dementia, anatomical, structural, and functional alterations occur in the brain [8–10], which can be visualized via neuroimaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Previous studies have demonstrated that the clinical progression to dementia is associated with progressive atrophy in gray matter (GM) [11, 12]. Moreover, cortical and white matter (WM) microstructures are significantly associated with cognition [13, 14] and pathology [15, 16] in AD. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) reveals that abnormal functional connectivity of blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals is associated with cognitive decline [17] and Aβ deposition [18, 19].

As for rs-fMRI, the traditional functional connectivity of BOLD defined by the correlation between pairs of GM areas ignores how WM is engaged in the brain network [20, 21]. Recent studies have demonstrated that BOLD signals in WM are robustly detectable and are modulated by neural activities [22–24]. Anatomically, GM processing regions are physically connected via WM tracts composed of axonal fibers that form structurally distinct bundles to subserve various information communication functions [25]. Changes to WM microstructure alter the fidelity of neural signal transmissions and consequently affect brain functions, which have been demonstrated during development and aging as well as in degenerative brain disorders [26, 27]. However, the alteration of BOLD signals transmission via WM in patients with AD is unknown.

A recent study introduced a triple-wise correlation coefficient (twCC) to characterize the BOLD signals transmission between pairs of GM through WM bundle, terming this measure communication connectivity, and found that the twCC is sensitive to detect age-related change [28]. In this study, we employed twCC to investigate the communication connectivity changes across the AD continuum. Considering that there are currently very few longitudinal rs-fMRI studies conducted in the AD continuum, we will examine group differences in twCC at baseline and evaluate longitudinal change in twCC within groups, aiming to provide a comprehensive and dynamic understanding of the communication connectivity changes with AD progression. Materials and methods

Subjects

A total of 178 individuals with baseline and follow-up MRI examinations from the ADNI dataset were initially included in this study, comprising 48 cognitive normal (CN) participants, 60 with early MCI, 46 with late MCI, and 24 with AD dementia. The ADNI study was launched in 2003 by the National Institute on Aging, the National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering, the Food and Drug Administration, private pharmaceutical companies, and non-profit organizations. The ADNI is a multi-site longitudinal study. Regional ethical committees of all participating institutions approved the ADNI protocol, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. In this study, each participant underwent both baseline and follow-up MRI examinations.

Data acquisition and processing

Three-dimensional T1-weighted and rs-fMRI images were acquired using 3 T Philips Medical Systems scanners. The rs-fMRI images were obtained with a repetition time of 3 s, an echo time of 30 ms, a flip angle of 80° or 90°, a matrix 64 × 64, 140 volumes, 48 slices per volume, and a voxel size of 3.3 × 3.3 × 3.3 mm3. For further details on MRI acquisitions, refer to the “MRI scanner protocol” at the ADNI website.

The rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using the GRETNA toolbox (http://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna) in MATLAB. Specifically, the first 10 volumes were discarded. Images then underwent slice timing correction (with the middle slice as the reference) and head motion correction. Subsequently, the images were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using individual T1-weighted images. Last, a band-pass filter of 0.01 to 0.1 Hz was applied to reduce low-frequency physiological noise. The entire brain was parcellated into 82 GM regions (i.e. 41 pairs of Brodmann areas in the two hemispheres) and 48 WM bundles (i.e. 21 pairs of WM bundles on two hemispheres and 6 commissure bundles) in MNI space, according to the Brodmann atlas [29] and JHU ICBM-81-DTI WM atlas [30], respectively.

Multigraph model and triple-wise correlation

We used each of the 82 Brodmann areas as a node in a multigraph model, producing 82 × (82-1)/2 pairs of GM nodes. Each pair of GM nodes potentially has 48 functional routes (WM bundles) for signal communication (i.e., edges), leading to a maximum number of 82 × (82-1)/2 × 48 ( = 94464) edges in this multigraph. Each edge in the multigraph was defined by the twCC of rs-fMRI signals from each pair of GM regions and an individual WM bundle.

The conventional functional connectivity calculated the correlation coefficient between each pair of GMs. In contrast, the twCC describes the statistical associations among three functional units and quantifies the degree of synchronization shared by a GM-WM-GM triplet. The twCC is calculated by first deriving a covariance matrix from the functional time series of the triplet and then deriving eigenvalues of the covariance matrix using principal components analysis. Last, each edge in the multigraph is calculated as the difference between the two largest eigenvalues divided by the sum of all three eigenvalues, with larger values indicating greater synchronization within the GM-WM-GM triplet. The corresponding code was described in the previous study [28]. This multigraph thus represents a complete communication model for any pair of GM regions via any WM bundles.

2D graph and pattern CC

2D graphs were normalized by dividing the twCC value of each matrix element in each 2D graph by the average of the corresponding element across all WM bundles. Normalized 2D graphs portray twCC values for a single WM bundle and all pairs of GM regions. Each element in a 2D graph quantifies how a single WM bundle supports the communication between each pair of GM regions. There are 48 such 2D graphs, with each representing the communication between all GM pairs via a specific WM pathway.

The pattern CC was defined as the correlation coefficient (CC) between different 2D graphs. We compared communication connectivity matrices obtained for the CN group with those of the EMCI, LMCI, and AD groups. For the longitudinal study, the pattern CC was calculated between baseline and follow-up 2D graphs within each group. The same group comparisons were performed using the GM-GM matrix, which contained only the correlation coefficient of the time series for each pair of GM regions only.

1D plot and k-means clustering

The twCC values in each 1D plot were divided by the average of the corresponding twCC values across all 2D graphs to produce normalized 1D plots. Each normalized 1D plot represents twCC values from a single pair of GM nodes across all WM bundles, illustrating how each specific pair of GM regions communicates via each WM bundle. Each 1D plot contains 48 twCC values, and there are 82 × 82 such 1D plots. The edge connectivity in 1D plots reflects how the distribution of communication between a pair of GM regions across different WM bundles.

We used k-means cluster analysis to identify similarities among these 1D plots. The Z scores of the normalized 1D plots from all pairs of GM regions across all diagnostic groups for both baseline and follow-up (82 × 82 × 4 × 2 = 53792) were organized into a matrix (53792 × 48). This matrix was subjected to the k-means cluster analysis, yielding eight clusters.

Statistical analysis

The one-way ANOVA test and χ2 test were employed to identify differences in demographic and clinical data. To test the significance of the pattern CC of the 2D graph between two groups, a bootstrapping-based test was applied [31]. For CN versus EMCI groups in the cross-sectional study, with the CN group as the reference, subjects (n = 58) were randomly selected from the paired groups (CN and EMCI) and served as the new group. Then the pattern CC between the reference group and the new group for each bootstrapping was recalculated and recorded. Similarly, bootstrapping-based tests were performed in CN versus LMCI and CN versus AD, respectively. Furthermore, a bootstrapping-based test was applied for the longitudinal study, with the baseline for each group as the reference. After 1000 bootstrapping, 1000 pattern CC scores were obtained, and we defined scores within the 1% tails of the distribution as significant at p < 0.01.

We examined cross-sectional group differences and longitudinal changes within each group for the pattern CC of the 2D graph. Pattern CC values between normalized 2D graphs from several representative WM bundles in the CN group versus other groups at baseline are shown (Fig. 3A). In the cross-sectional study, communication connectivity matrices showed moderate correlations between the CN group and both the EMCI and LMCI groups. These associations generally declined with the AD dementia group. Bootstrapping tests revealed that pattern CC values significantly decreased in the EMCI group for the right retrolenticular part of internal capsule (RLIC.R) and right posterior corona radiata (PCR.R) at baseline. For the LMCI group, pattern CC values significantly declined for several WM bundles, including the Fornix, right cerebral peduncle (CBRP.R), RLIC.R, and PCR.R. Figure 3B displays the pattern CC of normalized 2D graphs between baseline and follow-up. Pattern CC values significantly decreased in the AD dementia group across all nine WM bundles. The longitudinal analysis showed a high pattern CC in the CN group, except for the left posterior corona radiata (PCR.L) and right tapetum (TAP.R). In both EMCI and LMCI groups, pattern CC values declined for CBRP.R, RLIC.R, PCR.R, and PCR.L. In the AD dementia group, pattern CC values declined for the Fornix, CBRP.R, left cerebral peduncle (CBRP.L), RLIC.R, PCR.R, and left tapetum (TAP.L). The same comparisons were performed using the GM-GM matrix. Results showed strong correlations in the cross-sectional study between the CN group and other groups, as well as in the longitudinal study between baseline and follow-up within groups; however, pattern CC values did not reach significance in the Bootstrapping test.

Discussion

In this study, we defined communication connectivity to characterize the BOLD signals transmission between pairs of GM through WM bundles and found that the 2D graph was sensitive to capturing the alteration of signal transmission in AD progression. Bootstrapping tests showed that pattern CC values for RLIC.R and PCR.R significantly declined in the EMCI, LMCI, and AD groups in both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, suggesting that the alteration of signal transmission in these two WM bundles occurred at the early stage of AD and persisted across the AD continuum. The 1D plot showed that low values of the consistency matrix were observed in the AD group for clusters 1, 2, 4, and 8, demonstrating that the 1D plot can capture signal alteration in WM at the late stage of AD.

Conventional functional connectivity depends on the magnitude of the common signal components shared by a pair of GMs relative to all other signals [32, 33], and can be viewed as a weighted average of 2D graphs across all WM bundles [28]. The twCC values have different scaling effects on different 2D graphs because of local signal components in WM, resulting in replicated patterns across the 2D graphs. To address this, twCC values in each matrix element in each 2D graph are divided by the average of the corresponding element across all WM bundles to produce normalized twCC. The normalized twCC is more specific in reflecting the distribution of signal communications along different WM pathways. The larger variation of 2D graph across diagnostic groups suggests that the GM-WM-GM communication network is more sensitive to AD progression than conventional networks. Each normalized 2D graph reflects the relative engagement of the selected WM bundle with different pairs of GM regions. Higher values in the 2D graph suggest more extensive specific communication via the bundle. Similar 2D graphs were observed across the diagnostic groups, demonstrating that the GM-WM-GM communication network was reasonable.

In the EMCI and LMCI groups, several networks, including CBRP.L, left cingulum hippocampus (CGH.L), TAP.R, and TAP.L, more closely resembled both the CN network and the follow-up network, suggesting that these WM bundles are preserved in both the EMCI and LMCI stages. The network of the AD group significantly differed from the CN network across all 9 WM bundles, indicating abnormal signal transition in these WM bundles during the late stage of AD. This finding is consistent with previous studies, which reported WM structural injury in patients with AD with diffusion tensor imaging [34, 35]. Our study demonstrates a novel method for capturing WM functional injury for AD based on the rs-fMRI. In the future, combining rs-fMRI and DTI data enables the investigation of both functional and structural changes and their associations during AD progression.

Each 1D plot illustrates how communication between a single pair of GM areas is distributed across different WM bundles. Clustering 1D plots aims to identify GM pairs with similar patterns of WM engagement. In cluster analysis, the cluster number is a critical parameter. The optimal value yields high similarities within clusters and high differences among clusters. The previous study has demonstrated that eight clusters are the optimal clusters in 1D plots [28]. Thus, eight clusters are applied for the 1D plots clustering in this work. The cluster consistency matrix (Fig. 4B) shows higher values on the diagonal than in other regions, indicating that most GM pairs share similar 1D plots and they may maintain basic function across AD progression. Red color in the matrices suggests that two clusters of these regions have similar 1D plots (Fig. 5), and are prone to being confused with each other, suggesting that the GM pairs in these clusters may be co-engaged.

There are some potential limitations in the current study. First, the longitudinal fMRI data from the ADNI dataset had a relatively short time interval (9-30 months). Therefore, further long-term longitudinal studies are recommended to investigate the dynamic alternations in communication connectivity. Additionally, the varied follow-up time may introduce bias in longitudinal analysis. Second, our dataset included CN, EMCI, LMCI, and AD groups, with no inclusion of individuals with subjective memory complaints. Thus, we could not observe the change of signal transmission within WM in the super early stage of AD. Third, we relied on the Brodmann atlas for analyzing communication connectivity. More robust parcellation scales and test-retest reliability atlas should be explicitly investigated in our future work. Fourth, the sample size was small, and this study lacks external validation, so the current findings need to be validated in a large cohort with multiple center data in future research. Finally, this study focused on analyzing alterations in communication connectivity through pairwise comparisons of groups across AD stages, and could not reveal the causal relationships in AD progression.

In conclusion, alterations in signal transmission within RLIC.R and PCR.R were observed at the early stage of AD and persisted across the AD continuum. These findings identify potential markers for the early diagnosis of AD, as well as for tracking disease progression and evaluating therapeutic interventions. This study demonstrated that communication connectivity based on rs-fMRI emerges as a promising tool to investigate alterations in WM signal transmission and deepen our understanding of the pathological mechanism underlying AD.
