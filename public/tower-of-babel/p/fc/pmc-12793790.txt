Advances on design considerations in Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience
Authors: Margaret A Sheridan, Brenden Tervo-Clemmens, Ece Demir-Lira, Anthony Steven Dick, Jamie L Hanson, Leah H Somerville, Chad M Sylvester, Moriah E Thomason, Sarah Whittle, Deanna M Barch, Beatriz Luna
Published: 2025

The flagship journal of our field, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience has thrived as an outlet for high-caliber research investigating neurodevelopmental processes. We have maintained and increased our impact on the field at a time when many journals are observing declines in overall impact metrics. Our rate of article submissions continues to be high, and our editorial standards continue to ensure that we are a reliable source of novel and high-quality research for readers and an excellent outlet for our best work as scientists. Given the robust performance of our journal and our continuing work to form a mutually collaborative relationship with our publishers, we are excited for the future of this journal. Following a few years of ‘taking stock’, we wanted to communicate with our readers and with the Flux Society regarding some of our editorial policies (below). In addition, we want to reiterate the connection between the Flux Society and Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, a relationship we hope will continue to be mutually beneficial for our members and readers. In light of this connection, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience will be offering two cash prizes for ‘best paper’ to be awarded next year at the annual Flux Society conference.

Expanding consideration of within developmental stage studies

DCN was originally designed by its founder Sarah-Jayne Blakemore and her team to support neurocognitive studies throughout development, an area that was not well supported at the time, making an enormous impact on the field. When Bea Luna became Editor-in-Chief (EIC), she made an important change to focus on the developmental process as a requirement, emphasizing that the process of change is critical to our field. Thus, the editorial board emphasized consideration of research studies focused on cross-sectional comparisons across age or longitudinal comparisons across development to the exclusion of single-age studies. This emphasis within Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience added to existing growth in the field by showcasing studies with longitudinal design. Ultimately, the field pivoted to the development of large-scale national and cross-national longitudinal studies of brain development, as well as smaller investigator-led studies of longitudinal brain development. In addition, the emphasis on examining age-related associations in cross-sectional studies increased the likelihood that researchers within the field could compare these to those examining longitudinal change. These kinds of comparisons allow differential identification of developmental and cohort processes. There has now been a continuous increase of longitudinal studies published in Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, documenting and examining developmental change (e.g., Calabro et al., 2025; Paulsen et al., 2025; Rakesh et al., 2025).

Thus, as the field has expanded and research on neurodevelopment has rapidly grown across the last ten years, the variety of studies that are directly relevant to examining neurodevelopmental change has significantly improved. At this point, the editorial board believes that studies examining relevant developmental processes within a developmental stage can complement and inform longitudinal data collection. Across the last ten years, our methods for investigating neural structure and function during development have improved dramatically, and the range of processes we are able to observe in vivo have increased. In addition, the age-range across which we’re able to measure neural structure and function has expanded. In this context, studies examining brain behavior variation within a specific age delineate another important source of variance within neurodevelopment. Indeed, well-executed studies within a developmental stage can work in concert with longitudinal studies to further our knowledge of development. Thus, the editorial board has decided to return to the original approach of DCN considering all studies examining neurocognitive development, both those examining change across age (cross-sectional comparing different ages or longitudinal across ages) and those investigating within age (processes within a developmental stage) variation in neurodevelopmental processes.

The heart of developmental processes is change, and Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience will always maintain a focus on publishing high-quality longitudinal studies and cross-sectional studies that examine associations with age. We are excited to now return to our roots and welcome studies that examine, specifically, within-developmental stage variation in neurodevelopmental processes that will inform future developmental studies.

Design considerations and sample size

(contributed by Chad Sylvester, Leah Somerville, Anthony Dick, Brenden Tervo-Clemmens)

A second editorial policy, adopted by Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, which positively shaped the scientific efforts of our field, was an exacting emphasis on increasing standard sample sizes within neurodevelopmental studies. This emphasis is related, but not identical to, increasing calls within the field to diversify and identify the populations from which our samples are selected. To address the first concern, publication of small samples and consequently large effects with potentially poor replicability, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience thus adopted a sample size limit for consideration for publication. This clear emphasis on sample size has positively influenced the field, creating a standard within the field for larger samples of participants and likely increasing the replicability of observed effects across studies. However, the strict sample size limit is a simplification of sample size consideration within any scientific study. This was the topic of the Flux May 2024 Roundtable on Sample Guidelines with Brenden Tervo-Clemmens, Chad Sylvester, Leah Somerville, and Anthony Dick, who provided recommendations on best practices. Following these recommendations, the editorial board decided to expand the sample size requirement. Instead, to update the existing editorial policy, we now include a section, “Design considerations and sample size” within the instructions for authors. Here we reproduce this text for consideration by our readers and the Flux community.

To ensure transparency and promote rigor and reproducibility, all manuscripts are required to provide context and justification for the employed study design, under the subheading “Design Considerations” in the Methods section. Please justify the study design and sample size used in your study, with context for its analytic plan (e.g., cross-sectional correlation, longitudinal study, within-person manipulation) and other measures of robustness (e.g., pre-registration of hypotheses and/or analytic plan, out-of-sample or out-of-dataset replication) and measurement reliability. Authors should likewise discuss the range of effect sizes detectable in reference to similar studies in the literature, particularly well-powered efforts, in relation to the effect size in the present study and its analytic plan and design (e.g., precision, cohort, or population studies). Reproducibility and practical utility of detectable and reported effect sizes should also be considered. For example, very small studies will require conventionally large effect sizes (e.g., r’s > .50) for statistical significance that may fail to replicate. In contrast, very large population-level studies may find statistical significance with very small effect sizes (e.g., r’s < .01) that may lack practical utility. The journal requires authors to discuss and transparently report on these issues with respect to their own study for reviewers to assess. Notably, smaller sample sizes with multilevel approaches and repeated acquisitions can gain increased power.

Manuscripts that lack sufficient design details may be returned without review if a basic assessment of scientific merit cannot be conducted. Reviewers and editors will also take into consideration this design justification section in their full evaluation of the work.

The following are constructed, illustrative examples for common designs. Please modify and include/exclude relevant details as necessary and internally reference relevant additional manuscript sections.

Example for population-level imaging: This current study used a large-scale, publicly available dataset with a population design, where participants were recruited from multiple sites with minimal exclusion and inclusion criteria meant to maximize overall sample size (see above). This has the advantage of statistical power by way of a large sample size, but relatively small effect sizes with more limited practical utility may nevertheless be statistically significant. Therefore, we set an a-priori effect size threshold (r = .10) to jointly identify effects of interest along with statistical significance. To further increase rigor and reproducibility we analyzed data in matched, split halves of the dataset. An additional potential design consideration for this study is that the measurement reliability for the reviewed measures are relatively low (see Results).

Example for precision functional mapping: This study employed a precision functional mapping design using a well-characterized clinical cohort of children diagnosed with ADHD to investigate the developing brain's response during a continuous performance task. Participants were selected based on stringent inclusion criteria. The design included a neurotypical control group, and the study used high-resolution imaging to achieve fine-grained spatial mapping of brain activity. The sample size was determined based on previous studies showing medium effect sizes (r = .30) in similar contexts. To enhance the robustness of the findings, we pre-registered our hypotheses and analysis plan on a repository on the Open Science Framework (OSF), and all imaging data underwent rigorous quality control procedures to ensure measurement reliability (see Methods). Our analytic strategy included both within-subject and between-subject comparisons to account for individual variability and improve generalizability. The precision of our functional mapping allows for detecting smaller effect sizes (r = .20) that might be missed in studies with lower reliability, thus providing a balance between statistical significance and practical utility.

Example for cross-sectional studies: In this cross-sectional cohort study, we examined the relationship between sleep patterns and cognitive function in children aged 6–12 years, using structural imaging to measure the volume of the hippocampal subfields in each child. Participants were recruited from local elementary schools to ensure a diverse representation of socioeconomic backgrounds. Our final sample size of 200 children was determined based on expected effect sizes from prior studies of hippocampal development in this age range. We employed standardized sleep questionnaires, cognitive assessments, and high-resolution functional MRI (fMRI) scans to measure hippocampal volume related to cognitive functions. This multilevel approach ensured the consistency and reliability of our measurements (see Measures). Although the cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causation, we controlled for potential confounders such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and general health to strengthen the validity of our findings. We acknowledge that while our sample size is sufficient to detect significant associations, the practical implications of these associations may vary across different subgroups within the population.

Example for longitudinal cohort studies: This longitudinal cohort study investigated the development of social skills and brain function in children from second grade to sixth grade, using brain imaging methods to enhance the depth of our analysis. We recruited a diverse cohort of 60 children from multiple elementary schools to enhance the generalizability of our findings. Sample size calculations accounted for anticipated attrition and were based on detecting small to medium effects (r = .25), as reported in previous longitudinal studies (see Methods). Our analytic plan included annual assessments of social skills using validated observational and self-report measures, along with annual fMRI scans to observe changes in brain activity associated with social interactions and development. A total of four time points were collected. This comprehensive data collection protocol ensured robust measurement reliability and data integrity. By assessing multiple time points, we aimed to capture temporal changes and relationships between social skill development and brain function. The longitudinal design enables us to observe developmental trends and infer potential causal relationships over time, although individual variability in social development and adherence to study protocols may influence practical utility. All analyses were conducted with considerations for potential biases and confounding factors, and results were cross-validated using split-sample approaches to further ensure robustness.

In sum, in response to our rapidly evolving field and feedback from our Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience community, we have made editorial changes to once again consider within stage developmental studies and determine sample size based on justification. We believe these changes will encourage best design practices and support the expansion and growth of our field.
