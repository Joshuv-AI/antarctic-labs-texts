Lifestyle behaviors and health-related quality of life: a comparative analysis of older and non-older adults
Authors: Lipei Zhao, Jian Wu, WenYong Dong, Quanman Li, Mingze Ma, Yifei Feng, Zhanlei Shen, Xinghong Guo, Clifford Silver Tarimo, Yudong Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine and compare the associations between lifestyle behaviors and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among older and non-older adults.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from Feb. 1 to 18, 2023 in East, Central, West, and Northeast regions of China. HRQoL was assessed by five-level EuroQol-5D scale and visual analogue scale (VAS). Demographic information and lifestyle behaviors were compared between non-older adults (aged < 60 years) and older adults (aged ≥ 60 years). Tobit regressions and generalized linear model were employed to evaluate the associations of lifestyle behaviors with utility index (-0.391,1.000) and VAS score (0,100).ResultsA total of 5,780 adults were included in the analysis (21.33% older adults, median age: 66.00 years; 78.67% non-older adults, median age: 40.00 years). The mean utility index and VAS score were 0.953 and 76.22 among older adults, and 0.980 and 82.08 among non-older adults, respectively (all P < 0.05). The median lifestyle score was 33.00 for older adults and 34.00 for non-older adults (P < 0.05). Higher lifestyle scores were associated with higher HRQoL in both age groups. Engaging in physical activity, practicing psychological decompression, maintaining work and study routines, and undergoing regular physical examinations were significantly associated with the utility index and VAS score in both groups (all P < 0.05). Smoking status showed a stronger association with HRQoL among older adults, whereas maintaining a reasonable weight, limiting unhealthy diets, obtaining sufficient sleep, and maintaining social relationships were significantly associated with HRQoL among non-older adults (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsOlder adults reported lower HRQoL and lifestyle scores than non-older adults. Although higher lifestyle scores were associated with better HRQoL in both groups, the patterns of associations differed by age, with smoking-related factors more prominent among older adults and diet, sleep, weight, and social relationships more relevant among non-older adults. These findings highlight age-specific patterns in lifestyle-HRQoL associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01862-x.

Introduction

The issue of aging has garnered increasing attention due to its implications for the health of older individuals. With evolving health needs and heightened awareness, the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has emerged as a significant concern for both individuals and society at large [1]. HRQoL, as an assessment of health status within the modern concept of healthcare, reflects physical, mental, emotional, and social functioning in relation to overall well-being [2]. Older adults with enhanced quality of life and robust health not only experience reduced disease burden but also contribute to social and economic advancement. Conversely, illness can significantly reduce HRQoL, making its preservation a crucial therapeutic goal during disease management [3]. Moreover, maintaining and enhancing HRQoL can be achieved through the adoption of health-related lifestyle behaviors [4], which has become a pivotal focus of current research.

The significance of healthy lifestyle behaviors lies in their relative ease of modification compared to other health-related factors, thus garnering wide attention [5, 6]. In addition, the promotion of a healthy lifestyle as a crucial public health measure has been strongly advocated, owing to its remarkable cost-effectiveness [7]. Accumulating evidence shows that adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviors improves physiological function, enhances immune response, reduces the risk of comorbidities, and facilitates faster recovery and better management of existing conditions. Collectively, these benefits help preserve physical, mental, and social functioning, thereby preventing deterioration in overall HRQoL [8–10]. For instance, a survey in the United States revealed that sedentary older adults with declining mobility were more likely to report poorer quality of life [11]. Recent findings from China indicated that several lifestyle and health factors were positively associated with HRQoL. Specifically, each unit increase in BMI was associated with a 0.004-point higher EQ-5D score, good sleep quality corresponded to a 7.090-point increase in EQ- vertical visual analogue scale (VAS), each additional leisure activity was linked to a 0.092-point higher EQ-5D score, and stronger social connections with friends were associated with a 2.509-point increase in EQ-VAS [4]. Expert consensus guidelines also highlight exercise and physical activity as essential to optimizing health, preventing disease, and managing conditions in older adults [12, 13]. In addition, healthy lifestyle practices have been recognized as critical targets in geriatric medicine to promote healthy aging and preserve quality of life [14].

China’s role in promoting healthy aging is particularly important, not only because it accounts for nearly one-fifth of the world’s population [15], but also because its elderly demographic is growing at an unprecedented rate due to extended life expectancy and declining birth rates [16]. According to the seventh national population census, individuals aged ≥ 60 years constitute 18.7% of the population. Furthermore, older adults often experience reduced social engagement and physical capacity, along with higher prevalence of chronic conditions [17], which may present unique challenges in maintaining healthy lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL.

In contrast, younger adults are generally at a life stage where health behaviors are more malleable, social engagement is stronger, and chronic disease burden is lower. However, unhealthy behaviors such as physical inactivity, poor diet, smoking, and insufficient sleep are increasingly prevalent among younger populations [18, 19]. These behaviors may accumulate adverse health effects, contributing to impaired HRQoL and increasing the risk of future morbidity. International studies have also linked unhealthy lifestyles in younger adults with poorer mental health, reduced work productivity, and impaired social functioning, underscoring the long-term impact of lifestyle patterns established early in life [20, 21]. Yet, relevant evidence from China remains limited. Taken together, HRQoL in younger adults may be influenced differently by lifestyle behaviors, offering greater opportunities for preventive interventions and long-term health benefits.

Comparing older and younger adults provides a valuable opportunity to understand whether and how the determinants of HRQoL differ across life stages. Although international studies have examined age-related differences in HRQoL, there is still limited evidence from China. Given these considerations, we hypothesize that there may be differences in the associations between lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL among older and non-older Chinese adults. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL between older and non-older adults, and to evaluate the associations of healthy lifestyle behaviors with HRQoL in both groups.

Research design and methods

Participants and procedures

The study population was drawn from the Dynamic Evolution of COVID-19 Vaccination Study using a stratified random sampling method. The data for the present study were derived from the follow-up survey conducted in February 2023. At baseline, 6,781 participants were enrolled. After excluding those who declined to participate in the follow-up survey (n = 73), were lost to follow-up (n = 814), or had died (n = 3), a total of 5,891 participants completed the follow-up questionnaire. After further excluding participants with incomplete or low-quality questionnaire data (n = 111), 5,780 participants were included in this study. The age criterion for this study classified participants aged ≥ 60 years as older adults and those < 60 years as non-older adults. Detailed information regarding the sample selection and flow of participants can be found in Fig. 1 and in our previously published study [22]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University.

Measurement

Lifestyle behaviors

We referred to the healthy lifestyle behaviors advocated by the American college of Lifestyle Medicine to design the lifestyle factors questionnaire [14]. The lifestyle behaviors questionnaire consisted of ten items, including smoking status, drinking status, maintaining a reasonable weight, controlling unhealthy diets, getting enough sleep, engaging in physical activities, practicing psychological decompression, relationships with others, maintaining work and study routines, and keeping regular physical examinations. Reliability and validity were evaluated prior to the survey. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.765, and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure was 0.870, indicating acceptable internal consistency and good construct validity of the questionnaire. The details of each item were provided in Supplementary Table 1. Items 1–2 were scored on a scale of 1 to 3 (often = 1 point; give up = 2 points; never = 3 points), while items 3–10 were scored on a scale of 1 to 5 (never = 1 point; rarely = 2 points; sometimes = 3 points; often = 4 points; always = 5 points). A total score was calculated based on the sum of all individual scores. The total lifestyle scores ranged from 10 to 43, with higher scores indicating healthier lifestyles. To represent different health levels, the lifestyle scores were divided into Q1(first lifestyle score), Q2 (second lifestyle score), Q3(third lifestyle score), and Q4 (fourth lifestyle score) based on quartiles (P25=30, P50=34, P75=38). This approach allowed for the categorization of participants into groups based on their respective lifestyle scores, with the higher quartiles indicating better adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviors.

Health-related quality of life

EQ-5D, an instrument to measure HRQoL, is widely used to assess outcomes, measure utility index in healthcare interventions, and provide a consistent and transparent measurement for evaluating health policy programs [4, 23]. There is a growing interest in using EQ-5D to measure population health and the results demonstrate its validity [24, 25]. In this study, the health status of participants was assessed using EQ-5D-5 L and EQ-VAS. The former consisted of five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression), each scored on a 5-point scale ranging from no problems to extreme problems. The scores for these dimensions were combined into a 5-digit score that was then converted into a utility index ranging from − 0.391 to 1.000, from worst to best health state [24]. Studies that directly elicit preferences from general population samples to extract value sets for the EQ-5D-5 L were completed in several countries, including in China [24, 25]. The negative score indicated that some health states were considered worse than death. The Chinese version has been validated and has shown increased sensitivity in both healthy individuals and patients [24, 25]. The EQ-5D-5 L also included a vertical VAS, with participants asked to mark on the provided line or provided a whole number that represents how their health was on a scale of 0-100. The higher the VAS score, the better HRQoL.

Covariates

Demographic and health-related variables were collected, including age, gender (men and women), marital status (married and others), residence (urban and rural), education (senior high school or below, high school graduate, and university graduate or above), number of chronic diseases, primary vaccination of COVID-19, COVID-19 infection, subjective social status, and subjective community status. These covariates were selected based on previous evidence showing their potential associations with lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL, thereby allowing control for possible confounding factors [26–28]. The number of chronic diseases surveyed refers to the total count of diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, chronic kidney diseases, coronary heart disease, and other diseases (open-ended responses are allowed). COVID-19 vaccination status was assessed by asking: “Have you ever been vaccinated against COVID-19?” with three response options: (1) Yes, and I have completed all doses of the primary vaccination series; (2) Yes, but I have not completed all doses of the primary vaccination series; and (3) No. COVID-19 infection was determined by the question: “Did you get infected with the COVID-19 virus?” Responses indicating (1) a confirmed diagnosis by a medical professional, (2) abnormal results from nucleic acid testing, antigen testing, viral culture, or serological tests, (3) the presence of clinical symptoms, or (4) abnormal clinical examinations such as chest X-ray/CT, lung function, blood oxygen saturation, or blood routine, were coded as infected (1). Responses of “No” or “Uncertain” were coded as not infected (0). Subjective social status and subjective community status were assessed on a 10-point scale, where participants either marked their position on an online scale or entered an integer from 1 to 10. These scores were subsequently categorized into three levels according to quantiles.

Statistical analysis

This study was reported following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. Continuous variables were described using the means ± standard deviations (SD) or median (interquartile ranges, IQR). Meanwhile, the Student’s t-test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) or non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were carried out to compare differences in the utility index and VAS score across the subgroups and older and non-older adults as appropriate. The Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages, and intergroup differences were compared by the Chi-square test and the Kruskal Wallis test. The dependent variables were the EQ-5D utility index and VAS score. A collinearity test was conducted to evaluate the presence of correlation among the independent variables, utilizing a variance inflation factor (VIF) threshold of < 10. The results of the test indicated the absence of collinearity among the independent variables, suggesting that they were not highly correlated with each other. The Tobit regression model is designed to estimate the linear relationships between variables when either left- or right-censoring occurs in the dependent variable, for example, the EQ-5D index score [29]. Therefore, the multivariate Tobit regression model was utilized to assess the influence of lifestyle behaviors on the utility index due to the skewed distribution. As VAS were measured on nonlinear, ordinary linear regression is not appropriate due to the “retransformation bias” [30]. Therefore, a generalized linear model (GLM) was performed to assess the impact of lifestyle behaviors on VAS score. To identify candidate covariates for inclusion in the multivariate Tobit regression or GLM, univariate analyses were first conducted. Based on the results of the univariate analyses (Supplementary Table 2), two models were constructed: Model 1, unadjusted; and Model 2, adjusted for gender (only for the utility index), marital status (only for the VAS score), education, residence, number of chronic diseases, COVID-19 vaccination, COVID-19 infection, subjective social status, and subjective community status. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, we re-estimated the regression model adjusting all candidate covariates. Second, given that individuals with the same number of chronic diseases may have different disease combinations, we conducted an additional analysis in which each chronic disease (hypertension, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, chronic kidney diseases, coronary heart disease and other diseases) was included as an independent covariate, rather than using the total number of chronic diseases. Data analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and STATA 14.0. P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of older and non-older adults

Of the 5,780 participants, 1,233(21.3%) were older adults aged ≥ 60 years (median age: 66 years), and 4,547(78.7%) were non-older adults aged < 60 years (median age: 40 years). The mean utility index and VAS score of older adults were 0.953 and 76.22, while in the non-older group, there were 0.980, and 82.08, respectively (all P < 0.05). For the lifestyle score, the median of older adults was 33.00, while the non-older group was 34.00 (P < 0.05). Significant differences were observed between the two groups in several variables including residence, number of chronic diseases, COVID-19 infection, subjective social status, subjective community status, smoking status, drinking status, maintaining a reasonable weight, controlling unhealthy diets, getting enough sleep, engaging in physical activities, practicing psychological decompression, and maintaining work and study routines (all P < 0.05). In comparison to non-older adults, older adults exhibited a higher proportion of chronic diseases, and lower scores in subjective social status and community status. The associations between lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL differed between non-older and older adults. Discussion

Our study found that both older and non-older adults reported relatively good HRQoL and engaged in healthier lifestyle behaviors. Higher lifestyle scores were consistently associated with better HRQoL in both groups. Nevertheless, older adults had lower HRQoL and lifestyle scores compared with their non-older counterparts. Moreover, the associations between lifestyle factors and HRQoL differed by age group, suggesting potential heterogeneity across the life course.

Both age groups expressed better HRQoL compared to findings from previous studies conducted in different populations [31–33], and participants in the current study also reported relatively healthier lifestyles. One possible explanation is that individuals may have paid greater attention to health-related behaviors during the COVID-19 pandemic, which has been associated with changes in lifestyle patterns and perceived well-being [34]. In addition, regional and cultural factors have been shown to influence both lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL [35, 36]. However, compared with non-older adults, older adults reported lower scores in both lifestyle and HRQoL. This difference may be related to the higher prevalence of chronic diseases among older adults, as aging is commonly accompanied by declines in physical function and increased disease burden, which are often associated with lower HRQoL. Previous studies have also shown that older adults tend to experience reduced social participation after retirement, which has been associated with poorer cognitive function and mental health outcomes [1]. Consistent with this literature, our findings indicated that older adults were less engaged in work and study activities. In addition, consistent with earlier evidence showing that better psychosocial factors-such as higher socioeconomic status-are associated with better HRQoL [37], older adults in our study reported lower subjective community and social status, and these factors were negatively associated with HRQoL. Older adults with higher socioeconomic status generally have more opportunities to participate in social activities [38], which have been linked to higher HRQoL. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering the physiological and social characteristics of older adults when interpreting observed lifestyle-HRQoL associations and in the design of future research.

The study also showed that engaging in physical activity, practicing psychological decompression, maintaining work and study routines, and undergoing regular physical examinations were consistently associated with HRQoL in both older and non-older adults. Physical activity and psychological decompression showed particularly strong associations with HRQoL, which is consistent with previous research [39, 40]. Maintaining work and study routines was also related to HRQoL, possibly reflecting greater social engagement, structured daily activities, and perceived purpose [41]. Similarly, individuals who undergo regular physical examinations may place greater emphasis on health maintenance and preventive behaviors [42], which are commonly associated with better perceived health and HRQoL.

Notably, the associations between lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL differed between older and non-older adults. Among older adults, former smokers reported lower HRQoL than current smokers, a pattern that has also been observed in previous studies [43]. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, this association is highly susceptible to reverse causation and selection bias. Individuals with poorer health status or existing chronic conditions may be more likely to quit smoking, which could partly explain the lower HRQoL observed among former smokers. Indeed, former smokers in our study had a higher prevalence of chronic diseases than both never and current smokers (Supplementary Table 6). Therefore, the observed HRQoL differences are more likely to reflect underlying health status rather than any beneficial effect of continued smoking. Although nicotine has been reported to exert short-term mood-altering effects [44], our data do not support causal inferences regarding smoking behavior and HRQoL, and longitudinal studies are required to clarify the temporal relationships among smoking cessation, disease onset, and changes in HRQoL. In contrast, among non-older adults, maintaining a reasonable weight, limiting unhealthy diets, obtaining sufficient sleep, and having positive social relationships were positively associated with HRQoL. These associations were not statistically significant in the older adult group, suggesting that the strength and relevance of lifestyle-HRQoL associations may vary by age. This difference may reflect the fact that non-older adults are at a critical stage for building social networks and balancing work-life demands, and their mental health may be more sensitive to disruptions such as social distancing and lockdowns during the COVID-19 pandemic [45]. Moreover, healthy diet, optimal weight, and adequate sleep have been associated with better physical and mental health outcomes in prior research [46], which may provide context for the observed associations with HRQoL in non-older adults. These interpretations should be viewed as explanatory hypotheses rather than causal mechanisms.

Additionally, urban non-older adults reported better HRQoL, which may be related to differences in health awareness, access to healthcare services, and their greater emphasis on healthy lifestyle behaviors (Supplementary Table 7). Moreover, urban residents had access to better medical facilities and a wealth of medical and healthcare resources [47]. Non-older individuals without a history of COVID-19 infection also reported higher HRQoL, possibly reflecting differences in overall health status. Furthermore, HRQoL declined as the number of chronic diseases increased in both age groups, consistent with previous findings [48, 49]. Interestingly, when individuals were in a comorbid state, HRQoL did not differ significantly between older and non-older adults, suggesting that comorbidity itself may be a dominant factor associated with reduced HRQoL, regardless of age. Taken together, these findings underscore age-specific patterns in the associations between lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL. For older adults, physical activity, stress management, work or study engagement, and routine health examinations tended to co-occur with higher HRQoL, whereas for non-older adults, dietary habits, weight management, sleep quality, and social relationships showed stronger associations with HRQoL, alongside physical activity and stress management. These results may help generate hypotheses for future longitudinal and intervention studies, while lifestyle guidance should also consider comorbidity, residence, and prior COVID-19 infection.

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides one of the most comprehensive evaluations of the associations between healthy lifestyle behaviors and HRQoL among older and non-older adults. Compared with previous studies focusing on a limited number of lifestyle behaviors, this comprehensive assessment may help characterize age-specific patterns in lifestyle-HRQoL associations and provide empirical evidence to inform future public health research. The use of a composite lifestyle score allowed for the assessment of dose-response patterns, although these relationships should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Importantly, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and raises the possibility of selection bias. For example, the finding that former smokers had lower HRQoL than current smokers may reflect differences in underlying health status rather than the effects of smoking cessation itself. Accordingly, all results should be interpreted with caution, and prospective studies are needed to establish temporal and causal relationships.

Conclusion

Both older and non-older Chinese adults demonstrated generally favorable HRQoL and lifestyle profiles, although older adults reported lower scores than their younger counterparts. Lifestyle scores were positively associated with HRQoL across age groups, with notable differences in the patterns of associations between older and non-older adults. Among older adults, smoking status, physical activity, stress management, and engagement in work or study were more strongly associated with HRQoL, whereas among non-older adults, weight status, diet, sleep, and social relationships showed stronger associations. These findings highlight age-specific patterns in lifestyle-HRQoL associations and may help inform future longitudinal and intervention research.
