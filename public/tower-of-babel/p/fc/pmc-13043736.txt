Nature-inspired solid-state proton diode membrane for high-performance force-electric conversion
Authors: Dandan Lei, Qixiang Zhang, Yixiang Wang, Shuqi Wang, Naijia Zhao, Lei Jiang, Zhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bioinspired ion channel membranes with ultrafast and directional transport capabilities, akin to biological protein-based channel, are crucial for various energy and sensing applications. However, controlling highly active protons and advancing practical applications, which are often constrained by the use of liquid-based electrolyte, remains major challenges. Here, we report a bioinspired solid-state diode membrane with ultrafast and highly rectified proton transport, achieved through the hybridization of graphene oxide (GO) and Cu-coupled bacterial cellulose (BC-Cu) with inherent gradient in water content. The asymmetric proton transport pathways and potential differences between the two-dimensional GO interlayers and one-dimensional BC network create a stable diode effect, achieving exceptionally high rectification ratios of approximately 125. Theoretical calculations reveal that the distinct ion transport pathways and interfacial potential barrier disparities constitute the fundamental mechanism underlying the highly rectified ionic current. In particular, the enhanced proton transport greatly facilitates the force-electric conversion, allowing the diode membrane to accurately respond to external pressure. Our bioinspired approach to fabricate all-solid-state proton diodes hold great potential for next-generation solid-state rectification systems, with promising applications in areas such as self-powered pressure sensing and neuromorphic computing.

Introduction

Biological ion channels allow unidirectional and rapid ion transport across the cell membrane to ensure proper execution of various physiological processes in the organism1–3. Such unidirectional transport behavior is also known as ionic rectification, which has been proven to boost many applications such as osmotic and photoelectric energy conversion4–8. Bioinspired nanofluidic ion channel membrane emerges as promising candidates to mimic the delicate functionality of biological systems. In the past years, diverse nanofluidic diode membranes featuring unidirectional ion transport ability (i.e. ionic rectification) towards alkali metal (e.g. Na+ and K+) and halogen ion (e.g. Cl-) have been developed9–12. For the proton, the essential building block of atoms, it is still challenging to realize its unidirectional transport, which can be ascribed to the fact that protons are more active with less controllability due to their much smaller radius and higher ionic mobility. On the other hand, protons mostly need to be conducted in compounds with hydrogen bonds, while rational construction of proton conduction networks is also a major problem13–16. Actually, in nature, rectifying proton channels play key roles in many crucial life functions, such as cellular pH stabilization, generation of membrane potential, and energy transduction for ATP synthesis17–19. Therefore, the development of high-performance proton nanofluidic diode membranes is a necessity, which could not only help us understand the biological proton conduction process, but also largely improve the performance of proton-based energy devices.

At the same time, the current nanofluidic diode membranes are mostly applied in liquid environments, which limits their practical applications as liquid-based electrolytes have the risk associated with electrolyte leakage. There are some attempts based on semi-solid systems such as polyelectrolyte hydrogel and elastomer6,20–24. The formers suffer from limitations related to dehydration, while the latter exhibits poor ionic conductivities attributed to restrained transport of mobile counterions. Therefore, the development of efficient proton nanofluidic diode for all-solid-state applications will accelerate the realization of efficient information transfer functions and wide applications like those of living organisms. In the solid-state diode, the mechanism differs from the conventional liquid-state diode in which the electric double layer governs the ion transport behavior, where the proton transport relies on the proton hopping mediated by the water bridge caused by the inherent bound water and a little free water25,26.

In the human epidermis, restricted free water creates an inherent water gradient in the cuticle and dermis layer, playing an important role in the communication of the skin with the environment via directional water and ion transport (Fig. 1a). Inspired by that, a solid-state nanofluidic diode membrane with ultrafast and highly rectified proton transport behaviors was constructed by assembling two excellent proton conductors with distinct water gradient. The heterogeneous diode membrane comprises graphene oxide (GO) membrane with a two-dimensional (2D) interlayered structure and Cu-coupled bacterial cellulose (BC) membrane with one-dimensional (1D) chains containing a large number of oxygen-containing functional groups and extended chain spacing (Fig. 1b-c). The proton transport paths between the 2D interlayers and the 1D chains, as well as the differences in the proton transport potentials on GO and BC-Cu, construct a system-stabilized proton diode effect, which greatly facilitates the unidirectional proton transport from the BC to the GO layers. Such structure rectifies the proton current with a distinctly high ratio of 125 in the solid state. Results

Structure characterization and analysis

GO nanosheets and BC nanofibers have considerable structural differences, with their lateral size varying by about an order of magnitude. The lateral size of GO nanosheet is about 7 μm, while BC exhibits a 1D chain-like structure with a diameter of about 0.15 μm (Supplementary Fig. S1). The stacking of GO and BC results in solid-state membranes with different numbers of voids that can be considered ion transport pathways (Supplementary Fig. S2). The pH and Zeta potential characterization of both materials further confirms that the charge carriers in this system are protons (Supplementary Fig. S3). The resistance of GO (3.66×107 Ω) is about two orders of magnitude higher than BC-Cu (2.23×105 Ω), illustrating the difference in ion transport between the two materials (Supplementary Fig. S4). Actually, BC constructs a reticular ion pathway that provides greater freedom for proton transport, while GO constructs a 2D laminated structure in which only the ion channels within the interlayer are ion-conductive (Supplementary Description and Supplementary Fig. S5-7). This structural difference lays the basis for the observed proton rectification.

However, the ion transport properties of GO and BC based on intrinsic structural differences are comparable, which is not conducive to achieving high proton rectification performance. To achieve even more efficient rectification, we introduced Cu2+ into BC (Fig. 1b). Compared to the white brittle BC membrane, the BC-Cu features a dark blue color (Supplementary Fig. S8), reduced volume and enhanced mechanical properties. The Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of GO and BC-Cu both exhibit strong -OH vibration absorption peaks (the peaks at 3420 cm-1 and 1392 cm-1 for GO and at 3344 cm-1 for BC-Cu) (Fig. 1d), indicating the presence of rich oxygen-containing groups that ensure the abundant bound water of the system. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curves in Fig. 1e illustrate that GO and BC membranes have similar water contents of about 5.93% and 2.53%, respectively. Impressively, after the introduction of Cu2+, the water content of BC membrane increases substantially to about 56.04%. Within this framework, Cu2+ coordinates with hydroxyl groups on cellulose chains, forming stable metal-cellulose complexes. This coordination expands the interchain spacing, creating 1D proton transport channels and providing cation coordination sites. This expanded free volume and the coordinating ions enhance water uptake in the BC-Cu membrane. According to the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. S9), due to the introduction of BC, the hybrid membrane exhibits a high conductivity of 7.9 × 10−5 S/cm. The above characterization results indicate that heterogeneous membrane exhibit high proton conductivity and possess inherent differences in water content (free water and bound water) between the two components.

All-solid-state proton diode performance

The ion transport performance in solid state was characterized by scanning the I-V curves by sandwiching the membrane between two Pt electrodes (inset of Fig. 2a). The composite GO/BC-Cu exhibits unidirectional ion transport behavior, evidenced by a non-linear I-V curve (Fig. 2a), which is different from those of pristine GO and BC membranes with symmetric responses (Supplementary Fig. S10a-b). Such diode-like behavior (i.e. ion rectification) can be quantified by the current rectification ratio (I−1V/I+1V). For the GO/BC composite system, the rectification effect can reach 15.66 when the scan rate is 50 mV/s (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S10c). Notably, after Cu2+ activation, the rectification ratio of the composite membrane increases substantially to 31.46 (Fig. 2b), and can be further improved to 125 when scan rate is at 1 mV/s (Supplementary Fig. S11). In the hybrid membrane, the dominant current carriers are protons caused by inherent bound water as well as the free water inside the nanochannel networks. The efficient unidirectional proton transport behavior can be attributed to the asymmetric ion transport pathways due to structural differences of BC and GO membranes, and asymmetric ion migration potential barriers caused by varying water contents (Supplementary Description and Supplementary Fig. S12). The Cu2+ ion opens the BC chain, enriching the proton and H2O content, which further reduces the migration potential barriers and magnifies such difference in the structure and proton content, contributing to the enhancement of the rectification behavior (Fig. 2c). The performance of the diode membranes is dependent on the thickness of the GO membrane, which affects the longitudinal protons transport rate. The proton diode with thickness ratio of 4:1, with GO being 200 μm and BC-Cu being 50 μm, has the highest ion rectification ratio (Supplementary Fig. S13-S14). The stable current density of the GO/BC-Cu system is 4.27 μA/cm2 at a bias voltage of −1.0 V, which is 14 times larger than the current at reverse bias at +1.0 V (0.304 μA/cm2) (Fig. 2e-f). For BC-Cu membrane, insufficient thickness may compromise humidity retention and stable chemical gradient formation, while excessive thickness can introduce detrimental series resistance, adversely affecting both output power and rectification ratio. Importantly, the diode membrane exhibits obviously rectification at an AC voltage cycle of ±1.0 V (0.1 Hz) (Supplementary Fig. S15) and maintains 100% of its initial rectification ratio within 4 cycles (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, the rectification is still maintained at 82% under 100 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S16 and Fig. 2h-i). The slight degradation of the rectification performance is mainly due to the GO absorbing some of the H2O on the BC-Cu side with prolonged use, causing the ionic conductivity on the GO side to increase. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S17, the water transfer is slightly accelerated at higher temperatures or slowed under lower humidity levels. However, these changes have a negligible impact on the overall device performance. The performance degradation can be mitigated by encapsulating the device with heat-shrinkable polyethylene (PE) film to stabilize its water content. Correspondingly, such diode can also maintain long-term stability at DC voltage (Supplementary Fig. S18) with a stable rectification effect, offering great application prospects that will be discussed in the application section6,22,24,27–29.

Proton transport mechanism

The rectification mechanism of GO/BC-Cu was further investigated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The mean squared displacement (MSD) shows that the migration of protons in 1D BC-Cu membrane has a high degree of freedom, meaning that protons have similar migration distances in the X, Y, and Z directions (Fig. 3a-b). For 2D GO laminated membranes, proton migration along the X and Y components is much greater than that in the Z direction (Fig. 3c-d), mainly because most protons migrate within the nanolayers, making it difficult to achieve cross-layer transport. Then the influence of bound water was investigated, and GO and BC-Cu models with different H2O weights were established based on the TGA results. Among, the BC-Cu side, rich in free water, facilitates proton input with a lower energy barrier. In contrast, the GO side, featuring narrow interlayers with abundant functional groups and more bound water, imposes a higher energy barrier for proton output. The differential charge of Fig. 3e shows the difference in adsorption energies of protons at GO and BC-Cu membranes. Within GO membrane, the protons have a direct binding to the functional groups on the surface of GO nanosheets. On the contrary, cellulose contains a large number of H2O, which shields the adsorption of protons. And that, whether in the GO plane or at the interface, the adsorption energy of protons on GO is far greater than that on BC-Cu (Fig. 3f). In the next, the proton migration process across the BC/GO interface are investigated (Fig. 3g). Figure 3h-j shows in detail the migration barrier of protons from the BC chain to the interface and then to the GO nanolayers. The protons at the interface need to overcome a barrier of 1.6 eV to transport from BC-Cu to the GO side, while the barrier rises substantially to 4.9 eV upon reversing the direction. According to the Arrhenius law, this significant difference in energy barriers results in orders-of-magnitude disparity between the forward and reverse migration rate constants (k), ultimately leading to a high current rectification ratio at the macroscopic level (Supplementary Fig. S19). Therefore, the key reason for the unidirectional ion transport is the asymmetric ion adsorption energy and diffusion barrier between GO and BC-Cu membranes. The proton will flow from the small adsorption energy region with low transport barrier (BC-Cu) to the region of large adsorption energy with high transport barrier (GO), leading to asymmetric proton transport behavior in an alternating electric field, responsible for the efficient proton rectification effect.

High-performance force-electric conversion

The external mechanical pressure can serve as a driving force for protons, and ultrafast, highly rectified proton transport is found to greatly promote the force-electric conversion (Supplementary Fig. S20)30–32. We employed a device consisting of commercial pressure sensor and mechanical thruster to apply external pressure, which is capable of delivering a constant external pressure (Supplementary Fig. S21). Figure 4a-b shows the influence of varying the locations of GO and BC-Cu on force-electric conversion. Altering the position of BC-Cu and GO membranes, while keeping the mechanical pressure direction constant, results in a change in current direction. This is mainly due to the different binding abilities of protons on GO and BC-Cu membranes. When external pressure is applied, protons are transported directionally from the weakly bound BC-Cu side to the strongly bound GO side, creating a directional current. This indicates that the unidirectional proton migration is governed by the potential energy of both membranes, regardless of pressure direction. The mechanical pressure primarily regulates the nanochannels of the laminar membrane and controls the protons transport rate33,34. Specifically, the applied pressure alters the interlayer spacing of the GO membrane and compresses the internal structure of the BC-Cu fiber, prompting ion displacement. Due to the asymmetric energy barriers for ion migration, net ion movement occurs directionally from the BC-Cu layer to the GO membrane under pressure, enabling unidirectional ion transport (Supplementary Fig. S19). When stimulated by an external pressure of 20 N, a single proton diode can generate electrical signals up to 0.5 V and approximately 15 μA (Fig. 4b-c). This force-electric conversion efficiency, achieved via the ion diode, reaches 1.21% (Supplementary Fig. S22-23). In contrast, laminated membranes constructed from pure BC-Cu or pure GO exhibit significantly weaker force-electric conversion capabilities (Supplementary Fig. S24). This underscores the importance of the unidirectional proton transfer of the diode in achieving pressure-to-electrical signal conversion. Figure 4d depicts the output electrical signal of the proton diode at constant pressure, illustrating that the output current of the diode remains stable for up to 50 s, which facilitates the realization of low-frequency energy capture. Additionally, the output current of the diode also increases with the increasing applied pressure, making it suitable for precise pressure sensing (Fig. 4e). According to the formula (Vmax·Imax/A), where the A stands for the area of GO/BC-Cu membrane, the proton diode achieves a highly efficient pressure conversion of 8 µW/ cm2, surpassing many other membranes such as GO, oriented GO, carbon-based materials, and BC/carbon nanotube (Supplementary Fig. S25). Therefore, the all-solid-state proton diode developed in this work has the potential as a self-powered sensor for monitoring pressure signals. Supplementary Fig. S26 illustrates the human pulse signals obtained from this sensor. Through waveform analysis, the device captured all typical wrist pulse features, including the Percussion, Tidal, and Dicrotic peaks, highlighting its potential application in clinical pulse measurement. Figure 4f-g presents a schematic of a 6×6 pixel ion array encapsulated within a flexible Ag electrode array, together with an optical photograph of the flexible fully printed electrode array. Pressure imaging was demonstrated by placing a glass vial (≈ 4.18 Pa) onto the proton diodes array, as shown in Fig. 4h. Particularly, by incorporating the composite membrane into custom flexible electrode arrays shaped like insoles, the pressure-sensing arrays embedded in shoe soles can provide real-time feedback on the stress experienced by the human foot at rest (Supplementary Fig. S27). Naturally, according to the sensitivity curve, the operational pressure range required for the insole application falls within the dynamic range of the prototype pressure sensor (Supplementary Fig. S28).

Remarkably, under low-frequency (0.034 Hz, within 300 s) / high-frequency (0.2 Hz, 1000 cycles) pressure, the output voltage signals of the sensor demonstrates remarkable stability, showing great potential for practical applications in energy harvesting (Supplementary Fig. S29). Connecting proton diodes in series enables an almost linear increase in open-circuit voltage. For example, linking 10 diodes can produce 4.23 V, sufficient to power small devices (Supplementary Fig. S30). Furthermore, the series connection of 90 devices generates a voltage of 32.4 V, which corresponds to 72% of the theoretical value of 45 V, indicating that the system still maintains good scalability (Supplementary Fig. S31). A flexible single-channel diode array demonstrates this by lighting a LED when compressed, due to unidirectional proton migration (Supplementary Fig. S32). When the pressure is released and the diode array is stretched, unidirectional proton migration is halted, causing the LED to turn off. To further increase the output power, an elaborate stacked array structure with 100 diodes in series was designed, as shown in Fig. 4i. Applying external mechanical pressure to this optimized diode array generates a substantial voltage signal of up to 30 V (Fig. 4j). Remarkably, the electrical signal quickly returns to 0 after the external mechanical pressure is withdrawn, demonstrating the tight electrical signal-pressure dependence of the diode array. This efficient output power is capable of charging electronic devices, such as mobile phones, as shown in Fig. 4k. This work represents the first development of a force-electric conversion device based on the ion rectification principle of an all-solid-state proton diode. This approach differs from the vast majority of current energy conversion devices, which typically use triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG), piezoelectric, pyroelectric and solar cells devices35–37. In practice, force-electric conversion devices based on proton diodes demonstrate significant advantages for pressure sensing applications, including the ability to generate direct DC output without the need for rectification, compatibility with both static and low-frequency pressure detection, as well as excellent material stability and environmental adaptability.

Discussion

In summary, we report a bioinspired solid-state diode membrane with highly rectified proton transport, achieved through the hybridization of GO and Cu-coupled BC with inherent gradient in water content. The proton transport pathways between the 2D interlayers of GO and the open network 1D BC-Cu, as well as the differences in the proton transport barriers, construct a system-stabilized rectification effect. This design greatly facilitates the unidirectional proton transport from the BC to the GO layers, contributing to a distinctly record high ratio of 125 in the solid state. Importantly, the directional proton transport significantly boosts the force-electric conversion, allowing the diode membrane to accurately respond to external pressure. Flexible self-powered pressure sensors based on diode membrane structures can be developed into pressure sensing arrays using large-scale integration techniques. This advancement enables high-precision monitoring of pressure distribution at contact interfaces. Notably, the diode membrane unit is capable of generating a significant output voltage of up to 30 V through a multi-stage series connection. This feature enhances its potential for directly powering low-power electronic devices, such as smartphones. The flexible, efficient, and simple-to-prepare all-solid-state proton diodes are expected to have excellent applications in all-solid-state rectification and self-powered pressure sensing.

Methods

Preparation of GO laminated membrane

A modified Hummers’ method was used to prepare GO nanosheet dispersions38,39. The method is divided into four main steps: (i) Low temperature co-mixing stage. Firstly, 1 g of NaNO3 powder was fully dissolved in 46 mL of H2SO4 solution. Subsequently, 2 g of graphite powder was added to the above solution under low temperature conditions. This process was stirred sufficiently to enable the graphite powder to be intercalated by H2SO4. (ii) Medium temperature reaction phase. The graphene layers were oxidised by slowly adding 1 g of KMnO4 to the above solution every 15 min under continuous stirring, 6 times in total. After this step, the reaction temperature was adjusted to 35 °C, and stirring of the mixed solution until the solution was in a thick paste state. During this process, the purple KMnO4 dissolved in concentrated H2SO4 changes to the high manganic anhydride Mn2O7, causing the above solution to gradually change to a dark green colour. (iii) High temperature reaction and termination stage. To remove the H2SO4 between the GO layers, 92 mL of deionised water was added continuously and slowly under vigorous stirring. This process changes the above thick paste back into a homogeneous brown solution. The brown solution is then transferred to an oil bath at 98 °C and stirred continuously for 15 min. Immediately afterward, 300 mL of a 3% H2O2 solution is added to oxidise the unreacted manganese ions, stirred continuously for 3 h, and then left overnight. During this process, the solution rapidly changes from brown to bright yellow. (iv) Centrifugal washing stage. The supernatant of the above solution was poured off and the remaining yellow precipitate was taken. The yellow precipitate was washed by centrifugation several times using 3% HCl. Finally, the precipitate was washed by centrifugation at high speed again with deionised water until the solution was pH neutral. At this point, the GO dispersion was successfully prepared. The concentration of the GO dispersion we obtained was approximately 16 mg/mL.

Finally, the GO dispersions were stacked into self-supporting laminate membranes by vacuum-assisted filtration. Among them, the filtration of GO dispersions uses a 0.45 μm hydrophilic filter membrane with large pores.

Preparation of Cu2+-saturated alkaline solution

The Cu2+-saturated alkaline solution was made by immersing excess copper wire in NaOH solution (20 wt%)40, for example, 5 g of copper wire in 500 mL of NaOH solution. The solution was magnetically stirred until the NaOH solution turned blue after two to three days. Before use, this stock solution was stored with excess immersed copper wire.

Preparation of BC-Cu laminated membrane

We fabricated BC-Cu laminated membrane through the following two steps. (i) The purchased commercial BC was homogeneously mixed with deionized water in a 1:2 ratio and the BC nanosheets dispersions were stacked into a self-supporting laminated membrane by vacuum-assisted filtration technology (using a 0.45 μm filter membranes-aquo system). (ii) BC membranes were immersed in Cu2+-saturated alkaline solution (20% NaOH) for a week until the BC membranes gradually turned blue (reaching saturated Cu2+ coordination with the BC), with the product referred to as BC-Cu. Among them, the Cu concentration in the NaOH-Cu solution was 317.650 mg/L and the corresponding Cu loading in the BC-Cu membrane was approximately 1.42 wt% according to inductively coupled plasma (ICP).

Assembly of all-solid-state proton diode

All-solid-state proton diodes were made with titanium electrodes, GO membrane, and BC-Cu membrane, which are assembled by sandwiching GO and BC-Cu membranes between two titanium electrodes. The GO and BC-Cu laminated membranes were cut to 1×1 cm, while the titanium electrodes are cut to 1×2 cm. The extra length facilitates subsequent testing of the device.

Preparation of diode-array-based insole

A polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrate was cut to align with the size and shape of a customized insole. An inkjet printing device was then employed to print electrodes at predetermined positions on the PET substrate, with the electrode dimensions matching those of the diodes. Following this, two PET electrodes and a diode were assembled in a sandwich configuration and embedded within a standard insole, thereby producing the diode-array-based insole.

Preparation of flexible sensing arrays

A circuit printer (BroadTeko DP800) was used to print 6×6 electrode circuits arrays on a flexible PET transparent substrate. Among them, the electrode material adopts nano-conductive ink. The electrode area printed is 1×1 cm. The flexible sensing array was then constructed by sandwiching 36 GO and BC-Cu membranes between two flexible electrode arrays.

Characterization

The atomic force microscopy (AFM SPM 9700, Shimadzu) was used to analyze and compare the size of nanomaterials. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM FEI Nova Nano-SEM 450, 10 kV) was used to observe the surface and cross-section of laminated membranes. X-ray diffraction (XRD Panalytical) with Cu Kα radiation (15 mA and 40 kV) was used to analyze the structures of GO, BC, and BC-Cu. Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR VERTEX 70, Bruker) was mainly used to analyze the types of oxygen-containing functional groups in two types of membranes. In order to measure the water content of the membranes, we conducted thermogravimetric analysis (TGA STA2500, Germany-NETZSCH) on GO, BC, and BC Cu, respectively.

Electrical and electromechanical measurements

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and proton current rectification analysis were investigated by an electrochemical workstation (Chen Hua CHI660E). The current (Isc) and voltage (Voc) signals were measured by Keithley DMM7510. The pressured-released tests were conducted with the uniaxial motion controller (Force-measuring system, CLXT) at room temperature.

DFT calculations

The initial model is constructed through DFT calculations based on the BC-Cu layer spacing obtained by XRD analysis. Firstly, MD simulations are used to describe the protons migration process in materials with different dimensions. We divide the migration of protons into three components, such as X, Y, and Z. Secondly, MSD simulations are used to illustrate the migration process of protons on these three components. All calculations are based on DFT and use the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) function41. The convergence criterion for the ideal optimised structure is set to Fine, where the k-point is set to 3×3×1 and the energy cut off is set to 500 eV. In the structure of GO and BC, the pure GO structure without free water (including -O- bonds) and the cellulose structure containing 8 water molecules in a single cell were designed. In order to simulate the interface between BC and GO, and to ensure that they do not affect each other, the spacing between GO and BC is 15 Å.
