Presumptive pulmonary tuberculosis cases loss to follow up before diagnosis and its associated factors in tertiary care institute
Authors: Anubhuti Joshi, Mahendra Singh, Yogesh Bahurupi, Ruchi Dua, Amber Prasad, Pradeep Aggarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This study aimed to estimate the proportion of presumptive pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) cases lost to follow-up (LTFU) before diagnosis at a tertiary care facility and explore associated factors.Methods:A mixed-method study (sequential explanatory, QUAN-qual) was conducted at the pulmonary Outpatient Department of a tertiary care institute from November 2022 to June 2023. Quantitative data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire and analyzed with IBM SPSS trial version 25. Qualitative data were obtained through in-depth interviews using the Health Care Access Barriers model.Results:Out of 160 cases, 63.7% were males, and 84.4% underwent sputum testing. The qualitative findings highlighted structural, financial, and cognitive constraints causing diagnostic delays. A total of 15.6% of pulmonary Tuberculosis (TB) cases were LTFU before diagnosis.Conclusion:This study revealed significant pretreatment LTFU in pulmonary TB cases, influenced by factors such as healthcare access, socioeconomic status, literacy, and societal norms.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to be one of the top thirteen global public health concerns and remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.[1] According to the World Health Organization Global TB Report 2019, an estimated 10 million people developed TB in 2018, of whom 89% were adults, 57% were male, and 8.6% were human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive.[2] TB ranks among the ten leading causes of death globally and remains the foremost cause of death from a single infectious agent, surpassing HIV/Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS). The disease perpetuates a cycle of illness and poverty that affects families and communities, particularly among vulnerable groups such as women, children, and people living with HIV/AIDS.[3] Increasing drug resistance has further intensified the challenges of TB prevention and treatment worldwide.[4]

The End TB Strategy and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) have set ambitious targets of achieving an 80% reduction in TB incidence and a 90% reduction in TB mortality by 2030, compared to 2015 levels.[56] However, progress toward these goals remains inconsistent. Globally, only 6.3 million of the 10.4 million estimated new TB cases were diagnosed and notified in 2016, leaving nearly 4.1 million individuals undetected by national health systems.[7] These “missing” patients account for almost 40% of the global TB burden. Unless identified and linked to appropriate care, the targets set by the End TB Strategy and SDGs are unlikely to be met.[89]

A major bottleneck in TB control is pretreatment loss to follow-up (PTLFU), which refers to patients lost between diagnosis and treatment initiation.[10] PTLFU disrupts the care cascade, reducing the effectiveness of the National TB Elimination Program (NTEP). Studies have highlighted that factors such as limited healthcare access, socioeconomic hardship, low literacy levels, and prevailing social attitudes contribute significantly to loss to follow-up (LTFU) and diagnostic delays.[11121314] These barriers not only prolong infectivity but also worsen individual outcomes and increase community transmission.

Early diagnosis and prompt initiation of treatment are central to TB elimination efforts. PTLFU results in diagnostic delays, which can lead to more severe disease, complications, and increased mortality, while also imposing financial strain on affected households.[15] In India, the extent of this challenge varies regionally. The India TB Report 2023 documented a 2.6% rate of LTFU among newly notified TB cases in Uttarakhand’s public sector during 2021.[16] Similarly, a study from Pakistan reported that 12.9% of presumptive TB cases failed to complete diagnostic testing even after submitting a sputum sample.[17]

Despite these findings, evidence from tertiary healthcare institutions in India on the magnitude and determinants of PTLFU remains limited. Understanding the causes and patterns of lost to follow-up (LTFU) is crucial for improving diagnostic efficiency and ensuring programmatic success under the NTEP. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the proportion of presumptive pulmonary TB cases LTFU before diagnosis at a tertiary care institute and to identify the factors contributing to such attrition.

Materials and Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Pulmonary Outpatient Department (OPD) of a tertiary care institute in Uttarakhand, India. This department serves as a referral center for respiratory illnesses and caters to both local and referred patients from surrounding districts and neighboring states.

Study duration

The study was carried out over an 8-month period, from November 2022 to June 2023.

Study design

A mixed-method study with a sequential explanatory design (QUAN → qual) was adopted. The quantitative component assessed the proportion of presumptive pulmonary TB (PTB) cases LTFU before diagnosis, while the qualitative component explored underlying reasons through in-depth interviews (IDIs).

Sample size and sampling

For the quantitative component, universal sampling was employed. All 160 presumptive TB cases who visited the Pulmonary OPD during the study period and were advised to undergo sputum testing were included.

For the qualitative component, participants were selected purposively from those identified as LTFU before diagnosis, ensuring representation of different age groups, gender, and socio-economic backgrounds.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected using a pretested semi-structured questionnaire that included information on socio-demographic profile, health-seeking behavior, and diagnostic outcomes. The proportion of LTFU cases was estimated based on whether presumptive patients underwent sputum examination within 30 days of advice.

For the qualitative part, IDIs were conducted with purposively selected participants to explore barriers to diagnosis. The Health Care Access Barriers (HCAB) model was used to guide both the design and thematic analysis of interview data.[18] Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English for analysis.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize findings, and the Chi-square test was applied to determine associations between categorical variables.

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic framework analysis guided by the HCAB model, which classifies barriers into financial, structural, and cognitive domains.[18] Triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings was used to enhance validity.

Operational definitions

Presumptive Pulmonary TB Case: A person with any of the following symptoms or signs suggestive of TB—cough >2 weeks, fever >2 weeks, significant weight loss, hemoptysis, or any abnormality in chest radiography. In addition, high-risk individuals (contacts of microbiologically confirmed TB patients, people living with HIV, diabetics, malnourished individuals, cancer patients, and those on immunosuppressive or steroid therapy) were regularly screened for TB symptoms, as per NTEP guidelines.[19]LTFU Before Diagnosis: Defined as a presumptive pulmonary TB case not undergoing sputum testing within 30 days from the date of advice.[20]

Ethical issues

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the institute. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study’s objectives, confidentiality assurances, and voluntary nature of participation.

Guidelines for reporting

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for the quantitative component and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for the qualitative component to ensure transparency and methodological rigor.

Qualitative

We audio-recorded the IDIs, and the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim the following day, and the transcripts were coded for analysis. Recruitment continued until data saturation was reached, which was determined by the recurrence of similar responses and the absence of new emerging themes.

A thematic analysis using a framework approach was conducted. After familiarizing themselves with the transcripts, the authors performed open coding independently, categorizing and identifying the emerging themes. We discussed preliminary themes, patterns, and similarities after conducting the transcript analysis two or three times. We identified a set of codes for the initial analytic framework and used the HCAB model, which was first presented by Carrillo et al.[18] It takes a personal perspective on healthcare access. Three sorts of HCABs are distinguished by the model: financial, structural, and cognitive. It is believed that the three categories are interconnected. They may result in delayed presentation, less prevention, and less care, all of which may contribute to gaps in health outcomes.[18] The framework was later used to structure subsequent analyses.

Throughout the process, discussions were conducted regarding the differences in coding and delineating themes. The coding of the data was performed using MAXQDA software (MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2020, VERBI GmbH, Berlin, Germany). The COREQ guidelines were followed for reporting the study.

Financial barriers

Lack of Financial Support: A lot of individuals reported this as their main reason for not getting their sputum tested. Many people found the test’s cost and related costs, such as transportation, to be prohibitive, especially those from economically disadvantaged backgrounds.

“He was a poor man, a laborer, how would he get so much money to stay for 2–3 days. It could not have been so expensive.” (Male, 65 years)

Work’s Influence on Health Seeking: Participants indicated that they were too busy at work to take time off for doctor’s appointments. For people on a daily income and those without paid time off, who put their ability to make ends meet above their health, this posed a serious obstacle.

“There is household work too, so we are not able to look forward to it.” (Female, 28 years).

Structural barriers

Access to healthcare services

One major obstacle was finding it difficult to get healthcare services. Long travel times to the medical institution and a lack of consistent transportation were cited by participants from rural areas as the main challenges.

“I took so much time to get that X-ray done in the morning, I kept running for 2 days, but the X-ray report was not given, so I left it.” (Male, 22 years)

Issues with the system and care quality

Participants voiced their displeasure with the system’s shortcomings, including ineffective coordination and long wait periods for medical attention. There were also documented problems, such as sample misplacement and diagnostic difficulties, which hindered people from completing the sputum testing.

“We had also got the fluid test done, but our sample went missing.”(Male, 30 years)

“Someone has left his work in a hurry. His work also suffers loss; he has to stand in line on an empty stomach, and even then, his work may not be done.” (Male, 22 years)

Healthcare facility hours

Participants’ schedules frequently did not coincide with healthcare facilities’ operation hours. It was challenging for people who worked during typical business hours to get services due to limited evening and weekend hours.

“Madam, the next day was Sunday. Half day is held on Saturday he said, we had also gone for CT-scan but investigation could not be done.” (Male 35 years)

Cognitive Barriers

Lack of urgency

A large number of participants, particularly those who did not have severe symptoms, did not believe that sputum testing was urgently necessary. Treatment and diagnosis requests were frequently postponed as a result of this perceived lack of urgency.

“I was not coughing that much, I had a dry cough.” (Male, 22 years)

Influence of other healthcare professionals

According to the participants, they were influenced by other physicians who downplayed the significance of getting a sputum sample right away. This contradictory guidance added to the uncertainty and postponements in carrying out the suggested tests.

“There is a doctor who also treats my brother, I saw him and took medicine from him.” (Male, 30 years)

Frustration with the healthcare system

Participants expressed a general frustration with the healthcare system. Their disinclination to participate in the healthcare process was influenced by experiences with poor coordination, unfair treatment, and a dearth of patient-centered care.

“Ma’am, that doctor sir also wrote on my paper that it is necessary to see x-ray film immediately, after that he also said that we will meet tomorrow morning. Then I went in the morning and also got an X-ray for 2-3 hours.” (Male, 34 years)

Discussion

According to this study, 15.6% of cases of presumed PTB were LTFU prior to receiving a diagnosis at a tertiary care center of AIIMS Rishikesh. This percentage is greater than what Gopi et al., Buu et al., and Mohan et al. stated.[212223] Similarly, significant pre-diagnostic loss has been reported in research from Uttarakhand[24] and Bihar,[25] indicating ongoing systemic issues with patient monitoring and diagnostic completion.

Longer wait times, complicated diagnostic processes, and inadequate patient navigation systems may all contribute to the increased LTFU rate in tertiary facilities. Patients frequently encounter structural obstacles that can lower their motivation to finish diagnostic testing, such as crowded facilities, a lack of individualized attention, and poor communication with medical personnel.

In line with results from Pakistan, men were LTFU more frequently than women.[17] Gender disparities in health-seeking behavior may be reflected in this; men in lower-paying jobs may prioritize earning above healthcare and frequently experience wage loss when attending appointments. Furthermore, males may be discouraged by cultural norms from seeking repeated consultations for symptoms they consider to be mild. On the other hand, research by Balasubramanian et al. and Pala et al.[2026] did not find any gender imbalance, which may be because of variations in programming follow-up procedures and local accessibility.

The majority of LTFU cases in this study were older individuals (51–70 years), which is consistent with findings by Muhammad and Ethirajan and Ulaganeethi et al.[2728] Access to healthcare may be hampered for this group by age-related obstacles such as comorbidities, decreased mobility, or reliance on carers. Elderly patients with subtle or unusual TB symptoms further postpone diagnosis and reduce the perceived urgency of getting tested. On the other hand, Pala et al.[20] found that younger people had higher LTFU, which could be related to occupational mobility or ignorance.

Another important contextual component was living in a rural region; 76% of LTFU patients in this study were from rural areas, which is in line with results from Zimbabwe.[29] Missed follow-ups are caused by a lack of transportation, the distance to the diagnostic institution, and irregular health communication. These results highlight how patient retention in the TB care cascade is influenced by geographic disparities and health system accessibility.

Pre-diagnostic attrition is impacted by both patient-level factors and health system limitations, according to earlier Indian and international research, which is supported by the observed trends. Weak referral connections and insufficient patient counselling have been identified as factors contributing to diagnosis delay in studies from Bihar and Tamil Nadu.[2326] Similar findings among vulnerable tribal populations by Mishra et al.[11] highlight how diagnostic compliance is hampered by socioeconomic hardship, literacy gaps, and mistrust of formal healthcare.

Follow-up may be unintentionally discouraged in tertiary facilities due to the patient load and procedure complexity. Early attrition could be reduced by improving referral coordination and patient education at the initial point of contact.

Limitations

There were some limitations to the study. Inaccurate or out-of-date phone numbers in hospital records made it difficult to follow up with participants, which reduced the tracking’s thoroughness. Furthermore, generalizability might be restricted because the study was limited to a single tertiary care facility. Nonetheless, a thorough grasp of both quantitative trends and contextual factors was made possible by the mixed-method approach.

Conclusion

15.6% of presumed cases of PTB were LTFU prior to diagnosis at a tertiary care hospital, according to this mixed-method study. The qualitative results showed that these delays in diagnosis were caused by structural, financial, and cognitive impediments, including travel restrictions, indirect expenses, lengthy wait periods, and low patient awareness.

Targeted, patient-centered interventions are crucial to reducing pre-diagnostic LTFU. Testing compliance can be increased by strengthening patient counselling at the initial point of contact, putting in place tracking and reminder systems (such as SMS alerts or ASHA-based follow-ups), and guaranteeing transportation support or decentralized sputum collection.

Programmatically speaking, incorporating these treatments within the NTEP would improve the effectiveness of case detection, lower transmission, and hasten the achievement of the End TB Strategy goals.
