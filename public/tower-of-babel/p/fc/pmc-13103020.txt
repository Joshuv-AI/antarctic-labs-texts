AI driven interactive extraction of tomographic ultrasound imaging sequences for anal sphincter defects
Authors: Helena Williams, Bram Packet, Laura Cattani, Susanne Housmans, Adela Samesova, Lucie Hympanova, Florian Ramakers, Hans Peter Dietz, Jan D’hooge, Tom Vercauteren, Jan Deprest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnal incontinence is common after childbirth, often due to structural anal sphincter injury, affecting up to 42% of postpartum women, with 6% experiencing stool loss. Exo-anal ultrasound aids in detecting anal sphincter injury through tomographic ultrasound imaging (TUI), but manual TUI sequence extraction is expert-dependent and time-intensive. In this international multi-centre study, we developed and independently evaluated the performance of an interactive AI-assisted pipeline for automated TUI positioning and alignment with real-time adjustability, from exo-anal ultrasound images acquired in postpartum, pelvic floor clinic and antenatal clinical settings from a single ultrasound vendor ecosystem. The pipeline was developed using 245 cases (117 in-house, 128 external) and independently validated on 125 cases from pelvic floor dysfunction and postpartum clinics across multiple geographical locations, including UZ Leuven, an industrial dataset, and The Institute for the Care of Mother and Child, Prague. The anal canal region was localised using nnUnet. The pipeline achieved angular rotation accuracy within the range of expert manual extraction, reduced workflow time of exo-anal image analysis by threefold, and maintained visual clinical acceptability, enhancing standardisation for clinical users. Its interactive elements align with Food and Drug Administration and the European Union AI act guidelines for human oversight by fostering clinical trust in AI adoption in pelvic floor imaging and mitigating clinically relevant risks, such as false external anal sphincter defect detection due to cranial misplacement. Introduction

Anal incontinence defined as the involuntary loss of flatus or stool is one of the most bothersome forms of pelvic floor dysfunction because of its severe impact on the quality of life1. A primary cause is structural trauma to the anal sphincter complex, mostly sustained during childbirth. Up to 42% of women report symptoms of anal incontinence one year after childbirth, and 6% experiencing involuntary loss of stool at 6–8 weeks postpartum2. Anal incontinence and other birth-related conditions may present months, or even years, after childbirth, long after postpartum care has ended. Consequently, untreated conditions can lead to lifelong social, economic and health consequences3.

Even in countries aiming to meet global health targets, such as the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals, healthcare systems often lack resources or training to address such chronic conditions effectively, especially when presented after postpartum care has ended3. A lack of data further impairs the awareness of anal incontinence to key stakeholders, limiting visibility, funding and coordinated efforts to address the problem, particularly in low- and middle-income countries3.

Clinical examination of the perineum at delivery has poor diagnostic accuracy. In contrast, ultrasound studies reveal that 26% of women sustain structural anal sphincter damage following their first vaginal delivery4. As a result, experts propose offering ultrasound screening postpartum for all high-risk women, both for secondary prevention of obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) and to optimise diagnosis and treatment5, however, this is not routinely implemented.

Endo-anal ultrasound is considered the gold standard for structural evaluation of the anal sphincter complex6. However, its invasive nature and specialised hardware requirements have spurred the development of alternative protocols using exo-anal imaging techniques such as transperineal (TPUS) or introital ultrasound (IUS)7. These techniques use widely available ultrasound probes8, avoid anal sphincter distortion, and have demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy9 and inter-rater reliability10. International guidelines by the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine (AIUM) and the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) now recommend tomographic ultrasound imaging (TUI) for internal and external anal sphincter muscle assessment from a representative exo-anal ultrasound volume using a standardised protocol outlined by Dietz et al.11.

TUI sequences consist of eight equally spaced, aligned coronal slices spanning the anal sphincter from the cranial boundary of the external anal sphincter to the caudal boundary of the internal anal sphincter. This allows the clinician to assess muscle integrity by quantifying defects and measuring the angle of interruptions in the hyperechogenic appearance of the external anal sphincter when a defect is present11. However, manual TUI extraction is labour-intensive, requires a significant level of expertise12, with noticeably worse repeatability in novice sonographers13. These limitations may impact the assessment and detection of significant external anal sphincter injury in unspecialised settings.

To mitigate these challenges, we previously developed an automatic deep-learning-based TUI sequence extraction tool14. However, it suffered from alignment issues, where the anal canal was not properly oriented horizontally or vertically, resulting in oblique TUI slices that may distort the appearance of the external and internal anal sphincter muscles, impacting the appearance of a defect, and deviating from the protocol11. In addition, the lack of interactive features may restrict its clinical utility as the automatic cranial location of the TUI sequence was deemed clinically unacceptable in 30% of cases, which may may hinder the broader clinical adoption of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted TUI analysis tools.

Effective integration of AI in the clinical environment requires tools that are accurate, robust and user-friendly. Regulatory guidance from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Union Artificial AI act (EU-AI act), emphasises the importance of human oversight and correction in high-risk medical AI applications, particularly in medical imaging applications where clinically consequential errors may occur15. Human-in-the-loop designs are therefore critical to ensure safety, build trust, and support adoption15 for general ultrasound-proficient clinicians, including in postpartum assessment.

To address these challenges and to build clinical trust, we propose and evaluate an AI-driven, multi-step interactive pipeline to optimise the workflow and image analysis steps of TUI sequence extraction from exo-anal TPUS and IUS volumes. The pipeline focuses on workflow optimisation and standardisation and does not perform automated diagnosis.

The interactive AI pipeline (Fig. 1) adheres to international clinical guidelines described by Dietz et al.11 and integrates a robust nnU-Net16 segmentation model with post-processing steps. The segmentation boundary (referred to as the TUI-defined anal canal region) mimics the orientation, and the segmentation length mimics the placement of the TUI sequence. Weighted principal component analysis (wPCA) is used to align the sagittal and axial planes, with sagittal rotation remaining independent of probe fan shape and acquisition angle, enabling consistent performance across exo-anal ultrasound probes and acquisition setups. To account for variability in automated outputs on new unseen data, the pipeline incorporates clinician-guided interactive adjustments for TUI slice positioning and sagittal rotation.

Interactive pipeline outlined—a trained 3D nnUnet accepts a 3D TPUS or IUS volume and automatically segments the section of the anal canal between the cranial end of the external anal sphincter and caudal end of the internal anal sphincter (i.e., TUI-defined anal canal region). The central coordinate of the segmentation is used to extract the central axial plane and weighted principal component analysis (wPCA) is used to vertically align the anal canal, the central sagittal plane is then extracted and wPCA is applied to the top and bottom boundary of the anal canal to provide the user with two options for rotation. The segmentation boundary guides the user to the optimal TUI slice position, but this can be adapted in real-time using the interactive window provided. Finally, the TUI sequence is extracted.

This study describes the development and evaluation of an AI-driven interactive pipeline for TUI sequence extraction. The study aims are threefold: (1) to enhance clinical usability and adoption by integrating interactive elements that allow the clinician to adjust critical parameters, such as TUI slice positioning and mid-sagittal rotation; (2) to enhance accuracy and speed with an optimised rotation framework that more closely mimics the visual landmarks used for sagittal and axial alignment; (3) to ensure robustness and generalisability through comprehensive validation on a large, multi-centric clinical dataset from several locations and demographics (i.e., antenatal, postpartum and pelvic floor clinics). Importantly, the pipeline is independent of exo-anal probe type (i.e., endovaginal or transperineal), enabling use with both TPUS and IUS acquisitions and increasing its adoptability.

Results

Curated development dataset

The interactive AI pipeline was developed and evaluated using multiple datasets comprising of IUS and TPUS ultrasound volumes from diverse geographical and clinical settings (i.e., both in-house and external datasets; see Fig. 2). Data curation ensured representation of clinical reality17, with inclusion criteria focused on image quality suitable for visual assessment, and completeness of the external anal sphincter. Development dataset descriptions, based on the data used for convolutional neural network (CNN) model training and final model selection, are given within the Methods, “Study data set” section. The development dataset was annotated by an experienced clinician (LC) with over six years’ experience in 3D manual segmentation of pelvic floor structures using VOCAL, 4D View™ software (GE Healthcare, version 18Ext4 https://www.gehealthcare.com/products/ultrasound/voluson/4dview). The segmentation protocol is outlined in Methods (“Manual 3D segmentation of the TUI-defined anal canal region protocol”) and on average, manual segmentation required approximately four minutes per ultrasound volume, resulting in a total of at least 17 h of segmentation work for 256 volumes in the development dataset.

TUI interactive pipeline validation

The AI-driven pipeline was evaluated using 125 ultrasound volumes from 125 patients from multiple data sources, visualised in Fig. 2. The TUI extraction algorithm was used by a clinical expert (BP) with > 5 years of experience in TPUS anal sphincter imaging. BP selected the sagittal plane orientation between two options and confirmed or adjusted TUI slice positioning to extract TUI sequences from all ultrasound volumes. The clinical gold standard (GS) TUI sequences were identified by the same expert following the standard clinical protocol11 outlined in the previous section titled “Manual TUI extraction and analysis protocol”.

To evaluate the visual acceptability, a visual Turing test evaluated a sample of 40 TPUS volumes (i.e., 10 randomly selected from each testing dataset)18. A clinician (AS with > 3 years’ experience in TPUS imaging) blindly rated the GS TUI sequences identified using the manual approach and the TUI sequences extracted with our proposed interactive tool. The rating was based on a Likert scale (5 = excellent, 4 = above average, 3 = average, 2 = below average, 1 = poor, 0 = no clinical use) and a paired Wilcoxon test comparing the two methods to give a score ranging from − 5 to + 5 for each TPUS image. Three tests were performed: (1) clinical acceptability of TUI slices for the assessment of anal sphincter integrity, (2) sagittal rotation acceptability, (3) axial rotation acceptability. Details are given in the Methods (“Qualitative metrics for evaluation”).

Figure 5 illustrates the semi-qualitative averaged results from the visual Turing tests, comparing the performance of the AI-assisted pipeline to the manual GS. The violin plots provide a detailed representation of the distribution of Wilcoxon score differences across three tests (i.e., clinical acceptability, sagittal and axial rotation acceptability).

Turing Test per TPUS volume, a negative result indicates the GS performed better than the other method in comparison. A score of 0 means that the GS performed equally to the other method evaluated. A positive score would mean the method outperformed the GS. The scores range from − 5 to + 5 (dependent on the GS score and evaluated method score). The mean is shown as a red x and median as a white dot.

The results demonstrate that the pipeline produces visually comparable outputs to the GS. For clinical acceptability, the mean average and median score is zero, indicating no systematic visual difference between the AI-assisted pipeline and the GS. For mid-sagittal and axial rotation, the median score was 0 and the mean average score was − 0.175 for both tests, suggesting that the GS performed slightly better. However, the Wilcoxon scores for the AI-assistance pipeline fell within the range of expert-level inter-observer variability, and no statistically significant difference was observed compared with the GS (p = 0.237, p = 0.225) for sagittal and axial rotation respectively. Importantly, none of the volumes received a score lower than 3, confirming that all generated TUIs were clinical acceptable and considered at least ‘average’ in terms of visual quality. The small variability for all tests indicates that the AI-assisted pipeline consistently performed visually comparable to the GS. Across all tests and TPUS volumes, AI assistance did not negatively or positively impact the visual quality by more than 2 Likert points.

To verify the acceptability of the measured COD, the expert-level inter-observer differences (IEOD) were measured in our in-house UZ Leuven testing dataset. Automated sagittal rotation achieved a lower (albeit no significant difference was observed p = 0.143) angular difference than the IEOD for postpartum cases (Table 1), despite being a population demographic (i.e., women between 6 weeks to 9 months postpartum) not included within the training dataset. Workflow efficiency

The efficiency of the interactive pipeline was assessed by comparing the time required for manual and automated analyses on the in-house postpartum clinic dataset (n = 30). An experienced clinician (BP) conducted both the manual and automated analyses. The automated analysis was performed first to avoid introducing a positive bias towards the automated method’s efficiency. To ensure manual assessment accurately reflects the evaluation of a new patient volume, it was conducted at least one month after the automated analysis to minimise the risk of memory bias. The automated interactive pipeline proposed took 14.9 ± 3.30 s on average and the manual process took 45.4 ± 12.3 s per volume. This represents a three-fold improvement in workflow efficiency. The workflow proposed can also be ran in a fully ‘automatic’ mode, meaning that rather than offering the user the choice of two sagittal rotation planes, a default is set. This is either based on the upper or the lower boundary of the anal canal for all ultrasound volumes. In addition, the slicing is automatic and based on the extreme cranial and caudal border of the TUI-defined anal canal region segmentation within the mid-sagittal plane. When in the automatic mode, the time for TUI sequence extraction took 1.71 ± 0.06 s per volume.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a clinically applicable, interactive AI-assisted pipeline for optimising the extraction of TUI sequences of the anal sphincter from both the TPUS and IUS exo-anal ultrasound volumes. Current clinical TUI extraction relies on fully manual, time-intensive and operator-dependent workflows, while automation efforts14 have focused on fully automatic extraction approaches that can be vulnerable to clinically relevant failure modes, particularly localisation of the cranial boundary of the external anal sphincter. In contrast, the proposed pipeline introduces a human-in-the-loop design that combines automated standardisation of alignment and slice positioning with targeted clinician interaction to correct known, clinically consequential errors.

Compared to our previous fully automatic TUI extraction method14, this pipeline incorporates a TUI-defined anal canal region segmentation model (defined by the cranial boundary of the external anal sphincter and the caudal boundary of the internal anal sphincter), that closely aligns with visual indicators used by clinicians and the standardised protocol11. Interactive correction of cranial external anal sphincter muscle localisation and sagittal rotation is supported. Additionally, the weighted PCA-based rotation framework enables consistent analysis, independent to the acquisition angle, enhancing robustness against acquisition settings. Extensive validation across diverse clinical datasets acquired on a single vendor system shows that the automated TUI workflow optimisation performs within the range of expert manual extraction while achieving significantly faster analysis times, with a three-fold workflow efficiency improvement. The AI-assisted pipeline was evaluated across a variety of test cohorts representative of postpartum and pelvic floor clinic populations from several geographical locations.

Qualitative visual assessment showed that both manual and automated TUI sequence support the same clinical interpretation of anal sphincter integrity, with minimal visual differences (Fig. 3). Visual Turing tests (Fig. 5) indicated that the AI-assisted pipeline produces clinically acceptable TUI sequences with sagittal and axial alignment comparable to manual extraction.

All TUI sequences in this study were generated by a clinician with at least five years of experience. However, we assume that for novices, the task of selecting a 2D frame with horizontal structural alignment (out of two options) is cognitively simpler than manipulating a 3D ultrasound volume, and this hypothesis motivates evaluation in dedicated novice studies.

Variability observed in the visual Turing tests indicates that, in some cases, the AI pipeline either outperformed or underperformed the manual approach by an entire Likert scale point. Importantly, no generated or manual TUI was rated below ‘average’ (Likert scale 3) in visual quality, reinforcing the robustness and clinical potential of the pipeline. By adhering to a standard clinical protocol, the pipeline establishes a framework that may support broader standardisation of anal sphincter imaging workflows.

The accuracy and robustness of the pipeline were confirmed across antenatal, postpartum and pelvic floor clinic cohorts across in-house (i.e., UZ Leuven) and external datasets from different geographical locations acquired on GE Healthcare ultrasound systems. Comparable angular differences were observed across datasets (Tables 1 and 2), indicating consistent performance across the external and in-house datasets, patient demographics, geographic regions and clinical settings. In addition, performance on dataset containing IUS images did not under-perform compared to TPUS-only datasets, suggesting reduced dependence on specific imaging hardware within post-processing, the pipeline enhances its potential for wider adoption.

Within the in-house TPUS dataset, the automated algorithm achieved lower midsagittal angular differences than measured inter-observer variability in the postpartum dataset (Table 1). This highlights its robustness even in patient populations that were not represented during model training. Higher inter-observer and computer-observer angular differences were identified in the pelvic floor clinic dataset, this may be due to the lack of data cleaning based on image/acquisition quality. Visual examples from the four datasets (Fig. 4) demonstrate the reduced image quality in the in-house pelvic floor dysfunction clinic. This may be due to symptomatic patients having a higher prevalence of advanced structural pathology and history of pelvic floor surgery. Within the in-house pelvic floor dysfunction cohort, 31% of ultrasounds were from post-operative patients, which may impact visual clarity of the anal sphincter, thus making it increasingly difficult for both manual and automated analysis.

Ultrasound image analysis confirmed that 76.7% (23/30), 48.6% (17/35), 83.3% (25/30) and 93.3 (28/30) of women have at least one abnormal external anal sphincter TUI sequence (between slice 2 to 7) for the in-house postpartum, in-house pelvic floor clinic, external Czech and external GE dataset respectively. In addition, 3.3% (1/30), 2.9% (1/35), 20.0% (6/30) and 20.0% (6/30) of women are visually diagnosed from the ultrasound volume as having a residual external anal sphincter defect (i.e., at least 4 out of the 6 central TUI slices have a defect of at least 30 degrees) for the in-house postpartum, in-house pelvic clinic, external Czech and external GE dataset respectively. This demonstrates the robustness in our proposed method, as performance did not degrade in datasets with higher proportions of severe pathology. The pipeline demonstrated performance within the range of experienced clinicians, including in complex cases, suggesting that the segmentation model can effectively generalise beyond its training data, reinforcing its potential with future development for application in routine clinical practice and potentially reducing the barrier to entry for anal sphincter ultrasound imaging.

The inclusion of interactive elements was a deliberate choice to enhance clinical acceptability and flexibility. Two midsagittal rotation options are provided based on the upper or lower boundary of the anal canal are given, to mitigate for sub-optimal alignment, which may occur due to imaging artifacts. Secondly, TUI slice positioning was made fully interactive, as our previous study14 indicated that in 30% of TPUS volumes a lower visual clinical acceptability was identified due to incorrect location of the first TUI slice (i.e., the cranial end of the external anal sphincter). In addition, we assume interaction may be required in cases with severe pathology, or poor image quality.

A recent review20 echoes the importance for human oversight and interaction when deploying AI for medical diagnosis to mitigate clinically relevant failure modes. In exo-anal imaging, a key risk is the cranial misplacement of TUI slices, which may falsely suggest external anal sphincter defects. The proposed pipeline mitigates this risk through clinician-in-the-loop correction, including mid-sagittal alignment selection and real-time interactive slice repositioning. This approach aligns with the U.S. FDA’s Good Machine Learning Practice for Medical Device Development Guiding Principles21,22 and the European Union AI Act requirements for human oversight in high-risk medical AI systems23. In this case ensuring the extraction of clinically acceptable TUI slices for sufficient image-based diagnosis of anal sphincter defects, and this regulatory framing is supported by the observed cranial-caudal slice positioning discrepancies reported in this study.

The segmentation model demonstrated significantly lower slice-positioning error at the caudal boundary than the cranial boundary across all datasets (Table 3). This discrepancy may be attributed to the greater visual difficulty in defining the cranial end of the external anal sphincter muscle13, leading to increased variability of manual selection of the cranial limit (Table 4) and perhaps within the TUI-defined anal canal region segmentation dataset used for model training. On average the cranial limit had a discrepancy between 2.23 ± 5.23 and 6.63 ± 3.90 towards the cranial direction, which could lead to false positives of defects in the first TUI slices. This is because the external anal sphincter muscle appears shorter in length anteriorly than posteriorly within the sagittal view of the anal sphincter complex24. Therefore, when sliced within the coronal plane towards the cranial limit the muscle seems incomplete, which may be falsely mistaken as a defect, especially to a novice (Supplementary Fig. 5). The cranial distance measured (Table 3) highlights the need for an interactive user-interface, allowing real-time correction, of clinically consequential cranial over-slicing. While improved automated localisation of the cranial external anal sphincter muscle boundary remains an important target for future work, strategies that reduce reliance on precise localisation may offer more practical solutions.

For novices, who are more likely to rely on automatic slicing, a fixed slicing strategy was evaluated to mitigate cranial over-estimation. This approach has been suggested to be equally valid for the diagnosis of ‘residual or significant external anal sphincter defects’, compared to individualised interslice intervals19. While the adjustment did not reduce the absolute or actual error in slice positioning, it may reduce the risk of false positive diagnoses of anal sphincter defects for novice users, as it shifted the cranial limit significantly towards the caudal direction. By providing a standardised approach, fixed slicing may support consistent diagnostic outcomes, in training settings or resource-constrained environments.

The pipeline reduces the time for TUI extraction threefold and, when used without interaction, achieves a 96.2% time reduction, enhancing workflow efficiency and addressing a bottleneck in clinical practice. This could lower barriers for adoption, particularly in resource-limited settings. The interface supports real-time TUI slice re-positioning and midsagittal adjustment, enabling potential integration into ultrasound hardware for clinical deployment, rather than requiring separate software or offline analysis.

Despite its strengths, the pipeline has several limitations and areas for development. Currently, the training and evaluation dataset is limited to one ultrasound vendor (i.e., GE Healthcare), which may limit its use and generalisability to other ultrasound systems. Nonetheless, the pipeline incorporates probe-agnostic design principles, and external validation has demonstrated robustness across varied imaging qualities, and new clinical settings within this ecosystem. Future validation across multiple vendors and broader demographic groups e.g., geographical, age, racial and ethnic characteristics) is required to formally assess vendor-agnostic performance, generalisability, inclusivity and global applicability. If the pipeline underperforms, the training dataset should be updated accordingly. Based on results from this study, incorporating more ultrasounds of patients attending pelvic floor clinics may have a greater impact, as the performance on in-house postpartum patients (despite lack of representation within the training set) was higher than the pelvic floor cohort.

A methodological consideration is that the same expert defined the manual reference TUI sequences and performed interactive extraction. This allowed for direct comparison of manual versus AI-assisted extraction by the same operator. AI-assisted extraction was performed first, with manual extraction conducted a month later to minimise recall bias. Rotation accuracy was benchmarked against inter-observer variability; future studies will include independently defined reference TUI sequences.

The pipeline assumes that a clinically adequate TPUS volume has been acquired and does not assess acquisition quality, probe positioning, or image quality, which remain operator dependent. The reduced performance observed in the prospective in-house pelvic floor dysfunction dataset for both manual and AI-assisted analysis likely reflects this constraint, as these volumes underwent no pre-selection or quality control and therefore exhibited lower image quality. This highlights the need for complementary acquisition guidance and quality tools for anal sphincter ultrasound, which could enhance the performance of both manual and automated analyses. Such tools may support improved diagnostic workflows by providing real-time, standardised feedback on image quality to determine whether the acquired ultrasound is suitable for visual defect assessment.

Our previous work25 demonstrated the potential of a TPUS quality assessment algorithm to deliver standardised feedback on key image attributes, including target anal canal localisation, reverberation, and shadowing detection. While promising, further development is required to improve the accuracy of acoustic shadow detection, enable real-time feedback, and integrate the software into ultrasound hardware.

Finally, while this study focused on the automated extraction of TUI planes, manual visual assessment of the external anal sphincter is still required to identify and measure defects when present. Although experienced observers show high diagnostic performance, novice users often perform much poorer13. Diagnostic validation was not an objective of the present work; however, future studies may assess whether TUI sequences generated by the proposed pipeline support consistent clinical interpretation, including agreement and reproducibility of significant external anal sphincter defect angle measurements.

In parallel, we are exploring automated approaches for external anal sphincter defect detection to further standardise assessments and reduce inter-observer variability, particularly for less experienced users. We are currently building such a tool, and preliminary results suggest that this feature could further reduce inter-observer variability26,27 when detecting pathology; comprehensive validation across diverse clinical datasets is planned.

Together with acquisition guidance and formal novice evaluation, these extensions would expand the scope of the present work from supporting trained clinicians to enabling broader dissemination across postpartum and pelvic floor clinics. This may improve the uptake of exo-anal imaging globally while ensuring that high-quality ultrasound-based diagnostics are more accessible, and support earlier detection of anal sphincter injury4,5.

Conclusion

We propose a tool that achieves significantly faster analysis times, enabling substantial workflow efficiencies for anal sphincter assessment on exo-anal images. In addition, the pipeline achieves performance within the range of expert manual extraction for visual clinical acceptability and angular rotation. The pipeline was validated on several external datasets and showed comparable performance to the in-house dataset, showing the generalisability of the pipeline to new patient demographics and clinical settings within the evaluated vendor ecosystem. The interactive elements allow for correct positioning of the TUI slices within the ultrasound volume; however, this indicates that the quality of the TUI positioning will be based on the clinicians’ level of experience unless automatic slicing is used. Although TUI slice placement and orientation is automated, the assessment of these TUI sequences is still manual, therefore, automatic analysis of anal sphincter integrity could further standardise anal sphincter assessment.

Study data set

This study utilises several datasets for development and/or testing which include TPUS volumes acquired in women who attended the antenatal, post-partum or pelvic floor dysfunction clinic, either at UZ Leuven or The Institute for the Care of Mother and Child, Prague. In addition, this study utilises an industrial dataset from GE Healthcare of IUS and TPUS volumes. The entire study design and data flow are detailed in Fig. 2. All ultrasound volumes were acquired on GE Healthcare ultrasound machines (including Voluson E6/E8/E10, S10 Expert, Swift, and P8, GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria). TPUS volumes were acquired using a convex curved array volumetric transabdominal 4–8 MHz probe, following the imaging protocol defined by Dietz et al.11. IUS ultrasound volumes were acquired with a 3D/4D 5–9 MHz endovaginal probe. The visual differences between TPUS and IUS volumes are shown in Fig. 6.

Inclusion criteria

Ultrasound volumes were visually checked by an expert clinician (LC) with > 7 years of clinical experience with TPUS imaging before being included for model training. Inclusion was based on a favourable assessment for both the following rudimentary quality assessment criteria:

1) Completeness: anal sphincter complex visualised entirely in the volume, regardless of its proportional size with respect to the entire sample volume.

2) Image quality: ability to visually discriminate the different constituting parts of the anal sphincter complex (internal and external sphincter, and anal canal), without further restrictions regarding image settings (gain, contrast, etc.).

A visual example of an ultrasound excluded due to acquisition quality or completeness and imaging quality are shown in Supplementary Figure’s 1 and 2 respectively. We aim to account for variation in image quality perhaps due to patient pathology28, the presence of hematoma and/or edema9 and variation in acquisition quality28.

Dataset split and segmentation protocol

Dataset size and data split for training/validation

After conducting quality checks, we identified 245 ultrasound volumes from 231 patients eligible for model training and validation. In 13 patients, multiple ultrasound volumes (n = 27) were available, and these were incorporated into the training dataset. The remaining 218 volumes were randomly divided using python’s random module into training and validation sets to ensure an overall ratio of 0.8:0.2 respectively30, resulting in a training dataset of 196 volumes (182 patients), i.e. 177 TPUS and 19 IUS volumes. Of the training dataset, 92 TPUS volumes were from in-house, while 104 volumes were obtained externally. The validation dataset consisted of 44 TPUS and 5 IUS volumes, with 25 sourced in-house and 24 from external sources, as demonstrated in the Fig. 2 model training block.

This dataset size is in accordance with our previous work14, where we achieved suitable segmentation performance of the external anal sphincter for TUI extraction (n = 118). Since this study aimed to train and evaluate the model using both in-house and external data sources, we aimed to roughly double the dataset size. This approach aims to enhance the dataset diversity and size to train a more robust segmentation model beyond in-house data as in Liu et al.31.

Manual 3D segmentation of the TUI-defined anal canal region protocol

VOCAL, 4D View software (GE Healthcare) was used to segment the anal canal in 30-degree intervals in a 180-degree range. Therefore, after six manual delineations a complete 3D segmentation was formed with VOCAL allowing for manual contour corrections in other surface planes14,32. The segmentation process was designed to mimic the orientation and placement of the TUI sequence; therefore, it follows the boundary between the anal canal and hypoechoic stripes of the internal anal sphincter and spanned from the cranial end of the external anal sphincter and the caudal end of the internal anal sphincter.

Implementation details

The CNN model for automatic TUI-defined anal canal region segmentation was implemented using MONAI37 on a desktop computer with 24-GB NVIDIA Quadro P6000 (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The network architecture used was 3D UNet from the nnUnet implementation16, with deep supervision of two auxiliary losses in the decoder. An Adam optimiser with an initial learning rate of 0.01 and weight decay factor of 3e−5 were used. The activation function was Leaky ReLU, and the batch size was set to 2. A loss function of combined cross entropy and Dice score with a learning rate decay of 0.9 were used. Hyper parameters were selected a priori based on nnU-Net defaults and prior work and were not exhaustively tuned beyond validation-based model selection. To improve data variability, during training data augmentation methods listed in supplement S4 were implemented. The model was trained for 1200 epochs and the epoch with the lowest validation cross entropy and Dice loss on a held-out validation set was used as the final model checkpoint for model testing. This occurred at 800 epochs, and the validation loss and Dice score were evaluated every 50 epochs during training.

Validation of the TUI extraction algorithm

The total testing dataset contained 125 ultrasound volumes originating from each data source, visualised in Fig. 2. The TUI extraction algorithm was used by a clinical expert (BP). BP selected the sagittal plane orientation between the two options and confirmed the TUI slice positioning to extract TUI sequences from all ultrasound volumes.

The clinical gold standard (GS) TUI sequences were also identified by a clinical expert with over 5 years of experience (BP). The GS TUI sequences were identified following the standard clinical protocol11 outlined in the previous section titled, “Manual TUI extraction and analysis protocol”. The GS TUI slices were extracted offline using GE 4DView software.

To measure the inter-operator reliability of the orientation of the ultrasound volume, another operator (SH with over 5 years’ experience in TPUS imaging) identified the TUI sequence using 4D View, following the standard protocol11 in the internal postpartum clinic dataset. The inter-operator reliability for the prospectively acquired pelvic floor dysfunction dataset compares to the TUI sequences acquired by SH within the pelvic floor dysfunction clinic following the standard protocol11.

Quantitative metrics for evaluation

To validate the accuracy of the TUI rotation, both the absolute angular difference (e.g., |−5° − (− 3°)|) and the raw angular difference (e.g., − 5° − (− 3°)) between the algorithm-guided and the GS TUI slices were measured in the axial and sagittal planes. To measure the accuracy of automatic TUI slice positioning the distance between the automatic TUI slice position and the user-adjusted slice position was measured. The cranial and caudal discrepancy were measured separately. To identify whether the discrepancy in slice positioning is acceptable, the intra-rater variability of slice positioning was measured between the user-adjusted AI-assisted TUI sequence and the manual GS TUI sequence. Finally, the impact of fixed interval slicing on slice positioning was measured by comparing the caudal and cranial boundary distance to the user adjusted TUI sequence positions.

Qualitative metrics for evaluation

To evaluate the visual acceptability, a visual Turing test evaluated a sample of 40 TPUS volumes (i.e., 10 randomly selected from each testing dataset)18. We asked a clinician (AS with three years of experience in TPUS imaging and analysis) to blindly rate a random selection of unseen TUI sequences identified using the manual approach and from our proposed interactive tool. Three tests are performed where (1) they rate the clinical acceptability of the TUI slices for assessment of anal sphincter integrity, (2) whether the anal canal is suitably aligned in the sagittal plane and finally, (3) whether the anal canal is correctly aligned in the axial plane. The rating was based on a Likert scale from 0 to 5 (5 = excellent, 4 = above average, 3 = average, 2 = below average, 1 = poor, 0 = no clinical use) and a paired Wilcoxon test was performed to compare the performance of the proposed automated pipeline against expert 1’s GS TUIs. The paired Wilcoxon test is calculated by deducting the clinical expert’s (BP) GS score from the pipeline’s score. The score ranges from − 5 to + 5 depending on the performance of the automated TUI against the GS. The generated score was averaged per TPUS volume, meaning that a positive score suggests the detected TUI method performed “better” visually than the manual GS; conversely, a negative score suggests the detected method performed “worse”; and a score of zero means they performed the same. For example, a score of − 1 could equate to the pipeline achieving a visual score of ‘average’ and the GS achieving a visual score of ‘above average’ and the largest difference could be negative or positive 5 i.e., the GS achieved a visual score of ‘excellent’ and the pipeline of ‘no clinical use’ or vice versa.

Computer-observer and inter-observer differences

The computer-observer differences (COD) were evaluated separately on the four testing datasets. The inter-observer differences (IEOD) were evaluated on the in-house datasets only. COD were evaluated by calculating the similarity metrics between the AI-guided TUI sequence and clinician BP’s GS manually extracted TUI sequence. The IEOD was evaluated by calculating the similarity metrics between clinician SH and the manual GS manual assessment from clinician BP. The intra-observer difference (IAOD) was calculated for TUI slice positioning on all datasets between expert BP’s GS TUI slice positioning and the slice positioning selected with our interactive pipeline.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the reliability of the automated method compared with the measured inter-observer study, a paired two-sided F-test was used to test the null hypothesis being that the interactive automated method agreed with expert 1’s GS at least as well as expert 2 agreed with expert 1’s GS result (i.e., the variance of the differences between the automated method and the GS was not larger than the variance in the inter-observer difference). To evaluate potential bias between methods (i.e., automated, inter-observer) to clinician BP’s GS, a paired two-sided t-test was used to test the null hypothesis based on mean difference i.e., the bias rather than the variance of the differences. To further evaluate the reliability of the automated method, Bland-Altman limits of agreement were calculated.

Regarding the position of the TUI sequence, a paired two-sided t-test was performed to test the null hypothesis being that the AI model performs with a similar error at the cranial end as it does at the caudal end. Finally, a paired two-sided t-test was conducted to test the null hypothesis being that the measured intra-observer error of TUI slice positioning performed similar to the computer-observer error measured when automatic slicing is used (both with automatic slicing dependent on the segmentation boundary and based on fixed-interval slicing).

All statistical tests for p-values were performed using Python package ‘scipy.stats’. The p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. For actual and absolute sagittal differences, the p-value was corrected by a factor of 2 (resulting in a threshold of p = 0.025). Similarly, for the actual and absolute axial differences per dataset, the same correction was applied. Regarding the caudal and cranial differences, the locations of the caudal and cranial limits were assumed to be independent, and thus, a correction factor of 2 was applied (p = 0.025).
