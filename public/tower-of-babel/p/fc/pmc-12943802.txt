Multidimensional clinical profiles of Saudi adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis: an exploratory cross-sectional study
Authors: Nour Alhafdhi, Dalia Alimam, Lolwah Alrashed Alhumaid, Samia Alamrani, Abdulaziz Bin Shebreen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a prevalent musculoskeletal condition. We explore adolescents’ profiles with AIS, and associations between quality of life (QoL) with factors such as psychological distress, joint hypermobility, body mass index, and physical activity levels.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited adolescents (aged 10–18 yrs) with AIS from King Fahd Medical City, Riyadh, with a Cobb angle ≥ 10°. Consecutive patients from relevant clinics were invited to complete baseline physical assessment and questionnaires, including the Scoliosis Research Society-22r, Pain Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), and Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine relationships between QoL and a range of explanatory factors.ResultsSixty-nine participants age (median (IQR) 15 (4) yrs) were enrolled. Physical activity was low, with only 17.4% engaging in exercise weekly. Participants reported moderate pain intensity (median (IQR) NRS score 5 (5)) and generalized joint hypermobility (median (IQR) Beighton score 6 (4)). SRS-22 scores indicated a moderate QoL impact because of AIS. Multiple linear regression revealed pain intensity and physical activity to be associated with QoL (p < 0.05), accounting for 35.8% of its total score variability, with pain intensity being the most substantial contributor (β = −0.527).ConclusionThis study comprehensively assesses the multidimensional impacts of AIS on Saudi individuals with AIS. Pain is the most important factor associated with QoL, followed by physical activity. Clinically, addressing pain should be prioritized in the management of AIS to improve overall well-being and enhance patient QoL.Keywords: Scoliosis, adolescent, hypermobility, physical activity, quality of life

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), a common musculoskeletal disorder that is characterized by a lateral curvature of the spine, typically develops during adolescence [1]. It is often diagnosed between the ages of 10 and 18, and its aetiology remains largely unknown [2]. A summary of the prevalence of AIS in studies from Brazil, Germany, Greece, Korea, Singapore, and Turkey indicates a range of 0.47%–5.2% of the global population [3–5]. In Saudi Arabia, 29.44% of screened adolescents exhibited a high range of AIS based on scoliometer measurements [6]. Of screened schoolgirls, the prevalence of AIS was 2.5% [7], and of 1117 boys, just 0.78% [8]. This variability underscores the need for comprehensive screening programs to accurately assess AIS prevalence and its impact on the adolescent population in Saudi Arabia.

Psychological and physical outcomes associated with AIS are multifaceted and influence an individual’s physical functioning and their emotional well-being, social interactions, and overall quality of life (QoL) [9,10]. The psychological distress (anxiety, depression, and body image concerns) reported for adolescents with AIS may lead to persistent pain and reduced QoL [6,10–13]. Various physical factors such as joint hypermobility, body mass index (BMI), and physical activity levels may also influence the progression of AIS and the broader health outcomes of affected individuals [14,15]. However, relationships between these factors and the psychological and physical aspects of QoL in the Saudi population with AIS remain imperfectly understood.

This study explores the physical and psychological profiles of adolescents with AIS and investigates the relationships between AIS-related QoL and key factors, including psychological distress, joint hypermobility, BMI, and habitual physical activity levels. We hypothesized that in adolescents with AIS, QoL was negatively influenced by high pain intensity, psychological distress, joint hypermobility, elevated BMI, and low levels of physical activity. Understanding these associations can inform more comprehensive care strategies and interventions to improve the overall well-being of adolescents with AIS, and promote their physical health, psychological resilience, and social integration.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study.

Participants

Sixty-nine Saudi adolescents with AIS were recruited from King Fahd Medical city (KFMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, between 8 December 2023 and 11 March 2025. AIS is described as lateral curvature of the spine > 10° with a rotational component of the vertebrae [16]. Therefore, study inclusion criteria were: a primary diagnosis of AIS confirmed by a clinician and defined as a Cobb angle ≥ 10°, age between 10 and 18 years, and provision of written informed consent by both the participant and their legal guardian. Participants were excluded if they had other forms of scoliosis such as congenital or neuromuscular scoliosis, recent related injuries, or recent back surgeries.

Procedure

Consecutive patients attending the physiotherapy clinics, spine surgery clinics, or physical medicine and rehabilitation clinics were invited to participate in this study by the researcher (NA), who verbally explained the research objectives to potential participants. Participants were then screened by NA using a standardized screening form for exclusion, and signed a written consent form (by both the participant and their legal guardian) to participate. Participants were then asked to complete detailed questionnaires with the mandatory presence of NA to encourage answers, explain questions, and to prevent a participant’s family from influencing any answer. Ethical approval was granted from the Institutional Review Board at King Fahd Medical City (IRB 22545). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome measure, the Arabic Scoliosis Research Society-22r [17], was used to assess QoL for AIS; it comprises four domains (pain, functioning, mental health, and self-image). Responses to each item were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 being the worst and 5 being the best. A dimension score represents the mean of item points scored for that dimension; a total score is generated by averaging all item points. Content analysis, internal consistency reliability, test/retest reproducibility (ICC range 0.82–0.90), and a test of concurrent validity of the Arabic version showed satisfactory results [17].

Explanatory variables

Demographic data such as age and sex were collected on a standardised form. Clinical characteristics (e.g. if the patient wore a brace for AIS (yes/no), if they followed up with physiotherapy after a clinic visit, whether they took any related medication, and the presence of any comorbidity such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, respiratory diseases (e.g. asthma), and back pain) were collected. Back pain severity was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), in which the participant rates pain from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain) [18]. The scoliosis severity classification was determined based on Cobb angle: 0–10° (spinal curve, not clinically significant), 10–20° (mild scoliosis), 20–40° (moderate scoliosis), and > 40° (severe scoliosis), consistent with established definitions [19,20].

Physical factors included an assessment of joint hypermobility using the Beighton score. Joint hypermobility was scored by NA during the patient’s visit after observing the patient perform nine simple maneuvers. The test uses a 9-point scoring system that measures the flexibility of certain joints; the higher the score the higher the laxity; a score of 0–3 is considered to be normal, and one from 4–9 to represent ligamentous laxity and to indicate generalized joint hypermobility [21,22]. Second, BMI data were collected, and patients were classified into four groups (underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obese). The formula is weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2), determined by NA during the patient’s visit to the clinic. The habitual physical activity level was assessed using the short-form Arabic IPAQ questionnaire [23]. It evaluates the frequency and duration of walking, moderate, and vigorous physical activity, and sedentary behaviour over the past seven days. Responses were used to categorize individuals into low, moderate, or high physical activity levels.

Psychological factors were assessed using the Arabic version of DASS-21 [24,25]. This self-report questionnaire is designed to assess three negative emotional states: depression, anxiety, and stress. Participants rate their experiences over the past week using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much or most of the time). For depression, scores of 0–9 are considered normal, while scores ≥ 28 indicate extreme severity. Anxiety scores range 0–7 for normal levels to 20+ for extreme severity, whereas stress scores from 0–14 are normal, with 34+ classified as extremely severe. Higher scores suggest greater emotional distress.

Determining sample size

The estimated sample size was derived from the online Raosoft sample size calculator. Sample size was calculated based on a response rate of 50%, a confidence interval of 95%, and a margin of error of 5%, with a total adolescent population with AIS at KFMC of approximately 80. Data from only 69 patients treated at KFMC could be collected.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to report demographic and clinical features of participants; mean (SD) or median (IQR) values were reported for continuous data, and frequencies (percentage) for categorical data. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and assessments of histograms and Q-Q plots.

Correlations between QoL, sociodemographic, clinical, physical, and psychosocial outcomes were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients [26]; correlation coefficients were interpreted as being very strong (≥ 0.7), strong (0.5–0.7), moderate (0.3–0.5), weak (0.1–0.3), or with no correlation < 0.1 [27]. Collinearity between independent variables was assessed, with correlation coefficients ≥ 0.7 indicating high levels of collinearity [28].

Stepwise multiple linear regression analyses were used to explore the influence of sociodemographic clinical (pain intensity, age, time since diagnosis), physical (physical activity, BMI, thoracolumbar/lumbar Cobb angle, thoracic Cobb angle, and joint hypermobility) and psychological factors (stress, anxiety, and depression) on QoL scores. Although stepwise methods can increase the risk of overfitting, this approach was chosen to develop a clear and interpretable model in the absence of strong theoretical guidance for variable inclusion. Variables were entered into the model at p < 0.05 and removed at p > 0.10.

The multiple linear regression model respected previously published recommendations of 5–10 events (participants) per independent variable ratio [29,30]. Final model adequacy was evaluated using adjusted R2, variance inflation factor (VIF), and residual diagnostics. Collinearity was considered a problem when VIF > 10. Because this study was exploratory in nature, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons or correlated outcomes.

Of the 69 participants, most were nonsmokers (98.6%) and female (85.5%); their median age (IQR) was 15 (4) yrs. The BMI of 87% of participants fell within a normal range (18.5–24.9); the involvement in any type of physical activity was particularly low among participants (only 17.4% reported routine weekly physical activity) (Table 1).

Discussion

The clinical profiles of adolescents with AIS encompass a range of factors, including musculoskeletal characteristics and mental well-being, all of which can influence their overall health. Findings also provide valuable insights into those factors that influence QoL in adolescents with AIS. Our regression model explains 35.8% of the variance in QoL, revealing the significant role of pain intensity and physical activity as contributing factors. Among these, pain intensity emerged as the strongest factor, suggesting that higher pain levels are associated with poorer QoL. In contrast, factors such as age, BMI, time since diagnosis, psychological distress (stress, anxiety, depression), Cobb angle, and joint hypermobility did not significantly impact QoL. These findings reveal the importance of pain management and promoting physical activity in clinical interventions to improve the well-being of adolescents with AIS.

Pain, a prominent feature of AIS, with various degrees of severity, significantly affects the QoL of affected individuals [11,33–36]. In a previous study on a Saudi population with AIS, half the sample population (according to the Oswestry Disability Index) was effectively disabled [6]. We also report back pain intensity to be a central contributor to reduced QoL, possibly because of the suggested influence of pain on physical, emotional, and social well-being of patients reported in previous studies [11,35,36]. Accordingly, pain-management strategies should incorporate physiotherapy to improve QoL [37].

While physical activity contributed significantly to explaining variance in QoL in the regression model, its influence was weaker than that of pain. Although the association was statistically significant (β = 0.026, p < 0.05), the effect size was modest, suggesting that physical activity exerts a smaller but still meaningful impact on QoL. In clinical terms, this means that increasing physical activity alone may significantly improve an individual’s QoL when combined with other therapeutic approaches. However, promoting physical activity across the broader patient population may still yield measurable benefits. This modest relationship could be attributed to the influence of pain and fear of injury, which can lead to the avoidance of physical activities and limited participation in and enjoyment of sports or recreational activities [38,39]. Nevertheless, regular physical activity remains important because it helps to modulate pain, improve musculoskeletal function, reduce pain perception, and enhance psychological wellness in chronic pain conditions [14,15]. Furthermore, in individuals with AIS, maintaining an active lifestyle may improve spinal mobility and enhance overall long-term well-being [40,41]. Understanding the relationship between physical activity and pain in AIS is therefore essential for developing holistic pain-management strategies. Promoting even small increases in physical activity could lead to gradual improvement in overall health and QoL within this population [15].

Psychological distress variables, including stress, anxiety, and depression, did not significantly contribute to variance in QoL, in contrast to previous studies among individuals with AIS [6,38,39,42]. This may be because our sample population reported lower levels of psychological distress than that of [6], suggesting that these factors most influence QoL in individuals with severe cases. Although contextual factors such as social support, healthcare access, and coping mechanisms might have influenced these results, our dataset provides no direct evidence for these effects. Further research could examine how cultural values, health system characteristics, or stigma around mental health shape psychological variation in adolescents with AIS. Given the importance of psychological health during adolescence, healthcare professionals should evaluate the mental health of individuals with AIS, particularly if stress or worry may be more evident [43].

Our study sample manifested a high prevalence of joint hypermobility, with the mean Beighton score (6) consistent with that of [44] for Saudi primary school children. However, joint hypermobility did not significantly affect adolescent QoL in our analysis, or the likelihood of a patient requiring surgery [45]. Although joint hypermobility has been linked to musculoskeletal pain and an increased risk of developing scoliosis (51.4%) [46,47], it does not play a significant role in our regression model. This suggests that the impact of joint hypermobility on QoL may be indirect or that a larger sample size might be required to identify its effects.

We report that thoracic and thoracolumbar Cobb angles (two commonly used metrics to indicate the severity of scoliosis) do not affect the QoL score. This contrasts with previous research indicating that higher spinal curvature is associated with a lower QoL score [48,49]. These findings may be explained by perceived feelings of pain and function being more important to individuals with AIS in their everyday life than the magnitude of curvature alone [50,51]. It also suggests that the clinical presentation of scoliosis in terms of curvature may not always explain perceived health-related QoL [51,52].

We report descriptive factors related to AIS in a Saudi population that have not been previously measured. There are several clinical implications. Because of the association between pain and QoL, more emphasis should be directed towards pain management than correction of spinal curvature when managing AIS. Givin the importance of physical activity, promoting it should be a central component of managing AIS in this population. Clinicians should be aware that even without a direct relationship between psychological distress and QoL, mental health can still play an important role in QoL. It is important to recognize that some individuals may not report psychological distress because of cultural barriers pertaining to mental health. Therefore, clinicians should routinely screen for conditions such as anxiety and depression. Early identification of psychological problems in these screenings will help patients to receive appropriate and timely psychological support, which may significantly enhance their emotional well-being and overall treatment outcome.

A key limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which makes it difficult to draw conclusions about causality. The small, convenience-based sample limits the transferability of our findings to a broader population. Additionally, the sample size for regression analysis may increase the risk of overfitting and reduce generalizability. Although we followed recommendations of 5–10 participants per variable for exploratory analyses, more conservative guidelines suggest larger samples. Because this was an exploratory study in a new population with a distinct cultural context, relevant predictors were not fully established a priori. Future research could investigate changes over time with larger, more diverse groups with a focus on long-term pain management, and its impact on mental health and QoL of individuals with AIS.

Conclusion

We examined the clinical profiles of Saudi individuals with AIS. We report AIS to impact multiple health domains (pain, joint hypermobility, physical activity, and psychological well-being), and identify pain followed by habitual physical activity levels to be important factors associated with QoL in Saudi individuals with AIS. Treatment of individuals afflicted with AIS should focus on pain management to improve their overall well-being.
