Nasal Airway Transcriptome Reflects Selected Asthma‐Associated Gene Signatures in the Lower Airways
Authors: Hui Wen, Tessa Kole, Orestes A Carpaij, Tatiana Karp, Victor Guryev, Alen Faiz, Kian Fan Chung, Pankaj Bhavsar, Ian M Adcock, Salman Siddiqui, Andy Lan, Katie L Raby, Nazanin Zounemat‐Kermani, Chris Brightling, Dave Singh, Janwillem Kocks, Monica Kraft, Bianca Beghé, Klaus F Rabe, Alberto Papi, Machteld N Hylkema, Martijn C Nawijn, Maarten van den Berge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTranscriptomic analysis of bronchial brushes reveals asthma‐associated gene signatures but is limited by the invasiveness of bronchoscopy. Based on the “united airways” hypothesis, we evaluated whether and to what extent nasal brushes reflect asthma‐associated transcriptomic changes in the lower airways.MethodsIn the ARMS study, we included 26 asthma patients and 28 healthy controls, all fully clinically characterized. Nasal and bronchial brushes were collected for RNA sequencing. We used edgeR and WGCNA to identify asthma‐associated genes and modules in bronchial brushes. We assessed their expression patterns in ARMS nasal brushes and validated the findings in the independent ATLANTIS study (n = 427).ResultsWe identified 51 asthma‐associated genes in the lower airways, of which 40 were up‐regulated and 11 were down‐regulated in asthma compared to healthy controls. Seven of the 40 up‐regulated genes were also up‐regulated in ARMS nasal brushes and validated in ATLANTIS: CLCA1, FETUB, CST1, NTRK2, CDH26, TPSAB1, and DHX35. WGCNA revealed two modules that were consistently elevated in asthma across bronchial and nasal brushes in ARMS and confirmed in ATLANTIS. One module represents IL‐13 related inflammation, whereas the other represents mast cell activity.ConclusionThe asthma‐associated gene signatures of the lower and upper airways show a limited but biologically coherent overlap, with seven genes and two gene modules consistently elevated in asthma. The shared gene signatures reflect two separate components of type 2 inflammation: IL‐13 related inflammation and mast cell activity. Selected nasal gene signatures offer a non‐invasive tool to identify asthma endotypes with distinct molecular mechanisms driving underlying disease biology.Keywords: asthma, IL‐13 related inflammation, non‐invasive, type 2 inflammation, united airways

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disorder characterized by airway inflammation associated with variable airflow obstruction, leading to symptoms of cough, wheezing, and chest tightness [1, 2].

Over the past decade, the importance of phenotyping has been well established, and targeted treatments have become available for specific subgroups of patients, for example, those with evidence of type 2 (T2) inflammation [2]. Although this has significantly improved the management of severe asthma, variability in treatment response still persists in T2‐high patients, and the origins of T2‐low disease remain poorly understood. Previous studies have applied transcriptomic analysis to identify underlying molecular mechanisms of the different asthma endotypes associated with varied clinical outcomes and/or treatment responsiveness to inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) [3]. One of the earliest examples of this approach was provided by Woodruff et al., who performed genome‐wide expression analysis of bronchial epithelial brushes to identify a distinct gene expression profile associated with T2‐asthma and a favorable response to treatment with corticosteroids [4]. Another example comes from the U‐BIOPRED study, where transcriptomic analysis from bronchial brushes and biopsies identified several different asthma subgroups, including subsets characterized by T2 inflammation and/or relative corticosteroid insensitivity [5]. Further, Modena et al. used bronchial epithelial gene expression from the Severe Asthma Research Program (SARP), linked to fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels, and clustered asthma patients into five subgroups, uncovering novel molecular mechanisms associated with different asthma endotypes [6].

Since asthma is primarily an inflammatory disease of the lower airways, bronchial brushes or biopsies are considered the gold standard for studying its disease mechanisms. However, the invasive nature of bronchoscopy limits widespread application, and nasal brushes have been suggested as a non‐invasive alternative [7]. Poole et al. previously demonstrated that nasal brushes reflect the expression of a subset of asthma‐associated T2 inflammation genes in the lower airways [8]. However, this study was conducted in children.

Among adults, two of our previous studies employing bronchial and nasal epithelial gene‐expression datasets demonstrated that nasal gene signatures associated with asthma or COPD reflect specific lower‐airway transcriptomic patterns [9, 10]. However, these studies did not include paired bronchial and nasal samples from the same participants. In our previous NORM study, we showed that nasal brush gene‐expression profiles can serve as a less invasive proxy for bronchial epithelial gene expression in respiratory‐healthy participants [11]. Based on this, the current manuscript analyses paired bronchial and nasal brushes RNA‐sequencing data from adult asthma patients and healthy controls in the Asthma Origins and Remission Study (ARMS) to investigate how well asthma‐associated genes and gene modules identified in the lower airways are reflected in the upper airways. These shared findings are further validated for their replicability in the independent Assessment of Small Airways Involvement in Asthma (ATLANTIS) cohort [12].

Methods

Participants Enrolment

We included both asthma patients and healthy controls from the ARMS study (NCT03141814), a cross‐sectional study. In the ARMS study, asthma patients were included if they had an asthma diagnosis confirmed by objective measurements: either an increase in the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of ≥ 12% from the initial value and ≥ 200 mL after inhaling 400 μg of salbutamol, or airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) to methacholine (provocative concentration causing a 20% drop in FEV1 (PC20) ≤ 8 mg/mL). Patients with asthma were required to have clinically stable disease without asthma exacerbation or respiratory infection symptoms for at least 6 weeks before inclusion in the study. Prior to the start of the study, all asthma patients discontinued ICS/long‐acting beta‐agonists for a period of at least 6 weeks, using only short‐acting beta‐agonists as needed. Healthy controls were included if they had no respiratory diseases, had normal lung function with an FEV1% predicted > 90%, FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio above the lower limit of normal, and no AHR to methacholine (PC20 > 16 mg/mL). All participants underwent a comprehensive clinical assessment, including spirometry, body plethysmography, impulse oscillometry, differential cell counts in blood and induced sputum, nasal brushes, and bronchoscopy with bronchial brushes. Asthma severity was assessed using the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2012 criteria. In ARMS study, we used RNA‐seq data from participants included between 2014 and 2020. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University Medical Center Groningen (ABR nr—NL 53173.042.15) and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study. To assess the robustness of our identified shared nasal asthma‐associated gene signatures in the ARMS study, we validated them in the independent ATLANTIS cohort. ATLANTIS is an international multicenter observational study, with genome‐wide nasal gene expression data available from 369 asthma patients and 58 healthy controls [9, 12].

RNA‐Seq Library Preparation, Alignment, and Quality Control: ARMS Study

Ribosomal RNA depletion and RNA‐seq library preparation were conducted by GenomeScan (Leiden, the Netherlands). We used the NEBNext Single Cell/Low Input RNA Library Prep Kit (E6420L) for library preparation, which incorporates unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to identify PCR duplicates. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, California) with a 2 × 150 bp paired‐end configuration aiming for a depth of 30 million read pairs per sample. Quality control (QC) of raw RNA‐seq reads was first performed using FastQC, and further QC details were provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1A,B). We aligned the trimmed reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the STAR aligner (version 2.7.10b). Gene‐level quantification was performed using HTSeq (version 0.12.4) with Ensembl GRCh38 release 108 as the gene annotation database.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2). Descriptive statistics for baseline characteristics were generated using the tableone package (version 0.13.2). For continuous data, we first performed normality tests. A t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test was used for normally or non‐normally distributed data as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. In this study, we focused on protein‐coding genes identified based on Ensembl protein IDs from the Ensembl database using the biomaRt package (version 2.54.1) by referencing the human reference genome (GRCh38). We conducted differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis using the edgeR package (version 4.0.16) with trimmed mean of M‐values normalization, and lowly expressed genes were filtered using the built‐in filterByExpr function. DEGs between asthma patients and healthy controls were identified by fitting the negative binomial generalized linear model using the glmQLFit function, followed by quasi‐likelihood F‐tests with the glmQLFTest function. We adjusted for age and sex in the ARMS DEGs analysis. We additionally adjusted for smoking status in the ATLANTIS DEGs analysis, since the cohort included current smokers. p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control false discovery rate (FDR). An FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the identification of asthma‐associated genes in bronchial brushes. In nasal brushes from the ARMS study, no genes remained significant after FDR correction (FDR < 0.05). As the nasal brush DEGs analysis was primarily used to validate bronchial findings rather than to discover new genes. We therefore evaluated nasal DEGs using nominal p‐values < 0.05 together with a concordant direction of effect relative to the bronchial DEGs.

Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

We applied the WGCNA package (version 1.72‐5) to identify modules of co‐expressed genes in ARMS bronchial brushes. Module eigengenes (MEs), defined as the first principal component of each gene module, were calculated and correlated with asthma status to pinpoint asthma‐associated gene modules. To explore the principal intramodular connectivity within each module, the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE, version 2.0.2) plugin of Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) was then used to explore hub genes within each gene module. Details of network construction and hub genes' identification are described in the Supporting Information.

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)

GSVA is a non‐parametric, unsupervised method shifting focus from individual genes to gene modules by calculating GSVA composite scores for each module in each sample. This allows for the evaluation of gene module activity between asthma patients and healthy controls. Using GSVA package (version 1.50.0), we calculated composite scores of asthma‐associated gene modules identified in bronchial brushes. This analysis generated a new matrix of GSVA scores for each of the bronchial and nasal expression datasets. We then performed statistical tests to identify shared gene modules differentially expressed between asthma patients and healthy controls in both ARMS bronchial and nasal brushes. The ARMS findings were subsequently validated in the ATLANTIS study to confirm the shared gene modules in both airways.

Cell‐Type Deconvolution

We used CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) to perform cell‐type deconvolution for estimating the proportions of deconvoluted cell types in nasal and bronchial brush samples. We used counts per million (CPM) normalized ARMS RNA‐seq data as input mixture file, while single‐cell RNA‐seq signature reference matrix files were from the Human Lung Cell Atlas (HLCA) [13] based on CPM normalized data. Deconvoluted cell types with a mean estimated abundance below 3% were excluded from further analysis. Subsequently, statistical comparisons of estimated cell proportions between asthma patients and healthy controls were performed to identify cell types with differential abundance. Details about the cell deconvolution process for the ATLANTIS can be found in the previous publication [9].

Identification of Shared Asthma‐Associated Genes in Both Bronchial and Nasal Brushes

In ARMS, we identified 51 asthma‐associated genes in bronchial brushes (FDR < 0.05), of which 40 were up‐regulated and 11 down‐regulated in asthma compared to healthy controls (Figure 1). Among the 40 up‐regulated genes in bronchial brushes, we found nine genes to be also up‐regulated in nasal brushes from the ARMS study at a nominal p‐value < 0.05, a number that is nine times higher than expected by chance alone. Furthermore, seven of these nine up‐regulated genes were also higher expressed in asthma in nasal brushes from the independent ATLANTIS study (p‐value < 0.05): CLCA1, FETUB, CST1, NTRK2, CDH26, TPSAB1, and DHX35. Among the 11 down‐regulated genes identified from ARMS bronchial brushes, none were down‐regulated in ARMS nasal brushes and one was down‐regulated in asthma in ATLANTIS: EPAS1 (Table S1).

The red dots show the genes with higher expression in asthma versus healthy controls, while the blue dots show those with lower expression. The gray dots show genes not significantly different between the groups. The black dots indicate these seven genes with higher expression in asthma versus healthy controls in both ARMS bronchial and nasal brushes as well as in ATLANTIS nasal brushes.

We applied GSVA to calculate GSVA composite scores based on the 40 up‐ and 11 down‐regulated genes from ARMS bronchial brushes. As shown in Figure 2, the GSVA scores of the up‐regulated genes were higher in asthma compared to healthy controls in both ARMS bronchial and nasal brushes (p‐value < 0.0001 and p‐value = 0.0073, respectively) with validation in ATLANTIS nasal brushes (p‐value = 0.0019). For the down‐regulated genes, GSVA scores were lower in asthma than in healthy controls from ARMS bronchial brushes (p‐value < 0.0001), while no significant difference was detected in nasal brushes from either ARMS (p‐value = 0.83) or ATLANTIS (p‐value = 0.079).

Identification of WGCNA Modules Consistently Altered in Asthma Across the Lower and Upper Airways

Gene expression patterns in biological systems are often characterized by co‐regulated modules of genes that in concert contribute to a certain biological activity. Therefore, we assessed which gene modules were associated with asthma in bronchial brushes. Using WGCNA, we identified 19 asthma‐associated modules in ARMS bronchial brushes, with seven modules positively associated with asthma and 12 modules negatively associated with asthma compared to healthy controls (Figure S3).

GSVA was used to quantify the activity of all 19 asthma‐associated modules in ARMS bronchial and nasal brushes as well as in ATLANTIS nasal brushes (Figure S4–S6). Among the 19 gene modules, two modules (the IL‐13 related inflammation module and the mast cell activity module) consistently exhibited higher GSVA scores in asthma in both ARMS bronchial brushes (p‐value = 0.0026 and p‐value = 0.00013, respectively) and nasal brushes (p‐value = 0.01 and p‐value = 0.015, respectively), as well as in the nasal brushes from the independent ATLANTIS study (p‐value = 0.0021 and p‐value = 0.014, respectively) (Figure 3).

Box plots showing comparisons of GSVA scores for IL‐13 related inflammation and mast cell activity modules between asthma patients and healthy controls in (A) ARMS bronchial brushes and (B) ARMS nasal brushes, with validation in (C) ATLANTIS nasal brushes.

To gain deep insight into the biological relevance of these gene modules, we identified hub genes within each asthma‐associated module from WGCNA using the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape (refer to Supporting Information for details). Notably, the hub genes in the IL‐13 related inflammation module were IL13, BMP8A, CST1, CDH26, FETUB, CEACAM21, C1QTNF1, CLCA1, SERPINB10, PTCHD4, CCL26, NTRK2, and GSN. The hub genes for the mast cell activity module included TPSAB1, TPSB2, SIGLEC6, IL1RL1, CPA3, GATA2, SLC18A2, GCSAML, MS4A2, CDK15, and CTSG. The lists of hub genes for each of 19 modules are shown in Table S3.

Cell‐Type Deconvolution

We used CIBERSORTx to test whether the cellular composition was estimated to be different based on bulk RNA‐seq data, using single cell references from the HLCA [13]. No significant differences were observed in the estimated proportions of deconvoluted cell types between asthma patients and healthy controls in ARMS bronchial brush samples, including basal, monocytes, and multiciliated cells (Figure S7A). Similarly, ARMS nasal brush samples showed no significant differences in the estimated proportions of deconvoluted cell types, including basal, club, goblet, and multiciliated cells (Figure S7B). Consistently, the previous ATLANTIS study reported no significant differences in deconvoluted cell‐type abundance between asthma patients and healthy controls [9].

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that seven genes and two WGCNA modules are consistently elevated in asthma across both the lower and upper airways. Notably, these two gene modules capture distinct components of T2 inflammation, with one module representing IL‐13 related inflammation and the other predominantly consisting of genes highlighting mast cell activity. Together, our findings indicate that selected nasal gene expression profiles reflect specific asthma‐associated molecular mechanisms relevant to disease pathogenesis in the lower airways.

Among the seven genes associated with asthma in both bronchial and nasal brushes are NTRK2 and DHX35. Dragunas et al. showed that increased NTRK2 expression was detected in asthma patients, paralleled by increased cholinergic nerve density possibly contributing to AHR [14]. Furthermore, we found that DHX35 was up‐regulated in bronchial and nasal brushes from asthma patients compared to healthy controls. In this context, the finding of Qiao et al. is of interest. They found that DHX35 overexpression in human airway epithelial cells increased production of IFN‐β, IL‐6, and CXCL10, thereby modulating antiviral innate immunity [15]. Together, our findings show that the more easily accessible selected nasal gene expression profiles reflect biologically relevant changes associated with asthma in the lower airways.

Using WGCNA, we identified two distinct gene modules associated with asthma. The first gene module was the IL‐13 related inflammation module. The hub genes of this module include IL13, CLCA1, FETUB, CCL26, CDH26, and SERPINB10, all associated with IL‐13 driven inflammation [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. IL‐13 is a central cytokine in T2 asthma pathophysiology, driving eosinophilic inflammation, mucus secretion, and AHR [16, 22]. Previous studies reported elevated CLCA1 mRNA expression in airway epithelial cells of asthma patients [4]. Activation of the IL‐13 receptor upregulates CLCA1 expression, whose protein fragments subsequently stimulate MAPK13 and promote MUC5AC expression, contributing to mucus production in airway epithelial cells [17]. FETUB has also been reported to be induced by IL‐13 stimulation in 16HBE cells [18]. Lung‐specific knockdown of FETUB has been shown to reduce airway inflammation and AHR in asthma [18]. Furthermore, IL‐13 upregulates CST1 and CDH26 expression in 16HBE cells or bronchial epithelial cells [20, 23]. Du et al. demonstrated that epithelial CST1 can stimulate airway eosinophilic inflammation in asthma [24]. Additionally, findings from the MESOS randomized controlled trial showed that elevated FeNO levels are associated with high CST1 expression [25]. CDH26 has been shown to amplify the IL‐4 receptor signaling pathway, enhancing mucin gene expression and eosinophilic chemokines expression in airway epithelial cells [20]. IL‐13 additionally promotes the production of CCL26 and SERPINB10 in epithelial cells, both associated with airway eosinophilia [19, 21]. Consequently, our finding of the increased expression of the IL‐13 related inflammation module genes in asthma may be useful as a non‐invasive biomarker to identify IL‐13 related T2 high asthma in the lower airways.

The second gene module was the mast cell activity module. Hub genes of this module included TPSAB1, TPSB2, SIGLEC6, IL1RL1, GATA2, CPA3, SLC18A2, GCSAML, MS4A2, and CTSG, all associated with mast cell activity. Mast cells release a variety of mediators—including cytokines, lipid mediators, and proteases—that contribute to inflammation by promoting immune cell recruitment, disrupting airway structure, and driving airway remodeling [26]. TPSAB1 encodes either α or β‐tryptases, while TPSB2 encodes β‐tryptases including βI‐, βII‐, or βIII‐tryptase isoforms [27, 28]. Meanwhile, β‐tryptases stimulate airway smooth muscle proliferation and contraction [27]. In addition, SIGLEC6 is involved in IgE and non‐IgE‐mediated mast cell activation [29]. Mast cells express IL1RL1. It encodes the receptor for IL‐33, a cytokine that modulates type‐2 immune responses [30]. GATA2 is a key transcription factor that regulates IL1RL1 expression in mast cells [30]. CPA3 was increased in intraepithelial mast cells of patients with T2‐high asthma [31]. In murine models, CPA3 has been shown to convert angiotensin I into angiotensin II, contributing to airway bronchoconstriction and the enhanced AHR in asthma [31]. The solute transporter, encoded by SLC18A2, is involved in the loading of histamine into secretory vesicles, with mast cells being a primary source of histamine [32]. MS4A2 encodes the β‐subunit of FcεRI (FcεRIβ), which facilitates the trafficking of the FcεRI receptor complex to the cell surface and amplifies IgE‐mediated mast cell signaling [33]. Both GCSAML and CTSG are mast‐ cell associated genes [34]. Our findings support the role of mast cell‐related genes in airway inflammation and highlight the potential of the nasal mast cell activity gene module as a non‐invasive biomarker for mast cell activity in the lower airways. The identification of two distinct gene modules, reflecting IL‐13 related inflammation and mast cell activity is particularly noteworthy because they both represent key, yet separate components of T2 inflammation.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limits our ability to identify specific nasal gene signatures that can reliably reflect bronchial gene expression profiles across diverse asthma subgroups, such as T2 high and T2 low asthma or allergic and non‐allergic asthma. Larger, well‐characterized cohorts are needed to validate nasal surrogates for bronchial gene signatures, particularly across different asthma subtypes. Additionally, this study utilized bulk RNA sequencing, which lacks the resolution to pinpoint substitute nasal brush genes at the specific cell‐type level. Although we observed mast cell‐related genes in the upper airways reflecting those of the lower airways, the relationship between mast cell abundance in the two compartments remains unclear. Future studies employing single‐cell RNA sequencing of paired nasal and bronchial samples could provide more detailed insights into cell‐type specific gene expression.

In conclusion, nasal brush samples can capture selected asthma‐associated transcriptomic profiles that reflect those of bronchial brushes. This non‐invasive approach could serve as a promising tool to evaluate lower airway inflammation, ultimately contributing to personalized asthma management.
