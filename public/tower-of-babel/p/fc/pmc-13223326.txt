Childhood predictors of political voice across 22 countries in the global flourishing study
Authors: John Ssozi, John Kiweewa, Nicholas Norman-Krause, Matt Bradshaw, Tyler J VanderWeele, Byron R Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current study retrospectively examines childhood predictors among sampled respondents to assess linkages, if any, between various childhood experiences before age 12 and political voice in adulthood. We apply random effects meta-analysis on a diverse and international dataset of 202,898 individuals from 22 countries. Using specified reference exposures for each category of childhood experiences, we find that having a strong relationship with mother/father results in an individual reporting slightly greater political voice relative to having a bad relationship with a parent overall. Similar overall positive associations are observed among individuals who had single parents, including instances in which one or both parents died, reported having financially comfortable childhoods, were exposed to abuse, felt like an outsider, experienced excellent health, and attended religious services. However, in Egypt, Indonesia, Nigeria, Philippines, Poland, and Spain abuse associated with relatively lower political voice. Likewise, feeling as an outsider in Germany, Israel, Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Tanzania associated with relatively lower political voice. Our findings suggest that the strength and direction of these associations differ by country, reflecting diverse cultural influences. Concerns about unmeasured confounding variables were addressed using the e-value method and were reassuring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46513-x.Keywords: Childhood exposures, Political voice, CultureSubject terms: Human behaviour, Risk factors

Introduction

In accordance with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 19), every individual has the right to freedom of opinion and expression. This right encompasses the freedom to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers. Despite this principle, a growing body of research documents persistent inequalities in political voice among citizens1. Political voice—the capacity to express political views and influence public discourse—is shaped by a range of determinants spanning both early-life experiences and adult contexts. However, scholarship on the developmental antecedents of political engagement—such as identity formation, personality traits, and value development—has largely focused on the factors that motivate young people to support or participate in activism related to specific political or social causes, leaving broader human development influences comparatively understudied2. One reason for this gap is the methodological difficulty of measuring political inputs during childhood. Consequently, most studies rely heavily on adult experiences, when individuals begin actively participating in political processes.

This study addresses this gap in literature by examining how childhood exposures shape political voice in adulthood. Specifically, it investigates whether early-life experiences are linked to adult expressions of political voice, whether these associations vary across countries, and whether they remain robust in the presence of potential unmeasured confounding. To this end, we are interested in examining the role of several important early life factors or experiences such as relationship with parents, parent marital status, family financial status, physical/emotional/sexual abuse, feeling like an outsider, immigration status, personal health, religious attendance by age 12, generation (year of birth), and gender. Further, since the influence of these and other factors such as race/ethnicity and religious affiliation at age 12 are contextual and, as such, may vary from country to country, our direct analysis of these variables is on a country-by-country basis.

Understanding the antecedents of political voice is particularly important given its implications for individuals’ overall well-being and flourishing. Political voice— both internal and external political efficacy—contributes to individuals’ sense of agency, identity, and belonging1. Beyond its instrumental role in articulating and advancing citizens’ needs, political voice has intrinsic value as a fundamental dimension of human freedom and flourishing3. Indeed, like political liberty itself, political voice constitutes an essential component of what it means to live a fully human life3. Consequently, inequalities in political voice may imply that certain groups of citizens are less able to have their interests represented or to participate meaningfully in shaping the social and political conditions that influence their well-being1.

Childhood antecedents of political voice

This paper builds on and extends the insights of life-course studies that examine the effects of childhood experiences on adult outcomes4. Early-life experiences constitute a complex constellation of influences shaped by parental environments, the intergenerational transmission of social class and political cues5, and individuals’ own formative encounters. Such experiences can shape trajectories not only of social mobility6,7, but also of adult health, behaviors, and socioeconomic status. For example, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been consistently linked to increased risks of chronic disease, morbidity, mental health disorders, and high-risk behaviors in adulthood, including heavy alcohol use, smoking, high-risk HIV behaviors, and disability8–11. Conversely, positive childhood experiences (PCEs) may foster greater well-being and resilience across the life-course8. Several studies have also suggested that individuals who have had ACEs, ranging from physical and emotional abuse to household dysfunction, tend to report lower political voice in adulthood12. Using cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys, it was found that individuals with depressive symptoms related to higher ACEs scores tend to have low political interest and internal efficacy, and that such individuals are significantly less likely to vote or engage in political activism13,14. Other studies show a strong link between childhood adversity and cortisol habituation, particularly among men15,16. The dysregulation of the body’s stress response system may reduce the coping mechanism for the confrontational and high-stakes nature of political engagement. Direct effects have also been reported, where individuals exposed to political activities early in life are likely to report higher political voice2,17.

Political socialization research highlights the central role of early life experiences in shaping political attitudes and participation in adulthood17–21. Particularly, parents are widely recognized as influential agents in the political development of their children, transmitting political values, norms, and behaviors across generations. A substantial body of research shows that early life socialization is associated with later political participation and engagement22–25. Through family interactions and everyday socialization processes, children are introduced to the rules, expectations, and cultural norms of political life, and these early predispositions can exert lasting influences throughout the life course26,27. For example, individuals raised by politically active parents are more likely to develop an interest in politics and to engage in political activities themselves17,28,29.

In addition, research shows that family relationships and emotional environments in childhood play a critical role in shaping adult life outcomes. Parental love and support—both maternal and paternal—have been shown to influence psychological development and long-term well-being30. Conversely, parental rejection or withdrawal can lead to adverse psychological outcomes, including low self-esteem, emotional instability, anxiety, social withdrawal, and increased risk of mental health disorders31,32. These developmental consequences may have indirect implications for civic and political engagement, as psychological well-being and social functioning can affect individuals’ willingness and capacity to participate in public life. Moreover, experiences of childhood abuse have been linked to elevated risks of depressive symptoms and other psychological difficulties in adulthood, even after accounting for personality traits and socioeconomic characteristics33,34.

Beyond the family environment, participation in community organizations during childhood—such as youth groups, civic associations, and religious institutions—may also influence political engagement later in life2,5,35. Research using data from the National Educational Longitudinal Survey (NELS) has found that early involvement in community organizations is strongly associated with higher levels of civic engagement in young adulthood36. These institutions often serve as important sites for the development of civic skill, political awareness, other forms of political participation, and social networks that facilitate political participation37,38. For example, engagement with community organizations and places of worship has been shown to increase electoral participation, other forms of political involvement39, and broader civic engagement40,41. Despite this evidence, relatively little research has examined how childhood religious service attendance specifically influences political participation in adulthood.

Religion and religious socialization constitute another important pathway through which early experiences may shape later civic and political behavior. Previous studies have examined how parental religiosity influences children’s development across multiple domains, including education, sexuality, personality formation, and broader psychological outcomes42–44. However, relatively little research has examined the relationship between individuals’ own religious experiences in childhood—such as religious service attendance—and their subsequent political participation in adulthood. Although religious involvement alone may not directly generate political action, early religious engagement can provide a foundation for civic identity formation, social networks, and the acquisition of social capital5,45–47. Religious institutions have often been described as “civic incubators,” offering opportunities to develop civic skills and engage in community life, particularly for individuals who may lack other socioeconomic resources for political participation5. Longitudinal evidence suggests that adolescents actively involved in religious groups are more likely to engage in charitable activities and civic participation later in adulthood46.

Individual characteristics present at birth, such as race or ethnicity, also play an important role in shaping political participation. Research in Western democracies suggests that differences in political engagement between ethnic minorities and majority populations often reflect disparities in socioeconomic resources and levels of political representation in policymaking institutions48. Race and ethnicity frequently intersect with other structural factors, including immigration status, citizenship, and socioeconomic position. For example, studies have found that political attitudes can influence social relationships and even increase the risk of social isolation among both Black and White Americans49, even after controlling for demographic and socioeconomic variables. Given that racial and ethnic classifications vary substantially across national contexts, analyses that consider these factors often benefit from country-specific approaches.

Generational differences have also been widely examined as an important dimension of political behavior17,50,51. Scholars have argued that major historical events and social transformations can shape the attitudes, values, and political behaviors of individuals who experience them during formative years52. As a result, “political cohorts” may emerge, reflecting shared experiences that influence political engagement across the life course51. Research on generational patterns in political participation has emphasized the importance of age, period, and cohort effects in shaping political behavior. Members of the same generation will purportedly exhibit similar attitudes and behaviors throughout their lives due to shared socialization experiences. Empirical studies have documented significant generational differences in electoral participation, including evidence from Germany showing that earlier cohorts (those born between 1920 and 1949) often demonstrate higher voting rates than later generations52. Similar differences were reported between baby boomers and older generations in the United States53.

Finally, although not part of this study, another line of scholarship emphasizes the role of personality traits and biological predispositions in shaping political attitudes and behaviors54–57. Some studies suggest that political ideology and engagement may be partly associated with personality traits that emerge early in life and may have genetic components. For instance, openness to experience has been linked to more liberal political orientations, whereas conscientiousness has been associated with more conservative attitudes58. However, other research59 has found limited evidence of direct causal relationships between personality traits and political attitudes, instead highlighting the influence of early developmental and genetic factors. Importantly, early childhood programs that foster non-cognitive skills—such as self-regulation, sociability, and interpersonal competence—have been shown to increase political participation, including voter turnout, in adulthood60. Prior research suggests that the Big Five personality dimensions—extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and openness—are related to political efficacy and engagement33. Extraversion and openness to experience have been identified as key drivers of active political participation and voice. By contrast, agreeableness, which can be shaped by early childhood experiences and trauma, has been associated with lower levels of internal political efficacy61.

In summary, existing research highlights the importance of early life experiences—including family socialization, religious involvement, community participation, psychological development, personality traits, structural characteristics, and generational contexts—in shaping political engagement across the life course. However, the specific pathways linking childhood religiosity and early social experiences to political participation in adulthood remain insufficiently understood, underscoring the need for further empirical investigation.

Methods

The description of the methods used in this study has been adapted from the introductory Global Flourishing Study article62. Further methodological details are available elsewhere63–66. Four additional waves of panel data will be collected annually from 2024 to 2027.

Study sample

Data for this study come from the Global Flourish Study Wave 1 dataset62,67, which examines the distribution and determinants of well-being in a diverse and international sample of approximately 202,898 individuals from 22 countries. Countries were selected to ensure coverage of global geographic, cultural, and religious diversity, as well as feasibility of data collection. The 22 countries fall within three income levels, according to the 2023 World Bank classification of countries. Egypt, India, Kenya, Nigeria, Philippines, and Tanzania are classified as lower-middle income. Argentina, Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, Turkey, and South Africa are within the upper-middle income category, while Australia, Germany, Hong Kong, Israel, Japan, Poland, Spain, Sweden, United Kingdom, and the United States are categorized as high-income. The low-income classification is not represented in the dataset. Most of the data are from high-income countries which represent 57%, with upper-middle income and lower-middle income representing 18% and approximately 24% respectively. The United States and Japan lead with 19% and 10% of the data respectively, while Turkey has only 1%, the smallest number of participants in the sample.

The dataset that supports the findings of this article are openly available on the Open Science Framework at 10.17605/OSF.IO/3JTZ863. During the translation process, Gallup Inc. adhered to the TRAPD model (translation, review, adjudication, pretesting, and documentation) for cross-cultural survey research (ccsg.isr.umich.edu/chapters/translation/overview). Additional details about methodology and survey development can be found in the GFS Questionnaire Development Report63, GFS Methodology64, GFS Codebook, and Translations documents68.

Outcome

Political voice was assessed by the question: “Do you agree with the following statement? People like me have a say about what the government does.” Respondents could answer: agree, disagree, and unsure. The responses were dichotomized as “agree” versus “disgree/unsure,” and hence analyzed as a binary variable. Similar questions are applied in earlier studues69. Utilizing a single variable for political voice minimizes cognitive burden on respondents.

Childhood predictors

We evaluated 11 childhood predictors organized into five categories: demographic, socioeconomic, family dynamics and relationship, health and well-being, and religious and spiritual predictors. (i) The demographic predictors were: (a) Age/Year of Birth, respondents were asked for their age (in years) which were classified into the following cohorts: 18–24, 25–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80+. The birth year was accordingly established. (b) Gender: respondents reported their gender as: male, female, or other. (c) Immigration status: participants were asked whether they were born in the current country of residence. Response options were: Yes or No. (d) Race/ethnicity was also assessed but because its significance and meaning differs vastly across the 22 countries, it was analyzed only at the country-specific level.

(ii)The primary socioeconomic predictor was the subjective financial status of family during childhood. Participants responded to the question: “Which one of these phrases comes closest to your own feelings about your family’s household income when you were growing up, such as when YOU were around 12 years old?” Response options were: “lived comfortably”, “got by”, “found it difficult”, or “found it very difficult.”

Statistical analysis

For the full sample and for each country, descriptive statistics were weighted to be nationally representative within each country and estimated for each of the childhood antecedents. We then used a weighted linear regression model with complex survey adjusted standard errors, for each country, to analyze the simultaneous impact of all childhood predictors on political voice. A Wald-type test was conducted to obtain a joint test for all categories within each childhood antecedent and is reported as a single global p-value. The primary results consist of random effects meta-analyses of the regression coefficients70,71 along with confidence intervals, lower and upper limits 95% prediction intervals, heterogeneity (τ), and I2 for evidence concerning variation within a given demographic category across countries72. Forest plots of estimates are available in the online supplement. Religious affiliation/tradition and race/ethnicity were used within country as control variables, when available, but these coefficients themselves were not included in the meta-analyses because categories/responses varied by country. All meta-analyses were conducted in R using the metafor package73. Within each country, a global test of association of each childhood predictor variable group with outcome was conducted, and a pooled p-value74 across countries reported concerning evidence for association within any country. Bonferroni corrected p-value thresholds are provided based on the number of childhood demographic variables75,76. For each childhood predictor, we calculated E-values to evaluate the sensitivity of results to unmeasured confounding. An E-value is the minimum strength of the association an unmeasured confounder must have with both the outcome and the predictor, above and beyond all measured covariates, for an unmeasured confounder to explain away an association77. As a supplementary analysis, population weighted meta-analyses of the regression coefficients were estimated. All analyses were pre-registered with the Center for Open Science prior to data access, with only slight subsequent modification in the regression analyses due to multicollinearity https://osf.io/yrn8h. We diverged from our pre-registered analysis plan by combining purpose and meaning into a single variable. This decision was driven by the highly similar findings for each variable, which suggested that these constructs could be more effectively discussed as a unified concept. Given the ongoing debate in the field regarding the distinctiveness of purpose and meaning, this consolidation was carefully considered. All code to reproduce analyses are openly available in an online repository65.

Missing data

Missing data on all variables was imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations, and five imputed datasets were used78–81. It is more flexible and preserves statistical analytical strength. To account for variation in the assessment of certain variables across countries (e.g., religious affiliation/tradition and race/ethnicity), the imputation process was conducted separately in each country. This within-country imputation approach ensured that the imputation models accurately reflected country-specific contexts and assessment methods. Sampling weights were included in the imputation models to account for specific-variable missingness that might have been related to probability of inclusion in the study.

Accounting for complex sampling design

The GFS used different sampling schemes across countries based on availability of existing panels and recruitment needs64. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design components by including weights, primary sampling units, and strata. Additional methodological detail, including accounting for the complex sampling design, is provided elsewhere79.

Respondents ranged from age 18 to 80+, with the largest group aged 30–49 years (37%), followed by 50–69 (30%), 18–29 (23%), and ≥ 70 (10%). Gender was nearly balanced (51% female, 49% male). Regarding childhood economic conditions, 24% reported a difficult or very difficult upbringing, 41% reported “getting by,” and 35% reported living comfortably. Demographic predictors entail year of birth, gender, and immigration status. Year of birth (reference: 1998–2005; ages 18–24) shows a generally negative gradient: most older cohorts reported lower political voice than reference group, with the lowest levels among those aged ≥ 80, except individuals aged 70–79 who were associated higher political voice. With males as the reference group, females and respondents identifying as another gender reported lower political voice, the latter group showing the largest deficit. Compared with native-born respondents, those born abroad or who lived abroad before age 12 also reported lower political voice. Using distributional thresholds (< 0.90 and > 1.10), for instance, 41% of females had RR < 0.90 and none exceeded 1.10; among the “other gender” category, 50% fell below 0.90 and 44% exceeded 1.10. The global p-value for gender (< 0.01) indicates statistical significance in at least one country.

The socioeconomic predictor focused on the financial status of a person’s family growing up. Childhood family financial status (reference: “got by”) was associated with political voice. Growing up comfortably predicted higher political voice (RR = 1.11), whereas reporting financial difficulty or severe difficulty predicted lower voice (RR = 0.97 and 0.99, respectively). No respondents from comfortable households fell below 0.90, and 55% exceeded 1.10; by contrast, 9% and 5% of those reporting difficulty or severe difficulty fell below 0.90, and none exceeded 1.10. The global p-value (< 0.01) again indicates statistical significance in at least one country.

Regarding family dynamics, parental marital status, relationship with mother, relationship with father, and feelings of being an outsider/insider were used as predictors. In all 22 countries, results indicated that a good relationship with one’s mother (vs. poor) was associated with higher political voice (RR = 1.06), with similar estimates for father–child relationships. Only 5% of respondents fell below 0.90, whereas 32% exceeded 1.10 (global p < 0.01). Using married parents as the reference, parental divorce predicted lower political voice (RR = 0.98), while single-parent upbringing (RR = 1.01) and parental death (RR = 1.03) showed small positive associations. For divorced parents, 23% fell below 0.90 and 18% exceeded 1.10; among those with deceased parents, 14% exceeded 1.10. Feeling like an outsider during childhood (reference: not feeling like an outsider) was associated with a small higher political voice (RR = 1.02), with approximately 14% of estimates outside the < 0.90 or > 1.10 thresholds.

Using good childhood health as a reference group, results revealed that both excellent/good and poor childhood health were associated with higher political voice. None of those reporting excellent or very good health fell below 0.90, while 32% and 14%, respectively, exceeded 1.10.

Religious and spiritual predictors were captured by attendance of religious services before age 12. Compared with never attending religious services before age 12, any attendance predicted higher political voice. The proportion of estimates exceeding 1.10 was 55% for weekly attendance, 59% for monthly attendance, and 32% for less-than-monthly attendance.

Overall, demographic disadvantage (older age cohorts, immigrant background, and non-male gender), childhood financial hardship, and social marginalization were generally associated with lower political voice, whereas supportive family relationships, relative economic comfort, and early religious participation were associated with higher political voice, though effects varied across countries.

Differences in associations by country

The country specific results in the online supplementary Tables S1b-S22b show some variations from the overall results in the associations between childhood experiences and political voice. For instance, while on average those whose indicated gender was female or ‘other’ reported lower political voice compared to men, in countries such as Australia, Indonesia, Israel, Japan, Kenya, Nigeria, the Philippines, Poland, South Africa, Spain, and the United States those who specifically indicated ‘other’ reported higher political voice. In India, females reported higher political voice than males.

Second, while the overall results show that participants who experienced abuse during childhood and/or felt like outsiders reported higher political voice, there are several countries where they did not. For instance, in Germany, Indonesia, Israel, Nigeria, the Philippines, South Africa, and Tanzania, respondents who felt like outsiders were associated with lower political voice. Likewise, in Egypt, Nigeria, Philippines, Poland, and Spain, respondents who experienced abuse in childhood were associated with lower political voice. However, in Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Japan, Kenya, Mexico, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States, respondents who experienced abuse and/or felt like outsiders reported higher political voice than those in the sample who did not.

Third, using “ethnic plurality” as the reference category, childhood ethnic minority status was largely unrelated to adult political voice across the 22 countries. Associations were small and mostly non-significant. In Indonesia and the United Kingdom, minority status predicted slightly lower political voice (− 0.06%, p = 0.10), and in the United States a small but statistically significant decrease was observed (− 0.05%, p < 0.01). By contrast, in Nigeria minority status was modestly associated with higher political voice (+ 0.05%, p = 0.08).

Fourth, childhood religion was similarly non-significant in most settings, with several context-specific exceptions. Using “no religion/atheist/agnostic “as the reference group, higher political voice was observed among Christians in Hong Kong (+ 0.09%, p = 0.05), Buddhists in Japan (+ 0.08%, p < 0.01), adherents of other religions in Japan (+ 0.05%, p < 0.01), and Christians in the United States (+ 0.04%, p = 0.10). In contrast, in Tanzania both Christianity (− 0.11%, p < 0.01) and Islam (− 0.17%, p < 0.01) were associated with lower political voice, whereas other religions predicted higher voice (+ 0.19%, p < 0.01). When country-specific majority religions were used as the reference category, results remained heterogeneous. In Indonesia, childhood Christianity and other religions were associated with higher political voice relative to Islam (+ 0.09%, p < 0.01). In Nigeria, Islam (− 0.05%, p < 0.01) and other religions (− 0.01%, p < 0.01) predicted lower political voice compared with Christianity. In India, identifying as Muslim was associated with a marginal increase relative to the Hindu reference group (+ 0.02%, p = 0.11). Overall, both ethnic and religious childhood identities showed small, context-dependent associations with political voice rather than consistent cross-national patterns.

Lastly, Table 3 presents an Eigen-value sensitivity analysis to assess whether unmeasured confounding could account for the associations between the 11 childhood exposures and political voice. Using a random-effects meta-analytic framework, we estimated the strength an omitted variable would need—if related to both a childhood exposure and political voice—to render a given association statistically non-significant. For example, Table 2 reports a weighted relative risk (RR) of 1.06 (95% CI: 1.00–1.12) for having a “very/somewhat good” relationship with one’s mother, indicating approximately 6% higher political voice compared with a poor relationship. The largest E-values were observed for “other” gender (14.94) and being born in 1943 or earlier (age ≥ 80 years; 3.76), suggesting substantially greater robustness of these estimates to unmeasured confounding.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusion

While political voice is a multifaceted construct, it manifests itself through two main dimensions: internal efficacy and external efficacy which are correlated and have an effect of political participation. These dimensions encompass expressions of concern, approval, demand, dissent, etc., and can manifest through interpersonal discussion, protest, symbolic expression, or digital communication71. Since the forms and meanings of political voice vary across institutional and cultural settings, identifying its social determinants—particularly retrospectively across countries—can be methodologically challenging. Nonetheless, the present cross-national analysis provides evidence that early-life environments are systematically associated with adult political voice. Consistent with prior scholarship, childhood experiences not only differ across cultures but also exert heterogeneous effects on political behavior within the same society.

A central contribution of this study concerns family relationships. The existing literature on parental influence typically emphasizes explicit political socialization—namely, the transmission of political attitudes and participation from politically engaged parents to their children6,9–11,14,17,28. Our findings extend this framework by demonstrating that the quality of parent–child relationships matters even in the absence of direct political induction. Across countries, individuals reporting positive relationships with either parent tended to exhibit higher levels of political voice than those reporting poor relationships. However, the strength and statistical significance of this association varied by national context. In some countries (e.g., Argentina, Australia, Egypt, Germany, Hong Kong, the United States, and the United Kingdom) the association was not statistically significant. In others, the relationship differed by parent: for example, paternal relationships were found not to be significant in Turkey and Tanzania, whereas maternal relationships were similarly insignificant in countries such as Israel, Nigeria, Brazil, and Sweden. Family structure and belonging also appear relevant; parental marital status and childhood experiences of feeling like an insider or outsider capture broader family dynamics that are likely to shape later civic expression. Taken together, these findings suggest that political voice may be rooted less in direct ideological transmission than in early relational security and perceived agency within the family.

These findings indicate that family influences on political expression extend beyond explicit political socialization. Parent–child relationships likely cultivate trust, self-efficacy, and perceptions of institutional legitimacy—psychological orientations that facilitate the articulation of political preferences. Other family characteristics, such as parental marital status and childhood belonging, appear to reflect related dimensions of early social integration and may operate through similar pathways.

Cohort patterns further illustrate the contextual nature of political voice. Overall, individuals aged 18–24 reported higher levels of political voice than most older cohorts, with the lowest levels among those aged 80 and above. Yet cross-national variation was substantial. In some countries—including Australia, Brazil, Mexico, Poland, Sweden, Tanzania, and the United States—older cohorts reported greater voice, while in Hong Kong and Japan specific mid-century cohorts showed higher engagement than younger adults. In several countries, generational differences were not statistically detectable.

These divergences likely reflect historically contingent formative experiences. Political transitions, economic crises, democratization processes, and social movements affect cohorts differently depending on when they occur in the life course. Generational cohorts are therefore not analytically equivalent across countries. Future research should investigate not only which political activities different cohorts undertake but also how intergenerational transmission—particularly parental influence6—shapes contemporary political expression.

More stable demographic characteristics showed weaker and more context-dependent associations with political voice. Childhood religion and race/ethnicity were not significant predictors in most countries reported in Tables S1b-S22b, although notable exceptions emerged. Ethnic minorities tend to face marginalization and lower engagement82, although sometimes they may organize as a community. Similarly, in some contexts, minority religion can also form a strong community solidarity that can foster political voice, although sometimes it might also be marginalized. These patterns underscore that social identities may influence political expression through their social meaning rather than their intrinsic characteristics. Religious participation, for example, may provide opportunities for electoral participation43,44, civic engagement46,47, affective bonding, and the development of trust—resources that can translate into political participation45. Historical evidence shows that religious institutions can mobilize electoral and civic participation, especially among marginalized populations. Thus, the impact of identity categories may depend on institutional context, group mobilization, and social inclusion.

Two findings were particularly unexpected: childhood abuse and feelings of being an outsider were, on average, associated with slightly higher political voice. In several countries—including Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Japan, Kenya, Mexico, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States—individuals reporting such experiences expressed more political voice than their counterparts. Specifically, respondents reporting childhood abuse exhibited 1.04 times higher political voice than those without such experiences. Likewise, feeling like an outsider during childhood was also associated with slightly higher political voice (1.02). One interpretation is that adversity can generate political motivation when societies provide avenues for advocacy, redress, or collective mobilization; individuals who experience marginalization may become especially likely to assert rights and demand recognition.

However, this relationship was not universal. In Egypt, Nigeria, the Philippines, Poland, and Spain childhood abuse was associated with lower political voice, and in some contexts outsider status also predicted reduced voice. These findings align with research linking early adversity to poorer mental health and emotional stability, which may inhibit civic participation13–16. The contrasting patterns suggest that institutional support structures and cultural narratives about victimhood and empowerment may influence whether adversity suppresses or stimulates political expression.

There are some limitations to this study that warrant caution. First, the data are observational and cross-sectional with retrospective reporting of childhood experiences; therefore, causal inference is not possible. Second, the measure of “having a say in government” may have been interpreted differently across political systems, potentially introducing measurement heterogeneity. Third, although a wide range of childhood exposures was included, and subsequent use of eigen-values to test for robustness of data, unobserved factors may remain. Fourth, despite the literature that links political ideology and behavior to personality traits, this study does not adjust for the potential effects of genetics in shaping political voice83. Fifth, the simultaneous inclusion of childhood antecedents raised concerns for multicollinearity. While we attempted to mitigate it either by dichotomizing responses or omitting variables, it remains a potential concern particularly for highly correlated variables such as parental marital status and relationship quality. Sixth, although multiple imputations were used to address missing data in each country, this approach may not eliminate estimation bias entirely84,85. Finally, although retrospective reports are subject to recall bias, the magnitude of distortion required to invalidate the observed effects would need to be exceptionally large86.

Despite these limitations, the study offers several strengths. It analyzes a large, diverse sample spanning 22 countries and incorporates multiple dimensions of early-life experience (before 12 years of age)—including family relationships, socioeconomic conditions, health, belonging, migration, religious participation, and cohort membership. The random-effects meta-analytic method employed enabled comparison across heterogeneous contexts, and sensitivity analyses indicated that the findings are reasonably robust to unmeasured confounding. Overall, the results demonstrate that childhood environments are associated with adult political voice in at least some societal contexts. The findings imply that political expression is not solely a product of adult institutions but is also rooted in early social development. Since political voice is closely tied to democratic participation, human rights, and broader well-being, policies that foster supportive family environments, social inclusion, and opportunities for civic engagement during childhood may have long-term human flourishing effects.
