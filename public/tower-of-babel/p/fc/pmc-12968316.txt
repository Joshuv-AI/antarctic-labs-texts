ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe physician global assessment of lung disease (PGALD) is a recently proposed disease activity measure for patients with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis–associated lung disease (SJIA‐LD). This study evaluates the reliability and construct validity of the PGALD.MethodsFifty‐seven pediatric rheumatologists and pulmonologists with experience caring for children with SJIA‐LD were invited to rate 20 clinical vignettes using the PGALD, a 10‐point Likert scale (0 = inactive SJIA‐LD; 10 = highly active SJIA‐LD). Raters were also asked to rate a subset of eight repeat vignettes. Interrater and intrarater reliability were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), whereas SJIA‐LD features influencing PGALD ratings were assessed using univariate analysis.ResultsThe ICC for all raters was 0.68 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.54–0.82), indicating moderate to good interrater reliability. The retest ICC was 0.86 (95% CI 0.82–0.89), indicating good intrarater reliability. Factors associated with higher mean PGALD scores included the presence of crackles on auscultation (5.7 vs 2.9; P = 0.001), hypoxemia on pulse oximeter (5.6 vs 3.2; P = 0.01), current oxygen requirement (6.3 vs 3.5; P = 0.04), suggestive diagnostic imaging features (P ≤ 0.01), and three or more prescribed medications for SJIA‐LD (P ≤ 0.01). Pulmonary function measures demonstrated significant negative correlations with PGALD scores: forced vital capacity (r = −0.71; P = 0.01), total lung capacity (r = −0.92; P = 0.01), and lung diffusion capacity (r = −0.97; P = 0.0002). Only C‐reactive protein was weakly to moderately correlated with PGALD scores (r = 0.39; P = 0.09), whereas other laboratory markers (ferritin, interleukin 18, CXCL9, and sIL‐2R) were not significantly correlated.ConclusionsThe PGALD is a novel measure of SJIA‐LD activity. Its initial validation suggests acceptable construct validity and reliability. Additional studies are needed to assess its responsiveness to change over time.

INTRODUCTION

The Physician Global Assessment (PhGA) is an important tool for monitoring disease activity and aiding in the evaluation of patients with various rheumatic diseases. 1 , 2 , 3 It has predictive value, in concert with baseline variables, for joint damage, functional disability, and inability to achieve remission. 4 PhGA scores at the onset of rheumatic disease can predict disease trajectory five years after diagnosis and are valuable tools in trials of biologic and small‐molecule disease‐modifying therapies. 5 In juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), the PhGA is a critical part of multiple clinical and research tools, including the American College of Rheumatology response criteria, the Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score, and the Wallace criteria for clinically inactive disease. 6

The systemic form of JIA (SJIA) is a chronic disorder with autoinflammatory features characterized by dysregulation of the immune system, leading to widespread tissue inflammation and subsequent damage. 7 Although most commonly diagnosed in children under five years of age, SJIA can present at any age. 8 Symptoms at disease onset include daily persistent fevers, arthritis or arthralgias, evanescent rash, and other systemic features, including lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, serositis, and lung inflammation. Although lung disease in SJIA (SJIA‐LD) is a relatively rare manifestation, affecting 5% to 11% of patients, it is associated with a markedly increased mortality risk, ranging from 10% to 68%. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Additional complications, namely macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), also contribute significantly to the overall mortality associated with this condition. 8

With the growing recognition of lung disease as a severe and life‐threatening manifestation of SJIA, there is an unmet need for a clinical assessment tool to evaluate lung disease activity. This is particularly true given that lung disease activity can be discordant with other SJIA disease features. The physician global assessment of lung disease (PGALD) was recently developed (and is currently being used) as a measure of lung disease activity in a large longitudinal single‐center SJIA‐LD cohort and the multisite Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) SJIA‐LD cohort. 8 , 13 The PGALD is based on the well‐established PhGA framework and consists of a 10‐point Likert scale, where 0 = inactive SJIA‐LD and 10 = highly active SJIA‐LD. The PGALD reflects the clinician's overall impression of lung disease activity at each patient visit/encounter and is distinct from the PhGA, which reflects the clinician's overall impression of SJIA and MAS activity. The PGALD is essential for monitoring treat‐to‐target goals, conducting prospective lung disease studies, and establishing key endpoints for interventional clinical trials. However, a robust assessment of the psychometric properties of the PGALD is currently lacking. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the interrater and intrarater reliability and construct validity of the PGALD and to investigate clinical factors that influence raters’ scoring of the tool.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Cincinnati Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB# 2024‐1060). Before commencement of this study, we trialed the survey with six pediatric specialists experienced in using the PGALD in clinical practice and research. Their feedback and recommendations were incorporated into the design of the scoring platform, the development of the clinical vignettes, and selection of variables for analysis. Their input ensured that the study design reflected real‐world application. We then identified 57 pediatric rheumatologists and pulmonologists who had experience managing children with SJIA‐LD and/or familiarity with the PGALD tool. Identified practitioners were notified about the study via email. Participation was voluntary, and those who agreed to rate vignettes were given a unique link for access to the vignettes stored in REDCap. Raters were instructed to assign PGALD scores for all vignettes using a 10‐point Likert scale, where 0 = inactive SJIA‐LD and 10 = highly active SJIA‐LD. Raters who completed evaluations for all 20 vignettes in the first round were included in the interrater analysis. Three days later, these raters were invited to reevaluate a subset of eight vignettes. The second set of retest responses were used to assess the intrarater reliability of the PGALD.

Regarding nomenclature, SJIA, with its disease onset occurring during childhood, and adult‐onset Still disease are widely regarded as a single disease due to their comparable clinical and biologic features and striking similarities in gene expression profiles. 14 , 15 Consensus recommendations by the EULAR/Paediatric Rheumatology European Society advocate using the term Still disease for both entities. 16 This nomenclature has not yet been universally adopted; we use the term SJIA hereafter, acknowledging its interchangeability with the name Still disease.

To assess the interrater and intrarater reliability of the PGALD in SJIA‐LD, we created 20 unique SJIA‐LD clinical vignettes representing a wide spectrum of disease presentation and activity levels. Vignette creation was informed by input from a pilot trial (outlined earlier) and historical patient encounters. Importantly, no patient‐identifying information was included in their production. Each vignette provides a brief patient medical history, physical examination findings, a list of prescribed medications, laboratory test results, diagnostic imaging, pulmonary function measures with six‐minute walk tests, echocardiogram and right‐sided heart catheterization results, and biopsy data, when available (Supplementary Material).

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were used to evaluate the interrater and intrarater reliability of the PGALD. The ICC is a widely used index to evaluate the reliability of numerical or continuous measurements. 17 ICC estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using SPSS version 28 (SPSS Inc) based on a single measurement, consistency for interrater reliability and absolute‐agreement for intrarater reliability, two‐way mixed‐effects model. Based on the 95% CI of ICC estimates, values less than 0.5 indicate poor reliability, values between 0.5 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, values between 0.76 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and values greater than 0.9 indicate excellent reliability. 17 The construct validity of the PGALD was evaluated by analyzing how well PGALD scores correlated with other measures of lung disease activity‐including signs/symptoms, pulmonary function measures, and computed chest tomography results. P values ≤0.05 and correlation coefficients of r ≥ 0.50 indicated significant results and at least moderate correlations. The PGALD is not intended as an assessment tool for SJIA (without lung disease), for MAS, or for diagnosis of lung disease.

To assess score‐influencing variables within clinical vignettes, Student's t‐tests, analysis of variance, and Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. Variables included in the analysis included the presence of MAS at diagnosis or current MAS, soluble interleukin‐2 (IL‐2) receptor, IL‐18, ferritin, C‐reactive protein (CRP), crackles on auscultation, clubbing on examination, hypoxemia (defined as oxygen saturation less than 90%) on pulse oximeter, pulmonary function tests [PFTs]), (including forced vital capacity [FVC], total lung capacity [TLC], lung diffusion capacity [DLco]), chest computed tomography (CT) features (including ground glass opacities [GGO], honeycombing or peripheral reticulations [fibrotic features], pleural or septal thickening), and the number of prescribed medications for SJIA and lung disease (from one to five).

RESULTS

Physician raters

We contacted 57 pediatric subspecialty providers for participation in this study: 51 pediatric rheumatologists and 6 pediatric pulmonologists. Of those contacted, 40 physicians agreed to participate, and 38 completed round 1. The overall response rate was 66.7%, with 72.5% participation by pediatric rheumatologists (Figure 1). Raters who only completed round 1 were included in the interrater analysis. Thirty‐one raters (81.5%) also completed round 2 and were included in the intrarater analysis. Notably, all 31 raters who completed both rounds were pediatric rheumatologists. Background details about the physician raters who completed at least one round are shown in Table 1. Most participants were female (74%), pediatric rheumatologists (95%), and from academic centers across the United States (100%). Twenty‐eight (74%) of the raters had over 10 years of experience, and 24 (63%) reported caring for at least three patients with SJIA‐LD. The distribution of the PGALD scores for each vignette is shown in Figure 2. The spread of rater responses (minimum to maximum) for vignettes across all questions averaged 5.65, with an interquartile range (Q1–Q3) of 2.02.

Interrater and intrarater reliability of the PGALD

Interrater and intrarater reliability results across different rater experience levels are shown in Table 2. Thirty‐eight raters completed the initial round of 20 vignettes, and 31 raters completed round 2. ICC scores for both rounds ranged from 0.583 to 0.917, with a median of 0.735 and an SD of 0.112. The interrater reliability of the PGALD was 0.678 (95% CI 0.543–0.819), indicating moderate to good interrater reliability. For the 31 raters who completed round 2 and rerated eight vignettes, the intrarater reliability of the PGALD, as measured by the ICC, was 0.863 (95% CI 0.823–0.893), indicating good intrarater reliability. ICC scores were largely consistent regardless of the raters’ years of experience or the number of patients they managed. Raters with >10 years of experience (n = 28, 74%) had a slightly higher interrater and intrarater reliability than raters with <10 years of experience (n = 10, 26%) (ICC: 0.685 vs 0.642 and 0.872 vs 0.842, respectively), but this was not significantly different. For raters with <10 years of experience, those who reported caring for only one to two patients with SJIA‐LD (n = 4) tended to have lower interrater and intrarater reliability than those who reported caring for three or more patients (n = 6) (ICC 0.583 vs 0.685 and ICC 0.773 vs 0.917, respectively), but this was also not significantly different.

Clinical and diagnostic factors associated with PGALD scores

To identify factors influencing PGALD mean rating scores, we analyzed symptom frequencies, prescribed medications, laboratory test results, and other diagnostic studies from the 20 vignettes (Table 3). Higher mean PGALD scores were associated with the presence of crackles on auscultation (5.7 vs 2.9, P = 0.001) and hypoxemia on pulse oximetry (5.6 vs 3.2, P = 0.01), as well as the use of supplemental oxygen (6.3 vs 3.5, P = 0.048). CT features associated with higher mean PGALD scores included GGO (5.1 vs 2.8, P = 0.004); fibrotic features, such as honeycombing or peripheral reticulations (5.0 vs 3.0, P = 0.02); and pleural or septal thickening (5.0 vs 2.6, P = 0.002). The number of medications prescribed for SJIA and lung disease was also associated with higher mean PGALD scores. Differences were significant when comparing one medication to three (0.3 vs 4.0, P = 0.01), four (0.3 vs 4.7, P = 0.004), or five medications (0.3 vs 7.1, P = 0.003), but not when comparing one to two medications (0.3 vs 2.6, P = 0.21) (Table 3, Figure 3). All pulmonary function measurements demonstrated significant negative correlations with PGALD scores‐FVC < 80% (r = −0.71, P = 0.01), TLC < 80% (r = −0.92, P = 0.01), and DLco < 80% (r = −0.97, P = 0.0002) (Table 3, Figure 3). There was no statistically significant difference in mean PGALD scores when considering a history of or current MAS (4.2 vs 3.0, P = 0.18, and 4.0 vs 3.6, P = 0.66, respectively). When laboratory tests were considered (based on clinically established cutoffs), elevation of CRP and ferritin levels was associated with higher mean PGALD scores (5.2 vs 2.9, P = 0.006 and 4.7 vs 2.9, P = 0.04). However, actual CRP and ferritin values were not significantly associated with mean PGALD scores (P = 0.09 and P = 0.78, respectively) despite the moderate correlation for CRP (r = 0.39). The presence of an elevated IL‐18, sIL‐2r, or CXCL9 level did not correlate with differences in mean PGALD scores (Table 3).

Reliability of PGALD with pediatric pulmonologists

Acknowledging the critical role of pulmonologists in the management of SJIA‐LD, we performed a second survey to assess the reliability of the PGALD among these specialists. Twenty‐one additional academic pediatric pulmonologists were invited to participate in the study; 15 pulmonologists completed the first round of 20 clinical vignettes, yielding a 71.4% response rate, and 7 pediatric pulmonologists completed the second round of vignettes.

The interrater reliability of the PGALD as measured by the ICC was 0.778 (95% CI 0.659–0.884) for pediatric pulmonologists, indicating moderate to good interrater reliability. Notably, this was not significantly different from that seen by rheumatologists. The intrarater reliability of the PGALD as measured by the ICC was 0.890 (95% CI 0.711–0.949), indicating moderate to excellent intrarater reliability among pulmonologist raters. This was slightly higher than that of rheumatologist raters (0.863 [95% CI 0.823–0.893]) but was not significantly different.

DISCUSSION

Physician global assessments in the form of visual analog scales are important clinical practice and research measurement tools reflective of clinicians’ judgment of overall disease activity. In this study, we evaluated different measurement properties of the PGALD, a novel measure of disease activity, for use in SJIA‐LD, including its reliability and construct validity. The PGALD demonstrated moderate to good interrater and intrarater reliability and high construct validity among raters with varying levels of experience from academic institutions across the United States. In addition, to generalize the PGALD beyond pediatric rheumatologists, we specifically surveyed pediatric pulmonologists and found similar reliability.

Although the PhGA is widely used and critically important, there remains a lack of guidance and standardization in its application, contributing to heterogeneity in reliability studies. 18 , 19 , 20 Recent publications have begun to address this issue. Notably, an international task force developed consensus‐based recommendations for assessing disease activity in SJIA using the PhGA. 21 The task force recommended scoring PhGAs at the end of each clinical visit with a focus on current disease activity rather than chronic damage. They further advised incorporating the presence of active MAS and/or interstitial lung disease into traditionally scored SJIA features such as fever, serositis, and rash. These recommendations are expected to improve consistency in PhGA obtainment; however, there is a notable lack of specific guidance on the weighing and scoring of different clinical features. A similar consensus‐based framework could be developed for the PGALD in future studies, with the results herein used to provide such guidance in scoring.

Although the raters in this study were generally familiar with the PhGA, they received no specific instructions beyond rating vignettes based solely on lung disease activity, excluding underlying SJIA or MAS activity. Despite the lack of formal training before study commencement, both interrater and intrarater reliability results were strong and similar across a heterogeneous group of providers. Other studies have similarly explored how variables such as clinician training and experience affect the scoring of rheumatic disease activity tools. A study by Askanase et al compared Lupus Foundation of America‐Rapid Evaluation of Activity in Lupus (LFA‐REAL) scores between investigators formally trained in systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity instruments versus clinicians without any training. 22 They found a high degree of agreement in ICCs ranging from 0.68 to 0.86 (P < 0.001 for all ICCs) despite training differences. 22 Conversely, a study by Turk and Pope found significant differences in rheumatoid arthritis PhGA scores based on rater age (<35 years as the reference group) and experience (<200 patients/year as the reference group), though they found no difference based on practice setting (academic vs community). 23 In our hands, we found no significant difference in reliability between specialists (rheumatology or pulmonary). There was also no significant change in the results when stratified by years in practice or number of patients with SJIA‐LD seen. This supports the broad utility of the PGALD measure regardless of subspecialty or experience level.

Regarding validity, we focused on the construct validity of the PGALD which examines how well an instrument relates to other instruments measuring the same concept‐in this case, lung disease activity. Construct validity is typically demonstrated when instruments measuring similar constructs show correlation coefficients of r ≥ 0.50. 24 , 25 Our analysis demonstrated that raters primarily focused on lung disease activity when scoring the PGALD and not systemic inflammation, SJIA, or MAS activity. This is supported by the strong negative correlation with pulmonary function measurements, as well as other lung disease‐specific factors, including hypoxemia on pulse oximetry, crackles on auscultation, supplemental oxygen requirement, suggestive imaging features, and the number of prescribed medications for lung disease in SJIA. Equally important, is the lack of correlation between PGALD scores and markers typically elevated in SJIA and/or MAS (independent of lung disease), including sIL‐2r, IL‐18, CXCL9, and ferritin levels (r = 0.054, 0.001, −0.181, and 0.070, respectively).

These findings further support the distinct roles of the PhGA and PGALD in capturing different clinical domains in SJIA. The PhGA is expected to correlate with systemic markers of inflammation elevated in SJIA or MAS, whereas the PGALD is an assessment of lung disease activity and should not correlate with these same markers. Consistent with our data, the multisite CARRA SJIA‐LD cohort study also found no significant correlation between the two measures, 10 , 13 further reinforcing their distinct roles in monitoring SJIA disease activity (PhGA) and lung disease activity (PGALD) in SJIA‐LD.

The strong construct validity, similar reliability results between different subspecialties, and notable lack of association with SJIA‐specific activity markers suggest potential utility for the PGALD in other pediatric and adult rheumatic diseases complicated by lung involvement (including systemic sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, overlap conditions, juvenile dermatomyositis, sarcoidosis, and others). Broader use of the PGALD in other rheumatic diseases would be similarly advantageous for clinical practice and research.

Overall, the PGALD shows great promise as a lung disease activity scoring tool. Future work should focus on assessing discriminant and criterion validity, establishing clinically relevant cutoffs, defining thresholds for clinically meaningful change, and evaluating application in other rheumatic diseases and adult populations. There is also a need to standardize its application and create consensus statements for scoring the PGALD to improve its reliability. Additionally, there needs to be clarification on the distinction between lung disease activity and chronic lung damage‐an important distinction for clinical trials.

This study has several limitations. Our overall response rate was relatively low (67%) even after reopening the study to include pediatric pulmonologists (68%), compared to the 80% typically recommended for the Delphi process. 26 The vignettes were not complete charts‐rather, they were short synopses provided by the authors (Supplementary Material). Given the static framework of this study, we were not able to evaluate every variable influencing raters’ scoring of the PGALD, such as pulmonary infections or serositis. In practice, distinguishing these entities from underlying chronic lung disease is extremely difficult and relies heavily on clinical judgment. Additionally, the study did not include international rheumatology or pulmonology specialists, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the small numbers in certain variable categories limited our ability to fully evaluate their influence on PGALD scoring.

In conclusion, the PGALD has acceptable reliability and construct validity as a measure of SJIA‐LD disease activity, with measurement properties that did not appear to be influenced by provider experience or specialty. The PGALD is an easily obtained measure of lung disease activity, with potential for use in other rheumatic diseases complicated by lung involvement. These results are valuable in addressing the critical need for a reliable clinical tool for evaluating lung disease activity in routine practice and prospective SJIA‐LD studies distinct from the PhGA. Future efforts should aim to refine and standardize the PGALD to enhance its reliability and expand its applicability.
