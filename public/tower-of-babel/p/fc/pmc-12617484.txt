Trends, timing, and predictors of alcohol use disorder treatment among US adult cancer survivors
Authors: Jyun‐Heng Lai, Anton L V Avanceña, Minh H K Nguyen, Corwin M Zigler, Mary M Velasquez, Christopher R Frei, Michael Pignone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about the initiation of alcohol use disorder (AUD) treatments among cancer survivors, despite the unique harms that unhealthy alcohol use may have on this population. We assessed patterns, timing, and predictors of AUD treatment among cancer survivors.MethodsWe used commercial claims data on US adult cancer survivors from 2011 to 2021 in this retrospective cohort study. We included individuals with a new AUD diagnosis and continuous insurance enrollment. We measured initiation and timing of Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved (naltrexone, acamprosate, and disulfiram), non‐FDA‐approved (gabapentin, topiramate, and baclofen), and investigational (ondansetron and varenicline) medications for AUD (MAUDs), psychosocial therapies, and rehabilitation for AUD. We employed survival analysis to identify sociodemographic and clinical predictors of time to AUD treatment initiation.ResultsAmong 6,682,292 cancer survivors, 44,081 individuals newly diagnosed with AUD met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 14.7% initiated FDA‐approved MAUDs (3.0%), psychosocial therapy (13.0%), or both (1.3%). The median time to initiate FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy was 28 days. Overall, 6.3% and 10.4% of cancer survivors, respectively, initiated non‐FDA‐approved and investigational MAUDs. Age, use of antineoplastic agents, mental disorders, and comorbidity severity are potential determinants with varying effects on AUD treatment initiation.ConclusionLess than 15% of cancer survivors initiated recommended MAUDs or psychosocial therapy in the year following their new AUD diagnoses, and those who did initiate treatment did so nearly a month after receiving AUD diagnoses. Interventions are warranted to enhance treatment initiation among cancer survivors with AUD to reduce alcohol‐related harms.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, AUD treatment, cancer survivorship, cohort study, substance use disorder

Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) among cancer survivors, or individuals with a history of cancer, may lead to negative outcomes, including higher hospital readmission risk and increased healthcare costs, cancer recurrence, new malignancies, and mortality [1, 2, 3]. Studies suggest that as much as 5% of cancer survivors meet the criteria for, or have been diagnosed with, AUD, though a significantly higher proportion engage in unhealthy alcohol use [4, 5, 6, 7]. As cancer survivorship improves over time with advances in cancer treatment, the number of cancer survivors with AUD and other substance use disorders (SUDs) is likely to rise, highlighting the importance of AUD screening, diagnosis, and treatment.

Several pharmacotherapies and psychosocial therapies are used to treat AUD. In the United States, three medications for AUD (MAUDs)—disulfiram, naltrexone (oral and injectable forms), and acamprosate—can reduce alcohol consumption and promote abstinence and are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for AUD treatment [8]. Psychosocial therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and motivational enhancement therapy (MET), are also recommended interventions for AUD and are often delivered with MAUDs [9]. Since the FDA approval of injectable naltrexone in 2006, no new MAUDs have become available, limiting the treatment options of individuals for whom FDA‐approved MAUDs fail or whose comorbidities preclude them from receiving certain medications. For example, naltrexone is contraindicated for people who are taking opioids, and disulfiram, which has low tolerability and is hepatotoxic, is not recommended for people with liver diseases [10, 11, 12]. Thus, treatment guidelines have increasingly recommended the off‐label use of other medications to treat AUD based on research demonstrating their effectiveness. The US Veterans Administration recommends topiramate, an antiseizure medication, as a first‐line MAUD, along with FDA‐approved MAUDs [13]. Another non‐FDA‐approved MAUD is baclofen, which is recommended for individuals with alcohol‐associated liver disease [10, 14]. Other investigational medications, like ondansetron and varenicline, that are commonly prescribed to cancer survivors for other indications, have also been shown to be effective in reducing alcohol use and may concurrently benefit a patient with AUD and other conditions [15].

Despite the availability and variety of guideline‐recommended AUD treatments, their use remains low. According to the 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), only 7.8% of US adults with AUD receive any treatment, including 2% who received MAUDs [16]. Other studies among the general population have also reported low rates of FDA‐approved MAUD use, ranging from 1.3% to 6.0% [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. By contrast, research on the use of non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs and psychosocial therapies is scant, with most studies pooling all AUD treatments together or including only a specific type of treatment, such as topiramate or counseling [19, 22, 23]. Even less understood is the timing of treatment initiation among individuals diagnosed with AUD, which is an important component of AUD treatment quality and has been associated with AUD‐related outcomes [24]. Prior studies have also focused on the general population, limiting our understanding of AUD treatment access and use among cancer survivors who face unique consequences associated with unhealthy alcohol use [5, 25, 26].

To fill these gaps, we sought to assess trends, timing, and potential predictors of AUD treatment initiation among cancer survivors who were newly diagnosed with AUD. Our analysis differs from previous studies on the general population [17, 19, 22, 23, 27] by focusing on a broad set of AUD treatments and measuring the time between diagnosis and treatment initiation. We also explored factors associated with time to AUD treatment initiation. Findings from this study can inform cancer survivorship guidelines and future research on AUD treatments in cancer survivors.

Methods

Study design and participants

Following STROBE reporting guidelines [28], we conducted a multi‐year retrospective cohort study using Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims and Medicare Supplemental data from 2011 to 2021. Each year of MarketScan data includes demographic information, diagnoses using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10) codes, and records for inpatient and outpatient services and prescriptions for a convenience sample of ≥25 million individuals with employer‐sponsored private health insurance. This study was exempt from full review by The University of Texas Institutional Review Board (STUDY00004975).

In each year of our study period (2012–2020), we identified cancer survivors or individuals with a history of ≥1 malignant neoplasm diagnosis (Table S1 in Supporting Information 1). Cancer survivors were further included if they were ≥18 years old, had no missing values on key variables, and received a new AUD diagnosis on or after the date of their cancer diagnosis. We defined new AUD diagnoses based on the absence of any AUD diagnoses or AUD‐related claims in the year preceding the date of an individual's first AUD diagnosis on record (index date). Eligible patients were limited to individuals with ≥6 months of continuous insurance enrollment before and after their index date (Fig. S1 in Supporting Information 1). This approach, commonly used in real‐world studies, ensures that individuals meet consistent inclusion criteria and have adequate observation time to identify baseline covariates and multiple outcomes of interest, especially minimizing the risk of underestimating events due to loss of follow‐up [29].

Measures

Our outcome was the first AUD treatment initiated by cancer survivors within the year following their index date. We limited our analysis to new users, defined as cancer survivors who had no claims for the AUD treatment that they would eventually receive in the 180 days preceding their index date. We included 4 types of AUD treatments in our main analysis (Table S2 in Supporting Information 1). We assessed initiation of FDA‐approved MAUDs (acamprosate, disulfiram, and oral and injectable naltrexone) and non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs (gabapentin, topiramate, and baclofen), which clinical practice guidelines in the United States have recommended as first or second line pharmacotherapies for AUD (Table S2 in Supporting Information 1) [10, 13]. We identified initiation of MAUDs using national drug codes in outpatient pharmacy claims (Supporting Information 2). The third AUD treatment was psychosocial therapies, which include CBT, MET, and other types of behavioral counseling, which we identified using procedure codes that have been used in previous studies [19, 22, 30]. Because we are focused on the first AUD treatment received by cancer survivors after an AUD diagnosis, we excluded brief interventions offered in the context of screening, brief intervention, and referral to treatment (SBIRT) from the primary analysis. Brief interventions in SBIRT are short (∼15 min) counseling sessions intended as a preventive or early intervention for individuals with mild AUD and not recurring therapy [31, 32]. Finally, we assessed receipt of alcohol‐related rehabilitation, which is residential or inpatient rehabilitation and detoxification (Table S3 in Supporting Information 1).

In supplementary analysis, we examined the initiation of two investigational MAUDs, ondansetron and varenicline, which are medications that have not been formally recommended for AUD but have some evidence supporting their use in AUD [33, 34].

For covariates, we identified sociodemographic and clinical characteristics in our pooled study cohort. Sociodemographic factors, which were assessed on the index date, included patient age, sex, region of residence, metropolitan statistical area (MSA), type of insurance coverage, type of healthcare plan, and employment status. We calculated area‐level median family income (in 2023 US$) for those who resided in MSAs using data from the American Community Survey of the US Census Bureau [35]. MarketScan does not contain data on race and ethnicity.

Clinical characteristics and use of other medications were measured 1 year and 180 days before the index date, respectively. Healthcare resource use included the number of inpatient, emergency room, and outpatient visits and outpatient prescriptions. Oncology‐related covariates included history of metastatic cancer, use of antineoplastic agents (i.e., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and/or immunotherapy), hospice or palliative care visits, and use of full or partial opioids (Tables S1, S3, and S4 in Supporting Information 1). We also assessed physical and mental health comorbidities and other SUDs using major diagnosis codes and ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes in inpatient and outpatient claims (Tables S1 and S4 in Supporting Information 1). Additionally, we computed the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score to estimate the severity of comorbidities and identified the use of other outpatient medications (Table S4 in Supporting Information 1).

Statistical analysis

We examined time trends in AUD treatment initiation across all treatments and separately for each treatment. We combined unique cancer survivors across years and calculated the overall AUD treatment initiation rate during the study period. We also calculated the median (interquartile range [IQR]) and mean (standard deviation [SD]) time from the index date to AUD treatment initiation. For each outcome, we estimated results for all cancer survivors and three clinically relevant subgroups using diagnosis and service codes: those who received (1) antineoplastic therapy, (2) diagnosis of metastatic cancer, and (3) hospice or palliative care.

We used descriptive statistics, independent t‐tests, and chi‐square tests to compare the characteristics of our overall cohort and subgroups and to calculate the mean and median time to AUD treatment initiation. We employed multivariable Cox proportional hazard models to identify potential predictors associated with time to first use each AUD treatment. In sensitivity analyses, we applied Lasso Cox models that identified the set of covariates that generated the best fitting models. We used Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing, highlighting only results with p‐values near 0.001 [36]. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

The total number of cancer survivors with newly diagnosed AUD ranged from 6622 (of 2,528,303 or 0.26%) in 2012 to 4166 (of 1,004,315 or 0.41%) in 2020, giving a total of 44,081 unique individuals in the pooled cohort who met the inclusion criteria (Table S5 in Supporting Information 1). Overall, 5.24% (2308) of these individuals received ≥2 AUD diagnoses in the study period. The mean (SD) age was 60.6 (12.8) years, and 65.3% of patients were male (Table 1).

Main analysis

From 2012 to 2020, 14.7% of all cancer survivors with AUD initiated FDA‐approved MAUDs (3.0%), psychosocial therapy (13.0%), or both (1.3%) in the year following their AUD diagnosis (Fig. 1 and Tables S6 and S8 in Supporting Information 1). Among those who initiated FDA‐approved MAUDs, the most common was naltrexone (2.3%), while <1% of cancer survivors with AUD received acamprosate and disulfiram (Fig. 1 and Table S7 in Supporting Information 1). Oral naltrexone (2.1%) was more common than the injectable form (0.1%). Overall, 6.3% of cancer survivors initiated non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs, with the most common being gabapentin (4.8%; Fig. 1 and Table S7 in Supporting Information 1). Alcohol rehabilitation was used by 0.7% of cancer survivors with AUD (Fig. 1 and Table S8 in Supporting Information 1).

Between 2012 and 2020, initiation of FDA‐approved MAUD and/or psychosocial therapy increased slightly from 13.6% to 15.3% (Fig. 2 and Table S6 in Supporting Information 1). Use of FDA‐approved MAUDs increased from 0.7% to 5.1%, with naltrexone having the largest increase (0.7%–3.9%) compared with disulfiram (0.02%–0.9%) and acamprosate (0%–0.8%; Fig. S2 and Table S7 in Supporting Information 1).

Initiation of non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs nearly doubled during the study period (3.9%–7.4%; Fig. S2 and Table S7 in Supporting Information 1). Gabapentin initiation increased slightly from 3.2% to 5.7%, whereas the initiation of topiramate and baclofen remained stable at 1.0% or lower. Initiation of psychosocial therapies remained steady between 11% and 13% over the 9‐year study period, whereas alcohol rehabilitation showed only a modest change (0.6%–1.1%; Fig. 2 and Table S8 in Supporting Information 1).

The mean (SD) and median (IQR) time to initiation of an FDA‐approved MAUD and/or psychosocial therapy was 74 (96) and 28 (105) days, respectively (Fig. 3 and Table S9 in Supporting Information 1). The median (SD) time to initiate any psychosocial therapy was shorter than FDA‐approved MAUDs (30 [106] days vs. 34 [140] days). The multivariable analysis (Fig. 4 and Table S13 in Supporting Information 1) found that use of antineoplastic agents was associated with earlier initiation of an FDA‐approved MAUD (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.38; 95% CI 1.13–1.68; p = 0.001). A co‐occurring depression diagnosis was associated with a higher hazard of initiating an FDA‐approved MAUD (aHR 1.58; 95% CI 1.39–1.80; p < 0.0001) and psychosocial therapy (aHR 1.78; 95% CI 1.67–1.90; p < 0.0001). A similar pattern was observed for prior use of antidepressants (both p < 0.0001). Selected SUD diagnoses, such as opioid use disorder (aHR 1.31; 95% CI 1.15–1.50; p < 0.0001), cocaine use disorder (aHR 1.35; 95% CI 1.13–1.61; p = 0.001), and sedative, hypnotic, or anxiolytic‐related disorders (aHR 1.37; 95% CI 1.16–1.63; p < 0.0001) were associated with initiation of psychosocial therapies. In contrast, older age and supplementary Medicare coverage were associated with a longer time to initiation of FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy (p < 0.0001). Past chemotherapy use was associated with delayed initiation of an FDA‐approved MAUD (aHR 0.52; 95% CI 0.39–0.70; p < 0.0001). Higher CCI scores were associated with a lower hazard of initiating FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy (both p < 0.0001). Age and receipt of central nervous system medications were consistently associated with initiating FDA‐approved MAUD, non‐FDA‐approved MAUD, and psychosocial therapy. Lasso Cox regression models corroborated our multivariable analyses (Fig. S5 and Table S14 in Supporting Information 1).

Subgroup analyses

Between cancer survivors who received and did not receive antineoplastic therapy in the past 180 days, the initiation of FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy was not different (15.0% vs. 14.7%; p = 0.4978; Fig. S3 and Table S10 in Supporting Information 1). Cancer survivors receiving antineoplastic therapy had higher use of non‐FDA‐approved MAUD compared with those who did not (7.9% vs. 6%; p < 0.0001). Among cancer survivors with a history of metastatic cancer, the initiation of FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy was lower compared to those without metastatic cancer (11.7% vs. 15.1%; p < 0.0001; Fig. S3 and Table S11 in Supporting Information 1). Use of FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy was comparable between cancer survivors with and without hospice or palliative care visits (14.5% vs. 14.7%; p = 0.8057; Fig. S3 and Table S12 in Supporting Information 1). However, non‐FDA‐approved MAUD use was higher among those who received hospice or palliative care (10.4% vs. 6.1%; p < 0.0001).

We found that the rate of initiating FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy was different between cancer survivors with and without a history of metastatic cancer (Kaplan–Meier log‐rank test: p < 0.0001; Fig. S4 in Supporting Information 1) but not in the other cancer survivor subgroups.

Supplementary analysis

Overall, 10.4% of cancer survivors initiated investigational MAUDs (Table S7 in Supporting Information 1). The most common investigational MAUD was ondansetron (9.7%), whose use doubled from 5.6% to 11.8% between 2012 and 2020 (Fig. S2 and Table S7 in Supporting Information 1). The median (SD) time to initiation of investigational MAUDs was 63 (161) days (Table S9 in Supporting Information 1). Multivariable analysis found that nicotine use disorder diagnosis was associated with a higher hazard of initiating investigational MAUDs (aHR 1.37, 95% CI 1.28–1.46; p < 0.0001; Table S13 in Supporting Information 1), which comprises varenicline, an FDA‐approved treatment for tobacco cessation. As with other AUD treatments, age and receipt of central nervous system medications were associated with initiating investigational MAUDs. Use of SBIRT among cancer survivors in our study sample was only about 0.5% throughout the study period (Table S8 in Supporting Information 1).

Discussion

This cohort study found that less than 15% of cancer survivors initiated an FDA‐approved MAUD and/or psychosocial therapy within a year of their AUD diagnosis, with a median delay of about 28 days. Initiation of non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs was at 6.3%, which was twice the proportion of cancer survivors who initiated FDA‐approved MAUDs (3%). Overall, the initiation of FDA‐approved and non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs increased significantly from 2012 to 2020, particularly the use of naltrexone and gabapentin, whereas psychosocial therapy use was largely unchanged (11%–13%).

Nationally, only 2% of all US adults with AUD receive any MAUDs [16]. Higher AUD treatment rates have been reported among commercially insured US individuals (where 12.9% received FDA‐approved MAUDs in 2018) [23] and Medicaid beneficiaries (where 8.1% received FDA‐approved MAUDs and 19.0% received psychosocial therapy in 2019) [19]. Among veterans, AUD treatment was estimated at 13.8% in 2019–2020, dropping to 4.3% during the initial months of the COVID‐19 pandemic [37]. A US study also revealed that only 1.3% of Medicare enrollees who were hospitalized for AUD initiated any FDA‐approved MAUD within 30 days after discharge [17]. Although previous studies have evaluated the initiation and predictors of AUD treatment in different populations, few have focused on cancer survivors [26]. A recent analysis of NSDUH data (2015–2022) found that individuals with unhealthy alcohol use had a marginally higher probability of receiving alcohol screening in the past year compared to people without a history of cancer (40.9% vs. 39.5%). However, both groups had low rates of brief intervention (7.9% vs. 9.3%) and AUD treatment (2.1% vs. 2.4%) [25].

In our study, only around 0.5% of cancer survivors received SBIRT, and 14.7% initiated AUD treatment. The low levels of AUD treatment initiation among cancer survivors may be attributed to several reasons. At the individual patient level, several factors may influence AUD treatment‐seeking and initiation, including beliefs that the condition will resolve on its own, perceptions about the side effects of treatment, and negative feelings related to stigma [38, 39]. Previous studies among the general population have found that a co‐occurring mental or behavioral health condition is associated with initiating AUD treatment [18, 19, 21, 23, 40], which we also documented in our analysis. Cost of care is a commonly reported barrier to AUD treatment [38, 41], though this study and others [19, 22, 23, 37] have found equally low rates of treatment initiation among individuals with AUD who have health insurance, implying that coverage is not the most influential factor. Additionally, most MAUDs, like oral naltrexone, are generally inexpensive.

Health service‐level factors are possibly greater predictors of low AUD treatment and delays [41]. For example, complexities in the care pathway can affect the timeliness of patient follow‐up after an AUD diagnosis [17, 42]. Despite the emphasis of cancer survivorship guidelines on patients’ mental and psychosocial health and lifestyle‐related behaviors, including alcohol misuse [43, 44], providers caring for cancer survivors may be more focused on monitoring cancer recurrence or progression than addressing possible SUDs [45]. There is also variability in the colocation of mental health and chemical dependency services within cancer centers, with teaching status, hospital system affiliation, bed size, and government and/or nonprofit status being positive predictors of the availability of these services [46]. Another health service‐level factor is provider knowledge; research has consistently shown that providers (like their patients) lack knowledge about AUD treatment options [38, 47]. Because prescribing MAUDs is positively associated with a provider's knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about their effectiveness [48, 49, 50, 51], provider education may help improve rates of treatment initiation.

We observed a modest increase—from 13.7% to 15.3%—in the initiation of FDA‐approved MAUDs and/or psychosocial therapy over the 9 years of our analysis. With psychosocial therapy remaining steady during this period, the increase is primarily attributable to the growth in FDA‐approved MAUDs (from 0.7% to 5.1%), particularly naltrexone (from 0.7% to 3.9%). This increase, which has been documented in at least one other study with commercially insured adults with AUD [23], may be due to several factors, including the release of guidelines for cancer prevention and care, such as the American Cancer Society nutrition and physical activity guideline for cancer survivors (2012), the American Society of Clinical Oncology statement on alcohol and cancer (2018), and cancer survivorship guidelines for colorectal (2015), head and neck (2016), and breast (2016) cancers [52, 53, 54, 55, 56]. These guidelines, which influence healthcare quality metrics and cancer survivorship care [57, 58, 59], all recommend counseling cancer survivors about abstaining from or limiting their alcohol use. In 2018, the US Preventive Services Task Force released its recommendation for SBIRT, which coincided with investments by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration to scale up SBIRT in various healthcare settings [32, 60]. However, given the extremely low treatment rates in the baseline year (2012) and the small absolute changes in AUD treatment over time (e.g., an overall 3% increase in naltrexone use), the reasons behind the increased are difficult to ascertain. Additionally, although cancer survivorship guidelines encourage cancer survivors to reduce their alcohol consumption, none of them has directly endorsed SBIRT or AUD treatment, despite the unique risks that unhealthy alcohol use may pose for this population. Research is needed to understand the factors that influence AUD treatment rates among cancer survivors.

Clinical guidelines for managing AUD in the United States and other countries have increasingly recommended the use of non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs under specific conditions, especially when first‐line treatments fail [10, 13, 61]. Our study found that cancer survivors with AUD were more likely to initiate non‐FDA‐approved and investigational MAUDs than FDA‐approved MAUDs. This finding is likely explained by the other indications for these medications, which are common conditions in cancer survivors. For example, gabapentin, among the ten most prescribed medications in the United States, is widely used off‐label to mitigate pain, including neuropathic pain and pain associated with cancer and its treatment [62]. Similarly, ondansetron is an anti‐nausea medication that is FDA‐approved for treating nausea and vomiting caused by treatment for cancer [63].

Although we cannot ascertain whether these medications were prescribed for AUD treatment specifically, our findings highlight the opportunity to examine the effects of these non‐FDA‐approved medications in cancer survivors with AUD given proper follow‐up care. These “dual‐purpose” medications may concurrently treat AUD and other conditions and/or symptoms in cancer survivors without increasing polypharmacy, which is associated with increased adverse drug events, poor adherence, and higher healthcare costs [64]. Non‐FDA‐approved and investigational MAUDs may also serve as alternative treatments for cancer survivors with AUD whose comorbidities and/or medications make them ineligible for FDA‐approved MAUDs. For example, we found that nearly 29% of our sample were taking opioid medications, a contraindication for naltrexone [12]. Similarly, 63% of cancer survivors in our study had circulatory system diseases, 17% had liver and pancreatic diseases, and 28% had kidney diseases, which could make them ineligible to receive disulfiram [11] and acamprosate [65], respectively. Depending on their treatment history or disease severity, cancer survivors with complex medical needs may have limited pharmacological and psychosocial treatment options, including inpatient or residential rehabilitation. For example, we found that patients with a history of metastatic cancer had a lower rate of initiating FDA‐approved MAUD or psychosocial therapy. However, use of non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs was higher for cancer survivors who used antineoplastic agents or had hospice or palliative care visits in the past. In general, clinicians should work with their patients to identify the most appropriate and acceptable AUD treatment among the evidence‐based options [66, 67].

We found that psychosocial therapies were the most common type of AUD treatment received by cancer survivors, corroborating previous research in the general population [19]. Following policy changes during the COVID‐19 pandemic [68], telemedicine became the primary mode of delivering individual psychosocial therapy for AUD [37], and it led to an increase in psychosocial therapy use beyond levels in the pre‐pandemic period [69]. The higher prevalence of psychosocial therapy may also reflect the medical complexity of cancer survivors whose comorbidities may preclude them from receiving certain MAUDs. Future studies should seek to understand the experience of cancer survivors with telemedicine‐based psychosocial therapy to improve uptake and retention.

Timely AUD treatment has been associated with outcomes such as reduced all‐cause mortality and hospital readmission risk [70]; thus, minimizing treatment delays is emphasized by several healthcare quality standards [24, 71]. Our study, which is the only one of a few to investigate time to AUD treatment initiation [17, 40, 72], found that half of cancer survivors with AUD who initiated treatment did so 28 days after they were newly diagnosed. Our results match data from the National Committee for Quality Assurance, which show that only 37%–42% of all newly diagnosed US patients with AUD initiate treatment within 14 days of diagnosis [24].

Limitations

Our study has limitations. First, our data represent a convenient sample of cancer survivors with employer‐sponsored private health insurance and may not be generalizable to other populations. Second, restricting our study cohort to individuals with continuous health insurance coverage may introduce selection bias. However, our previous analysis indicates that the overall results for AUD treatment initiation remain consistent when applying alternative approaches, such as treating disenrollment or loss of follow‐up as a censoring event (14.3% vs. 14.7%) [26]. Third, some important variables, such as race, ethnicity, education, severity of AUD, and cancer stage, were not available in the data but may be related to the initiation and timing of AUD treatments. For example, we found that different subgroups of cancer survivors varied in their initiation of AUD treatments. Fourth, some treatments, especially non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs, have other indications and may have been prescribed for conditions other than AUD. Our study suggests that the use of non‐FDA‐approved MAUDs is relatively common and can potentially benefit cancer survivors with AUD and other comorbidities for which these medications are indicated. Fifth, we could not assess the use of AUD treatments that were not reflected in claims data, including treatment sought outside of traditional healthcare settings, such as mutual help and other group‐based therapies (e.g., Alcoholics Anonymous) that are also effective in promoting abstinence [73]. Sixth, we defined initiation of AUD treatment based on at least one claim for MAUDs, psychosocial therapy, or inpatient rehabilitation. Future studies should assess the quality of AUD treatment, such as adherence to MAUDs, which previous studies have found to be low [74]. Finally, our analysis relies on diagnosis, procedure, and pharmaceutical codes, which are prone to misclassification and underreporting. For example, we were unable to determine the specific psychosocial therapies (e.g., CBT, MET) delivered to patients due to the general descriptions in service codes. Similarly, psychosocial therapies may have been recorded or billed erroneously, such as when brief alcohol counseling is coded as SBIRT [75, 76].

Conclusions

This retrospective cohort study found that less than 15% of US adult cancer survivors with newly diagnosed AUD received FDA‐approved MAUDs and psychosocial therapies, with treatment delays exceeding 2 months on average. Individual‐ and system‐level interventions are needed to enhance the use of AUD treatments among cancer survivors to mitigate alcohol‐related harms.
