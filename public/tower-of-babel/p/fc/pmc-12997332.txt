Extreme weather and sanitation in Bangladesh: a mixed-methods study of lived experiences of people with disabilities
Authors: Shahpara Nawaz, Jane Wilbur, Tasnia Alam Upoma, Kazy Farhat Tabassum, Arka Goshami, Bithy Podder, Jarin Akter, Mehedi Hasan, Lauren D'Mello-Guyett, Dewan Muhammad Shoaib, Doug Ruuska, Lena Morgon Banks, Sari Kovats, Shanquan Chen, Mahbub-Ul Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundClimate change exacerbates weather extremes, damaging sanitation infrastructure and undermining personal hygiene. Disasters disproportionately impact people with disabilities. This study explored how flooding and cyclones affect the sanitation experiences of people with disabilities in Bangladesh.MethodsThis mixed-methods study comprised a cross-sectional survey, in-depth interviews, and photovoice. Survey data was collected from 1021 people with disabilities and 909 without disabilities. Purposive sampling selected 28 people with disabilities and 16 caregivers for qualitative data collection. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, multi-level Poisson regression, and thematic analysis.FindingsPeople with disabilities had a modestly higher risk of experiencing sanitation damage by cyclones or floods compared to people without disabilities (adjusted risk ratio [aRR] 1.28, 95% CI 1.00–1.63). Among people with disabilities, those in the wealthiest quintile were 88% less likely to experience sanitation facility damage than those in the poorest quintile. Qualitative findings showed that muddy, inaccessible paths caused injuries to people with disabilities and their caregivers. Accessing alternative facilities during flooding was difficult for persons with disabilities, with some experiencing verbal abuse. Some reported resorting to open defecation. People with disabilities with incontinence often could not reach facilities in time, resulting in soiled clothing and bedding. Privacy concerns and reliance on caregivers led many, especially women, to restrict toileting, while both women and men adjusted food and water intake, increasing health risks.InterpretationFlooding and storms worsen sanitation challenges for people with disabilities and caregivers, increasing injury risk, dependence, and exposure to human waste. Climate-resilient sanitation must prioritise inclusive sanitation to protect dignity, health, and well-being, and prevent the deepening of health inequalities.FundingAustralian Government's Water for Women (WfW) Fund.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

People with disabilities experience barriers to accessing and using sanitation facilities safely and independently. Extreme weather events, such as floods and cyclones, damage sanitation facilities, further limiting access and increasing reliance on caregivers. Studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have shown that households including people with disabilities are less likely to have access to safely managed sanitation services. Additionally, people with disabilities and their caregivers are often excluded from disaster response and recovery efforts, exacerbating disparities and contributing to negative health outcomes. However, there is a significant gap in understanding the specific sanitation-related challenges people with disabilities face in Bangladesh during cyclones and floods, as well as how they and their caregivers manage sanitation access and use during and after these events. No study has examined this intersection in Bangladesh, leaving an urgent need for evidence to inform disability-inclusive climate adaptation strategies.

Added value of this study

This is a nationwide mixed-methods study to investigate the effects of weather events on sanitation access and use among people with disabilities in Bangladesh. The study included a survey across 32 districts and in-depth qualitative assessments in Satkhira and Gaibandha, two of the country's most climate-vulnerable districts. Participants had diverse limitation types, with specific consideration given to those experiencing incontinence. In addition to identifying the challenges people with disabilities face in meeting their sanitation needs during cyclones and floods, this study also provides evidence on the strategies they use to manage these challenges and their impact on the health and well-being of both individuals and their caregivers. Qualitative findings provide insights into these challenges and coping mechanisms through a gender lens. Findings offer critical new evidence on the disproportionate effects of extreme weather events on the sanitation experiences of people with disabilities, informing disability-inclusive climate-resilient WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene) programming and emergency response. This study was a collaborative effort across academic institutions, public health research organisations, organisations of persons with disabilities, and non-government organisations.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study underscores the urgent need for climate-resilient, disability-inclusive sanitation solutions in Bangladesh. It highlights the importance of ensuring full inclusion of people with disabilities and strengthening caregiver support systems in climate adaptation strategies to protect their health and dignity. The findings highlight disability-specific sanitation challenges that policymakers and humanitarian actors must prioritise when developing inclusive, climate-resilient sanitation infrastructure and services. They provide a deeper, context-specific understanding of these challenges, enabling the development of evidence-based, disability-inclusive WASH principles to improve services for people with and without disabilities in Bangladesh and other LMICs. The evidence can inform policymakers' and programme implementers' future actions to integrate inclusive sanitation measures into disaster preparedness policies, mobilise resources, and foster community-based adaptation solutions. This is essential in reducing inequalities, enhancing resilience, and ensuring that people with disabilities are not left behind in climate adaptation and emergency response efforts.

Introduction

Poor access to sanitation and the use of inadequate sanitation facilities increase exposure to infectious diseases, contribute to undernutrition and antimicrobial resistance,1 and compromise privacy, safety, and dignity.2 Improvements in sanitation services reduce the risk of diarrhoea by 24–47%.3 However, over 3.6 billion people globally lack access to “safely managed sanitation facilities”, with marginalised groups, such as people with disabilities, women and girls, and older adults, facing worse access.1,4,5

Analysis of cross-sectional multi-country survey data from Bangladesh, Cameroon, India, and Malawi shows that households with a person with disabilities are less likely to have basic or safely managed sanitation.6 Specific barriers include inaccessible routes to a sanitation facility and infrastructure, and increased caregiver dependence.4,7, 8, 9 Those experiencing incontinence face additional health and dignity challenges due to frequent sanitation needs, limited access to incontinence products, inadequate facilities like commodes and bedpans, and increased caregiving tasks.10,11

Disasters amplify these pre-existing vulnerabilities among people with disabilities. During floods and cyclones, they face heightened risks due to functional limitations and social barriers, including limited participation in public life, limited ability to express WASH needs, and limited access to early warning.9,12, 13, 14 These challenges increase injury risk and worsen health outcomes.4,13 Caregiver tasks also intensify as sanitation needs become harder to meet.6,9,15 The lack of evidence-based, disability-inclusive sanitation interventions that are resilient to disasters further exacerbates these issues.

Bangladesh frequently experiences climate-related events, including floods, droughts, storms, tropical cyclones, coastal surges, and saltwater intrusion16 and 136 storms and 88 floods struck the country between 1980 and 2024.17 Cyclone Amphan (2020) affected 10 million people across 19 southern districts, damaging 18,235 water points and 40,894 sanitation facilities. Riverine and monsoon flooding frequently submerge sanitation facilities in northern districts,17 contaminating water sources and increasing open defecation.16,18, 19, 20 Yet, empirical evidence remains limited on how floods and cyclones affect sanitation access, use, and management among people with disabilities and their caregivers. This study aims to address this gap by examining how extreme weather events affect the sanitation experiences of people with disabilities and their caregivers in Bangladesh. It addresses two key research questions:1.How do floods and cyclones affect the sanitation experiences of people with and without disabilities across Bangladesh?2.How do people with disabilities manage sanitation during and after these events, and what are the implications for them and their caregivers?

Methods

This was a mixed-methods study comprised of a quantitative, nationwide, population-based survey to identify people with disabilities and compare the access and experience of sanitation services affected by cyclone and flood between people with and without disabilities, and an in-depth qualitative analysis of the sanitation experiences of people with disabilities and their caregivers during and immediately after extreme weather.

Using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling, 32 districts across eight divisions were selected to achieve nationwide representation. From each district, three rural (mouzas) and two urban (wards) units were selected using PPS (S1 contains details on sampling procedure and participant enrolment). These 162 units were divided into clusters of 30 households, with one cluster randomly selected per unit (S2 contains a flowchart of the sampling procedure).

The study population comprised two comparison groups: people with disabilities and age- and sex-matched (±5 years) people without disabilities (S1). To identify people with disabilities, we screened individuals aged 15+ years in 30 households of each cluster using the Washington Group Short Set on Functioning–Enhanced (WG-SS Enhanced).21 Those who answered ‘a lot of difficulty or ‘cannot do at all’ across any of the first six functional domains (vision, hearing, mobility, communication, cognition, self-care) were classified as people with disabilities (S2).

Based on the Bangladesh Sample Vital Statistics 2018, the all-age prevalence of disability in Bangladesh is 8%.22 The sample size was computed based on 30% of the general population and 10% of people with disabilities, who are assumed to be aware of the various indicators for WASH services, as reported in previously published studies.23 It was estimated that 1312 respondents were required to detect a minimum 20 percentage-point difference in the use of WASH services across all eight administrative divisions of Bangladesh.

The quantitative component of this mixed-methods study was embedded within an existing nationwide population-based survey. This was funded by the UK's Foreign and Commonwealth Development Office (FCDO) through the Programme for Evidence to Inform Disability Action (PENDA) and led by the International Centre for Diarrheal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b).4 Additional questions focussing on the effect of extreme weather events on access to and use of sanitation facilities were incorporated into the survey questionnaire (S3). Members of the mixed-methods study research team and the existing survey team administered this additional module to all eligible participants during the same field data collection period (March–June 2023), using the survey's existing sampling frame.

The survey team included individuals with and without disabilities, with experience working with people with disabilities and conducting surveys using the KoboToolbox for data collection. They also received thorough training in disability identification and survey data collection before fieldwork. The research team carried out quality checks each day. Data was cleaned and analysed using STATA 15.0 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA). The outcome of interest comprised three binary measures:•Damage to sanitation facilities, defined as any damage to sanitation facilities, including structural collapse, platform breakage, waste overflow, or debris infiltration caused by cyclones, floods, or both. Participants selecting any damage option were classified as “having damaged a sanitation facility”.•Use of alternative sanitation facilities defined as the use of a different facility (e.g., piped sewer system, septic tank, pit latrine) due to damage to the primary facility. Participants reporting using any alternative facility were classified as “used an alternative sanitation facility”.•Changes in sanitation-related behaviour included modifications in toileting practices, routines, or behaviours to adapt to disrupted sanitation access, such as restricting the use of sanitation facilities or limiting food or fluid intake. Participants reporting any of these behaviours were classified as “Changes made to sanitation behaviours”.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were produced on the types of sanitation facilities used, the kind of damage, and changes to sanitation-related behaviours, routines, or practices during and after extreme weather events. A multi-level Poisson regression model with robust standard error was used to estimate the risk ratio for outcome variables i) damaged sanitation facilities, ii) used alternative sanitation facilities, iii) changes made to sanitation behaviour differences between the populations and exploring factors associated with damaged sanitation facilities among people with disabilities. All the models were adjusting for cluster-level correlation and demographic factors (age and sex). The model excluded wealth quintiles due to their strong correlation with disability status, which may mediate the relationship between disability and sanitation behaviours. Given the hierarchical nature of the multi-level data, the division level was treated as a random effect. All analyses report multivariable results with crude and adjusted risk ratios (aRR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and 5% significance levels across all analyses.

Qualitative in-depth analysis

We selected Satkhira (coastal) and Gaibandha (inland) for the qualitative component based on Bangladesh's multi-hazard risk assessments.16 Satkhira faces cyclones, sea level rise, and saltwater intrusion, while Gaibandha faces frequent floods and river erosion.16 We purposively selected individuals with disabilities from rural areas of Satkhira and Gaibandha using World Vision Bangladesh member lists, which included impairment type, age, gender, and location. Additional participants were identified through snowball sampling. Eligible participants had disabilities (15+ years) and had experienced a cyclone or flood in the past five years. Sixteen caregivers who regularly provide sanitation support to people with disabilities, including during extreme weather events, were also selected. Data collection occurred from 23 August to 30 November 2023. Trained researchers from the International Centre for Diarrheal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM) conducted in-depth interviews (S4 and S5), and photovoice (S6) using tailored topic guides for people with disabilities and caregivers. Topic guides were pilot tested with participants in Satkhira and refined based on feedback.

Interviews were conducted in Bangla and recorded with the participant's written consent, which was witnessed (S7 contains an overview of the qualitative data collection process). The research team reviewed field notes daily, discussed findings and emerging themes and revised the topic guides accordingly. Interview voice recordings were translated and transcribed into English. Transcriptions were checked against the voice recordings by Bangla-speaking research team members for data accuracy and quality.

Data analysis

We applied Braun and Clarke's (2006)24 iterative reflexive thematic analytical approach, with a priori codebook developed before data collection. Inductive codes (e.g., ‘availability of water’, ‘managing menstrual product’, ‘caregiver's incontinence support’) were added to the codebook as they emerged during data familiarisation to capture new insights. Transcripts were coded using NVivo 14 (Lumivero, Colorado, USA). Inter-coder and intra-coder reliability were assessed after coding to enhance consistency, ensure analytical rigour, and address any discrepancies. The research team developed a case-by-case data display matrix to compare the coded data and identify themes and pertinent quotes. Coded data were triangulated and findings were reviewed and compiled by thematic area after analysis.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval was received from icddr,b (PR-23072) and the LSHTM ethics board (#28925). Written informed consent was sought and obtained before data collection. For participants under 18 or those unable to fully understand the consent process, assent was obtained using simplified information sheets, followed by caregiver consent. Caregivers were then interviewed as a proxy, but efforts were made to involve participants directly. Interviews were conducted privately in Bangla, and participants were assured of the confidentiality of their data.

Only adults participated in the photovoice underwent two informed consent processes. Initial written informed consent was obtained before participants took photos, and a second written informed consent was sought before interviews were conducted with participants about the images. This process allowed participants to select how they wanted their photos used (e.g., in research articles, workshops, and presentations), and to decide whether they wanted their real names or pseudonyms credited when images were used, and whether their faces were blurred or visible. All photovoice participants wanted their real names credited against their photos, so we have used those in this article. Pseudonyms are used for all other quotes.

Role of funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or manuscript writing.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

For the survey, 1930 participants were enrolled, with 1021 (53%) with and 909 (47%) without disabilities (S8 and S9). Age distribution varied from 9% to 59%, and persons with disabilities were generally older. Economic status was evenly distributed across both groups. The most common types of functional limitations were mobility (52%) and multiple (50%), while communication was less common (15%).

We collected qualitative data from 28 people with disabilities through 24 in-depth interviews (IDIs) and 4 photovoice sessions (S8). Among people with disabilities, 13 were female, and 15 were male, resulting in a balanced gender distribution. Participants varied in age, with the majority aged 18–64. Participants experienced a range of limitations, most with multiple limitations, followed by mobility limitations. We collected data from 16 caregivers, all of whom were female. Most supported individuals with multiple limitations.

Cyclones in Satkhira and flooding in Gaibandha damaged sanitation infrastructure and made paths to facilities impassable. People with disabilities, particularly those with mobility limitations, faced greater challenges reaching facilities. Participants from both districts reported injuries from slipping and falling on muddy, waterlogged paths. Montu, who uses crutches, described a fall that left him dependent on others for toileting, starkly contrasting with his previous independence.“When I was going to the latrine with my crutch, my leg slipped, and I fell in the mud. Then, there was a brick on the ground, and my amputated leg side fell on that brick. I cut the upper part of the amputated leg, and blood came out. I used a plastic pot for my toileting at that time, and my mother or wife cleaned it after defecation and urination.”(Montu, male with mobility limitation, Satkhira).

Caregivers encountered challenges when assisting family members with disabilities in reaching the sanitation facilities. In Gaibandha, where flooding made routes impassable, they struggled to carry or transport individuals with disabilities to the facility. Some caregivers sustained injuries while navigating through the floodwaters, as Jorina, the mother of a man with multiple limitations, described.“During the flood, I slipped and fell frequently while carrying him to the open place. After the flood, when the path to the latrine became muddy and slippery, I slipped most during that time.”(Jorina, caregiver of a male with mobility, self-care and cognitive limitations, Gaibandha).

Changes to sanitation-related behaviours

People affected by flooding and storm damage who could not use their toilets used coping strategies to manage toileting, such as using alternative facilities and changing their sanitation behaviours. The population-based survey found that 45% (n = 111) of people used alternative sanitation facilities when cyclones and floods damaged their facilities. Additionally, when they used alternative sanitation facilities, 22% (n = 24) of participants reported changing their sanitation-related behaviours, such as restricting their use of the facility and intake of fluids and food (Table 1).

However, whilst more people with disabilities (48%, n = 69) reported using alternative sanitation facilities during cyclones and floods than people without disabilities (42%, n = 42), this was not statistically significant. Additionally, changes in sanitation-related behaviours were greater among people with disabilities (28%, n = 19) than those without disabilities (12%, n = 5), though no significant difference was observed (AOR 2.19, 0.83–5.81 95% CI, p = 0.115) (Table 2).

Qualitative interviews revealed that people with disabilities and their caregivers in Gaibandha relied on neighbours' or relatives' undamaged sanitation facilities during and after the flood. However, access depended on the owner's permission, which was not always granted. I had to go to [defecate in] open places.”(Rana, male with mobility limitation, Gaibandha).

People with disabilities experiencing incontinence in Satkhira and Gaibandha reported heightened challenges in toileting during and immediately after extreme weather. Discussion

This study found that cyclones and floods worsened sanitation challenges for people with disabilities, who experienced a modestly higher risk of sanitation facility damage compared to those without disabilities. Among people with disabilities, socioeconomic status emerged as a key protective factor, with those in higher wealth quintiles substantially less likely to experience sanitation facility damage than those in the poorest quintile, underscoring the role of structural and material resources in shaping vulnerability to climate-related hazards. In both districts, flooding rendered paths inaccessible, leading to injuries, while caregivers struggled to assist, often getting injured themselves. Despite these heightened risks, people without disabilities were not more likely than people with disabilities to use alternative sanitation facilities or to change sanitation-related behaviours following damage. Qualitative findings revealed gendered impacts, with women facing greater challenges in maintaining privacy and dignity. Additionally, incontinence posed risks, including soiled clothing and bedding that increased exposure to disease. The intensified sanitation support required of caregivers contributed physical and emotional strain. Together, these findings highlight how climate-related extreme weather events may deepen existing health and social inequalities for people with disabilities and their caregivers.

Sanitation facilities in this study were often constructed with lightweight materials and were highly vulnerable to cyclones and floods,18 resulting in damaged pit latrines used by both people with and without disabilities. Similar patterns have been documented elsewhere, for example, a 2021 survey in Satkhira found that 23% of respondents reported damage to water, sanitation, and bathing facilities following Cyclone Amphan.25 In our study, flood damage exacerbated accessibility challenges for people with disabilities and caregivers, leading to physical injuries from navigating slippery, uneven paths.18 Widespread submersion of sanitation facilities during floods was also observed, consistent with findings elsewhere in Bangladesh, where 57% of pit latrines were flood-damaged, leading to a threefold increase in open defecation.19

In our study, people with disabilities were more likely than those without disabilities to have damaged sanitation facilities, with the greatest disparity observed among the poorest. Multidimensional poverty may limit their ability to invest in resilient infrastructure. Evidence from several LMICs shows that people with disabilities are more disproportionately economically disadvantaged,26 constraining their capacity to build or retro-fit climate-resilient facilities. Additionally, an analysis of Bangladesh's WASH policies highlights a lack of clear, adequately funded disability-inclusive sanitation initiatives,27 potentially reinforcing reliance on fragile infrastructure among people with disabilities living in poverty. This intersection of disability and poverty likely contributes to heightened vulnerability to floods and cyclones.

In both Satkhira and Gaibandha, people with mobility limitations frequently fell enroute to the sanitation facility, while caregivers were also injured while assisting them. Other studies highlight similar challenges, where intense rainfall makes steep, muddy paths hazardous. In Bangladesh, Alam et al.4 found that rural women with mobility limitations frequently fell when accessing sanitation during rainy days, emphasising the urgent need for flood-resilient, disability-inclusive solutions. For people with disabilities, such injuries can increase dependence on caregivers, who may then struggle to balance other responsibilities. A 2022 study in Vanuatu found that caregivers of daughters with intellectual disabilities had reduced earning potential and less time to rebuild their homes after cyclones, as their daughters required increased support due to post-cyclone trauma.28

Studies from Bangladesh describe the use of neighbours' or relatives' sanitation facilities as a18,19 response strategy during extreme weather events.18,19 However, people with disabilities and caregivers in our study often reported facing abuse when attempting this, making this option unviable. Some participants continued using damaged facilities over seeking alternatives due to the fear of mistreatment. In contrast, Iyer et al.19 found communal solidarity in Jamalpur, where nearly half of the flood-affected people used their neighbours' toilets, although authors did not examine verbal abuse. Whether this mistreatment stems from disability discrimination or crisis-related stress remains unclear.

In our study, people with disabilities frequently came into contact with urine and faeces during cyclones and floods, causing intense feelings of disgust. This aligns with a nationwide Bangladeshi survey showing that those with mobility, communication, and cognitive limitations were twice as likely to face these challenges, even in the absence of weather events.4 Exposure to excreta increases health risks, including infection and chronic conditions, and contributes to hygiene-related stigma, highlighting the urgent need for inclusive, climate-resilient sanitation solutions.10

In Gaibandha, floodwaters submerged sanitation facilities and household surroundings, forcing some people with disabilities to resort to open defecation, use makeshift banana-tree rafts with caregivers, or defecate directly into the floodwaters. Similar practices have been reported in Bangladesh's cyclone- and flood-prone areas.18,19 Many people with mobility or self-care limitations relied more on caregivers for transportation, which reduced their autonomy in managing sanitation activities.18,19

Jerin et al. (2023) found that 22% more women than men in flood-prone Jamalpur resorted to open defecation due to damaged sanitation, lack of clean water, and limited access to neighbours' facilities.19 Nuzhat et al. (2023) highlighted that flooded toilets increased the incidence of open defecation among women.20 While these studies did not include women with disabilities, our analysis reveals their heightened vulnerability.

Privacy and safety risks during open defecation are particularly high for women, including risks of accidents and assault.2 These risks are greater for women with disabilities and are further intensified during flooding. Evidence from 29 low- and middle-income countries shows that women with disabilities are 85% more likely to experience violence than women without disabilities.29

We found that men and women with disabilities restricted food and water intake to avoid open defecation, a common strategy among people with disabilities, as reported in Vanuatu10 and Guatemala.7 In our study, these practices led to self-reported constipation, weight loss, and weakness. Weather events further amplify these effects, as highlighted in a literature review that explored the health impacts of extreme weather events on persons with disabilities in LMICs.13

In our study, cyclones and floods exacerbated toileting challenges for people with disabilities experiencing incontinence, increasing exposure to waste for both individuals and caregivers. Limited water access, long distances to sanitation facilities, and manual cleaning by caregivers heightened infection risks. Evidence from humanitarian settings and several LMICs shows that even in non-emergency conditions, people with disabilities struggle to manage incontinence, placing physical strain on caregivers.9, 10, 11,15 Weather events further intensify these challenges.

A key strength of this study was its mixed-methods approach, combining a nationwide survey to assess the broad effects of extreme weather on sanitation with qualitative research that captured the nuanced challenges and coping strategies of people with disabilities. As a limitation, the survey found no significant differences in sanitation-related behaviours between people with and without disabilities during extreme weather events. This may reflect the random selection of participants from affected areas, rather than focussing on the most severely impacted locations. Conversely, qualitative data from heavily affected locations provided deeper insights into the sanitation experiences and coping strategies of people with disabilities and their caregivers. Additionally, participants were asked to recall experiences of past floods and cyclones during qualitative interviews, which may have introduced recall bias. To minimise this, interviews followed structured guides covering periods before, during, and after extreme weather events, with probing to support recall. The analysis adopted an interpretive stance, recognising that accounts reflected both remembered events and their meanings.

In Bangladesh, floods and cyclones exacerbate existing inequalities for people with disabilities and their caregivers. Our study underscores the critical link between disability, disruptions in sanitation services due to climate change, and adverse health outcomes for people with disabilities. These disruptions lead to injuries, increased contact with urine and faeces, restricted usage of sanitation facilities, limited food intake, and heightened dependency on caregivers. Damaged sanitation infrastructure forces a shift to open defecation, increasing health risks and violating privacy, particularly for women with disabilities. Caregivers face heightened physical and emotional strain. Climate adaptation strategies must integrate accessible, resilient sanitation options and support for caregivers to carry out their vital role. Prioritising the participation of people with disabilities is crucial for developing inclusive, climate-resilient solutions.
