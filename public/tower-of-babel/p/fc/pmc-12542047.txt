National and subnational trends of years of life lost and reduced life expectancy caused by bladder cancer in China from 2005 to 2020
Authors: Zhe Liu, Lingling Yu, Fanshu Yan, Jinlei Qi, Lijun Wang, Maigeng Zhou, Peng Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the most common neoplasm of the urinary system. In 2020, there will be an estimated 573,000 new cases of bladder cancer worldwide and 213,000 deaths [1]. Bladder cancer was the 10th most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide [1]. Among males, bladder cancer ranks 9th among cancer-related deaths. In 2021, the age-standardized prevalence rate of bladder cancer is 34.91/100,000 worldwide [2]. In China, there were 66 200 new cases of bladder cancer in males, accounting for 15% of global male bladder cancer cases in 2020 [3]. The age-standardized prevalence rate of bladder cancer is 28.32/100,000 in China [4].

Previous studies [5–7] have described the temporal trends of mortality in bladder cancer and examined sex, age, and regional differences. In these studies, the temporal trends were presented using the average annual percent change (AAPC) and annual percent change (APC) [7]. Further, these studies also estimated the epidemiological distribution traits of bladder cancer among different sexes, areas, and age groups. Few other studies have shown that both the mortality and the incidence rates of bladder cancer in urban areas are higher than those in rural areas [3]. The disease burden caused by bladder cancer is likely to gradually increase in the coming years in China [7]. However, the data are insufficiently representative of China and its provinces. Thus, the reduced life expectancy and years of life lost (YLL) due to bladder cancer in China remain largely unexplored.

Although mortality rate is the main indicator used to measure the total mortality of a population, it does not accurately show the disease burden of disease. In contrast to mortality, YLL can quantify the degree of influence of different diseases on life lost in a population and is often used to measure levels of premature death and quantify the burden of specific diseases. In addition, life expectancy, as a comprehensive evaluation indicator, is often used to assess the overall health status of a population. Life expectancy is also a good indicator for comparisons over time or among different populations as it is not affected by population size or age structure [8].

Therefore, to better assess the epidemiological situation of bladder cancer in China and obtain relevant information on time distribution, regional distribution, and population distribution, as well as provide more sufficient and accurate scientific basis for alleviation of the prevalence of bladder cancer, this study aims to use data from the National Mortality Surveillance System (NMSS) to assess trends in different indicators due to bladder cancer during the period from 2005 to 2020 including mortality, YLL and reduction in life expectancy.

Methods

Relevant data on bladder cancer and mortality rates for the period 2005–2020 were obtained from the NMSS, which is available from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The data source has strict quality control measures and good national and provincial representativeness [9]. Mortality was adjusted for underreporting, which was derived from retrospective underreporting field surveys [10]. Considering the issue of underreporting for children’s death in the NMSS, the under-5 mortality rate (U5MR) at the county level from the Global Burden of Disease Study was used as an independence data source for this study [11–13]. Data for surveillance population were from the China National Bureau of Statistics, and the age-standardized rate was estimated using 2010 Chinese census data [14]. In our previous study, we presented a flowchart of the estimation process and detailed descriptions of the estimates were published elsewhere [11, 15, 16].

Bladder cancer mortality estimation

The adjusted all-cause mortality rates, U5MR, and mortality rates of those aged 15–60 years were calculated based on previous studies [11, 12]. Finally, we estimated age-specific mortality rates. Garbage codes were redistributed by location-year-sex-age to the most likely causes of death, based on the method developed by Naghavi et al. [17]. The proportion of bladder cancer cases (ICD-10 code: C67-C67.9) was calculated using province-age-sex data from the NMSS after garbage code redistribution. The death registration’s permanent residential address was used to create an urban/rural division. Municipalities or prefectural cities are classified as urban areas, while the remaining areas are classified as rural. We calculated bladder cancer mortality from 2005 to 2020 for different location, region (urban and rural) sex and age groups.

Years of life lost (YLL) and cause-eliminated life expectancy estimation

Years of Life Lost (YLL) is an indicator of disease burden status calculated using a theoretical minimum risk reference life table [18]. The theoretical minimum risk reference life table is taken from the lowest observed risk of death for each 5-year age group in all populations greater than 5 million from Global Burden of Disease Study as the standardized life expectancy in YLL estimation for bladder cancer during 2005–2020 [18]. YLL is calculated by multiplying standard life expectancy and total deaths [17]. We calculated YLL for region (urban and rural), age groups in different provinces by sex for the period 2005–2020.

We calculated life expectancy, and the life expectancy after eliminating deaths caused by bladder cancer. This was Calculated by developing two abridged life tables to calculate life expectancy and cause-eliminated life expectancy. The latter is calculated by deducting the number of deaths from bladder cancer in all age groups. The reduction of life expectancy owing to bladder cancer was thus calculated by subtracting the life expectancy after eliminating bladder cancer deaths from the life expectancy.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the number of deaths, YLL, age-standardized mortality rate(ASMR), and age standardized YLL rate by sex, age group, province, and region (urban or rural) grouping. ASMR and age standardized YLL rate were calculated by combining the age composition ratios of the standard population with the actual mortality rates and YLL rates for different age groups. We used the log-linear poisson regression model to calculate APC, AAPC, and 95% confidence interval (CI) in ASMR and age-standardized YLL rate during 2005–2020. The APC and AAPC > 0 indicate an upward trend in ASMR and age-standardized YLL rate over time, and < 0 indicates a downward trend in ASMR and age-standardized YLL rate over time. We calculated the reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer during the study period, and the proportion of bladder cancer deaths in all cancer deaths, for both urban and rural areas and all provinces.

All analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), R version 4.0.4 (The R foundation for Statistical Computing) and the Joinpoint software (Version 4.8.0, April 1, 2020).

Results

As shown in Table 1, bladder cancer accounted for 29 730 deaths (24 027 males, 5703 females) and 492 859 YLL (405 949 males, 86 909 females) in China in 2020. The ASMR of bladder cancer decreased from 1.72/100 000 in 2005 to 1.57/100 000 in 2020, and the age-standardized YLL rate per 100 000 decreased from 33.06 in 2005 to 26.22 in 2020. The ASMR and age-standardized YLL rates in males were consistently higher than those in females, with a downward trend from 2005 to 2020. The ASMR of bladder cancer decreased (AAPC = −0.58, 95% CI: −0.74 to −0.42) during the study period. The age-standardized YLL rate of bladder cancer decreased in total (AAPC = −1.50, 95% CI: −1.65 to −1.35) during 2005 to 2020.

The proportion of bladder cancer-specific deaths among all cancer-related deaths in males remained stable at approximately 1.0% in most provinces from 2005 to 2020. Shanghai had the highest growth rate of bladder cancer-specific deaths among all cancer-related deaths (1.9% in 2005 and 3.1% in 2020) (Fig. 2a). From 2005 to 2020, the proportion of affected females in these two provinces was higher than that in the other provinces (including Shanghai and Beijing). Ningxia had the highest bladder cancer growth rate among females (0.16% in 2005 and 0.98% in 2020) (Fig. 2b).

Life expectancy at birth in China gradually increased from 72.997 years in 2005 to 77.742 years in 2020. From 2005 to 2020, the reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer increased from 0.018 to 0.021 years. Without bladder cancer deaths, life expectancy reached 77.763 years in 2020 (Appendix 2). The reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer in urban areas was consistently higher than that in rural areas, since it increased from 0.013 years to 0.018 years in rural areas from 2005 to 2020. The reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer in males was higher than that in females, and increased from 0.024 to 0.029 years in males between 2005 and 2020. This reduction in life expectancy also occurred in rural areas and among male patients between 2005 and 2020 (Fig. 3).

The reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer in China during 2005-2020

In 2020, the reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer in the northeastern and eastern coastal areas of China was higher than that in other regions. The highest reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer was observed in Beijing (0.058), followed by Shanghai (0.052), and Tianjin (0.046) (Fig. 4). The reduced life expectancy due to bladder cancer increased to varying degrees in two-thirds of the provinces in China from 2005 to 2020. Five provinces (Tianjin, Beijing, Shanghai, Liaoning, and Zhejiang) had a significantly higher reduction in life expectancy resulting from bladder cancer than the other provinces. Tianjin had the highest growth rate (0.021) in decreased life expectancy, followed by Beijing (0.02) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In our study, the reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer gradually increased in China. Bladder cancer has led to the largest reduction in life expectancy in the economically developed provinces of China, such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Tianjin. However, the mortality and YLL rates due to bladder cancer showed a downward trend from 2005 to 2020. The mortality rates and age-standardized YLL rates due to bladder cancer in urban areas were higher than those in rural areas, and similarly that of males was higher than those of females. The proportion of bladder cancer deaths among the total cancer deaths increased. Under stratification by sex, the proportion of male bladder cancer deaths in most provinces increased, whereas the proportion of females in half of the provinces increased.

In this study, the significant downward trend of mortality and YLL was observed, which may be attributed to the increase in healthcare resources, improvement in people's health awareness, and effectively reduce and control the exposure level of risk factors including smoking, occupational carcinogen exposure, environmental factors, air pollution, and other risk factors[19]. Tobacco is one of the main risk factors for bladder cancer[20]. A previous survey showed that the risk of bladder cancer increases three to four times as a result of smoking [21]. The standardized smoking prevalence decreased from 26.0% in 2003 to 25.2% in 2013 among people older than 15 in China[22]. This may be one of the explanations for the small decrease in mortality and YLL rates in bladder cancer. The decrease in occupational carcinogen exposure levels affecting bladder cancer may have also led to the downward trend seen in this study. Previous evidence had shown that long-term exposure to aromatic amines increases possibility of disease occurrence [20].

Our study found that males have higher bladder cancer mortality and deaths than females in all the age categories, which is consistent with previous findings[23]. The difference between the sexes can be explained by the influence of smoking and other risk factors. The proportion of male smokers was much higher than that of female smokers [24–26]. Previous study revealed the chromosomal difference (XX versus XY) between sexes is an intrinsic cause of sex difference in bladder cancer[27]. The influence of age on mortality showed an upward trend. When older individuals become sick, they cooperate less with various treatment measures, have a greater disease burden, usually have a reduced prognosis. Our study therefore indicates that the prevention and treatment of bladder cancer in China should focus on the older male population.

Life expectancy is a comprehensive indicator that reflects the disease burden of bladder cancer and can be used to evaluate the health status of residents. Compared with mortality, life expectancy is less susceptible to the influence of population size and age structure. Among the previous studies on bladder cancer, only few used life expectancy to measure the disease burden. From 2005 to 2020, the life expectancy of Chinese residents continued to increase, from 73.00 years to 77.74 years. However, the disease burden from various diseases is still at a high level. The reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer reported in this study increased from 0.018 to 0.021 years. Although the reduction in life expectancy caused by bladder cancer is relatively low, it is the 6th most common cancer among male, and thus should not be ignored. As smoking is the most important behavioural risk factor for bladder cancer, our study lends more support to stronger public health campaigns targeted at reducing smoking prevalence, considering the rapid aging in China and particularly higher smoking prevalence in Chinese adult men. The effective prevention and control of bladder cancer risk factors should be strengthened, the level of bladder cancer diagnosis and treatment technology should be improved. Through all-round, multilevel, and targeted bladder cancer intervention measures, the impact of bladder cancer on life expectancy can be reduced and the health of the population can be improved.

In economically developed regions, the proportion of bladder cancer-specific deaths compared to the total number of deaths caused by all cancers increased. Firstly, there is a correlation between economic level and bladder cancer. Previous study indicated a positive correlation between the level of GDP and the incidence of bladder cancer[28]. Early screening, better access to medical services and better health literacy of the residents in economically developed regions may diagnose more bladder cancer patients. Secondly, the combined effect of risk factors on bladder cancer may have an impact on the premature deaths of bladder cancer[29–31]. In addition to tobacco, other poor lifestyle habits such as increased consumption of meat and sugar-sweetened beverages may be more prevalent in economically developed regions. Thirdly, in economically developed regions, the level of treatment, early screening and targeted interventions for digestive system cancers (such as stomach and colorectal cancers) are higher. Death rates from these leading cancer causes were decreasing [32], resulting in a decrease in the proportion of digestive system cancer in the total number of cancer deaths, which may contribute indirectly to a relative increase in the proportion of deaths due to bladder cancer in these areas. The reduction in life expectancy caused by cancer was also greater than those in these regions. the difference between life expectancy and the decrease in life expectancy due to cancer mortality has grown the fastest, indicating the negative impact of cancer-related deaths on population health, and is rapidly increasing in economically developed areas, which might be connected with poor mode of life, smoking, diet, and other factors[21, 22, 29–31]. Cancer causes a greater disease burden in these provinces than in other provinces. Smoking, drinking, and other risk factors may affect the incidence rate of and mortality from bladder cancer[33]. Evidently, we must pay more attention to the differences between economically developed coastal provinces and other regions. This is conducive to the formulation of targeted policies that effectively reduce the burden of bladder cancer and extend life expectancy. It is essential to increase awareness of bladder cancer, implement targeted measures, particularly in economically developed areas and among high-risk groups. It is also important to reduce the level of exposure to behavioural risk factors such as smoking. This helps to reduce the disease burden caused by bladder cancer deaths and increase the life expectancy in the general population.

The strength of this study lies in the national and provincial representativeness of the data. The research data were from the NMSS, which covers a large time period and has a huge sample size. To reflect the actual level of death, mortality rates were adjusted for under-reporting derived from the under-reporting field surveys in NMSS. We also stratified the disease burden based on different dimensions, such as urban and rural areas, sex, and provinces, and used different indicators to analyze the disease burden, including YLL, life expectancy, and proportion of malignant tumor deaths. Thus, this study reflects the most recent status of bladder cancer burden in China.

Our study had a few limitations. Firstly, the mortality rate of bladder cancer in our study may have been underestimated, because we only included bladder cancer deaths when coding for underlying cause of death. Second, there is poor quality of coding in a few rural areas in western provinces of the NMSS. However, to ensure the accuracy of bladder cancer as a cause of death, we used garbage code redistribution and underreporting surveys. We were not able to analyze the risk factors for bladder cancer due to data availability. Future studies should be carried out to illustrate the effects of various risk factors, such as occupational exposures, environmental factors and dietary habits, on the burden caused by bladder cancer. Lastly, for the observed provincial trends and variations, such as the rapidly increasing share of bladder cancer deaths among women in Ningxia, we did not obtain sufficient explanations and further researches explore the latent reasons or possible influencing factors for these variations.

In conclusion, the number of deaths for bladder cancer in China has been increasing in recent years. Although the loss of life expectancy caused by bladder cancer in China was relatively small, the gradually increasing trend warrants attention, especially in economically developed regions.
