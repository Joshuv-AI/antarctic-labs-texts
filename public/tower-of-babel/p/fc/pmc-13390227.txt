Background

Since about 85% of fetal growth occurs in the late stages of pregnancy, so the energy requirements for pregnant ewes carrying one to two lambs are increase by 150% to 200% compared to those of non-pregnant and non-lactating ewes [1]. However, increased fetus size and weight result in an enlarged uterus which compresses the rumen, and feed intake decreases resultantly. This increased energy demand coupled with reduced feed intake during late gestation leads to nutritional restriction, causing ewes to often suffer from hypoglycemia, hyperketonemia, and pregnancy toxemia due to disruption of metabolic homeostasis [2]. Moreover, fetal growth and development in the uterus are severely constrained by maternal undernutrition [3–5]. The placenta plays a crucial role in pregnancy, serving as a vital conduit for material exchange between the mother and fetus. It facilitates the transfer of essential nutrients and oxygen from the maternal circulation to the developing fetus, supporting its growth and development, while the placenta enables the removal of metabolic waste from the fetus to the mother for elimination [6]. Moreover, fetal development is highly dependent on placental development, and the placenta develops earlier than the fetus [7]. The placenta undergoes rapid growth during the early to mid-stages of gestation and reaches its maximal mass by mid-gestation. Placental development in early pregnancy is dependent on tissue nutrient support secreted by endometrial glands, and if nutrient support is inadequate at this time, the structure and function of the placenta may be compromised [7]. In the middle and later stages of gestation, the nutrient transport system of the placenta is rebuilt, and the placenta loses weight and becomes fully functional [8]. To date, studies on placental responses to maternal nutritional restriction have predominantly focused on early to mid-gestation, a critical window for placental growth and developmental programming [9–11]. Maternal feeding restriction during early to mid-gestation has been associated with an increased placental to lamb birthweight ratio [12]. Nutritional restriction during early to mid-pregnancy results in decreased cotyledon weight and placental surface area [13] and an increased number of caruncle vessels [10]. One study has reported that nutritional restriction during late pregnancy can lead to decreased blood flow to the placenta [14]. Our previous study has shown that nutritional restriction during late gestation reduce fetal liver weight and fetal weight in the uterus [15], which might correlate with the placental functions to some extent. Nevertheless, it remains unclear which placental gene networks are systematically reprogrammed in late gestation to coordinate nutrient transporter expression, substrate handling, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and immune-related functions under nutritional stress, and how such transcriptional changes relate to concurrent alterations in maternal and fetal circulating metabolites.

The transport of nutrients to the fetus through the placenta is not only affected by placental blood flow [16], but also limited by the expression and/or activity of specific transporters [17]. However, there are only a few studies on the effects of nutritional restriction on the placental nutrients transport process and on transporters. Rat model studies have found that a low-protein diet decreases the activity of the amino acid system A transporter [18]. Maternal nutrient restriction in ewes reduces placental amino acid transport and lowers the concentrations of amino acids and polyamines in fetal umbilical cord blood (UCB) [9, 19]. One mouse model study has reported that undernutrition alters the mRNA expression of the placental glucose transporter and the amino acid system A transporter [20]. Similarly, placental glucose transporter SLC2A3 expression is reduced in malnourished rats [21]. However, current research mainly focuses on the effects of late gestational undernutrition on placental size and fetal growth [22–24]. We hypothesized that late gestational undernutrition may alter transcriptional programs associated with placental nutrient transport and metabolic processes, and reshape placental cellular composition. Therefore, in this study, we establish an undernourished sheep model through feed restriction during late gestation and investigate the effect of nutritional restriction on placental metabolism, transport, and other functions based on transcriptome sequencing and maternal blood and UCB biochemical analyses.

Methods

Animal experiment and sample collection

The methods used in this study conformed to the standards of the Laboratory Animal Management Principles of China. All animal experiment procedures received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Approval SYXK(Su)2015 − 0656). This study belonged to a large project which aimed to study maternal and fetal nutrition in response to nutritional restriction during late gestation, and the animal model construction had been described [25]. Similarly, 16 pure-bred Hu-sheep with 108 days gestation, similar parity (2 to 3 parity), and similar body weight (60.6 ± 4.9 kg) were selected for this experiment. All ewes were placed in separate pens and subjected to a 7-day pre-feeding period during which all ewes had free access to feed and water ad libitum. Thereafter, ewes were randomly divided into 2 groups with 8 ewes in each group. Ewes in the control (CON) group were fed normally according to the feed intake detected in the pre-feeding period (1.56 kg total mixed ration, dry matter basis) while ewes in the treatment (TR) group were restricted to 30% feeding level (0.47 kg total mixed ration, dry matter basis) for 15 days to establish an undernourished sheep model. As described in our previous paper [26], maternal blood β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA) concentration in the TR group (3.9 mmol/L) was higher than that of the CON group (0.7 mmol/L) and higher than the threshold (0.8 mmol/L) while glucose concentration in the TR group (1.81 mmol/L) was lower than that of the CON group (2.87 mmol/L) and lower than the threshold (3 mmol/L) after 15 days treatment, confirming the successful establishment of undernourished sheep model. These sheep were humanely euthanized using electrical stunning followed by neck exsanguinations, allowing animals to quickly lose consciousness and minimize pain. The diet formula and nutrient compositions were detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Samples of cotyledons connected to the male fetus were collected and immediately placed in liquid nitrogen for transcriptome and real time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis. Maternal jugular vein blood and umbilical vein blood samples were collected to analyze metabolite levels.

Biochemical indicators analysis

Glucose, BHBA, and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) in the maternal jugular vein blood and Umbilical vein blood were measured using commercial kits (A154-1-1, intra-assay CV = 5%, inter-assay CV = 10%; E030-1-1, intra-assay CV = 3.5%, inter-assay CV = 10%; and A042-1-1, intra-assay CV = 2.40%, inter-assay CV = 5.55%) from Nanjing Jian-Cheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of free amino acids

Free amino acids in maternal jugular vein blood and Umbilical vein blood were detected by high-speed amino acid analyzer L-8900 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, 200 µL serum sample was thoroughly mixed with 600 µL 4% sulfosalicylic acid, and centrifuged at 13,000 g 4℃ for 30 min. Then, the supernatant was collected using 1 mL syringe and filtered through 0.22 μm water membrane. Finally, the filtered supernatant was loaded into the sample bottle and used to detect free amino acid contents. The parameters to analyze amino acids were set according to the method described by Lu et al. [27], using lithium citrate as mobile phase and proceeding UV-visible detection at 570 nm and 440 nm wavelengths. The flow rate of mobile phase and derivatization reagent were 0.35 mL/min and 0.3 mL/min, respectively. The automatic injector temperature, column temperature, and post-column reaction equipment temperature were kept at 4℃, 38℃, and 130℃, respectively. The identification and quantification of amino acids and their derivates were performed according to retention time and the ratio of peak areas to those of standard amino acids sample.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Ten placental samples (5 in each group) were randomly selected from the two groups for transcriptome sequencing. Total RNA was extracted from frozen placental samples (approximately 100 mg for each sample) in the CON and TR groups using the TRIzol (TSINGKE, Nanjing, China) method described by Chomczynski and Sacchi [28]. RNA concentration was detected using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and diluted to 500 ng/µL for all the samples. We used the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser system to assess RNA quality and made sure the integrity number of RNA was between 8 and 10. Simultaneously, the A260 / A230 and A260 / A280 of all the RNA samples were confirmed between 1.8 and 2.0. Subsequently, sequencing libraries were constructed according to previously described protocols [15, 29]. Simply, the mRNA is separated from the total RNA by magnetic beads attached to poly-T oligos, and the purified mRNA is fragmented by divalent cations in the reaction buffer (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) with increasing temperature. First and second strand cDNA are then synthesized by M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase, random hexamer primers, and DNA polymerase I. Next, appropriate cDNA fragments (approximately 200–250 bps) were selected for adapter ligation and PCR amplification. Finally, the prepared cDNA libraries were detected using Bioanalyser 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA) and sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform of Biomarker Technologies Company (BMK, Beijing, China).

Inhouse Perl script is used to remove poor reads, clean reads were then compared with the sheep reference genome (Oar v4.0) using HISAT2 [30]. In addition, to evaluate the expression of gene transcripts, String Tie (version 1.3.4d) was used to calculate the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped values (FPKM) value [31]. The BMK cloud platform (http://www.biocloud.net/) was used for volcano plot, clusters of orthologous groups for eukaryotic complete genomes (KOG) functional classification and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. In order to evaluate the enrichment significance of differentially expressed genes (DGEs) in KEGG, KOBAS (Version 2.0) was used [32]. The criteria of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) selection between the CON group and TR group were fold change (FC) larger than 1.5 and P value less than 0.01. For KEGG pathway enrichment, enrichment significance was corrected for comparisons across pathways using Benjamini-Hochberg, and the resulting adjusted values are reported as q values. The principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) of the placental total genes between the two groups were conducted using SIMCA-P 13.0 software.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression levels of genes related to carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid transport and metabolism, extracellular matrix, and complement in the placenta. Using the TRIzol (TSINGKE, Nanjing, China) method [28] to extract total RNA from frozen placental samples, and subsequently total RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed to complementary DNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit (TSINGKE, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. As shown in supplemental Table S2, primers used in this study were designed using Primer 5 software (Whitehead Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA) and Primer BLAST program (National Center for Biotechnology Information, Bethesda, MD, USA) and synthesized by commercial suppliers (Generalbiol, Chuzhou, China). After reverse transcription, complementary DNA was quantified using the ABI 7300 RT-qPCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The PCR mixture contained 2.0 µL of complementary DNA template, 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µM), 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (TSINGKE, Nanjing, China), 0.4 µL of ROX Reference DyeⅡ (50*), and PCR-grade sterile water to a final volume of 20 µL. The process for PCR amplification was as follows: 95℃ for 30 s for pre-denaturation, and 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 34 s, followed by a melting curve program (60℃ to 99℃ with a ramping rate of 0.1 ℃/s). The mRNA expressional levels of the target genes were analyzed using the 2−∆∆Ct method (normalized to β-actin) [33].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software was used to perform statistical analysis. Independent sample t-test was used to determine significant differences between the CON and TR groups, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Placental parameters

Our previous studies also demonstrated that nutritional restriction in late pregnancy significantly reduced the ewe and average fetal weights and disrupted liver and gastrointestinal homeostasis [25, 26]. Nutritional restriction during late gestation did not affect placental weight (P = 0.259) and cotyledon number (P = 0.185) compared with the CON group (Fig. 1A-B). However, surprisingly, the placental efficiency (P = 0.048) in the TR group was significantly lower than that in the CON group (Fig. 1C), suggesting that the reduction in placental efficiency during late gestational nutritional restriction may be associated with impaired fetal growth and development.

Transcriptome sequencing and the selection of differentially expressed genes

To minimize fetal sex-related variability, RNA-seq was conducted on cotyledons connected to male fetuses, enabling assessment of global placental transcriptional changes. PCA of placental total genes can basically separate the CON and TR groups, and axis 1 and axis 2 explained 51.2% and 11.9% of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 1D). Moreover, PLS-DA showed that the CON and TR groups were clearly separated and axis 1 and axis 2 explained 9.7% and 44.1% of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 1D). After statistical analysis, a total of 504 DEGs were screened out between the CON and TR groups, of which 219 genes were up-regulated and 285 genes were down-regulated in the TR group compared with the CON group (Fig. 1E).

Nutritional restriction inhibits placental carbohydrate metabolism and enhances glucose transport

KOG functional classification was conducted to further analyze the biological functions of the DEGs. As shown in Fig. 2A, the DEGs were mainly enriched in amino acid transport and metabolism (E), carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G), lipid transport and metabolism (I), transcription (K), post-translational modification, protein turnover, chaperones (O), and extracellular structures (W) in addition to general function prediction (R) and signal transduction mechanisms (T). The DEGs related to carbohydrate degradation including HEXB, HEXBL, CHI3L1, NAGA, ST6GAL2, GNSB, and GNS were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) in the placenta of the TR ewes compared to the CON ewes (Fig. 2B). The SLC37A2 involved in glucose-6-phosphate transport was down-regulated (P < 0.05) while the SLC2A9 related to glucose transport was up-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared to the CON group. Furthermore, RT-qPCR results showed that the mRNA expressional level of the glucose transporter SLC2A9 in the placenta of the TR group was significantly higher (P < 0.05) than those of the CON group while the mRNA expression of SLC2A3 and SLC2A1 remained unchanged (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, even though the glucose level in maternal blood reduced (P < 0.05) significantly upon nutritional restriction during late gestation [26], glucose level in the UCB of the TR ewes was significantly higher (P < 0.05) than that of the CON ewes (Fig. 2D). Together, these findings indicated that maternal nutritional restriction was associated with reduced expression of placental genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, accompanied by increased expression of a glucose transporter gene (Fig. 2E).

Nutritional restriction promotes placental lipid metabolism but fails to rescue the high lipids to fetus

For lipid transport and metabolism, compared with the CON group, the expression of DEGs involved in fatty acid oxidation including CPT1B, CPT1A, CYP4A6, and ACSL1 as well as fatty acid synthesis and triglyceride synthesis including FADS3, ELOVL6, DGAT2L, and DGAT2 was all up-regulated (P < 0.05) in the placenta of the TR group (Fig. 3A). Further, RT-qPCR results also confirmed higher (P < 0.05) mRNA expression levels of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation (CPT1B, CPT1A, and ACSL1), fatty acid synthesis (FADS3 and ELOVL6), and triglyceride synthesis (DGAT2) in the placenta of the TR ewes than those in the CON ewes (Fig. 3B). Surprisingly, DEGs associated with short-chain, middle-chain, and very long-chain fatty acid activation, including ACSS3, ACSM3, and ACSVL1 were all down-regulated (P < 0.05). In addition, DEGs related to glycerophospholipid biosynthesis (SLC44A3), bile acid synthesis (HSD3B7), prostaglandin metabolism (HPGD), glucose catabolism (CRYL1), and retinoic acid binding (CRABP1) were all down-regulated (P < 0.05). Similarly, the concentrations of BHBA (Fig. 3C) and NEFAs (Fig. 3D) in the UCB of the TR ewes were significantly higher (P < 0.05) than those in the CON ewes, which was possibly driven by the high levels of BHBA and NEFAs in the maternal blood upon nutritional restriction during late gestation [34]. Because ketone bodies can be easily diffuse readily or mediated by low-specificity carriers, they can quickly reach the fetus through the placenta [35, 36]. In general, nutritional restriction can lead to high levels of NEFAs and BHBA in the maternal blood, which may pass through the placenta to the fetus, while also causing lipid metabolism disorders in the placenta (Fig. 3E).

Nutritional restriction changes placental amino acid metabolism and transport

Regarding amino acid transport and metabolism, GCSH and GCAT involved in the degradation of glycine and threonine, respectively, were down-regulated (P < 0.05) in the placenta of the TR ewes compared to the CON ewes (Fig. 4A). C2, CFB, C1S, C1R, and CFD, which are serine proteases and are related to the complement reaction, were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) in the placenta of ewes in the TR group compared to the CON group. For amino acid transport, SLC7A2 and SLC38A4 were down-regulated (P < 0.05) while SLC7A3L and SLC7A8 were up-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared with the CON group. RT-qPCR results also confirmed a higher (P < 0.05) mRNA level of SLC7A8 and a lower (P < 0.05) mRNA level of SLC38A4 in the placenta of the TR group than those of the CON group (Fig. 4B). In addition, PM20D1 involved in condensing free amino acids and free fatty acids to N-fatty acyl amino acids and catalyzing the reverse hydrolysis reaction was up-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared to the CON group.

In maternal blood, the concentrations of essential amino acids including lysine, leucine, and histidine in the TR group were significantly higher (P < 0.05) than those in the CON group (Fig. 4C). Among the nonessential amino acids and amino acid derivatives, the concentration of α-amino n-butyric acid was significantly higher (P < 0.05) while the concentrations of glutamine, arginine, citrulline, alanine, serine, and ornithine were significantly lower (P < 0.05) in the maternal blood of the TR group than those of the CON group (Fig. 4D-E). In UCB, the concentrations of essential amino acids for the fetus including lysine, leucine, histidine, and threonine were all significantly increased (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared to the CON group (Fig. 4F). Among the non-essential amino acids and amino acid derivatives, the concentrations of proline, glycine, and α-amino n-butyric acid were significantly increased (P < 0.05), while the concentrations of glutamine, arginine, citrulline, alanine, serine, ornithine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid were significantly decreased (P < 0.05) in the UCB of the TR group (Fig. 4G-H).

Both maternal and fetal nutrient levels can affect the transport of nutrients to the fetus through the placenta. Therefore, the altered amino acid profile in UCB from undernourished ewes likely reflects combined contributions from maternal circulating levels and placental transport and metabolism. In particular, the elevated levels of lysine, leucine, and histidine may be associated with their increased concentrations in maternal blood, together with upregulated expression of the placental transporter SLC7A8. The increased threonine and glycine levels may instead reflect reduced placental catabolism, consistent with downregulation of genes involved in glycine and threonine degradation. By contrast, the reduced levels of serine, glutamine, and arginine appear more closely linked to their decreased maternal blood concentrations than to changes in the corresponding placental transporters (Fig. 4I).

Nutritional restriction inhibits placental transcription and protein homeostasis

In addition to the transport and metabolism of carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid, terms related to posttranslational modification, protein turnover, and partner, transcription, and extracellular structures were also enriched among the DEGs in the KOG functional classification. For the post-translation modification, protein turnover and partner pathway, UBB, CUEDC1, UBE2L6, RBBP6, TRIM36, and HECTD2 involved in ubiquitination were significantly changed, indicating a disruption of the ubiquitination process in the placenta under nutritional restriction (Fig. 5A). PCSK6, PCSK5, FOLH1, CTSW, MMP16, MEP1B, TFPI2, BPT1, ADAMTS5, and ADAMTS1 encode proteases, and almost all the DEGs involved in proteolysis including PCSK6, PCSK5, C3, C4, FOLH1, CTSW, MMP16, MEP1B, and ADAMTS5 were down-regulated (P < 0.05) upon nutritional restriction, except for CAPN8 and ADAMTS1. TFPI2 and BPTI, which have protease inhibitory functions, were also down-regulated (P < 0.05). The gene expression of pregnancy-related glycoproteins PAG1, PAG1L, PAG2, PAG4, and PAG6 was up-regulated (P < 0.05) for all members except PAG2. In addition, DEGs related to prostaglandin synthesis (GSTM1), protein folding (DNAJC12), and oligosaccharide biosynthesis (GSTM1) were all down-regulated (P < 0.05), while DEGs involved in reactive oxygen species production (ERO1A and ERO1B) were up-regulated (P < 0.05). In terms of transcription, GATA4, SOX9, SIX2, ZNF770, ATF3, JAZF1, BHLHA15, FOS, BATF3, CREB3L1, EEF1B2, and CLOCK, which encode transcription factors were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) except for CLOCK (Fig. 5B). These results suggested that nutritional restriction during late gestation inhibited transcriptional factors, proteolysis, and the ubiquitination process, which might further affect placental remodeling and functions.

Nutritional restriction represses placental ECM synthesis and immune function

KEGG enrichment pathway analysis showed that extracellular matrix (ECM) -receptor interaction (q < 0.001) and complement and coagulation cascades (q < 0.001) were significantly enriched among the DEGs between the CON and TR groups (Fig. 6A). Extracellular structures enriched in the KOG functional classification and ECM-receptor interaction enriched in the KEGG pathway analysis shared many overlapping DEGs, so we integrated these DEGs for further analysis (Fig. 6B). DEGs involved in collagen synthesis including TNXB, COL1A1, COL1A1L, COL1A2, COL2A1, COL4A4, COL6A3, and COL14A1 were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared with the CON group. DEGs involved in cell adhesion and migration including LGALS3, LAMC2, SDC1, and VIT were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared with the CON group. For DEGs involved in capillary formation, PLXDC1, NTN4, and THBS2 were down-regulated (P < 0.05) while only OLFML3 was up-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared with the CON group. RT-qPCR results also confirmed the lower (P < 0.05) mRNA expression levels of genes involved in collagen synthesis (COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL14A1) and angiogenesis(NTN4)in the placenta of the TR ewes than those in the CON ewes (Fig. 6C). ARMC2, MUC5AC, LINGO1, and MMP16 involved in complex axonemal center assembly, phosphatidylinositol-mediated signal transduction, axon regeneration, and ECM degradation, respectively, were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) upon nutritional restriction. SPON2 binds directly to bacteria and bacterial components and acts as an opsonin for phagocytosis by macrophages. SPP1 promotes the production of interferon-gamma and interleukin-12, while NTN1 and NELL2 have been linked to tumorigenesis. SPON2 and SPP1 were down-regulated (P < 0.05) while NTN1 and NELL2 were up-regulated (P < 0.05) during nutritional restriction, suggesting that the placental immune response may be compromised. Taken together, nutritional restriction during late gestation inhibited collagen synthesis, cell adhesion and migration, capillary formation, and likely immune response for the ECM and ECM-receptor interaction in the placenta.

In the complement and coagulation pathway, DEGs involved in the complement system including C1S, C1R, C1QB, C2, C4, C3, CFD, CFB, CR2, and SERPING1 as well as DEGs linked to hemostasis such as FGB and TGM1 were all down-regulated (P < 0.05) while only SERPINE1, which inhibits thrombosis degradation was up-regulated (P < 0.05) in the TR group compared to the CON group (Fig. 7A). RT-qPCR results also confirmed that the mRNA expression levels of genes involved in the complement system including C1S, C1R, C1QB, C2, CFB, CFD, and CR2 in the placenta of the TR ewes were lower (P < 0.05) than those in the CON ewes (Fig. 7B). In summary, nutritional restriction during late gestation inhibited the expression of genes associated with the complement and coagulation cascade responses in the placenta (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

Maternal nutritional restriction during pregnancy can affect fetal birth weight [37]. Fetal development highly relies on the placenta since the placenta not only regulates the nutrient supply to the fetus but also regulates the differentiation and maturation of major fetal organs [38]. Therefore, this study established an undernourished sheep model to characterize transcriptional alterations associated with male placental metabolism, nutrient transport, and related biological processes during late pregnancy.

Glucose transport by the placenta is one of the primary energy sources for the fetus before fetus is capable of gluconeogenesis. Placental transport of glucose requires the activation of glucose transporter under a concentration gradient from mother to fetus [39]. As a member of the glucose transporter family, SLC2A9 (GLUT9) has recently been shown to contribute to placental glucose transport [40]. Although reduced placental glucose uptake has been reported in fetal growth restriction [41–44], our data do not directly assess placental glucose transfer. The elevated umbilical glucose concentrations and increased placental SLC2A9 mRNA expression observed here suggest altered glucose handling under maternal undernutrition, but direct quantification of placental glucose flux will require blood flow and tracer-based measurements. NEFAs are the precursors of bioactive molecules, the structural components of cells, and the main source of energy, which are important for the development of specific tissues and organs [45, 46]. Nutritional restriction during late gestation induced body fat mobilization and caused NEFAs accumulation in the maternal blood [26], and therefore, many NEFAs enter the placenta. In the current study, we found that nutritional restriction upregulated placental fatty acid oxidation and synthesis and triglyceride synthesis to mitigate fetal NEFAs transfer. For example, placental triglyceride and lipid content is increased under certain maternal metabolic disturbances, supporting the notion that placental lipid storage can be a component of metabolic adaptation late in gestation [47, 48].

In addition to fatty acids and glucose, amino acids are also important nutrients for fetal development [49–51]. Placental active transporters play crucial roles in facilitating the movement of amino acids from the mother to the fetus, resulting in higher concentrations of amino acids in fetus [45] or in UCB [52] compared to maternal blood, as confirmed by this study. Nutritional restriction during the mid-gestation (28–78 days) reduced 9 amino acids levels in the blood of Baggs ewes, while the amino acids levels in fetal plasma remained unchanged [53]. However, maternal nutritional restriction imposed between days 28 and 78 of gestation, or extended from days 28 to 135, significantly reduced the concentrations of multiple amino acids in both maternal and fetal plasma [19]. In this study, it was found that the contents of some non-essential amino acids in maternal blood and UCB were decreased, while the contents of some essential amino acids were normal or increased in response to nutritional restriction during late gestation. SLC38A4 transports small and short-chain neutral amino acids such as serine, arginine, and glutamine [54], SLC7A2 and SLC7A3L transport arginine, lysine, and ornithine [55, 56], and SLC7A8 transports essential amino acids [57]. According to the nomenclature of amino acid transporters, system A consists of SLC38A1, SLC38A2, and SLC38A4, system Y+ consists of SLC7A1, SLC7A2, SLC7A3, and SLC7A4, and system L consists of SLC7A5, SLC7A8, SLC43A1, and SLC43A2. The study showed that maternal nutritional restriction downregulated placental SLC38A4 and SLC7A2 and upregulated SLC7A8 and SLC7A3L. This was confirmed by previous studies, which showed that the placental amino acid transport system A and system Y+ were downregulated in a rat model with maternal dietary protein deficiency [58, 59]. Therefore, maternal nutritional restriction could lead to reduced placental amino acid metabolism and increased placental transport, resulting in increased supply of some essential and non-essential amino acids to the fetus. This was consistent with previous studies which found that Baggs ewes were able to maintain fetal amino acid concentration by improving placental efficiency upon nutritional restriction [53].

Maternal nutritional restriction also significantly decreased the number of DEGs involved in placental transcription and posttranslational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones, which would be very likely to affect placental cell components and molecular functions. Ubiquitin ligase can regulate the ubiquitin of placental amino acid transporter [60], which is essential for the normal transport of amino acids. Proteases are related to the invasion of trophoblast cells, such as matrix metalloproteinases and plasminogen activators, which can regulate the penetration of trophoblast into deciduae. It was reported that inhibition of cysteine proteases with E-64 during the peri-implantation period impaired trophoblast differentiation and invasion, leading to stunted embryos and a reduced decidual reaction [61]. In this study, it was found that the DEGs encoding placental ubiquitination and proteinase were inhibited overall during maternal nutritional restriction, which might affect the development of the placenta and the growth of fetus. ECM is made up of various molecules such as collagen or adhesive glycoproteins, which regulate cell attachment and diffusion, cell proliferation and differentiation, and cell functions. Collagen and reticular fibers constitute the villi matrix, so collagen is the basic component of the placenta and an important site for the conversion of fetal and maternal nutrients and metabolites [62]. Research has proved that protein deficiency hinders angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation, and collagen synthesis. Moreover, nutritional restriction during pregnancy inhibits placental cell proliferation and angiogenesis [63]. In sheep studies, it was found that nutritional restriction in mid-pregnancy reduced umbilical blood flow in ewes, placental vascular endothelial growth factor mRNA expression, and the capillary area density on the surface of the placenta [64–66]. In this study, maternal nutritional restriction almost inhibited all the DEGs involved in ECM and ECM-receptor interaction including collagen synthesis, cell adhesion and migration, and capillary formation, which might dramatically affect placental remodeling and function.

In this study, we found that the expression of the transcription factors such as BATF3 and CREB3L1 was inhibited in the placenta during maternal nutritional restriction, which might impair the placenta’s immune function. Complement system regulates adaptive immune processes by neutralizing infectious agents and removing immune complexes and apoptotic cells to maintain host defense and protect fetus and mother from attack [67–69]. Therefore, a normal complement system is essential for pregnancy. Complement deficiency can have adverse effects on the body, for C3-knockout mice, the conception rate, placental weight, and fetal weight were all decreased compared to wildtype mice, indicating that C3 was necessary for successful pregnancy [70]. C1q is also essential for normal and stable pregnancy [71, 72]. C1q deficiency in mice inhibited placental maze development and spiral artery remodeling, which ultimately reduced litter size and fetal weight [73]. A goat study has found that maternal nutritional restriction in late pregnancy reduces the plasma concentrations of C3 and C4 in newborn lambs [74]. In the current study, we found maternal nutritional restriction inhibited the complement system in the placenta which might be a key factor for the significantly repressed fetal growth and development.

We focused RNA-seq on cotyledons associated with male fetuses to minimize sex-related variability; however, this limits the generalizability of our findings, as placental transport and inflammatory programs can exhibit sexual dimorphism. Future work should include both sexes and test nutrition-sex interactions to determine whether female placentas exhibit distinct adaptive responses. In addition, we did not measure umbilical blood flow in this study, which limits our ability to further infer placental nutrient transport.

Conclusions

Maternal nutritional restriction during late pregnancy induced pronounced transcriptional alterations in male placental pathways related to substrate transport and metabolism, characterized by upregulation of genes associated with fatty acid oxidation and lipid synthesis, alongside downregulation of genes involved in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism. Substrates metabolic changes might affect placental energy system, the processes of transcription and post-translational modification. At this point, impaired energy metabolism, transcription, and post-translational modification caused by maternal nutritional restriction further influenced the ECM, complement cascade, and immune response in the placenta. Together, these affected the placental remodeling and function, resulting in altered nutrient transport between mother and fetus and final fetal growth retardation and maldevelopment.
