Period-Specific Determinants of Mortality After Geriatric Hip Fracture: Insights into Inflammation, Comorbidity and Surgical Timing
Authors: Mustafa Bulut, Muhammed Furkan Darilmaz, Çağlar Tuna Issi, Berat Can, Hakan Yolaçan, Serkan Güler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMortality remains high after geriatric hip fractures. Evaluating postoperative mortality across distinct time windows may allow a more accurate clinical interpretation of phase-specific risk determinants.ObjectiveTo assess clinical and laboratory factors associated with mortality at 0–30 days, 30–90 days, and 90–365 days after hemiarthroplasty for geriatric hip fractures.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, 433 consecutive patients aged ≥65 years who underwent hemiarthroplasty between January 2019 and November 2024 were analyzed. Phase-specific determinants of mortality were evaluated using a three-interval landmark approach with multivariable logistic regression models (0–30, 30–90, and 90–365 days). Discrimination was assessed with ROC analysis; Kaplan–Meier analyses were reported as supportive findings.ResultsMortality was 17.1% at 0–30 days, 6.2% at 30–90 days, and 12.9% at 90–365 days. In multivariable analyses, 0–30-day mortality increased with age (OR 1.17) and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (OR 1.72), while female sex was protective (OR 0.28). Shorter time-to-surgery showed an inverse association with early mortality (OR 0.97 per hour). For 30–90 days, mortality was associated with log(CRP+1) (OR 1.62). For 90–365 days, mortality was associated with age (OR 1.06), higher ASA score (OR 2.60), and lower albumin (OR 0.92 per g/L). In ROC analyses, age yielded an AUC of approximately 0.70; CRP and CCI showed time-dependent AUC values ranging from 0.55 to 0.66, indicating limited discrimination for use as standalone clinical decision-support tools.ConclusionDeterminants of mortality after geriatric hip fracture vary across postoperative phases. Early mortality is more closely associated with comorbidity burden, intermediate mortality with inflammatory markers, and late mortality with indicators related to physiological reserve and nutritional status. Given the retrospective design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, and triage-related bias/residual confounding cannot be excluded.Keywords: geriatric hip fracture, postoperative mortality, risk stratification, inflammatory and nutritional markers, time-to-surgery

Introduction

Hip fractures are among the most frequently encountered injuries in elderly patients and represent a major cause of morbidity and mortality. 1 Due to the rapidly aging global population, the incidence of hip fractures continues to rise, currently affecting more than 1.5 million individuals annually.2,3 These fractures typically occur after low-energy falls in osteoporotic patients and are associated with high mortality because of the substantial burden of cardiac, pulmonary, and metabolic comorbidities. Reported mortality rates range between 8% and 36%, underscoring their importance as a significant public health problem. 4

As the elderly population continues to grow, morbidity and mortality related to hip fractures are expected to increase proportionally. It is projected that by 2050, the incidence of hip fractures will rise by 310% in women and 240% in men. 5 Approximately 20% of patients who sustain a hip fracture die within the first year following the injury, primarily due to medical comorbidities and postoperative complications. 6

In clinical practice, preoperative optimization of comorbid conditions frequently leads to delays in surgical intervention. Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that performing surgery within the first 24–48 hours can significantly reduce both short- and long-term mortality. 7 In contrast, some studies have reported that delays in surgical timing do not have a significant impact on mortality in patients with a high burden of comorbidities.8,9 Due to the generally moderate-to-poor general condition in elderly patient groups, and the potential impact of both early and delayed interventions on postoperative outcomes, the optimal surgical timing for hip fracture remains a subject of debate.10,11

The aim of this study was to examine the effects of surgical timing, operative duration, ICU admission, comorbidity burden, and perioperative laboratory markers on 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year mortality following hemiarthroplasty for geriatric hip fractures. We hypothesized that acute disease burden indicators—particularly the Charlson Comorbidity Index and preoperative C-reactive protein—would be the most influential predictors of early mortality (0–30 days), whereas long-term mortality (90–365 days) would be more strongly associated with chronic physiological reserve as reflected by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score and nutritional parameters.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care center between January 2019 and November 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (approval number blinded).

Study Population

A total of 502 consecutive patients aged ≥65 years admitted with acute hip fracture between January 2019 and November 2024 were screened for eligibility. Sixty-nine patients were excluded based on predefined criteria (pathological fractures, prior ipsilateral hip surgery or fracture, non-operative management, or missing mortality data), leaving 433 patients who underwent hemiarthroplasty and were included in the final analysis. Inclusion criteria were hip fracture due to a low-energy fall, surgical treatment with hemiarthroplasty, and availability of complete clinical and laboratory data in the electronic medical record system (Figure 1). Because this was a retrospective cohort study including all consecutive eligible patients during the predefined study period, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Post-hoc power analysis was not performed because the study included all eligible consecutive patients.

Data Collection

Demographic variables included age and sex. Fractures were classified using the AO/OTA system (femoral neck and pertrochanteric patterns). Pre-fracture mobility status (independent, assisted, or non-ambulatory) and cognitive status as documented in the medical record were noted. Preoperative laboratory parameters included hemoglobin, white blood cell count, albumin, and C-reactive protein (CRP). Comorbidity burden was quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and anesthesia risk was evaluated using the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score.

Perioperative data included time from hospital admission to surgery (in hours), surgical start time, duration of surgery, intensive care unit (ICU) requirement, and length of hospital stay. Reoperation during follow-up was also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 0–30 days, 30–90 days, and 90–365 days postoperatively. Mortality was cross-validated using hospital records and the national population registry. Secondary outcomes included ICU admission, length of stay, and reoperation.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most continuous variables were non-normally distributed, comparisons between groups were performed using the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To evaluate phase-specific determinants of mortality, a landmark analysis approach was applied across three predefined postoperative intervals (0–30, 30–90, and 90–365 days). Multivariable models were based on complete-case analysis. Therefore, the number of individuals included in regression models was slightly lower than the descriptive landmark cohorts presented in Table 1. Separate multivariable logistic regression models were fitted using the enter method. Candidate predictors were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and further screened in univariable analyses (p < 0.10). Automated stepwise selection procedures were not used. To reduce the risk of overfitting, the number of predictors included in each model was restricted according to the events-per-variable principle. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a threshold of >5 considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. No clinically relevant multicollinearity was observed. Model calibration for the 30–90-day logistic model was evaluated using bootstrap resampling with the rms package, and predictive accuracy was assessed using the Brier score. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess discriminative ability. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index, and area under the curve (AUC) values with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Only patients with complete data for predefined variables were included in the analyses; no multiple imputation procedures were performed. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding ultra-early surgery cases (<12 hours) to explore potential triage bias in the association between time-to-surgery and early mortality. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed as a complementary global survival analysis. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Given evidence of time-dependent effects, primary interpretation was based on phase-specific logistic regression models. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Mortality Incidence

Mortality occurred in 17.1% of patients within 0–30 days, 6.2% between 30–90 days, and 12.9% between 90–365 days; 63.7% survived the first postoperative year (Table 2).

Comparison of Continuous Variables

Across all three timeframes, patients who died were significantly older than survivors (all p = 0.001). Time to surgery was consistently shorter in mortality groups (all p = 0.001). Surgical duration did not differ for 0–30 and 30–90 days but was slightly longer in the 90–365-day mortality group (p = 0.001). CCI was higher in the early mortality group (0–30 days, p = 0.001), while ASA score was significantly higher across all time intervals (p = 0.020, 0.004, 0.001). ICU stay was longer in all mortality groups (all p = 0.001). Preoperative CRP was markedly higher in early and mid-term mortality groups (p = 0.001), while preoperative albumin was lower in early and long-term mortality (p = 0.039 and p = 0.011). Comparison of Categorical Variables

Male sex was significantly more common among patients who died in the early postoperative period (0–30 days) and in the 30–90-day interval, but not in the 90–365-day period. Intraoperative fractures were significantly associated with mortality only in the 30–90-day interval. Reduced pre-fracture mobility and dementia were strongly associated with higher mortality across all timeframes (all p < 0.01). Reoperation was associated only with 90–365-day mortality (p = 0.001). Cox Regression Analysis

Cox proportional hazards models were additionally constructed to evaluate overall survival dynamics across the study period. Age and ASA score remained significantly associated with increased mortality risk in multivariable models, while female sex demonstrated a protective effect. Effect sizes were directionally consistent between logistic and Cox models.

However, testing of the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals indicated time-dependency for certain covariates, particularly age and preoperative albumin (global test p < 0.001). This suggests that the effect of some predictors varied over time.

Accordingly, Cox regression findings should be interpreted as supportive survival estimates rather than definitive time-constant hazard ratios. Given this time-dependent behavior, the primary interpretation of mortality predictors in this study is based on the period-specific logistic regression models.

Discussion

A key finding of this study is that the predictors of mortality in geriatric hip fracture patients vary substantially depending on the postoperative time interval. Advanced age remained a consistent independent predictor across intervals, whereas the effect of sex was predominantly observed in the early postoperative phase. Markers of acute comorbidity burden, particularly the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and elevated preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP), were stronger predictors of early mortality (0–30 days). In contrast, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score was a more powerful determinant of long-term survival (90–365 days). Additionally, we observed an inverse association between time-to-surgery (TTS) and early mortality; however, this finding should not be interpreted as causal and most likely reflects confounding by indication and triage-related processes, whereby patients with greater acute clinical instability may have been prioritized for earlier surgical intervention.

Analysis of the demographic data in our study revealed that the majority of hip fracture cases were female (70.2%); however, mortality analysis demonstrated that male sex was associated with a significant survival disadvantage. This epidemiological distribution, consistent with the literature, supports the observation that although osteoporotic fractures are more common in women, post-fracture mortality risk is disproportionately higher in men.12-14

In our cohort, 30-day and 1-year mortality rates were 17.1% and 36.3%, respectively, which are higher than the commonly reported ranges for geriatric hip fracture populations (20%–30%).13-15 National registry data typically describe 30-day mortality between 8–12% and 1-year mortality between 20–30%, with marked increases among very elderly patients and those with substantial comorbidity burden. The relatively advanced mean age of our cohort (83.6 years), together with elevated CCI and ASA scores, likely accounts for this difference. Moreover, our study included exclusively patients treated with hemiarthroplasty, a subgroup often characterized by greater frailty compared with mixed-treatment cohorts. Therefore, the observed mortality rates should be interpreted within the context of case-mix severity rather than as an outlier finding. Consistent with this interpretation, age remained an independent predictor of mortality across all three postoperative intervals, underscoring the well-established impact of age-related decline in physiological reserve on postoperative outcomes.16,17

Independent of age, the most notable demographic finding of our study is the marked sex-based difference in mortality. Male sex emerged as a strong independent predictor of early postoperative mortality, while its association attenuated in later postoperative intervals. This pattern, commonly described in the literature as the “male disadvantage,” is frequently attributed to the higher comorbidity burden in male patients at the time of fracture and their greater susceptibility to postoperative complications—particularly pneumonia and cardiac events—compared with females.16-22

Another key finding of our study concerns the time-dependent predictive value of comorbidity assessment tools. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was a significant independent predictor of 0–30-day mortality, but its predictive strength declined in the 90–365-day period. In contrast, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, although not significant in the early postoperative phase, became one of the strongest independent risk factors for mortality in the 30–90-day and 90–365-day intervals.

CCI quantifies the number and severity of specific comorbid diseases, which, under the physiological stress of hip fracture and subsequent surgery, may precipitate early complications such as acute organ failure or cardiovascular events, thereby increasing early mortality.6,23

The ASA score, in contrast, provides a global assessment of the patient’s overall physiological status, organ function, and surgical resilience. In the medium- and long-term postoperative periods, when the acute effects of trauma have resolved, survival is influenced less by individual comorbid conditions and more by the patient’s baseline physiological reserve and chronic frailty. The increasing prognostic strength of the ASA score in our models highlights the dominant role of chronic frailty in postoperative recovery and long-term outcomes.24,25

The distinction between early- and long-term mortality is further supported by the temporal variability of inflammatory and nutritional markers. In our study, elevated preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were strong independent predictors of early (0–30 days) and intermediate (30–90 days) mortality. High CRP likely reflects an intensified systemic inflammatory response triggered by fracture trauma, surgical stress, and pre-existing comorbidities, which may rapidly exhaust physiological reserves and increase susceptibility to early postoperative complications. 26

In contrast, preoperative albumin—an established indicator of nutritional and metabolic reserve—did not significantly affect short-term mortality but emerged as an independent predictor of 90–365-day mortality. Low albumin reflects not only acute malnutrition but also chronic frailty, sarcopenia, and impaired protein synthesis. As the acute inflammatory phase subsides, these chronic deficits appear to play a dominant role in long-term survival. 27 Albumin did not demonstrate an independent association with early mortality after multivariable adjustment but emerged as a significant predictor in the 90–365-day model.

In ROC analyses, age yielded an AUC of approximately 0.70 across postoperative intervals, whereas CRP and CCI demonstrated AUC values ranging between 0.55 and 0.66 depending on the time window. Although statistically significant, these levels indicate only moderate discriminatory capacity and do not support the development of a standalone clinical prediction or decision-support model. Rather, these variables should be interpreted as contributory risk markers within a broader multidimensional clinical assessment framework.

The observed association between time to surgery (TTS) and mortality warrants cautious interpretation. Although an inverse relationship between TTS and early mortality was identified in both univariate and multivariable analyses, this pattern most likely reflects confounding by indication and perioperative triage rather than a causal effect of surgical timing.

Reverse causality is plausible, as clinical instability or acute medical complications may influence both surgical prioritization and mortality risk. Accordingly, the observed association between time-to-surgery and mortality should be interpreted as reflecting underlying disease severity rather than a causal effect of surgical timing. 28

These findings are consistent with previous large-scale observational studies demonstrating that the apparent association between early surgery and increased mortality is largely attributable to triage bias. Furthermore, randomized evidence from the HIP ATTACK trial suggests that accelerated surgery does not independently reduce short-term mortality. 29

The overall intraoperative fracture rate was 24.9%, with periprosthetic fractures accounting for 8.3%. Reported intraoperative periprosthetic femoral fracture rates in elderly patients undergoing hemiarthroplasty generally range between approximately 3% and 10%, with higher rates observed in cementless stems and in patients with advanced age and poor bone quality. 30 Within this context, the 8.3% rate observed in our cohort falls within the upper range of previously reported values.

Although intraoperative complications may impose additional physiological stress—particularly in frail individuals—causal inference regarding their direct contribution to mortality cannot be established in this retrospective study. Nonetheless, diminished baseline physiological reserve has consistently been associated with poorer postoperative outcomes in elderly hip fracture populations. 31

Therefore, we believe that in hip fracture management, not only acute surgical decisions but also the management of frailty and optimization of surgical quality are of critical importance.

Our study provides meaningful contributions to the literature on mortality risk assessment in geriatric hip fracture patients. The principal strengths of the study are as follows:

• Depth of temporal analyses: By examining mortality predictors across three clinically meaningful postoperative intervals (0–30 days, 30–90 days, and 90–365 days) using a predefined landmark modeling framework, this study provides a dynamic understanding of how the prognostic value of key variables—such as CCI, ASA, CRP, and albumin—evolves over time.

Although the findings of our study are valuable, several limitations should be considered. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which may limit external validity and generalizability. The cohort included only patients who underwent hemiarthroplasty at a tertiary care center; therefore, patients managed non-operatively, referred to other institutions, deemed unfit for surgery, or who died prior to surgical intervention were not captured. This design may have introduced selection bias, and the results should be interpreted as reflecting outcomes among surgically treated patients rather than the entire hip fracture population.

Additionally, no a priori sample size calculation was performed because this study included all consecutive eligible patients during the predefined retrospective study period. Although the overall sample size was moderate, the limited number of events in certain time intervals—particularly the 30–90-day model—may have reduced statistical power for detecting smaller effect sizes.

Second, the dataset lacked standardized and prospectively assessed frailty measurements such as the Frailty Index, Clinical Frailty Scale, or objective sarcopenia assessment. Although ASA score and preoperative albumin were included as indirect indicators of physiological reserve, these measures cannot fully capture the multidimensional construct of frailty, which encompasses functional status, cognitive performance, muscle mass, and resilience to stressors.

Third, the observed association between time to surgery and early mortality—an association subject to reverse causality and triage bias—cannot be interpreted causally due to the observational study design. Residual confounding cannot be excluded, as granular markers of acute clinical instability—such as hemodynamic compromise, sepsis, acute myocardial infarction, perioperative troponin elevation, and acute organ failure—were not available in this retrospective dataset. These factors may have influenced both surgical timing and mortality risk. Further prospective studies with standardized assessments of perioperative clinical severity are required to better elucidate this relationship.

Finally, although key biochemical markers such as CRP and albumin were included, the absence of more advanced cellular and molecular indicators of immunosuppression or immunosenescence (e.g., interleukin levels) limits a deeper understanding of the mechanistic pathways underlying early inflammatory responses.

Conclusions

The determinants of mortality in geriatric hip fracture patients demonstrate substantial temporal variability across the postoperative period. Advanced age was consistently associated with increased mortality across intervals, whereas the association with male sex was predominantly observed in the early postoperative phase.

Acute disease burden and systemic inflammatory response were more strongly associated with early postoperative mortality, whereas markers of chronic physiological reserve and frailty appeared to have greater relevance for longer-term survival.

These findings underscore the temporal heterogeneity of mortality risk following hip fracture. Given the retrospective and observational design, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these observations in different clinical settings.
