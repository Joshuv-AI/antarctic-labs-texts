Diabetes as a risk factor for tuberculosis disease
Authors: Juan VA Franco, Brenda Bongaerts, Maria-Inti Metzendorf, Agostina Risso, Yang Guo, Laura Peña Silva, Melanie Boeckmann, Sabrina Schlesinger, Johanna AAG Damen, Bernd Richter, Annabel Baddeley, Mathieu Bastard, Anna Carlqvist, Maria Nieves Garcia-Casal, Bianca Hemmingsen, Farai Mavhunga, Jennifer Manne-Goehler, Kerri Viney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is amongst the leading causes of death from an infectious disease, with an estimated 1.3 million deaths from TB in 2022. Approximately 25% of the global population is estimated to be infected with the TB bacterium, giving rise to 10.6 million episodes of TB disease in 2022. The prevalence of diabetes influences TB incidence and TB mortality. It is associated not only with an increased risk of TB disease but also death during TB treatment, TB relapse after treatment completion and multidrug‐resistant TB. Since 2011, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended collaborative TB and diabetes activities as outlined in the Collaborative Framework for Care and Control of TB and Diabetes.ObjectivesTo determine the prognostic value of diabetes mellitus (DM) in the general population of adults, adolescents and children for predicting tuberculosis disease.Search methodsWe searched the literature databases MEDLINE (via PubMed) and WHO Global Index Medicus, and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) on 3 May 2023 (date of last search for all databases); we placed no restrictions on the language of publication.Selection criteriaWe included retrospective and prospective cohort studies, irrespective of publication status or language. The target population comprised adults, adolescents and children from diverse settings, encompassing outpatient and inpatient cohorts, with varying comorbidities and risk of exposure to tuberculosis.Data collection and analysisWe used standard Cochrane methodology and the Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool. Prognostic factors assessed at enrolment/baseline included diabetes, as defined by the individual studies, encompassing patient‐reported status, abstracted from medical records or claims data, or diagnosed by plasma glucose/glycosylated haemoglobin. The primary outcome was the incidence of tuberculosis disease. The secondary outcome was recurrent TB disease. We performed a random‐effects meta‐analysis for the adjusted hazard ratios, risk ratios, or odds ratios, employing the restricted maximum likelihood estimation. We rated the certainty of the evidence using the GRADE approach.Main resultsWe included 48 cohort studies with over 61 million participants from the six WHO regions. However, the representation was variable as eight population‐based studies were from South Korea and 19 from China, with overlapping study periods, and only one from the African region (Ethiopia). All studies included adults, and nine studies also included children and adolescents. Most studies diagnosed DM based on clinical records, including fasting blood glucose levels or glucose‐lowering treatments. The studies did not distinguish between type 1 and type 2 DM; only one study focused on type 1 DM. Diagnosis and exclusion of TB were performed using culture or molecular WHO‐recommended rapid diagnostic tests (mWRD) in only 12 studies, which could have biassed the effect estimate. The median follow‐up time was five years (interquartile range 1.5 to 10, range 1 to 16.9), and the studies primarily reported an adjusted hazard ratio from a multivariable Cox‐proportional hazard model.Hazard Ratios (HR)The HR estimates represent the highest certainty of the evidence, explored through sensitivity analyses and excluding studies at high risk of bias. We present 95% confidence intervals (CI) and prediction intervals, which show between‐study heterogeneity represented in measuring the variability of effect sizes (i.e. the interval within which the effect size of a new study would fall considering the same population of studies included in the meta‐analysis).DM may increase the risk of tuberculosis disease (HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.51 to 2.40; prediction interval 0.83 to 4.39; 10 studies; 11,713,023 participants). The certainty of the evidence is low, due to a moderate risk of bias across studies and inconsistency. Considering a risk without diabetes of 129 cases per 100,000 population, this represents 102 more (59 to 153 more) cases per 100,000.When stratified by follow‐up time, the results are more consistent across < 10 years follow‐up (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.47 to 1.57; prediction interval 1.45 to 1.59; 7 studies; 10,380,872 participants). This results in a moderate certainty of the evidence due to a moderate risk of bias across studies.However, at 10 or more years of follow‐up, the estimates yield a wider CI and a higher HR (HR 2.44, 95% CI 1.22 to 4.88; prediction interval 0.09 to 69.12; 3 studies; 1,332,151 participants). The certainty of the evidence is low due to the moderate risk of bias and inconsistency.Odds Ratio (OR)DM may increase the odds of tuberculosis disease (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.27 to 2.04; prediction interval 0.96 to 2.70; 4 studies; 167,564 participants). Stratification by follow‐up time was not possible as all studies had a follow‐up < 10 years. The certainty of the evidence is low due to a moderate risk of bias and inconsistency.Risk Ratio (RR)The RR estimates represent the highest certainty of the evidence, explored through sensitivity analyses and excluding studies at high risk of bias. DM probably increases the risk of tuberculosis disease (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.80; prediction interval 1.38 to 1.85; 6 studies; 44,058,675 participants). Stratification by follow‐up time was not possible as all studies had a follow‐up < 10 years. The certainty of the evidence is moderate due to a moderate risk of bias.Authors' conclusionsDiabetes probably increases the risk of developing TB disease in the short term (< 10 years) and may also increase the risk in the long term (≥ 10 years). As glycaemic control and access to care may be potential effect modifiers of the association between diabetes and the risk of TB disease, the overall estimates should be interpreted with caution when applied locally. Policies targeted at reducing the burden of diabetes are needed to contribute to the aims of ending TB. Large population‐based cohorts, including those derived from high‐quality national registries of exposures (diabetes) and outcomes (TB disease), are needed to provide estimates with a high certainty of evidence of this risk across different settings and populations, including low‐ and middle‐income countries from different WHO regions. Moreover, studies including children and adolescents and currently recommended methods for diagnosing TB would provide more up‐to‐date information relevant to practice and policy.FundingWorld Health Organization (203256442)RegistrationPROSPERO registration: CRD42023408807

Background

Description of the health condition and context

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide and the second‐leading cause of death for a single infectious agent after COVID‐19 [1]. The estimated number of deaths from TB increased between 2019 and 2021, in contrast with the previous trend of a continuing decline [1]. The burden of TB has a widely inequitable distribution across the globe. More than two‐thirds of the estimated 10.6 million TB episodes are detected in the World Health Organization (WHO) Southeast Asia and Africa regions, with a lower proportion found in the Western Pacific, the Americas, the Eastern Mediterranean and Europe. Due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, there was an 18% decline in TB notifications between 2019 and 2020, although there has since been some recovery. Fluctuations in both TB notifications and deaths have impacted the lives of individuals and have negatively affected global efforts to end TB as a public health problem, aligned with the WHO 'End TB Strategy' and the UN 'Sustainable Development Goals' [1].

An estimated 2 billion people, equal to 25% of the global population, have been infected with the TB bacteria [1]. Approximately 5 to 10% of infected individuals will develop TB disease at some point in their lives, but some people – especially those with immunosuppressive conditions such as HIV infection – are at a higher risk of falling ill [2]. WHO estimated that in 2022, about 10.6 million people fell ill with TB, and 1.3 million (including 167,000 people with HIV) died from the disease [1].

Efforts to prevent the spread of TB include the early detection and effective treatment of TB and the provision of TB preventive treatment to people who are at highest risk of progressing from TB infection to TB disease, infection prevention and control measures, bacillus Calmette‐Guérin (BCG) vaccination, and the detection and management of the underlying health‐related risk factors that increase the risk of TB, such as HIV infection [3]. Tackling the social determinants of health that lead to higher transmission, morbidity and death rates amongst more at‐risk populations is also a critical component of preventive efforts [4]. This includes, for example, protecting and promoting the health of people in prisons and other closed settings, migrants, other socially disadvantaged groups, people living with HIV, diabetes, or other immunosuppressive disorders, and children [5].

Diabetes is a heterogeneous metabolic disease characterised by high blood glucose levels (hyperglycaemia) [6]. In type 1 diabetes, the body does not produce enough insulin, a hormone that helps regulate blood glucose. Due to the autoimmune destruction of insulin‐producing β‐cells of the pancreas, the resulting absolute deficiency of insulin necessitates treatment with exogenous insulin. Type 2 diabetes, which accounts for more than 95% of all diabetes, is hallmarked by insulin resistance. The body's cells become increasingly less responsive to insulin, resulting in an inability to utilise glucose effectively. In 2017, there were 9 million people with type 1 diabetes, the majority of them living in high‐income countries (7). And whilst very few studies report on the incidence of type 2 diabetes, its prevalence is ever‐increasing [8]. Worldwide, it is estimated that 537 million people between the ages of 20 and 79 years (10.5% of all adults in this age group) live with diabetes. Furthermore, the prevalence of diabetes is estimated to increase to 12.2% by 2045 [8].

Health outcomes

TB starts with tuberculosis infection (TBI) and, in subsets of individuals, progresses to tuberculosis disease (TB disease), depending on the ability of the host to control the infection. Individuals with TBI are infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis but do not have TB disease (sometimes called active TB) and cannot spread the infection to others. In some people, M tuberculosis overcomes the immune system and multiplies, progressing from TB infection to TB disease [2]. Half of the people who progress to TB disease do so within the first year after infection, whilst the incidence of progression decreases beyond five years [9].

TB disease can occur in pulmonary (lungs) and extrapulmonary sites (elsewhere in the body). Pulmonary TB disease is the most common form, and people with this form of TB usually present with a cough and an abnormal chest radiograph. Extrapulmonary TB can involve any other organ, most commonly affecting the larynx, lymph nodes, pleura, brain, kidneys, bones, and joints; extrapulmonary TB is usually not infectious, although laryngeal TB is an exception [2].

The diagnosis of TB commonly involves a clinical evaluation, imaging studies, and laboratory microbiology tests [10]. A clinical evaluation involves the assessment of disease symptoms, medical history (including a history of contact with someone with TB), and a physical examination. Imaging diagnostics, including a chest X‐ray or computed tomography (CT) scan, can indicate characteristic patterns associated with pulmonary TB. Rapid molecular tests, which detect the genetic material of the bacteria, are recommended by WHO as the initial diagnostic test for people presenting with signs and symptoms of TB (11). Culture‐based techniques are also used as they have increased sensitivity for detecting M tuberculosis. Other diagnostic tests include sputum smear microscopy and, for people living with HIV, lateral flow urine lipoarabinomannan assay (LF‐LAM). However, these tests have less sensitivity than the other methods and sputum smear microscopy is no longer recommended by WHO as the primary diagnostic test. The diagnostic approaches used in the included studies vary depending on local resources. In resource‐limited settings or those with limited access to diagnostics, diagnosis based on clinical and radiological findings may be relied upon for prompt treatment initiation. However, diagnostic testing with a molecular test is always encouraged (10). TB in children is typically characterised by paucibacillary disease. Therefore, a higher proportion of children may receive a clinical diagnosis, although molecular tests are also encouraged in this group, and a greater range of specimens can now be used, including stool (12).

TB can also occur in individuals following a successfully completed treatment for TB, also known as recurrent TB, which encompasses both relapse and reinfection [13]. Relapse refers to the reactivation of the same strain of M tuberculosis that caused the initial infection, whereas reinfection involves acquiring a new strain of M. tuberculosis following a previous TB infection. For people with recurrent TB disease, it can be challenging to differentiate between relapse and reinfection due to a similar clinical presentation and an inability to know whether the same strain caused the subsequent infection. However, a large proportion of recurrent TB is related to relapse [13].

Diabetes may increase the risk of both TB infection and TB disease. While the distinction between these two outcomes is crucial, it can be challenging to delineate in empirical studies, as the progression from TB infection to TB disease is not always tracked in individuals at risk due to the lack of systematic identification of TB infection. WHO issued a conditional recommendation to screen for TB in people seeking care or already in care and having clinical risk factors, including diabetes, in high‐prevalence areas (14). However, individuals who are at higher risk of developing TB disease may be targeted for TB screening. People accessing healthcare who have clinical risk factors for TB are in such a group. Screening aims to reduce the risk of poor treatment outcomes, health sequelae and adverse social and economic consequences, and also to reduce the community‐level prevalence of TB disease and subsequent incidence of new infections (14). Therefore, the focus of this review is on diabetes and TB disease.

Why it is important to do this review

Previous systematic reviews have provided estimates for the multivariable‐adjusted risk of TB attributable to alcohol (in 2017), diabetes (in 2018), tobacco (in 2010), HIV (data from UNAIDS), and undernutrition (in 2010) [15, 16, 17, 18]. WHO has commissioned updated reviews to assess the current prognostic value of some of these risk factors to update the Population Attributable Factor (PAF) estimates. PAFs inform policy and can help plan and prioritise prevention strategies. Two of these risk factors (diabetes and undernutrition) were prioritised to inform the WHO Global TB Report. A review on undernutrition and TB disease was recently published (19), and this review reports on diabetes and TB disease.

To determine the prognostic value of diabetes mellitus in the general population of adults, adolescents and children for predicting tuberculosis disease.

Methods

We followed the Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) (20) when conducting the review and the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 for the reporting (21). The methods of the protocol for this review [22] are based on a previous protocol co‐authored by the lead author of this protocol [23], and a recently published Cochrane review [24].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We included retrospective and prospective cohort studies. We included studies regardless of their publication status or language.

Targeted population

We included adults and children from the general population. These may include individuals from different settings (outpatient and inpatient cohorts) with variable distribution of comorbidities and risk of exposure to tuberculosis (TB) infection. We included studies across all regions of the world.

Types of outcomes to be predicted

Primary outcome

Incidence of tuberculosis (TB) disease (sometimes called 'active TB'), including pulmonary (lungs) and extrapulmonary sites (elsewhere in the body). The diversity of diagnostic approaches, including clinical evaluation, imaging studies, and study microbiology, inherently contributed to clinical heterogeneity across the included studies.

Secondary outcomes

Incidence of recurrent TB disease, including due to relapse, reinfection, or nonspecified.

Time points for outcome assessments

We considered the overall follow‐up, as well as the subgroups of medium‐term (< 10 years) and long‐term (≥ 10 years) follow‐up.

Search methods for identification of studies

Electronic searches

We conducted a unified search for two risk factors, diabetes[22] and undernutrition[19], informing two Cochrane Reviews of each risk factor independently due to the expected overlap in studies assessing multiple risk factors simultaneously. To identify relevant studies, we developed an empirically derived search strategy for MEDLINE. First, we sourced relevant cohort studies from five known systematic reviews on 'tuberculosis and diabetes' (= 4) [15, 26, 27, 28] and 'tuberculosis and undernutrition' (= 1) [18]. This yielded a set of 25 relevant references as a study pool for diabetes and five relevant studies for undernutrition. We complemented the undernutrition topic by carrying out exploratory searches, resulting in an expanded study pool for undernutrition of 20 studies. After removal of duplicates, the final study pool for both topics combined resulted in 43 relevant references of cohort studies (year range = 1957 to 2022), which were all indexed in MEDLINE.

We conducted a text analysis of these studies using the tools PubReMiner (hgserver2.amc.nl/cgi‐bin/miner/miner2.cgi) and Yale MeSH Analyzer (mesh.med.yale.edu/) [29]. Our final search strategy retrieved 39 of 43 relevant references from MEDLINE.

Using this strategy (see Supplementary material 1), we conducted a comprehensive search with no restrictions on language of publication, publication status, or study filter in the following sources from inception of each database to 3 May 2023 (date of last search for all databases):

Searching other resources

We screened the reference lists of the 43 relevant studies identified by extracting studies from known systematic reviews and exploratory searches described in the previous section. We also screened the reference lists of other potentially eligible studies or ancillary publications identified by our search. Furthermore, we contacted the study authors of included studies to identify any articles that we may have missed.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

We used Covidence software to identify and remove potentially duplicate records [30]. Two review authors (BB, JVAF) independently scanned abstracts and titles to determine which studies should be assessed further following an initial pilot test of the inclusion criteria. Two review authors categorised all potentially relevant records as full‐text, mapped records to studies, and classified studies as included studies, excluded studies, studies awaiting classification, or ongoing studies, according to the criteria in the Cochrane Handbook [31]. Any disagreements between the two review authors were resolved through consensus or by recourse to a third review author (BR). If a resolution was not possible, we designated the corresponding study as 'awaiting classification'. We contacted the study authors as needed for clarification to determine the health status or diagnostic criteria of included participants. If we received no response, clinical experts in our review group classified the study, or we listed studies as 'awaiting classification'. In Supplementary material 3, we documented the reasons for excluding studies and presented a PRISMA flow diagram showing the study selection process [21].

Data extraction and management

Measures of association to be extracted

We only included association measures adjusted for other confounding factors (e.g. undernutrition, sex, age, history of TB, immunosuppressive therapy or status such as HIV, alcohol use disorder, tobacco use, and socioeconomic status). We initially planned to extract unadjusted estimates, but we restricted our review to adjusted estimates (see 'Potential biases in the review process'). The type of effect measure was considered as a source of heterogeneity.

Dealing with missing data

We contacted study authors for additional or updated information that was not available from the published articles. If measures of the association had not been reported, or details on precision were missing, we estimated these from other information, including sample size, number of events, log‐rank P values and CIs, and Kaplan‐Meier curves [35, 36, 37].

Assessment of reporting bias

It is likely that reporting bias is an important problem in prognostic factor research. In addition, the prospective registration of prognostic factor studies is not common, making it impossible to check whether there were reporting deficiencies for all included studies [38].

We explored the presence of small‐study effects by creating a funnel plot with the measure of association and its standard error. We tested funnel plot asymmetry using the 'meta' package in STATA by producing contour‐enhanced funnel plots (39). We interpreted the results with caution, as these tests often have limited power to detect asymmetry, and many tests yield inadequate type‐I error rates [40].

Data synthesis and meta‐analysis approach

We performed meta‐analyses for the adjusted hazard ratios, risk ratios, or odds ratios for the incidence of TB disease. We presented the results of different effect measures separately and stratified our analyses by time points. We only conducted meta‐analyses when studies were sufficiently similar, and other covariates in the models were similar. If there had been sufficient studies at low risk of bias (e.g. 10 or more), we would have restricted our main meta‐analyses to this subgroup. We conducted our main meta‐analyses on studies that adjusted the analysis, if possible, for the following risk factors [2].

Results

Results of the search

The search identified 5328 records through databases and none from other sources, of which 5084 remained after deduplication; 5084 records were screened on title and abstract, and 4864 were classified as irrelevant. Then, 220 references were screened in full‐text, of which 165 studies were excluded for various reasons (Supplementary material 3), four were not retrievable (Supplementary material 4), and two were identified as ongoing studies (Supplementary material 5). Finally, we included 48 studies (49 references) reporting on 48 cohorts (Supplementary material 2). The details can be found in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Study design

We included 48 prospective and retrospective cohort studies with 61,216,819 participants. The median sample size was 46,028 participants (interquartile range 14,189 to 253,349, range 433 to 22,249,912). The median follow‐up was five years (interquartile range 1.5 to 10, range 1 to 16.9).

Eleven studies recruited participants between the 1960s and 1990s (Agarwal 2017 [45]; Cegielski 2012 [46]; Franke 2013 [47]; Hu 2014 [48]; John 2001 [49]; Lee 2013 [50]; Morán‐Mendoza 2010 [51]; Pealing 2015 [52]; Soh 2019 [53]; Wu 2015 [54]; Young 2012 [55]), 33 in the 2000s (Aibana 2016 [56]; Anaam 2020 [57]; Antonio‐Arques 2022 [58]; Baker 2012 [59]; Byberg 2012 [60]; Chen 2013 [61]; Cheng 2018 [62]; Choi 2021 [63]; Chu 2019 [64]; Dobler 2012 [65]; Fang 2015 [66]; Golub 2019 [67]; Hsien 2017 [68]; Ko 2022 [69]; Kuo 2013a [70]; Lee 2008 [71]; Lee 2016 [72]; Leung 2008 [73]; Li 2021 [74]; Liao 2016 [75]; Lin 2018 [76] ‐ NTC cohort; Lin 2018; Lo 2016 [77]; Mor 2016 [78]; Park 2023 [79]; Pearson 2019 [80]; Rajan 2017 [81]; Shen 2014 [82]; Teng 2019 [83] ‐ NHIS cohort; Tipayamongkholgul 2016 [84]; Yen 2017 [85]; Yoo 2021a [86]; Yoo 2021b [87]; Youn 2022 [88]), and four from the 2010s onwards (Eksombatchai 2023 [89]; Gedfew 2020 [90, 91]; Lu 2021 [92]; Park 2019 [93]).

Most studies used data from national health or insurance databases (Baker 2012; Cheng 2018; Choi 2021; Chu 2019; Dobler 2012; Eksombatchai 2023; Fang 2015; Golub 2019; Hsien 2017; Hu 2014; Kuo 2013a; Lee 2008; Lee 2013; Liao 2016; Lin 2018 ‐ NIHS cohort; Lo 2016; Lu 2021; Mor 2016; Park 2019; Park 2023; Pealing 2015; Pearson 2019; Shen 2014; Teng 2019; Wu 2015; Yen 2017; Yoo 2021a; Yoo 2021b) or TB registries (Aibana 2016; Ko 2022; Lee 2016; Morán‐Mendoza 2010). However, 11 studies collected data from clinical health records, one of which focused on patients living with chronic kidney disease (CKD) (Li 2021). Four studies were performed in population‐based cohorts (Cegielski 2012; Chen 2013; Lin 2018 ‐ NTC cohort; Soh 2019). One study was unclear about the origin of the data (Agarwal 2017).

We checked for the overlap of study populations between studies to avoid double‐counting participants in meta‐analyses (the details are presented in Supplementary material 7). We found eight studies with data from the National Health Insurance System in South Korea and 19 from the National Health Insurance Database in Taiwan and China, with overlapping study populations. We included those with different types of study data (effect estimates and outcomes) and study periods while prioritising studies in the general population. This approach narrowed the number of studies that could be included in a single analysis; in addition, we found three large studies reporting the same outcome for the general population in overlapping study periods (Ko 2022 2005‐2013, Kuo 2013a 2000‐2011, Lee 2013 1996‐2007). In this case, we included the two studies with the smallest overlap in the analysis (Lee 2013 and Ko 2022). This approach was explored in a sensitivity analysis in which we only included Kuo 2013a (see below).

The studies were conducted in the following WHO regions: five in the Americas (Peru, USA, Canada and Brazil), six from Europe (Spain, Greenland/Denmark, and the UK), one in Africa (Ethiopia), one in the Eastern Mediterranean region (Yemen), 25 from the Western Pacific region (China and Singapore) and 11 in the South‐East Asia region (India, South Korea and Thailand).

Study populations

The majority of the studies were conducted in the general population (36 studies), usually by recruiting large population‐based cohorts or using national databases with data linkage to clinical records and other registries (e.g. insurance database, TB notification systems) (Aibana 2016; Baker 2012; Byberg 2012; Cegielski 2012; Chen 2013; Cheng 2018; Choi 2021; Chu 2019; Dobler 2012; Eksombatchai 2023; Fang 2015; Golub 2019; Hsien 2017; Hu 2014; Ko 2022; Kuo 2013a; Lee 2013; Lee 2016; Liao 2016; Lin 2018 ‐ NHIS cohort; Lin 2018 ‐ NTC cohort; Lo 2016; Lu 2021; Morán‐Mendoza 2010; Park 2019; Park 2023; Pealing 2015; Pearson 2019; Shen 2014; Soh 2019; Teng 2019; Wu 2015; Yen 2017; Yoo 2021a; Yoo 2021b; Youn 2022). Eleven studies were based on data extracted from clinical health records (Anaam 2020; Franke 2013; Gedfew 2020; John 2001; Leung 2008; Rajan 2017; Tipayamongkholgul 2016; Young 2012), records within a CKD program (Li 2021) or linked reports from TB registries (Antonio‐Arques 2022; Lee 2008).

All studies included adults, and nine also included children and adolescents (43.1% ≤ 19 years Aibana 2016; 1 to 20% < 15 years Dobler 2012; 14.8% < 15 years Lee 2008; 8.2% ≤ 10 years Morán‐Mendoza 2010; 1.3% < 15 years Pearson 2019; 49% < 18 years Rajan 2017; 37 to 57% < 20 years Shen 2014; 5.5% < 20 years Youn 2022; ~15% < 19 years Young 2012).

Definition of diabetes

Most studies diagnosed diabetes mellitus (DM) based on the information in clinical patient records, including fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels or prescription of glucose‐lowering treatments. Most studies did not distinguish between type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Only one study included people with type 1 diabetes mellitus (Shen 2014). However, two studies lacked relevant descriptions (Cegielski 2012; Gedfew 2020), whereas eight based a diabetes diagnosis on self‐reported information, either with or without validation by clinical records (Aibana 2016; Baker 2012; Byberg 2012; Chen 2013; Lee 2008; Lu 2021; Shen 2014; Soh 2019).

Outcomes

Except for three studies (Anaam 2020; Eksombatchai 2023; Franke 2013), all studies reported the primary outcome of the incidence of TB disease (46 studies). Amongst the 36 studies based on national databases or population‐based cohorts, data on the incidence of TB disease was mostly collected from registries where TB was notified, which is mandatory in most countries. In other studies, the data on incident TB were collected from clinical records or TB programmes. In most studies, diagnosis was reached through clinical assessment or active surveillance and a positive acid‐fast smear on a sputum sample. Few studies, however, included a molecular diagnosis or a culture‐based confirmation of the diagnosis in their algorithm, which was considered to increase the risk of bias in the outcome assessment (see below).

Seven studies reported the incidence of recurrent TB disease (relapse or reinfection) (Anaam 2020; Eksombatchai 2023; Franke 2013; Golub 2019; Tipayamongkholgul 2016; Yoo 2021b; Youn 2022).

Diabetes type and control (glycosylated haemoglobin)

Only one study, with 25,975 participants, included people with type 1 diabetes mellitus (Shen 2014), which was not included in the main analysis due to the outlier nature of the estimate and the different nature of the diagnosis (HR 4.23, 95% CI 2.43 to 7.36). Nonetheless, this study provided important information on sex and age differences, so it was incorporated into previous subgroup analyses, contributing to substantial statistical heterogeneity.

The included studies evaluated diabetes control, using multiple definitions based on fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and complications, including emergency visits and hospitalisations due to diabetes complications. Therefore, we could not pool the different subgroups, and we present them by study in Figure 11. Several studies found a higher HR for TB disease amongst participants with higher FPG (Ko 2022, P = 0.02; Lee 2016, P < 0.01; Yoo 2021b, P < 0.01) and higher HbA1c (Leung 2008, P < 0.01). Moreover, a higher HR was also found in participants with a higher number of annual emergency visits (P < 0.01) or hospitalisations (P < 0.01) (Shen 2014). Nonetheless, one study could not identify differences between participants with or without complications (P = 0.30) or those with a higher vs lower Diabetes Complications Severity Index (a measure that uses diagnostic codes and laboratory values into a metric quantifying the diabetes complications across body systems, P = 0.09) (Baker 2012).

Sensitivity analyses

In our protocol, we noted that our primary analyses would be based on studies with a low risk of bias in some domains of the risk of bias tool, QUIPS, and that further sensitivity analyses for other domains would be explored subsequently. Supplementary material 8 describes the different sensitivity analyses for HR, including 95% CIs and prediction intervals. Estimates were robust across all analyses. Furthermore, considering that we also had to select which studies to include from overlapping study populations, we also explored the robustness of the analysis by including Kuo 2013a instead of Lee 2013 and Ko 2022 and found no substantial differences to the main estimates. Sensitivity analysis for RR was conducted following the same process, and we found our main analysis robust for all risk of bias domains except prognostic factor measurement, where the estimates were higher when restricted to studies at a low risk of bias in this domain (see Supplementary material 8). Each analysis restricting studies to a low risk of bias in one domain included studies with a high risk of bias in another domain. Therefore, the certainty of the evidence would have been lower, given the need to downgrade two levels due to the risk of bias. We chose, therefore, an alternative approach by excluding studies at high risk of bias, retaining precision and downgrading one level of the certainty of the evidence due to moderate risk of bias for 'overall' and '< 10 years' estimates. Sensitivity analysis for OR was not possible due to the few studies reporting on these estimates.

Recurrent tuberculosis

Six studies with 1,478,559 participants reported this outcome (Anaam 2020; Eksombatchai 2023; Franke 2013; Golub 2019; Tipayamongkholgul 2016; Youn 2022). Diabetes may result in a higher risk of recurrence, but the results were heterogeneous in magnitude across effect measures and their confidence intervals were wide (HR 1.35, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.42, I2 = 95.51%; OR 3.78, 95% CI 1.84 to 7.78; RR 2.76, 95% CI 1.66 to 4.60, see Figure 12). Discussion

Summary of the main results

We included 48 cohort studies with over 61 million participants from the six WHO regions. All studies included adults, and nine also included children and adolescents. The median follow‐up time was five years, and the studies primarily reported an adjusted HR from a multivariable Cox‐proportional hazard model.

Based on moderate‐to‐low certainty of evidence, diabetes mellitus (DM) probably increases the risk of TB disease. The certainty of the evidence is moderate at less than 10 years follow‐up and low at 10 or more years follow‐up. DM may also increase the risk of recurrent TB.

Overall completeness and applicability of the evidence

The geographical representation of the countries with the highest burden of TB is partially represented in the current body of evidence. However, the representation of the different regions and populations was variable. We found eight studies from the National Health Insurance System in South Korea and 19 from the National Health Insurance Database in Taiwan and China, with overlapping study populations and only one from the African region (Ethiopia). Moreover, few studies assessed the risk of diabetes in children, with inconsistent results, even though they represented 12% of the new cases of TB in 2022 [1]. Children usually have a higher risk of developing TB, including severe forms, and they are usually more affected by type 1 diabetes mellitus; therefore, the differential effects of diabetes need to be further investigated in children (12). Our review only included one study on people with type 1 diabetes mellitus (Shen 2014), which constitutes the minority (< 5%) of diabetes cases, so these risk estimates might be useful for population‐level estimates of the burden of TB attributable to diabetes. Still, they might be less informative for the subgroup of people with type 1 diabetes mellitus.

Most studies diagnosed DM based on clinical records, including fasting blood glucose levels or glucose‐lowering treatments; however, the diagnosis and exclusion of TB were usually not performed using culture or molecular WHO‐recommended rapid diagnostic test (mWRD), which could have biased the estimate of the effect. However, clinical diagnosis has an important role, as the sputum samples from people with reduced immunity or paucibacillary disease may have poor sensitivity. The diagnosis of TB has evolved, and the WHO Consolidated guidelines on TB diagnosis recommend mWRD as the initial diagnostic test, while culture remains the gold standard for TB diagnosis [11]. Both culture and molecular methods were rarely used in the included studies; therefore, the applicability of the findings might need to be adjusted to new evidence on the true incidence of TB disease based on more accurate diagnostic technologies. Overall, current diagnostic tools, including mWRD and culture, are less sensitive for children; hence, TB incidence may have been underestimated in this population [11, 12].

The proportion of people with diabetes in the represented countries is also variable. Our main estimates are based on studies from China, South Korea, Spain, Peru, and the USA, representing a mix of middle‐income and high‐income countries. Nonetheless, they may have different realities regarding the epidemiology of diabetes and TB. For instance, diabetes has a higher prevalence in the Eastern Mediterranean Region [94], whereas diabetes‐related mortality is higher in Southeast Asia (95). Diabetes foot complications are more frequent in the Latin American and African Region (96). We found that glycaemic control and complications may be potential effect modifiers of the association between diabetes and the risk of TB disease, although these inferences were based on few studies. Nonetheless, even the differing aspects of the epidemiology of diabetes control in these countries may affect the overall estimate. Even within high‐income countries, there might be internal variability in glycaemic control, particularly amongst ethnic minorities and other marginalised groups (97).

The link between diabetes, poor glycaemic control, and immunosuppression is well known and involves different mechanisms, including the change in biochemical parameters (pH), an enriched environment for pathogens and an increased inflammatory response and oxidative stress (98). One of our included studies also identified an increased risk of other infections, including urinary tract and skin infections (Mor 2016).

The results of this review will be used to calculate the annual Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) of diabetes for TB in the WHO Global TB Report. Even when we pooled overall estimates and estimates stratified for follow‐up in this review, it remains a challenge to identify the most optimal estimate to inform the PAF, considering the small variations across effect measures (HR, RR, OR) and the composition of populations. As we found glycaemic control as a potential effect modifier for TB risk, our main estimates may not be applicable across populations with a variable degree of diabetes control. Moreover, case‐mixed populations of adults, people with immunosuppression from HIV and other risk factors may pose other challenges in estimating an accurate PAF with the results of this review. Upcoming Cochrane reviews on other risk factors (e.g. undernutrition) will inform updated PAF estimates of the WHO Global TB Report.

Certainty of the evidence

The certainty of the evidence was mostly moderate for the large body of evidence, which contributed to the estimates based on 0 to 10 years of follow‐up. The main reason for downgrading the certainty of the evidence was a moderate risk of bias, considering the study limitations, primarily due to the limitations in the adjusted models in terms of variables included and the assessment of the outcome (lack of confirmation by culture or other methods). Additionally, a common reason for downgrading the certainty of the evidence was inconsistency, as the prediction intervals for some estimates were wide and included the null. Unlike the related review on undernutrition as a risk factor for tuberculosis (19), we did not downgrade due to indirectness for the single outlier study conducted between 1971 and 1992, where the characteristics of the population and context of outcome assessment are substantially different to current practice (Cegielski 2012), as this study contributed primarily to statistical heterogeneity, for which the evidence was already downgraded due to the wide prediction interval.

We did not downgrade due to imprecision, as the effect estimates and confidence intervals substantially excluded the scenario of null risk, which is the minimally contextualised approach suggested for GRADEing prognostic research [43, 44]. Moreover, we were unable to identify a strong suspicion of publication bias in our contour‐enhanced funnel plot (Figure 4). We did not consider upgrading the certainty of the evidence as we could not identify a large unequivocal effect or a confirmed dose‐response gradient [44].

Potential bias in the review process

Our review has several limitations. Firstly, we restricted our analysis to studies of low‐to‐moderate risk of bias, which is a small deviation from our protocol, in which we specified that our analysis would be based on studies with low risk of bias in specific domains of the QUIPS tool. We explored the effects of this decision through sensitivity analyses, and we believe that the current estimates provide a higher certainty of the evidence in terms of minimising bias and maximising precision. Secondly, we could not sufficiently explain statistical heterogeneity through subgroup analysis and had scarce data to run metaregression. Furthermore, we had limited data for some effect measures (OR and RR) to conduct sensitivity analyses related to the risk of bias. Thirdly, we initially set out to analyse unadjusted and adjusted estimates. However, considering the large volume of information, we decided to restrict our analysis to studies with adjustments for covariables. Fourth, we had planned to conduct a subgroup analysis based on the type of effect measure, but we presented these data separately instead (HR, RR, and OR). Finally, we initially planned to assess the recurrence of TB as an outcome. However, considering our inclusion criteria (individuals free from TB at baseline), we might not have been able to capture the right study design for this outcome. Instead, a review focusing on the long‐term follow‐up of people with TB disease might provide a more comprehensive assessment of this outcome [13].

Agreements and disagreements with other reviews and studies

The previous review that informed the TB report included 14 studies (eight cohort studies) and estimated a 1.5‐fold increased risk of developing TB disease for people with diabetes compared to people without diabetes (15). This is largely concordant with the highest certainty estimate of our review (HR 1.52 for studies with less than 10‐year follow‐up).

An earlier review from 2008 included three prospective cohort studies and estimated a pooled risk ratio of 3.11 (95% CI 2.27 to 4.26; I2 = 39%) (27), which differs from our estimate from six cohort studies (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.80). Nonetheless, newer reviews, including recent evidence, found more similar estimates. A review in 2021 included 47 studies, and, amongst the ten included cohort studies, found an OR for TB disease of 1.92 (95% CI 1.53 to 2.4, I2 = 96.2%). However, when the authors restricted their analysis to studies at low risk of bias (assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute instrument), the OR was 1.6 (95% CI 1.4 to 1.7; I2 = 88%), similar to our findings (99). A more recent systematic review estimated a pooled OR of 2.77 (95% CI 1.9 to 4.05, I2 = 57.4%) but included eight studies of various designs (cross‐sectional, cohort, and case‐control) (28).

Authors' conclusions

Diabetes mellitus probably increases the risk of tuberculosis (TB) within the first decade of follow‐up and may also increase the risk in the long term (10 or more years). Policies targeted at the reduction of diabetes could contribute to the aim of ending TB.

Large population‐based cohorts, including those derived from high‐quality national registries of exposures (diabetes) and outcomes (TB disease), are needed to provide high‐certainty estimates of this risk across different settings and populations, including low‐ and middle‐income countries from different WHO regions. Furthermore, a thorough analysis of the characteristics of diabetes (glycaemic control, duration of the diagnosis, amongst others) could provide more clarity about how these factors affect the risk estimates of TB disease. Moreover, studies that include children and adolescents and state‐of‐the‐art methods for diagnosing TB would provide more up‐to‐date information relevant to practice and policy.
