Palonosetron-loaded binary ethosome-incorporated transdermal gel: Formulation optimization, in vitro/in vivo evaluation, and therapeutic efficacy in chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting
Authors: Qianru Wang, Yu Luo, Taomin Hu, Wen Xiong, Yuan Xu, Huijun Liang, Tao Zhang, Xiurong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop a transdermal gel formulation of palonosetron-loaded binary ethosomes (PAL-BE-G) and systematically evaluate its in vitro physicochemical properties, in vivo pharmacodynamics, and pharmacokinetics. PAL-BE was fabricated via the injection-ultrasonication method, and its formulation was optimized using Box–Behnken design. The optimized PAL-BE appeared as a clear, transparent dispersion with pale blue opalescence and exhibited excellent stability at 4 °C. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy verified the formation of hydrogen bonds between palonosetron (PAL) and excipients, with no chemical interactions detected. Subsequently, PAL-BE-G was prepared with 0.5% carbomer 940 as the gel matrix, which maintained favorable storage stability at 4 °C for 120 days. In vitro release assays showed a 94% cumulative release rate of PAL within 36 h, confirming the sustained-release property of the gel. In vitro transdermal permeation studies demonstrated that PAL-BE-G possessed superior skin penetration efficiency. Safety evaluations confirmed the excellent biocompatibility of PAL-BE-G with no significant skin irritation. In vivo pharmacodynamic assays revealed that PAL-BE-G exerted comparable antiemetic efficacy against chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) to commercial PAL injection. Pharmacokinetic studies indicated that the absolute bioavailability of PAL-BE-G was 18.74 ± 3.26%, with sustained drug release in vivo. In conclusion, the successfully developed PAL-BE-G integrates high transdermal permeability, ideal sustained-release behavior, and favorable safety profiles. It provides a novel therapeutic alternative for CINV patients, especially those unable to tolerate oral administration, and offers experimental and theoretical support for the transdermal delivery of poorly soluble drugs.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) is one of the most prevalent adverse reactions in cancer chemotherapy. Its pathogenesis mainly involves the stimulation of gastrointestinal chromaffin cells by chemotherapeutic agents to release serotonin (5-HT), which further activates the vagus nerve and central chemoreceptor trigger zone, ultimately triggering emesis (Gao et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2021). CINV not only causes dehydration, metabolic disorders, and impaired self-care ability in patients but also severely compromises their quality of life and compliance with antitumor therapy (Shirley, 2021a). Therefore, improving the control rate of CINV has become a critical and urgent issue in clinical cancer chemotherapy.

Palonosetron (PAL), a second-generation 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, exhibits higher efficacy, selectivity, and binding affinity than first-generation agents such as ondansetron and granisetron (Fung, 2025; Shirley, 2021b). By binding to 5-HT3 receptors, PAL blocks the 5-HT release induced by chemotherapy, thereby effectively preventing and treating both acute and delayed emesis (Yoo et al., 2025). Clinical studies have confirmed the sustained and potent antiemetic efficacy of PAL in CINV management, significantly reducing emetic episodes, alleviating nausea severity, and improving patients' quality of life. Currently, commercial PAL formulations are mainly limited to injections and oral capsules (Kraev et al., 2023; Aapro et al., 2022). Intravenous injections require professional puncture and carry risks such as injection site pain (1.2%), phlebitis (0.8%), and local tissue necrosis. Although oral formulations avoid invasive administration, their absorption is severely compromised by chemotherapy-related gastrointestinal reactions (e.g., delayed emesis, intestinal obstruction). Studies report that approximately 18% of patients fail to complete oral dosing due to intractable vomiting. Thus, the clinical limitations of existing PAL formulations highlight an urgent demand for novel delivery systems to meet clinical needs.

Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDS) represent the third major administration route following oral and parenteral delivery (Sabbagh and Kim, 2022; Phatale et al., 2022; Karve et al., 2024). Compared with conventional routes, TDDS offers distinct advantages: it bypasses the gastrointestinal first-pass effect to improve bioavailability, reduces systemic adverse reactions, enhances patient compliance, and is particularly suitable for patients unable to take oral medications due to vomiting. However, the stratum corneum of the skin acts as a major barrier, resulting in low drug bioavailability (Andrej et al., 2020).

To overcome the skin barrier, various novel transdermal nanocarriers have been developed, among which ethosomes are a promising candidate. Ethosomes are flexible liposomes composed of phospholipids, high concentrations of low-molecular-weight alcohols (e.g., ethanol), and water (Atoosa et al., 2022; Mahajan et al., 2024). With high deformability and permeability, ethosomes effectively facilitate drug penetration through the stratum corneum into the deep dermis and even systemic circulation. Nevertheless, the volatility of ethanol compromises the stability of ethosomes (Paiva-Santos et al., 2021). To address this issue, binary ethosomes (BE) have been proposed. BE incorporates propylene glycol to markedly reduce ethanol content while preserving transdermal efficiency (Xiong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). This formulation inhibits vesicle aggregation, regulates particle size, enhances drug penetration depth, and resolves the stability limitations of traditional ethosomes, providing an innovative strategy for transdermal delivery of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs.

PAL is a weak base (pKa = 9.1) with moderate lipophilicity (log P = 2.2) and a low molecular weight (296.4 Da), enabling efficient encapsulation into the lipid bilayer of ethosomes. Its aqueous solubility (1.2 mg/mL at 25 °C) allows sufficient loading in the hydro-ethanolic core of BE. Furthermore, PAL is stable at 4–60 °C and shows no significant degradation in 20–45% ethanol, making it highly compatible with the BE formulation.

Despite the superior transdermal performance of BE, its high fluidity leads to practical challenges, including uncontrollable drug distribution, short skin retention time, and high transdermal drug loss (Natsheh and Touitou, 2020; Wang et al., 2024). This study innovatively combines BE with hydrogel to retain the high permeability of BE while leveraging the excellent adhesion and biocompatibility of hydrogel to prolong skin retention. The enhanced local drug concentration reduces administration frequency, offering a novel approach for optimizing topical drug delivery. As an advanced semi-solid delivery system, gel formulations employ pH-responsive polymers (e.g., carbomer 940) that form a three-dimensional network upon pH-induced swelling (Rahbar et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2018). This endows the system with thixotropy, good spreadability, and skin conformability, enabling precise drug adhesion and controlled release at the target site to better meet the requirements of topical administration.

Current research on PAL formulations focuses on conventional transdermal patches (Karve et al., 2024; Ghaferi et al., 2024), injectable sustained-release microspheres (Su et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020), and orally disintegrating tablets (Lin et al., 2015). However, all these formulations have critical drawbacks and fail to fundamentally solve the problem of insufficient CINV control. Conventional transdermal patches suffer from low bioavailability; injectable sustained-release microspheres cannot overcome the invasiveness of parenteral administration; orally disintegrating tablets do not address the inability of vomiting patients to tolerate oral dosing. Notably, no domestic or international studies have reported PAL transdermal lipid-based formulations, and no such products are commercially available. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a novel PAL-loaded binary ethosome transdermal gel (PAL-BE-G) to overcome the limitations of existing formulations and further enhance the antiemetic efficacy of PAL for CINV. PAL-BE-G resolves the failure of oral administration in patients with severe vomiting, improves the bioavailability of traditional transdermal formulations, and enhances patient medication adherence.

In this study, PAL-BE was first prepared via the injection-ultrasonication method and then formulated into PAL-BE-G with carbomer 940 as the gel matrix. The physicochemical characterization and in vitro performance evaluation were conducted. Furthermore, the safety, in vivo antiemetic efficacy, and pharmacokinetic behavior of the formulation were systematically investigated. This research aims to overcome the limitations of traditional PAL formulations, provide a safer and more effective antiemetic strategy for clinical cancer patients, and offer a theoretical basis and experimental reference for the development of novel PAL formulations and innovative delivery routes.

Materials and methods

Materials

Palonosetron reference standard (LK-021812-2,110,001) was purchased from Jinan Hongkangda Chemical Co., Ltd. Soybean phospholipid (T25S11F125782) was obtained from Aweito Shanghai Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Polysorbate-80 (C15064851) was supplied by Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Anhydrous ethanol (20220306) was purchased from Chengdu Haixing Chemical Reagent Factory. Sodium lauryl sulfate (T105485) was obtained from Tianjin Keoumi Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Hematoxylin-eosin (BL700B) was supplied by White Shark Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Ultrafiltration tubes (3 kDa) were purchased from Millipore China Co., Ltd.

Malvern laser particle size analyzer (ZEN3690, Malvern Panalytical, UK), high-performance liquid chromatograph (LC-2030CPlus, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan), ultrasonic cell disruptor (JY92-IIN, Ningbo Xinzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), refrigerated centrifuge (Sorvall ST16R, Thermo Fisher Scientific China Co., Ltd.), thermostatic magnetic stirrer (DF-101S, Shanghai Lichenbangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.), transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 12, Thermo Fisher Scientific), tissue dehydrator (JT-12S, Wuhan Junjie Electronics Co., Ltd.), paraffin embedding machine (JB-L5, Wuhan Junjie Electronics Co., Ltd.), and intelligent transdermal diffusion tester (TP-6, Tianjin Jingtuo Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.) were used in this study.

Preparation of PAL-L-G

PAL liposomes (PAL-L) were prepared using the thin-film dispersion method. Briefly, 0.30% PAL was dissolved in 24% anhydrous ethanol, followed by the addition of 1.24% Tween-80 and 2.46% soybean phospholipids. After ultrasonic dissolution, 3 mL of ultrapure water was added. The mixture was placed in a rotary evaporator (70 °C, 75 rpm, 15 min) to remove ethanol, and 10 mL of ultrapure water was added for hydration via rotary evaporation for another 15 min. The mixture was then treated with a cell disruptor (probe No. 3, 130 W, 5 min) to obtain PAL-L. PAL-L was mixed with 1% blank carbomer gel at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio to prepare PAL-L-G.

Preparation of PAL-E-G

0.30% PAL was dissolved in 24% anhydrous ethanol, followed by the addition of 1.24% Tween-80 and 2.46% soybean phospholipids. After ultrasonic dissolution, the mixture was stirred at 60 °C and 300 rpm, and 71.90% ultrapure water was slowly added dropwise. The mixture was maintained at 60 °C for 20 min and then treated with a cell disruptor (probe No. 3, 130 W, 5 min) to obtain PAL ethosomes (PAL-E). PAL-E was mixed with 1% blank carbomer gel at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio to prepare PAL-E-G.

Preparation of PAL-BE

PAL-BE was fabricated via the injection–ultrasonication method (Jiang et al., 2020). The finalized preparation parameters were set as follows: PAL concentration of 0.3% (w/w), SDS concentration of 0.1% (w/w), ethanol/propylene glycol volume ratio of 7:3, preparation temperature of 60 °C, ultrasonication duration of 5 min, and ultrasonic power of 130 W. Among all formulation variables, the aqueous phase proportion, soybean phospholipid concentration, and Tween 80 concentration exerted the most significant effects on encapsulation efficiency; accordingly, these three factors were selected for multifactor optimization to determine the optimal formulation. The formulation was optimized using a Box-Behnken design with encapsulation efficiency (EE) and particle size as dual response variables, as both are critical for transdermal delivery.

Preparation of PAL-BE-G

Preliminary experiments confirmed that carbomer gel exhibits good spreadability, uniformity, skin adhesion, and non-irritation. Briefly, 1 g of carbomer 940 was weighed and added to 100 mL of water, stirred, and allowed to swell fully. The pH was adjusted to 7.4 with triethanolamine to form the blank gel. PAL-BE (3 mg/mL) was mixed with the blank gel at a 1:1 ratio to prepare PAL-BE-G. Additionally, 4% azone and 4% menthol were added as transdermal penetration enhancers.

HPLC method

The content of PAL was determined using an LC-2030C Plus HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a Shimadzu WondaSil C18 Superb column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile–0.025 mol/L sodium dihydrogen phosphate buffer (containing 0.1% triethylamine) (69:31, v/v). The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, column temperature was 30 °C, detection wavelength was 305 nm, and injection volume was 10 μL.

Method validation confirmed excellent specificity, linearity (2.00–100.00 μg/mL, R2 = 0.9997), precision (RSD < 2.0%), and accuracy (recovery rate: 98.59%–103.45%), indicating that the HPLC method was suitable for quantitative analysis of PAL.

Characterization of PAL-BE

Investigation of particle size, polydispersity index, and potential

Take a sample of fresh PAL-BE and measure its particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer.

Stability assessment

Freshly prepared PAL-BE samples were divided into three groups and stored at 4 °C, 25 °C, and 32 °C in sealed, light-proof containers. Particle size and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were measured periodically until significant changes indicated vesicle oxidation or rupture. Formal accelerated stability studies (40 °C/75% RH) and long-term stability testing will be performed in subsequent industrial development and preclinical research.

Evaluation of PAL-BE content and encapsulation efficiency

The EE of PAL-BE was determined using the ultrafiltration centrifugation method (Wang et al., 2025). Briefly, 0.5 mL of PAL-BE was placed in an ultrafiltration tube and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The free PAL content in the filtrate was measured by HPLC. In parallel, 0.5 mL of PAL-BE was demulsified with acetonitrile, and the total PAL content was determined. EE and loading efficiency (LE) were calculated as follows:(1)EE%=W2−W1W2×100%(2)LE%=W2−W1W3×100%

Note: In the formula, W1 represents the weight of the free drug, W2 represents the weight of the prescription drug, and W3 represents the total weight of the prescription drug and lecithin.

Determination of deformability

Deformability (DF) was determined using the standardized filter membrane extrusion method. DF reflects the ability of vesicular carriers to deform under mechanical stress, which is critical for transdermal penetration (Weng Larsen and Larsen, 2009). The initial particle size of PAL-BE was recorded as S₁. The sample was extruded 10 times through a 0.1 μm microporous membrane under constant pressure, and the post-filtration particle size was recorded as S₂. DF was calculated as:(3)DF%=S2S1×100%

Note: In the formula, S1 represents the initial particle size, and S2 represents the particle size after 10 filtrations.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

TEM specimens were prepared using the negative staining method. A clean copper grid was placed on sterile filter paper, and 20 μL of blank binary ethosomes (BE) and 50-fold diluted PAL-BE suspension were separately pipetted onto the grid and allowed to air-dry at room temperature. Next, 2% phosphotungstic acid solution (pH 6.8) was added to stain the vesicles for 5 min. After removing excess staining solution, the grid was air-dried again at room temperature, and the vesicular morphology was observed and characterized under transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra of PAL raw material, physical mixture, and PAL-BE were recorded using the KBr pellet method. KBr powder was ground and compressed into pellets, and samples were evenly coated on the pellets. Spectra were collected at 4 cm−1 resolution over the range of 4000–400 cm−1.

Characterization of PAL-BE-G

pH and viscosity determination of PAL-BE-G

The pH of PAL-BE-G was determined according to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). Three 1 g samples were diluted 10-fold with ultrapure water, and pH was measured in parallel at room temperature. Viscosity was measured using an NDJ-4S rotary viscometer (No. 4 rotor, 6 rpm) at room temperature with three replicate samples.

Examination of preliminary stability

Fresh PAL-BE-G was divided into three groups and stored at 4 °C, 25 °C, and 32 °C in sealed, light-proof containers. Changes in appearance, color, and drug content were monitored regularly.

PAL-BE-G in Vitro Release

In vitro release was evaluated using a modified paddle method in 500 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) at 32 °C with a stirring speed of 100 rpm [29]. Equivalent doses of PAL raw material gel (PAL—S—G), PAL physical mixture gel (PAL-PM-G), and PAL-BE-G were sealed in 3.5 kDa dialysis bags and fixed to the paddles. At predetermined intervals, 1 mL of release medium was sampled and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The PAL content was analyzed by HPLC, and the cumulative release rate was calculated to plot the release curve.

PAL-BE-G transdermal delivery

Preparation of excised skin

A 250 g male SD rat was euthanised by dislocation following ether anaesthesia, with its limbs secured to the operating table. The abdominal hair was shaved clean using a razor, and hair debris was rinsed away with physiological saline solution. The abdominal skin was excised, along with the subcutaneous fat and tissue, and the area was thoroughly rinsed repeatedly with physiological saline. Excess saline was absorbed using filter paper. The prepared rat skin was wrapped in aluminium foil, sealed within a self-adhesive pouch, and stored at −80 °C in a freezer for subsequent use.

Determination of cumulative transdermal permeation rate

Transdermal permeation was evaluated using the Franz diffusion method (Sguizzato et al., 2020; Hallan et al., 2021). The effective permeation area was 1.43 cm2, receiver chamber volume was 15 mL, receiver fluid was saline containing 30% ethanol (to ensure PAL solubility), water bath temperature was 32 °C, and stirring speed was 600 rpm. Although this concentration may enhance permeation compared to physiological conditions, all formulation groups (including controls) were tested under identical conditions, ensuring the validity of comparative ranking. This is a widely accepted approach for in vitro screening of poorly soluble drugs.

PAL—S—G, PAL-L-G, and PAL-E-G were used as control formulations. Rat skin was mounted between the donor and receiver chambers (stratum corneum facing the donor chamber), and air bubbles were removed. After 30 min equilibration, 1 g of gel (PAL concentration: 0.79 mg/g) was applied to the donor chamber. At predetermined intervals, 0.3 mL of receiver fluid was sampled and replaced with fresh medium. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was analyzed by HPLC. The cumulative permeation amount per unit area (Q) was calculated as:(4)Q=∑n=1iCnVA

Note: In the equation, C₁ denotes the drug mass concentration (μg·mL−1) measured at the first sampling point, A represents the effective permeation area of the diffusion reservoir (A = 1.43 cm2), and V denotes the volume of the receiving reservoir (15 mL). The steady-state transdermal flux rate (μg·cm−2·h−1) is defined as the slope of the linear portion when cumulative permeation (Q, μg·cm−2) is plotted against time (t).

Evaluation of skin retention

After 24 h of transdermal testing, the skin was removed, rinsed with saline, minced, and added to 15 mL of 75% ethanol. The mixture was homogenized, sonicated for 30 min, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The PAL content was analyzed by HPLC, and skin retention (Q_z) was calculated as:(5)Qz=CVA

Note: In the equation, C denotes the concentration of PAL retained in the skin, V represents the volume of 75% ethanol added, and A indicates the effective permeation area of the diffusion cell.

Transdermal release model fitting

The in vitro release data were fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models to elucidate the release mechanism.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis

PAL-BE-G was labeled with Nile red as a fluorescent probe. Rat dorsal skin was mounted in a Franz diffusion cell, and PAL-BE-G was applied for 24 h. Skin samples were collected, rinsed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, sectioned, and observed under CLSM to visualize the penetration depth and distribution of the formulation in the skin.

Safety assessment of PAL-BE-G

The cytotoxicity of PAL-BE-G was evaluated by the CCK-8 assay

Cytotoxicity against HaCaT human immortalized keratinocytes and WS1 human embryonic skin fibroblasts was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay (Laeeq and Faust, 2007; Yu et al., 2022). PAL-BE-G was diluted to 64 μmol/L PAL with culture medium, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and serially diluted to 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 μmol/L. Cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. The medium was replaced with drug-containing medium, and cells were incubated for another 24 h. CCK-8 working solution (CCK-8: serum-free medium = 1:9, v/v) was added, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured after 3 h incubation. Cell viability (CV) was calculated as:(6)CV=Ai−AbA0−Ab×100%

Note: CV denotes cell viability; Ai represents the absorbance of wells containing PAL at varying concentrations (pH 7.4); Ab denotes the absorbance of wells containing medium and CCK8 solution but no cells; A0 denotes the absorbance of wells containing cells and CCK8 solution but no PAL.

Rat Skin irritation test

Skin irritation was evaluated in SD rats using a self-left-right control method [34]. The dorsal skin was depilated and cleaned. Low- (0.5 mg/g), medium- (1 mg/g), and high-concentration (1.5 mg/g) PAL-BE-G were applied to the left dorsal skin, and blank gel was applied to the right side as a control.

Single-dose irritation test

Skin reactions (erythema, edema) were observed at predetermined intervals after a single application.

Multiple-dose irritation test

Formulations were applied daily for 14 consecutive days, and skin reactions were monitored after the final application.

Histopathological examination

Skin samples were collected, stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), and observed microscopically.

Pharmacodynamic experiments

Animal modelling

Given that rodents lack a fully developed vomiting reflex centre, they do not directly exhibit nausea or vomiting when subjected to vestibular stimulation. Instead, their response typically manifests as pica behavior (i.e., increased consumption of non-nutritive kaolinite by rats) (Takeda et al., 1993; Yamamoto et al., 2002). This study employed cisplatin as an emetic agent to establish a rat pica-induced vomiting model. Using kaolinite intake as the primary evaluation metric, the antiemetic efficacy of PAL-BE-G following transdermal administration in rats was investigated.

Preparation of kaolin

Dissolve an appropriate quantity of gum arabic in ultrapure water to prepare a 5% gum arabic solution. Add kaolin powder and stir continuously until a dough-like consistency is achieved. Subsequently, pour the mixture into a 5 mL syringe, dry it to set, and form it into strips. This yields pellets with smooth edges, similar in length, thickness, and appearance to rat feed.

Animal grouping and administration

Twenty-four SD rats (200–220 g) were individually housed for 3 d for acclimatisation. Kaolinite particles were then co-administered with standard feed to habituate the rats to its presence. Once the rats ceased ingesting kaolinite, the formal experiment commenced. The rats were randomly divided into four groups of six rats each: Group A: Blank control group (normal rats) Group B: Model group (modelling performed without drug administration) Group C: Positive control group (administered palonosetron injection, 0.05 mg/kg) Group D: Experimental group (administered PAL-BE-G, 0.79 mg/kg, via dorsal injection).

A rat model of pica was established by intraperitoneal injection of cisplatin (6 mg/kg). Eight hours post-injection, the consumption of kaolinite was weighed. When Group A exhibited a significantly higher kaolinite intake compared to Groups B, C, and D, with statistically significant differences, this indicated successful establishment of the rat pica model. Following successful modelling, Group C received an injections of palonosetron, while Group D was administered PAL-BE-G via dorsal injection.

General evaluation indicators

Daily records of rat feed and kaolin intake were maintained, alongside observations of mental state and faecal condition, for a period of five days. The antiemetic efficacy of PAL-BE-G was assessed based on changes in rat feed and kaolin consumption.

Pharmacokinetic investigation

Animal grouping and administration

Twelve SD rats (200–220 g) were acclimatised for 3 d before being randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group). Group A received an intravenous tail vein injection of palonosetron (30 μg/kg), while Group B received transdermal administration of PAL-BE-G (600 μg/kg). The intravenous dose (30 μg/kg) was selected based on the clinical dose of palonosetron (0.25 mg/70 kg human, equivalent to approximately 26 μg/kg in rats). The transdermal dose (600 μg/kg) was scaled up 20-fold to ensure uniform drug distribution within the gel matrix and to overcome the skin barrier, which is a common strategy for transdermal formulation studies. Blood samples were collected at specified time points post-administration in both groups. Plasma was obtained by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, then stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

Biological sample handling

A 50 μL plasma sample was mixed with 20 μL diazepam (internal standard), vortexed for 5 min, and 150 μL acetonitrile was added for protein precipitation. After vortexing and centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 10 min), 100 μL supernatant was mixed with 100 μL 0.1% formic acid and analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

Pharmacokinetic parameters

The measured plasma PAL concentrations in rats were analyzed using pharmacokinetic software DAS 3.0 to obtain primary parameters such as half-life (t₁/₂), time to peak concentration (Tmax), peak plasma concentration (Cmax), and area under the concentration-time curve (AUC). These pharmacokinetic parameters were then statistically compared between the intravenous and transdermal formulations. The absolute bioavailability of PAL-BE-G, reflecting its transdermal absorption, was calculated using the following formula.(7)Fa=AUCtransAUCiv×DivDtrans×100%

Note: Fa denotes absolute bioavailability, AUCtrans represents the area under the plasma concentration-time curve for PAL-BE-G, AUCiv denotes the area under the plasma concentration-time curve for lonosetron injection, Dtrans indicates the administered dose of PAL-BE-G, and Div denotes the administered dose of lonosetron injection.

Pharmacokinetic analytical method

PAL was quantified using an LC-MS/MS system (Waters HPLC, AB Sciex 5500 QTRAP mass spectrometer) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). Mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid +5 mmol/L ammonium acetate in methanol. Gradient elution was performed at 0.3 mL/min with a 2 μL injection volume. The method was validated over 0.40–320.00 ng/mL with good linearity (R2 = 0.9923–0.9994), precision (RSD < 2.0%), and accuracy, meeting the requirements for biological sample analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the One-Way ANOVA parametric test, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post-hoc test. A p value <0.05 was denoted as a significant difference.

Results and discussion

Characterization of PAL-BE

Particle size, polydispersity index, zeta potential, and morphology of PAL-BE

PAL-BE exhibited a mean particle size of 109.6 ± 6.4 nm (Fig. 4a), which is optimal for transdermal delivery (<200 nm) to penetrate the stratum corneum via intercellular and follicular pathways without skin irritation. The PDI was 0.196 ± 0.012, indicating uniform particle distribution, and the zeta potential was −9.37 ± 1.55 mV (Fig. 4b). Although the negative surface charge may cause electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged skin, the high ethanol content and membrane fluidity of ethosomes are the primary drivers of stratum corneum disruption and penetration. The negative zeta potential also enhances colloidal stability by preventing particle aggregation.Fig. 4Characterization results of PAL-BE a: Particle size distribution, b: Potentials, c: Transmission electron microscopy patterns.Fig. 4

Characterization results of PAL-BE a: Particle size distribution, b: Potentials, c: Transmission electron microscopy patterns.

TEM images showed that PAL-BE vesicles were nearly spherical with no aggregation (Fig. 4c). The EE was 70.34 ± 1.58%, and LE was 7.83 ± 0.70%. The DF of PAL-BE was 95.72%, confirming excellent membrane stability that prevents drug leakage during penetration. The small particle size (∼110 nm) enables efficient skin penetration, and the transdermal advantage of PAL-BE stems from the synergistic effect of small size and moderate deformability.

Stability assessment

After 120 days of storage at 4 °C, 25 °C, and 32 °C, PAL-BE showed no significant changes in particle size or EE at 4 °C (P > 0.05). In contrast, particle size and EE changed significantly at 25 °C and 32 °C (P < 0.01). Characterization of PAL-BE-G

pH and viscosity determination of PAL-BE-G

The final gel exhibited a pH of 7.41 ± 0.02 and a viscosity of 54.53 ± 0.31 Pa·s. PAL-BE-G showed good spreadability, skin conformability, and biocompatibility suitable for transdermal administration.

Preliminary stability of PAL-BE-G

PAL-BE-G showed no significant changes in appearance or drug content at 4 °C for 30 days (P > 0.05) (Fig. 7). At 25 °C, no hydration occurred within 15 days, but gradual hydration and slight drug content reduction were observed with prolonged storage. At 32 °C, hydration and degradation occurred by day 7 with a significant decrease in drug content (P < 0.05). PAL-BE-G in vitro release

PAL—S—G and PAL-PM-G reached release equilibrium at 4 h and 12 h with cumulative release rates of 96% and 94%, respectively (P > 0.05) (Fig. 8). Transdermal release of PAL-BE-G

Transdermal permeability followed the order: PAL-BE-G > PAL-E-G > PAL-L-G > PAL-S-G (Fig. 9, Table 1). The 24 h cumulative permeation of PAL-BE-G was 1670.01 ± 21.22 μg·cm−2, 2.98-, 2.47-, and 1.13-fold higher than PAL—S—G, PAL-L-G, and PAL-E-G, respectively. PAL-BE-G safety assessment

Assessment of PAL-BE-G cytotoxicity using the CCK-8 assay

The blank excipients of PAL-BE-G promoted the proliferation of HaCaT and WS1 cells, likely due to the cytoprotective effect of soybean phospholipids. PAL-BE-G showed a slight proliferative effect on HaCaT cells at low concentrations. At concentrations below 32 μmol/L, cell viability remained above 90% (Fig. 11), indicating no significant cytotoxicity. At 64 μmol/L, HaCaT cell viability was 89.85%, showing minor toxicity, which is far above the therapeutic dose. After i.v. administration, PAL showed a Cmax of 32.37 ng/mL and t₁/₂ of 5.64 h. Although the bioavailability is lower than i.v. administration, PAL-BE-G offers distinct clinical advantages: (1) sustained release over 72 h for prolonged antiemetic protection; (2) bypass of gastrointestinal first-pass metabolism and vomiting interference; (3) therapeutically relevant plasma concentrations. These benefits, combined with improved patient compliance, highlight the clinical potential of PAL-BE-G.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and evaluated a novel palonosetron-loaded binary ethosome transdermal gel (PAL-BE-G). The optimized formulation exhibited suitable physicochemical properties, high transdermal permeability, sustained-release behavior, and good extended stability at 4 °C for 120 days. Accelerated stability at 40 °C/75% RH and long-term stability will be investigated in future industrial development. In vitro and in vivo assessments demonstrated favorable biocompatibility, significant deep skin penetration confirmed by CLSM, and antiemetic efficacy comparable to commercial palonosetron injection. The pharmacokinetic profile supported sustained systemic exposure with an absolute bioavailability of 18.74 ± 3.26%, which is reasonable and therapeutically relevant for transdermal delivery.

All present findings are limited to in vitro characterizations and animal experiments. Further in vivo safety assessments following OECD guidelines, pharmacokinetic validations, preclinical studies, and clinical trials are still required to confirm its clinical feasibility. These findings indicate that PAL-BE-G represents a promising transdermal delivery system for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, particularly for patients who cannot tolerate oral administration.
