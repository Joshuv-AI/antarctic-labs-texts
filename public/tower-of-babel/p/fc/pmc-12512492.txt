Elucidating the role of IgA plasma cells and PECAM1-CD38 interaction in intestinal fibrosis: a single-cell RNA sequencing analysis in Crohn's disease
Authors: Shuyang Sun, Jiheng Wang, Kai Li, Hua Jin, Shuwen Du, Jingyan Feng, Limin Zhang, Lei Li, Yuqi He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal fibrosis-associated stricture is a frequent complication of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), often necessitating endoscopic or surgical interventions. This study aims to investigate the pathogenesis of fibrosis in Crohn's disease (CD), a type of IBD, by analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) data from colon biopsies of both CD and ulcerative colitis (UC) patients.ResultsThe analysis revealed significant cellular heterogeneity, altered cell–cell interactions, and key molecular pathways in both CD and UC. CD showed a marked increase in IgA plasma cells. Ligand–receptor analysis indicated that IgA plasma cells strongly interacted with inflammation-associated fibroblasts (IAFs) in CD, predominantly through the PECAM1–CD38 axis, whereas these interactions were less pronounced in UC. Our study indicated that IL-1β was upregulated in Th17 cells in CD. MAPK signaling pathway were upregulated in Th17 cells.ConclusionsThese findings may provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying early intestinal fibrosis in CD by identifying critical cell types and molecular players. Nonetheless, these observations are exploratory and will require validation in future experiments.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04312-0.Keywords: Intestinal fibrosis, Crohn's disease, Ulcerative colitis, Single-cell RNA sequencing, IgA plasma cells, Inflammation-associated fibroblasts, PECAM1, CD38, IL-1β

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a chronic immune-mediated disorder affecting the gastrointestinal tract [1]. It has become a global disease with accelerating incidence in newly industrialized countries, such as those in Asia, Africa and South America [2]. UC is primarily restricted in the mucosa layer of colon, manifesting as continuous superficial inflammation. In contrast, CD is characterized by initial transmural inflammation, initially impacting both the small and large intestine [3]. A significant complication of CD is fibrosis, leading to intestinal obstruction, which affects approximately one-half of all patients. Most strikingly, more than 50% patients with luminal narrowing in CD have a recurrence of intestinal stenosis even after undergoing surgery [4]. Currently, there is a lack of targeted medical treatments available for intestinal fibrosis indicating the urgent need for studies focused on evaluating the underlying disease mechanisms of intestinal fibrosis.

Intestinal fibrosis, and the underlying pathogenic factors have not been fully elucidated. It is well established that the combined increase of mesenchymal cell numbers and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) secretion are considered the hallmark features of intestinal strictures [5]. Intestinal mesenchymal cells, encompassing fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and smooth muscle cells (SMC) are the main cell types responsible for ECM secretion. Although traditional mesenchymal cell phenotypes have strongly contributed to intestinal fibrosis, the nature and extent of molecular variation among subtypes across the development of intestinal fibrosis is largely unknown. Besides the previously mentioned cells and factors contributing to intestinal fibrosis, evidence has recently emerged suggesting that fibrosis may also arise from entirely distinct mechanisms involving cell differentiation, transformation and recruitment, expanding our understanding of the development of this condition [6]. Fibroblasts contributing to intestinal fibrosis can derive from migration into the inflamed area, proliferation of local fibroblasts through autocrine or paracrine processes [7]. The complexity and heterogeneity of the fibroblasts involved in intestinal fibrosis have not been thoroughly characterized. Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics have revealed that fibroblasts in organs are highly heterogeneous, comprising distinct subpopulations with unique molecular and functional characteristics [8–10]. Direct cell-to-cell contact with inflammatory cells, such as mast cells or eosinophils, which are found in greater quantities in the mucosa during active IBD, can promote cell proliferation in vitro [11]. Most probably, T cells can also stimulate fibroblast proliferation by the release of profibrotic cytokines or through a direct cell-to-cell contact mechanism [12–15]. Th17 cell can promote intestinal fibrotic responses in experimental colitis and human patients with CD. Scientific advances have greatly enhanced our understanding of intestinal fibrosis. Nevertheless, this complex condition remains a formidable challenge, as there are still no effective therapies available despite the discovery of numerous potential drug targets. Although numerous preclinical studies have been conducted, there are currently no approved anti-fibrotic treatments capable of preventing or reversing intestinal fibrosis [16]. Despite the urgency for anti-fibrotic therapy and the numerous molecules identified as potential anti-fibrotics in CD, no phase III clinical trial is currently ongoing or recruiting to our knowledge [16]. One key limitation may be the prevailing focus on individual cells or molecules, which often overlooks the broader context of the disease [17]. Current research lacks detailed investigations into various specific immune cell subtypes within strictures, such as T-cell subsets, B cells, and NK cells, etc. Moreover, less is known about their roles and cell–cell interactions in the context of strictures.

Additionally, far less is known of the diverse factors and events that contribute to the pathological process of intestinal fibrosis, particularly in distinguishing between CD and UC. Due to the diagnosis of strictures is mainly based on symptoms, the previous studies likely underestimate the real incidence of fibrosis. It has been proved that fibrosis might occurred in the early stage of CD [18]. To date, there are no established biomarkers or diagnostic tools available to identify and quantify the overall fibrotic burden in Crohn’s disease patients, particularly during the early stages when anti-fibrotic therapy might be most effective. In this regard, the advent of single cell genomics approaches providing unprecedented resolution allows a more precise interrogation of cell types and their functional states, offering a novel perspective on the underlying mechanisms of disease [1, 19]. Furthermore, several studies have profiled the single-cell landscape of cells heterogeneity in CD [20–22]. However, a comprehensive depiction of the ecosystem and immune phenotypes in intestinal fibrosis in CD at the single-cell resolution level remains lacking. Here, we performed scRNA‐seq to comprehensively investigate the cellular, molecular microenvironment of CD, with a particular focus on different subsets of fibroblasts, aiming to delineate various cell types, cell–cell interactions, and pivotal molecules that are intimately linked with the development of intestinal fibrosis in CD.

Materials and methods

Patient intestine samples for scRNA-seq

All research activities were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Seventh Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital and in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki (ID: 2017–46). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the colonoscopy and secured at the Seventh Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital. The study enrolled patients aged 14–65 years, with detailed information provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, at first we enrolled 7 CD and 3 UC, after quality control we eliminated 3 CD (IBD4, IBD8, IBD11) because of the high mitochondrial genes (Supplementary Fig. 1), enventually comprising confirmed cases of CD (n = 4) and UC (n = 3). The CD group included patients labeled as IBD2, IBD3, IBD6, and IBD9, while the UC group included IBD1, IBD5, and IBD7. UC and CD patients were enrolled if they had a clinical diagnosis of ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease and demonstrated active disease upon macroscopic evaluation by a physician during endoscopy. Only patients without intestinal strictures were enrolled. Four biopsies were obtained during endoscopy, using biopsy forceps that were used in standard of care. All biopsies were collected from inflamed regions of the colon and terminal ileum. Biopsy bites were immediately placed into cryovials containing Advanced DMEM F-12 and then transported to the laboratory on ice for subsequent processing.

Cell preparation

Typically, four biopsies from the same patient were received directly in hand and processed in parallel with an average time from patient to loading on the 10X GemCode or Chromium platform of 2.5 total hours, and never exceeding 3.5 h. After harvested, tissues were immediately washed in ice-cold PBS (Hyclone, SH30256.01) and dissociated using SeekMate Tissue Dissociation Reagent Kit A Pro (SeekGene, K01801301) or SeekMate Tissue Dissociation Kit C (SeekGene, K01501) from SeekGene as instructions. DNase Ⅰ (Sigma, 9003–98-9) treatment was optional according to the viscosity of the homogenate. Cell count and viability was estimated using Fluorescence Cell Analyzer (Countstar® Rigel, S2) or SeekMate Tinitan Fluorescence Cell Counter (SeekGene, M002C) with AO/PI reagent after removal erythrocytes (Solarbio, R1010) and then debris and dead cells removal was decided to be performed or not (Miltenyi, 130–109-398/130–090-101). Finally fresh cells were washed twice in the RPMI1640 (Gibco, 11,875,119) and then resuspended at 1 × 106 cells per ml in RPMI1640 and 2% FBS (Gibco, 10100147 C).

Nucleus preparation

The nucleus was isolated using a Nucleus Isolation Kit (SHBIO, 52,009–10) as instructions. RNase inhibitors (Sigma, 3,335,399,001) were added to the reagents before use. The samples were cut and transferred to a 5 mL tube containing lysate, mixed and lysed for 3 min on ice, then filtered through a 40 μm cell filter (Sigma, BAH136800040). Nucleus count and viability was estimated using Fluorescence Cell Analyzer (Countstar® Rigel S2) or SeekMate Tinitan Fluorescence Cell Counter (SeekGene M002C) with AO/PI reagent. After staining with 0.4% trypan blue (Sangon Biotech, E607320-0001), the nucleus was observed under a 40X microscope (Jiangnan Novel Optics, XD-202). Subsequent experiments were performed if the nuclear envelope was intact and there were fewer impurities.

Single cell RNA-seq library construction and sequencing

Single-cell RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3' library preparation kit (SeekGene, Catalog No. K00202). Briefly, appropriate number of cells were mixed with reverse transcription reagent and then added to the sample well in SeekOne® chip S3. Subsequently Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were dispensed into corresponding wells separately in chip S3. After emulsion droplet generation reverse transcription were performed at 42℃ for 90 min and inactivated at 85℃ for 5 min. Next, cDNA was purified from broken droplet and amplified in PCR reaction. The amplified cDNA product was then cleaned, fragmented, end repaired, A-tailed and Ligated to sequencing adaptor. Finally, the indexed PCR were performed to amplified the DNA representing 3' polyA part of expressing genes which also contained Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index. The indexed sequencing libraries were cleanup with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411-01), analyzed by Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33226) and Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic, Qsep400). The Libraries were then sequenced on illumina NovaSeq 6000 with PE150 read length or DNBSEQ-T7 platform with PE150 read length.

Single nuclei RNA sequencing library construction and sequencing

Single Nuclei RNA (snRNA-seq) libraries were prepared using SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3' library preparation kit (SeekGene, Catalog No. K00202). Briefly, appropriate number of cell nucleus were mixed with reverse transcription reagent and then added to the sample well in SeekOne® DD Chip S3. Subsequently Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were dispensed into corresponding wells separately in chip S3. After emulsion droplet generation reverse transcription were performed at 42℃ for 90 min and inactivated at 85℃ for 5 min. Next, cDNA was purified from broken droplet and amplified in PCR reaction. The amplified cDNA product was then cleaned, fragmented, end repaired, A-tailed and Ligated to sequencing adaptor. Finally, the indexed PCR were performed to amplified the DNA representing 3' polyA part of expressing genes which also contained Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index. The indexed sequencing libraries were cleanup with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411-01), analyzed by Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33226) and Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic, Qsep400). The Libraries were then sequenced on illumina NovaSeq 6000 with PE150 read length or DNBSEQ-T7 platform with PE150 read length.

Quality control

Initially, raw sequencing data were processed using Fastp to trim primer sequences and low-quality bases. Subsequently, the sequencing data were processed using Seek One ® Tools, and aligned with the human GRCh38 genome to obtain a gene expression matrix. Low-quality cells were filtered out using Seurat (v4.0.0) [23], removing cells with fewer than 200 or more than 5000 genes. Additionally, cells influenced by mitochondrial gene expression were excluded using the MAD-variance normal method [23]. Doublets and multiplets were identified and removed using the DoubletFinder R package and by manual inspection of cell clustering and UMI/gene count distributions. Only cells passing all these filters were included in downstream analyses.

Sample integration, dimension reduction and clustering

After quality control and filtering, library size normalization for each cell was performed using Seurat (v4.0.0) Normalized Data. Find Variable Genes was used to calculate highly variable genes (2000 high variable genes). To remove donor effects, canonical correlationanalysis (CCA) was performed using the FindintegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions. CCA works by calculating linear combinations of two (or more) datasets to maximize the correlation between these combinations, thereby identifying shared biological signals across different datasets. In single-cell data integration, CCA finds “anchor cells” between batches as matching points—these anchors represent biologically similar cells across batches. The main purpose is to project multiple datasets into a shared low-dimensional space, allowing data from different batches to be aligned and integrated. Following integration, all samples were merged into a single Seurat object with the “integrated" assay. Non-linear dimension reduction was conducted using Run PCA (npcs = 30) and Run UMAP(reduction = "pca", dims = 30). Cells were clustered using Find Neighbors(dims = 30) and Find Clusters(resolution = 0.6).

Cell type annotation

Find All Markers was employed to compare each cluster against all others, identifying cluster-specific marker genes. Each retained marker gene was expressed in at least 25% of cells, with an avg_log2FoldChange of at least 0.25. Thus, differential genes were considered significant if the adjusted P-value was less than 0.05 and avg_log2FC was greater than 0.25. Clusters were annotated using cell type-specific feature marker genes, for example, T cells: CD3D, CD3E, CD3G; B cells: CD19, MS4A1; Plasma cells: JCHAIN, IGKC, etc.

Subgroup analysis

Based on the major group annotations, T, plasma, and stromal cells were extracted, and UMI counts were normalized using Scale Data. Non-linear dimension reduction was then performed using Run PCA (npcs = 20) and Run UMAP(reduction = "pca", dims = 20). Cells were clustered using Find Neighbors(dims = 20) and Find Clusters(resolution = 0.6).

Find All Markers was used to identify cluster-specific marker genes by comparing each cluster with all others. Each retained marker gene was expressed in at least 25% of cells, with an avg_log2FoldChange of at least 0.25. Differential genes were considered significant if the adjusted P-value was less than 0.05 and avg_log2FC was greater than 0.25.

Pseudotime trajectories analysis

The Monocle2 software package (v2.3.6) [24] was used to determine potential lineage differentiation. The import CDS function in Monocle2 was employed to convert raw counts from the Seurat object into a CellDataSet, followed by the selection of genes for pseudotime ordering using the dispersion Table function. The DDR Tree method was utilized for dimensionality reduction and cell ordering along the pseudotime trajectory. The root cells were not specified, and the ordering was performed in an unsupervised manner. Branch analysis was conducted using the BEAM function. Based on different gene expression patterns, the plot_genes_branched_pseudo_time function was used to cluster genes that showed significant changes at the branch points.

Cell–cell interactions

We used Cell Phone DB [25] to infer cell–cell interactions, Log transformed and normalized counts, and cell type annotations were used as an input. To narrow down the most relevant interactions, we looked for specific interactions classified by ligand/receptor expression in more than 10% of cells within a cluster and where log2 mean expression of ligand/receptor pair is greater than 0.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of marker genes was implemented by the cluster Profiler R package. GO terms with corrected P value less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched by marker gene.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [26] is a database resource for understanding high-level functions and utilities of the biological system, such as the cell, the organism and the ecosystem, from molecular-level information, especially large-scale molecular datasets generated by genome sequencing and other high-throughput experimental technologies (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). We used cluster Profiler R package to test the statistical enrichment of marker genes in KEGG pathways.

Gene set variation analysis

Pathway analyses were performed on the 50 hallmark pathways described in the molecular signature database [27]. To assign pathway activity estimates to individual cells, we applied GSVA using standard settings, as implemented in the GSVA [28] R package (version 1.36.3) as described previously.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) method

Paraffin-embedded intestinal tissue Sects. (4 μm) from CD and UC patients were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CD38: Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, Cat# 25,284–1-AP) at 1:400 dilution. IL-1β: Monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, Cat# 66,737–1-Ig) at 1:200 dilution. CD31: Monoclonal antibody (CST, Cat# 3528S) at 1:800 dilution. After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, visualized using DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired under a light microscope. Staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells were assessed using an H-score method for semi-quantitative analysis.

Statistics analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) unless otherwise stated. Comparisons of cell proportions between two groups were performed using the t test. Monocle 2 was used for cell type identification, differential expression analysis and cell ordering [29]. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted across clusters generated at various resolutions through the Wilcoxon rank sum test, using the FindMarkers function. P-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction across all genes in the dataset, with adjusted p-value (p_val_adj) < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package, with p-value adjustment by the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method. Enriched terms with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were deemed significant. Ligand–receptor interaction analysis was conducted using the CellChat package. Interaction strength was calculated based on average expression levels of interacting molecules, and statistical significance was assessed by permutation tests (p < 0.05).

ScRNA-seq Atlas of colon biopsies from UC and CD patients

To investigate the difference in pathogenesis between UC and CD, we performed a single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of 4 ileum biopsies and 3 colon biopsies from colonoscopic examinations of 4 CD patients and 3 UC patients (Fig. 1a).

After quality control filtering (Supplementary Fig. 1a-c), we obtained a total of 64,267 cells. We identified and visualized 6 clusters using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Fig. 1b, f) and labeled these cells according to differential expression of established marker genes, and ended up with 2 kinds of non-immune cells (epithelial and stromal cells) and 4 immune cell lineages (T cells, B cells, plasma cells and myeloid cells) (Fig. 1c). IgA plasma cells are significantly increased in CD

We identified four subpopulations of plasma cells (Fig. 2a,b,c). In comparison with UC group, the abundance of IgA plasma cells was significantly increased in CD group (p = 0.029) (Fig. 2d). Meanwhile, the abundance of B cells was decreased in CD (Fig. 1d).

A study demonstrated that more IgA+ memory B-cells and plasmablasts were found in lungs of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients compared to healthy control [30]. To explore how Th17 cells alters IgA plasma cells, we analyzed the DEGs in the IgA plasma cells in CD group (Fig. 2e). Expressions of both AREG, CCL4L2, SLAMF1, PECAM1 (a cell adhesion molecule involved in leukocyte transmigration), CD38 (an enzyme involved in immune cell activation) were upregulated in the CD samples compared with the UC samples. The up-regulated genes were enriched in Rheumatoid arthritis, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, ECM-receptor interaction, IL-17 signaling pathway, Inflammatory bowel disease (Fig. 2f).

IL-1β is upregulated in Th17 cells from CD patients

According to the previous study, Th17 cells can trigger B cells differentiating into IgA plasma cells and IgM plasma cells. Subsequently, we further explored whether increased Th17 cells might impact the proportion of IgA plasma cells.

According to a recent study [14], Th17 cells play a pivotal role in inducing intestinal fibrosis in CD. Therefore, we first focused on analyzing the Th17 cell subset. Based on this, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups. The analysis revealed that the expression of IL-1β was significantly upregulated in CD samples relative to UC samples (Fig. 3e). Moreover, multiple pathways, including Lipid and atherosclerosis, MAPK signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, Inflammatory bowel disease and Rheumatoid arthritis were upregulated in Th17 cells (Fig. 3f).

We conducted an in-depth analysis of the connection between Th17 cells and IgA plasma cells. Our findings indicate that several signaling pathways, including the NOD-like receptor, IL-17, TNF, Th17 cell differentiation, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways, were enhanced in both Th17 cells and IgA plasma cells. Notably, JUN, IL1B, and STAT1 were among the genes that showed increased expression in these pathways.

It has been demonstrated that Th17 cells can induce B cells to differentiate into both IgA and IgM plasma cells. Given the crucial role of Th17 cells in intestinal fibrosis, and previous studies confirming IgA plasma cells’ involvement in liver and lung fibrosis, it is plausible that IgA plasma cells may also contribute to the development of intestinal fibrosis in CD. This hypothesis is further supported by the discovery of shared pathways and distinct genes between Th17 cells and IgA plasma cells, pointing towards a potential role for IgA plasma cells in CD-related intestinal fibrosis.

We then comprehensively analyzed the cell subsets which are closely associated with IgA plasma cells by conducting cell–cell interaction.

Cell–cell interaction between IgA plasma cells and inflammation associated fibroblasts

The relationship between stromal cells and fibrosis is closely linked. Stromal cells, including fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, are the primary effector cells in fibrosis.

We identified seven stromal cells, including inflammation associated fibroblast (IAFs), VCAN + fibroblast, PDGFRA + fibroblast, RSPO3 + fibroblast, S100B + fibroblast, Myofibroblast, CD34 + endothelial (Fig. 4a, b). GSVA analysis indicates that the signatures of MTORC1 signaling APICAL junction, inflammatory response, epithelial mesenchymal transition and Hedgehog signaling, which are associated with fibroblast activation, promoting fibroblast migration and tissue fibrosis, are significantly enriched in IAFs. The signatures of PI3K-AKT-MTOR signaling, adipogenesis, NOTCH signaling are enriched in myofibroblasts (Fig. 4c).

To further investigate the direct immune-stromal cells interaction, we characterized the molecular interactions of ligand-receptor (L-R) pairs on the surface of IgA plasma cells and each subtype of stromal cells by CellPhoneDB (Fig. 4e). Distinct L-R pairs were identified in CD and UC, implying the difference in fibrogenesis between the two diseases (Fig. 4d).

The L-R pair exhibiting the greatest difference between IgA plasma cells and IAFs includes PECAM1-CD38, SEMA4A-PLXND1, HLA-C-FAM3C, and ADORA2A-ENTPD1. As shown in Fig. 3e, the expression of PECAM1 in CD is higher than that in UC. Among these interactive molecule pairs, PECAM1 and CD38 were identified as the most intensive interactions between IgA plasma cells and IAFs in CD. Whereas, the interaction in UC is relatively weaker.

To understand relationship of these fibroblasts during their development, we performed pseudotime analysis and observed three developmental trajectories. To unveil the differentiation dynamics of fibroblast lineage cells, we established the developmental trajectory for both fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. The RNA velocity analysis was used to unambiguously demonstrate the trajectory (Fig. 5b,c). The majority of VCAN + fibroblast and RSPO3 + fibroblast was found at the start point of the trajectory, and then split into two main branches with IAFs (fate 2 branch) and myofibroblasts (fate 1 branch). The myofibroblasts was found near the terminal end of the trajectory. Besides, we observed that three clusters of VCAN + fibroblast, RSPO3 + fibroblast and PDGFRA + fibroblast were found at different stages of differentiation.

Next, we assessed the gene expression patterns responsible for the differentiation of fibroblasts during the two cell fates. The expression of MMP1 was found to be upregulated with cell differentiation. The expression of MMP3 was downregulated during fate 2 branch but notably upregulated during fate 1 branch. The expression of SPARCL1 was in the opposite way (Fig. 5e).

The gene expression patterns involved in the continuum transition were further dissected. The cluster 1 genes, the expressions of which increased with fate 1 branch, were enriched for the GO terms “extracellular matrix organization” “extracellular structure organization” “connective tissue development” (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 4a). Meanwhile, the cluster 3 genes were also enriched for the GO terms “extracellular matrix organization” “extracellular structure organization” “immune response” “neutrophil chemotaxis”, etc. (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

To further characterize the functions of subgroups of stromal cells in CD, we performed GO enrichment analysis of these clusters. The results indicated enrichment of ECM accumulation terms in IAFs, including extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, collagen metabolic process, collagen fibril organization, many of which are associated with intestinal fibrogenesis (Fig. 6c). We then performed KEGG enrichment analysis, which revealed that multiple pathways were significantly enriched and upregulated in IAFs. These included the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, lipid and atherosclerosis, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, focal adhesion, IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, rheumatoid arthritis, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. 6d).

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed markedly higher IL1B expression in inflamed intestinal mucosa from CD patients compared to UC (Fig. 7a). PECAM1 (CD31) expression, indicative of endothelial activation, was elevated in both groups but more extensive in CD (Fig. 7b). Discussion

In this study, we mainly compared the fibrosis-related cells and genes between CD and UC at single-cell scale. A predominant interaction between IgA plasma cells and IAFs based on the PECAM1-CD38 was specifically identified (Fig. 8).

Graphic abstract

Emerging evidence suggests that B-cell activation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of multiple fibrotic lung diseases [31]. It has been revealed that the profibrogenesis function of B cells appeared to be mainly related to the elevated level of plasma cells. Notably, in autoimmune hepatitis, an increased number of plasma cells has been associated with more extensive fibrosis [32]. Meanwhile, genes associated with ECM organization, response to hypoxia and tissue remodeling were upregulated in plasma cells [33]. A study showed that there were higher numbers of IgA + memory B cells and plasmablasts in the lungs of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients compared to those in healthy controls [30]. In addition, IgA + plasma cells also contribute to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) by promoting liver inflammation, injury and fibrosis [34]. There is no research that clearly indicates a correlation between IgA plasma cells and intestinal fibrosis. Interestingly, a 2024 study reported that stricturing CD is characterized by a selective increase in B cells and IgG plasma cells [35]. However, our findings showed an increase in IgA plasma cells in CD without intestinal stricture, suggesting that distinct plasma cell subsets may contribute to different pathological features of CD.

The role of T-cell lineage populations in the pathogenesis of CD has been extensively studied. Accumulating evidence supports the pivotal role of inflammation in intestinal fibrosis of CD [36–38]. Indeed, infiltrating leukocytes, and particularly T cells enhanced by STAT4 activity, stimulate the activation of fibroblast and the production of collagen through the release of profibrotic cytokines [15]. Therefore, we firstly focused on the immune cell composition, especially T and NK cells.

In this study, we observed no significant differences in the subtypes of T cells between CD and UC. However, given the role of Th17 cells in the early stages of intestinal fibrosis in CD, we focused on the DEGs within Th17 cells. This analysis revealed an upregulation of IL-1β (a key pro-inflammatory cytokine) in CD. Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the enrichment of both the MAPK and IL-17 signaling pathways in Th17 cells, both of which are associated with fibrosis [39, 40]. Our findings align with previous research, demonstrating that while Th1 and Th17 cells both contribute to intestinal inflammation at comparable levels, Th17 cells are more potent inducers of severe intestinal fibrosis. This increased fibrotic response is primarily driven by Th17 cell secretion of Amphiregulin (Areg) [14]. CD4 + T helper cells secrete factors and cytokines that, along with co-stimulatory ligands (CD40/CD40 L) and direct cell-to-cell contact, selectively guide B cells to switch to specific antibody classes and subclasses [41]. Th17 cytokines, including IL-1β, RA, TGF-β and IL-6, can regulate mucosal IgA secretion. Our findings showed a significant increase in IgA plasma cells in CD, while there was no significant difference in Th17 cells between CD and UC. Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that IL-1β was upregulated in Th17 cells. Consistent with this finding, IHC staining demonstrated higher IL-1β expression in CD tissues compared to UC, potentially contributing to the increased recruitment of IgA plasma cells in CD.

Subsequently, we explored the function of IgA plasma cells in fibrogenesis of CD by conducting cell–cell interaction. A predominant interaction between IgA plasma cells and IAFs based on the PECAM1-CD38 was specifically identified in CD but not in UC. In the process of fibrosis, stromal cells can regulate the activity of immune cells by secreting cytokines and chemokines, further influencing the inflammation and fibrosis of the tissue. IAFs displayed potential abilities in proinflammatory responses and intestinal fibrosis [42]. Given the intimate relationship between IgA plasma cells and IAFs in our results, it may help shed light on the mechanism of fibrosis in CD. In the present study, PECAM1 (a cell adhesion molecule involved in leukocyte transmigration), and CD38 (an enzyme involved in immune cell activation)were identified as the key molecules involved in the most intensive interactions between IgA plasma cells and IAFs in CD. Remarkably, PECAM1 expression was found to be upregulated in IgA plasma cells, while CD38 expression was increased in IAFs. Consistent with the scRNA-seq data, IHC staining demonstrated higher CD38 expression in CD tissue compared to UC, whereas PECAM1 (CD31) was more abundant in CD sections. A study proposed that PECAM1 may play a role in modulating the proliferation of endothelial cells and fibroblasts during the process of wound healing [43]. Patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) had more severe atrial fibrosis and higher PECAM1 [44]. Blockade of increased PECAM-1 expression could be a promising tool to inhibit fibrogenesis and metastasis in the liver [45]. It has also been demonstrated that CD38 levels are significantly increased in the skin of patients with Systemic Sclerosis (SSc), with these elevated levels correlating with both the severity of the clinical disease and the activity of profibrotic signaling [46]. In mouse models of lung fibrosis, upregulation of CD38 in alveolar epithelial cells contributes to the enhanced susceptibility of aged mice to lung fibrosis, suggesting that targeting CD38 may represent an effective therapeutic strategy for treating lung fibrosis [47]. A recent 2025 study reported that CD-associated fibrosis develops within the mesenchymal compartment, driven by PECAM2 signaling via the PECAM1–CD38 interaction. Furthermore, inhibition of CD38 signaling significantly alleviated colitis symptoms and reduced colon wall thickening in a TNBS-induced chronic inflammation model [48]. However, the relationship between PECAM1-CD38 and intestinal fibrosis remains unexplored. To investigate the underlying mechanism of the interaction, we conducted KEGG pathway analysis on Th17 cells and IAFs. The analysis indicated that the MAPK signaling pathway might be significantly active in both cell types. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that the increases in CD38 levels induced by IL-1β are regulated through the MAPK signaling pathway [49]. It appears that the elevated production of IL-1β in Th17 cells leads to an upregulation of CD38 levels in CD via the MAPK signaling pathway. Previous studies have established the crucial role of inflammation in initiating fibrosis. Our work suggests that Th17 cells may play a pivotal role in the early stages of intestinal fibrosis in CD; however, this alone seems insufficient to fully explain the development of fibrosis.

Stromal cells, encompassing both fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, serve as the primary effector cells in the process of fibrosis. Fibrosis is characterized as an excessive response marked by the accumulation of collagen-rich ECM, resulting from a sustained or transient increase in the number of mesenchymal cells, such as fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells [50]. Mesenchymal cells can be activated through multiple pathways, with inflammation being a particularly strong activator. The chronic inflammation associated with CD leads to alterations in gene expression patterns, thereby mediating the development of intestinal fibrosis. Consequently, we delved deeper into investigating the changes in IAFs and other stromal cells.

IAFs and myofibroblasts were placed in two cell fates, whereas VCAN + fibroblasts, RSPO3 + fibroblasts and PDGFRA + fibroblasts were found at different stages of differentiation, so we confirmed that as the accumulation of genes alteration, IAFs are activated into myofibroblasts, thereby promoting the formation of fibrosis. The findings suggest that while IAFs are associated with the inflammatory condition, their increase alone is insufficient to drive fibrosis. This implies that the presence of Th17 cells, IgA plasma cells, and the interaction of PECAM1-CD38 are also essential in the fibrogenesis process.

Our work provides a foundational framework for understanding the early development of human intestinal fibrosis in CD at single-cell resolution. Nevertheless, several important limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of both type I and type II errors, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, the absence of a healthy control group restricts our ability to determine whether the observed differences between CD and UC are disease-specific or simply reflect general inflammation. As such, our conclusions are limited to comparisons between IBD subtypes, and future studies incorporating healthy controls will be essential to define disease-specific changes. Moreover, although patients had varying disease durations, we included only those without intestinal stricture and did not perform formal histological scoring of fibrosis at the time of biopsy collection. Consequently, our results mainly reflect early-stage molecular changes that may be associated with the initial development of intestinal fibrosis, while variations in disease duration may have influenced the proportions of certain cell clusters and gene expression profiles. Third, biopsies were collected from the colon in UC patients and from the terminal ileum in CD patients, and patients in the two groups received different medications. Consequently, some observed differences might have been influenced by biopsy location or treatment effects. In addition, our mechanistic hypothesis—that increased IL-1β production in Th17 cells may contribute to elevated CD38 expression in CD via the MAPK pathway, and that subsequent interactions between IgA plasma cells and IAFs through PECAM1-CD38 could activate myofibroblasts and promote fibrosis—remains speculative and will require further validation in vitro and in vivo. Methodologically, while we employed CCA-based methods to minimize batch effects, some residual technical variability may persist, as no batch correction method is fully effective. Taken together, further studies are needed to elucidate the functional significance of the PECAM1–CD38 interaction and IgA plasma cell–IAF crosstalk in intestinal fibrosis, particularly across patient cohorts with different degrees of histological fibrosis. These findings require validation in larger, more uniform cohorts, and future studies should aim to include a greater number of patients and, ideally, healthy controls to better define disease-specific alterations.

Conclusions

The present study provides a comprehensive analysis of cellular and biological processes involved in the early stage of intestinal fibrosis in CD through scRNA-seq. The study highlights changes in certain cell types, primarily IgA plasma cells and IAFs, as well as alterations in genes such as IL-1β, PECAM1, and CD38 that occur within the context of the disease. These findings may offer new insights into the mechanisms underlying early intestinal fibrosis in CD. Furthermore, the identified genes hold potential as biomarkers for early fibrotic changes and as promising targets for therapeutic intervention. Nonetheless, these observations are exploratory and will require validation in larger cohorts. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to confirm whether IgA plasma cells can drive fibroblast activation via the PECAM1–CD38 interaction.
