Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure and Successful Liver Transplantation in Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency
Authors: Baila Elkin, Daniela S. Allende, Shreya Sengupta
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLiver transplant is a rare phenomenon for pyruvate kinase deficiency (PKD)-related liver disease and can be mediated by multiple mechanisms. In this report, we present a 55-year-old man with PKD who had acute-on-chronic liver failure with kidney failure and marked hyperbilirubinemia. His liver disease was from recurrent cholangitis, cholestasis from hemolysis, and iron deposition (likely from both repeated transfusions in youth and chronic hemolysis), all consequences of his PKD. He received a liver transplant and had a good outcome. Our case highlights the mechanisms of liver injury in PKD and successful transplantation for this rare complication.

INTRODUCTION

Pyruvate kinase deficiency (PKD), an autosomal recessive disorder, leads to impaired erythrocyte glycolysis, erythrocyte membrane instability, and chronic hemolysis.1 There is heterogeneity in disease phenotype, ranging from mild anemia to transfusion dependence, as well as differences in rates of other complications. These include sequelae of hemolysis, such as gallstones, and iron overload from chronic transfusions, from hemolysis itself, and, in some patients, from coinheritance of hemochromatosis genes.1,3,4 Historically, treatment has been mainly supportive, with transfusions, vitamins, and splenectomy in some patients with severe anemia and transfusion dependence.1

End-stage liver disease is a rare but serious complication of PKD, reported in several cases.4–7 Liver dysfunction may be because of biliary sludge and stones from hyperbilirubinemia from chronic hemolysis causing biliary obstruction, as well as iron overload. Successful liver transplantation has also been reported in some cases with infants and young adults.5,7

CASE REPORT

A 55-year-old man with a history of PKD with confirmed PKLR mutation presented with acute-on-chronic liver failure, with acute renal failure and marked hyperbilirubinemia. His history was significant for PKD diagnosed at the age of 2 years, splenectomy as a toddler, cholecystectomy at 15 years, mesenteric and portal vein thrombosis at 18 years with small bowel resection, and continued biliary stones with episodes of cholangitis with eventual choledochojejunostomy at 22 years. Severe hemolytic anemia necessitated multiple blood transfusions in his youth. A liver biopsy at 25 years showed portal fibrosis, portal hepatitis, and significant iron deposition. At that time, he began iron chelation therapy and phlebotomy, which was complicated by recurrent vein thrombosis, indwelling port placement for phlebotomy access, and resulting episodes of endocarditis with eventual removal of the port. He had recurrent episodes of cholangitis in the 2010s, leading to secondary sclerosing cholangitis. At that time, his cirrhosis was fully compensated without ascites, encephalopathy, or varices.

He presented to an outside hospital with cholangitis, underwent an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, had a percutaneous transhepatic cholangiogram through the right hepatic duct and drain exchange showing decompressed bile ducts, and received multiple antibiotics for bacteremia. He developed oliguric acute kidney injury, began intermittent hemodialysis, and transferred to our center.

On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable with normal mental status. His laboratory test results showed a Model for End-Stage Liver Disease-Na score of 37 and acute-on-chronic liver failure grade 2, driven by a total bilirubin of 48.2 mg/dL and creatinine 4.25 mg/dL, with an international normalized ratio of 1.3, alkaline phosphatase 100 U/L, aspartate transaminase 174 U/L, and alanine transaminase 94 U/L. A tunneled dialysis catheter was placed, and liver transplant workup was initiated.

His course became complicated during this workup, with venogram for vein mapping before transplant and a left heart catheterization because of a nondiagnostic dobutamine stress echo. He decompensated the day after his heart catheterization and venogram, with hypotension and elevated lactate to 15 mmol/L. A bedside echo showed a large pericardial effusion that had not been present on echo 2 days earlier. An emergency pericardiocentesis drained 200 mL of blood, and a pericardial drain eventually drained over 5.5 L of bloody output. After drainage, his clinical status improved, and he underwent orthotopic liver transplantation with piggyback, interposition graft for the portal vein anastomosis, simultaneous reperfusion of the hepatic artery and portal vein, exteriorization of the donor bile duct, and skin-only closure, followed by completion of hepaticojejunostomy and abdominal wall closure the following week. At the time of transplant, his Model for End-Stage Liver Disease-Na score was 38.

His postoperative course was complicated by 2 episodes of small bowel obstruction that resolved with conservative treatment, as well as cytomegalovirus viremia. His clinical status improved, with recovery of hepatic and renal function, and his laboratory test results normalized.

After transplant, he was started on the pyruvate kinase activator mitipivat for PKD, and the dose was titrated up to maximum with good tolerance. He had actually been approved for this drug shortly before his decompensation, but because mitipivat is hepatically metabolized, his acute-on-chronic liver failure precluded its use until after transplant. His haptoglobin levels, which were previously very low, normalized, as did his lactate dehydrogenase levels.

Histopathological evaluation of his explanted liver showed established cirrhosis (Figure 1) with marked canalicular cholestasis and bile infarcts (Figure 2). Sections from the bile ducts revealed mild epithelial injury and periductal fibrosis in keeping with the history of secondary sclerosing cholangitis (Figure 3). Increased hepatocellular and Kupffer cell iron deposition (3+ of 4+) was noted in Prussian blue stain denoting secondary iron overload (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

End-stage liver disease and acute-on-chronic liver failure are rare complications of PKD. Our patient's case was likely mediated by multiple mechanisms. Chronic hemolysis led to hyperbilirubinemia and cholelithiasis and eventually to repeated bouts of cholangitis and secondary sclerosing cholangitis. In addition, he showed signs of iron overload with increased hepatocellular and Kupffer cell iron deposition. His portal vein thrombosis, with possible thrombotic risk in the context of his splenectomy as a thrombosis risk factor, likely also contributed.

The mechanisms at play in our patient are in consonance to reported cases of PKD-induced liver dysfunction in the literature, which are hypothesized to be because of both biliary sludge and stones with cholestasis,5–7 as well as direct cellular damage from iron overload.4,5 Our patient's repeated bouts of cholangitis contributing to acute-on-chronic liver failure with secondary sclerosing cholangitis from microlithiasis is similar to the case reported by Rinehardt et al.5

In our patient, iron overload was likely partially mediated by repeated transfusions. However, PKD can cause iron overload even in the absence of transfusions.2,4 A recent retrospective analysis of complications in PKD found no significant difference in rates of liver cirrhosis based on transfusion history and liver iron overload in 62% of patients, irrespective of transfusion history.2 Our patient's iron overload was recognized early, and treatment was initiated. Repeated phlebotomy in his case was complicated, first by peripheral access issues with recurrent vein thrombosis and then by central access issues with recurrent episodes of endocarditis necessitating removal of his indwelling port. Interestingly, splenectomy is considered as a risk factor of both iron overload3,4 and gallstones,8 pointing toward our patient's splenectomy early in life as a possible mediator of his eventual need for liver transplant.

To our knowledge, this is the first case reported of a liver transplant because of PKD in our patient's age range. Rinehardt et al reported successful liver transplantation in an 18-year-old woman with PKD, and Chartier et al reported liver transplant in one infant and one 21-year-old man with PKD.5,7 Hilgard and Gerken4 described a 56-year-old man with cirrhosis from PKD who declined liver transplantation, but who did surprisingly well on iron chelation therapy. In this article, we report a 55-year-old man with multifactorial PKD-induced end-stage liver disease, in his case, with secondary sclerosing cholangitis and iron overload, who underwent liver transplantation with a good outcome.
