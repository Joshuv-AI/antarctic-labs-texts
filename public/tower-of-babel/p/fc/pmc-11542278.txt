Sex Differences in Neuropsychiatric Symptoms in Alpha‐Synucleinopathies: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Vincent Couture, Jean‐François Carmel, Katerine Rousseau, Geneviève Létourneau, Doris Clerc, Philippe Huot, Marie‐France Forget, Quoc Dinh Nguyen, Richard Camicioli, Philippe Desmarais
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlpha‐synucleinopathies, such as Parkinson's disease (PD), Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD), and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), demonstrate sex differences with regard to prevalence, age of onset, and motor manifestations. Neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) are common early and late manifestations of these disorders.ObjectivesWe aimed to describe sex differences in NPS across alpha‐synucleinopathies.MethodsWe searched Web of Science Core collection databases to identify observational studies published between January 1, 2000, and June 1, 2022, reporting the prevalence or severity of NPS among individuals with a diagnosis of PD, PDD, or DLB. Prevalence and severity were pooled for each NPS according to sex using random‐effects models.ResultsTwo‐hundred‐and‐forty studies, representing 796,026 participants (45% females), were included in the meta‐analysis. Female sex was associated with a higher prevalence of anxiety (OR = 1.60 [95% CI: 1.40, 1.82]), depression (OR = 1.56 [1.45, 1.67]), fatigue (OR = 1.21 [1.02, 1.43]), and psychotic symptoms (OR = 1.26 [1.14, 1.40]) and more severe anxiety (g = 1.35 [95% CI: 0.58, 2.13]), depression (g = 1.57 [1.05, 2.08]), and fatigue (g = 0.86 [0.41, 1.32]), while male sex was associated with a higher prevalence of apathy (OR = 0.77 [0.63, 0.93]), impulse control disorders (OR = 0.67 [0.55, 0.82]), REM sleep behavior disorder (OR = 0.54 [0.42, 0.70]), hypersomnolence (OR = 0.67 [0.56, 0.80]), and suicide (OR = 0.30 [0.20, 0.44]).ConclusionsNPS have different prevalences and severities in alpha‐synucleinopathies according to sex. These findings support consideration of sex in the elaboration of clinical tools.

Alpha‐synucleinopathies are neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the abnormal production and accumulation of misfolded and aggregated alpha‐synuclein (aSyn) within neurons, causing the intracellular formation of Lewy bodies and the development of Lewy neurites in neuronal processes. 1 Clinical disorders secondary to the cerebral and brainstem accumulation of abnormal aSyn include Parkinson's disease (PD), Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD), and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). PDD and DLB are commonly regrouped under the umbrella term Lewy body dementia (LBD) as both disorders share several similar clinicopathological features. Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is also classified as an alpha‐synucleinopathy, although aSyn accumulates within oligodendrocytes instead of neurons, causing glial cell inclusions (GCI). 2

Alpha‐synucleinopathies present clinically with diverse manifestations, which often overlap across clinical disorders, and with considerable clinical heterogeneity within each specific disorder (ie, various clinical phenotypes). These manifestations encompass motor features (ie, parkinsonism), cognitive impairment (eg, in attention, processing speed, executive function, and visuoperceptual abilities), dysautonomia (eg, orthostatic hypotension, gastroparesis, bladder dysfunction), and neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS). 3 , 4 NPS are ubiquitous and pervasive in alpha‐synucleinopathies and include psychotic manifestations (notably visual hallucinations), anxio‐depressive symptoms, apathy, and sleep‐related manifestations. 5 Their occurrences have major impacts on individuals’ quality of life 6 and independence 7 and cause substantial repercussions on the quality of life of their caregivers, particularly in the case of psychosis, apathy, and mood symptoms. 8

Sex has recently emerged as an important contributing factor to clinical heterogeneity in alpha‐synucleinopathies. Males are at a higher risk of developing PD than females, with higher incidence and prevalence, as well as younger age at onset than females. 9 , 10 Furthermore, males with PD appear to exhibit more cognitive deficits at diagnosis than females 11 and a higher risk of transitioning from PD to PDD. 12 Regarding motor features and pharmacological intervention responses, females with PD have been reported to present more frequently with a tremor‐dominant phenotype, 13 more likely to develop motor and non‐motor fluctuations, and more frequent dyskinesia than males. 14 , 15 Currently, NPS have not been comprehensively studied with regard to their sex‐specific prevalence and severity across alpha‐synucleinopathies. We conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of observational studies to identify significant sex differences in the expression of NPS in individuals with diagnoses of alpha‐synucleinopathies.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of the literature through a predesigned strategy. We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 16 The protocol was registered prospectively on PROSPERO, where it can be accessed (trial registration number: CRD42022336844).

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

We performed a systematic review of the literature by using the Web of Science Core collection databases to retrieve observational studies involving participants with diagnoses of alpha‐synucleinopathies. We used keywords and variations of keywords pertaining to “Parkinson's disease,” “Dementia with Lewy bodies,” and “Neuropsychiatric symptoms” (see Supplementary Material 1). We retrieved studies published between January 1, 2000, and June 1, 2022, without restrictions on language. References of relevant reviews and articles were searched for additional studies, while movement disorder, cognitive neurology, and geriatric psychiatry experts were consulted to ensure that no relevant studies were overlooked. Three reviewers (JFC, PD, and VC) performed the literature search in parallel and independently.

Observational studies were included in the present review and meta‐analysis if they (1) reported the prevalence or the severity of at least one NPS through the use of a validated tool; (2) involved participants with a diagnosis of either PD, PDD, or DLB according to conventional consensus diagnostic criteria 4 , 17 , 18 , 19 ; and (3) reported sufficient sex‐disaggregated data to permit meta‐analysis (eg, counts, frequencies, means, standard deviations). Studies could have either a cross‐sectional or a longitudinal design. We actively sought common NPS, such as the 12 domains of the Neuropsychiatry Inventory (NPI) 20 : delusion, hallucinations, agitation/aggression, depression/dysphoria, anxiety, elation/euphoria, apathy/indifference, disinhibition, irritability/lability, motor disturbance, nighttime behaviors, and eating behaviors. Our definition of what constitutes a NPS was broad and non‐restricted; we also included constellations of NPS (eg, impulse control disorders), specific behaviors (eg, punding, suicide), and neuropsychiatric syndromes/diagnoses [eg, depression, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorders]. Studies were excluded if they (1) were experimental or quasi‐experimental in nature; (2) recruited participants according to the presence or severity of a NPS (ie, selective sampling); or (3) matched participants based on sex. In cases of studies or cohorts generating multiple publications, the article with the largest size cohort was selected for the meta‐analyses. While considered as an alpha‐synucleinopathy, we did not include studies conducted in MSA as this disorder is clinically and pathologically distinctive from PD, PDD, and DLB.

Data Extraction

A predesigned template was used for data extraction and the following data were extracted from eligible articles: author, publication year, country, study design, clinical diagnoses, diagnostic criteria used, total sample size, average age at onset of disease and at study baseline, female percentage, average disease duration, NPS studied, instruments used, and nature of the instruments (eg, self‐rated, caregiver‐rated, clinician‐rated). For longitudinal studies, baseline data were extracted. Extraction was performed by two reviewers (VC and PD) independently and in duplicate to ensure data quality. Extraction discrepancies were resolved by discussion.

Quality Assessment

Two reviewers (VC and JFC) separately assessed included studies' quality using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross‐sectional Studies, 21 which assesses 14 quality criteria pertaining to, among others, study population, participation, selection and eligibility criteria, outcome measures, and key potential confounding variables. Disagreements in quality assessment were resolved by discussion and involvement of a third reviewer (PD).

Statistical Analysis

Meta‐analyses were conducted by pooling sex‐specific prevalence and severity of each NPS across studies using random‐effects models when at least two study results were available and appropriate for pooling. We present summary estimates as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) using the Mantel–Haenszel random effects model for dichotomous data (ie, prevalence of NPS) and mean differences (MD) as Hedges’ g with 95% CI for continuous data (ie, severity of NPS), where OR >1 and positive MD indicate more frequent and severe NPS in females, and OR <1 and negative MD indicate more frequent and severe NPS in males. Forest plots were produced for each meta‐analyze conducted (see Fig. S1–S23).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating statistical procedures while controlling for clinical diagnosis. We planned a priori subgroup analyses to explore sex differences in prevalence and severity of NPS according to average disease duration (<5 years, ≥5 years) and average age at baseline (<65 years, ≥65 years) of studies’ cohorts when at least two study results were available. Heterogeneity was assessed across studies with the I 2 statistic as well as the Q‐test. 22 Risk of publication bias was assessed by generating funnel plots and assessing plot asymmetry for meta‐analyses with 10 or more studies. 23 Statistical analyses were done with SPSS 29.0 24 and Cochrane RevMan software (Review Manager (RevMan) (Computer program), V.5.4, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020). 25

Results

Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 3289 records were identified and screened for eligibility (Fig. 1). Of the 735 records reviewed in the full‐text stage, 365 articles (50%) were excluded as sex‐disaggregated data were unavailable. A total of 240 studies were included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis. Based on the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross‐sectional Studies, 71% of included studies were considered of good quality, while 26% and 3% were considered of fair and poor quality, respectively (see Table S8).

The 240 articles included a total of 796,026 participants (45% females) with an alpha‐synucleinopathy (Table 1). PD was the main clinical diagnosis of focus across studies, with 225 studies (94%). The weighted average age of participants was 76.2 ± 8.5 years and the average disease duration at baseline was 6.1 ± 2.9 years.

A total of 19 different NPS and conditions assessed through 64 distinct validated instruments were identified in the literature; data were sufficient to perform meta‐analyses on 13 specific neuropsychiatric symptoms/conditions: anhedonia, anxiety, apathy, daytime somnolence, depressive symptoms/depression, fatigue, impulse control disorders (ICD), nighttime disturbances, punding, psychosis (delusion, hallucination), REM sleep behavior disorders (RBD), and suicide. Depression was the most studied NPS with regard to prevalence, with 66 studies (28%; 195,125 participants), followed by psychosis with 39 studies (16%; 104,084 participants), ICD with 36 studies (15%; 12,300 participants), and anxiety with 35 studies (15%; 94,350 participants). Individuals being assessed for nighttime disturbances (not RBD specifically; difficulty initiating sleep, fragmented sleep, nightmares, etc.) were the most numerous across all studied NPS, with a total of 411,558 participants (52%; 26 studies). Depression was also the most studied NPS with regard to severity, with 35 studies (15%; 10,227 participants), followed by anxiety with 18 studies (8%; 5968 participants).

Sex Differences in the Prevalence of Specific Neuropsychiatric Symptoms

Meta‐analyses were performed to identify significant sex differences in the prevalence of 13 specific NPS and neuropsychiatric conditions (Table 2). Female sex was associated with a higher prevalence for anxiety [k = 35, OR = 1.60 (1.40, 1.82), P < 0.00001], depression [k = 66, OR = 1.56 (1.45, 1.67), P < 0.00001], fatigue [k = 18, OR = 1.21 (1.02, 1.43), P = 0.02], and psychosis [k = 39, OR = 1.26 (1.14, 1.40), P < 0.00001]. For the psychotic symptoms, female sex was associated with a higher prevalence of hallucination [k = 20, OR = 1.24 (1.06, 1.44), P = 0.006] while there was no significant sex difference in the prevalence of delusion [k = 6, OR = 1.19 (0.79, 1.78), P = 0.41]. Male sex was associated with a higher prevalence for apathy [k = 17, OR = 0.77 (0.63, 0.93), P = 0.008], ICD [k = 36, OR = 0.67 (0.55, 0.82), P < 0.00001], RBD [k = 24, OR = 0.54 (0.42, 0.70), P < 0.00001], daytime somnolence [k = 11, OR = 0.67 (0.56, 0.80), P < 0.00001], and suicide [k = 2, OR = 0.30 (0.20, 0.44), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 2). Overall, fatigue was the most prevalent NPS (pooled average and standard deviation: 47 ± 18%), followed by nighttime disturbances (43 ± 20%), depression (41 ± 20%), and RBD (40 ± 18%). Heterogeneity was high (I 2 ≥ 75%) for nighttime disturbances, moderate (25% ≤ I 2 < 75%) for anxiety, apathy, delusion, depression, ICD, psychosis, RBD, and low (I 2 < 25%) for anhedonia, fatigue, hallucination, punding, daytime somnolence, and suicide.

Sex Differences in the Severity of Specific Neuropsychiatric Symptoms

Meta‐analyses were performed to identify significant sex differences in the severity of eight specific NPS and conditions (Table 2). Female sex was associated with greater severity in anxiety [k = 18, g = 1.35 (0.58, 2.13), P = 0.0006], depression [k = 35, g = 1.57 (1.05, 2.08), p < 0.00001], and fatigue [k = 5, g = 0.86 (0.41, 1.32), P = 0.0002] (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity was high (I 2 ≥ 75%) for anxiety, depression, RBD, and daytime somnolence, moderate (25% ≤ I 2 < 75%) for delusion and fatigue, and low (I 2 < 25%) for apathy and hallucination.

Meta‐analysis could not be performed for several neuropsychiatric symptoms because of insufficient available data, or a lack of studies retrieved (see Table S7).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

Robustness of results was assessed by repeating the meta‐analyses while controlling for clinical diagnosis (see Tables S5 and S6). Exclusion of clinical diagnoses other than PD (ie, PDD, DLB, LBD) modified effect size estimates for six meta‐analyses: five sex differences remained statistically significant (ie, prevalence of anxiety, depression, psychosis, and RBD; severity of depression), while the sex difference found for the prevalence of hallucination was no longer significant [k = 15, OR = 1.08 (0.95, 1.23), P = 0.25].

We performed prespecified subgroup analyses to explore sex differences in the prevalence and severity of NPS and conditions according to average disease duration and average age at baseline of observational studies cohorts (see Table S1–S4). Most notably, female sex was associated with a higher prevalence of anxiety and depression than male sex in cohorts with an average disease duration of <5 years [k = 12, OR = 1.80 (1.56, 2.09), P < 0.00001; k = 16, OR = 1.37 (1.23, 1.53), P < 0.00001, respectively] and ≥5 years [k = 11, OR = 1.94 (1.39, 2.71), P = 0.00001; k = 27, OR = 1.25 (1.18, 1.32), P < 0.00001, respectively]. Male sex was associated with a higher prevalence of ICD and daytime somnolence than female sex in cohorts with an average disease duration of ≥5 years [k = 22, OR = 0.62 (0.48, 0.80), P = 0.0002 and k = 6, OR = 0.68 (0.54, 0.87), P = 0.002, respectively]. Depression was greater in severity for female participants in cohorts with an average disease duration of <5 years and ≥5 years [k = 7, g = 1.64 (0.52, 2.75), P = 0.004 and k = 20, g = 1.37 (0.72, 2.02), P < 0.00001]. Female sex was associated with a higher prevalence of anxiety, depression, and psychosis than male sex in cohorts with an average age at baseline of <65 years [k = 10, OR = 1.59 (1.19, 2.12), P = 0.002; k = 23, OR = 1.29 (1.17, 1.41), P < 0.00001; k = 14, OR = 1.24 (1.05, 1.47), P = 0.01, respectively] and ≥65 years [k = 19, OR = 1.71 (1.42, 20.7), P < 0.00001; k = 36, OR = 1.35 (1.26, 1.45), P < 0.00001; k = 20, OR = 1.34 (1.11, 1.63), P = 0.002, respectively]. Male sex was associated with a higher prevalence of RBD than female sex in cohorts with an average age at baseline of <65 years and ≥65 years [k = 7, OR = 0.61 (0.45, 0.83), P = 0.001 and k = 17, OR = 0.52 (0.37, 0.72), P = 0.0001, respectively]. Finally, anxiety and depression were greater in severity for female participants than male participants in cohorts with an average age at baseline of <65 years [k = 13, g = 1.40 (0.49, 2.31), P = 0.002 and k = 21, g = 1.97 (1.33, 2.60), P < 0.00001, respectively] and ≥65 years [k = 6, g = 1.89 (0.70, 3.08), P = 0.002 and k = 15, g = 1.20 (0.02, 2.39), P = 0.05, respectively].

Funnel Plots and Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed for meta‐analyses of NPS and conditions where there were at least ten studies by generating funnel plots and assessing asymmetry (see Fig. S24). Visual inspection of funnel plots revealed apparent asymmetry for the meta‐analyses on the prevalence of nighttime disturbances and hallucinations and on the severity of anxiety and depression.

In this comprehensive meta‐analysis of observational studies of participants with alpha‐synucleinopathies, we demonstrated that female sex is associated with a higher prevalence of anxiety, depression, fatigue, and psychosis while male sex is associated with a higher prevalence of apathy, ICD, RBD, daytime somnolence, and suicide (Fig. 3). We also demonstrated that female sex is associated with greater severity of anxiety, depression, and fatigue than male sex. Considering the different types of rating instruments used (self‐, caregiver‐ and clinician‐rated), those results may reflect sex‐related inherent differences but also gender‐related biases in the way symptoms are experienced by participants or perceived by their caregivers.

Depression and anxiety were found to be common in alpha‐synucleinopathies, with pooled prevalence of 41 ± 18% and 34 ± 19%, respectively. Female sex was associated with more frequent anxio‐depressive symptoms than male sex, similar to reported sex differences in anxiety and mood disorders in the general population. 26 , 27 While male sex has been associated with a higher prevalence and earlier onset of some psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia, 28 female sex has been reported to be associated with more prevalent positive symptoms (ie, hallucinations) and later onset of psychosis in the general population. 29 In our study, female sex was associated with more frequent psychotic symptoms than male sex, notably with longer disease duration and older age. However, controlling for clinical diagnosis, female sex was no longer associated with more prevalent hallucinations than male sex in PD. This might be explained by the fact that hallucinations are more commonly reported in cognitively impaired individuals (ie, PDD and DLB). 30 Apathy and ICD were also found to be common in our study, with pooled prevalence of 34 ± 16% and 24 ± 20%, respectively. Male sex in alpha‐synucleinopathies was associated with more frequent apathy, ICD, and suicide than female sex. Apathy has previously been reported to be more prevalent and severe in males in other neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease 31 and frontotemporal dementia. 32 These observations could potentially be explained by more frequent and severe co‐pathology, such as cerebrovascular disease and white matter hyperintensities, in frontal lobe structures and networks in males that contribute to action, inhibition, valuation, and planning. 33 , 34 , 35 Moreover, male sex in alpha‐synucleinopathies was associated with more RBD. Our findings are in concordance with previous meta‐analyses reporting a male predominance in RBD. 36 , 37

Significant sex differences with regard to NPS prevalence and severity have been previously reported in other neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease 31 as well as frontotemporal dementia (FTD) and related disorders. 32 In a recent meta‐analysis on NPS in Alzheimer's disease, 31 female sex was found to be associated with higher prevalence of depressive symptoms, psychotic symptoms, aberrant motor behaviors, whereas male sex was found to be associated with more severe apathy. Frequent concomitant Alzheimer's disease pathology in alpha‐synucleinopathies may as well explain some of those similarities. In a cohort study of individuals with FTD, 32 males were found to exhibit more apathy, irritability, aggressivity, and hypersexuality than females.

As expected, we found moderate‐to‐high heterogeneity in our results, which might be explained by the different clinical diagnoses studied, the heterogenic and multidimensional nature of NPS, and the different instruments and measures. Moreover, prevalent co‐pathologies, both vascular and neurodegenerative, in alpha‐synucleinopathies could also explain some of the heterogeneity. We also found significant asymmetry in some of the funnel plots generated, which could illustrate true heterogeneity and the presence of reporting biases: publication bias in general but, most importantly, selective analysis and outcome reporting of sex differences.

While these findings may suggest possible underlying sex‐related biological factors, gender issues (ie, psychosocial factors) may explain some of the differences identified and should be considered as well. Potential gender‐related differences in prescription patterns could exert an influence. For example, higher doses of dopaminergic agents prescribed to males could lead to increased ICD prevalence, a hypothesis which remains to be explored. Caregivers of males with an alpha‐synucleinopathy, more commonly spouses who are women, may report more frequently symptoms suggestive of apathy and ICD as these contribute to their caregiver burden. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 Older women, who may have an alpha‐synucleinopathy, are also less likely to live with a spouse than older men of their own age‐group, hence potentially contributing to the underreporting of some NPS. Furthermore, since NPS are most commonly measured through questionnaires and interviews, assessment, interpretation, and reporting of some NPS may be gender‐biased. 41 , 42 The nature of the instruments used, whether self‐ or caregiver‐rated, likely exerts an influence on this assessment. Passiveness and lack of interest in men may be more frequently interpreted by caregivers, or endorsed by the individuals themselves as apathy, while similar observations in women may be more frequently interpreted or reported as anhedonia, fatigue, or depression. 43

Our study has several strengths. Our eligibility criteria and definitions were broad, allowing us to describe sex differences in several neuropsychiatric symptoms, conditions, and behaviors. Our pooled prevalences of NPS compare with results of previously published studies, attesting to the validity of our statistical models. As well, our prespecified subgroup analyses allowed us to identify disease duration and age at onset as potential factors of neuropsychiatric heterogeneity in alpha‐synucleinopathies. Nonetheless, we must note some limitations of our study. Firstly, while there was a total of 240 studies included in our meta‐analyses, we had to exclude 365 studies because sex‐disaggregated data were unavailable. This affected our ability to perform subgroup analyses according to the specific clinical diagnoses (other than PD). Given the minority of specific PDD and DLB studies in comparison to PD, and the lack of precise data or stratification regarding cognitive status in many studies, the results mostly apply to non‐demented PD. Future observational studies should investigate and report neuropsychiatric symptoms differences between females and males specifically in early stages of PD, such as prodromal PD and PD‐MCI. Secondly, our exploratory subgroup analyses must be interpreted with caution: our statistical models did not use individual participant data (IPD) but average cohort data, which may not entirely capture the effects of age and disease duration. Thirdly, we investigated a priori sex differences in alpha‐synucleinopathies, as sex, the biological variable, is historically and commonly the measured and reported variable in research, not gender, a psychosocial variable. Sex and gender concepts are frequently conflated. We found that 60% of eligible articles inadequately defined sex and/or gender or used the terms interchangeably. Assumptions were, hence, made in the extraction of data. Fourthly, we were unable to assess the effect of psychotropic or dopaminergic medications on the prevalence and severity of NPS as studies infrequently reported current psychotropic medications taken by participants and rarely presented data on dopaminergic medications stratified according to sex. This could have been relevant given the effect of medication on sleep and NPS, in addition to possible sex‐related differences in prescription patterns, adherence, and response. This would make for a very relevant topic for subsequent studies. Lastly, while we reported and analyzed the effects of disease duration and age of onset on neuropsychiatric symptoms according to sex, we did not investigate other important clinical variables such as motor severity and phenotype, which would have been relevant additional factors to consider.

Conclusions

Our systematic review and meta‐analysis described the sex differences with regard to NPS in alpha‐synucleinopathies. Our findings suggest that the prevalence and severity of NPS in alpha‐synucleinopathies, most notably PD, differ significantly according to sex, with female sex having higher prevalence of anxiety, depression, fatigue, and psychotic symptoms and exhibiting more severe anxiety, depression, and fatigue, while male sex having higher prevalence of apathy, ICD, RBD, daytime somnolence, and suicide. Sex contributes to the clinical heterogeneity of alpha‐synucleinopathies through expression of diverse NPS. These findings, while warranting replication in future studies, support consideration of sex in the development of clinical guidelines and tools for the screening, diagnosing, and, potentially, treatment of NPS in alpha‐synucleinopathies.
