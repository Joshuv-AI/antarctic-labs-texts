YOLO-DCF: dual distillation and context-aware fusion for defect detection
Authors: Hongyu Xing, Zhihui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial surface defect detection is critical for ensuring product quality and manufacturing efficiency across steel, electronics, and semiconductor sectors. However, practical deployment remains challenging due to the diversity of defect types, scale variations, and complex background noise. To address these issues, we propose YOLO-DCF (YOLO with Dual Distillation and Context-Aware Fusion), a novel and lightweight detection framework built upon YOLO11. The Context-Guided Dynamic Fusion FPN decomposes global context into orthogonal directional components, enabling precise localization of fine-grained defects while suppressing background noise, the C3k2-Dilated Multiscale Contextual Residual module leverages hierarchical receptive fields with parallel multi-dilation design to capture both local textures and global dependencies, and the Dual Block-Channel Knowledge Distillation module enhances model compression via a self-distillation mechanism by decoupling spatial and semantic knowledge flows, preserving essential representations during lightweight deployment. These modules enhance detection precision while maintaining real-time inference capability. Extensive experiments on NEU-DET and PKU-Market-PCB datasets validate the effectiveness of YOLO-DCF, which achieves mAP50 scores of 79.3% and 96.5%, respectively, representing significant improvements of 1.6% and 0.8% over baseline methods. Notably, YOLO-DCF demonstrates stronger recall and robustness in detecting fine-grained and low-contrast defects, while maintaining competitive real-time inference capability despite the increased model complexity. This work offers a practical and deployable solution for industrial quality inspection, and sets a new direction for efficient, distribution-aware visual recognition in manufacturing contexts.

Introduction

Surface defect detection in industrial products is crucial for ensuring product quality and maintaining manufacturing efficiency. With the increasing demand for high-precision standards, automated defect detection systems have become indispensable across various industries, including steel, electronics, and automotive. These systems not only reduce dependence on manual inspections but also ensure faster and more accurate quality control.

Despite advancements in computer vision, detecting surface defects remains challenging due to diverse defect types, scale variation, low contrast, and complex backgrounds. For example, traditional image processing methods often overlook subtle defects such as fine cracks or low-contrast stains, leading to false negatives. While existing deep learning models are effective, they still struggle with issues such as scalability and accuracy under these complex conditions. To address these challenges, more robust, scalable, and computationally efficient solutions are needed.

The evolution of industrial defect detection has progressed from handcrafted feature extraction to machine learning, and more recently, to end-to-end deep learning systems. Several recent surveys1–4 comprehensively review progress in this field, highlighting the emergence of diverse methodological paradigms that address defect detection from different perspectives. Current deep learning approaches span from direct detection frameworks that learn explicit defect patterns, to reconstruction-based methods that model normal sample distributions, feature-based techniques that leverage representation learning, and knowledge distillation strategies that combine multiple learning paradigms. These methods have collectively enhanced classification accuracy, localization precision, and segmentation quality.

However, significant gaps remain between laboratory benchmarks and real-world deployment. High-performing models often require large-scale labeled datasets, which are expensive and time-consuming to obtain in industrial settings5,6. Moreover, the diversity of surface textures, environmental lighting, and manufacturing artifacts hampers model generalization7. Defect detection systems must also adapt to dynamic production environments, where defect patterns evolve over time and vary across product lines8,9.

To address these challenges, we propose YOLO-DCF, a novel architectural paradigm that introduces the concept of distribution-aware feature harmonization for precise defect localization. YOLO-DCF introduces three key modules:Context-Guided Dynamic Fusion FPN (CFPN): We develop a geometric-aware multi-scale feature integration mechanism that employs axial context decomposition through horizontal and vertical pooling operations. This module employs strategically coordinated Dilated Reparam Blocks with varying kernel sizes and dilation rates, enabling simultaneous capture of dense and sparse spatial patterns while maintaining optimal receptive field configuration. The parallel architecture enhances multi-scale contextual modeling capability essential for handling diverse defect characteristics.Dual Block-Channel Knowledge Distillation (DBKD): We implement a dual-stream knowledge preservation framework that orchestrates complementary distillation mechanisms. Block-Correlation Knowledge Distillation models spatial dependencies between feature blocks, while Channel-wise Distillation aligns semantic features at the channel level. This coordinated approach ensures comprehensive knowledge transfer from enhanced spatial modeling and multi-scale semantic information to lightweight student models.We validate YOLO-DCF on two widely recognized defect detection datasets: the NEU-DET dataset and the PKU-Market-PCB dataset. The model outperforms baseline YOLO11, achieving mAP50 scores of 79.3% and 96.5% respectively, with improvements of 1.6% and 0.8%. The model consistently outperforms YOLO11, achieving better recall and precision while maintaining real-time efficiency. Through the integration of advanced feature fusion and distillation strategies, YOLO-DCF provides a scalable and deployable solution for real-time industrial defect detection.

Related work

This section reviews recent research on industrial surface defect detection using deep learning techniques. Based on the underlying detection mechanisms, existing methodologies can be categorized into four principal classes: detector-based bounding box methods, image reconstruction-based approaches, feature comparison-based techniques, and teacher-student network-based frameworks. For each category, representative models are summarized, and their respective advantages and limitations are discussed to provide insights and references for future developments. Several previous works have laid a solid foundation in this field, offering extensive reference points and background knowledge4,10,11. These studies serve as a basis for the further development of advanced detection methods.

Detector-based bounding box methods

Detector-based bounding box methods represent the most intuitive solution for industrial defect detection, training deep learning models to directly predict bounding boxes and categories of defective regions. The YOLO series exemplifies this approach, employing end-to-end detection architectures that simultaneously accomplish object localization and classification in a single forward pass12–14. YOLO leverages convolutional neural networks to extract image features and directly predicts bounding boxes and categories through anchor box generation, thereby achieving real-time object detection capabilities.

Faster R-CNN further advanced this paradigm by introducing the Region Proposal Network (RPN), establishing an end-to-end learning framework that significantly enhances detection performance15. The RPN serves as a nearly cost-free region proposal algorithm, substantially improving object detection speed. Hu et al.7 applied Faster R-CNN to PCB surface defect detection, proposing a novel network architecture that integrates residual units ResNet50 and GRAPN components.

Recent Transformer-based methods have shown strong potential in surface defect detection. Swin Transformer variants, such as Cas-VSwin16, improve feature transfer via enhanced window-shift schemes and demonstrate superior performance on small-scale industrial datasets through effective transfer learning. Additionally, Gao’s work17 combining traditional operators (e.g., Gabor filters) with deep networks address complex background interference, highlighting the value of Transformer extensions and hybrid designs in challenging industrial scenarios. More recently, hierarchical attention mechanisms and fusion-based architectures have emerged as powerful paradigms for efficient detection. For instance, Zhang et al. proposed ForestBerryNet18, which introduces a hierarchical attention fusion module within a dual-backbone architecture to synergistically optimize multi-scale feature integration. Similarly, Lu et al. developed IMobileTransformer19, utilizing a hybrid design that effectively fuses local features from MobileNet with global representations from Transformers to enhance feature discriminability in complex backgrounds.

Despite the superior performance of detector-based bounding box methods in supervised scenarios, they suffer from severe dependence on large-scale annotated datasets. In industrial defect detection, acquiring defective samples is often prohibitively expensive, accurate annotation requires domain expertise, and the process inherently involves subjectivity. Furthermore, the severe class imbalance between normal and defective samples exacerbates the challenges in model training.

Image reconstruction-based methods

Image reconstruction-based methods detect anomalous regions by learning the data distribution of normal samples and utilizing reconstruction errors for anomaly identification. The core principle underlying these approaches is to train models to reconstruct normal samples effectively; when encountering defective samples, reconstruction errors increase significantly, thereby enabling anomaly detection. Autoencoders (AE) represent the most classical reconstruction-based approach. Mei et al.20 proposed a multi-scale convolutional denoising autoencoder with high accuracy and robustness, capable of synthesizing results across multiple pyramid levels. Yang et al.21 introduced a multi-scale autoencoder incorporating fully convolutional neural networks (FCAE), where each FCAE sub-network directly extracts raw feature maps from input images and performs feature clustering. GANomaly22 combines the advantages of Generative Adversarial Networks and autoencoders, improving reconstruction quality through adversarial training.

However, reconstruction-based methods typically rely on global feature extraction, such as reconstruction error and feature contrast. In industrial defect detection, certain defects—such as fine cracks or low-contrast stains—are often difficult to detect through global features, resulting in poor performance for subtle defect detection. Additionally, real-world industrial data often contain interference factors such as illumination variations, noise, and surface textures, leading to misclassification of non-defective regions as defects.

Feature comparison-based methods

Feature comparison-based methods detect anomalies by comparing feature differences between test samples and normal samples. These approaches typically employ pre-trained feature extractors to obtain deep feature representations, subsequently utilizing statistical analysis or distance metrics for anomaly determination. SPADE23 utilizes deep pyramid correspondences for sub-image anomaly detection, achieving precise localization through multi-scale feature comparison. PatchCore24 represents a seminal work in this category, constructing coresets to compress feature representations of normal samples and utilizing nearest neighbor search for anomaly detection. This method achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple industrial defect detection datasets, demonstrating remarkable generalization capabilities.

The primary advantage of feature comparison-based methods lies in their ability to operate without extensive annotated data, requiring only normal samples for training. However, the performance of these methods heavily depends on the quality of pre-trained feature extractors, and they may exhibit insufficient generalization capabilities when confronted with test samples that significantly differ from the training data distribution.

Teacher-student network-based methods

Teacher-student frameworks have shown strong potential in defect detection by leveraging knowledge distillation. Pei’s work25 proposes a dual-teacher reverse distillation strategy with attention fusion and heterogeneous architectures to enhance feature diversity and avoid overfitting, achieving over 97% pixel-level ROC on MVTec and BTAD datasets. Another approach, DeSTSeg26, introduces a denoising student and segmentation network to improve robustness and feature alignment, trained using corrupted inputs and synthetic masks. It achieves 98.6% image-level AUC and 75.8% pixel-level AP, demonstrating the effectiveness of ST-based methods in anomaly localization and classification.

Teacher-student network-based methods combine the advantages of supervised and unsupervised learning, achieving satisfactory detection performance with limited annotated data. However, these methods involve relatively complex training processes, requiring careful balance between teacher and student networks, and incur higher computational costs.

Methodology

This section introduces the general architecture of the proposed YOLO-DCF network, designed for the industrial surface defect detection. We elaborate on the design of each core module, emphasizing their plug-and-play nature and synergy.

Overall structure of the YOLO-DCF

The proposed YOLO-DCF network builds upon the original YOLO11 framework14 by incorporating three novel components to enhance detection performance under complex industrial conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the YOLO-DCF architecture preserves the classic three-stage detection pipeline: Backbone, Neck, and Distill, while incorporating significant enhancements to improve feature representation and knowledge transfer capabilities.

Fig. 1YOLO-DCF network architecture.

YOLO-DCF network architecture.

First, we introduce the CFPN in the Neck layer to enhance contextual awareness and multi-scale feature fusion. CFPN employs a Rectangular Self-Calibration Module (RSM) for axial global context capture and integrates Dynamic Interpolation Fusion (DIF) with Fuse Block Multi (FBM) for adaptive feature alignment, enabling effective integration of fine-grained details with semantic information. Second, we propose the C3k2-DMCR structure to replace the original C3k2 module in YOLO11. This component addresses scale variation challenges through parallel branches with varying kernel sizes and dilation rates, enabling simultaneous capture of dense and sparse spatial patterns while maintaining computational efficiency. Finally, we design the DBKD framework for efficient knowledge transfer. DBKD integrates Block-Correlation Knowledge Distillation (BCKD) for spatial dependency modeling and Channel-wise Distillation (CWD) for semantic feature alignment, ensuring that the student model retains both spatial and semantic capabilities while reducing computational overhead.

These three innovations establish a unified multi-dimensional enhancement system: CFPN provides enriched multi-scale feature representations as the foundation, C3k2-DMCR leverages these enhanced features to perform advanced spatial relationship modeling, and DBKD effectively transfers the accumulated knowledge from both geometric-aware contextual modeling and hierarchical spatial processing to achieve efficient model compression. This layered enhancement approach enables superior defect detection performance while maintaining computational efficiency for industrial deployment.

Context-Guided dynamic fusion FPN

Industrial defect detection requires specialized contextual modeling that can capture geometric characteristics while integrating multi-scale semantic information. The CFPN is proposed to achieve this goal and its specific structure is shown in Fig. 2. It addresses this fundamental requirement through axial context decomposition and adaptive feature harmonization, establishing the enhanced feature extraction foundation for our Multi-Dimensional Enhancement System. The Rectangular Self-Calibration Module employs horizontal and vertical pooling operations to capture axial global context along two orthogonal directions, generating distinct directional context vectors that emphasize rectangular key regions characteristic of industrial defects. Feature maps P3, P4, and P5 extracted from the backbone undergo average pooling with a downsampling factor of 64, followed by axial context decomposition to produce refined feature representations P3’, P4’, and P5’.

Fig. 2Detailed structure of Context-Guided dynamic fusion FPN.

Detailed structure of Context-Guided dynamic fusion FPN.

The RSM serves as a residual feature transformation block, incorporating Rectangular Self-Calibration Attention (RSA), Batch Normalization (BN), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) transformation, and a layer scaling mechanism. Initially, RSA enables both local and global feature interactions. The transformed features then undergo BN normalization to stabilize training and pass through a ConvMLP to refine channel-wise representations. Furthermore, a Layer Scale parameter is introduced to regulate feature transformation intensity, mitigating gradient explosion risks. The incorporation of DropPath further enhances model generalization by randomly dropping paths during training. Ultimately, the residual connection framework, inspired by the ResNet architecture, improves training stability and feature expressiveness.

Ultimately, the multi-scale feature maps, after feature fusion, are fed into the detection head. C3k2-DMCR establishes a hierarchical spatial processing architecture that leverages the enriched feature representations from CFPN to perform comprehensive contextual modeling through parallel multi-branch design.

The parameter design of the DMCR module thoroughly considers the receptive field sizes required at different network levels. The kernel sizes and dilation rates of the parallel branches are flexible, and the only constraint is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(k-1)r + 1 \le K$$\end{document}, where k represents the kernel size of the small convolutional filters, K denotes the target large kernel size, and r is the dilation rate. Based on the DMCR module, we propose the C3k2-DMCR structure. C3k2-DMCR inherits the overall architecture of the C3k2 module in YOLO 1114 but replaces its core convolution module with our newly designed DMCR. Specifically, C3k2-DMCR can simultaneously capture contextual information at different scales through parallel multi-dilation DRB modules, thereby better adapting to object size variations in detection tasks. Meanwhile, the reparameterization technique in DRB can merge multiple parallel branches into a single equivalent convolution during the inference phase, reducing computational overhead. This ”complex during training, simple during inference” strategy enables the model to maintain high performance while achieving high inference efficiency.

Dual block-channel knowledge distillation

Effective model compression requires comprehensive knowledge transfer that preserves both spatial relationship modeling capabilities and semantic feature representations. DBKD establishes a dual-stream knowledge preservation framework that leverages the enhanced spatial modeling from C3k2-DMCR and multi-scale semantic information from CFPN to achieve optimal knowledge transfer during model optimization.

The dual-stream distillation framework integrates two complementary mechanisms: spatial dependency modeling through block correlations and semantic feature alignment through channel-wise distributions. This coordinated approach ensures comprehensive knowledge preservation across both spatial and semantic dimensions. As illustrated in Fig. 4, our DBKD framework systematically transfers knowledge from a high-capacity teacher model to a compact student model through multiple complementary distillation loss functions.

To preserve the spatial modeling capabilities from C3k2-DMCR, we develop a block-correlation mechanism that captures inter-block dependencies. For block features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B = {b_1,..., b_n}$$\end{document} extracted from both teacher and student models, we compute spatial correlations and apply adaptive feature alignment. The spatial correlation modeling integrates block-wise relationships with correlation-wise dependencies:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{bc} (b_s, b_t)&= \sum _{i=1}^{n} \mathcal {H} ( \psi (b_{si}), \psi (b_{ti}) ) + \sum _{j=1}^{n-1} \mathcal {H} ( \phi (c_{sj}), \phi (c_{tj}) ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi (\cdot )$$\end{document} applies adaptive feature transformation through learned projections, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi (\cdot )$$\end{document} extracts correlations between adjacent blocks, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {H}(\cdot )$$\end{document} represents the Huber loss that combines L1 and L2 advantages for robust transfer. The correlation extraction process models spatial relationships as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} c_j = \text {softmax}\left( \frac{ ( \hat{b}_{i-1} \odot \hat{b}_i)^T}{\sqrt{d}} \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{b}_i = \text {AdaptivePool}(b_i)$$\end{document} generates spatially normalized features and d provides dimension normalization.

For transferring multi-scale semantic knowledge from CFPN, we employ channel-wise distribution alignment that emphasizes salient regions within each channel. The channel-wise distillation loss is formulated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{cwd} = \frac{\sigma ^2}{C} \sum _{n=1}^{C} \text {KL}(P_n^{teacher} \Vert P_n^{student}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where C represents the number of channels, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} denotes the temperature parameter controlling distribution softness, and KL denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The probability distributions for teacher and student networks are defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} P_n^{teacher}(i)&= \frac{\exp (a_n^T(i)/\sigma )}{\sum _{j=1}^{WH}\exp (a_n^T(j)/\sigma )}, \quad P_n^{student}(i)&= \frac{\exp (a_n^S(i)/\sigma )}{\sum _{j=1}^{WH}\exp (a_n^S(j)/\sigma )} \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_n^T(i)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_n^S(i)$$\end{document} represent the activation values of the teacher and student networks at spatial position i in the n-th channel, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 4, H and W denote the height and width of the feature map.

The asymmetric nature of the KL divergence naturally emphasizes foreground regions while reducing background interference, which is particularly crucial for defect detection where target regions are typically small and localized. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_n^{teacher}(i)$$\end{document} exhibits high probability, the student network is encouraged to produce similar activation patterns. Conversely, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_n^{teacher}(i)$$\end{document} is low, the divergence imposes less constraint on the student’s response, allowing for adaptive background suppression.

This unified DBKD framework enables the lightweight student model to simultaneously inherit spatial correlations from C3k2-DMCR’s multi-scale processing and semantic knowledge from CFPN’s contextual fusion, ensuring comprehensive knowledge preservation while achieving the computational efficiency required for real-time industrial deployment.

Experiments and results

This section presents the experimental setup and results for evaluating the proposed YOLO-DCF model. The experiments were conducted on two widely used defect detection datasets: the NEU-DET dataset for steel surface defect detection and the PKU-Market-PCB dataset for printed circuit board defect detection. The purpose of these experiments is to demonstrate the model’s ability to generalize across different industrial domains and assess its performance improvements.

Datasets

Fig. 5Datasets.

Datasets.

To thoroughly validate the effectiveness and generalization capabilities of the proposed YOLO-DCF method, this study employs two widely recognized public datasets as illustrated in Fig. 5: the NEU-DET dataset28 for steel surface defect detection and the PKU-Market-PCB dataset29 for printed circuit board defect detection.

The NEU-DET dataset comprises 1,800 grayscale images that capture six typical surface defects in hot-rolled steel strips: crazing (Cr), inclusion (In), patches (Pa), pitted surface (PS), rolled-in scale (RS) and scratches (Sc). Each defect category contains 300 samples, with images standardized at 200\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}200 pixels resolution. This dataset poses two major challenges for defect recognition. One challenge lies in the imbalanced distribution of defect types across the dataset. Another stems from the high visual similarity between certain defect categories, particularly between crazing and scratches, which makes accurate differentiation difficult, further complicating defect classification.

The PKU-Market-PCB dataset is a synthetic PCB dataset consisting of 693 defective images that showcase six distinct defect types: missing hole (MH), short (Sh), mouse bite (MB), spurious copper (SC), spur (Sp) and open circuit (OC). The defects are evenly distributed across the images but are relatively small in size.

Both datasets serve as benchmarks to evaluate the robustness and generalizability of defect detection methods in different industrial domains. While NEU-DET focuses on metallurgical surface defects with natural variations, PKU-Market-PCB addresses the more structured environment of electronic component manufacturing with synthetically generated defects. For both datasets, a random 7:2:1 split was applied to create training, validation, and test sets, respectively.

In the PCB context, the contextual modeling capabilities of CFPN proved particularly effective for circuit-related defects. The module improved open circuit (OC) detection from 0.966 to 0.972 and short (Sh) detection from 0.953 to 0.979, demonstrating enhanced ability to model fine-grained structural relationships in electronic components. The multi-scale dilated architecture of C3k2-DMCR showed particular strength in capturing small defects typical in PCB environments, achieving improvements in both precision (0.972) and recall (0.928) while maintaining stable mAP@50 (0.956).

The final YOLO-DCF model achieved the highest mAP@50-95 score of 0.514, representing significant improvement in strict localization accuracy. This metric holds particular importance for PCB inspection where defect boundaries must be precisely identified for automated repair systems. The consistent performance improvements across both datasets indicate robust architectural generalization rather than dataset-specific optimizations.

The ablation results demonstrate three key insights. First, CFPN excels in contextual defect detection but requires complementary modules for precision optimization. Second, C3k2-DMCR provides robust multi-scale feature extraction with inherent false positive suppression capabilities. Third, DBKD enables deployment-ready models without sacrificing sophisticated detection capabilities, addressing both recall and precision challenges simultaneously.

Sensitivity analysis and distillation strategy

We performed an extensive sensitivity analysis with respect to the teacher model selection and hyperparameter setup in order to confirm the robustness of the proposed framework and support our architectural decisions.

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis of the distillation weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} (Block-Correlation) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} (Channel-wise).

Instead of employing a heterogeneous large-scale teacher (e.g., Swin Transformer), we adopted a self-distillation approach, in which the teacher and student share the same architecture. Since recent studies30 show that a significant capacity gap between teacher and student can result in feature misalignment, making it difficult for the student to mimic the teacher’s complex distributions, we prioritised this approach primarily to mitigate the capacity gap. In contrast, an isomorphic teacher provides supervision signals strictly within the student’s representational capacity. Furthermore, the feature maps in the DBKD module are naturally aligned in both spatial resolution and channel dimensions because the teacher and student have identical architectures. This guarantees lossless feature alignment and eliminates the need for layer alignment.

While \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} follow to the recommended values from the original work 31, we performed a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis to examine the weights for Block-Correlation loss (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}) and Channel-wise loss (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}) in Eq.1, which are crucial for balancing spatial and semantic knowledge transfer. Figure 6 shows the mAP performance across various combinations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}, revealing different optimisation landscapes for various industrial scenarios, using a dense grid search within a local neighbourhood of the optimal hyperparameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4 \times 4$$\end{document} grid). With a balanced configuration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =1.0, \delta =1.5$$\end{document}), the model achieves optimal performance on NEU-DET (Fig. 6a). The optimisation landscape is smooth and convex, with performance fluctuations staying within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 0.3\%$$\end{document} in the vicinity of the peak, exhibiting high robustness to parameter perturbations. As opposed to the texture-centric defects in NEU-DET, the optimal weight for spatial distillation on PKU-Market-PCB (Fig. 6b) shifts significantly higher (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =1.8$$\end{document}), which is consistent with the physical characteristics of the dataset where PCB defects involve rigid geometric structures, requiring stronger spatial constraints enforced by the Block-Correlation module.

Additionally, although models like DsP-YOLO and YOLOv9t report comparable or slightly better performance in mAP@50 or mAP@50–95, their architectural complexity is significantly higher than that of YOLO-DCF. For instance, DsP-YOLO has 11.1M parameters and 28.5 GFLOPs, making it less suitable for deployment on edge devices or in resource-constrained environments. In contrast, YOLO-DCF achieves a favorable balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency, rendering it highly practical for real-world deployment in industrial edge-based quality inspection systems.

Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we proposed YOLO-DCF, a compact yet high-performing framework specifically designed for industrial surface defect detection. By integrating three novel components: CFPN, C3k2-DMCR, and DBKD into the YOLO11 backbone, YOLO-DCF achieves a balanced trade-off between accuracy, efficiency, and deployability.

YOLO-DCF’s performance gains stem from the synergistic integration of three core innovations: CFPN enhances contextual awareness and multi-scale feature fusion through Rectangular Self-Calibration Module, Dynamic Interpolation Fusion, and Feature Balance Module, particularly improving detection of low-contrast and fine-grained defects such as crazing (Cr) and scratches (Sc). C3k2-DMCR introduces parallel multi-scale dilated residual structures with optimized receptive field configuration, enhancing spatial relationship modeling and localization precision across varying defect scales, especially under stricter IoU thresholds (mAP@50-95). DBKD enables effective model compression through dual distillation mechanisms—BCKD for spatial dependency transfer and CWD for semantic feature alignment—resulting in a lightweight architecture suitable for real-time inference and industrial deployment. Experimental results demonstrate that YOLO-DCF achieves mAP@50 of 79.3% on NEU-DET (+1.6%) and 96.5% on PKU-Market-PCB (+0.8%) compared to baseline YOLO11n, while maintaining only 3.2M parameters with inference latency as low as 3.6 ms.

This work presents a systematic enhancement framework for industrial defect detection, making four fundamental contributions: (1) Enhanced Feature Extraction: CFPN implements geometric-aware multi-scale feature integration through axial context decomposition and adaptive feature harmonization; (2) Advanced Spatial Processing: C3k2-DMCR introduces hierarchical spatial relationship modeling through parallel multi-branch architecture with strategically coordinated dilated convolutions; (3) Intelligent Model Optimization: DBKD establishes dual-stream knowledge preservation that effectively transfers spatial and semantic knowledge during model compression; (4) Systematic Enhancement Framework: Our layered approach demonstrates how coordinated improvements across feature extraction, spatial processing, and knowledge transfer collectively enhance performance.

Despite these promising results, several limitations warrant further investigation. Current evaluation focuses primarily on controlled benchmark datasets, which may not fully capture real-world industrial challenges such as motion blur, varying material reflectivity, equipment occlusions, or dynamic lighting conditions. The generalizability to unstructured or noisy manufacturing environments requires additional validation with diverse industrial partners. DBKD performance is inherently dependent on teacher model quality and domain similarity, where suboptimal teachers or significant domain shifts may reduce distillation effectiveness while introducing additional training complexity. Furthermore, while YOLO-DCF achieves an optimal balance between accuracy and speed, the architectural complexity introduced by CFPN and C3k2-DMCR modules increases GPU memory consumption during training. Although structural re-parameterization reduces inference latency, the high transient memory cost of multi-branch operators may still pose challenges for direct deployment on low-power edge devices with limited memory bandwidth.

Future research directions should address these limitations through several approaches: developing domain adaptation techniques that enable robust generalization to diverse real-world environments by collaborating with industrial partners to collect comprehensive datasets covering edge cases, while minimizing labeled sample requirements through semi-supervised learning, adversarial training, or contrastive learning methodologies. Additionally, we plan to investigate model quantization and structural pruning to mitigate GPU memory bottlenecks. Creating optimized YOLO-DCF variants through knowledge transfer to efficient architectures will enhance deployment capabilities on embedded systems such as FPGAs and edge devices, ensuring broader industrial adoption while maintaining detection performance.
