Extracellular Vesicle‐Inspired Minimalist Flexible Nanocapsules Assembled with Whole Active Ingredients for Highly Efficient Enhancement of DC‐Mediated Tumor Immunotherapy
Authors: Ao He, Qiang Li, Meng Dang, Wei Lu, Xiaoye Li, Zhuo Dai, Meng Ding, Yu Zhang, Heng Dong, Zhaogang Teng, Yongbin Mou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of nanovaccines capable of eliciting tumor‐specific immune responses holds significant promise for tumor immunotherapy. However, many nanovaccine designs rely heavily on incorporating multiple adjuvants and carriers, increasing the biological hazards associated with these additional components. Here, this work introduces novel flexible nanocapsules (OVAnano) designed to mimic extracellular vesicles, primarily using the ovalbumin antigen and minimal polyethylenimine adjuvant components. These results show that the biomimetic flexible structure of OVAnano facilitates enhanced antigen uptake by dendritic cells (DCs), leading to efficient antigen and adjuvant release into the cytosol via endosomal escape, and ultimately, successful antigen cross‐presentation by DCs. Furthermore, OVAnano modulates the intracellular nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway, promoting DC maturation. The highly purified antigens in OVAnano demonstrate remarkable antigen‐specific immunogenicity, triggering strong antitumor immune responses mediated by DCs. Therapeutic tumor vaccination studies have also shown that OVAnano administration effectively suppresses tumor growth in mice by inducing immune responses from CD8+ and CD4+ T cells targeting specific antigens, reducing immunosuppression by regulatory T cells, and boosting the populations of effector memory T cells. These findings underscore that the simple yet potent strategy of employing minimal flexible nanocapsules markedly enhances DC‐mediated antitumor immunotherapy, offering promising avenues for future clinical applications.

Introduction

In recent years, tumor immunotherapy has emerged as a promising approach for cancer management,[ 1 ] with tumor vaccination becoming one of the most reliable immunotherapy strategies.[ 2 ] Tumor vaccines use antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) to activate cancer‐specific cytotoxic T‐lymphocytes, thereby inhibiting tumor growth through long‐term anti‐cancer immunity.[ 3 ] Among APCs, dendritic cells (DCs), are the most crucial, playing a pivotal role in tumor immunotherapy.[ 4 ] DCs capture and process tumor antigens, activate and proliferate T cells, and initiate a specific immune response against the tumor.[ 5 ] Additionally, DCs migrate to lymph nodes to transmit antigen information to T cells, fostering the formation of immune memory for long‐term tumor defense.[ 6 ] Effectively activating the function of DCs is essential for successful tumor treatment, and ongoing research seeks to enhance their antitumor activity.[ 7 ] To date, numerous engineered biomaterials loaded with antigens, known as nanovaccines, have been developed and investigated as tumor vaccines to promote DC‐mediated immunotherapy.[ 8 ] However, most tumor vaccines fail to meet expectations in clinical trials because simple ingredients often struggle to elicit an adequate immune response, or the complex components of the vaccine are not conducive to clinical translation.[ 9 ] Therefore, developing methods to overcome these obstacles and enhance the clinical effectiveness of nanovaccines is imperative.

A wide array of nanocarriers has been designed to concurrently transport adjuvants and antigens to DCs. However, most nanocarriers demonstrate limited capabilities in boosting antigen presentation and DC maturation without additional adjuvants.[ 10 ] The conventional approach involves tweaking biomaterial characteristics, such as size, surface modifications, shape, and flexibility, to amplify the release of immunostimulatory molecules from DCs and improve antigen delivery and presentation.[ 11 ] Regrettably, this often necessitates additional carriers to act as vehicles and support the immune components, as free components are difficult for immune cells to uptake. Consequently, the potential risks of biomaterials in vivo remain a concern. Recently, “carrier‐free” nanovaccines have been proposed to mitigate the risks associated with carriers.[ 12 ] Although these nanovaccines are compact, do not require carriers, and are self‐adjuvant, they often exhibit inadequate antigen delivery, insufficient biocompatibility, and unstable physical and chemical properties.[ 13 ] Nevertheless, carrier‐free strategies offer several advantages in nanovaccines synthesis,[ 14 ] such as longer blood circulation time, better cellular penetration, high drug‐carrying capacity and low toxicity. Advances in biomimetic nanomedicine have enabled enhancements in the functionality of designed nanomaterials by emulating natural structures.[ 15 ]

The most effective form of intercellular communication is facilitated by the transfer of extracellular vesicles, including exosomes, shed microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, which are readily delivered and absorbed directly.[ 16 ] This process facilitates natural interactions with the cells of the organism, thereby enhancing cellular uptake and biocompatibility.[ 17 ] These functions were attributed to the biomimetic structural advantages of vesicles.[ 18 ] The flexibility of vesicles enables easier fusion with or uptake by target cell membranes, significantly enhancing the efficiency of intracellular delivery of drugs and therapeutic agents.[ 19 ] In addition, their ability to adapt to various physiological conditions and microenvironmental changes ensures structural and functional stability, which is crucial for applications in complex biological systems.[ 20 ] Consequently, constructing nanovaccines with flexible, extracellular vesicle‐like structures holds great promise for promoting robust DC‐mediated immune responses.

In this study, we utilized a core‐assisted protein coating and selective etching method to fabricate a biomimetic antigen nanocapsule (OVAnano) that mimics the structure of flexible extracellular vesicles (Scheme 1 ). Characterized by its uniform size, OVAnano possesses a distinct hollow interior and a flexible outer layer similar to natural vesicles. Our in vitro findings highlighted the superior antigen delivery and remarkable ability of OVAnano to facilitate antigen escape from lysosomes. We conducted extensive transcriptome analysis to explore the potential mechanisms of OVAnano in antitumor immunotherapy, focusing on its capacity to activate DCs. Concurrently, OVAnano effectively suppressed the growth of B16‐OVA tumors and increased the infiltration of immune cells into the tumor microenvironment in vivo. Additionally, OVAnano induces durable immune memory against tumors in vivo, providing sustained protection against targeted tumor challenges. This study reveals that OVAnano, as a biomimetic antigen nanocapsule, potently elicits a robust immune response against tumors, demonstrating its potential as an effective strategy for DC‐mediated tumor immunotherapy.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of OVAnano and Biocompatibility Evaluation

OVAnano was produced using a core‐assisted protein coating and selective etching method (Figure 1A). Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) synthesized via the modified Stöber method exhibit perfectly spherical shapes and uniform particle sizes that can be adjusted by modifying synthesis conditions. This method enhances the preparation of consistent, stable protein capsules, making them cost‐effective and facilitating large‐scale production due to their simple synthetic processes. The surface of MSNs, rich in hydroxyl groups, improves drug loading by enhancing electrostatic adsorption capabilities. Their mesoporous structure allows easy removal of the template using etching agents, streamlining the capsule preparation process. To produce OVAnano, spherical MSNs were coated with the OVA antigen and infused with the adjuvant polyethylenimine (PEI). The mesoporous silicon core was then selectively etched away. Preferential etching primarily stems from compositional differences. Hydrofluoric acid (HF) has a high chemical affinity for SiO₂, allowing it to rapidly react with SiO₂ to form the water‐soluble hexafluorosilicic acid (H₂SiF₆). In proteins, HF primarily induces weak protonation and fluorination reactions. Therefore, HF penetrates the protein shell and preferentially etches the inner MSNs.

In contrast to certain “carrier‐free” nanovaccines that rely on liposomal frameworks or tumor cell membranes,[ 21 ] OVAnano, constructed solely from an antigen and a portion of PEI, offers unparalleled advantages. The inclusion of PEI, which acts as an immune adjuvant to enhance both innate and adaptive immunity,[ 22 ] has been confirmed for clinical application as an adjuvant ingredient.[ 23 ] This process preserves the vesicle‐like structure characterized by the protein shell. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealing the hollow interior and wrinkled surface of the OVAnano protein capsule, demonstrating its flexibility and extracellular vesicle‐like structure (Figure 1B) with a uniform diameter of 262.91 ± 26.99 nm (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Cross‐linking of proteins with PEI ensures a stable structure after removal of the MSN core, resulting in consistent hollow OVAnano nanocapsules (Figure 1C). High‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) and energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images demonstrated the homogeneous distribution of C, N, and O in the OVAnano framework, resulting from the protein component of the shrunken shell (Figure 1D). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) results also reveal that OVAnano has a uniform hydrodynamic diameter of ≈314 nm (Figure S2, Supporting Information).

Advances in nanovaccines design and implementation have enhanced cancer vaccine efficacy and reduced toxicity within immune cells and their microenvironment, mitigating side effect risks and underscoring the importance of biocompatibility.[ 24 ] DC2.4 cells underwent Calcein‐AM/PI staining and a cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay to assess in vitro cytotoxicity of OVAnano. After 24 h of co‐culture, the DC2.4 cells and bone marrow‐derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) exhibited negligible apoptosis (Figure 1E and Figure S3, Supporting Information) and maintained high viability (Figure 1F) across a range of OVAnano concentrations (0~80 µg mL−1). This suggests that OVAnano has excellent biocompatibility. In addition, normal tissue cell lines, such as HaCaT cells, were used to confirm that OVAnano has good biocompatibility with Calcein‐AM/PI staining (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Moreover, OVAnano displayed negligible hemolytic effects (Figure S5, Supporting Information), and the morphology of red blood cells remained unchanged after co‐incubation with different concentrations of OVAnano. In contrast, intact erythrocytes were hardly found after co‐incubation with water (Figure S6, Supporting Information), indicating its favorable blood compatibility (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Furthermore, OVAnano was injected into mice through the tail vein, and after 30 d of observation, the main organs were removed. Histological analysis (Figure S7, Supporting Information) confirmed the in vivo biocompatibility of OVAnano, revealing the absence of notable toxicity or inflammatory infiltrates in the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of treated mice.

OVAnano Enhanced Antigen Internalization and Presentation

Antigen internalization is crucial for the activation of DCs and subsequent process of antigen cross‐presentation.[ 25 ] Typically, internalized antigens are sequestered within intracellular compartments with no escape from endosomes, culminating in lysosomal degradation. Cytoplasmic delivery facilitated by OVAnano possesses cationic PEI components owing to its efficiency in the proton sponge effect of PEI (Figure 2A). The entry of unbuffered amines coupled with a relentless proton supply from the V‐ATPase proton pump results in the accumulation of chloride ions and water, which induces osmotic swelling and subsequent endosomal disruption.[ 26 ] This mechanism paves the way for the effective cross‐presentation of antigens, entailing the cytosolic release of exogenous antigens from endosomal vesicles, subsequent proteasomal breakdown, ER transport, and eventual loading onto MHC‐I molecules. The positively charged surface of OVAnano further confirmed PEI doping (Figure 2B). Next, we tested DC antigen internalization using OVAnano containing Alexa Fluor 647 dye (AF647)‐labeled OVA (OVA‐AF647), referred to as OVAnano‐AF647. Flow cytometry (FCM) was used to quantitatively evaluate the cellular internalization of OVA‐AF647 and OVAnano‐AF647. DCs internalized OVAnano‐AF647 at markedly elevated levels compared with free OVA‐AF647 (Figure 2C). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of OVAnano‐AF647 absorbed by DCs was determined to be 76.6 ± 10.7, 92.7 ± 16.3, 143.3 ± 17.7, 211.6 ± 22.6, 311.6 ± 54.9, and 415.3 ± 14.7 at time intervals of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, and 12 h, correspondingly. For the free OVA‐AF647 group, the MFI was 33.3±4.8, 38.2±3.0, 44.9±1.6, 44.4±20.3, 66.6±16.1, and 97.3±12.8 at the matched time intervals (Figure 2D). Similarly, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images after treating DCs with OVAnano and free OVA for 6 hour also showed that the fluorescence signal of AF647 was stronger in the OVAnano group, whereas AF647 fluorescence signals were barely detectable in the OVA group (Figure 2E). OVAnano displayed stronger red fluorescence intensity of OVA located in the cytoplasm of DCs with higher separated red and green fluorescence channels compared to free OVA (Figure 2F), suggesting that OVAnano could deliver more antigens escaping from the endosomes to the cytoplasm after internalization.

Furthermore, we conducted an in vitro B3Z T‐cell activation assay using the chlorophenol red‐β‐d‐galactopyranoside (CPRG) chromogenic reaction to evaluate the activation of T cells and cross‐presentation by DCs. Upon TCR recognition of OVA257‐264‐H‐2kb+ complexes, B3Z cells activate the transcription of IL‐2 promoter elements, leading to the expression of the lacZ gene and synthesis of β‐galactosidase. This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of CPRG into chlorophenol red. Hence, a quantitative analysis of B3Z T‐cell stimulation demonstrated that PBS, OVA, and OVAnano activated ≈35.5 ± 2.2%, 35.9 ± 3.1%, and 87.0 ± 9.1% of B3Z cells, respectively, compared to the affirmative control SIINFEKL (Figure 2G). Through the cross‐presentation pathway, foreign antigens are presented on MHC I molecules, which are crucial for triggering CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses against tumors. We next evaluated the formation of antigen‐MHC I complexes in DCs exposed to OVAnano. Compared to BMDCs exposed to OVA alone, BMDCs treated with OVAnano showed a progressive enhancement in the expression of the cross‐presentation marker SIINFEKL‐H‐2Kb on the DC surface compared to BMDCs exposed to OVA alone, with increasing concentrations of OVAnano (Figure 2H). The levels of OVA257‐264‐H‐2kb+ on the surface of BMDCs increased ≈20.07‐fold, 37.25‐fold, and 124.07‐fold after 24 h when OVAnano was administered at concentrations of 20, 40, and 80 µg mL−1, respectively, compared to OVA‐treated DCs (Figure 2I). Additionally, MHC‐I (Figure 2J,K) and MHC‐II (Figure S8, Supporting Information) were significantly upregulated in the OVAnano group. These findings indicate that OVAnano is superior in promoting antigen internalization and cross‐presentation.

OVAnano Augmented Maturation and Activation of DCs

Antigen presentation and immune response activation depend largely on mature DCs, whereas upregulation of costimulatory molecules and increased cytokine secretion characterize DC maturation (Figure 3A).[ 27 ] Adequate stimulation is crucial for triggering a potent immune response; insufficient stimulation can lead to immune tolerance.[ 28 ] Previous studies have reported that PEI not only enhances vaccine uptake and antigen presentation in DCs through its cationic nature and proton sponge effect[ 29 ] but also promotes DC maturation and T‐cell activation by upregulating critical surface costimulatory molecules.[ 30 ] FCM analysis showed that DCs co‐cultured with OVAnano exhibited a significant increase in the expression of CD80/CD86 (Figure 3B,C) and CD40 (Figure 3D,E) compared to DCs treated with PBS and OVA. Additionally, OVAnano treatment upregulated CD197 (Figure 3F,G), a chemokine receptor that induces rapid but transient migration of DCs to draining lymph nodes. This migration is essential for initiating protective immunity and maintaining immune homeostasis.[ 31 ] Consistent with the FCM data, we observed that OVAnano effectively induced DC maturation by causing the secretion of elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)−12p70 (Figure 3H), IL‐6 (Figure 3I), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α (Figure 3J), and IL‐1β in vitro (Figure 3K).

The maturation of DCs enhances their antigen presentation capacity and the efficiency of T‐cell activation. To explore the immune mechanism of OVAnano in promoting DC maturation, genomic mapping of BMDCs treated with PBS (control) or OVAnano was performed using RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq). The transcriptome of OVAnano‐treated BMDCs exhibited different features compared to PBS‐treated BMDCs (Figure S9, Supporting Information), identifying 2209 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Of these, 1026 DEGs were up‐regulated and 1183 DEGs were downregulated (Figure S10, Supporting Information). The volcano plot and heatmap showed that the differential expression profiles were primarily attributed to genes involved in innate immune and proinflammatory responses (Figure 4A and Figure S11, Supporting Information). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to link the biological functions of DEGs, revealing that the enriched terms were mainly related to immune processes, including innate immune response, cellular response to interferon‐β (IFN‐β) and IFN‐γ, inflammatory response, and nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway (Figure 4B). To investigate the network connections among DEGs, the protein‐protein interaction (PPI) network was also visualized and showed that the expression of antigen presentation genes (H2‐DMa, H2‐Aa, Cd74, H2‐Ab1, and H2‐DMb2), interferon regulatory factor genes (Irf7), and proinflammatory cytokines (Ccl5, Cxcl9 et al.), were upregulated and highly correlated in the connection network (Figure S12, Supporting Information). Collectively, the transcriptomic analysis revealed that the majority of DEGs from OVAnano‐treated BMDCs were highly related to innate immune responses mediated by IFNs.

The transcription factor NF‐κB regulates multiple aspects of innate and adaptive immune functions and serves as a key mediator of inflammatory responses. NF‐κB plays a key role in regulating the survival, activation, and differentiation of innate immune cells and inflammatory T cells.[ 32 ] The activation of NF‐κB involves two major signaling pathways, classical and non‐classical (or alternative). Both pathways are essential in regulating immune responses, despite differences in their signaling mechanisms. The results demonstrated that OVAnano activates NF‐κB through the classical pathways, specifically via inducible degradation of IκBα, triggered through its site‐specific phosphorylation by a multi‐subunit IκB kinase (IKK) complex. Pervious research highlights the importance of NF‐κB and IFN pathways in mature conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1). However, the tumor microenvironment's specific inhibition of NF‐κB impairs the IFN‐γ gene response in cDC1, limiting their capacity to attract and activate antitumor CD8+ T cells.[ 33 ] Given the crucial link between IFN production and NF‐κB activation, our investigation aimed to determine if OVAnano influences DC immune responses via the NF‐κB pathway. Western blot results (Figure 4C,D) confirmed that OVAnano significantly elevates NF‐κB levels, indicated by the phosphorylation of p65 and IκB kinase (IKK). Following NF‐κB pathway activation, downstream products like NOD‐like receptor family, pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes and cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) were notably increased in the OVAnano group (Figure 4E,F), with NLRP3 also promoting NF‐κB signaling enhancement.[ 34 ] Furthermore, NCR1, part of the immunoglobulin superfamily,[ 35 ] was upregulated in OVAnano‐treated DCs (Figure S13, Supporting Information). These findings collectively suggest that OVAnano significantly promotes DC maturation via NF‐κB pathway activation, presenting a promising avenue for bolstering antitumor immune responses.

OVAnano Facilitates DC‐Mediated Immune Responses In Vivo

Considering the outstanding efficacy of OVAnano in stimulating dendritic cell maturation, facilitating antigen cross‐presentation, and enhancing antigen‐specific T‐cell proliferation, we assessed its immunization potential in vivo. C57BL/6 mice were immunized with PBS, OVA, or OVAnano on days 0, 5, and 10. The efficacy of immunization was assessed on day 15 through comprehensive analyses of immune responses in harvested lymph nodes, serum, and splenocytes, as shown in Figure 5A. Serum analysis revealed that OVAnano vaccination resulted in the highest levels of OVA‐specific immunoglobulin G (IgG), indicating a more potent humoral response to OVAnano than to the other treatments (Figure 5B). Since vaccines can elicit both Th1 and Th2 immune responses, where Th1 generates IgG2a antibodies associated with IFN‐γ production to promote CTL responses and Th2 produces IgG1 antibodies linked to IL‐4 secretion for antibody production,[ 36 ] the higher IgG2a/IgG1 ratio in OVAnano‐immunized mice suggests a predominant Th1‐type immune response (Figure 5C). Notably, of all mice assessed on day 15, those vaccinated with OVAnano displayed the highest serum levels of IFN‐γ, underscoring its potent immunization effect (Figure 5D).

After being exposed to the same antigen for a second time, there is an occurrence of more robust immune responses that are specific to the antigen, along with the secretion of IFN‐γ. To evaluate the OVAnano‐induced immune response in T cells, splenocytes from vaccinated mice were isolated and restimulated with SIINFEKL. Intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) via flow cytometry (FCM) was used to measure levels of intracellular IFN‐γ. As shown in Figure 5E,F, the proportion of CD3+CD8+IFN‐γ+ CTLs in the spleen of mice immunized with OVAnano was ≈6.46 and 2.25 times higher compared to PBS‐ and OVA‐immunized mice. Beyond FCM, the IFN‐γ enzyme‐linked immunosorbent spot (ELISPOT) assay was conducted on splenic cells after antigen re‐stimulation. Figure 5G,H demonstrated that the number of IFN‐γ spot‐forming cells (SFCs) in the OVAnano group significantly exceeded that of other groups. This further confirmed the efficacy of OVAnano in stimulating OVA‐specific CD8+ T cell activation. Similarly, in antigen‐re‐stimulated splenocytes from OVAnano‐immunized mice, the ratios of CD3+CD4+IFN‐γ+ T cells (Figure 5I,J) and CD3+CD8+Granzyme B+ T cells (Figure 5K,L) were significantly higher compared to those immunized with PBS and OVA. Consistent with the in vitro results, the quantity of DCs (CD11c+SIINFEKL‐H‐2Kb+ cells) presenting antigens (Figure 5M,N) and the levels of mature DCs (CD11c+CD80+CD86+ cells) (Figure 5O,P) in the inguinal lymph nodes (iLNs) of mice immunized with OVAnano were greater than those in the free OVA and PBS groups (Figure 5M–P). These results indicate that OVAnano can effectively stimulate antigen‐specific T‐cell immune responses, promote DC maturation and activation in vivo (Figure 5Q).

Ovanano Vaccine Significantly Inhibits Tumor Growth and Alleviates Tumor Microenvironment

Inspired by the impressive prophylactic efficacy of the OVAnano vaccine, we explored the potential of our nanovaccine to target and eliminate preexisting tumors. To test this hypothesis, we established a mouse tumor model by subcutaneously injecting OVA‐expressing melanoma cells (B16‐OVA cells). The mice were then subjected to three rounds of immunization with PBS, OVA, or OVAnano to evaluate the in vivo antitumor immunity triggered by OVAnano (Figure 6A). In contrast to PBS, which served as a control, and free OVA, which showed negligible effects on tumor suppression (Figure S14, Supporting Information), OVAnano vaccination led to a marked reduction in tumor growth, underscoring its significant therapeutic advantage (Figure 6B,C). Specifically, on day 21, the OVAnano‐treated group exhibited an average tumor volume of merely 332.24 mm3, whereas the PBS group and free OVA group recorded volumes of 1081.90 and 1209.27 mm3, respectively. Meanwhile, the resected tumor weight in the OVAnano group was the lowest compared with that in the other groups (Figure 6D).

Tumor tissues were subjected to histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Figure 6E) and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick‐end labeling (TUNEL) (Figure 6F). Notably, the OVAnano‐treated tumors exhibited significantly fragmented and smaller nuclei and an increased TUNEL positive cells, indicating pronounced apoptosis among the tumor cells following OVAnano administration. In addition, a substantial increase in the proportion of mature DCs (CD11c+CD80+CD86+ cells) within the tumor tissues of the OVAnano‐treated group was observed, as depicted in Figure 6G,H, highlighting the potent immunotherapeutic effect of OVAnano. We evaluated the infiltration of effector memory T cells (CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L−) in tumor tissues, known for their role in mediating antitumor protection against rechallenged tumors.[ 37 ] OVA‐nano‐treated mice displayed a significant increase in effector memory T cells compared to the other groups (Figure 6I,J). The accumulation of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) (identified as CD45+CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) within tumors is associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis, largely because of the capacity of Tregs to dampen immune responses against the tumor via mechanisms of immune tolerance or escape.[ 38 ] Emerging evidence suggests that Tregs undermine vaccine efficacy of vaccines.[ 39 ] Notably, as shown in Figure 6K,L, OVAnano treatment resulted in a decreased presence of Tregs (average 4.65%) in the tumor microenvironment, in contrast to PBS (average 11.5%) and OVA (average 7.61%). These results demonstrate that OVAnano‐treated mice effectively generated immunological memory, offered lasting protection, and managed to lower Treg levels, thereby countering the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. To confirm the presence of functional T cell groupings in tumors, cryostat sections of the tumors from mice treated differently were stained using anti‐CD4 antibody (green), anti‐CD8 antibody (red), and anti‐INF‐γ antibody (yellow) (Figure 6M). Tumors of OVAnano‐immunized mice exhibit higher infiltration and accumulation of CD4+, CD8+ and IFN‐γ+ T cells, indicated by green, red, and yellow signals. FCM results within the tumors of OVAnano‐treated tumor‐bearing mice showed that the proportions of CD3+CD8+ (Figure 6N) and CD3+CD4+ (Figure 6O) T cells were the highest.

To evaluate immune responses and further explore the mechanism underlying the antitumor effect of the nanovaccines, we collected lymph nodes, serum, and spleens from different treatment groups of tumor‐bearing mice (Figure 7A). FCM analysis showed that the numbers of CD11c+SIINKFEL‐H‐2Kb+ DCs (Figure 7B,C) and CD11c+MHC‐II+ cells (Figure S15, Supporting Information) were higher than those in mice treated with PBS or OVA. The proportion of mature lymph node‐infiltrating DCs treated with OVAnano was greater than that in PBS and OVA treatments (Figure 7D,E). The levels of IFN‐γ, IL‐6, and TNF‐α cytokines in the serum were notably elevated in the OVAnano group compared to the remaining groups, indicating that OVAnano stimulated a cellular immunity biased toward Th1 response (Figure 7F–H).

To assess the tumor recurrence prevention capabilities of OVAnano, spleen cells from tumor‐bearing mice in various treatment groups were analyzed. Notably, upon a second exposure to the antigen peptide, the ratios of CD3+CD8+IFN‐γ+ (Figure 7I,J), CD3+CD4+IFN‐γ+ (Figure 7K,L), and CD3+CD8+Granzyme B+ T cells (Figure 7M,N) in splenocytes from tumor‐bearing mice were significantly elevated in the OVAnano‐immunized group compared to those immunized with PBS or OVA. Furthermore, IFN‐γ ELISPOT assays revealed that the OVAnano group exhibited a substantially higher number of IFN‐γ SFCs than the other groups (Figure 7O,P), demonstrating the superior ability of OVAnano to evoke immune responses and mitigate tumor recurrence.

These results suggest that the OVAnano vaccine successfully initiated a robust OVA‐specific immune response in B16‐OVA‐bearing mice (Figure 7Q). The use of OVAnano resulted in significant tumor tissue necrosis and tumor cell apoptosis along with the activation of DCs, potent antigen‐specific T cell responses, decreased Treg infiltration, and a notable boost in effector memory T cell populations. Together, these effects contribute to the effective suppression of tumor growth in vivo.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated the remarkable efficacy of OVAnano, a biomimetic nanocapsule mimicking flexible extracellular vesicles, in stimulating DC‐mediated antitumor immune responses. The flexibility of OVAnano enhances the efficiency of endosomal antigen release upon DC internalization, facilitating substantial delivery and release of cytosolic antigens for improved antigen cross‐presentation. Furthermore, OVAnano successfully drives DC maturation and activation through the activation of the NF‐κB signaling pathway. In vivo, tumor‐bearing mice vaccinated with OVAnano exhibited excellent tumor growth inhibition, reduced immunosuppressive effects, and enhanced significant immune memory, underscoring the pivotal role of biomimetic nanovaccines in enhancing DC‐mediated tumor immunity and providing insightful directions for vaccine development. In future, the substitution of the model antigen OVA with personalized antigens opens up broader applications for these biomimetic, flexible, hollow nanocapsules. Their simplicity, biosafety, and effectiveness make them ideal candidates for developing personalized vaccines tailored to patient‐specific neoantigens, promising a more targeted and effective approach for immunotherapy. Moreover, as nanotechnology continues to evolve, antigenic nanocapsule‐based “carrier‐free” strategies can integrate multiple inorganic metal ions (such as Mn2+) to promote more effective antitumor immunity.
