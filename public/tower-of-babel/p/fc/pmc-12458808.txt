LTO1 and YAE1 regulate MHC-I expression via nonsense-mediated RNA decay in tumor cells
Authors: Zhengning Yang, Zhongxuan Meng, Shangyuan Liu, Yuxin Chen, Xuhong Gan, Zi‑Mei Zhang, Xiaoshan Luo, Jiahong Yan, Huijin Chen, Danqing Huang, Lihong Huang, Xiaomin Zeng, Wenyu Ma, Jun Liu, Linlin Wang, Haotian Huang, Xinyi Yang, Mei Hu, Lin Chen, Hongli Du, Qibin Leng, Li Qiu, Xiaoling Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNonsense-mediated messenger RNA decay (NMD) is a highly conserved surveillance system that degrades mRNAs with premature termination codons (PTCs), and regulates the mRNA quantity. Cancer cells hijack NMD to compensate for the imbalanced DNA levels and modulate their antigenicity. We identified the LTO1/YAE1 complex as NMD factors regulating ribosome biogenesis. Our study aimed to investigate their roles in modulating NMD and major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) antigen presentation, which is vital for cancer immunotherapy.MethodsClustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and the CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9)-mediated gene knockouts, overexpression, and mutational analysis were used to explore the role of LTO1 and YAE1 in NMD and MHC-I expression across various tumor cell lines, assayed by fluorescent reporter-based assays, fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis, reverse transcription-quantitative PCR, mRNA decay assay, and polysome profiling. Transcriptomic analyses were used to assess the expression of LTO1/YAE1 and their correlation with MHC-I molecules in human cancers. T cell receptor (TCR)-T cells and tumor cells coculture were employed to monitor the effect of LTO1 and YAE1 loss on T cell activation. Iron chelators were further tested as NMD inhibitors to enhance cancer immunotherapy using in vitro coculture assay and a mouse tumor model for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy.ResultsWe demonstrate that deficiency in LTO1, YAE1, or their downstream target ABCE1 impairs NMD, causes the overexpression of key regulators of MHC-I, including NLR family CARD domain containing 5 (NLRC5), interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1), and nuclear factor-kappa B (NFκB), which results in enhanced T cell activation and tumor cell killing in TCR-T models. Transcriptomic analyses reveal that the LTO1/YAE1 complex is frequently overexpressed in human cancers, where it negatively regulates MHC-I. Moreover, low doses of iron chelators inhibit NMD and enhance MHC-I expression, leading to improved recognition and activation of CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting antigen-specific killing and increasing the efficacy of TCR-T and ICB therapy.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings establish novel roles for the LTO1/YAE1 complex in regulating MHC-I expression via NMD. As MHC-I is crucial for antigen presentation and T cell activation, these results reveal a previously unappreciated link between NMD and tumor immunogenicity, with potential implications for cancer immunotherapies.Keywords: immune checkpoint inhibitor, major histocompatibility complex - MHC, T cell

Background

Nonsense-mediated messenger RNA decay (NMD) is one of the translation-dependent surveillance systems, which modulate mRNA quality and quantity of translation to ensure appropriate cellular responses to various stimuli, including development, differentiation, and stress.1,4 NMD was originally discovered degrading mRNAs containing premature termination codons (PTCs).4 5 Recently, large-scale transcriptome analyses have shown that NMD can be activated by unaltered mRNA targets, providing a post-transcriptional strategy to regulate gene expression.6,8 NMD defects can cause dysregulation of 5%–20% of genes across different organisms.6 8 To date, >20 NMD factors that are involved in NMD have been identified.1 3 Although the molecular mechanism of NMD has not yet been fully delineated, plenty of evidence has shown that NMD is involved in the pathophysiology of multiple human genetic diseases, including cancer.2 9

The role that NMD plays in tumorigenesis is complicated.3 4 NMD may inhibit tumor growth by degrading normal mRNAs encoding stress response proteins and signaling factors. However, NMD can also promote tumorigenesis in several ways. PTC-introducing mutations contribute to a substantial portion of mutations in tumors.6 7 NMD actively degrades mRNAs encoding immunogenic neoantigens.2 10 Recently, Ippolito et al reported that tumor cells with aneuploidy hijacked NMD factors to counteract the transcriptional burden, making them vulnerable to NMD inhibitors (NMDi).11 Additionally, studies have shown that NMD preferentially targets the mutations in tumor suppressor genes rather than oncogenes, leading to the downregulation of tumor suppressor genes.2 4 Consequently, strategies that interfere with NMD can benefit immunotherapy against tumor types with high mutation loads, either to stimulate the expression of immunogenic neoantigens or to enhance the translation of tumor suppressor genes.7 10 12 13

LTO1, also named oral cancer overexpressed 1, is overexpressed in many solid tumors, including esophageal squamous cell cancer, laryngeal squamous cancer, hepatocellular cancer, and gastric cancer.14,16 LTO1 is located at chromosomal band 11q13, one of the most frequently amplified regions associated with human cancer, alongside CCND1 (cyclin D1), FGF19, and 11 other genes.17 Downregulating LTO1 reduces tumor cell viability.14 LTO1 partners with YAE1 via deca-GX3 motifs to function in the cytosolic Fe-S protein assembly (CIA) machinery, conserved across virtually all eukaryotes.18,20 In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, LTO1 is essential for 60S ribosomal subunit maturation and translation initiation.18 ABCE1, targeted by LTO1/YAE1, is a known NMD regulator, mediating 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR)-directed regulation through maintaining ribosome recycling.20,22

In this study, we identified two NMD factors, LTO1 and YAE1, which regulate hundreds of known NMD targets and modulate ribosome biogenesis in mammalian cells. We observed that LTO1/YAE1 deficiency leads to defective ribosomal activity in tumor cells and the deca-GX3 motif is critical for LTO1 function. Importantly, disruption of LTO1, YAE1, and their downstream target ABCE1, together with other NMD regulators (SMG6, SMG7, and known NMDi), uncovers that inhibition of NMD activity results in MHC-I upregulation in tumor cells, providing additional mechanism by which NMD inhibition may benefit cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Cell lines and plasmids

Cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 (A549, Raji, H292, and J.RT3-T3.5) or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (293T, A375, and HeLa), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Lonsera) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies). HeLa cells were purchased from Meisen Chinese Tissue Culture Collections (Zhejiang, China), while the others were obtained from American Type Culture Collection. Monthly PCR-based mycoplasma tests were conducted to ensure that all cell lines used were mycoplasma-free.

Jurkat CD8-NFAT (JK8NF) reporter cell line was used to generate JK8NF-1G4 cells as previously described.23 JK8NF cells were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 with the lentivirus packaged with pLKO-1G4 TCR. A375-ar151-reporter cell lines, stably expressing the ar151 guide RNA and a reporter, were generated by transducing wild-type A375 cells with the lentiviruses encoding ar151 and the reporter sequence at a MOI of 10 (PLKO-ar151-CMV-BFP; PLKO-Reporter). mCherry and BFP double-positive cells were sorted by the FACSAria III. Thawed PBMCs were activated with Dynabeads human T-activator CD3/CD28 (Life Technologies). The next day, T cells were transduced twice with lentiviral particles by spinoculation at an MOI of 1 in the presence of protamine (10 μg/mL). TCR-transduced T cells were then expanded in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 200 IU/mL of interleukin-2. The transduction efficiency was calculated by staining for mouse CD8 and tetramers. LTO1 complementary DNA (cDNA) was cloned and constructed into the pLKO plasmids using Clone Express II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). Two synonymous mutations were introduced (cytosine to adenine at position 78 and thymine to cytosine at position 81 of the LTO1 gene) to avoid being targeted by sgLTO1-1. Using this backbone, LTO1 mutant proteins at positions G38; G42; G46 of the deca-GX3 motif (mut_1) and G54; G58 (mut_2) were generated, in which guanines were mutated to cytosines.

Poly(I:C) (InvivoGen, #tlrl-picw) was transfected by forward transfection using Lipo8000 Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, C0533) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with 1 µg/mL Poly(I:C) for 16 hours. DFX (aladdin, D126658), 5-azacytidine (MCE, HY-10586), and amlexanox (HARVEYBIO) were added directly to the cells at the indicated concentration and time points.

Flow cytometry

Fluorochrome-labeled antibodies are listed in online supplemental table S2. Phycoerythrin-labeled tetramers were produced in-house following production protocols from National Institutes of Health tetramer core facility. All antibodies and tetramers were used at 1:100 dilution. Data were quantified using BD FACSAria III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed with FlowJo V.10 and CytExpert software.

Puromycin incorporation assay

Puromycin incorporation assays were performed as described in Manjunath et al.24 For knockout cells, the puromycin-resistant gene in lentiCRISPR V.2 backbone was replaced with blasticidin-resistant gene. After lentiviral infection and blasticidin selection (5 µg/mL, Beyotime Biotech, ST018) for 7 days, A375 cells were treated with puromycin (10 µg/mL, Sangon Biotech, A610593) for 10 min prior to fixation and staining for flow cytometry analysis.

Library preparation and CRISPR screening

The Toronto human knockout pooled library (TKOv3) was a gift from Jason Moffat (Addgene #90294). The library was prepared following the depositor’s protocols.25 The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform to generate paired-end reads. At least 5G clean data were obtained for each sample. Raw data were analyzed with the MAGeCK (0.5.9.3) package and genes were ranked by Robust Rank Aggregation score. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package for genes with absolute log2 fold change (FC) >1 obtained from differential analysis.

Construction of knockout cell lines and validation of the gene editing efficiency

The top two guides for each gene were validated. gRNAs (online supplemental table S1) were cloned into lentiCRISPR V.2 backbone as described in Sanjana et al.26 LentiCRISPR V.2 was a gift from Feng Zhang (Addgene plasmid # 52961; http://n2t.net/addgene:52961; RRID: Addgene_52961). Knockout cell lines were generated by transduction followed by puromycin selection for 7 days. The T7 endonuclease I assay (NEB M0302L) was performed to verify the gene editing efficiency following manufacturer’s recommendations. Primers used are listed in online supplemental table S1.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Total RNA of tumor cells was extracted using RaPure Total RNA Micro Kit (Magen, R4012-03) and reverse-transcribed using a HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R323-01). The cDNA was used as the template for the subsequent RT-qPCR analysis using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02). GAPDH was used as the normalization gene. Primers of targeted genes are listed in online supplemental table S1.

RNA decay assay

To evaluate the RNA stability, actinomycin D (AbMole, M4881) was added to cells with a final concentration of 10 μg/mL. Cells were then collected after 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 hours, respectively, and RNA was isolated for RT-qPCR to calculate the relative abundance of mCherry, EGFP, and ATF3 mRNA. The relative expression level was normalized to 0 hour, and the levels of remaining transcript were calculated based on the mathematical formula: relative expression=2–ΔCt, where ΔCt=(X hour)–Ct (0 hour).

RNA-seq and data analysis

RNA was isolated using the RaPure Total RNA Micro Kit (Magen, R4012-03). RNA purity was assessed using NanoDrop and its integrity was evaluated using Agilent 4200 TapeStation. For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was used as input for library preparation according to standard protocols. Library preparations were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Chi-Biotech, Guangzhou, China) and 150 bp stand-specific paired-end reads were generated. The quality of raw reads was assessed using the FANSe algorithm. Adapters were trimmed using Cutadapt (V.4.8), and low-quality reads were filtered out based on having more than 10% N bases or matching ribosomal RNA sequences. Clean reads were subsequently mapped to the human genome (HUMAN GENCODE 34 GRCh37) using START (V.2.7.10b) software. To assess data reproducibility, principal component analysis plots were generated. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the edgeR package (V.3.18.1) and defined by a Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p value <0.05 and an absolute log2 FC >1. GSEA and GSVA were implemented using the clusterProfiler (V.4.10.1) package to characterize the biological functions associated with DEGs.

Immune-related signatures

The immune phenotype scores were computed as previously described27 and the continuous total scores were adopted. The ssGSEA algorithm was used to calculate the related gene signature scores. The tertiary lymphoid structure signature using the following gene markers: CD79B, EIF1AY, PTGDS, RBP5, CCR6, SKAP1, LAT, CETP, CD1D.28 The antigen processing and presentation signature cited genes from previous literature.29

Tumor cell coculture

1G4-NFAT-Jurkat cells and A375 cells with LTO1, YAE1, or ABCE1 knockout were seeded at the indicated E:T ratio with or without DFX (15 µM) in 96-well plates (Thermo) at a density of 5×104 cells per well. Plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 48 hours. The 1G4-NFAT-Jurkat cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry.

LTO1 and control knockout A375 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. PBMCs were then seeded at the indicated E:T ratio. Plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 48 hours. Images were taken at 24-hour intervals using the IncuCyte ZOOM system. The killing efficiency was assessed by comparing the area of confluence over time.

In vivo mouse xenograft models

OT-I mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (ID#: 003831) and bred in a specific pathogen-free animal facility of Guangzhou Medical University. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Guangdong Experimental Animal Center. Female C57BL/6 mice were injected subcutaneously with 2×106 MC38-OVA cells. When tumor volume reached 100–150 mm3, C57BL/6J mice were treated with DFO (15 mg/kg) or vehicle control (PBS) once every 3 days for a total of five treatments. Tumor size was measured twice per week and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: volume (mm3)=length×width2/2. All mice were sacrificed at the end of the experiment, and the tumors were excised and weighed. For analysis of T-cell infiltration, lymph node single-cell suspensions were prepared in PBS by mechanical digestion. The tumor tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspension by enzyme digestion (Sigma), then stained and analyzed by flow cytometry. To generate murine subcutaneous tumors, 2×106 MC38 cells were injected into the flanks of recipient C57BL/6J mice, which were then treated intraperitoneally with PD-1 antibody (InVivoMAb, BE0146) (2.5 mg/kg), in the absence or presence of DFO (15 mg/kg), once every 3 days.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism V.9. Differences between the two groups were evaluated using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while differences across multiple groups over various time points were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Tumor growth curves between treated and control groups were compared using two-way analysis, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. Data are presented as mean±SD. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of LTO1/YAE1 as NMD factors regulating mRNA degradation and translation termination

We identified LTO1 and YAE1 as NMD regulators in a whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 screen initially aimed at investigating RNA editing regulation in A375 human melanoma cells. This screen used an mCherry reporter gene with a GFP gene positioned downstream of the stop codon (figure 1A). A-to-I editing was engineered using the LEAPER model, with a 151 bp guide RNA reverse complementary to the sequence surrounding the stop codon and containing a U-to-C mutation to recruit the endogenous adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1). Successful ADAR-mediated RNA editing can convert the UAG stop codon into a UGG coding sequence, resulting in GFP fusion protein expression (figure 1A).30 The A375 reporter cells were then infected with a genome-wide Toronto human lentiviral CRISPR library targeting approximately 18 000 genes.25 Six days post-transduction, cells were trypsinized and fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis (FACS) sorted (figure 1A,B). We collected the top and bottom 10% of the population, representing the GFPhigh and GFPlow groups (figure 1B). We hypothesized that genes targeted by single-guide ribonucleic acids (sgRNAs) enriched in the GFPhigh group and depleted in the GFPlow group function as negative regulators of RNA editing. Sequence analysis revealed LTO1 and YAE1 as top hits in both groups (figure 1C). These results were validated by individually knocking out each gene using lentivirus-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 with two corresponding guide RNAs (gRNAs) per gene (figure 1D). Due to the lack of specific antibodies for western blot analysis, we confirmed efficient gene editing in A375 cells using reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and the T7E1 assay (online supplemental figures S1 and S2). Compared with sgLTO1-2, sgLTO1-1 mediated a more pronounced GFP induction effect, consistent with its higher knockdown efficiency (70% vs 34%) and gene modification efficiency (41.1% vs 30%) (online supplemental figures S1 and S2). Additional validation of enhanced GFP expression was conducted in another tumor cell line, HeLa cells (online supplemental figure S3A–C).

Interestingly, LTO1 and YAE1 demonstrated unique mCherry reporter expression patterns compared with other candidates (figure 1D, online supplemental figure S3A,B). GFP expression was increased in LTO1 or YAE1 knockout cell lines, and mCherry expression was likewise significantly elevated in a time-dependent manner (figure 1E). RT-qPCR analysis revealed an increase in mRNA levels of these reporter genes, suggesting the involvement of a mechanism independent of A-to-I editing (figure 1F). To further rule out RNA editing effects, we used a reporter cell line lacking ADAR-recruiting RNA and observed the same elevated GFP and mCherry expression in LTO1 knockout cells via flow cytometry analysis, indicating that this was an ADAR1-independent effect (online supplemental figure S4). Consistently, western blot analysis revealed substantial upregulation of mCherry protein levels in A375 reporter cells following knockout of LTO1 (4.1-fold), YAE1 (19-fold), and ABCE1 (18-fold). Notably, GFP protein exhibited dramatically stronger upregulation than mCherry (45-fold vs 4.1-fold in LTO1-KO; 26-fold vs 19-fold in YAE1-KO) despite being transcribed from the same mRNA (figure 1G). This disproportionate induction was further supported by the detection of ~27 kDa native GFP (rather than the expected 55 kDa edited fusion product), suggesting a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism independent of RNA editing (figure 1G, online supplemental figure S5A,B). Similar results were observed in HeLa-reporter cells with LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1 knockout (online supplemental figure S5C). Surprisingly, GFP polyclonal antibody recognized two ~27 kDa bands in all three knockout cells (figure 1G, online supplemental figure S5C), indicating translation initiation at downstream sites despite the absence of canonical ATG start codons. These results suggest that impaired translation termination in knockout cells enables ribosome readthrough, followed by reinitiation at non-canonical sites downstream of termination codons (TCs) within the same reading frame.

LTO1/YAE1 does not possess an RNA-binding domain. In yeast, LTO1 and YAE1 form an adaptor in the CIA machinery, which is necessary for the maturation of ABCE1, a known NMD regulator, through the insertion of the [4Fe-4S](Cys)4 cluster.20 In our genome-wide screen, ABCE1 sgRNAs were highly depleted in the GFPlow group, although they were not significantly enriched in the GFPhigh group (figure 1C). A375 reporter cells lacking ABCE1 also showed a marked increase in mCherry and GFP signals (figure 1D, online supplemental figure 3A–C). We hypothesized that the reporter mRNA might be recognized as containing a PTC, with the enhanced mCherry expression resulting from compromised NMD leading to transcript stabilization. In this context, GFP expression likely reflects abnormal translation termination and subsequent readthrough events extending into the 3′UTR region. This interpretation aligns with the findings by Annibaldis et al, which show that ABCE1 depletion caused ribosome stalling at TCs leading to NMD inhibition. The resulting ribosome collisions push the stalled ribosomes into the 3′UTR, where translation resumes both in-frame and out-of-frame.22

To directly assess NMD activity, an mRNA decay assay was performed in LTO1 knockout A375 cells (figure 1H,I). Actinomycin D was used to stop transcription, and mRNA stability was measured over time by RT-qPCR. LTO1-deficient cells exhibited a significantly delayed degradation in reporter mRNA and ATF3 mRNA (a canonical NMD substrate) (figure 1H,I). Together, these results suggest that LTO1 plays an important role in NMD and translation termination fidelity.

LTO1 and YAE1 are required for NMD and ribosome biogenesis

To further investigate the functions of LTO1 and YAE1, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on knockout cells from two independent biological replicates. mRNAs were collected from both A375-reporter cells and wild-type A375 cells 7 days after CRISPR/Cas9-induced LTO1 knockout via lentiviral transduction and puromycin selection, with irrelevant gRNAs targeting LacZ or luciferase as negative controls (figure 2A). The same approach was applied for YAE1 and ABCE1 knockouts (online supplemental figure S6). Notably, 720 upregulated genes were shared across LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1 knockout cells, indicating closely related functions (figure 2B). Compared with potential NMD target genes previously identified by Colombo et al,31 32 LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1 knockouts displayed overlaps of 197, 119, and 180 genes, respectively, with 49 shared across all (figure 2B). The expression of several known NMD targets, ATF3, GADD45A, GADD45B, and DNAJB21 was confirmed in LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1-deficient A375 cells by RT-qPCR (figure 2C). These data strongly suggest that all three genes are broadly involved in NMD regulation.

Annibaldis et al and others have shown that ABCE1 depletion leads to reduced translation activity and NMD defects, likely due to ribosome stalling at TCs.21 22 33 The yeast counterpart of LTO1 is required for ribosome biogenesis, specifically in the maturation of the 60S ribosomal subunit.18 Consistent with these findings, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set variation analysis (GSVA) revealed that factors involved in cytoplasmic translation and ribonucleoprotein biogenesis were significantly downregulated in LTO1 knockout cells, paralleling the results in YAE1 and ABCE1 knockouts (figure 2D,E, online supplemental figure S7A–C). In line with its role in 60S subunit maturation in yeast, a reduction in several ribosomal proteins (RPS27, RPL35A, RPL26, RPS27A, RPL34, RPL21, RPS25, RPS21, RPL36A, RPL14, RPL31, RPS23, RPS7, RPS3A, and RPL7) was downregulated in LTO1-deficient A375 cells (online supplemental figure S8). RT-qPCR confirmed the downregulation of two 60S ribosomal proteins, RPL7 and RPL14, in all three knockout cells (figure 2F). The global protein synthesis in LTO1-deficient cells was measured by puromycin incorporation assay. Puromycin, a structural analog of aminoacyl transfer RNAs, can incorporate into nascent peptides and be detected using antipuromycin antibodies.22 24 In LTO1 knockout cells pulsed with puromycin, an increased population of puromycin-negative cells was observed, indicating inhibited translation initiation (figure 2G,H). To further assess the translation efficiency, polysome profiling was conducted on the LTO1 and YAE1 knockout A375 cells. Both knockouts yielded a clear increase in the 80S/60S ratio (2.12 in control cells vs 3.375 in LTO1 knockout cells and 3.17 in YAE1 knockouts), indicating impaired 60S biogenesis (figure 2I). Additionally, reduced size and abundance of polysomes were observed, with the polysome/monosome ratio decreasing by 24% in LTO1 knockouts (3.68 vs 2.8) and 15% in YAE1 knockouts (3.28 vs 2.8) (figure 2I), suggesting defective translation. Overall, these data demonstrate that LTO1 or YAE1 is required for NMD and ribosome biogenesis in mammalian cells.

LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1 deficiency enhances overall MHC-I expression

Interestingly, RNA-seq results also revealed that LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1 are closely linked to immune responses (figure 2D,E, online supplemental figure S7A–C), with upregulation of antigen presentation genes in knockout cells (figure 3A,B). These findings were verified by flow cytometry using an antibody recognizing human HLA-A, B, and C (figure 3C). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed the elevated expression of HLA-A, B, and C at the mRNA level (figure 3D). This result was replicated in HeLa and A549 using lentivirus-mediated knockout of LTO1 and YAE1 by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure S9A,B) and RT-qPCR analysis (online supplemental figure S9C,D). Previous studies have demonstrated that IRF1, nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), and NLRC5 regulate MHC-I expression by binding its promoter.34 Interestingly, RNA-seq showed upregulation of all these genes in LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1 knockout cells (figure 3A,B). Indeed, increased NF-κB expression was confirmed by western blot analysis (figure 3E), and RT-qPCR also confirmed elevated IRF1 mRNA levels (figure 3F, online supplemental figure S9E,F). Additionally, the elevated expression of transporter associated with antigen processing 1 (TAP1) was validated by RT-qPCR, indicating broad induction of MHC-I molecules in LTO1-deficient, YAE1-deficient, and ABCE1-deficient cells (figure 3F, online supplemental figure S9E,F). Given interferon (IFN)-γ’s role in upregulating MHC-I molecules, we explored whether LTO1 modulates IFN-γ-induced response and found that LTO1 knockout further enhanced IFN-γ effects in A375 cells (figure 3G). These findings suggest LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1 act as negative regulators of MHC-I expression.

To further validate the roles of LTO1 in NMD and MHC-I upregulation, we generated targeted mutations at conserved amino acid residues critical for LTO1 function. LTO1, a 137-amino-acid protein, interacts with YAE1 through its evolutionarily conserved deca-GX3 motif, which spans 40 amino acids and includes several conserved glycine residues (figure 3H). In yeast, glycines at positions 33, 37, 41, 49, and 53 are critical for its function.20 Mutations in these residues completely disrupt the binding with YAE1 and impair the maturation of ABCE1.20 To investigate this in human cells, we generated two codon-optimized mutant forms of human LTO1 by introducing mutations in the glycine residues at positions 38/42/46 (Mut_1) and 54/58 (Mut_2) to alanine, which correspond to the glycines at positions 33/37/41 and 49/53 in yeast (figure 3H). In LTO1 knockout cells, the reporter assay revealed that the overexpression of either the wild-type LTO1 or the Mut_2 neutralized GFP and mCherry overexpression (figure 3I). The Mut_1 was unable to rescue the phenotype, indicating that mutations at these three glycine residues (38/42/46) completely abolish protein function (figure 3I). Similar results were observed in attempts to rescue the phenotype of MHC class I induction caused by LTO1 knockout (figure 3J). Consistent with the reporter assay, Mut_2 was able to compromise the deficient LTO1-induced MHC-I upregulation, whereas Mut_1 did not (figure 3J). Taken together, these data support that the observed effects are LTO1-specific, and G38/42/46 is essential for its function in NMD and MHC-I upregulation, underscoring the critical role of the deca-GX3 domain in LTO1 function in mammalian cells.

LTO1 and YAE1 are dysregulated and negatively associated with immune infiltration in human cancers

Then, we examined the global expression of LTO1 and YAE1 by interrogating the transcriptomes of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pan-cancers compared with the relevant normal tissues. The results showed that in 13 out of 24 cancer types, both genes are significantly overexpressed, suggesting these genes are frequently hijacked by tumor cells to support tumor growth (figure 4A,B, online supplemental figure S10). We then examined the correlation of HLA-related gene expression with the level of LTO1 and YAE1. Pan-cancer analysis further showed that the expressions of LTO1 and YAE1 are negatively correlated with HLA-A, B, C expression in 10 cancer types (figure 4C,D).

We then used the single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm35 to infer the infiltration of intratumoral immune cells36 among all TCGA cancer types. We observed that LTO1 and YAE1 are negatively correlated with T cell infiltration in 19 and 24 cancer types (figure 4E,F). In addition, other immune cells, including B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, and NKs, all demonstrated negative correlations with LTO1 and YAE1, indicating that these two genes are negatively associated with immune cell infiltration (figure 4E,F). Consistently, negative correlation between immune cell infiltration with ABCE1, SMG6, and SMG7 was also observed (online supplemental figure S11). Furthermore, LTO1 and YAE1 are also significantly negatively correlated with immune phenotype scores,27 antigen processing and presentation signature,29 and tertiary lymphoid structure signature28 in most cancer types (figure 4G). These findings suggest that these NMD factors may be potential therapeutic targets for enhancing immunoregulatory activities.

LTO1/YAE1 deficiency sensitized T cell activation

Last, we aimed to determine whether LTO1 or YAE1 deficiency could enhance tumor cell recognition by T cells. We employed a previously engineered JK8NF reporter cell line (derived from J.RT3-T3.5), expressing both CD8 for TCR coreceptor function and a nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT)-responsive ZsGreen under the control of the NFAT promoter as a reporter for T cell activation23 (figure 5A). We then transduced this reporter cell line with lentivirus overexpressing 1G4 TCR, which recognizes the HLA-A*02:01-presented NY-ESO-1 peptide (SLLMWITQC)37 (figure 5B). The reporter cells were functionally validated through coculture assays with either: (1) TAP-deficient T2 cells (HLA-A2+) pulsed with SLLMWITQC peptide or (2) HT1080 cells (NY-ESO-1+/HLA-A*02:01−) transduced to express HLA-A*02:01. Coculture induced ZsGreen expression in >60% JK8NFAT-1G4 cells, confirming reporter functionality (online supplemental figure S12). A375 cells, which naturally express HLA-A*02:01 and NY-ESO-1, served as target cells. We then cocultured JK8NF-1G4 cells with A375 cells with LTO1, YAE1, or ABCE1 knockout for 24 hours (figure 5C–F). LTO1, YAE1, or ABCE1 deficiency significantly increased the number of ZsGreen+ cells after the tumor cell coculture, suggesting that T cell recognition and activation are sensitized by targeting these three genes (figure 5G).

We further confirmed these findings using an in vitro 1G4 TCR-T model. 1G4 TCR was expressed on human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by lentivirus transduction. At day 9 post-transduction, flow cytometry analysis revealed that 27% of the total cell population was double-positive for both 1G4-TCR and CD8 (figure 5H). Tumor cell killing efficiency was measured at 24 hours on LTO1 knockout A375 cells cocultured with 1G4 TCR-T cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios. Consistent with earlier results, LTO1 deficiency enhanced 1G4 TCR-T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumor cells (figure 5I). Taken together, these results suggest that LTO1 and YAE1 serve as potential targets to enhance T cell activity by increasing the expression of MHC-I.

Compromised NMD induced MHC-I upregulation and enhanced immune cell activation

Next, we asked whether MHC-I upregulation is triggered by impaired NMD, or instead a phenotype specific to LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1. We conducted CRISPR/Cas9-mediated SMG6 and SMG7 knockout in A375 cells. Interestingly, SMG6 and SMG7 knockouts exhibited increased MHC-I expression (figure 6A). These findings suggest that compromised NMD, or at least specific types of NMD, triggers MHC-I upregulation.

The knockout cells used in this study were constructed through 7 days of puromycin selection. We then sought to determine whether 2-day treatment of NMDi generated similar phenotypes (translation abnormality and MHC induction) as LTO1 deficiency-induced effects. First, we treated A375-reporter cells with known NMDi, 5-azacytidine, amlexanox, NMDI14 (SMG7-UPF1 inhibitor), and hSMG-1 inhibitor 11e.2 4 13 38 39 Iron chelators deferasirox (DFX) and deferoxamine (DFO) were also included, as Zhu et al demonstrated that transferrin-mediated iron uptake and iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster biogenesis are required for NMD.21 Dose-response curves showed significant upregulation of mCherry expression in a dose-dependent and time-dependent manner, confirming their inhibitory role in NMD using our cell model (figure 6B, online supplemental figure S13A,B). Their effects on GFP expression varied (figure 6C, online supplemental figure S13A). NMDI14, hSMG-1 inhibitor 11e, and iron chelators caused a significant increase in GFP expression, suggesting defective translation termination (figure 6C, online supplemental figure S13A).

We first measured the MHC-I expression using flow cytometry analysis in A375 cells treated with NMDi (figure 6D. Consistent with LTO1 knockouts, all NMDi induced significant MHC-I overexpression in A375 and A549 cells (figure 6D and online supplemental figure S14). We then assessed whether treatment with NMDi leads to abnormal translation. Indeed, all NMDi resulted in enhanced translational shutdown in A375 cells, measured by puromycin incorporation assay (online supplemental figure S15A). They also exacerbated the translational defect induced by poly(I:C) (online supplemental figure S15A). These results suggest that these NMDi successfully replicate the effects observed in cells with LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1 knockout, suggesting impaired NMD is directly associated with the upregulation of MHC-I. We further asked whether translation inhibition alone caused MHC-I overexpression. A375 cells were treated with poly(I:C), a drug known to inhibit translation.24 Interestingly, although poly (I:C) strongly inhibited translation (online supplemental figure S15B), it did not affect MHC-I expression (online supplemental figure S15C). This suggests that NMD defects, rather than translation inhibition alone, lead to MHC-I upregulation. Taken together, these data suggest that NMD defects induce MHC-I overexpression in tumor cells.

LTO1 knockout inhibited tumor formation in mice, preventing further in vivo testing (online supplemental figure S16). We then picked iron chelators for further investigation due to their favorable safety profile in humans, as a drug already in clinical use for the treatment of iron overload. Interestingly, we observed time-dependent overexpression of mCherry and GFP at lower concentrations of both iron chelators, DFX and DFO (figure 6E, online supplemental figure S13A,B). Compared with the concentrations of 100–200 µM, which are widely used in tumor inhibition, 5–30 µM iron inhibitors barely inhibit tumor growth. However, they generated the highest activity, and higher doses led to a reduction in these signals, indicating that iron inhibitors are more potent to inhibit NMD at lower doses (figure 6E, online supplemental figure S13A). The GFP expression resulting from iron chelator treatment seems to be more sensitive to dose, as it peaked at around 10 μM and dropped at higher doses (figure 6E). Coincident with the effects on NMD, as demonstrated by the reporter assay, low doses (10–30 µM), but not high doses of iron chelators (DFO and DFX) showed greater effectiveness in inducing MHC-I expression (figure 6E,F, online supplemental figure S13C). These data also indicate that MHC-I upregulation is correlated to impaired NMD, but not the defective translation termination activity.

We then examined whether low doses of iron chelators induce MHC-I overexpression in other tumor cell lines. In addition to A375, four human tumor cell lines (A549, 293T, Raji, and H292) and three mouse tumor cell lines (B16, Panc02, and CT26) all responded to 15 μM DFX, as measured by flow cytometry using antibodies recognizing human HLA-ABC, mouse H2-Kb, or mouse H2-Kd (figure 6G–I). Similarly, a low dose of DFO was found effective in A549, H292, Panc02, and CT26 cells (online supplemental figure S17).

Consistent with the reporter assay, RT-qPCR revealed an accumulation of NMD targets, ATF3, and GADD45B, in low doses of iron chelator-treated A375 cells, further confirming that iron deficiency induces NMD defects (figure 6J, online supplemental figure S18). RT-qPCR also confirmed increased MHC-I mRNA levels following DFX treatment (figure 6K). Similar to LTO1-deficient, YAE1-deficient and ABCE1-deficient cells, a low dose of DFX treatment activated key regulators of MHC signaling, including TAP1, NLRC5, and IRF1 (figure 6L). Additionally, FACS analysis demonstrated that DFX-induced HLA-ABC overexpression was further enhanced by IFN-γ supplementation in A375 cells (figure 6M). Iron chelator is also known to inhibit ferroptosis by reducing iron availability. To exclude the possibility that iron chelator enhances MHC-I expression through ferroptosis inhibition, we treated A375 cells with ferroptosis inhibitor, ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), which blocks lipid peroxidation. Notably, we found that MHC-I expression remained unaffected by Fer-1 (online supplemental figure S19). This suggests that iron-chelator-induced NMD defects rather than ferroptosis inhibition, play a key role in associated MHC-I upregulation. Taken together, these data indicate the potential of using low doses of iron chelators to induce impaired NMD-related MHC-I upregulation.

Low-dose iron chelator enhances immune cell recognition and activation

First, we aimed to determine whether low doses of iron chelator could enhance tumor cell recognition by T cells. To directly measure T cell activity, JK8NF-1G4 cells were cocultured with A375 cells for 48 hours, with or without DFX treatment. As expected, DFX induced MHC-I overexpression in A375 cells (figure 7A). Flow cytometry was used to measure the percentage of ZsGreen-positive cells (figure 7B). A low dose of DFX treatment significantly increased the number of ZsGreen+ cells after the tumor cell coculture, suggesting that DFX enhances T cell recognition and activation (figure 7B,C).

Next, we confirmed these findings using an in vitro mouse OT-I model. We used the mouse colorectal cell line MC38-OVA overexpressing GFP (MC38-OVA-GFP) as target cells and splenocytes from OT-I mice as effector cells. MC38-OVA-GFP cells were treated with 15 μM DFX for 24 hours before OT-I cells were added. As expected, DFX-treated MC38 cells exhibited significantly higher H2-Kb expression (figure 7D). Tumor cell killing efficiency was measured at 24 hours after DFX treatment in coculture with OT-I T cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios. Consistent with earlier results, DFX enhanced OT-I T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumor cells (figure 7E).

We then aimed to explore whether low doses of iron chelator could increase MHC-I expression in tumor cells in vivo and how it affects immune infiltration. MC38 colon cancer cells expressing ovalbumin (MC38-OVA) were transplanted into C57BL/6 mice subcutaneously. The dose of 50 mg/kg DFO is widely used for in vivo applications. We first compared 15 mg/kg with 50 mg/kg treatment in C57BL/6 mice. Consistent with our in vitro data, we found that low-dose induced higher H2-Kb expression (online supplemental figure S20). These tumor-bearing mice then received DFO (15 mg/kg) or vehicle control (phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) every 3 days for a total of five treatments (figure 7F). While low-dose DFO did not significantly inhibit tumor growth (figure 7G–I), flow cytometry analysis revealed increased H2-Kb expression in tumor cells (figure 7J,K). Furthermore, a higher number of CD45+ cells, CD8+ T cells, and OVA-specific T cells were detected within the tumor (figure 7L, online supplemental figure S21), although the ratio remained unchanged in the lymph nodes (online supplemental figure S22). These findings suggest that low doses of DFO hav the potential to enhance immune cell cytotoxicity, at least in part, by upregulating MHC-I expression.

We also investigated whether low-dose DFO could improve tumor inhibition when combined with ICB in vivo. MC38 cells were transplanted into C57BL/6 mice and treated with PBS, DFO, anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody, or a combination of DFO and anti-PD-1 (figure 7M). Similar to the previous findings with DFO treatment in vivo, while low-dose DFO alone moderately slowed tumor growth, the combination of DFO and ICB achieved the highest tumor inhibition (figure 7N). Collectively, these findings reveal that low-dose iron chelators mimic the phenotypes observed in LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1 knockouts, suggesting their potential to enhance immune therapy efficacy by increasing MHC-I expression.

Discussion

In this study, we manifest LTO1 and YAE1 are NMD factors regulating ribosome biogenesis in mammalian cells. Notably, we demonstrated that impaired NMD upregulates MHC-I expression. CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which are critical for eliminating tumor cells, depend on the effective sensing of tumor antigens presented by MHC-I.40,42 Tumors often downregulate the positive regulatory pathways of MHC-I and MHC-II molecules to evade host immune surveillance, thereby contributing to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors.40 41 In contrast, elevated MHC expression correlates with extended survival and enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy across the majority of cancer types.43 Our findings establish a novel connection between NMD and antitumor immunity through the regulation of MHC-I expression, positioning NMD as a therapeutic target to improve cancer immunotherapy.

NMD has been implicated in tumor progression by degrading tumor suppressor genes, impairing immunogenicity, and influencing the mutational landscape.13 44 45 Our findings suggest that NMD also affects tumorigenesis through the regulation of translation and MHC-I expression in tumor cells. Consistent with its protumor role in NMD, the LTO1 gene is located at chromosomal band 11q13, one of the most frequently amplified regions associated with human cancer, along with CCND1, FGF19, and 11 other genes.17 We analyzed TCGA data and found that LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1 expression is inversely correlated to HLA-related gene expression and immune cell infiltration across multiple cancer types. We observed increased expression of antigen presentation regulators, including TAP1, NLRC5, and IRF1, in cells with defective NMD. However, the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. Poly(I:C) induces translation arrest, but does not upregulate MHC-I, ruling out the possibility of translational shutdown as a cause. One direct factor could be that compromised NMD leads to the accumulation of specific NMD targets that regulate MHC-I. Another possibility could be ribosomal abnormality. Wei et al demonstrated that certain ribosomal proteins, such as RPS28, RPLP1, RPL7, and RPL3, inhibit MHC-I expression.46 Similarly, we found that the loss of LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1 downregulates RPL7 and RPL14, suggesting that ribosomal defects may drive MHC-I upregulation. The time-dependent pattern observed in the reporter assay suggests that the NMD abnormalities result in cumulative effects, likely reflecting a cellular stress response. Further investigation into stress response pathways could help elucidate the mechanism underlying the preferential regulation of MHC-I expression.

LTO1 partners with YAE1 via deca-GX3 motifs to function in the CIA machinery, which is responsible for the maturation of ABCE1.18,20 Our mutation analysis demonstrated that indeed the conserved deca-GX3 motifs are crucial for LTO1 function in NMD and MHC-I upregulation, suggesting that during these processes, LTO1 and YAE1 function as a complex, which also explains why LTO1, YAE1, and ABCE1 show similarities in transcriptome analysis and cellular functions. Our study identified that the loss of LTO1/YAE1 results in phenotypes similar to those previously described for loss of ABCE1, such as ribosomal defects and NMD inhibition,21 suggesting that LTO1/YAE1 influences NMD, at least partially, through ABCE1 regulation. However, polysome profiling of LTO1/YAE1-deficient cells reveals distinctions from ABCE1, as depletion of ABCE1 causes ribosome stalling at stop codons, as reported by Annibaldis et al, whereas LTO1/YAE1 deficiency leads to reduced 60S ribosomal subunit biogenesis and overall translation, indicating broader roles in translation beyond ABCE1 regulation.22 Further investigation using pull-down assays and mass spectrometry could reveal additional factors regulated by LTO1 and YAE1. Ribosome profiling could also help map NMD targets controlled by LTO1/YAE1/ABCE1.

Our data suggest that suppressing MHC-I expression might be one more reason tumors hijack NMD machinery to support tumor growth, making NMD a potential target for cancer treatment. We tested four known NMDi and focused on iron chelators in the in vivo studies due to their favorable safety profile in humans, as a drug already in clinical use for the treatment of iron overload. Iron promotes cellular proliferation through the regulation of many cellular processes including mitochondrial metabolism and DNA synthesis. Cancer cells reprogram iron metabolism to support their uncontrolled growth, which results in iron accumulation in tumors.47 Depleting iron has been shown to be selectively cytotoxic to cancer cells.48 DFO caused leukemic cytoreduction in a patient with acute leukemia and a 20% overall response rate in a study of 10 patients with metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma.49 Although iron chelation may have a broad impact on cellular processes, our results show that low-dose, but not high-dose, iron chelators mimic the phenotypes seen with impaired NMD. The dose curve of iron chelators correlated well between NMD defects and MHC upregulation in the reporter cells, indicating the link between the two phenomena. Zhu et al demonstrated the requirement of TFRC-mediated iron uptake in NMD and established iron chelators as NMDi.21 Similar to our finding, they also used a low dose of DFO (8 μM). In vivo, low doses of DFX and DFO produced optimal effects on MHC-I upregulation and increased tumor infiltration in mice. Previous studies have shown that iron chelators can increase the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α, complicating their use in cancer therapy.50 These studies typically apply concentrations of 100–200 µM of DFO or DFX. Woo et al reported that 25 μM minimally activates inducible nitric oxide synthase and cyclooxygenase-2.51 Low-dose iron chelators may present a drug repurposing strategy for enhancing the sensitivity and efficacy of ICB and TCR-T therapy.
