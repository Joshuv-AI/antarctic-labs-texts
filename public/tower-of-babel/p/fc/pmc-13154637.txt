Feasibility and Impact of Invasive Coronary Function Testing in Ischemia and No Obstructive Coronary Arteries: A Single-Center Study
Authors: Deeksha Acharya, Yulith Roca Alvarez, Bruce Samuels, Janet Wei, Ana Iribarren, Galen Cook-Wiens, Paulina Skaff, Sachini Ranasinghe, Jenna Maughan, Mamoo Nakamura, Tarun Chakravarty, Aakriti Gupta, Babak Azarbal, C. Noel Bairey Merz, Martha Gulati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with suspected ischemia and no obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), coronary function testing (CFT) helps identify underlying endotypes. Despite guideline support, adoption of CFT remains limited. This study assessed the feasibility of CFT and its impact on medical management.MethodsThis single-center, prospective study (2020-2025) evaluated CFT implementation and subsequent medication changes in patients with clinically indicated CFT. Interventional cardiologists completed postprocedure surveys rating CFT feasibility, preparation, lab support, and diagnostic value. CFT results were categorized into coronary microvascular disease (CMD), (coronary flow reserve <2.5 in response to adenosine and/or endothelial dysfunction indicated by abnormal epicardial coronary response to acetylcholine without evidence of ischemia); vasospastic angina (VA)—ischemic ECG changes with or without angina in response to acetylcholine together with either: (1) epicardial VA (>75% epicardial constriction), or (2) microvascular VA (<75% epicardial constriction); mixed disease (MD) (CMD and VA), and no vasomotor diagnosis (no CMD or VA). Medication use was compared at baseline and 30 days after CFT.ResultsFive of the 10 interventional cardiologists completed 71 surveys. Overall, 98% rated preparation, training, procedural ease, and diagnostic value as “above average” and would recommend CFT to peers. Mean procedural time was 19 minutes, 7 seconds. There were 10 (14%) adverse events, of which 90% were self-limited. Among 71 patients, 60.6% had CMD, 26.8% VA, and 22.5% MD. CMD patients showed increased angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker use (39.5% to 55.8%, P = .008) and statin use (53.5% to 65.1%, P = .02). VA patients had increased calcium channel blocker use (68.4% to 89.5%, P = .10). MD and no vasomotor diagnosis had no significant medication changes.ConclusionsCFT was rated highly for feasibility, safety, and clinical value by the interventional cardiologists. CFT appeared to impact the medication management of INOCA endotypes.

Introduction

Ischemia and no obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) is an increasingly recognized clinical entity, particularly among women undergoing invasive coronary angiography for angina.1 INOCA is estimated to affect more than 3 to 4 million people in the United States.1 Patients with INOCA experience a significant symptom burden, often undergoing repeated testing and hospitalizations, leading to worse quality of life and substantial health care financial burden.2, 3, 4 Furthermore, women with suspected INOCA who demonstrate abnormal coronary microvascular function are at increased risk for major adverse cardiovascular events, as shown in the Women's Ischemia Syndrome Evaluation (WISE) study.5

Evaluating coronary vasomotor function with coronary function testing (CFT) can be useful to guide medication therapy. CFT is used for a comprehensive assessment of INOCA underlying pathways, including coronary microvascular dysfunction, endothelial dysfunction, and epicardial and microvascular spasm.6 The CORonary MICrovascular Angina (CorMicA) trial demonstrated that invasive diagnosis of INOCA endotypes linked with targeted medical therapy can significantly improve angina and quality of life compared to standard care.7 Consequently, the 2021 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines for the evaluation and diagnosis of chest pain offered a class IIb recommendation for CFT and guide wire–based assessment of coronary flow reserve (CFR) and acetylcholine (ACh) provocation testing.8 Similarly, the 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for chronic coronary syndromes gave a class IB recommendation for guide wire–based assessment of CFR and performing ACh provocation testing for coronary vasomotor disorders, in patients with persistent symptoms and angiographically normal coronaries.9

Despite current evidence supporting CFT for the diagnosis and management of coronary vasomotor disorders, it is not widely used among interventionalists in clinical practice to date. This is likely because of limited training in performing CFT, concerns about adding time to the procedure, perceived risks, and potential technical challenges. We aimed to assess the adoption, feasibility, and streamlining of CFT in a high-volume catheterization lab and its impact on the medical management of INOCA patients at 30-day follow-up.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, prospective observational study was conducted at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (CSMC) between 2020 and 2025. The study was conducted following approval from the CSMC institutional review board. Given that CFT included only data collection and did not require interaction or interventions with participants, a waiver of informed consent was granted. The study evaluated 2 components of CFT in clinical practice. The first aim was focused on determining the ease of performing CFT by surveying the interventional cardiologists (Appendix 1). The second aim was to assess the impact of CFT on the medication management of patients with INOCA. All interventional cardiologists were provided with a standardized protocol to perform CFT (Appendix 2). For aim 1, the interventionalists completed questionnaire surveys after the procedure was performed. For aim 2, the results of the CFT were classified into 4 endotype categories, and medications were reviewed prior to the CFT and then 1 month after to assess for any changes (Appendix 3).

Study population

The study population included interventional cardiologists who performed clinically indicated CFT at CSMC for patients with suspected ischemia in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease, defined as <50% stenosis on coronary angiography.

INOCA pathway classification

The CFT results were classified into 1 of 4 endotype categories: based on CFR and ACh testing including coronary microvascular disease (CMD) (CFR <2.5 in response to adenosine and/or endothelial dysfunction indicated by abnormal epicardial coronary response to ACh without evidence of ischemia); vasospastic angina (VA)—ischemic ECG changes and/or angina in response to ACh together with either (1) epicardial VA (>75% epicardial constriction) or (2) microvascular VA (<75% epicardial constriction); mixed disease (MD) (CMD and VA), and no vasomotor diagnosis (ND) (no CMD or VA) (Supplemental Methods S2). This classification was informed by internal data review and exploratory analyses of our cohort, given that the presence of vasospasm (epicardial or microvascular) may be identified by objective findings (angiographic or ECG) even in the absence of symptoms during CFT10 and following existing trials such as CorMicA.7

Medical therapy

Medications were recorded before and at 30 days follow-up after CFT. The medications were grouped into 5 drug classes: calcium channel blockers (CCB), beta-blockers (BB), angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-I)/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), statins, and nitrates. This was based on expert consensus and state-of-the-art reviews that emphasize that, after endotype identification, the first-line pharmacological options are chosen based on the predominant underlying pathway: BB or CCB for microvascular dysfunction and CCB for VA.6

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages. Medications were obtained from medical records at baseline, prior to CFT, and at 30-day follow-up during a cardiologist visit. The comparison of pre- and post-CFT medication use was performed using McNemar’s test for paired categorical variables. Tests between sexes were χ2 tests or t tests. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all tests.

Results

Interventionalist survey

Overall, 5 of 10 interventional cardiologists (4/5 men) performed CFT. A total of 71 CFT and surveys were completed from 2020 to 2025. The majority (3/5) of CFT were performed by interventional cardiologists aged 55 to 65 years, 2of 5 had over 20 years of experience, and the remaining 3 of 5 had 0 to 3, 4 to 9, and 10 to 19 years of experience, respectively, in interventional practice.

The CFT survey results demonstrated that 98% rated an “above average” experience in the preparation/training of the procedure, ease of procedure, support from cath lab staff during procedure, and value of the procedure for diagnosis and treatment (Supplemental Table S1). After each CFT, 80% (4/5) interventional cardiologists would recommend performing the CFT to other physicians. Overall, there were 10 of 71 (14%) adverse events in response to ACh, of which all but 1 were brief, self-limited, and did not require any intervention. These events included transient second-degree AV block (2/10), third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block (3/10), bradycardia (2/10), nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (1/10), and sinus pause (1/10). One patient experienced atrial fibrillation (1/10) that was medically cardioverted with intravenous amiodarone and metoprolol. Fifty percent of interventionalists surveyed reported adoption of CFT for management of patients with suspected INOCA, and all rated the procedure highly in terms of feasibility, safety, and clinical value. The average CFT time was 19 minutes 7 seconds, and the majority of interventionalists expressed a high likelihood of recommending CFT to peers for managing INOCA.

Patient characteristics and CFT endotypes

The mean age of the cohort was 56.6 ± 13.6 years; the majority were women (71.8%, n = 51) and of White race (76%, n = 54). CFT endotypes included 43 (60.6%) patients with CMD, 19 (26.8%) with VA, 16 (22.5%) had MD, and 8 (11.2%) had ND. In the CMD group, 42 (98%) had endothelial dysfunction, 24 (56%) had CFR <2.5, and 14 (32%) had evidence of both endothelial dysfunction and CFR <2.5. Among the patients with VA, 3 (16%) had evidence of epicardial coronary vasospasm only, and 16 (84%) had evidence of MD (Figure 1). Medication changes after CFT diagnosis

Changes in medication use were observed at the 30-day follow-up across all classes of medications except for BB, with drug use differing based on the underlying pathway (Table 1). Patients with CMD had a significantly increased use of ACE-I/ARB, with baseline use of 39.5% increasing to 55.8% at 30 days (P = .008), and statin use increasing from 53.5% to 65.1% at 30 days post-CFT (P = .02) (Figure 2). Similarly, CCB use increased among this group from 53.5% to 72.1% (P = .02). Among patients with VA (n = 19), there was no significant change in the use of CCB from baseline to 30 days after CFT (68.4% vs 89.5%; P = .10). Similarly, there were no statistically significant increases in the use of ACE-I/ARB, statins, or nitrates, despite numerical increases. BB use showed a nonsignificant decrease at 30 days post-CFT. Similar trends were observed in the MD cohort, which also did not reach statistical significance. In the ND patients, no medication changes were observed from baseline to follow-up for BB, statins, and ACE-I/ARB. Although the use of CCB increased from 62.50% to 100% and nitrate use increased from 37.5% to 62.5%, it was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study supported that CFT in patients with suspected INOCA was feasible, safe, and provided a high clinical value in patient management. CFT was highly rated for procedural ease, and 4 out of 5 interventional cardiologists expressed that they would recommend CFT to peers.

The CFT endotypes were related to medication change. Precision-based treatments based on INOCA endotypes diagnosed with CFT have the potential to improve angina and result in an improvement in the quality of life.7 But despite this, CFT is infrequently performed.11,12 Data from the Coronary Microvascular Disease Registry at the MedStar Health Research Institute reported a median time of approximately 6 months from the onset of pain symptoms to CMD diagnosis.13 Others report even longer durations from the onset of symptoms to diagnosis.4 In the Coronary Microvascular Disease Registry, it was reported that one-fourth of the patients had undergone a prior left heart catheterization without CFT, demonstrating the challenges and missed opportunities to arrive at an accurate diagnosis in a timely manner. The current study demonstrates interventional cardiologists' positive experiences with a procedure not commonly incorporated into clinical practice despite guideline recommendations.14

Like the CorMicA trial,8 our findings related to CFT endotype categorization facilitated tailored medication changes. At the 30-day follow-up, a detailed medication review highlighted an overall significantly increased use of CCB, ACE-I/ARB, statins, and nitrates, reflecting adjustments guided by the specific INOCA endotype diagnosis. For those with CMD, the overall use of ACE-I/ARB particularly increased in our study. The renin-angiotensin system inhibition has been shown to play a key role in CMD management,15,16 evidenced by a previous WISE ancillary trial of quinapril vs placebo, which demonstrated a significant improvement in both low CFR and patients’ anginal symptoms at 16 weeks of treatment.17 Although the medication changes were modest, this only represents changes made within 30 days of the CFT. It is likely that over time, other changes might be made in a stepwise fashion, after tolerance and symptom improvement is assessed.

In this study cohort, the overall use of statins increased from baseline to post-CFT, particularly in the CMD endotype. Several observational studies and randomized clinical trials have evaluated the effects of statins, which have shown to improve endothelial function and nonendothelial pathways, and exercise tolerance in patients with INOCA, likely by its effects on the microvasculature with inhibition of vascular inflammation, upregulation of endothelial nitric oxide (NO) synthase, and enhancing vascular NO availability.18, 19, 20, 21 Most recently, the WARRIOR trial was designed to compare intensive medical therapy vs standard of care in women with suspected INOCA.22 The beneficial role of statins in CMD may be related to their pleotropic effects on the molecular pathways involved in inflammation and oxidative stress. Statins have been demonstrated to reduce nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase activity and promote endothelial NO synthase activity directly.23

We also observed an overall increase in CCB use, which was significant in the CMD group. These findings align with the 2023 ChaMP-CMD trial, which reported improved anginal symptoms and exercise tolerance in patients with CFR < 2.5 treated with amlodipine and ranolazine.24 In the VA group, statin use was greater than 50% at baseline and increased to 70% following CFT. A clinical trial in Japan reported lower cardiovascular event rates among patients with VA and no obstructive coronary arteries who were treated with statins.25 This effect may be attributed to underlying mixed vasomotor disorders—consistent with our cohort, in which many patients exhibited both endothelial dysfunction and reduced CFR. Statins can often pose a therapeutic dilemma among clinicians when treating patients with nonobstructive disease and not traditional cardiovascular risk factors, indicating statin therapy. We observed a trend to decrease BB use following the diagnosis of VA, consistent with the established role of CCB as first-line therapy for VA26 and the recommendations to avoid BB, given the risk of exacerbating coronary vasospasm.27 In the ND, no medication changes were observed from baseline to follow-up except for CCB and nitrate use; these medications were used to treat other conditions, such as hypertension.

In patients with suspected INOCA, CFT has been demonstrated to provide diagnostic utility of coronary vasomotor disorders in over 80% of cases.28 In our study, 88% of patients had an underlying endotype identified. Living with suspected INOCA has been shown to have an adverse impact on patients' quality of life,4 is associated with distress because of the lack of definitive diagnosis, and with an increase in major cardiovascular events. We did not collect data on changes in quality of life after the medication changes.29,30

Interventionalists reported the protocol as easy to implement and highly recommendable to peers, which aligns with existing literature supporting a tailored approach to CFT.31,32 This study implements the guideline-directed protocol supported by the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology, European Society of Cardiology, and the COVADIS group. Our study demonstrated the practicality of the procedure when applied in a streamlined manner. The lack of uptake of the use of CFT may be because of concerns of time for the procedure. Certainly, the CorMicA trial reported an average of 60 minutes for the CFT procedure,7 but in our single-center study, we have demonstrated that the average procedural time for the CFT was under 20 minutes, highlighting that CFT offers a time-efficient option to arrive at an accurate diagnosis for patients with INOCA. Admittedly, time is likely reduced in our study because of the built experience of this group, but it is an example for other catheterization laboratories, which have experience with far more complicated procedures, with the duration decreasing with experience.

Adverse events were infrequent, occurring in 14% cases, with all but one being self-limited, likely attributable to the very short duration of action ACh.12,33 There were no serious adverse events (death, VT/VF, shock, MI). Limitations

This single-center study is limited by its relatively small sample size and short-term follow-up. The COVID-19 pandemic, coupled with limited awareness among insurance providers, created significant challenges and frequent denials for a clinically indicated procedure, which led to overall slow enrollment. Additionally, supply chain disruptions occurred, resulting in a national shortage of ACh, limiting the ability to perform CFT from 2023 to 2024.

This is an experienced center that has been performing CFT for >2 decades; therefore, this may not represent the time needed by operators new to the procedure. Nonetheless, it is reassuring that with experience, the time for a CFT is significantly shorter than what has been reported in other studies. Assessment of quality of life and angina symptoms was not performed in this study, limiting the extension of the interpretation of the medication changes.

Future directions

Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are needed to extend the impact of CFT on patient outcomes and refine training protocols in clinical practice. The favorable feedback from interventional cardiologists suggests that with proper training and support, CFT can and should become a standard diagnostic tool in cardiac catheterization labs.

Conclusion

This study supports the use of CFT by a vast majority of interventional cardiologists who reported the procedure as feasible, well-supported, and rated highly for preparation, execution, safety, and value. CFT appeared to impact the medication management of INOCA endotypes. These findings underscore the potential utility of invasive assessment in patients with suspected INOCA to better inform and guide their medical treatment.
