Association of Body Mass Index with 28-Day Mortality in Adult Intensive Care Patients with Sepsis: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Olcay Dilken, Mustafa Altinay, Batuhan Bostan, Mehmet Nuri Yakar, Betul Duran, Feyzullah Orkut Duran, Sevde Sumeyye Iraz Soyalp, Merve Ipekoglu, Ziya Eren Cayir, Ayse Surhan Cinar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe effect of obesity on sepsis outcomes remains controversial. Several studies have described an “obesity paradox,” whereby a higher body mass index (BMI) correlates with improved survival in critically ill patients. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between BMI and 28-day mortality in adult patients with sepsis admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study including 83 adult patients diagnosed with sepsis according to the Sepsis-3 criteria who were admitted to the ICUs of Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital between June 2023 and June 2025. BMI was reported using World Health Organization categories and analyzed using three BMI groups based on the final cohort distribution. The primary endpoint was 28-day mortality. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted with adjustment for age, sex, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, creatinine, and C-reactive protein.ResultsThe median age was 75 years (interquartile range, 66–82), and 55% of the patients were male. Survivors had a higher BMI than non-survivors (27.3 vs. 24.9 kg/m2; p=0.004) and higher albumin levels (p=0.024). BMI was inversely associated with 28-day mortality in the multivariable analysis (odds ratio, 0.74 per 1 kg/m2; 95% confidence interval, 0.58–0.91; p=0.008).ConclusionHigher BMI was associated with lower 28-day mortality in septic ICU patients. Further prospective studies are needed to validate this relationship and explore the underlying potential mechanisms.

Sepsis is a potentially fatal clinical syndrome defined by a dysregulated host response to infection that results in organ failure and high mortality.[1] It continues to be one of the main causes of death among critically ill patients worldwide. In addition, it represents a significant source of morbidity and mortality in individuals admitted to intensive care units (ICUs).

Obesity can be assessed using body mass index (BMI) and is recognized as an important risk factor for chronic illnesses, including cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, and malignancy. However, several studies have paradoxically reported lower short-term mortality in overweight or obese patients with acute and critical illnesses, including sepsis. This phenomenon has been described as the “obesity paradox.” A meta-analysis reported lower mortality among overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2) and obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) adults with sepsis compared with patients with normal BMI.[2] Similarly, other systematic reviews have suggested increased mortality among underweight patients and reduced mortality among overweight/obese individuals.[3] In contrast, some meta-analyses have suggested that the reduced mortality may be limited to the overweight range, while the correlation is weaker or insignificant in obese and morbidly obese patients, and that there may be a possible U-shaped or J-shaped relationship between BMI and mortality.[4]

Importantly, BMI has inherent limitations in critically ill populations. BMI does not distinguish between fat mass and lean body mass and may be strongly confounded by frailty, sarcopenia, and malnutrition—factors that are particularly prevalent in elderly ICU patients and independently associated with sepsis-related mortality. Real-world ICU studies have demonstrated heterogeneous and potentially nonlinear associations between BMI and short-term outcomes in septic patients.[5] Furthermore, acute changes in body weight due to fluid resuscitation and capillary leak can further compromise the accuracy of BMI assessment in sepsis cases. These limitations may partially explain the heterogeneity of findings among studies and contribute to the apparent paradoxical relationships.

Population-related differences may also affect the observed relationship between BMI and sepsis outcomes. Studies including Asian cohorts have suggested that the obesity paradox may be less pronounced due to differences in BMI distribution and body composition, with more consistent associations between low BMI and higher mortality and less consistent evidence for a protective effect of higher BMI.[6] Moreover, a systematic review of animal models highlighted that obesity does not have a consistent protective effect on sepsis outcomes and that there is significant variability depending on the type and conditions of the experimental model.[7]

Given the abovementioned conflicting results and methodological limitations, further clinical research is needed to clarify the correlation between BMI and sepsis outcomes, especially in high-risk groups such as elderly ICU patients. Therefore, this study aimed to contribute to the literature by addressing inconsistencies and heterogeneity in previously reported findings by evaluating the relationship between BMI and 28-day mortality in adult ICU patients with sepsis using both categorical and continuous BMI approaches.

Methods

This retrospective, descriptive, and analytical observational study was carried out in the adult intensive care units (ICUs) of Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: 5098; Date: September 30, 2025). All procedures included in the study were performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The medical records of patients admitted between June 1, 2023, and June 30, 2025, were retrospectively reviewed. Individuals aged 18 years and older who were hospitalized with a diagnosis of sepsis during this period were screened for eligibility.

The diagnosis of sepsis was established according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3), published in 2016. Accordingly, patients presenting with suspected infection accompanied by an increase of ≥2 points in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score were defined as having sepsis. Suspicion of infection was supported by clinical examination findings, laboratory results, microbiological cultures, and radiological imaging. Organ dysfunction was determined based on acute deterioration in any of the SOFA components, including respiratory, hematologic, hepatic, cardiovascular, neurological, and renal parameters. Septic shock was defined as the need for vasopressor therapy to maintain adequate blood pressure despite fluid resuscitation, accompanied by a serum lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L.

Patients younger than 18 years, pregnant women, individuals with a history of organ transplantation, those with immunodeficiency syndromes or severe chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression, and patients readmitted to the ICU (second or subsequent admissions) were excluded. Patients with missing BMI data or incomplete clinical or laboratory information related to the diagnosis of sepsis were also excluded. Specifically, patients with missing height and/or weight values within the first 24 hours of ICU admission were excluded from the final analytical cohort. Additionally, patients admitted only for palliative care in the terminal phase, for whom active sepsis management was not performed, were not included in the analysis.

Data were obtained from patients’ electronic medical records and ICU follow-up forms. The following information was recorded: demographic characteristics (age, sex), hospital and ICU admission dates, admission diagnosis, body weight, height, and calculated body mass index (BMI). BMI was calculated using the height and weight documented within the first 24 hours of ICU admission. Patients lacking height and/or weight data were excluded. To minimize the impact of fluid overload, the most reliable weight value recorded in the medical charts was used (e.g., pre-ICU baseline weight when explicitly documented as “dry weight” in clinical notes). In addition, net fluid balance was extracted from ICU intake/output charts when available and used for sensitivity analyses.

In addition, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, chronic liver disease, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, malignancy, and cardiovascular disease), vital signs at ICU admission, laboratory findings (complete blood count, biochemical parameters, lactate, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin), microbiological culture results, and radiological findings related to the site of infection were collected. Organ dysfunction was evaluated using SOFA and APACHE II scores. Treatment-related variables, such as vasopressor therapy, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and lengths of ICU and hospital stay, were also recorded.

Body Mass Index (BMI) classification

BMI was calculated by dividing body weight in kg by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). For descriptive purposes, BMI values were classified based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria into the following categories: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m2).

For comparative analyses within the final analytical cohort (n=83), BMI was additionally categorized into three groups based on the distribution of BMI values to ensure an adequate sample size per category: low BMI (<22.5 kg/m2), intermediate BMI (22.5–27.5 kg/m2), and high BMI (≥27.5 kg/m2). All patients with BMI ≥30.0 kg/m2 were included in the high BMI group.

The primary outcome measure was 28-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay, total hospital length of stay, requirement for mechanical ventilation, use of vasopressor therapy, and application of renal replacement therapy.

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables demonstrating a normal distribution were reported as mean±standard deviation, whereas those not normally distributed were expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical data were summarized as numbers and percentages (%). Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

The main outcome was 28-day mortality. To evaluate the relationship between BMI and 28-day mortality, BMI was analyzed both as (i) a continuous variable (odds ratios [ORs] reported per 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI) and (ii) a categorical variable based on the predefined BMI groups described in the Methods section. Univariate logistic regression analyses were initially performed. Variables with clinical relevance and/or statistical significance in univariate analyses were considered for inclusion in multivariable logistic regression models. Collinearity among covariates was evaluated, and highly collinear variables were not simultaneously included in the same model.

Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. In addition, to explore potential nonlinear associations between BMI and mortality, restricted cubic spline regression was performed with BMI as a continuous predictor and adjustment for age, sex, and SOFA score.

For secondary outcomes, continuous variables were analyzed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric methods according to distribution characteristics, whereas binary outcomes were evaluated using logistic regression models. A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

A total of 83 adult patients diagnosed with sepsis and followed up in the intensive care units of Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital between June 1, 2023, and June 30, 2025, were included in the study. The median age was 75 years (IQR, 66–82), the median SOFA score was 9 (IQR, 6–11), and the median APACHE II score was 21 (IQR, 17–26).

The distribution of BMI in the final analytical cohort showed that 19% of the patients were in the lowest BMI group (BMI <22.5 kg/m2), 55% were in the intermediate BMI group (BMI 22.5–27.5 kg/m2), and 26% were in the highest BMI group (BMI ≥27.5 kg/m2). Baseline demographic characteristics, severity scores, and laboratory parameters were largely comparable across BMI groups (Table 1). Infection sources and ICU supportive therapies were also similar among BMI groups (Table 2). However, 28-day mortality differed significantly across BMI categories (p=0.021), with the highest survival proportion observed in the highest BMI group (Table 3). In addition, the prevalence of diabetes tended to be higher in the highest BMI group, although this did not reach statistical significance (p=0.057) (Table 1).

Discussion

The findings of this retrospective analysis—demonstrating an inverse relationship between body mass index (BMI) and 28-day mortality among adult ICU patients diagnosed with sepsis—align with the increasingly recognized concept of the “obesity paradox.” In our study, mortality differed across BMI groups, and higher BMI was inversely associated with 28-day mortality in the multivariable analysis. Despite the limited final analytical cohort size, our findings add to the existing literature by focusing on an elderly, critically ill ICU population and by evaluating BMI as both a categorical and continuous variable.

Recent evidence has generally suggested lower short-term mortality among overweight and obese patients with sepsis compared with normal BMI categories. Meta-analyses have reported reduced short-term mortality in overweight/obese groups, although substantial heterogeneity exists across studies and the magnitude of the effect may vary according to population characteristics and BMI distribution.[2,3] Importantly, population-related differences may influence the observed relationship. For example, data from Japanese cohorts have shown a strong association between low BMI and increased 28-day mortality, while the protective effect of higher BMI appears less consistent in Asian populations.[6] Furthermore, evidence from experimental animal models has not consistently demonstrated a protective effect of obesity on sepsis outcomes, suggesting that the observed “paradox” may reflect complex host–pathogen interactions and model-specific variability.[7]

Several analyses indicate that the BMI–mortality relationship may attenuate after adjustment for baseline risk and clinical confounders and may differ across clinically relevant subgroups.[2–4] In particular, evidence suggests that the obesity paradox may not persist in morbidly obese patients and may vary in septic patients with concurrent malignancy, supporting the possibility of a non-uniform effect across BMI ranges and patient phenotypes (e.g., cancer-associated sepsis).[9] These inconsistencies suggest that the observed relationship may depend on the burden of comorbidity, disease severity, and residual confounding factors.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the obesity–sepsis relationship. One hypothesis suggests that larger metabolic reserves may confer a survival advantage during catabolic stress, while another proposes that chronic low-grade inflammation and altered adipokine signaling may modulate the acute inflammatory response. Importantly, in older adults, BMI may also reflect frailty, sarcopenia, and malnutrition rather than adiposity. In a large critical-care database analysis, Xu et al.[4] reported that the sepsis–obesity paradox may be more pronounced in elderly individuals and may vary according to immune-inflammatory status, indicating that host phenotype modifies the BMI–outcome association. Given that the median age of our study group was 75 years, such mechanisms may partly explain our findings; nevertheless, part of the observed association may reflect a “frailty/malnutrition paradox” rather than a direct protective effect of adiposity.

Notably, increasing evidence suggests nonlinearity in the BMI–mortality relationship, particularly at extreme BMI values. Underweight status has consistently been associated with increased mortality in sepsis, and contemporary analyses demonstrate L-shaped or threshold-type relationships in which mortality decreases up to a certain BMI and then plateaus.[10,11] Among chronically critically ill septic patients, Xu et al.[12] reported an association between BMI categories and 90-day mortality, supporting the possibility that the BMI–outcome relationship may differ depending on the clinical trajectory beyond the acute phase. In addition, long-term outcomes after sepsis may differ from early mortality outcomes, highlighting the need to interpret BMI effects within a broader survivorship framework.[8]

From a clinical perspective, BMI may provide additional prognostic information in septic ICU patients; however, BMI alone should not be interpreted as a direct marker of protection. Given pharmacokinetic differences and the potential for altered drug distribution in obese patients, individualized management remains important. Critically, our findings should be interpreted as an observational association rather than evidence of causality, and further multicenter prospective studies incorporating frailty indices and body composition measures are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Study Limitations

Due to its retrospective nature, this study has limitations, including an inherent risk of incomplete or missing data. Additionally, recorded body weights may not always reflect patients’ true “dry weight,” as factors such as fluid overload or edema could introduce bias in BMI calculations. Furthermore, BMI is a simple ratio of weight to height and does not account for body composition (e.g., fat versus muscle mass) or fat distribution, which are physiologically relevant variables that may influence outcomes more accurately than BMI alone. As this was a single-center study conducted within a specific ICU protocol framework, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Moreover, the study focused solely on 28-day mortality as the primary outcome; thus, long-term outcomes and post-discharge functional status were not evaluated. The sample size imbalance between BMI categories and the absence of certain clinical variables (e.g., detailed fluid management or nutritional status) may also have limited the statistical power of the analyses. Finally, important potential confounders, such as frailty indices, objective measures of sarcopenia/body composition, comorbidity burden scores, timing of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and detailed ventilatory parameters, were not available in this retrospective dataset and could not be adjusted for, leaving the possibility of residual confounding.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated an inverse association between BMI and 28-day mortality in adult septic ICU patients. This observational association should not be interpreted as causal, and further multicenter prospective studies are needed to elucidate potential mechanisms and residual confounding.

Moreover, BMI should not be evaluated in isolation; incorporating assessments of muscle mass, metabolic profile, and inflammatory status may enhance the understanding of underlying patient physiology and facilitate the development of more individualized approaches to sepsis management.
