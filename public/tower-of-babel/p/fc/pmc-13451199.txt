Six-month outcomes in a French cohort of patients receiving a safety planning type intervention after suicide attempt
Authors: Sibylle Mauries, Anne Perozziello, Feriel Zehani, Séverine Yung, Ariane Lengereau, Rodolphe Borand, Laurine Carnandet, Estelle Taupinard, Geoffrey Dufayet, Guillaume Pineau, Michel Lejoyeux, Pierre A Geoffroy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) is an intervention validated by the Stanley team, as a strategy for preventing suicidal recurrence. It allows the patient to develop a personalized emergency plan that can be used in the event of a suicidal crisis In a previous study, it was shown that, in routine post-emergency clinical settings, that the SPI was well accepted by patients and feasible for healthcare professionals. Nevertheless, limited data exist regarding the long-term follow-up. The study aimed to assess the factors associated with the likelihood of recurrence of a suicide attempt at 6 months among patients who received SPI, as well as the usefulness of this brief intervention. This was alongitudinal cohort study with a six-month follow-up, involving patients hospitalized at Bichat Hospital (Paris, France) for a suicide attempt between June 2022 and December 2023, who received a SPI, aged over 18 years, agreeing to participate in the study. Self-questionnaires were conducted to assess mood and the presence of substance use disorders. A descriptive analysis of the data was performed, factors associated with suicide recurrence was based on a dichotomized variable, and a logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with recurrence. One month after inclusion, 85.4% of the patients still participating in the study remembered the SPI, and 68.8% still had their personalized SPI in their possession At six months, among the 40 patients contacted, 25.0% of of those still being followed had made another suicide attempt, and 70.0% of them had sought care at the emergency department. No factor was associated with the usefulness of the SPI. Regarding the probability of recurrence of a suicide attempt at 6 months, it appeared to decrease with increasing age (OR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.84 ; 0.99], p-value = 0.035). Being a student (OR = 5.25 95% CI [1.02 ; 26.98], p-value = 0.047)and a history of suicide attempts (OR = 9.00 95%CI [1.00 ; 80.8], p = 0.050) increased the odds of suicide reattempt at 6 months. In the multivariate analysis, a history of suicide attempt was the only factor independently associated with reattempt at 6 months (OR = 26.6 95%CI [1.28 ; 551.87], p-value = 0.034). This study supports potential importance of brief interventions in managing suicidal crises and preventing recurrence. This work makes it possible to propose new hypotheses regarding the importance of regularly revisiting suicide crisis prevention strategies throughout follow-up. Introduction

Death by suicide affects around 800,000 people globally each year, with an estimated death every 40 s1. In the United States, about one million suicide attempts occur annually2. In France, the annual suicide mortality rate is 13.07 per 100,000 inhabitants, which is higher than the European average3.

Among the risk factors for suicide are psychiatric disorders (depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, substance use disorders, etc.)4, gender, age, traumatic experience, and a family history of suicide. However, the primary risk factor is a previous suicide attempt5,6 with an increased risk during the 6 months following the attempt7. Thus, preventing further suicidal acts involves addressing these comorbidities, such as depression, which has long been a primary focus of treatment8. However, suicidal thoughts require specific management9. The Safety Planning-Type Intervention (SPI), developed by Stanley’s group10,11, is one of these interventions. The SPI helps patients create a personalized safety plan to manage crises and reduce the risk of future self-harm. This plan includes strategies to limit access to means of suicide and alternative ways to handle suicidal thoughts10. This brief intervention was initially developed for specific populations, such as U.S. veterans, showing a 45% reduction in suicidal behaviors12. Subsequent studies conducted by Stanley’s team demonstrated the effectiveness of SPI in the general population13,14. However, few studies exist in Europe, and no cohort data are available in France15.

Thus, in 2020, we conducted an initial study to evaluate the feasibility of implementing SPI for patients at risk of suicide presenting to the emergency department after a suicide attempt. However, our study revealed that implementing SPI in this setting was neither appropriate, feasible, nor acceptable16. Consequently, in 2021, we conducted a second study aimed at assessing the feasibility of implementing this intervention in a short-term post-emergency psychiatry unit. This unit provides the management of suicidal crises in patients in the immediate aftermath of emergency care.The results showed high rates of patient adherence and satisfaction (85.1%), with 81.1% of patients believing that this intervention could be useful in the future. Professionals also found the intervention feasible in 80.5% of cases17.

In this context, we wanted to assess whether this SPI intervention had sustained long-term effects. The primary objective was to assess 6-month outcomes of patients receiving SPI on recurrent suicide attempts. The secondary objectives were to assess patients’ perceived usefulness of this brief intervention, and to identify the clinical factors that may influence adherence to the SPI and factors associated with recurrence of a suicide attempt.

Methods

Population

This cohort study was conducted at Paris Bichat University Hospital, within the psychiatric department of Assistance Publique – Hôpitaux de Paris (AP-HP) and GHU Paris Psychiatrie Neurosciences. The study was offered to all hospitalized patients in the post-emergency short-term psychiatric unit (24 to 72 h hospitalization) after visiting the emergency room for a suicide attempt. The participants were identified by medical staff of the unit. The exclusion criteria included: “a) individuals under 18 years of age, “b) individuals unable to be informed about or comprehend the study (due to language barriers or their medical condition), and “c) individuals who objected to the collection of data for research purposes. A sample of 87 participants was expected at inclusion. Figure 1.

Study design

This is a 6-month longitudinal cohort study (PLUS cohort), patients were recruited between June 2022 and December 2023. At the time of inclusion, clinical characteristics (anxiety, depression, and the presence of substance use disorders) were assessed using self-administered questionnaires. The French validated version of the Fagerström Test, a 6-item self-administered questionnaire, was used to assess tobacco use, with a score ≥ 8 defining high dependence18. The French validated version of the Cannabis Abuse Screening Test (CAST), a 6-item self-administered questionnaire, was used to assess cannabis use disorder, with a score ≥ 7 defining a high risk of dependence19. The French validated version of the CAGE, “Decrease, Entourage, Too much, Alcohol” (DETA questionnaire), a 4-item self-administered questionnaire, was used to assess alcohol use disorder, with a score ≥ 2 defining a risk of dependence20. Finally, the French validated version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HAD), a 7-item self-administered questionnaire, with two sub-scale (anxiety (HAD-A) and depression (HAD-D))was used to assess anxiety and depressive symptoms, with a score ≥ 11 indicating a probable anxiety or depressive disorder21.

Clinical and socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, housing situation, educational level, and occupationn), medical history (emergency unit visit for more recent, number of previous attempts, and psychiatric history), and psychiatric follow-up history (healthcare professional contact during previous year and discharge mode from hospitalization) were collected at inclusion. The feasibility and acceptability of implementing the SPI were assessed with questionnaires submitted respectively to the caregivers and the patients. The SPI acceptability score for patients corresponds to a set of questions that generates a score ranging from − 8 (not acceptable) to + 8 (fully acceptable). The feasibility score for healthcare professionals corresponds to a different set of questions that generates a score ranging from − 4 to + 6. A more detailed description of the methods and the descriptive information of the cohort can be found inthe PLUS-study article22. Figure 1.

Intervention Description

This study constitutes a follow-up of a program already implemented and validated by Stanley’s team. The SPI is a brief intervention (lasting 20 to 45 min) developed by Stanley and collaborators, designed to reduce the risk of suicide attempt recurrence8. The intervention involves creating a personalized emergency plan for patients to use during a suicidal crisis. Key components of the SPI include: (a) identifying warning signs of an impending crisis; (b) applying internal coping strategies; (c) using social contacts to distract from suicidal thoughts; (d) reaching out to family or friends who can assist in managing the crisis; (e) contacting mental health professionals or organizations; and (f) limiting access to lethal means2.

Follow-up procedures

Patients were contacted by telephone by a psychologist from the department at 4 to 6 weeks (1 month) and again at 6 months after their inclusion. The SPI was discussed and reviewed if necessary. This follow-up call provided support, encouraged the patient to continue their medical care, assessed the patient’s psychological state, and identified any potential risk of a new suicide attempt. If a suicidal risk was identified, patients were reminded that they could go to the emergency department and were encouraged to seek their safety. These calls lasted approximately 15 min each. The reason for not completing the telephone questionnaire was recorded in cases where the patient refused or could not be reached after three attempts, and if the trusted person identified by the patient could not be contacted. At one month, patients were asked whether they had been discharged from hospitalization since inclusion or had been rehospitalized, as well as whether they had made a new suicide attempt, and if so, whether they had gone to the emergency department or contacted healthcare professional or trusted person. At six months, patients were asked whether they had been hospitalized again since inclusion. They were also asked if they remembered the SPI, if they still had it in their possession, and if they consulted it, and if so, when and under what conditions (alone, with friends, family, or an healthcare professional). Primary outcome – long-term effectiveness of the SPI at 6 months

The second follow-up call, conducted 6 months after inclusion, was used to determine the effectiveness of the safety plan based on: the occurrence of at least one new suicide attempt reported by the patient, occurring during the period between the delivery of the SPI and the end of the 6-month follow-up. Secondary outcomes - utility of the SPI

The assessment of SPI usefulness, conducted at the 1-month follow-up, was partially based on a questionnaire, developed by Stanley and colleagues23. This assessment was based on an “utility score” including several aspects. “1) The use of the SPI (including wheter if the patient remembered the SPI, still had in their posession, the methods of consulting and completing the SPI, and the use of the provided helpline numbers); “2) The application of the strategies proposed by the SPI (such as identifying warning signs of a suicidal crisis, implementing personal strategies and activities to counter negative thoughts, seeking help, contacting healthcare professionals, and securing the home); ”3) The subjective evaluation of the utility of the SPI using a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 100. The utility score ranged from 0 to 19. Details of score conception are provided in supplementary material S1 and S3.

Statistical analyses

A descriptive analysis was performed for all parameters collected: patient’s understanding of the SPI and ability to explain it to the evaluator, having consulted or modified the SPI, the utility of the SPI strategies, being hospitalized again at 1 or 6 months of follow-up, having made a new suicide attempt, and having contacted a mental health professional. We first described the utility score, then investigated the determinants associated with this score through a linear regression model: sociodemographic (age, gender, education level, living alone or not), clinical (anxiety or depressive symptoms, alcohol and cannabis use, history of suicide attempt, history of health professional follow-up), intervention-related characteristics (acceptability of the intervention, ability to recall, still possess, consult or complete the SPI), and being hospitalized since inclusion. Efficacy was assessed based on a dichotomized variable: newsuicide attempt within 6 months: yes/no. A logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with recurrence: sociodemographic (age, gender, education level, living alone or not), clinical (anxiety or depressive symptoms, alcohol and cannabis use, history of suicide attempt, history of health professional follow-up), intervention-related characteristics (acceptability of the intervention, to recall the SPI, utility score), and being hospitalized since inclusion.

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 1.0.5 software, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The SPI was proposed to 80 patients, with 75 agreeing to participate. The majority of participants were women (73.3%). 64.0% of the participants had alcohol use disorder, 68.0% presented anxiety symptoms, and 60.8% had a history of suicide attempt prior to the current attempt. Most participants found the safety plan acceptable, with a high acceptability score of 5.18 (SD = 2.56). Also, the intervention was feasible with a feasibility score of 3.97. Table 1and Fig. 2.

Discussion

Over the six-month follow-up period, the study showed that just over half of the patients initially included remained in follow-up. What stood out was that after one month, the majority of participants still involved (85.4%) remembered the SPI, and 68.8% still had it in their possession. Nearly half of these participants reported better recognizing the warning signs of a crisis, and one-third had applied the internal coping strategies suggested by the SPI. However, by the end of the six-month follow-up, one-quarter of the remaining patients had made a new suicide attempt. Among them, 70% sought emergency care. This recurrence rate of 25% at six monthsaligns with the figures reported in the literature, whether patients received brief interventions or not24.No clinical or sociodemographic factor were associated with the usefulness of the SPI.Regarding the probability of a suicide reattempt at 6 months, it appeared to decrease with increasing age while it increased among students (compared to employed individuals) and among those with a history of suicide attempts. In the multivariate analysis, a history of suicide attempt was the only factor independently associated with reattempt at 6 months. These findings highlight the importance of regularly reviewing and revisiting the SPI with patients during follow-up and suggest ways to enhance its impact for preventing suicidal reattempt.

Regarding the perceived usefulness of the SPI after hospitalization, the results of our study were lower than those reported by Stanley’s team. In our study, 85.4% of patients remembered the SPI compared to 98.98% in Stanley’s study, and 68.8% compared to 88.88% still possessed it. As for the implementation of strategies proposed by the SPI, our implementation rates were also lower: 64.6% versus 73.3% of patients reported implementing internal coping strategies, 68.9% versus 85.5% reported developing social contacts, and 50.0% versus 87.7% reported contacting professionals. However, our results were much higher regarding the restriction of access to lethal means: 52.1% versus 6.6%. These differences may be explained by variations in the populations studied: the patients in Stanley’s study had not been hospitzalized, whereas all our participants had been hospitalized, likely due to more severe initial symptoms. Additionally, the frequency of post-intervention follow-ups was higher in Stanley’s initial study23. Regarding the restriction of access to lethal means, we hypothesize a greater involvement of the patient’s family in this regard. Indeed, hospitalization—which was more frequent in our study population—often represents a key moment for the engagement of family members. During the hospital stay, recommendations to secure the patient’s environment are frequently provided to family members.

Regarding the interpretation of our results, we observed a significant decline in the ability to remember the existence of the SPI after six months compared to the first month of follow-up (62.5% versus 85.4%). Since the perceived usefulness of the brief intervention is linked to remembering it, this result highlights the importance of regularly discussing it and encouraging the patient to refer to it frequently and adapt it, throughout the follow-up, as part of the strategies to prevent suicide reattempt. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that reviewing the SPI with patients helps maintain its clinical usefulness25.

We can also hypothesize that providing the SPI in another format, such as a digital version, might have allowed for better identification of the intervention and more regular consultation by patients. Thus, previous studies have shown that the use of an electronic version of the SPI may reduce by 50% the likelihood of returning to the emergency department for a suicidal crisis26,27.

Regarding the factors that may influence the recurrence of a suicide attempt, we found that having a history of a suicide attempt is a risk factor in itself, which is consistent with the literature. This factor increases the risk of a new suicide attempt by 20%, meaning that one in five individuals who have already made a suicide attempt28. Regarding the effect of age on the decrease in the recurrence of a suicide attempt, this result is again consistent with the existing literature, which shows that younger patients are at higher risk of suicide attempts, probably in relation to greater impulsivity29,30. The protective effect of age seems to be confirmed by a higher risk of reattempt among students compared to employed individuals.

The limitations of this study stem from the fact that it is an observational study andthe absence of a control group does not allow for conclusion about causal relationships between the impact of the SPI andthe probability of a new suicide attempt. Indeed, although some ongoing studies plan to compare effectiveness using a control group, its efficacy had already been demonstrated in other populations by Stanley’s team; therefore, we chose not to risk reducing the chances of benefit for the included patients12,32. Additionally, this was a single-centre study conducted in an urban French hospital, with a limited sample size (n = 75 at inclusion and n = 40 at the 6-month follow-up). Another limitation is the attrition bias, with a dropout rate of around 46.7%, leading to a loss of statistical power and a reduction in the validity of the results. This bias may influence the results, particularly regarding the recurrence of suicide attempts and the perceived usefulness of the SPI. Indeed, patients who were unreachable may have been more severe and unstable in terms of suicide risk33. However, this bias was anticipated, given the severity of the patients included in this study, both in terms of prior suicide attempts and addictive comorbidities.Additionally, the follow-up was conducted by staff from the same department that initially proposed the SPI, so a social desirability bias in assessing the perceived usefulness of the SPI cannot be excluded. However, the main outcome evaluating, 6-month outcomes of patients receiving SPI evaluated by whether or not a new suicide attempt occurred, remains independent of the evaluator. Additionally, we cannot rule out a selection bias, as patients who initially agreed to participate and completed the SPI may have been more engaged in their care and more motivated. Also, telephone follow-up, as well as the reliance on self-reported events (notably suicide attempts), may have introduced bias in the absence of a clinical medical assessment. Finally, the small size of the sample can limiting the generalization of the results.

The strengths of this study lie in the fact that it is the first French cohort follow-up study of patients who have attempted suicide and received an SPI, aimed at evaluating its usefulness and integration into the patient’s routine care process. This study highlights the importance of brief interventions during suicidal crises, which help prevent recurrence. It underscores the value of revisiting and refining relapse prevention strategies with the patient after the crisis and throughout their follow-up, thereby increasing the intervention’s effectiveness and improving adherence to care. Finally, this study is based on a real-world clinical cohort, including patients from the general civilian population, whose profiles differ from those in Stanley’s initial studies, which involved former veterans, a more captive population11.

Conclusion

In this French post-emergency cohort, many patients continued to remember and retain their safety plan at 1 and 6 months, and a minority consulted it during follow-up, particularly in calm periods. New suicide attempts still occurred in 25% of followed patients at 6 months, underscoring that SPI should complement, not replace, comprehensive psychiatric care and ongoing follow-up. Because this is a single arm cohort with substantial attrition and a small number of events, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger controlled studies. These descriptive and preliminary findings are consistent with the existing literature showing that short, targeted actions can have a significant impact on stabilizing individuals during acute crises. However, these preliminary findings highlight the potential importance of ongoing engagement with patients over time, the therapeutic alliance seems to be essential in the management of psychiatric disorders. Regularly revisiting and adapting prevention strategies during follow-up is essential to ensure that the patient’s needs are continuously addressed and that they remain supported throughout their recovery process, in order to gradually regain autonomy in managing their symptoms, with the help of accessible tools.
