Impact of Coronary Artery Disease Extent on GRACE Risk Score Prognostic Performance After ST‐Segment–Elevation Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Kayode O. Kuku, Chris Lenselink, Constantijn S. Venema, Hector M. Garcia‐Garcia, Pim van der Harst, Erik Lipsic, Adriaan A. Voors, Joanna J. Wykrzykowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) score is well validated for risk stratification in non–ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction, but less well established in ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), particularly relative to coronary disease burden. We therefore assessed its prognostic performance in a STEMI cohort, accounting for baseline coronary disease extent.MethodsWe studied 1099 patients with STEMI from the CardioLines Coronary Biobank (2015–2021) with evaluable GRACE score and follow‐up data. Patients were stratified by the number of coronary vessels with significant stenosis. The primary end point was all‐cause death at 1 year; the secondary end point was a combined end point, including all‐cause death, recurrent acute coronary syndrome, stroke, and revascularization at 1 year.ResultsMean GRACE scores increased with the number of diseased vessels (P<0.001). In multivariable models, each 10‐point increase in GRACE score was associated with a 31% higher risk of all‐cause death and a 14% higher risk of the combined end point at 1 year. In single‐vessel disease, discrimination for all‐cause death was excellent early (30‐day area under the curve, 0.874 [95% CI, 0.755–0.991]) and moderate at 1 year (area under the curve, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.613–0.927]), but consistently poor in 3‐vessel disease (area under the curve, 0.52–0.57). Discrimination was significantly higher in single‐ versus multivessel disease at earlier time points, with differences attenuated by 1 year. Stratified regression analyses confirmed stronger associations in single‐vessel versus multivessel disease.ConclusionsIn this contemporary STEMI cohort, the GRACE score effectively stratifies event risk in single‐vessel but performs poorly in multivessel disease. These findings suggest variable prognostic utility of the GRACE score across anatomic disease burden post‐STEMI and caution when applying it across STEMI subgroups.

Acute coronary syndromes (ACSs) remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 Approximately one‐third of ACS presentations are attributable to ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), which typically results from acute coronary thrombosis following plaque rupture. 4 Despite significant advances in reperfusion therapies and medical management that have reduced disease incidence and improved STEMI outcomes, 5 the residual risk of adverse cardiovascular events in this population remains substantial, including recurrent myocardial infarction (MI), heart failure, stroke, and death. As a result, risk prediction tools have been developed to guide management and prognostication.

The GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) risk score is a validated tool with a class IIa recommendation in European guidelines for predicting in‐hospital and long‐term mortality in ACS. 1 , 6 , 7 The GRACE score comprises 8 demographic and clinical variables: age, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, ST‐segment deviation, Killip class, cardiac arrest at admission, serum creatinine level, and elevated cardiac enzymes, and has demonstrated strong prognostic value, particularly in non–ST‐segment–elevation MI. 8 However, its predictive performance in patients with STEMI has been less extensively characterized. 9 , 10 , 11

In STEMI, the extent of coronary disease is integral in risk stratification and management decisions. 12 Approximately 40% to 65% of patients with STEMI undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) have multivessel disease, which confers a differential risk of adverse cardiac events. 13 , 14 , 15 In this setting, both anatomic disease burden and completeness of revascularization play key roles in prognosis. 16 , 17 Contemporary trials and guidelines support complete revascularization in selected patients with multivessel disease, either during the index hospitalization or as a staged approach. 3 , 18 , 19 , 20 Despite the recognized prognostic importance of coronary anatomy and revascularization strategy, widely used tools such as the GRACE score do not incorporate anatomic disease burden.

Although angiography‐based scores, such as the synergy between percutaneous coronary intervention with taxus and cardiac surgery (SYNTAX) score, provide detailed anatomic assessment and procedural guidance, their use is largely confined to the catheterization laboratory and is less frequently integrated into bedside clinical decision‐making for risk stratification. In contrast, the number of diseased coronary vessels is a simple, universally available anatomic parameter readily obtained during diagnostic angiography.

Accordingly, evaluating whether the prognostic accuracy of the GRACE score varies by the extent of coronary disease provides a pragmatic and clinically relevant approach to understanding its performance across anatomic disease profiles. We therefore hypothesized that the baseline burden of significant anatomic coronary stenosis in patients with STEMI may modify the prognostic performance of the GRACE score. To test this, we first characterized the GRACE score in a contemporary real‐world population with STEMI. We then examined whether its prognostic and discriminatory performance varied according to the extent of anatomic disease, as defined by the number of coronary vessels with significant stenosis at baseline.

METHODS

Data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

This retrospective study was conducted in the CardioLines Coronary Biobank, which is a single‐center, observational biobank aimed at studying prognostic factors and pathophysiology of patients with ACS. The design of CardioLines has been previously described. 21 Briefly, all patients with suspected ACS and indications for acute coronary angiography between 2015 and 2021 were asked for study participation and signed informed consent. In the case of early mortality, patients were included by deferred consent. For the current study, patients aged ≥18 years with a definitive STEMI diagnosis according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines, 5 undergoing PCI, were analyzed. The study protocol was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee (Groningen, the Netherlands) and national regulatory authorities, and all patients gave informed consent.

Study Data

Study data, including clinical, periprocedural, laboratory, angiographic, and event details, were retrieved from electronic health records. We studied the GRACE 2.0 score, which has been validated for predicting events beyond the initial hospitalization. 22 The GRACE 2.0 score was calculated using age, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, serum creatinine concentration, cardiac arrest at admission, elevated cardiac enzymes, ST‐segment deviation, and Killip class, all collected at study enrollment. Elevated cardiac enzyme was set to “yes” if the admission high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin was elevated. ST‐segment deviation was uniformly present in this STEMI‐only cohort and, therefore, was effectively set to “yes” for all patients in the calculation.

Baseline coronary angiographic and procedural data included the culprit vessel (left main artery, left anterior descending artery, circumflex artery, or right coronary artery), the number of vessels with significant stenosis, and stenosis severity. Significant stenosis was defined by >50% in the left main, or >70% in the left anterior descending, left circumflex, or right coronary artery. Completeness of revascularization was also recorded. Patients without angiographically significant coronary stenosis were excluded to focus on graded levels of confirmed obstructive coronary disease.

Event Definition and Time Points

The primary end point was all‐cause death evaluated at 1 year. The secondary combined end point included all‐cause death, recurrent ACS, stroke, and revascularization by PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting at 1 year. To assess model discrimination over time, we evaluated performance at 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and SDs for continuous variables and frequencies or percentages for categorical variables. Group differences based on event status were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. A complete case analysis was performed to calculate the GRACE risk score. GRACE risk scores were compared by the number of vessels with significant stenosis at baseline. Univariable Cox proportional hazards models were fit for descriptive purposes. Prespecified multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between GRACE risk score and the risk of all‐cause death and the combined end point up to 1 year after STEMI, adjusting for the number of diseased vessels, sex, history of MI, prior PCI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and revascularization completeness, selected a priori based on clinical relevance, and evidence from prior literature. Given the number of events, the primary death model was restricted to a parsimonious set of covariates. The GRACE risk score was analyzed per 10‐point increase in the multivariable Cox models. To assess whether the prognostic value of the GRACE risk score differed according to the extent of angiographic coronary artery disease with time, an interaction term between the GRACE risk score and the number of angiographically significant vessel disease groups was included in the Cox proportional hazards model.

The discriminatory performance of the GRACE score was assessed for primary and secondary end points. Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC) estimates were generated at 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year to evaluate predictive accuracy. Differences in discrimination by vessel‐disease subgroups were examined by comparing AUCs between subgroups using Wald z‐tests through 1 year.

Calibration of the GRACE score for both end points was evaluated at 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year by comparing mean predicted probabilities with observed event rates estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival probabilities.

In secondary analysis, Cox regression analyses were performed within vessel disease groups stratified as single vessel and multivessel (combining 2‐ and 3‐vessel disease) to account for limited events and potential power constraints while exploring effect modification. Proportional hazard assumptions were checked using Schoenfeld residuals.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted, modeling GRACE risk score categorized risk categories: low (1–108), intermediate (109–140), and high (>140) based on established clinical thresholds. 1 , 3

A 2‐tailed P<0.05 indicates statistical significance, and the CI was 95%. All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (R version 4.4.1 [2024‐06‐14]).

Among 1099 patients, single‐vessel disease patients were more often women (29.8%), compared with 2‐vessel (21.5%) and 3‐vessel disease (23.0%). Mean age and prevalence of diabetes increased with vessel involvement, whereas hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and family history of cardiovascular disease prevalence were similar across groups. Smoking was slightly more common in single‐ and 2‐vessel disease patients than in 3‐vessel disease.

GRACE Risk Score Distribution

Mean±SD GRACE scores increased with vessel involvement, with 132.76±21.1 in single‐vessel disease, 138.50±20.8 in 2‐vessel disease, and 144.63±22.8 in 3‐vessel disease (P<0.001) (Table 1).

Events at 1 Year

There were 44 cases of all‐cause death, and 94 patients experienced at least 1 component of the combined end point at 1 year. Table S1 shows the distribution of the events by vessel‐disease groups.

Multivariable Analyses

In multivariable Cox regression with GRACE score modeled continuously, a 10‐point increase was associated with a 31% higher risk of all‐cause death at 1 year (hazard ratio [HR], 1.31 [95% CI, 1.16–1.47]; P<0.001) and a 14% higher risk of the combined end point (HR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04–1.25]; P=0.006). The extent of coronary disease was also independently associated with increased risk of all‐cause death (HR, 1.49 per vessel [95% CI, 1.01–2.18]; P=0.043) and the combined end point (HR, 1.50 per vessel [95% CI, 1.14–1.98]; P=0.004) (Table 2). A formal test for interaction between GRACE score and the number of diseased vessels was not significant for all‐cause death (P=0.167) or the combined end point (P=0.231) at 1 year.

GRACE Risk Score Discrimination of Events

In the overall cohort, GRACE score demonstrated modest discriminative performance for all‐cause death, with AUCs of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.59–0.76) at 30 days, 0.69 (95% CI, 0.55–0.69) at 6 months, and 0.69 (95% CI, 0.53–0.67) at 1 year. For the combined end point, discrimination was slightly lower, with AUCs of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.59–0.76) at 30 days, 0.62 (95% CI, 0.55–0.69) at 6 months, and 0.60 (95% CI, 0.53–0.67) at 1 year, indicating only modest predictive accuracy over time.

When stratified by extent of coronary disease, the GRACE score showed excellent discrimination for all‐cause death in single‐vessel disease (AUC, 0.87 at 30 days, 0.84 at 6 months, and 0.77 at 1 year), moderate discrimination in 2‐vessel disease (AUC, 0.76 at 30 days, declining to 0.62–0.68 over 1 year), and poor discrimination in 3‐vessel disease (AUC, 0.53–0.57 across all time points) (Figure [A]Figure1[A]).

For the combined end point, discrimination was highest in single‐vessel disease (AUC, 0.84 at 30 days), moderate in 2‐vessel disease (AUC, 0.79 at 30 days), and consistently poor in 3‐vessel disease (AUC, 0.48–0.52 across all time points), with predictive performance declining over time in all subgroups (Figure [B]1[B]).

In formal analyses comparing time‐dependent AUCs of the GRACE score, discriminative performance was higher in single‐vessel disease compared with multivessel disease, particularly 3‐vessel disease, at 30 days and 6 months, with attenuation by 1 year. Differences between 2‐ and 3‐vessel disease were limited to early post‐STEMI and not significant thereafter (Table 3). Calibration analysis demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed risks of all‐cause mortality at 30, 180, and 365 days (Figure S1).

In secondary analysis in stratified Cox models, each 10‐point increase in GRACE score was more strongly associated with outcomes in patients with single‐vessel disease (all cause death: HR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.23–1.83]; P<0.001; combined end point: HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.05–1.45]; P=0.01) compared with patients with multivessel disease (all cause death: HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.05–1.42]; P=0.008; combined end point: HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.00–1.26]; P=0.04) (Table S2).

With GRACE score modeled categorically, the risk of all‐cause death was significantly lower in the intermediate group compared with the high group (HR, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.24–0.88]; P=0.018), but not in the low group (HR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.27–3.07]; P=0.87). For the combined end point, there were no significant differences across GRACE categories. The number of diseased vessels was significantly associated with both all‐cause death (HR, 1.60 per vessel [95% CI, 1.09–2.35]; P=0.017) and the combined end point (HR, 1.59 per vessel [95% CI, 1.20–2.09]; P=0.001).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the GRACE score across graded levels of anatomic coronary disease from 30 days to 1 year after STEMI. In this contemporary clinical cohort, (1) GRACE scores progressively increased with the number of diseased vessels; (2) both GRACE and anatomic disease burden independently predicted all‐cause death and the combined end point; (3) GRACE score demonstrated excellent short‐term and moderate 1‐year discrimination in patients with single‐vessel disease; however, its prognostic performance was attenuated in 3‐vessel disease, with consistently weaker associations between GRACE and outcomes in multivessel compared with single‐vessel disease; and (4) although regression analyses did not reveal an interaction between GRACE and anatomic disease burden, AUC‐based comparisons demonstrated significant heterogeneity, with GRACE score showing stronger early prognostic performance in single‐vessel compared with multivessel disease. Together, these findings indicate that although GRACE remains useful for risk prediction in lower‐burden disease, particularly early after presentation, its discriminative utility diminishes as anatomic vessel disease burden increases.

GRACE Risk Score in STEMI Event Prediction

The GRACE risk score was originally developed and validated to predict the risk of death in ACS, 7 but its performance in populations with STEMI has been relatively inconsistent. Discrimination is often lower in STEMI, likely because the score was not specifically optimized for this subgroup, the risk distribution is narrower, and important STEMI‐specific factors, such as reperfusion success and infarct size, are not captured. Moreover, only a few studies have evaluated GRACE exclusively in populations with STEMI, most at a single time point, generally reporting moderate short‐term discrimination that declines over time, without accounting for the extent of anatomic coronary disease. In a tertiary center study, Kumar et al reported moderate discrimination for major adverse cardiovascular events (AUC, 0.695) at a median follow‐up of 244 days, 23 whereas Kong et al, in a smaller population of 209 patients with STEMI, observed AUCs of 0.75 to 0.76 at 6 months and 1 year, respectively, that declined substantially by 4 years (AUC, 0.62). A larger study of 562 patients with STEMI reported similar moderate discrimination for mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (AUCs, 0.64 and 0.66) at ≈2 years. 24 Other studies focused on early‐phase (30‐day) outcomes showed differing poor to moderate predictive value AUCs (0.54–0.65). 25 , 26

Coronary Disease Burden and GRACE Risk Score

The discrimination results in our overall population were consistent with prior studies. However, stratifying the population by the extent of coronary disease unmasked differences in GRACE discrimination across groups that have not been previously reported. Predictive accuracy for all‐cause death was consistently higher in single‐vessel disease than in the overall cohort, whereas patients with 2‐ or 3‐vessel disease showed lower discrimination at 6 months to 1 year. Similar trends were observed for the combined end point, supported by significant AUC differences. These subgroup variations may reflect a broader range of baseline clinical risk in single‐vessel disease, whereas high‐risk anatomic features in multivessel disease reduce score variability, inducing ceiling effects, and limiting separation between event and no‐event groups. Clinically, this underscores the importance of interpreting GRACE estimates in the context of coronary disease extent to guide risk stratification and follow‐up strategies.

The GRACE risk score was developed as a bedside risk stratification tool incorporating only clinical and laboratory parameters. Our stratified analyses extend prior ACS evidence by demonstrating incremental prognostic insight according to coronary artery disease burden in STEMI. Although angiography‐integrated scores, such as the controlled abciximab and device investigation to lower late angioplasty complications (CADILLAC) risk score, have shown predictive abilities comparable to GRACE for in‐hospital and 2‐year outcomes in contemporary MI cohorts, 24 studies comparing GRACE with the SYNTAX score, which captures coronary disease complexity, have reported only weak correlations, 9 , 10 suggesting GRACE's limitation in capturing the complete anatomic disease burden. Our findings demonstrate that although the GRACE risk score may not predict anatomic severity, its prognostic performance is significantly influenced by the extent of vessel disease.

In this study, incremental increases in GRACE score and each additional diseased coronary vessel were independently associated with higher risks of all‐cause death and combined event end points within 1 year of STEMI. These findings demonstrate that both clinical risks, as captured by the GRACE risk score, and anatomic disease burden retain independent and additive prognostic value in patients with STEMI, highlighting their complementary roles in post‐STEMI risk stratification.

Clinical Implications

Our findings have important implications for post–PCI risk stratification in STEMI. Although reaffirming the prognostic utility of the GRACE risk score for adverse outcomes, our results indicate that reliance on clinical variables alone may underestimate ischemic risk in patients with extensive coronary artery disease. Furthermore, GRACE demonstrates limited performance in predicting composite ischemic events, supporting a more comprehensive, multimodal risk assessment approach that incorporates anatomic disease burden alongside clinical parameters. 19

These findings support a more tailored management approach in patients with extensive coronary disease, including: Intensified lipid lowering, stricter blood pressure and glycemic control, and earlier initiation of cardioprotective agents.Closer clinical surveillance and follow‐up, particularly within the first 6 months after STEMI, is a period in which our findings suggest residual risk remains substantial in those with extensive coronary disease.

Future Directions

Future studies should validate these findings in larger cohorts with STEMI to assess generalizability across diverse settings. Prospective studies are also needed to develop and test integrated risk models that combine clinical and anatomic variables. Also, trials are needed to evaluate whether intensified pharmacotherapy or earlier staged revascularization can mitigate the excess risk observed with extensive coronary artery disease.

Limitations

The study should be interpreted in the context of a few limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias, and the number of events within subgroups was low. We considered all‐cause death as our primary outcome, consistent with GRACE derivation for robustness and comparability; however, this may conservatively attenuate associations with coronary disease burden. Third, post‐STEMI medical therapy, which may influence clinical outcomes, was not captured. Although completeness of revascularization was adjusted for in models, it was not explored in detail with respect to differences in revascularization strategy and the timing of staged procedures, which warrant dedicated evaluation in future analyses.

Moreover, unlike studies that incorporate comprehensive anatomic risk scores, such as the SYNTAX score, or directly compare clinical and anatomic scoring systems, our objective was to examine how GRACE performance varies according to overall coronary vessel disease burden. As such, detailed anatomic complexity scores were not calculated, and our findings should not be interpreted as a comparison of these scoring systems.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. It leverages a contemporary, real‐world population with STEMI reflective of current management practices, enhancing clinical applicability. In addition, we evaluated discrimination across multiple clinically relevant time points, offering a nuanced understanding of how risk prediction evolves. Third, the findings support a simplified precision risk stratification approach in STEMI by highlighting the value of baseline angiographic disease burden, reinforcing guideline trends, and future directions in ACS care.

CONCLUSIONS

In this contemporary cohort with STEMI, we demonstrate that although the GRACE score retains strong prognostic value in patients with lower anatomic vessel disease burden, its discriminative performance diminishes with increasing vessel involvement. Reliance on clinical risk models alone for risk prediction after STEMI may underestimate risk in patients with extensive coronary disease, underscoring the need for more integrated approaches that combine clinical and anatomic information. Further evaluation in larger, adequately powered studies is warranted. Integrating anatomic context into prognostic assessment may ultimately enhance early risk stratification and individualized management strategies.
