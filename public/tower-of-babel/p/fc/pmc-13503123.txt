The impact of preoperative mechanical support on long-term survival following early lung re-transplantation for graft failure1
Authors: Vikram Krishna, Allen A. Razavi, Brian Lim, Zachary Brennan, Qiudong Chen, Joanna Chikwe, Michael E. Bowdish, Laura DiChiacchio, Reinaldo Rampolla, Dominick Megna, Pedro Catarino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSevere graft failure after lung transplantation is a devastating outcome with limited therapeutic options. Mechanical support provides temporary stabilization but may compromise re-transplantation candidacy. This study examined postoperative outcomes and long-term survival of graft failure re-transplants by preoperative mechanical support use.MethodsThe UNOS database was queried for adults undergoing early re-transplantation for graft failure (EGF) performed within 1 year of the primary transplant from 2005 to 2025. Primary transplants, multiorgan, and re-transplants from unknown causes were excluded. Patients were stratified by preoperative mechanical support use (ventilation and/or ECMO). The primary outcome was 5-year survival.ResultsAmong 1453 re-transplants, 140 (9.6%) were for EGF. 85 (61%) required preoperative mechanical support (61%), and 55 did not (39%). Within the mechanical support group, 72 (85%) were ventilated, 44 (52%) received ECMO, and 31 (36%) had both ECMO and ventilation. Supported patients had higher LAS scores (89.3 vs 71.6, p<0.001) and more dialysis use (24% vs 1%, p<0.001). Postoperatively, supported patients experienced more prolonged ventilation (34% vs 16%, p=0.02) and more ECMO support (20% vs 9%, p=0.03). Ninety-day mortality was equivalent (27.1% vs 16.4%, p=0.14). However, mechanical support use of any form was associated with higher 5-year mortality (adjusted HR 1.71 [1.02-2.85], p=0.04). Combined ventilator and ECMO use had the worst long-term survival compared to no mechanical support (aHR 2.13 [1.03-4.40], p=0.04).ConclusionPreoperative mechanical support was associated with greater postoperative morbidity and worse long-term survival. These findings underscore the need to critically evaluate whether re-transplantation truly benefits this high-risk population.

Lung transplantation remains the definitive therapy for select patients with end-stage pulmonary disease. However, early graft dysfunction continues to limit long-term success. Primary graft dysfunction (PGD) is the leading cause of early morbidity and mortality following lung transplantation and is associated with worse short-term and long-term survival.1, 2, 3 Severe PGD may progress to graft failure, a devastating complication that often necessitates re-transplantation in carefully selected candidates. Despite advances in donor selection, organ preservation, and perioperative management, outcomes after lung re-transplantation remain inferior compared to primary transplantation.4, 5

Mechanical support, including invasive mechanical ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), plays a central role in the management of severe primary graft failure and cardiopulmonary dysfunction.6, 7 ECMO is used to bridge patients with severe PGD to recovery. However, when adequate recovery does not occur, ECMO has increasingly been utilized as a bridge to transplantation and as a rescue for refractory graft dysfunction, particularly in high-acuity patients. However, the implications of persistent mechanical support use at the time of re-transplantation for severe graft failure remain poorly defined. A prior multicenter study demonstrated inferior survival among recipients bridged to pulmonary re-transplantation with extracorporeal life support.8 However, it is unclear whether preoperative mechanical support represents a reversible bridge to sustainable recovery or whether its necessity acts as a marker of systemic physiologic compromise associated with poor long-term benefit.

Given ongoing organ scarcity and the ethical imperative to balance urgency with utility and allocation decisions, a clear understanding of outcomes in mechanically supported graft failure patients is essential. The purpose of the study was to evaluate postoperative morbidity and long-term survival among adult lung re-transplant recipients for graft failure within 1 year of primary transplant, stratified by preoperative mechanical support use. It could be argued that mechanical support, especially VV-ECMO, gives the opportunity to recondition these patients. Nevertheless, we hypothesize that preoperative mechanical support use would be associated with increased postoperative morbidity and inferior long-term survival, compared with unsupported recipients.

Materials and methods

Data source

This national retrospective cohort study was performed using the United Network of Organ Sharing (UNOS) national thoracic organ transplantation database for all adult lung transplants from January 2005 to March 2025. The database included information from all transplant recipients, wait-listed candidates, and donors in the United States, which was submitted by members of the Organ Procurement and Transplant Network. The study was exempt from review by the Cedars-Sinai Medical Center Institutional Review Board because all data were deidentified.

Study design

The UNOS database was queried for adult lung re-transplants (> 18 years) performed during the study period. Our study included all re-transplants for bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), restrictive allograft syndrome (RAS), primary graft failure, and acute rejection performed within 1 year of the primary transplant, which was defined as early re-transplantation for graft failure (EGF). EGF is not a formal diagnosis through ISHLT guidelines or a definition within UNOS, but the nomenclature was used as an operational study definition. Re-transplant indication was identified using the UNOS code “TCR_DGN,” which refers to the primary diagnosis at listing. The 1-year threshold was selected because prior re-transplantation studies have demonstrated clinically meaningful differences between early and late re-transplantation, with inferior outcomes for re-transplantation within 1 year of primary transplant.9, 10 Primary transplants, those with multiple re-transplants, combined multiorgan transplants, re-transplants from unknown causes, patients younger than 18 years of age, and those with missing survival data were excluded. Patients were then stratified by preoperative mechanical support use at the time of re-transplantation (ventilation and/or ECMO). We also performed a prespecified subgroup analysis after separating patients into 3 mutually exclusive groups: ventilator only, no ventilator and no ECMO, and combined ventilator and ECMO. Patients supported with ECMO alone (without a ventilator) were excluded from the subgroup analysis due to limited sample size (n=13). The primary outcome was 5-year survival. Secondary outcomes included postoperative morbidity and 90-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and postoperative outcomes between groups were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. Continuous variables were reported using mean (± standard deviation) or median (quartile 1 to quartile 3) based on their distribution. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and stratified by preoperative mechanical support use. Adjusted survival comparisons were performed using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model, accounting for covariates selected based on clinical relevance. A sensitivity analysis was performed, incorporating transplant era (Era 1: 2005-2015, Era 2: 2016-2025) into the multivariable Cox regression model to assess for changes in perioperative management over the study period. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated. Analyses were performed using complete-case data, and observations missing any variables for a given model were excluded. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 19.5 (StataCorp).

Results

Recipient and donor characteristics

Among 1453 re-transplants, a total of 140 (9.6%) patients underwent lung re-transplantation for EGF within 1 year of primary transplant (Supplemental Figure 1). Among the 140 EGF patients, 85 required mechanical support prior to re-transplant (61%), and 55 did not (39%). Of the 85 mechanical support patients, 72 (85%) were ventilated, and 44 (52%) required ECMO. Baseline donor and recipient characteristics are listed in Table 1. Recipient age was similar between the 2 groups, along with sex, race, and BMI (all p>0.05). There were no differences in donor characteristics between the 2 groups. However, mechanical support patients had significantly higher lung allocation scores at match (89.3 [85.2-90.6] vs 71.6 [48.6-88.3], p<0.001) and higher total bilirubin levels (0.7 [0.5-1.1] vs 0.4 [0.2-0.6] mg/dL, p<0.001). Dialysis at transplant was markedly more common among supported patients (23.8% vs 1.8%, p=0.001), and mechanical support patients demonstrated more severe functional impairment (92.9% vs 69.1%, p=0.003). Patients requiring mechanical support also had significantly shorter waitlist times (5 [3-12] vs 23 [6-44] days, p<0.001). Postoperative Outcomes

Patients requiring preoperative mechanical support experienced significantly greater postoperative morbidity (Table 2). Prolonged ventilation beyond 48 hours occurred more in the preoperative mechanical support group compared to unsupported patients (85.9% vs 61.8%, p=0.001). Intubation greater than 72 hours (34.1% vs 16.4%, p=0.02), postoperative dialysis (42.9% vs 12.7%, p<0.001), and ECMO use at 72 hours (20.0% vs 9.1%, p=0.03) were all greater in the preoperative mechanical support group, compared to the unsupported group. Rates of airway dehiscence, post-transplant stroke, re-intubation, and acute rejection were all similar between the 2 groups (p>0.05). Long-term survival analysis

Upon Kaplan-Meier analysis, 5-year overall survival was significantly greater in patients without preoperative mechanical support compared to those requiring mechanical support (37.0% [23.8-50.2] vs 20.5% [11.9% to 30.7%], log-rank p=0.04, Figure 1). On multivariable Cox regression analysis, preoperative mechanical support was associated with increased 5-year mortality (adjusted HR 1.71 [1.02-2.85], p=0.04, Table 3). Donor and recipient covariates, including donor age, donor oxygenation status, recipient age, sex, total bilirubin, waitlist time, and ischemia time, were not associated with mortality. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a reduced covariate Cox regression model to assess model robustness, and preoperative mechanical support remained independently associated with increased mortality (aHR 1.82 [1.12-2.97], p=0.02; Supplemental Table 1), supporting the stability of the primary model. Discussion

In this national analysis of adult lung re-transplantation for EGF within 1 year of primary transplant, preoperative mechanical support was associated with significantly higher postoperative morbidity and inferior long-term survival. Although early mortality did not differ significantly between groups, patients who underwent ventilation and/or ECMO experienced markedly higher rates of prolonged postoperative ventilation, post-transplant renal failure requiring dialysis, and early postoperative ECMO dependence. Furthermore, these perioperative factors translated to inferior longer-term outcomes. Five-year survival was significantly lower among supported mechanical support patients, and preoperative mechanical support remained independently associated with increased mortality after multivariable adjustment.

Importantly, our definition of EGF includes multiple parenchymal etiologies for severe graft dysfunction leading to re-transplantation within 1 year of primary transplant, including BOS, RAS, primary graft failure, and acute rejection. Despite the heterogeneity, these patients all required re-transplantation within 1 year, representing a uniquely high-risk population.

These findings align with prior studies that demonstrate inferior long-term survival following early lung re-transplantation compared with primary transplantation, particularly in high-acuity recipients.4, 5 Importantly, upon our era-adjusted sensitivity analysis, preoperative mechanical support remained associated with worse mortality. Although ECMO utilization and perioperative management evolved substantially over the study period, the findings were not solely due to temporal changes in practice patterns. Together, our results suggest that while contemporary perioperative management may mitigate immediate procedural risk in critically ill re-transplant candidates, preoperative mechanical support identifies a phenotype in which the survival benefit of re-transplantation may be limited.

A notable finding of the study is the dissociation between early and late outcomes. Despite substantially greater postoperative morbidity, 30-day and 90-day mortality did not differ significantly between mechanical support and unsupported recipients. This pattern suggests that many mechanically supported patients can be successfully bridged through the early postoperative period, which is consistent with prior studies describing improved perioperative survival in ECMO-bridged transplant recipients.7, 11 However, survival curves diverged over time, and the association between mechanical support use and mortality persisted.

Clinically, this supports the interpretation that mechanical support is less a determinant of immediate technical success and may be more a surrogate for systemic illness severity, organ dysfunction, and diminished physiologic reserve. Mechanical support recipients demonstrated higher allocation scores, greater rates of dialysis at the time of transplant, elevated bilirubin, and more severe functional impairment, which are clinical factors that may reflect multisystem stress and have been associated with worse post-transplant survival.6, 12, 13, 14 For transplant programs, this distinction provides insight. Candidacy decisions often focus on short-term procedural risk, whereas ethical stewardship requires consideration of long-term survival benefit and durability.15, 16 Our data suggest that mechanically supported EGF candidates can survive in the early postoperative period. However, their long-term trajectory is frequently poor, raising the threshold for selecting patients most likely to achieve meaningful benefit.

The magnitude of postoperative morbidity among supported patients was substantial. Rates of prolonged ventilation, intubation greater than 72 hours, dialysis, and postoperative ECMO were markedly elevated. These complications represent established markers of prolonged critical illness and downstream vulnerability.17, 18, 19 Renal failure after lung transplantation is associated with infection risk and reduced long-term survival.20 Similarly, persistent ventilator dependence and ECMO use may reflect ongoing graft dysfunction, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and systemic inflammatory burden, which are all associated with severe PGD.2, 21 These complications may provide biologically driven pathways linking preoperative critical illness to late mortality.

The prespecified subgroup analysis adds important granularity. Patients requiring combined preoperative ventilator and ECMO support experienced the highest postoperative morbidity, which was associated with increased mortality compared to unsupported recipients. The use of both mechanical ventilation and ECMO reflects severe cardiopulmonary failure and likely identifies recipients with profound functional impairment. The requirement for both forms of support likely serves as a surrogate for severe illness, which may explain the inferior long-term survival observed in this subgroup.22, 23 We emphasize that the sample size is small, and this finding is meant to be hypothesis-generating. However, the directionality of our results supports that “dual-support” patients may represent a threshold of physiological compromise beyond which re-transplantation yields limited durable benefit.

Our study also provides further insight into the downstream consequences of PGD in the form of severe EGF. PGD remains a leading cause of early mortality after lung transplantation and is associated with short- and long-term mortality.1, 24, 25 Most PGD studies focus on early outcomes (usually within 72 hours), donor and recipient risk factors, and short-term outcomes after primary transplant. However, little is known about patients in whom severe graft injury progresses to graft failure requiring re-transplantation. Our study attempts to extend PGD knowledge beyond the initial grading window into long-term survival implications for refractory graft dysfunction. Mechanical ventilation and ECMO are central to PGD management algorithms and can be used as salvage strategies for grade 3 PGD.3, 22 The data suggest that when support persists to the point of re-transplant consideration, it may serve as a clinical surrogate for non-resolving severe graft injury. The study suggests that mechanical support is not only a rescue modality but also acts as a marker of biological threshold beyond which long-term outcomes may be compromised. Our data also support the idea that every effort should be made to wean these patients from mechanical support.

Rehabilitation likely represents an important modifiable factor in this population. Contemporary transplant experience has increasingly emphasized early mobilization and prehabilitation strategies to preserve functional status and physiologic reserve among critically ill candidates.26, 27 These factors may influence both retransplant candidacy and long-term outcomes. Given the marked functional impairment observed among mechanically supported recipients in our cohort, strategies aimed at minimizing deconditioning and optimizing rehabilitation may be particularly important.28 Future studies should evaluate whether rehabilitation-centered approaches can improve long-term outcomes among mechanically supported retransplant candidates.

Re-transplantation for EGF addresses a uniquely difficult ethical dilemma of providing urgent rescue therapy for a sick lung transplant population in the setting of profound organ scarcity. Our findings add to the body of literature on re-transplantation by identifying preoperative mechanical support as an additional risk to consider in this already high-risk population. The study does not support the broad exclusion of mechanical support lung re-transplant candidates. Rather, it supports a more nuanced, evidence-based framework in which preoperative mechanical support use, particularly when combined with ventilator and ECMO use, triggers further multidisciplinary review, prognostic counseling discussions, and explicit consideration of long-term survival. For EGF re-transplant candidates on mechanical support, differentiating patients likely to recover graft function from those progressing to irreversible failure is critical. As progression to re-transplant in the setting of ongoing mechanical support limits long-term benefit, greater emphasis on PGD prevention, early mitigation strategies, donor optimization, and ischemia-reperfusion injury attenuation may help improve survival. For transplant programs and policymakers, the data also add to the ongoing discussion of balancing urgency and utility in allocation decisions.

Our study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, registry-based design. Classification of re-transplant indication relied on registry coding and may be subject to misclassification. Our cohort was intentionally restricted to recipients undergoing early re-transplantation for graft failure resulting from parenchymal allograft dysfunction, including primary graft failure, acute rejection, BOS, and RAS. Although this approach reduced cohort heterogeneity and allowed evaluation of a more clinically comparable population, the methodology adds selection bias that may limit the generalizability of our findings to all early lung re-transplant recipients. The UNOS database also lacks granular data on immunologic, histopathologic, and perioperative physiological variables, limiting assessment of biological mechanisms underlying graft failure. Although functional status was available, the UNOS database lacks details on awake ECMO strategies before re-transplantation. Furthermore, the UNOS database does not distinguish elective continuation of preoperative ECMO through the operative period from newly initiated postoperative ECMO due to graft dysfunction. Although a small number of patients listed on ECMO or mechanical ventilation may have been successfully weaned before re-transplantation, the UNOS registry does not capture all temporary support episodes before listing. Such patients would be expected to have greater illness severity than recipients who never required support, and their inclusion in the unsupported cohort may have reduced the observed differences between groups and led to an underestimation of the adverse impact of ongoing mechanical support at re-transplantation. We also could not account for center-level variation in re-transplant operative experience and perioperative management. Changes in allocation system and listing practices over time also may have influenced cohort composition. Additionally, the focus on transplanted patients precludes assessment of waitlist mortality or delisting, which may further inform access disparities. Our small sample size limits statistical power. Lastly, an ECMO-only bridging strategy represents a clinically relevant approach for EGF; however, due to the small number of patients in this subgroup (n=13), we were unable to include it in our subanalysis.

Future work should integrate registry data with granular center-level clinical details, including formal ISHLT PGD grading, histopathology, and longitudinal trajectories. Risk prediction models fitted for EGF re-transplant candidates may inform candidacy decisions. As technical graft failure represents a distinct pathophysiologic process, future studies evaluating all-cause early re-transplantation may further clarify whether the impact of mechanical support differs according to retransplant indication. Also, comparative effectiveness studies evaluating re-transplantation to non-retransplant management for mechanical support patients would give more insight into the net survival benefit.

In a national cohort of lung re-transplantation for EGF within 1 year of primary transplant, preoperative mechanical support use was associated with substantially greater postoperative morbidity and inferior long-term survival. While early mortality did not differ significantly, survival diverged over time, suggesting that mechanical support identifies a high-risk phenotype with limited sustained benefit. The findings support careful, multidisciplinary evaluation of re-transplant candidacy, transparent patient counseling, and underscore the need to balance urgency against the likelihood of meaningful, sustained survival in this exceptionally vulnerable population.
