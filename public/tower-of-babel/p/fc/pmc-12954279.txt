A pragmatic phase II trial evaluating treatment strategies using immune checkpoint inhibitors for metastatic esophageal cancer patients with severe dysphagia
Authors: Y. Nagata, H. Arai, N. Izawa, C. Inagaki, A. Sugaya, K. Hirata, T. Tsushima, T. Moriwaki, T. Masuishi, Y. Nagatani, K. Harada, M. Taguri, Y. Sunakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to chemotherapy has significantly improved the prognosis of patients with metastatic esophageal cancer. Although fluorouracil and cisplatin (FP) combined with an ICI is considered one of the most effective first-line treatments, patients with severe dysphagia have not been adequately evaluated. Radiotherapy (RT) and chemoradiotherapy (CRT), however, have been established as effective treatment options for dysphagia, based on the results of a phase III clinical trial conducted before the approval of ICIs for esophageal cancer. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of FP plus ICI and FP plus ICI following RT/CRT in patients with stage IVB esophageal cancer and severe dysphagia.MethodsThis is a randomized, non-comparative, pragmatic phase II trial enrolling untreated patients with metastatic esophageal or esophagogastric squamous cell carcinoma and severe dysphagia. Patients are randomized to receive either FP plus ICI (arm A) or FP plus ICI following RT/CRT (arm B) in accordance with clinical practice. The primary endpoint is dysphagia relief rate at 9 weeks from the initiation of treatment. This trial has been ongoing since December 2024.

Description of protocol

Background

Fluorouracil and cisplatin (FP) combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are considered as one of the most effective first-line treatments for stage IVB esophageal cancer; however, patients with severe dysphagia caused by the primary tumor have not been adequately evaluated in previous clinical trials. In contrast, radiotherapy (RT) and chemoradiotherapy (CRT) are established treatment options for dysphagia relief, as demonstrated in the phase III TROG 03.01 trial conducted before the approval of ICIs for esophageal cancer. Therefore, the optimal ICI-based treatment strategy for stage IVB esophageal cancer patients with severe dysphagia remains unclear.

Rationale

Esophageal cancer is the seventh leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide as of 2022 and remains a disease with a poor prognosis.1 The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate is 26% for locally advanced disease and 5% for metastatic disease.2 Systemic chemotherapy is the standard treatment of metastatic esophageal cancer, and FP therapy has long been established as the standard first-line regimen, with an objective response rate (ORR) of ∼30% and a median OS of <10 months.3, 4, 5 Although the efficacy of FP therapy has been limited, the advent of ICIs has recently transformed the therapeutic landscape of esophageal cancer.

Immune checkpoints such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) play a critical role in regulating T-cell activation and maintaining immune balance.6, 7, 8 Overexpression of PD-L1 in cancer cells promotes tumor progression by creating an immunosuppressive environment,9 whereas blockade of these pathways with anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 antibodies can restore T-cell function and induce antitumor effects. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, which is characterized by a high frequency of PD-L1 overexpression and somatic mutations, has been considered a promising therapeutic target for ICI therapy, leading to active clinical development.10,11 FP in combination with pembrolizumab or nivolumab, both humanized immunoglobulin monoclonal anti-PD-1 antibodies, has significantly improved clinical outcomes as a first-line treatment based on the results of the KEYNOTE-590 and CheckMate 648 trials (Table 1). Dysphagia caused by the primary tumor is a common and challenging clinical problem associated with particularly poor prognosis. Dysphagia greatly reduces patients’ quality of life and, through inadequate oral intake, results in malnutrition and deterioration of the general condition, thereby exerting a substantial impact on the continuation of chemotherapy. RT and CRT are considered treatment options for esophageal cancer patients with dysphagia, as demonstrated in the phase III TROG 03.01 trial which evaluated the dysphagia relief rate at 9 weeks after the initiation of 30-40 Gy of RT, with or without FP, in patients with unresectable esophageal cancer and dysphagia. No significant difference in the dysphagia relief rate at 9 weeks was observed between the two groups (45% versus 35%, P = 0.13), although RT demonstrated efficacy in local disease control. Thus, although RT and CRT demonstrated some efficacy in improving dysphagia, their effectiveness remains limited, and they cannot be regarded as fully satisfactory treatment options. Furthermore, as this trial was conducted before the approval of ICIs for esophageal cancer, the efficacy of FP plus ICI therapy for dysphagia has not been sufficiently evaluated in previous studies, remaining an unmet clinical need.

Given that FP plus ICI has demonstrated substantial antitumor activity, tumor shrinkage in the primary lesion and consequent improvement in dysphagia may be anticipated. Moreover, FP plus ICI administered after RT may further enhance dysphagia relief, as RT has been suggested to enhance the efficacy of ICIs by promoting antigen-specific immune responses, including immunogenic cell death, activation of dendritic cells, cross-presentation of antigens, and proliferation of cytotoxic T cells.15 Supporting this concept, additional analyses from the phase III CheckMate-577 trial have been reported. This trial demonstrated that adjuvant nivolumab significantly prolonged disease-free survival compared with placebo in patients who underwent curative resection after neoadjuvant CRT without achieving a pathological complete response [22.4 months versus 11.0 months, hazard ratio (HR) 0.69, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.56-0.86, P = 0.0003).16 A post hoc analysis further showed that the combined positive score, which reflects PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages, increased in 51% of patients after receiving CRT. In these patients, adjuvant nivolumab further improved disease-free survival compared with placebo (not reached versus 8.9 months, HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.11-0.78).17

Taken together, these findings indicate that both FP plus ICI and FP plus ICI following RT/CRT may represent effective treatment strategies for esophageal cancer patients presenting with severe dysphagia. Nevertheless, the absence of clinical data specifically addressing dysphagia relief underscores the necessity for prospective clinical trials to rigorously evaluate their efficacy.

Objective

We initiated a prospective pragmatic trial (IMPULSE trial, UMIN000056051) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of FP plus ICI, and FP plus ICI following RT/CRT, with a particular focus on dysphagia relief (Table 3). As no direct comparative data are currently available between these two strategies, each regimen will be assessed in relation to previously reported outcomes on dysphagia relief achieved with RT/CRT.

Methods

Study design

This study is a non-comparative, phase II clinical trial being conducted at 62 hospitals across Japan (Figure 1). It was designed as a pragmatic clinical trial with minimized eligibility criteria, flexible interventions, and simplified data collection. This design facilitates the enrollment of a heterogeneous patient population across multiple institutions and enables the evaluation of treatment effectiveness in real-world clinical settings, thereby allowing the results to be readily translated into routine clinical practice. Key eligibility criteria

Patients are eligible if they have histologically confirmed diagnosis of unresectable metastatic esophageal or esophagogastric junction squamous cell carcinoma with a primary tumor, severe dysphagia defined as a Mellow DS of 2-4, aged ≥18 years, ECOG PS of 0-2, and no prior anticancer treatment. Patients who are candidates for curative resection or definitive RT/CRT are excluded. Additional exclusion criteria include a history of hypersensitivity to the investigational drugs; concurrent active malignancy (with the exception of early-stage cancers such as superficial esophageal or head and neck cancers that are not considered to affect prognosis, or intraepithelial/intramucosal lesions judged to be cured by local therapy); pregnancy or suspected pregnancy; and any condition deemed by the investigator to render the patient unsuitable for trial participation by the investigator. Treatment

In both arms, the choice of ICI—either pembrolizumab (200 mg on day 1 every 3 weeks) or nivolumab (480 mg on day 1 every 4 weeks)—is at the discretion of the treating physician. ICIs are administered in combination with fluorouracil (800 mg/m2 on days 1-5) and cisplatin (80 mg/m2 on day 1) in each cycle. In arm B, physicians may select RT alone or RT combined with FP chemotherapy (fluorouracil 700 mg/m2 on days 1-4 and cisplatin 70 mg/m2 on day 1). The prescribed RT dose ranges from 30 to 40 Gy, with flexibility in fractionation and treatment intervals permitted. Subsequent FP plus ICI administration following RT or CRT is identical to the regimen used in arm A. Although these regimens are recommended, dose and schedule modifications are allowed to accommodate the pragmatic design of the trial. Treatment will be continued until confirmed disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, patient withdrawal, or, for patients in arm A, a switch to RT/CRT due to worsening dysphagia.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint is dysphagia relief rate at 9 weeks from the initiation of treatment. Dysphagia relief rate is defined as the proportion of patients whose baseline DS improves by at least one point. The DS was assessed by the treating physician based on interviews regarding allowable diet texture and actual oral intake during hospitalization, using a predefined assessment manual combining allowable diet texture and DS. To minimize inter-observer variability, the same physician assessed DS both before initiation of protocol treatment and at 9 weeks (patient-reported outcomes are not used). Secondary endpoints include time to dysphagia relief, defined as the time from initiation of protocol treatment to the first occurrence of an improvement of at least one point in DS from baseline; dysphagia progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time to the first occurrence of a worsening of at least one point in DS; PFS, defined as the time from initiation of protocol treatment to radiographic or clinical disease progression, including worsening of dysphagia requiring treatment switching to RT/CRT in arm A, or death from any cause; time to treatment failure, defined as the time to disease progression, treatment discontinuation due to intolerance or adverse events, treatment discontinuation or modification at the discretion of the patient or physician, or death from any cause; and OS. OS was defined as the time from initiation of protocol treatment to death from any cause, with censoring at the date of last confirmed survival. They will be estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the median values along with their 95% CIs will be calculated. Additional secondary endpoints include the ORR, disease control rate, and safety assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version.5.0.

The primary and secondary endpoint will be analyzed in the full analysis set. A supplementary analysis of the primary endpoint will be conducted in the per-protocol set.

Planned sample size and study period

The planned sample size is 27 patients in each arm. The study started in December 2024 and the estimated study completion date is December 2027.

Statistical analysis

This trial is non-comparative two-arms design, and dysphagia relief rate in each arm will be evaluated in comparison with the previous reported results of RT/CRT. The threshold value of 35% corresponds to dysphagia relief rate at 9 weeks in the RT group of the TROG 03.01 trial. The expected value of 60% was calculated by adding a 15% increase in the ORR attributable to ICIs, based on the results of the KEYNOTE-590 and CheckMate 648 trials, to the 45% of dysphagia relief rate at 9 weeks in the CRT group of the TROG 03.01 trial. Considering that this study targets patients with DS ≥2, that the enrolled population may have a poorer general condition compared with previous phase III trials, and that the pragmatic study design may result in treatment effects differing from those previously reported, however, both the threshold and expected values were reduced by 5%. Therefore, the threshold and expected value for the primary endpoint in each treatment arm were set at 30% and 55%, respectively. To achieve 80% power with a one-sided alpha level of 5%, 24 patients per arm were required, and assuming a 10% dropout, the target sample size was set at 27 patients per arm.

Sensitivity analyses will be carried out to assess the robustness of the primary findings. Dysphagia relief rates will be evaluated after stratification by DS (2 versus 3-4) and PS (0-1 versus 2) to examine consistency across clinically relevant subgroups. In addition, analyses will be conducted using both the full analysis and per-protocol populations to assess the impact of analysis set definitions.

Limitations

First, the evaluation of treatment efficacy in this study is based on comparisons with historical controls, and differences in patient characteristics and treatment era may have influenced the results.

Second, the primary endpoint—improvement in dysphagia—is assessed by physicians, and inter-observer variability and expectation bias cannot be fully excluded in this multicenter study. The absence of patient-reported outcomes is another limitation in the interpretation of dysphagia-related outcomes.

Third, because no prior data exist on dysphagia relief rates with ICI-containing regimens, the expected value for the statistical assumptions was extrapolated from previously reported improvements in ORR associated with the addition of ICIs. As the primary tumor is not considered a measurable lesion, ORR with ICIs does not necessarily correlate directly with improvement in dysphagia. We hypothesized, however, that clinically meaningful improvement in dysphagia would be achievable when tumor shrinkage of ≥30% is obtained at the primary site, similar to metastatic lesions.

Accordingly, the analyses in this study are exploratory, however, by focusing on the important unmet need of severe dysphagia, this study is expected to provide useful real-world data applicable to clinical practice.

Conclusion

Findings from the KEYNOTE-590 and CheckMate 648 trials have demonstrated promising antitumor activity of ICIs in patients with metastatic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma who do not present with severe dysphagia. The IMPULSE trial was therefore designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of FP combined with an ICI, either alone or following RT/CRT, in patients with metastatic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma complicated by severe dysphagia. The results of this study are expected to provide valuable evidence to guide treatment strategies for this high-risk patient population, which is typically associated with poor prognosis.
