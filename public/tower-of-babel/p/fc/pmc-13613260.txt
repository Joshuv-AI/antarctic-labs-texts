Minimal Important Change and Minimal Clinically Important Difference in Pain and Function With Exercise in Hip Osteoarthritis
Authors: Yareni Guerrero, Fiona Dobson, Venkatesha Venkatesha, Kim Allison, Gabrielle Knox, Libby Spiers, Travis Haber, David J. Hunter, Michelle Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to estimate the minimal important change (MIC) and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for pain and physical function in individuals with hip osteoarthritis (OA) following a physiotherapist‐guided exercise intervention.MethodsSecondary analysis from a randomized controlled trial of 196 adults with hip OA allocated one of two nine‐month exercise programs. Patient‐reported outcomes measures for hip pain severity (Numeric Rating Scale [NRS], Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index [WOMAC] pain subscale) and physical function (WOMAC physical function subscale, Patient‐Specific Functional Scale [PSFS]) were collected at baseline and three and nine months. Global ratings of change in pain and physical function at three and nine months served as anchors.ResultsMIC estimates were 2.1 and 2.4 points for NRS pain at three and nine months, respectively; 2.8 and 3.0 points for WOMAC pain at three and nine months, respectively; 8.7 and 8.3 points for WOMAC physical function at three and nine months, respectively; and −2.1 and −2.0 points for PSFS at three and nine months, respectively. The MCID estimates were 2.0 and 2.4 points for NRS pain at three and nine months, respectively; 2.8 and 3.0 points for WOMAC pain at three and nine months, respectively; 9.2 and 8.3 points for WOMAC physical function at three and nine months, respectively; and −3.5 and −0.7 points for PSFS at three and nine months, respectively.ConclusionThis study provides robust, context‐specific MIC and MCID estimates for outcomes in hip OA following exercise. These values can inform the interpretation and design of exercise‐based clinical trials for hip OA.

INTRODUCTION

Hip osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent and disabling condition affecting approximately 10% of adults over the age of 60 in high‐income countries. 1 Clinical guidelines recommend exercise as a core treatment to manage hip OA. 2 Clinicians and researchers rely on patient‐reported outcome measures to assess treatment efficacy and guide therapeutic decision‐making. However, statistical significance alone does not indicate whether an observed change is meaningful to patients. Therefore, there is a need for patient‐informed thresholds to aid clinical interpretation.

Interpreting patient‐reported outcomes requires understanding whether observed changes are meaningful to patients. The minimal important change (MIC) refers to the smallest within‐person change perceived as important, 3 whereas the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) represents the smallest between‐group difference considered clinically meaningful. 4 Existing MIC and MCID estimates for hip OA are limited and largely derived from surgical 5 , 6 and pharmacological interventions, 4 in which the magnitude of clinical improvement is typically greater than that expected from exercise‐based interventions. These thresholds are often higher because patients undergoing surgery or receiving pharmacotherapy anticipate and experience larger symptom improvement compared with behavioral or exercise programs. 7 , 8 Therefore, applying these higher thresholds to exercise trials may underestimate clinically meaningful benefit. This highlights the need for context‐specific estimates for hip OA exercise interventions.

In hip OA, pain is commonly assessed using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) 9 pain subscale and the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). 10 Physical function is often evaluated using the Patient‐Specific Functional Scale (PSFS) 11 and WOMAC 9 physical function subscale. One study has reported MCID values for exercise in knee OA, 12 but these are not necessarily generalized to hip OA given differences between the conditions. 13 In hip OA, only two studies have reported MIC estimates, with 1 point on the NRS and 3.0 to 6.8 points on the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS) function subscale in digitally delivered and group‐based programs (noting that the HOOS function subscale is equivalent to the WOMAC physical function subscale). 14 , 15 Importantly, no prior studies have provided MIC or MCID estimates derived specifically from physiotherapist‐guided exercise. As a result, hip OA exercise trials often rely on thresholds developed in nonexercise contexts or other OA populations, which may not reflect the expected magnitude or nature of benefit in home‐based exercise programs. Given that the MIC and MCID are sensitive to interventions and comparators and are context specific, 3 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 the aim of this study is to estimate MIC and MCID for pain (NRS and WOMAC pain subscale) and physical function (WOMAC physical function subscale and PSFS) in people with hip OA following an exercise program with follow‐up at three and nine months.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This is a secondary analysis from a superiority, two‐group comparative effectiveness randomized controlled trial in Australia. The trial protocol 20 and primary outcomes 21 were previously reported. Ethics approval was obtained by The University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (12710). This trial was registered in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN: 12619001297112) and has been completed.

Participants (n = 196) with symptomatic hip OA were recruited between October 2019 and September 2022 from the community across metropolitan Melbourne, Australia. Key eligibility criteria included clinically diagnosed hip OA, 22 NRS pain ≥4 of 10, the ability to safely participate in exercise, and exclusion of individuals scheduled for or awaiting hip arthroplasty. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed elsewhere. 20

Participants were randomized to either a combination of aerobic physical activity and resistance exercise or resistance exercise only. Both groups received nine individual online consultations with a physiotherapist over three months and continued their program independently thereafter. All participants were prescribed a home‐based progressive lower limb resistance exercise program, and the combined exercise group was prescribed moderate‐intensity aerobic physical activity targeting 150 min/wk. Further details of the exercise programs can be found elsewhere. 20 , 21 The patient‐reported outcome measures were obtained at baseline and three and nine months. Hip pain was assessed via NRS (0–10), in which 0 = no pain and 10 = worst pain possible, 23 and the WOMAC pain subscale (0–20), in which higher scores indicate more pain. 9 Physical function was assessed via the WOMAC function subscale (0–68), in which 0 = no difficulty and 68 = extreme difficulties. 9 For the PSFS, 11 at baseline, participants listed up to five daily living activities for which performance was limited by hip symptoms and rated these on a 0 to 10 scale in which 0 = unable to perform and 10 = able to perform at the same level as before injury or problem. A total score was calculated as the sum of the ratings for each activity divided by the number of activities, with higher scores indicating better function. At three and nine months, participants were asked about the activities they listed at baseline and provided a rating on the same scale.

Participant‐perceived global ratings of change 21 in hip pain and hip‐related physical function were collected at three and nine months. For hip pain, participants were asked to “select the option which best represents the overall change in pain of your hip since you began the study,” and for hip physical function, participants were asked to “select the option which best represents the overall change in physical function of your hip since you began the study.” Responses for each were scored using a 7‐point Likert scale, where 1 = much worse, 2 = moderately worse, 3 = slightly worse, 4 = no change, 5 = slightly better, 6 = moderately better, and 7 = much better. In line with recommended anchor‐based methods, which specify that the anchor threshold should reflect a small but important improvement rather than a trivial fluctuation, 24 we classified “moderately better” and “much better” as “improved” and all other responses as “not improved.” We recognize that some participants may have perceived “slightly better” as important. However, because our anchor did not explicitly distinguish between trivial and important change, we chose a conservative threshold to avoid misclassifying minor fluctuations as clinically important change.

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, LLC). Continuous variables were summarized as means (SD) or medians (interquartile range), and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentage). Ceiling effects were assessed by identifying participants who scored at the best possible value at baseline. Change scores were calculated as baseline minus follow‐up. For outcomes in which higher scores indicate worse symptoms (NRS pain, WOMAC pain, WOMAC physical function), positive change scores represent improvement, and negative scores represent worsening. For the PSFS, in which higher scores indicate better function, negative change scores represent improvement and positive scores represent worsening. An anchor‐based approach based on a predictive model using logistic regression analysis was used for MIC estimates. 3 A dichotomized anchor is the dependent variable, and the change score is the independent variable. The MIC was determined as the value of the change score corresponding to a predicted probability of 0.5 for reporting minimal improvement. To enhance robustness, bootstrapping with 1,000 replications was used to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the MIC estimate as previously described. 25 The MCID compares the magnitude of change in outcome scores between responders and nonresponders, as determined by an anchor. It is estimated as the difference in mean change scores between responder and nonresponder groups. This approach reflects between‐group minimal meaningful change by contrasting patients who experienced clinically significant improvement with those who did not. Complete methodologic estimates can be accessed in the Supplementary Material.

DISCUSSION

This study estimates both MIC and MCID for recommended outcomes in individuals with hip OA following a nine‐month home‐based exercise program guided by a physiotherapist during the first three months. Exercise is the core recommended treatment to manage hip OA, 2 , 22 yet interpretation of outcomes remains largely statistically driven. Our findings provide context‐specific thresholds for interpreting clinically meaningful within‐ and between‐group change in hip OA following home‐based exercise guided by physiotherapists.

Our MIC estimates for pain on the NRS (2.2 points at three months and 2.4 points at nine months) and WOMAC pain subscale (2.8 points at three months and 3.0 points at nine months) are higher than other MIC estimates in people with hip OA using digitally delivered exercise (1.0 points on NRS pain; 0–10 scale) 15 and group‐based exercise (1.6 points on WOMAC pain subscale; 0–20 scale). 14 Similarly, our MIC estimates for physical function on the WOMAC physical function subscale (8.3 points at three months and 8.7 points at nine months) were higher than other MIC estimates in people with hip OA using digitally delivered exercise (4.6 points on WOMAC physical function subscale; 0–68 scale) 15 and group‐based exercise (2.0 points on WOMAC physical function subscale; 0–68 scale). 14 These differences may reflect variation in intervention type and delivery method (individualized online session vs digital app/groups based). Our methodologic approach 25 is consistent with previous work and is therefore unlikely to explain why our estimates are higher. One potential explanation for higher MIC estimates is the small between‐group treatment effects observed in the parent trial (eg, NRS pain: 0.3 [95% CI −0.3 to 0.8] at three months, 0.0 [95% CI −0.7 to 0.6] at nine months; WOMAC physical function: −0.9 [95% CI −3.6 to 1.8] at three months, −1.8 [95% CI −4.6 to −0.5] at nine months). The MIC estimates for PSFS were 2 units at both time points, within the range of 1.0 to 2.5 reported in musculoskeletal conditions other than hip OA. 26 Overall, these findings provide the first physiotherapist‐guided, home‐based, context‐specific MIC estimates for hip OA and reinforce the need for setting‐specific thresholds.

This study extends previous research by providing exercise‐specific MCID estimates for pain and physical function in people with hip OA. To our knowledge, no prior studies have reported MCID estimates for hip OA following exercise. Our MCID estimates for NRS pain (2.0 and 2.4 points for three and nine months, respectively) and WOMAC physical function (8.3 and 9.2 points for three and nine months, respectively) are notably larger than estimates used to inform sample size calculations in clinical trials, 4 including our own 21 (eg, 1.8 points in NRS pain and 6 points in WOMAC physical function). The implications of larger intervention‐specific MCID estimates are that fewer participants are required to address research questions and reduce costs associated with larger sample sizes needed to detect meaningful change. Although replication is needed, these values inform more accurate effect size estimations for power calculations in future hip OA exercise interventions. The MCID for PSFS was 3.5 points at three months but was less stable at nine months (0.7 points). This was likely due to poor correlation to the anchor (Supplementary Table 2), possibly shifting patient priorities or difficulty recalling baseline limitations, bringing into question the PSFS's utility in this context.

Contemporary methodologic and regulatory guidance recommends combining anchor‐based estimates with complementary approaches and, where possible, reporting multiple thresholds rather than a single MCID value. Current best practice also encourages incorporating patient‐centered alternatives such as the smallest worthwhile effect 27 and the patient acceptable symptom state, 28 which offer conceptually distinct insights into what patients consider meaningful or acceptable. The smallest worthwhile effect was not feasible in the current study because the necessary benefit‐burden trade‐off judgment was not collected. Integrating such approaches in future trials would strengthen patient‐centered interpretation. Importantly, MCID estimates remain central to clinical trial design, sample size justification, and interpretation of treatment effects. As Revicki et al 29 articulate, meaningful thresholds should be refined cumulatively across studies and clinical contexts. Our MCID estimates provide new evidence in hip OA and exercise and offer an alternative to inappropriately extrapolating MCID estimates from surgical, pharmacological, or knee OA studies.

The key strength of this study is its focus on hip OA, which is often underrepresented in OA research or pooled with knee OA, and assessment at multiple time points. A further strength is that the outcomes examined (eg, pain and self‐reported physical function) align with Osteoarthritis Research Society International clinical trial recommendations. 30 Limitations should be considered. First, our eligibility criteria (eg, NRS ≥4 of 10, ability to exercise safely, exclusion of those awaiting arthroplasty) means our sample reflects people with hip OA typically receiving conservative management but may not represent those with very mild or advanced hip OA. Second, MIC and MCID estimates are inherently context dependent and influenced by population characteristics such as baseline symptoms severity, measurement properties, and the methodologic approaches used. 3 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 As such, our resulting thresholds are specific to this physiotherapist‐guided, home‐based exercise program and may not generalize to digital, group‐based, center‐based, or other delivery modalities or patient groups. An important consideration of our study is the exclusive use of anchor‐based methods to estimate clinically important change. Although widely used, anchor‐base thresholds are influenced by sample characteristics, baseline symptom severity, and the choice of anchor, which may restrict their generalizability across settings and patient groups. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Global rating of change scales are susceptible to recall bias and current health‐state bias, particularly at longer follow‐up, which may inflate or attenuate MIC and MCID estimates. Third, although our predictive modeling approach helps mitigate bias, the classification of “responders” may still be compromised. Dichotomizing global rating of change responses into “improved” versus “not improved” can oversimplify patient experience, and the relatively wide CIs for NRS pain at nine months further suggest instability in long‐term estimates. In addition, the PSFS demonstrated weak correlation with the anchor at nine months, indicating limited stability of this measure for long‐term MIC or MCID estimation. Finally, generating a single MIC/MCID point estimate for ordinal measures (eg, WOMAC) is inherently limited, as equal numerical changes do not represent equivalent clinical change across the continuum of the scale.

This study provides context‐specific MIC and MCID values for pain and physical function outcomes in hip OA following a home‐based exercise program prescribed according to widely used guidelines. These estimates enhance clinical interpretation and offer empirically supported parameters for future exercise‐based trials.
