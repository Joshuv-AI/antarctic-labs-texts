Multivariate Regression With Dependence Structures: Evaluating Associations Between Plasma Metabolomics and Alcohol Intake in Older Adults
Authors: Yifan Yang, Chixiang Chen, Hwiyoung Lee, Ming Wang, Yuan Zhang, Shuo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐dimensional omics data often exhibit complex yet organized dependencies, characterized by intelligent network properties like high modularity, small‐worldness characteristics, and scale‐free topology. However, integrating these structured interdependencies between omics variables into multivariate regression models presents challenges. The primary difficulty lies in accurately specifying and estimating dependency parameters that capture these network patterns within regression frameworks. Common covariance estimation methods may not preserve these network properties and can also be computationally intensive. To address these challenges, we propose a novel multivariate regression model that incorporates an interconnected community structure, reflecting the organized relationships among omics outcome variables. Our approach includes efficient estimation algorithms, featuring closed‐form regression estimators and likelihood‐based dependence estimators. We also establish the asymptotic properties of estimators to ensure theoretical robustness and hypothesis testing. Extensive simulations demonstrate the enhanced accuracy and sensitivity of our method, as evidenced through benchmarking against existing regression models. We applied our approach to a dataset to assess the associations between 249 metabolomic biomarkers, measured using nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, and alcohol intake among 3984 participants. Results indicate that light alcohol consumption is positively associated with high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL, i.e., the good cholesterol), high‐density lipoprotein particles, and Apolipoproteins A1, indicators linked to cardiovascular health.Keywords: autoregressive regression, interconnected community structure, multivariate dependent outcomes, nuclear magnetic resonance data

Introduction

Simultaneous measurement of hundreds of thousands of biological features has revealed complex scientific mechanisms across various fields [1, 2]. Numerous statistical methodologies have been developed to address the challenges associated with high‐dimensional data analysis. For instance, shrinkage or penalty techniques have been employed in linear regression models, leading to the establishment of theoretical properties for the sparse estimators [3]. Analogous regularization methods have successfully extended to estimate large‐scale covariance or precision matrices, incorporating the assumption of bandability, sparsity, or low rank [4, 5, 6]. From an inference perspective, multiplicity‐adjusted procedures have been proposed to enable the simultaneous testing of numerous hypotheses, irrespective of whether the underlying test statistics are independent [7] or heavily correlated [8].

Conventionally, statistical methods handling high‐dimensional variables can be broadly categorized into two classes. In the first class, high‐dimensional variables are considered as predictors. Penalty‐based regression methods, for example, the lasso [9] and its many variants [1, 10], are utilized to select the variables. In the second class, high‐dimensional features are treated as outcomes (of regression). For instance, high‐dimensional imaging data are often modeled as outcomes while considering spatial dependence. Additionally, multiple testing methods widely employed in omics data analysis fall into this category [8]. In this paper, our emphasis is on the second category of data analysis. Our research is motivated by the controversial debate regarding the health effects of alcohol consumption [11, 12, 13]. Plasma metabolomics can provide insight into alcohol consumption levels within the context of a healthy lifestyle [14]. Hence, we aim to assess the association between metabolomic profiles and alcohol intake in the older populations using data from the UK Biobank, a large‐scale prospective cohort study that offers high‐quality biomarker measurements and detailed lifestyle information [15]. Specifically, our goal is to assess the associations between multivariate outcomes (i.e., measurements of metabolomic biomarkers) and alcohol intake, while accounting for the covariates and the structured dependence patterns of biomarkers. This is achieved by incorporating a dependence parameter matrix into the regression model, thereby enhancing the accuracy of statistical inferences.

Incorporating the dependence structure of omics data into a multivariate regression model presents a twofold challenge. First, it is difficult to specify a parametric model with dependence coefficients that accurately reflect the latent and intricate interdependencies among omics variables. Analyses of co‐expression network data and multi‐omics data [16, 17] demonstrate that the intercorrelations between omics variables form complex structures with advanced network properties such as high modularity, small‐worldness characteristics, and scale‐free topology. These complex relationships go beyond the commonly assumed independent community structures [18, 19], where network modularity is addressed, but the parameters in the covariance structures are insufficient to represent the small‐worldness characteristics, because nodes in different communities cannot be connected through a few links. Consequently, existing multivariate regression models struggle to accommodate these complex network structures [20, 21, 22, 23]. Second, it is difficult to efficiently and accurately estimate the parametric covariance matrix in the multivariate regression model when dealing with a large number of omics variables. Traditional methods often face heavy computational burdens due to the complexity of large‐scale matrix inversion algorithms, for example, Fisher scoring [22, 23] or iterative procedures, for example, feasible generalized least squares (FGLS) [24]. The inclusion of more sophisticated covariance structures further increases computational demands, making these methods less practical for general end‐users.

To address the aforementioned challenge, we propose the multivariate autoregressive regression model with uniform‐block dependence (MAUD), which incorporates an interconnected community structure to capture the complex interdependencies among outcome variables within a multivariate regression model. Interconnected community structures extend the independent community structures by modeling the connections between communities, thereby preserving small‐worldness and scale‐free properties. These structures, although often latent, are prevalent in the co‐expression patterns of various types of omics data [25, 26], as illustrated in Figure 1. Recent advancements in network structure detection can reliably and consistently unveil interconnected community structures from data [27, 28, 29]. In light of these findings, we incorporate the interconnected community structure‐based dependencies between multivariate outcomes into the MAUD and develop efficient estimation and inference methods.

The MAUD offers noteworthy contributions in three key aspects. First, improved model interpretability. The MAUD introduces new dependence parameters in the multivariate regression model that reflect the interconnected community structure of multivariate outcomes, such as co‐expression patterns found in omics data. This model specification not only enhances the characterization of interdependencies among outcome variables but also preserves the structure during subsequent matrix operations (e.g., squaring or inverting matrices) involved in likelihood‐based parameter estimation. Second, computational efficiency. We have developed computationally efficient methods for estimating both regression and dependence parameters. In practice, computations involving thousands of outcomes are completed (on average) in a matter of seconds rather than hours, making the MAUD an attractive toolkit for general users. Third, reliable hypothesis testing. We derive a closed‐form exact (or asymptotic) covariance matrix for the regression (or dependence) estimator, which facilitates fast Wald‐type hypothesis tests.

This paper proceeds with Section 2.1, where we introduce the proposed MAUD for multivariate outcomes. Section 2.2 details the MAUD for features within communities and establishes the relationship between the community structure and the covariance matrix of marginal outcomes. Section 2.3 outlines the estimation procedure along with its theoretical properties, and Section 2.4 discusses hypothesis tests. In Section 3, we conduct simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed methodology. We then apply the proposed method to real data in Section 4 and provide a comprehensive discussion in Section 5. All proofs, additional figures, and tables can be found in the Supporting Information.

Methods

A Multivariate Regression Model With Dependence Structure: MAUD

Before introducing the MAUD, we begin with the definitions of interconnected community structure and the singletons. Based on whether the outcome features are singletons, the MAUD can be separated into two components: the community model, which contains all features within communities, and the singleton model, which contains the features outside communities, that is, the singletons. Throughout the paper, we use the notation ∗ to emphasize a variable associated with the singletons.

Let Y∗=y1∗,…,yn∗ represent the S by n outcome feature matrix, where yi∗=yi1,…,yiS⊤, i=1,…,n, are n independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies of the S‐dimensional features in outcomes y∗=(y1,…,yS)⊤, and ⊤ denotes the transpose of a vector or matrix. We assume that the covariance structure of y∗ follows an interconnected community structure with singletons, as illustrated in Figure 3B. More precisely, we suppose that there are R<S features forming G interconnected communities and refer to the remaining (S−R) features as the singletons. Except for the singletons, the (population version) interconnected community structure in Figure 3C exhibits that: (1) any two features r1 and r2 within community g are coherently (and positively) correlated, for example, coryr1,yr2 is a positive constant depending on g only; and (2) communities g and g′ can further be interconnected, denoted by g∼g′, for example, coryr,yr′≠0 for any feature r in community g and any feature r′ in community g′, depending on g and g′ only.

Thus, a structure of interconnected communities with singletons implies a community assignment function ϕ∗:{1,2,…,S}→{0,1,…,G}: these R features within communities can be assigned to G mutually exclusive and exhaustive groups, that is, ϕ∗(r)=1 for r∈{r1,1,…,r1,L1}⊆{1,…,S}, ϕ∗(r)=2 for r∈{r2,1,…,r2,L2}⊆{1,…,S}, and so forth, ϕ∗(r)=G for r∈{rG,1,…,rG,LG}⊆{1,…,S}; while the singletons outside communities are assigned to a “null” group, ϕ∗(r)=0 for r∈{1,2,…,S}/∪g=1G{rg,1,…,rg,Lg}, where we let Lg>1 denote the cardinality of community g for g=1,…,G, as illustrated in Figure 3C. Moreover, we let L0 denote the number of singletons, as illustrated in Figure 3B, such that S=R+L0 with R=L1+⋯+LG. For the sake of simplicity, we suppose that the first R outcome features are successively categorized into G communities, that is, r1,1=1, …, r1,L1=L1, r2,1=L1+1, …, r2,L2=L1+L2, and so forth, rG,1=R−LG+1, …, rG,LG=R throughout the paper.

(Community assignment) A community assignment function ϕ∗, or equivalently, a community assignment of outcome features, is assumed to be pre‐determined. In practice, the proposed model accommodates flexible grouping of outcome variables, as long as the communities are well‐defined (i.e., mutually exclusive and exhaustive). In real‐world applications, three types of community detection methods are most commonly used. First, in some cases, community assignments are naturally defined based on experimental design or variable assignments, without the need for any community detection algorithm. Second, community partitioning can be learned using clustering algorithms such as K‐medoids [36]. The main advantage is its ability to produce stable community assignments; however, it requires a pre‐specified number of communities. Third, statistical inference for covariate‐related subnetworks (SICERS) [29] automatically identifies groups of outcome variables without requiring a pre‐specified number of communities. It is particularly well‐suited for extracting interconnected community structures from data. For further details on the community assignment, please refer to the Supporting Information.

Model (1) extends conventional autoregressive models that are popularly adopted in multivariate analysis and neuroimaging studies [22, 23] by incorporating a generalized framework that accounts for community‐wise dependence. Conventional statistical inference methods for the MAUD present challenges. It frequently necessitates iterative algorithms, for example, the FGLS method [24], to separately estimate regression coefficients and dependence parameters [37, 38]. However, for regression coefficient estimation, explicit variance estimation or the finite‐sample properties of an FGLS estimator are generally intractable [24]. Meanwhile, the Fisher scoring method can be computationally expensive [22, 23]. For dependence parameter estimation, directly estimating a high‐dimensional covariance matrix based on the residuals is essentially difficult due to certain constraints in dependence parameters (please refer to Remark 6 for details). To address these issues, one of our contributions is the development of a fast and computationally efficient inference approach.

(High‐dimensional issue) A classical estimation approach (e.g., the FGLS approach) typically requires a consistent estimate for the S by S covariance matrix of y∗ in the intermediate steps. Two conventional definitions of a consistent covariance‐matrix estimator are discussed [39, 40]. One element‐wisely defines a consistent covariance‐matrix estimator, using consistent estimators for S(S+1)/2 elements. The other one defines it as a plug‐in estimator when the covariance matrix depends on a fixed number d of parameters. Regardless of the definition used, for a large‐scale unstructured covariance matrix, d is as large as S(S+1)/2, leading to a high‐dimensional issue due to n<p+d.

(Separate analysis) We observe that the MAUD in (1) for the singletons (i.e., the singleton model) simplifies to a general linear model. Thus, instead of following the traditional autoregression scheme, we aim to conduct separate statistical inferences about the parameters in model (1). For the singletons, we address the MAUD using the conventional approaches [20, 21]. For the features within communities, we reformulate the MAUD in matrix form, which will be detailed in the following sections.

Simulation Studies

In this section, we conduct Monte Carlo simulation studies to evaluate the performance of MAUD in terms of parameter estimation and statistical inference about both dependence parameters and regression coefficients. We also compare its performance against several competing linear regression models. These include the autoregressive model for multivariate block‐diagonal outcomes (AMBD), which estimates γgg for g=1,…,G while assuming γgg′=0 for g≠g′; and the mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) [43], which models ∑ using first‐order autoregressive dependences. Additionally, since many differential expression analyses of omics data traditionally rely on univariate methods that ignore dependence, we include the multivariate linear regression model (MLRM) [21] for comparison. Without loss of generality, we focus on the MAUD for features within communities, as all models handle the singletons identically. Finally, we perform a sensitivity analysis for the MAUD under model misspecification when the underlying covariance matrix does not have the interconnected community structure.

Evaluation of (Scaled) Dependence Parameter Estimation by MAUD

For each replicated dataset, we employ the MAUD to estimate β and γ using (9). We aim to assess the accuracy of the MAUD in estimating the vector of (scaled) dependence parameters γ. To evaluate the performance of estimator γ^, we utilize several evaluation metrics, including the average bias among Monte Carlo replicates (denoted as “average bias”), the Monte Carlo standard deviation (denoted as “MCSD”), the average asymptotic standard error (denoted as “ASE”), and the 95% coverage probability based on the Wald‐type confidence interval (denoted as “95% CP”) for each element of γ. Evaluation of Regression Coefficient Inference by MAUD

We further conduct an assessment of the statistical inference regarding the regression coefficients using the MAUD and compare it with models of AMBD, MMRM, and MLRM. Our primary focus is on identifying a subset of features associated with the covariates. It is worth noting that a more accurate estimate of ∑β^ in (11) always implies more precise results in hypothesis testing. To achieve this, we initially analyze the relative loss of ∑β^, defined as ‖∑^β^−∑β^‖/‖∑β^‖, and generate boxplots of relative losses for all models under various settings in Figure 2A, utilizing the Frobenius norm and the spectral norm. We then calculate rejection proportions for components of β and present them in the Supporting Information.

Overall, as illustrated in Figure 2A, the MAUD exhibits the lowest relative loss among all models across various settings, aligning with the most precise inference results detailed in the Supporting Information. The AMBD demonstrates the highest relative loss due to the inability of its parameter estimation procedure to accommodate cases where the covariance matrix ∑ deviates from the block diagonal structure. Notably, both the MMRM and the MLRM show slightly lower but almost uniform relative losses compared to the AMBD. This can be attributed to our specification of ∑ with an autocorrelation AR(1) structure in the MMRM and a diagonal structure in the MLRM.

Misspecification Analysis for MAUD

In the last two studies, we operated under the assumption of a known interconnected community structure for the MAUD. However, in practical scenarios, the latent covariance structure may deviate from the interconnected community structure. Considering this, we assess the robustness of the MAUD under potential structural misspecifications.

To evaluate this, we introduce a perturbation term Eσ∼WishartR,σIR, where noise level σ∈{0,3,6,9}×10−2 and Eσ=0R×R for σ=0. We generate simulated datasets 1000 times, incorporating the covariance matrix ∑=∑A∑,B∑,ℓ+Eσ under the setting of n=50, G=3, R=100, and ℓ=(30,30,40)⊤.

For each dataset, we compute estimates for β and ∑β^. Then, we similarly calculate the relative losses and present the resulting boxplots in Figure 2B based on all replicates. We label the results as “0 level”, “3 level”, “6 level”, and “9 level” corresponding to the respective levels of σ.

As anticipated, the boxplots in Figure 2B illustrate increases in relative loss of ∑β^ as the noise level becomes larger. We note that if σ≠0, then the average of random matrix Eσ equals σRIR∈{3IR,6IR,9IR}. In other words, the elements of Eσ approximate the same order of magnitude as those of the true covariance matrix. The results in Figure 2B exhibit that the relative loss just doubles when the interconnected community structure is disrupted by noise of comparable magnitude. This suggests that the MAUD can effectively handle mild to moderate levels of noise in the covariance structure.

Evaluation of Associations Between Plasma Metabolomics and Alcohol Intake in Older Adults

Motivation: A healthy lifestyle is associated with a lower risk of premature death and greater longevity, as reflected in plasma metabolomic profiles [13]. Compared to younger adults, older individuals over the age of 65 are at a higher risk of adverse outcomes [44]. However, the role of alcohol consumption within a lifestyle remains a topic of debate [11, 12, 13]. To explore the impact of alcohol consumption on plasma metabolomics in older adults, we leverage the comprehensive UK Biobank dataset, moving beyond small‐scale case‐control studies, to provide a more comprehensive understanding and ultimately promote better health outcomes.

Data description: We applied our model to a dataset from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) plasma metabolomics. The dataset is publicly accessible through the UK Biobank and has been comprehensively described [15]. The exposure variable is alcohol intake frequency, with 912 participants reporting daily intake, 1175 ones reporting three or four times a week, 1017 ones reporting once or twice a week, 424 ones reporting one to three times a month, 300 ones reporting on special occasions only, and 156 ones reporting never consuming alcohol. Additionally, we considered the intercept, age (at the time of attending the assessment center, 63.39±7.61 in year), sex (with 1840 male and 2144 female participants), BMI (body mass index, 26.28±4.11), and heavy smoking (with 883 participants categorized as smokers and 3101 as non‐smokers) as covariates. Our primary focus was on understanding the associations between S=249 NMR metabolic biomarkers and alcohol intake, given n=3984 participants and p=6 covariates. This gives rise to a high‐dimensional problem, as discussed in Remark 2.

The interconnected community structure in NMR data: We fitted the data to a multivariate linear regression model using the least‐square method and obtained the estimated regression coefficient matrix B˜249×6∗ of B249×6∗=β1∗,…,β249∗⊤. We next calculated the residual matrix and applied the SICERS algorithm [29], because, as preliminary studies demonstrated, various omics data may exhibit high‐modularity network structures. Specifically, we applied the SICERS method to the sample covariance matrix of the residuals: S∗=n−1∑i=1nyi∗−B˜249×6∗xiyi∗−B˜249×6∗xi⊤, where yi∗∈ℝ249×1 and xi∈ℝ6×1, yielding a community assignment function ϕ˜∗. After reordering the biomarkers according to ϕ˜∗, the correlation matrix of the residuals revealed that R=170 biomarkers are categorized into G=5 interconnected communities with sizes ℓ=(L1,L2,L3,L4,L5)⊤=(77,47,19,11,16)⊤. The remaining L0=79 biomarkers were found to be isolated (singletons), depicted in the heatmap in Figure 3B. Furthermore, we created the boxplots of community‐wise and community‐singleton correlations, as shown in Figure 3E. These boxplots demonstrate that complex interdependence relationships exist between communities, while variables within communities are nearly independent of the singletons (outside communities). Additionally, we conducted the separate analysis in Remark 3 to obtain the estimated regression coefficient matrix B^249×6∗ based on B˜249×6∗, by arranging its rows according to the reordered biomarkers. In other words, the first R=170 rows of B^249×6∗ correspond to the estimates for the biomarkers in the interconnected communities, while the last L0=79 rows represent the estimates for the singletons. The names of the biomarkers, the community membership, and B^249×6∗ are available in the Supporting Information.

Statistical inference about B249×6∗: Utilizing the detected interconnected community structure, we aim to conduct statistical inference about the regression coefficient matrix B249×6∗ (reordered according to ϕ˜∗) separately. Following Remark 3, we partitioned B^249×6∗ into two submatrices: one comprising its initial 170 rows and the other containing the remaining 79 rows. We then estimated the standard errors for the elements of the first submatrix using the proposed approach and for the elements of the second submatrix using the classical approach. The detailed results can be found in the Supporting Information. Subsequently, we proceeded to identify alcohol intake‐related NMR biomarkers based on the inference outcomes at the false discovery rate (FDR) level of 0.05. The results of the numbers of biomarkers along with their signs of estimated regression coefficients are presented in Table 2. Additionally, we illustrated the 95% confidence intervals for (partial) regression coefficients in Figure 4. In contrast, we also performed the MLRM and conducted inference for B249×6∗ (reordered according to ϕ˜∗). The inference results are available in the Supporting Information.

Results: Our findings reveal significant positive associations between alcohol intake and both high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and Apolipoprotein A1 (Apo A1) at the FDR level of 0.05. Additionally, we observed a significant negative association between alcohol consumption and the Apolipoprotein B (Apo B) to Apo A1 ratio. Apo A1, a primary structural protein in HDL, serves as an indicator of anti‐atherosclerotic function, whereas elevated levels of Apo B are linked to a higher cardiovascular disease risk. Clinically, the Apo B to Apo A1 ratio is commonly used as a metric to assess cardiovascular disease risks. Previous studies have suggested that a low to moderate amount of alcohol intake can increase HDL‐C levels and lower the Apo B to Apo A1 ratio, reducing risks of cardiovascular diseases [45, 46]. One plausible mechanism is that this increase in HDL‐C levels may result from an enhanced transport rate of Apo A1 associated with low to moderate alcohol consumption [47]. A recent study, accounting for risk factors, examined the association between the Apo B to Apo A1 ratio and various cardiometabolic disease risks [48]. Our findings indicate that, after adjusting for risk factors and accounting for biomarker interdependencies, low to moderate alcohol consumption is positively associated with HDL‐C concentrations and negatively associated with the Apo B to Apo A1 ratio, implying a potential reduction in the cardiovascular disease risk. These findings may reveal a metabolic pathway linking low to moderate alcohol consumption with a healthy lifestyle.

Furthermore, we found significantly positive associations between alcohol intake and high‐density lipoprotein particles (HDL‐P), the average diameter for HDL‐P, and concentrations across all HDL‐P sizes (small, medium, large, and very large) at the FDR level of 0.05. Compared to HDL‐C, HDL‐P has been shown to have a stronger negative association with cardiovascular disease risks [49]. However, the relationship between alcohol intake and HDL‐P is nuanced. For example, alcohol consumption exhibited positive associations with medium and large HDL‐P but exhibited a negative association with small HDL‐P [50]. After adjusting for risk factors and considering biomarker dependencies, our results indicate a consistent association between low to moderate alcohol consumption and HDL‐P sizes. Specifically, the coefficients increase from 0.14 to 0.18 for small and medium HDL‐P, then decrease to 0.10 and 0.06 for large and very large HDL‐P.

Discussion

We have introduced a novel multivariate regression technique, the MAUD, designed to jointly analyze correlated multivariate outcomes. In comparison to the conventional linear regression approaches that treat each outcome separately, the MAUD can effectively enhance statistical inference, that is, it leads to higher statistical power or fewer false positive findings compared to competing methodologies. This framework is broadly applicable in various omics data analyses, for example, differential expression analysis, where datasets often exhibit underlying interconnected community structures.

The MAUD is constructed using an autoregressive model while accommodating the interconnected community structure. We bridge the gap between autoregressive dependence parameters and the parameters in a large covariance model. By leveraging the interconnected community structure, we develop computationally efficient estimators for the dependence parameters in the autoregressive model. Accounting for the dependence among multivariate features in outcomes, we achieve more accurate inference, enabling precise selection of omics features. This advancement holds the potential to improve reproducibility and replicability in high‐throughput data analysis. Notably, the MAUD exhibits robustness to model misspecification, as demonstrated in our simulation studies. While we utilized the interconnected community structure for the MAUD, a more comprehensive framework can be devised to incorporate a wider array of covariance structures under the autoregressive model.

In our application to real‐world data, we found evidence suggesting that light to moderate alcohol intake is associated with the levels of “good cholesterol” (HDL) for older adults. Furthermore, our observations regarding HDL and associated pathways involving Apolipoprotein A1 align well with known biological understanding. However, caution is warranted regarding potential cancer risks associated with excessive alcohol consumption. Further analysis can be performed to identify the causal effects. Collectively, our findings offer a new perspective on alcohol intake within the realm of public health.

In conclusion, our proposed MAUD, along with its estimation and inference procedures, holds relevance and applicability in a wide spectrum of network studies characterized by an interconnected community structure.
