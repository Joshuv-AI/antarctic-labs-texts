Primary teachers’ acceptance and sustained adoption of AI powered learner corpora for writing instruction through TAM and ECM perspectives
Authors: Na Zhang, Wei Lun Wong, Jessie Barrot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) offers significant potential to enhance writing instruction in primary education. However, its sustained adoption by English language teachers remains insufficiently understood. This study examines the factors influencing teachers’ acceptance and continued use of AI-powered learner corpora, drawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM). A structured questionnaire was administered to 355 primary school English teachers in Malaysia to assess variables related to external support (e.g., facilitating conditions, teacher-learner interaction), individual characteristics (e.g., perceived self-efficacy, expectancy effects, growth mindset, interest) and technology perceptions (e.g., perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, satisfaction, continuance intention). Data were analyzed using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, followed by Structural Equation Modeling. Results highlight perceived self-efficacy and interest as key predictors of perceived usefulness and continuance intention. They underscored the role of intrinsic motivation. In contrast, facilitating conditions did not significantly affect perceived ease of use, possibly due to increased digital familiarity among teachers. These findings validate the applicability of TAM and ECM in the primary education context and offer practical insights for designing AI tools that align with teachers’ pedagogical needs, enhance user experience and support the sustainable integration of AI in writing instruction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21205-0.

Introduction

Writing proficiency remains a foundational pillar of primary education. It serves as a critical enabler of academic achievement and cognitive development1. Nonetheless, primary school learners across diverse educational contexts continue to struggle with core aspects of written composition, including limited lexical diversity, syntactic inaccuracies and fragmented discourse structures2. These persistent challenges are often attributable to traditional pedagogical practices that emphasise rote memorisation over metacognitive and strategic writing instruction. Thereby, they constrained learners’ creativity and critical engagement3. Exacerbating these issues, large class sizes frequently limit teachers’ capacity to deliver timely and individualised feedback. It is an essential driver of writing improvement4. Such structural and instructional shortcomings underscore the urgent need for innovative approaches that bridge the gap between curricular aspirations and learner performance.

Following that, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative force in education. It offers tools that deliver automated error detection, adaptive scaffolding and real-time feedback5. These affordances have the potential to empower learners through iterative revision while alleviating the feedback burden on teachers6. Despite these promises, the application of AI tools in primary school writing instruction remains limited and often misaligned with pedagogical goals. AI tools such as ChatGPT are frequently used for superficial tasks or entertainment. The superficial use reveals a disjunction between technological capability and instructional intentionality7. Poor implementation can further result in plagiarism and cognitive offloading. Thereby, it compromises the cultivation of critical thinking and independent writing skills8. This underscores the imperative to align AI affordances with developmentally appropriate and ethically grounded pedagogical frameworks.

In this present study, learner corpora as curated collections of authentic learner writing offer a promising means to humanise AI feedback. Unlike AI systems trained on generic data, learner corpora preserve the linguistic and cognitive characteristics of young learners. It captures patterns of error, stylistic variation and developmental progression9. Embedding learner corpora in AI tools links each error to peer examples. It makes feedback contexualised and prompts learners to reflect on their writing instead of simply accepting corrections10. For example, a learner grappling with tense consistency can analyse anonymised peer texts to identify and correct recurring issues. It is an approach that aligns with Vygotsky’s11 zone of proximal development through socially mediated learning. Importantly, it discourages plagiarism by centering original production within a community of practice rather than dependence on machine outputs.

Grounded in TAM, the AI-powered learner corpora translates perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use into tangible classroom routines. The researchers first uploaded 152,187 words of learner corpus in 20 files to a retrieval augmented ChatGPT 4.0. They created a new GPT then released it for use. As learners draft, scaffolded prompts could surface concordance lines and lexical clusters drawn exclusively from that corpus. Each revised essay could be anonymised and appended to the dataset. It tightens a feedback loop similar to the incremental enrichment cycles that have boosted grammatical accuracy in Write & Improve and Pigai implementations12. For learners, the immediacy of developmentally matched exemplars raises perceived usefulness, while AI-powered learner corpora prompting that minimise cognitive effort could heighten perceived ease of use. The two attitudinal drivers that predict behavioural intention in extended TAM research on ChatGPT adoption13. Thus, the AI-powered learner corporainstantiates TAM constructs through a concrete and corpus-mediated writing cycle that aligns theory with daily classroom applications.

For clarity and conceptual precision, the researchers define AI-powered learner corpora as digital repositories of learner writing integrated with AI systems to deliver feedback and learning analytics. In in this study, 560 anonymised writing samples were described as being uploaded to a retrieval-augmented ChatGPT environment to create a searchable corpus. The AI model would then use this corpus to generate feedback linked to peer examples. For instance, a learner struggling with run-on sentences might receive a concise and high-quality excerpt from a peer’s text illustrating correct clause boundaries, along with a prompt for revision. Unlike general-purpose AI tools, these systems are designed to evolve with classroom-authored texts to ensure developmental appropriateness. They create a feedback loop where learners’ writing informs and is informed by the corpus, aligning with Vygotsky’s11 zone of proximal development. This implementation grounds the constructs of PU and ease of use in concrete instructional practice to strengthen this study’s alignment with TAM and ECM.

However, the pedagogical efficacy of AI-powered learner corpora is contingent upon primary school teachers’ willingness to adopt and meaningfully integrate it. Despite growing access to AI, many teachers remain hesitant due to concerns about diminished professional autonomy, the opacity of algorithms and uncertainties around tool reliability14. These concerns reflect the broader dynamics articulated in TAM. It posits that PU and ease of use significantly shape adoption intentions15. To be embraced, AI must enhance instructional effectiveness by offering clear and actionable insights into learners’ writing challenges and developmental trends16. Yet, most TAM applications have focused on tertiary settings. They often overlook the distinct pedagogical, ethical and developmental considerations central to primary school education17. Without addressing it, AI risks becoming another underutilised innovation in the edtech landscape.

Despite increasing research attention, critical gaps remain in the literature regarding the role of AI-powered learner corpora in promoting sustainable writing instruction at the primary school level. First, existing research has disproportionately centered on secondary and tertiary contexts. It neglects the specific developmental needs and instructional practices of younger learners18. Second, while short-term gains in error correction are documented, long-term effects on writing motivation, self-regulation and genre versatility remain underexplored19. Third, questions of systemic sustainability, such as equitable access, infrastructure readiness and alignment with ethical norms are insufficiently addressed20. These omissions hinder evidence-based policymaking and leave teachers without clear guidance on how to balance innovation with pedagogical integrity.

To address these gaps, this study poses two central research questions:

Theoretical background

AI applications in primary school writing instruction

Past studies demonstrate the potential and the limitations of AI for primary school learners to write. In a recent systematic review of the empirical research on K12 AI education, only 25 past studies published between 2018 and 2023 were identified. Only four focused on writing development21. Such scarcity prompts concern about ecological validity. Theoretical statements on AI writing are often ahead of classroom practice. There is little indication as to when such practice might be age-appropriate. To the extent that writing is reviewed, it is directed more to the sentence level than to organising discourse and that focus might themselves instead lead to more formulaic production.

This is also consistent with the results of the meta-analysis on automated writing evaluation systems. Huang et al.22 pooled 42 experimental contrasts and obtained an average medium effect (d = 0.67) for overall L2 writing quality. They indicated that the smallest advances were observed for primary school learners due to algorithmic mismatch with adolescent calibrated algorithms and with prefabricated linguistic repertoires of younger learners. A meta-analysis of treatments with ChatGPT also found a large overall effect size in favor of “learning performance” (g = 0.87)23. However, the researchers did not test the treatment by primary school learner moderator for lack of a large sample to reliably produce an effect estimate. It is a failure that contributes to the lack of research in the developmental period.

Similarly, qualitative assessments reflect these quantitative worries. Steiss et al.24 analysed 200 similar pairs of formative comments. They found that while ChatGPT was similar to humans in how much rubric content was cited, it was less balanced in its attention to developmental appropriateness. It frequently recommended a level of lexical sophistication that surpassed learners’ productive control. Hence, teachers continue to serve as indispensable mediators. They translate AI-generated suggestions into contextualised feedback and guard against over-correction that risks silencing the authorial voice.

As shown above, few studies have critically examined how AI-powered learner corpora function within actual primary classroom settings. Unlike general-purpose AI tools such as ChatGPT, which are often repurposed for writing tasks without pedagogical calibration, AI-powered learner corpora are designed to provide contextually anchored and data-driven feedback rooted in learners’ authentic writing. For example, the Write & Improve platform by Cambridge English allows learners to receive automated feedback based on iterative drafts, while systems like Pigai (used widely in East Asia) incorporate learner corpora to generate error-focused feedback aligned with L2 writing norms. However, both tools are primarily calibrated for secondary and tertiary users. This misalignment underscores the need to design AI corpora that accommodate the developmental stage, genre exposure and cognitive strategies of younger learners. In this regard, classroom implementations that incorporate learner corpora collected from primary school writing portfolios demonstrate potential for developmentally appropriate scaffolding. These implementations support zone of proximal development based learning when learners engage in pattern noticing from anonymised peer exemplars. Thus, it links Vygotskyan theory with practical applications. Nevertheless, current research lacks a systematic critique of how such systems are integrated into formative feedback cycles, peer interaction and revision routines within the primary curriculum. Addressing this gap is critical to informing both the design of AI-powered feedback systems and the professional development of teachers tasked with their integration.

Therefore, these results point to two urgent research agendas. One that can inform the creation of primary school level learner corpora to retrain detection algorithms to developmental norms. The other that determines whether AI-generated feedback encourages higher level discourse moves, such as cohesion, stance and narrative structure rather than simply promoting surface-level accuracy. Without such a study, AI threatens to amplify superficial literacy learning gains while overlooking the rhetorical abilities to be integral to the primary school writing curriculum.

The concept of the technology acceptance model

Davis15 introduced TAM to explain how users accept and utilise new technology. It is grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior and the Theory of Reasoned Action25. The core components are PU and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU). PU refers to users’ belief that a technology enhances their work or learning performance. In writing, AI is recognised for improving writing skills, optimising word selection and enhancing accuracy. Eventually, it could raise writing quality26. High levels of PU lead to positive evaluations and stronger intentions to use the technology.

Next, PEOU reflects the effort required to operate a technology. In education, this includes user-friendly interfaces, operational simplicity and low learning costs associated with AI27. High PEOU lowers usage barriers, enhances acceptance and promotes continued use. TAM suggests that PU and PEOU directly shape users’ attitudes toward usage. Subsequently, they influence behavioural intention and actual use. This study utilises TAM to examine the impact of PU and PEOU on primary school English teachers in writing instruction28. Extended TAM models incorporate variables like perceived self-efficacy (PSE), expectancy effects, growth mindset and interest. They enhance its explanatory power29. In AI, PSE denotes learners’ confidence in using AI tools effectively. It strengthens both usage intention and continuity. Expectancy effects relate to the expectations primary school English teachers hold about the AI outcomes. Positive expectations foster proactive use30. A growth mindset reflects the belief that writing skills can improve with an effort to promote tool adoption. Interestingly, reflecting learners’ engagement with AI boosts usage frequency and effectiveness. This study identifies key factors influencing the effectiveness of AI learner corpora and offers theoretical and empirical support for educational practices.

The expectation confirmation model

The Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM) offers a robust framework for understanding the factors that influence users’ continued engagement with technology following its initial adoption. As articulated by Thong et al.31, continuance intention is the decision to persist in using a technological tool. It is primarily determined by user satisfaction and the extent to which initial expectations are confirmed during actual use. Within the domain of educational technology, continuance intention is shaped not only by early perceptions of usefulness and ease of use but also by ongoing experiential feedback31. In educational AI, factors such as satisfaction, perceived value and instructional effectiveness play a pivotal role in sustaining both teacher and learner engagement. Satisfaction emerges as a critical mediating variable. Higher satisfaction substantially strengthens users’ intention to persist in AI tools’ use32.

Moreover, facilitating conditions such as accessible technical support and reliable infrastructure are essential enablers of continued use. They help to mitigate disruptions and reinforce user confidence33. PSE is defined as learners’ belief in their ability to complete writing tasks using AI. It also contributes positively to continuance intention34. Expectancy effects regarding the AI future performance further reinforce motivation to engage with AI to meet learning goals35. In parallel, a growth mindset characterised by the belief that writing skills can improve with sustained effort and practice enhances users’ persistence when AI is perceived as a developmental scaffold36. Intrinsic motivation as an internal driver has also been shown to significantly influence both the frequency and durability of AI tools’ use37.

Additionally, social influences such as primary school teacher endorsement and perceived learner engagement are instrumental in shaping continuance intention. They actively recommend that AI instills confidence in learners while heightened learner engagement signals a recognition of AI value and relevance. Collectively, these interrelated variables underscore ECM utility as a theory for explaining sustained technology adoption. By accounting for cognitive, affective and contextual influences, ECM provides a comprehensive model to understand and support the long-term integration of AI in educational environments.

Conceptual model and hypotheses

The integration of AI in primary school writing instruction necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing their acceptance and sustained use among primary school teachers. Drawing on TAM and ECM, this study proposes a conceptual model that elucidates the relationships between key variables affecting primary school teachers’ continuance intention to use AI-powered learner corpora. The model incorporates both intrinsic factors (PSE), growth mindset and interest) and extrinsic factors (facilitating conditions and learner engagement) to capture the multifaceted dimensions influencing technology acceptance. By systematically examining these factors, the model provides a robust foundation for investigating how PEOU, PU, satisfaction and continuance intention are interrelated in primary school writing instruction. Satisfaction and continuance intention

In TAM and educational technology applications, satisfaction is recognised as a pivotal factor influencing users’ continued use of a technology. In writing instruction, the satisfaction of both learners and teachers with AI tool is intrinsically linked to their continuance intention. Continuance intention refers to users’ commitment to persist in using a technology based on their cumulative experiences and expectations. Empirical studies consistently demonstrate a positive correlation between satisfaction and continuance intention within educational technology settings, where high satisfaction often translates into sustained use38.

In AI tool for writing instruction, satisfaction primarily hinges on several critical elements, such as ease of use, functional efficiency, writing effectiveness and the quality of feedback mechanisms. Specifically, ease of use and intuitive interfaces facilitate usage intentions by minimising cognitive load and fostering motivation. Writing effectiveness in vocabulary selection and grammatical precision significantly contributes to user satisfaction. Moreover, personalised feedback and real-time corrections empower learners to address errors. Thereby, it refines writing quality and enhance overall satisfaction.

Past research underscores the positive relationship between satisfaction and continuance intention. For instance, Bergmann et al.39 concluded that educational technology with high levels of user satisfaction significantly elevate continuance intention. Similarly, Pham et al.40 identified a correlation between teachers’ satisfaction and the frequency of learners’ tool usage. Teachers who experience high satisfaction are more likely to advocate for the tool. Thus, it fosters its long-term adoption among learners. Consequently, satisfaction among primary school teachers emerges as a fundamental driver of the sustained integration of AI tool in writing instruction.

Perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness with satisfaction

In TAM, PEOU and PU are foundational determinants of user satisfaction. PEOU reflects the perceived simplicity of operating a tool, while PU pertains to the perception of the tool capacity to enhance performance or learning efficiency41. In AI for writing instruction, satisfaction is heightened when learners and teachers perceive these tools as both user-friendly and effective in improving writing skills.

Studies consistently affirm a positive association between PEOU and satisfaction. AI tool that is intuitively designed and simple to navigate reduce operational complexity and cognitive demands. Thereby, it fosters greater user satisfaction42. Similarly, PU significantly influences satisfaction because users who perceive AI as enhancing writing quality, including vocabulary, grammar and expressive accuracy report higher levels of satisfaction43. Moreover, AI tool that offers instant, personalised feedback and guidance enhance learners’ ability to improve writing. Then, it positively impacts satisfaction. Primary school teachers are also more likely to recommend it when their effectiveness is evident. Thereby, it reinforces their acceptance44.

Method—measurement model

The questionnaire used 5-point Likert-type items, which are ordinal categorical. To minimize bias from treating ordinal levels as continuous and to increase robustness to non-normality and class imbalance, the CFA/SEM models employed weighted least squares with mean and variance adjustment (WLSMV). Item thresholds and loadings were estimated using polychoric correlations under the theta parameterization. This approach aligns with simulation evidence on Likert-type ordinal data. WLSMV is recommended when K < 5 or when distributions depart from normality, whereas for K ≥ 5 a robust ML estimator is often used as a benchmark.

For EFA, we analyzed the polychoric correlation matrix using MINRES/ULS extraction with oblique rotations (promax/oblimin) to obtain interpretable factors. CFA and the SEM were then estimated with WLSMV. We also performed sensitivity analyses. Treating the Likert items as continuous, we re-estimated key paths with MLR (robust maximum likelihood). The signs and statistical significances matched the WLSMV results to indicate that our conclusions are robust to estimator choice.

Questionnaire design

This study aims to investigate the factors influencing the adoption and sustained use of AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction. To achieve this, the questionnaire is structured into three sections, namely external support factors, individual characteristics and technology perception variables. Each section is designed to systematically capture the multifaceted dimensions that affect primary school teachers’ satisfaction, PEOU, PU and continuance intention when utilising AI-powered learner corpora.

The first section focuses on external support factors, including FC and learner engagement (teacher perception). These variables are essential for evaluating the contextual and environmental support available to primary school teachers who integrate AI-powered learner corpora into writing instruction. By assessing the availability of resources, technical assistance and the level of pedagogical support, this section provides insights into the external determinants that may shape technology acceptance.

The second section addresses individual characteristics, which encompass PSE, EE, GM and interest. These variables reflect primary school teachers’ intrinsic motivation, cognitive and affective dispositions toward technology adoption. Specifically, this section examines how their confidence in using AI-powered learner corpora, expectations of its efficacy, belief in the potential for improvement and interest in technological innovation influence their willingness to integrate AI-powered learner corpora into writing instruction.

The third section evaluates technology perception factors, focusing on PEOU, PU, satisfaction, and continuance intention. These constructs are critical for understanding how primary school teachers perceive the practicality and effectiveness of AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction. This section not only captures their experiential evaluations but also gauges their intentions to continue using the AI-powered learner corpora in the future.

To ensure the scientific rigor and validity of the instrument, the questionnaire items were carefully derived from prior studies. It reflects best practices in educational technology research. Table 1 provides a comprehensive overview of the categories, variables and references that informed the questionnaire design. All items employed a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree). Construct scores were computed as mean composites with no reverse-keyed items retained in the final instrument. Items were randomized within constructs and construct blocks were sectioned to mitigate response-set bias. Classical frameworks, TAM for PEOU/PU and ECM for Satisfaction/Continuance together with established engagement and mindset measures were contextually tailored to the AI-powered learner corpora by replacing domain-general terms (e.g., “system,” “work task”) with “AI-powered learner corpora” and “essay writing” while preserving each construct’s conceptual core. To facilitate replication, the complete questionnaire items are provided in the Appendix.

The questionnaire was conducted between June 2022 and August 2022. It yielded 355 valid responses from primary school teachers representing 28 primary schools in Malaysia. To address potential variation in participants’ understanding of the term “AI-powered learner corpora,” the questionnaire provided a standardised operational definition accompanied by illustrative scenarios. Specifically, respondents were informed that AI-powered learner corpora referred to digital systems that integrate authentic learner writing samples to generate feedback or suggestions. Examples included classroom platforms where learners’ essays are anonymised, stored in a growing database and used to provide real-time comparisons or scaffolded prompts based on peer-generated texts. Although this study focused on an AI-powered learner corpus tool developed for research purposes, it did not restrict respondents to a single named commercial platform to ensure the inclusivity of varying local implementations. This ensured a minimum baseline of shared understanding and minimised interpretive inconsistencies across responses. Nonetheless, the researchers acknowledge that the diversity of AI experiences and contexts remains a limitation and recommend follow-up qualitative studies to explore how these tools are understood and enacted in different classroom environments.

Participants were recruited on a voluntary basis and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. The study strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines set by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Sultan Idris Education University (Approval Number: 2021-0454-01) to ensuring compliance with ethical standards and safeguarding participants’ rights and confidentiality. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Descriptive analysis of the questionnaire

This subsection provides an overview of the research sample. It focusses on the demographic characteristics of participants, their familiarity with AI tools, usage patterns and perceived effectiveness in writing instruction. The analysis offers valuable insights into the factors shaping their acceptance and sustained use of AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction. A total of 355 valid responses were collected from primary school teachers involved in primary school writing courses. It reflects a diverse representation of teaching experience and grade levels. Demographic characteristics

The analysis reveals a balanced distribution across grade levels with fourth-grade primary school teachers comprising the largest group (106 respondents, 29.9%). It is followed by third-grade primary school teachers (86, 24.2%) and sixth-grade primary school teachers (84, 23.7%). Fifth-grade primary school teachers constituted a smaller proportion, totaling 79 respondents (22.3%). These proportions indicate that the study encompasses a broad representation of grade levels. It allows for nuanced comparisons across different teaching contexts.

In terms of teaching experience, the majority of participants reported 4 to 6 years of experience (132 respondents, 37.2%). It is followed by those with 7 to 10 years (103, 28.9%), more than 10 years (67, 18.9%), and 1 to 3 years (53, 14.9%). This diversity highlights the inclusion of both relatively new and experienced primary school teachers which enriches the analysis by incorporating varied pedagogical perspectives.

Familiarity and usage of AI tools

Familiarity with AI tools varied among participants. Approximately 29.9% (106 respondents) reported having used AI tools in their teaching practices, while 49.3% (175 respondents) indicated awareness of AI tools without direct usage. Notably, 20.8% (74 respondents) were unfamiliar with AI tools in writing instruction. It indicates that AI-powered learner corpora was the first AI tool they used for writing instruction. These findings suggest that while awareness is relatively widespread, practical engagement with AI tools remains inconsistent among them.

Classroom usage frequency also showed significant variation. A notable 40.6% (144 respondents) reported using AI tools multiple times per week. It reflects a strong integration into their teaching practices. 34.6% (123 respondents) used the AI tools occasionally, while 14.9% (53 respondents) never employed them. A smaller proportion (9.9%, 35 respondents) reported daily use. These patterns indicate that although some primary school teachers actively incorporate AI into their writing instruction, others remain hesitant or inconsistent in their usage.

Perceived effectiveness and support needs

Primary school teachers’ perceptions of the effectiveness of AI-powered learner corpora in improving learners’ writing skills varied considerably. The majority (71.5%, 254 respondents) observed significant improvements in writing performance. It highlights the perceived educational benefits of AI integration. However, 24.2% (86 respondents) reported no discernible changes, while 4.2% (15 respondents) remained uncertain. These divergent perceptions underscore the need for more targeted training and professional development to enhance the effective implementation of AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction.

When asked about key support needs for integrating AI-powered learner corpora, policy support emerged as the most cited requirement (114 votes), followed by teacher training (89 votes), school funding (63 votes) and functionality upgrades (57 votes). Other factors received 32 votes. It reflects the multifaceted challenges primary school teachers face when adopting AI-powered learner corpora. These insights emphasise the importance of institutional commitment and ongoing professional development to support AI adoption.

It indicates that fostering an interactive and engaging learning environment can indirectly support technology adoption.

Next, primary school teachers were grouped by teaching tenure, namely, 1 to 3 years, 4 to 6 years, 7 to 10 years, and over 10 years. Therefore, future training should reinforce hands-on practice and confidence building for novice primary school teachers. For experienced primary school teachers, it should focus on optimising interface design and workflow support. Such tiered and personalised strategies will enhance acceptance and continuance intention toward AI-powered learner corpora across teacher tenure groups.

Discussion

This study significantly advances the TAM and the ECM by critically examining their continued relevance for AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction for primary education (see Fig. 4). Notably, intrinsic motivators emerged as the most influential factors, with PSE (β = 0.448, p < 0.001) and interest (β = 0.222, p = 0.002). It demonstrates significant impacts, while FC (β = 0.005, p = 0.943) and EE (β = 0.119, p = 0.123) were found to be statistically insignificant. These findings challenge conventional narratives within educational technology research. It suggests that AI adoption in education is driven more by teachers’ psychological ownership and pedagogical alignment than by institutional support. This insight necessitates a deeper examination of human-AI collaboration in the broader sociotechnical critiques of AI integration in education.

PSE emerged as the most dominant predictor. It aligns with Bandura60 assertion that self-belief is critical in adopting complex technologies, including AI. This finding raises an intriguing question: Why does interest outweigh institutional factors, especially where institutional support is inconsistently distributed? According to61 Self-Determination Theory, intrinsic motivation driven by personal interest underpins sustained engagement. This observation challenges traditional TAM literature that emphasises extrinsic factors such as training62. This perspective necessitates a willingness to critically engage with and evaluate AI-generated feedback63. It highlights the duality of AI tools’ potential simultaneously to empower teacher agency and require new literacies20.

The non-significant impact of FC challenges the assumptions of the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology. It typically foregrounds institutional support62. This discrepancy may reflect the post-pandemic rise in digital fluency wherein teachers expect intuitive tools to reduce reliance on external scaffolding64. Alternatively, it may indicate systemic complacency. Schools perceive AI as inherently “plug-and-play”. They underestimate the need for contextualised training and professional development65. If teachers must navigate AI independently, AI adoption risks exacerbating educational inequities by privileging tech-savvy teachers and marginalising those less adept66.

Learner engagement (teacher perception) has a lack of significant effect on usability perceptions. It challenges the assumption that AI interactivity universally enhances teaching. As13 caution, AI’s value lies not in mimicking human interaction but in cultivating metacognitive skills. While AI-generated prompts may facilitate basic engagement, they often fall short of fostering deep revision strategies essential for writing development67. This calls for a design paradigm in which AI tools move beyond gamification to provide adaptive and pedagogically sound feedback that approximates expert tutoring4.

Although AI-powered learner corpora offer tailored feedback, they also pose algorithmic-bias risks. Mittelstadt et al.78 warn that corpora trained on homogeneous datasets can reinforce dominant linguistic norms. It might marginalise dialect diversity10. AI systems that penalise non-standard grammar may suppress creative language use. It could endorse prescriptive norms67. Further, learners may replicate AI-generated patterns without mastering underlying skills. They might confuse mimicry with competence68. Addressing these challenges requires robust bias-auditing frameworks and technology integration aligned with emancipatory pedagogies69.

Reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases. It warrants methodological triangulation through classroom observation to validate findings. This current study’s geographic focus on Malaysia limits generalizability. It highlights the need for cross-context research that accounts for socioeconomic, cultural and curricular variation20. Longitudinal studies are essential to determine whether AI-augmented writing gains persist or decline over time due to over-reliance19.

This study therefore rejects reductionist views of AI as a simplistic educational fix. Instead, it argues for a nuanced understanding of AI as both enabler and disruptor with sustainable integration contingent on leveraging teacher expertise and maintaining ethical vigilance. Cultivating a growth mindset toward AI embracing experimentation while critically appraising algorithmic outputs is crucial70. Policymakers and developers must prioritise co-design frameworks that allow teachers to shape AI tools according to pedagogical values rather than passively accepting predefined functionalities18. Through such participatory and localised approaches, AI can advance innovative writing instruction without entrenching inequity or dependency.

Implications

Theoretical implications

This study significantly advances the theoretical discourse surrounding AI integration in writing instruction by critically examining the applicability of the TAM and the ECM. The findings underscore the pivotal roles of PSE, interest, and satisfaction in driving technology acceptance and sustained use. Thereby, it enriches the existing body of educational technology research. In particular, PSE emerged as a critical determinant. It significantly influences both PEOU and PU with a notably stronger impact on the latter. This finding aligns with the Self-Efficacy Theory. It posits that user confidence fundamentally shapes their evaluation of technological tools43. Therefore, boosting user confidence emerges as a strategic priority in promoting the adoption and sustained utilisation of AI tools.

This study also highlights interest as a powerful intrinsic motivator that significantly enhances both PU and continuance intention. This insight aligns with Self Determination Theory. It posits that engaging and personalised features can foster exploratory and proactive user behavior71. Consequently, the design of educational AI should prioritise features that stimulate intrinsic motivation because this approach not only fosters initial adoption but also encourages sustained engagement.

Furthermore, satisfaction is shown to mediate the relationships between PEOU, PU and continuance intention. This finding reinforces the ECM proposition that user satisfaction is integral to long-term tool adoption72. Improving user experience through optimised feedback and seamless functionality thus becomes essential to promoting positive attitudes toward educational technology.

Interestingly, this study reveals that FC did not significantly impact PEOU. This outcome challenges the conventional view that external support structures are crucial for technology acceptance. Instead, the results suggest that familiarity with AI tools reduces the need for institutional support. It indicates that user experience levels shape the relevance of external scaffolding. This finding is consistent with73. They contend that technical support is more crucial in environments where users lack technological proficiency. By demonstrating the context-dependent nature of facilitating conditions, this study offers nuanced insights into the conditions under which external support is most beneficial.

Practical implications

This study offers practical insights for integrating AI into writing instruction. The findings suggest that fostering users’ PSE and stimulating learning interest are pivotal in enhancing PU and encouraging continuance intention. Antonietti et al.74 assert that users with high self-efficacy demonstrate greater persistence and proactive engagement with technology. Based on these observations, the following recommendations are proposed for key stakeholders. Educational institutions and teacher trainers should prioritize the development of targeted professional development programs. Such programs ought to include hands-on practice with AI tools, clear operational guidelines and scenario-based tasks to strengthen educators’ and students’ self-efficacy. Since PSE significantly influences technology adoption, building user confidence through structured training should be regarded as an early priority.

Moreover, interest plays a crucial role in fostering continued use. Referring to Self-Determination Theory, engaging features can boost user participation and sustained motivation75. Therefore, integrating interactive elements, such as gamification, personalised feedback and progress tracking can enrich the learning experience and foster a sense of achievement among users. Such strategies are essential for maintaining learner engagement and promoting sustained tool usage.

Optimising user experience is equally vital for long-term adoption. Satisfaction as a mediator between PEOU, PU and continuance intention highlights the importance of providing a seamless and efficient user experience. Ye et al.76 emphasise that user feedback should continuously inform the development of new functionalities. Educational technology developers are encouraged to optimise user experience by refining system usability and interface design. Given that satisfaction mediates the relationship between PEOU, PU, and continuance intention, improvements such as multilingual support, real-time error correction, context-aware feedback and simplified workflows are recommended. Iterative design informed by user feedback should be ongoing to align tool development with pedagogical needs.

Following that, the non-significant effect of FC on PEOU suggests that support strategies should be differentiated based on users’ digital literacy. Cascone et al.77 note that while novice users may require comprehensive training, experienced users benefit more from customisation and enhanced functionality. Novice users may benefit from comprehensive technical and organisational assistance, whereas advanced users might prefer customisation options and functional enhancements. Policymakers and school administrators should therefore adopt adaptive support frameworks that accommodate.

Specific strategies to enhance teacher self-efficacy and interest

To boost teacher self-efficacy and interest in AI-powered learner corpora, a tiered mentoring program could be implemented. Experienced teachers are paired with novice teachers based on teaching tenure and technical proficiency. Each month, mentors deliver two classroom demonstrations and provided coaching. These sessions offer immediate feedback and opportunities to build on successful experiences. They could develop modular online micro-credential courses lasting five to ten minutes each. These modules focus on key skills such as natural language processing error annotation, exemplar retrieval and rewrite suggestion generation. Upon completion, participants receive digital badges and professional development credits. An online community supports peer review to sustain motivation. In addition, they could organise an “AI Writing Innovation Design Competition.” Teachers are invited to propose instructional designs based on the AI-powered learner corpora and submit practical case studies. Expert panels review winning entries which they receive research grant funding. These competitions and resource incentives foster teacher creativity and encourage continued use of AI-powered learner corpora. Ultimately, this strategy supports the effective integration and sustainable application of AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction.

Teacher–developer co-creation implementation path

To bring the concept of co-design into practice, schools should adopt iterative and collaborative frameworks that empower teachers to directly shape AI tool development. Co-design involves participatory design cycles where teachers contribute to the ideation, prototyping and refinement of AI systems based on real classroom needs. This approach aligns with user-centered design principles and ensures that technology development is pedagogically anchored. First, each school forms an AI co-creation team. The team includes two in-service teachers, one research coordinator and one technical development engineer. Quarterly workshops analyse writing class challenges and functional requirements. The team then drafts a feature list and integration plan aligned with curriculum materials and instructional objectives. Next, two to three prototype iteration workshops take place at the start, middle and end of each semester. These workshops apply the Design Thinking methodology. They merge classroom observations, teacher–learner interviews, prototype demonstrations and simulated use. Collected feedback refines the design. Teachers test new features in real classrooms and offer enhancement suggestions. The development team then adjusts model parameters, interface layouts and interaction logic to ensure close alignment with instructional practices. Finally, two on-site developer days occur each semester. The technical team visits classrooms and teacher offices in person. They gather user challenges and demonstrate the latest software version on the spot. They also provide brief training sessions and online support. This process fosters dynamic interaction through ongoing requirements gathering, prototype iteration and in-person feedback. It supports the effective deployment and continuous optimisation of AI tools in primary school writing classrooms.

Data privacy and security

To safeguard the privacy and security of primary school essays, this study integrates key measures, such as data de-identification, parental informed consent, encrypted storage and role-based access controls. Prior to system import, the researchers remove names, learner IDs, class details and location metadata. They replace them with randomly generated pseudonymous identifiers. At the start of each semester, schools distribute detailed informed consent forms through parent Whatapps groups. The forms specify the purpose of sample use, retention period and secure destruction procedures. Essays are collected only after parents provide signatures or electronic confirmation. All consent receipts are archived centrally. Essays are protected with the AES symmetric encryption algorithm during transmission and at rest. Transmission occurs over HTTP. At rest, essays reside in encrypted volumes to prevent interception or unauthorised access. Access to decrypted data is restricted to authorised researchers. Two-factor authentication is required. Access logs are audited regularly to monitor data access. After study completion or when the retention period expires, data enters a secure destruction workflow. Dedicated software deletes encrypted files according to international standards and generates a destruction report for archive. This end-to-end process ensures comprehensive coverage from data collection to archival. It safeguards the privacy and security of primary-school writing samples.

Ethical implications

As an illustrative scenario for the adoption of AI-powered learner corpora, several ethical considerations must be proactively addressed.To reduce algorithmic bias in AI-powered learner corpora, schools can adopt a layered governance model that blends independent auditing with classroom-level safeguards. At the system level, each semester, an external review board should draw a random sample of learner texts, run fairness diagnostics, such as comparing error-flag rates and praise distribution across gender, dialect and proficiency tiers. Then, they could publish an open “model card” that summarises findings, corrective actions and residual risks. Publishing the card online invites community scrutiny and creates regulatory memory. It makes silent drift less likely. At the classroom level, teachers could complete a micro-credential in critical AI literacy that shows them how to detect biased feedback, rebalance an over-narrow corpus by adding underrepresented language varieties and lead short reflective talks where learners weigh automated suggestions against human judgement. These can be embedded in existing professional development hours and assessed through practice-based portfolios rather than multiple-choice tests. It ensures that teachers leave with transferable skills. The two layers interact productively, such as audit results feed into training scenarios and teacher observations feed back to auditors. They form a live feedback loop that gradually improves both the model and its oversight. An equally critical consideration is data privacy. In any real-world implementation, since bias audits and corpus enrichment require access to student writing, strict anonymisation protocols must be enforced. All data should be stored on secure servers and informed consent must be obtained from both parents and learners at the start of each term. It clearly explains how the data will be used, stored and eventually purged. Learners used the AI-powered learner corpora for essay writing. However, they had selected not to use the data for ChatGPT model training. It could protect the data. All survey responses and teacher feedback were collected after it. The human ethical approval was obtained. Any future application of such technology must receive explicit ethical approval and comply with local data protection regulations concerning minors’ data.Only by uniting technical audits, human expertise and robust privacy safeguards can AI-powered learner corpora promote equity and trust to ensure that technological integration aligns with the fundamental values of educational ethics.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the research relied exclusively on a single-country sample of primary school English teachers in Malaysia. It may limit the generalizability of the results. Cultural, curricular and infrastructural specificities could influence technology acceptance in ways not captured here. Future studies would benefit from multi-national or comparative designs to enhance external validity. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the survey means that all data were collected at a single point in time. While this offers a snapshot of teachers’ perceptions and intentions, it does not capture how these might evolve through actual and sustained use of AI-powered learner corpora. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the dynamics of continuance intention and real classroom integration over time. Third, the use of self-report measures introduces the possibility of social desirability bias and common method variance. Participants may have provided responses they perceived as favorable rather than reflecting their actual beliefs or practices. Although procedural remedies were applied, future research could incorporate behavioral data or observational methods to triangulate findings. Finally, the voluntary participation approach may have attracted teachers who are more positively predisposed to technology or AI, potentially skewing the sample toward higher levels of motivation and self-efficacy. This self-selection bias implies that the results may not fully represent the broader population of primary school English teachers. In light of these limitations, we caution against overgeneralizing the findings. The implications of this study are most applicable to contexts similar to the Malaysian primary education environment, particularly those with comparable technological access, teacher training frameworks and curricular structures. We encourage further research in diverse settings to validate and extend the proposed model.

Conclusion

This study explores the integration of AI-powered learner corpora in writing instruction by examining the relevance of the TAM and ECM. Through model validation and empirical analysis, it identifies PSE, interest and satisfaction as critical determinants of PEOU, PU and Continuance Intention. PSE, significantly influences primary school teachers’ evaluations of AI-powered learner corpora. It highlights the importance of confidence-building initiatives to foster sustained use. Similarly, interest underscores the value of integrating engaging features that enhance learning experiences and support continuous use. Satisfaction acts as a crucial mediator. It links PEOU and PU to sustained adoption. Thereby, it emphasises the importance of optimising user experience.

Drawing on the results of the strong link between primary school teachers’ self-confidence and both the PEOU and PU of the AI-powered learner corpora, four clear design features could be suggested. First, small “click-for-help” pop-ups inside the interface that show short explanations or models whenever a learner meets a new feedback label could be included. This boosts self-confidence at the moment it is needed most. Second, when a learner hovers over an AI suggestion, appropriate example sentences drawn from the learner corpus could be shown so that feedback feels directly useful and relevant. Third, a simple switch that lets primary school teachers choose how much feedback appears could be added. Beginners are not overwhelmed while advanced users still get deep insights. Finally, progress could be displayed in an engaging way, such as a small “cohesion streak” bar and badges that highlight growth in vocabulary or structure across drafts. It turns interest into steady use. These features translate the statistical drivers of acceptance into practical design choices teachers can apply right away.

While most hypotheses were supported, the non-significant relationship between FC and PEOU suggests that external support is less relevant when users are familiar with AI-powered learner corpora. This finding implies that while experienced users benefit from advanced functionalities, less proficient users still require structured training and technical assistance. Although the findings offer useful insight for Malaysian primary school teachers, they may not transfer directly to other cultural settings because technology acceptance is shaped by local norms and school structures. Malaysia’s collectivist ethos and relatively high power distance can make teachers more willing to follow top-down directives and to rely on peer consensus when judging new AI tools. In contexts that value individual autonomy and lower hierarchy, self-efficacy and personal experimentation might carry greater weight than institutional signals. Likewise, Malaysia’s bilingual curriculum and national push for digital learning may inflate PU compared with regions where English is not a priority or where internet access is uneven. Future studies should therefore test the model in contrasting cultures, such as highly individualist Scandinavian systems or low-resource rural schools to see whether the same predictors hold or whether factors like managerial support, language policy or infrastructure become more important. Thereby, it could fine-tune AI design and professional development strategies for diverse settings. Moreover, it should broaden the geographic scope to encompass diverse educational settings and adopt mixed-method approaches, including longitudinal and experimental designs to further explore AI’s educational impact. Addressing potential biases and conducting cross-contextual studies will enhance the generalisability and robustness of findings.
