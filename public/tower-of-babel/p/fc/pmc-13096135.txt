Machine learning identifies distinct movement control impairment clusters in patients with chronic neck pain
Authors: Ziva Majcen Rosker, Jernej Rosker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with chronic neck pain experience various impairments, with reduced movement control suggested as a significant contributing factor. The heterogeneity of this patient population and suboptimal rehabilitation outcomes suggests the existence of latent subgroup characteristics. The aim of this study was to identify distinct groups among patients with neck pain based on the movement control test and pain intensity and to provide information on cluster-specific impairments. 135 patients with idiopathic neck pain performed a movement control test (the Butterfly test) at three difficulty levels and were assessed for pain intensity, providing 13 different parameters (classifiers). Louvain, hierarchical and k-means clustering methods were applied and the number of clusters determined by observing the symmetry and size of silhouette scores. Further, different machine learning algorithms were applied to develop and evaluate a classification framework (based on AUC, classification accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity) and to provide information on individual cluster characteristics using the Shapley Additive Explanations. The k-means and deep learning neural network methods provided the most efficient clustering and classification performance extracting 4 meaningful clusters. Patients between groups differed in the amount of impairment, with cluster 2 and 1 representing the most severe impairments and with clusters 3 and 4 the least severe impairments. Additionally, specific motor control impairments were observed in individual clusters suggesting distinct neck movement control adaptations. Identifying subgroups of patients with neck pain and their specific characteristics based on the results of the Butterfly test may inform future development of targeted rehabilitation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43903-z.

Introduction

Neck pain (NP) is one of the most commonly reported musculoskeletal disorders1, with an increasing number of patients suffering from NP for longer than three months, often leading to chronicity2. Although the current model of NP management often focuses on alleviating acute episodes of NP, one of society’s biggest problems is not an acute episode, but rather its recurrence and chronicity3. Various patients with chronic NP who seek physical therapy care fall under the umbrella of non-specific NP (without identifiable structural pathology), accompanied by heterogeneous signs and symptoms4–6. Outcome measures of signs and symptoms used in clinical practice generally provide moderate to excellent sensitivity and specificity when differentiating patients with NP from healthy individuals7–9, but information on potential subgroups within patients with non-specific NP is lacking. This information would be important because, to date, research suggests the existence of subgroups based on the results of some functional impairments10,11, which could potentially improve rehabilitation approaches12. In addition, it has been suggested that current rehabilitation outcomes are suboptimal with high recurrence rate reported13–16. Therefore, potential latent characteristics of outcome measures related to functional impairments (i.e., neuromuscular function) should be investigated to better identify and understand the underlying mechanisms of non-specific NP.

There is strong evidence that individuals with a history of non-specific NP, even when in remission from pain, present with similar impairments in neuromuscular functions (i.e. sensorimotor functions) as during the pain period17,18, suggesting that altered neuromuscular functions may be the reason for the development of chronicity and recurrence. In addition, a recent review suggests that, as a result of persistent proprioceptive impairments, long-term sensorimotor alteration (e.g., kinesthetic awareness) and neural plasticity changes may contribute to the recurrence and chronicity (pain persisting linger than three months) of NP19,20. Based on these suggestions, addressing alterations in kinesthetic awareness is recommended for patients with NP19.

Kinesthetic awareness is a complex functional ability composed of various aspects of sensorimotor control (sense of velocity, direction, movement onset, force and effort)21 and its involvement in motor responses22. A recent systematic literature review reported conflicting findings regarding the extent of various aspects of kinesthetic impairment in patients with NP23. The authors suggest that these conflicting results are not so likely due to inconsistencies in assessment methodology but rather due to the different approaches used across studies to recruit patients with NP disorders that could lead to greater variability within their functional impairments.

An important challenge in understanding the underlying characteristics of kinesthetic awareness in patients with NP is to understand the contribution of multiple parameters (outcome measures) usually provided by movement sense tests24–27. The review of the current literature suggests that researchers currently focus primarily on studying and reporting individual parameters when searching for alterations in kinesthetic awareness23. Although studying individual parameters provides information about specific aspects (dimensions) of kinesthetic alterations in patients with NP, potential high-dimensional information is lost. Data mining and machine learning approaches provide an alternative to commonly used statistical approaches and allow searching for multidimensional features in non-linearly distributed data28–31. Therefore, data mining and machine learning approaches should be applied to provide a better understanding of kinesthetic impairments and consequently identify different clusters of patients with non-specific NP, potentially enabling more efficient rehabilitation approaches.

Data-driven stratification approaches have increasingly been applied across clinical fields to address patient heterogeneity and move beyond single-parameter classification systems. In musculoskeletal and chronic pain conditions, unsupervised learning techniques have revealed latent subgroups based on multidimensional functional profiles that are not captured by conventional assessments11,28,30. Such methodological advances support the use of multivariate clustering approaches in complex clinical populations.

The first aim of the study was to focus specifically on chronic non-specific NP and to identify clusters within a heterogeneous group of patients based on different parameters and difficulty levels of a movement control test and pain intensity. The second aim was to develop and evaluate a classification framework capable of assigning patients to previously identified clusters and provide information on cluster-specific kinesthetic impairments.

Results of clustering and classification analysis

The silhouette scores for Louvain clustering, k-means and hierarchical clustering are presented in Table 1. Hierarchical and k-means clustering methods showed comparable mean silhouette scores across the two-, three-, and four-cluster solutions. Therefore, our selection was based primarily on cluster-wise balance: the k-means solution showed less asymmetry across individual cluster silhouette scores, whereas hierarchical clustering showed greater variability between clusters, particularly as the number of clusters increased. When more than four clusters were produced, silhouette asymmetry increased, indicating less efficient clustering. In the Louvain clustering method, the number of clusters cannot be predetermined; in our analysis it produced six clusters (using 24 neighbours) with asymmetric silhouette scores and was therefore considered less efficient than the other approaches. In addition to the standard overall silhouette score (sample-averaged), we examined the unweighted mean of cluster-wise silhouette scores to assess balance and symmetry between clusters, as solutions with highly uneven cluster quality were considered less informative despite similar overall silhouette values.

Sensitivity of classification methods

The performance of different classification methods and their ability to classify individual patients with NP into specific, previously identified clusters are presented in Table 2. All classification approaches demonstrated very high classification performance, which tended to slightly decrease with an increased number of clusters (especially sensitivity). The stacking of all applied classification methods and neural networks method presented with highest AUC, CA, sensitivity and specificity when applying three or four clusters of NP patients. No important differences were observed between the stacking and neural network approach, indicating that the neural network approach is sufficiently efficient in classifying patients with neck pain into individual clusters.

In addition to supervised classification approaches, a distance-based baseline was evaluated to represent a simple cluster-assignment rule. Specifically, a 1-nearest neighbour classifier using Euclidean distance on standardized features was applied within the same cross-validation framework. The distance-based baseline demonstrated slightly lower classification accuracy compared with the supervised machine-learning approaches (Table 2). This indicates that while cluster membership can be approximated by local distance-based assignment, supervised models provide more stable decision boundaries, particularly for patients near cluster borders. Importantly, this finding confirms that the reported classification performance primarily reflects internal consistency with the identified cluster structure rather than validation against an external clinical ground truth.

Discussion

Previous suggestions regarding the existence of heterogeneity among patients with chronic idiopathic NP11,23,32 have been confirmed by the results of our study. However, the novelty of this study is the discovery of two to four clinically and statistically justifiable clusters based on the results of the Butterfly test and pain intensity. In addition, we developed a machine learning–based classification framework and demonstrated its feasibility within the identified clustering structure that was able to assign patients in one of the four clusters with excellent classification accuracy. These results are, to some extent, expected, since some previous studies using the Butterfly test have shown wide dispersion of the results33–35 indicating greater variability amongst patients with NP disorders. Interestingly, the four clusters observed in our study indicate the severity of impairments as well as specific movement control deficits, which is in line with previous research36.

In our study, two to four clusters of patients with neck pain were identified. Qualitative assessment of cluster characteristics indicated that a greater number of clusters included in the analysis allowed for a more specific clustering of patients with neck pain. As the four-cluster analysis is a more detailed representation of the two- and three-cluster analysis, the four-cluster analysis will be discussed below.

In addition, it is important to state that the identified clusters were derived from multidimensional integration of cervical movement control parameters across task difficulty levels and pain intensity rather than from predefined clinical thresholds. At present, no validated composite clinical indices exist that capture the high-dimensional structure of cervicocephalic movement control assessed during dynamic and unpredictable tasks such as the Butterfly test. Importantly, the observed subgroup structure could not be replicated through univariate thresholding of individual outcome measures, as clusters differed not only in overall severity but also in qualitative profiles of impairment, including predominant undershoot versus overshoot patterns. The clinical plausibility of this clustering solution is supported by coherent, cluster-specific motor control deficits consistent with established theories of motor adaptation and previous subgrouping studies in neck pain populations11,36.

The most severe disability across all difficulty levels of the Butterfly movement control test was observed in cluster 4. This cluster is characterized by moderate to severe levels of NP, and was the only cluster to present with relationship between pain intensity and time-on-target or amplitude accuracy of the Butterfly test. This is in line with previous studies showing greater functional impairments in patients with moderate to severe NP levels37,38. However, our results suggest that only a specific subgroup of patients present with such relationship, which is supported by other observational studies39. This observation is in line with theories on pain and motor control, in which pain induces individualized adaptations with or without affecting quality of movement40,41, however the exact underlying mechanisms cannot be easily explained. In contrast to other clusters, this cluster additionally presented with large impairments not only at the medium and difficult levels but also at the easy level. According to Meisingest et al.42, the easy level of the Butterfly test correlated with Neck disability index score suggesting more severe and wider range of encompassing impairments. In addition, impairments at medium and difficult levels might be related to different factors affecting movement control. Precision movements performed at higher velocities (i.e. medium and difficult levels) might be associated with increased cervical spine stiffness42,43. These observations are further supported by the results reported by Vikne et al.44, where movement velocity deficits in patients with NP disorders were observed.

In cluster 3, the second most impaired group of patients was identified based on accuracy of head and neck movement control. Overall, impairments in this cluster presented with similar but slightly less prominent movement control impairments as those in cluster 4 (Table 3) accompanied by mild to moderate pain levels. High overshoot was the most prominent feature of this cluster, that could be related to altered deceleration ability45 leading to delayed movement corrections. In addition, overshoot could be related to less efficient control of muscle synergies, especially during the attempts to accurately change directions of movement22,36,43 as is distinctive for the Butterfly test. Another important factor that could lead to increased overshoot is cervical hypermobility and segmental instability, which can be associated with early disc degeneration46 and herniation47. The relation between local cervical spine instability and overshoot parameter was partially indicated by the recent study where patients were evaluated before and after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (surgically alleviated cervical disc herniation) using the Butterfly test48. Patients in their study presented with statistically significant decrease in overshoot at one-week post-surgery suggesting that segmental stabilization, which led to improved passive movement of the spine, resulted in less challenged active movement control. In contrast, undershoot parameter did not present with statistically significant changes post-surgery, which was expected as this parameter could be related to pathology induced central changes found in patients with neck pain49 leading to delayed and slower muscle activation patterns45 most probably not directly affected by acute improvement in passive stabilization.

Cluster 2 presented with mild to moderate pain levels as well as less severe and smaller movement control deficits as in cluster 3 and 4. The most prominent feature of this cluster is the undershoot at medium and difficult levels (i.e. lagging behind the target) which indicates slower and less responsive head and neck movements. Decreased velocity of head and neck movements has been previously identified in patients with NP disorders42. One of the proposed factors commonly investigated in the literature is increased fear of movement which decreases preferred head and neck movement velocity50–52. Such a decrease could result in diminished accuracy of head and neck movements (i.e. increased undershoot) found in cluster 2 during the Butterfly test. An interesting observation found in this cluster was that impairments were less prominent at easy level. As reported by Meisingest et al.42, patients with NP could potentially move their head and neck faster but choose to decrease their movement velocity during different head and neck movements. Such a strategy could indicate that in some patients with NP movement control deficits are less prominent at slower movement velocities, which is in line with results of this cluster.

The smallest impairments were observed in cluster 1. The values of amplitude accuracy observed in this cluster were comparable to the ones reported in the study by de Zoete et al.33. In their study, although not directly investigated, no major differences were observed between patients with NP and asymptomatic individuals. It could be speculated that patients allocated to cluster 1 in our study present with most similar features of head and neck movement control as asymptomatic individuals. One of the reasons that could affect head and neck movement control in asymptomatic individuals are dysfunctional postural adaptations that are maintained for a prolonged period of time (e.g. forward head posture)53. In recent years increased use of modern mobile devices and computers has led to greater postural adaptations which can lead to decreased cervical head and neck proprioception in asymptomatic individuals54,55. Based on these suggestions certain subgroups of asymptomatic individuals might present with greater proprioceptive impairments than others, but to our knowledge this has not yet been investigated. This might be the reason for many discrepancies in the results across different studies when trying to compare asymptomatic individuals and NP patients since it might depend on which subgroup a certain individual belongs to. Since the goal of many functional diagnostic tests is to discriminate between patients and healthy individuals’ results could potentially depend on which cluster is the most represented in the studied cohort.

A simplified summary presented in SUPPLEMENTARY Table 6 is intended to support clinical interpretation of cluster profiles. However, prospective validation is required before such patterns can be used for individual clinical decision-making.

Our study found four clusters of patients based on the Butterfly test. A distinctive feature of this test is that the target moves unpredictably; therefore, other factors could influence the results. According to Majcen Rosker et al.56 unpredictable target movements led to increased involvement of cognitive resources in patients with NP especially in neck torsion maneuver which could be due to cervical spine impairments. Other studies also indicate that some patients with NP might present with cognitive disfunction57, therefore this should be investigated in the future studies especially in older adults with NP.

An important limitation of our study was that, although different patient recruitment strategies were used, this factor was not considered in the analysis. Future studies should incorporate such information in the classification analysis along with participants occupational characteristics, age and gender. Our study additionally evaluated pain intensity, however since many patients with NP also experience other symptoms (e.g. dizziness, headaches) these should also be taken into consideration in future studies along with other functional impairments.

A final limitation of this study is that it represents a model-development and proof-of-concept investigation. Cluster assignments were generated using unsupervised machine learning and subsequently treated as pseudo-labels for supervised classification; as such, the reported performance reflects internal consistency rather than validation against an external clinical ground truth. Accordingly, future studies should prioritize external validation in independent cohorts, which is essential before any consideration of clinical implementation.

Methods

Participants

One hundred and forty-seven patients with chronic NP were enrolled in the study. As this study was an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation, the sample size was guided by common heuristic recommendations for multivariable modelling, including a subject-to-feature ratio of approximately 10:158,59, which is consistent with sample sizes used in comparable clustering studies of patients with neck pain60. Due to the high heterogeneity of this patient group, four different recruitment approaches were applied in this study. Patients with chronic non-specific NP (> 3 months) were recruited from orthopaedic outpatient clinics, physical therapy clinics, via advertisement in social media, as well as from office-based workplace settings (office workers recruited from three different companies). Prior to measurements, patients were assessed for suitability via a telephone interview by an experienced physical therapist. To be enrolled, all patients had to meet the following inclusion criteria: age between 18 and 65 years, and a minimum pain intensity of 3 out of 10 on the visual analogue scale (VAS) at the time of the interview as well as on the day of the measurements. Participants were excluded if they reported upper extremity pain or radiculopathy within the last year; traumatic injury to the head and/or neck; any neurological or vestibular disorders; or diagnosed psychiatric disorders. Furthermore, all participants were required to refrain from taking medication or alcohol 24 h prior to the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the National medical ethics committee of Republic of Slovenia (No. 0120 − 48/2023/8) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later additions.

Measurement protocol

Patients were required to mark pain intensity on a 10-cm VAS61 immediately prior to performing the movement control test. Pain intensity was treated as mild (VAS < 3.5), moderate (VAS between 3.5 and 7.4), and severe (VAS > 7.5) pain61. Movement control test was assessed using the Butterfly test24,34,35 by an experienced physical therapist. During the test, participants were instructed to accurately follow an unpredictably moving target with their head and neck wearing a head-mounted inertial measurement unit (NeckCare, Reykjavik, Iceland). Two repetitions of three different movement trajectories of increasing difficulty (easy, medium, and difficult) were performed in a random order. The three difficulty levels differed in predefined velocities at which the target moved through different trajectories. In addition, the difficulty level increased with introducing higher number of movement direction changes. Target movement path characteristics and test duration were predefined by the NeckSmart software (NeckCare, Reykjavik, Iceland).

Data analysis

Accuracy of head and neck movements during the Butterfly test was analysed using the NeckSmart software applying the following parameters; mean of the relative time spent on the target during each trial expressed as percentage of total trial time (Time-on-target), relative time spent behind the target expressed as percentage of total trial time (undershoot), and time spent in front of the target expressed as percentage of total trial time (overshoot). In addition, amplitude accuracy was calculated expressed as the average mismatch between the target and actual position of the head expressed in millimetres (average amplitude). Averages for all parameters at all difficulty levels individually were used for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Clustering and classification analyses were performed in Orange data mining and machine learning software (Orange 3.36.2, University of Ljubljana, Ljubljana, Slovenia). The final analysis of differences between clusters were performed in SPSS statistical software (SPSS 23.0 software, SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA).

Identifying clusters of patients with NP

Searches for clusters of patients with neck pain were performed using three different clustering methods: (i) hierarchical clustering62, (ii) k-means62 and (iii) Louvain clustering63 to identify the most efficient clustering method. Prior to clustering (and classification), data were pre-processed using z-score normalization of all thirteen classifiers (all Butterfly test parameters and pain intensity level), which were used for identification of clusters.

For further analysis all clustering methods were adjusted to produce two, three or four clusters, except for the Louvain clustering. The performance of each clustering method producing two, three or four clusters was analysed using cluster-wise silhouette scores, the unweighted mean of cluster-wise silhouette scores, and the overall silhouette score (sample-averaged across all observations). The silhouette score measures the quality of clusters, where high scores indicate good clustering (i.e. silhouette scores closer to 1 indicate individual scores belong to the specific cluster, 0 indicates individual scores might belong to some other cluster and − 1 indicate individual scores are in a wrong cluster)64,65. When analysing a five-cluster model the average silhouette scores was low and silhouette plots presented with prominent asymmetries between individual clusters, therefore we decided not to include this model in further analysis.

Classifying individual patients with NP into individual clusters

In the next step, the classification accuracy was analysed to provide information on how individual patients with NP can be classified into previously identified clusters. Performance of six machine learning approaches to classify individual patients into specific clusters were compared; Naïve Bayes66, Random Forest66, Support vector machines66, deep learning Neural network67, kNN68,69 and Gradient Boosting70. Stratified five-fold cross-validation was used on all models to mitigate the effects of unequal cluster sizes and ensure balanced representation during model training and evaluation. The classification performance was analyzed using area under the curve (AUC), classification accuracy (CA), true positive rate (sensitivity) and true negative rate (specificity).

In addition to the supervised classification models, a distance-based baseline was evaluated to represent a simple cluster-assignment rule. Specifically, a 1-nearest neighbour classifier using Euclidean distance on standardized features was applied. This baseline was evaluated using the same stratified five-fold cross-validation framework to avoid information leakage and to ensure direct comparability with the supervised models. The purpose of this analysis was not to improve classification performance, but to assess the extent to which cluster assignment could be approximated by local distance-based rules.

Identifying cluster specific head and neck movement deficits

A SHAP library (Shapley Additive Explanations) was used to explain the output of each classification model when allocating patients with NP to individual clusters71. The SHAP values for each parameter in each cluster were used to identify specific head and neck movement deficits. More specifically, the parameter having the highest contribution to the prediction of the model was plotted first, followed by other parameters in a descending order. Further, each patient`s SHAP value (data point) for a specific parameter is displayed on the horizontal axis presenting how much it affects the model output. Higher deviations from the centre of the horizontal axis indicate higher impact on the prediction of the cluster. Positive values were impactful for predicting the selected cluster while negative values indicate predictions against the selected cluster.

In addition, box-plots were produced for all clusters individually. Differences between clusters for individual parameters (all Butterfly test parameters and VAS score) were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis test and pairwise comparisons with Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni corrections (6 comparisons in four cluster model and 3 comparisons in a 3 cluster model) and statistical significance set at p < .05.

Analysis of relation between pain and cervical movement control deficits for individual clusters

For each individual cluster, the relation between pain intensity level (independent variable) and individual cervical movement control test parameter (dependent variable) was analyzed using the coefficient of determination (0 indicating no relation between pain and parameter and 1 indicating perfect relation between pain and parameter) based on Spearman correlation analysis where statistical significance was set at p < .05.
