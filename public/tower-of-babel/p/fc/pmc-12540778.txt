Crocetin curbs radiation induced intestinal injury by blocking KAT2A/NLRP3 succinylation
Authors: He Chen, Yanping Wang, Yi Xu, Fu Liu, Quanxiao Xu, Lei Dong, Changxiang Yang, Xu Xie, Jincong Yang, Xinping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Irradiation (IR) is known to damage the intestinal mucosal barrier, leading to serious intestinal injury. Crocetin is a natural active compound derived from the enzymatic deglycosylation of crocin in the intestines. The role of crocetin and its underlying molecular mechanism in IR-induced intestinal injury have not yet been reported. Here, we investigated the role of crocetin in protecting against IR-induced intestinal injury and explored its downstream molecular mechanisms. First, we established an in vivo animal model and performed histological analyses. The results indicated that crocetin ameliorated IR-induced intestinal injury. Functionally, crocetin treatment enhanced the viability of intestinal cells subjected to IR while suppressing pyroptosis. Succinylation is a post-translational modification regulating various cellular functions. Here, we found that the mRNA and protein levels of the succinylation regulator, lysine acetyltransferase 2A (KAT2A), were decreased in both in vitro and in vivo models following crocetin treatment. Further investigation revealed that KAT2A stabilizes the NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) protein by promoting the succinylation of NLRP3 at the K21 site. Finally, rescue assays indicated that overexpression of NLRP3 attenuated the effects of KAT2A silencing on the viability and pyroptosis in IR-induced HIECs. Collectively, our study demonstrates that crocetin protects against IR-induced intestinal injury by suppressing KAT2A-mediated succinylation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20740-0.

Introduction

Radiotherapy is one of the most common therapeutic methods for malignant tumors. However, irradiation (IR)-induced damage to normal tissues limits its clinical application1. Previous studies have demonstrated that continuous ionizing radiation can disrupt the intestinal mucosal barrier, leading to life-threatening gastrointestinal (GI) syndromes due to the high radiosensitivity of the small intestine2–4. Radiation enteritis is a common IR-induced intestinal complication during radiotherapy for pelvic, abdominal, and retroperitoneal malignant tumors5. At present, no effective drugs are currently available for preventing and controlling radiation enteritis6. Therefore, the development of effective drugs to mitigate IR-induced intestinal injury is urgently needed.

Crocetin is a natural active compound derived from the hydroalcoholic extract of flower stigmas of Crocus sativus, and is formed via the enzymatic deglycosylation of crocin in the intestines7. Crocetin possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties8–12. Crocetin treatment has been shown to efficiently suppress tumor growth and metastasis13–15. Additionally, a previous study has demonstrated that crocetin protects against burn-induced intestinal injury by inhibiting the associated oxidative stress and inflammatory responses16,17. Compared to current clinical options that are limited by side effects or partial efficacy, such as amitriptyline or mesalazine18,19, crocetin, as a natural compound that provides a multi-target approach, may have good safety20. However, the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the protective effects of crocetin in IR-induced intestinal injury remain unreported.

Succinylation is a recently discovered post-translational modification that modulates various biological processes. It is a dynamic process modulated by several succinyltransferases, including carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A)21 and lysine acetyltransferase 2A (KAT2A)22, and several desuccinylases, including Sirtuin5 (SIRT5) and Sirtuin7 (SIRT7). A growing body of evidence suggests that succinylation regulators play critical roles in the progression of various human diseases by modulating the succinylation level of target proteins. KAT2A has been shown to enhance 14-3-3ζ protein levels by regulating its succinylation level in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma23. Moreover, it can promote glycolysis in gastric cancer by inducing the succinylation of PKM224. KAT2A-mediated succinylation of CTBP1 promotes prostate cancer progression by blocking CTBP1-induced transcriptional repression of CDH125. However, to date, whether KAT2A-mediated succinylation affects IR-induced intestinal injury remains unknown. This study, therefore, focuses on the underlying mechanism of KAT2A-mediated succinylation and its role in IR-induced intestinal injury.

NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome can be activated in inflammatory processes of human diseases, which can induce pyroptosis through transforming pro-caspase-1 into cleaved caspase-1 and cleaving gasdermin (GSDM) family proteins26. Studies have reported that NLRP3 can affect various inflammation-related diseases27–29. However, few studies have revealed the role of NLRP3 in IR-induced intestinal injury and its succinylation modification.

In summary, this study aimed at investigating the role of crocetin in protecting against IR-induced intestinal injury as well as its regulating effect on KAT2A-mediated succinylation of NLRP3.

Materials and methods

Irradiation (IR) and crocetin treatment in intestinal injury model

A total of 24 male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–22 g) were purchased from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China), and housed under standard conditions at the Laboratory Animal Centre of Nanyang First People’s Hospital. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanyang First People’s Hospital. A clinical linear accelerator delivering 6 MV X-rays (Elekta Precise, Stockholm, Sweden) at a dose rate of 0.98 Gy/min was used for in vivo experiments. A three-dimensional (3D) water scanning system (IBA blue phantom, Germany) was used to verify the IR dose. Mice were randomized into five groups: Control (Con), Sitagliptin, IR, IR + Crocetin (25 mg/kg), and IR + Crocetin (50 mg/kg). To establish animal models of IR-induced intestinal injury, all mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane during IR; the sham-irradiated group served as the Con group. Mice in the treatment groups were administered Crocetin (25 mg/kg or 50 mg/kg) via oral gavage 1 h before irradiation, followed by daily administration for three consecutive days16,30. Finally, mice were placed in an induction chamber containing 5% isoflurane (4 L/min oxygen flow rate) for 5 min to ensure deep anesthesia. After an overdose of isoflurane, cervical dislocation was performed to ensure humane euthanasia.

Histological analysis

For all in vivo analyses, a 2-cm segment of the mid-jejunum (≈ 10 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz) was harvested. The isolated small intestinal sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and then cut into 4 μm sections for hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining. The stained sections were then examined under an optical microscope (Leica, Germany), and the results were evaluated and quantified by two blinded pathologists. For bacterial quantification, the distal ileum (last 2 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve) was excised under sterile conditions, luminal contents were collected into pre-weighed tubes containing 1 mL sterile PBS, vortexed for 30 s, serially diluted, and plated onto MacConkey agar. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted after 24 h incubation at 37 °C and expressed as CFU per gram of intestinal content.

Cell culture and treatment

Human intestinal epithelial cells (HIECs) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Hyclone, Hudson, NH, United States) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. HIECs were maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Cells were seeded at 3 × 104 cells/cm2, cultured for 48 h until they reached 80–90% confluency, and used between passages 4 and 8 to ensure phenotypic stability. No differentiation-inducing agents were added; therefore, HIECs remained undifferentiated throughout all experiments.

To establish an IR-induced injury model, HIECs were irradiated with X-rays at a rate of 2 Gy/min and cultured for an additional 24 h with indicated doses (0, 5, 10, or 20 μM) of Crocetin before analysis.

To analyze the stability of the NLRP3 protein, HIECs were treated with 50 μg/ml of cycloheximide (CHX; MCE, NJ, USA) for 1, 4, 8, or 12 h.

Cell transfection

Short hairpin RNA targeting KAT2A (sh-KAT2A) was synthesized by Ribobio (Guangzhou, China) to induce KAT2A silencing. For NLRP3 overexpression, the full-length NLRP3 coding sequence was cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (NLRP3); the empty pcDNA3.1 vector (designated as Vector) was used as a negative control. HIECs, cultured in 6-well plates, were transfected with the aforementioned plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from HIECs and murine intestinal tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). cDNA was then synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). qRT-PCR reactions were performed on an ABI PRISM 7500 system (Applied BioSystems, Waltham, MA, USA) using the SYBR Green I PCR kit (TaKaRa). Relative expression levels were normalized to GAPDH (the internal control) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in this study were listed as follows: human CPT1A forward primer TCCAGTTGGCTTATCGTGGTG, reverse primer TCCAGAGTCCGATTGATTTTTGC; mouse CPT1A forward primer AGATCAATCGGACCCTAGACAC, reverse primer CAGCGAGTAGCGCATAGTCA; human KAT2A forward primer CAGGGTGTGCTGAACTTTGTG, reverse primer TCCAGTAGTTAAGGCAGAGCAA; mouse KAT2A forward primer CTGTTCATGTCTGTTCACAAGGA, reverse primer CCCTGTTCAATGTTAGGCTTCTC; human KAT3B forward primer GCTTCAGACAAGTCTTGGCAT, reverse primer ACTACCAGATCGCAGCAATTC; mouse KAT3B forward primer AGCCAAGCGGCCTAAACTC, reverse primer CGCCACCATTGGTTAGTCCC; human SIRT5 forward primer GCCATAGCCGAGTGTGAGAC, reverse primer CAACTCCACAAGAGGTACATCG; mouse SIRT5 forward primer AATATGGCAGACTTTCGGAAGTG, reverse primer ACACCTGTGATGGGTTTCGAG; human SIRT7 forward primer ACGCCAAATACTTGGTCGTCT, reverse primer AGCACTAACGCTTCTCCCTTT; mouse SIRT7 forward primer GAAGCGGAGCCTACCCTCA, reverse primer GGCAGTACGCTCAGTCACATC; and human GAPDH forward primer GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT, reverse primer GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG; mouse GAPDH forward primer TGACCTCAACTACATGGTCTACA, reverse primer CTTCCCATTCTCGGCCTTG.

Cell viability detection

HIECs (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in each well of a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h. Twenty-four hours after the indicated treatments or transfections, 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent (MedChemExpress, USA) was added to each well. Cell viability was then assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

LDH release assay

HIECs (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in each well of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 overnight. Following the indicated treatments or transfections, LDH release from HIECs was assessed using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

Pyroptosis in HIECs after the indicated treatments or transfections was measured by flow cytometry using the FLICA 660-YVAD-FMK (FLICA® 660 in vitro Active Caspase-1 Detection Kit; ImmunoChemistry Technologies, Bloomington, MN, USA), as described previously31.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP was performed to investigate the interaction between KAT2A and NLRP3. Briefly, HIECs grown in culture flasks were washed with cold PBS and then lysed using Triton lysis buffer. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatant (100 µL) was incubated with 20 µL of Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz, CA, USA) along with either anti-KAT2A (ab217876, 1:30, Abcam, CA, USA) or anti-NLRP3 (ab263899, 1:30, Abcam, CA, USA) at 4 °C overnight. The immunoprecipitants were then washed three times and boiled with 2 × SDS Loading Buffer. Immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by western blot as described below.

In vitro succinylation assay and mutagenesis

Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail). After centrifugation at 12 000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was pre-cleared with 20 µL Protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz, sc-2003) for 30 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein (500 µg) were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg anti-NLRP3 antibody (ab263899, 1:30, Abcam, CA, USA). The immune complexes were captured by adding 30 µL Protein A/G agarose beads for an additional 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with lysis buffer and once with PBS, then boiled in 1 × SDS loading buffer for 10 min. Eluted proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with anti-succinyl-lysine antibody (PTM Bio, PTM-401) diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA/TBST. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using ECL reagent (Bio-Rad) and visualized by the ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

The generic and species-specific succinylation sites prediction server (GPSuc, http://kurata14.bio.kyutech.ac.jp/GPSuc/index.php) was used to predict the potential succinylation sites of NLRP3. The top three loci with the highest Random Forest (RF) scores were selected for further validation. NRF2, NLRP3, and ASC peptides were succinylated in vitro in a 15 µL reaction mixture containing 50 ng/µL of peptide and 0.5 mM succinyl-CoA (Sigma) in 30 mM HEPES (pH 7.4). Following incubation at 37 °C for 3 h, the peptides were desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore).

The K21R, K375R, and K828R mutants were separately generated using a QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis kit (#200,518, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from the indicated HIECs or murine intestinal tissues using RIPA buffer (Servicebio, China). Protein samples were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-Caspase-1 (PA5-77,886, 1:500, Invitrogen, CA, USA), anti-GSDMD-N (PA5-116,815, 1:400, Invitrogen, CA, USA), anti-CPT1A (ab220789, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-KAT2A (ab217876, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-KAT3B (ab275378, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-SIRT5 (ab259967, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-SIRT7 (ab259968, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-NRF2 (ab62352, 1:200, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-NLRP3 (ab263899, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), anti-ASC (ab283684, 1:600, Abcam, CA, USA), and anti-GAPDH (ab9485, 1:2500, Abcam, CA, USA). The membranes were then washed, incubated with secondary antibodies (ab175781, 1:10,000, Abcam, CA, USA), and visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Briefly, supernatants from the indicated HIEC cultures were collected, and the levels of IL-1β and IL-18 were measured using an Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay Kit (Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA) and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Differences between two groups or among multiple groups were compared using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, respectively. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Crocetin ameliorates IR-induced intestinal injury

To investigate the potential role of crocetin in IR-induced intestinal injury, we established animal models and performed in vivo experiments. The morphological changes in intestinal tissues obtained from the different groups of mice were observed by H&E staining. Compared with the control group, irradiated mice exhibited significant tissue damage, as indicated by disorganized intestinal crypts and mucosal edema, whereas IR-induced intestinal injury was ameliorated following crocetin treatment (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we observed that the intestinal bacterial colony counts were markedly increased in mice that received IR, while this increase was dose-dependently reversed by treatment with crocetin (Fig. 1B). A lower intestinal villus height was detected in the IR group compared with the control group, while villus height increased following crocetin treatment (Fig. 1C). Moreover, the number of crypts in mice that received IR was reduced compared with control mice, while the number was increased by crocetin in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1D). Crocetin regulates the viability and pyroptosis in cell model of IR-induced intestinal injury

To elucidate the underlying cellular mechanisms, we next established a cell model of IR-induced intestinal injury and performed a series of functional assays. CCK-8 assays revealed a decrease in the viability of HIECs after IR compared with the control group, while the viability of IR-induced HIECs was restored by treatment with crocetin in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, changes in pyroptosis were analyzed by flow cytometry. The results showed that the pyroptosis rate of HIECs was significantly increased after IR but was efficiently reduced by treatment with crocetin in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2B, C). Meanwhile, the levels of pyroptosis-related proteins were measured in HIECs under the same conditions. It was observed that the protein levels of both active Caspase-1 and GSDMD-N were higher in IR-induced HIECs compared with control HIECs, while both levels were dose-dependently decreased by crocetin treatment (Fig. 2D). Similarly, we found that LDH release was increased following IR, but attenuated by crocetin treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, we determined whether excessive inflammation occurred during IR and whether crocetin could alleviate inflammation in IR-induced HIECs. As detected by ELISA, the levels of the two inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β and IL-18, were increased in the IR-induced injury model, while these increases were significantly reversed after crocetin treatment (Fig. 2F, G). Crocetin inhibits KAT2A expression in IR-induced HIECs

Succinylation is a post-translational modification that regulates various cellular functions. We therefore investigated whether crocetin could regulate succinylation in IR-induced intestinal injury. First, we explored the expression of succinyltransferases in the different groups of animal models. As revealed by qRT-PCR and western blot, among the enzymes tested, only the mRNA and protein levels of KAT2A were significantly higher in IR-induced mice, and were subsequently reduced in IR-induced mice that received crocetin treatment (Fig. 3A, B). Consistent with the in vivo results, only the levels of KAT2A mRNA and protein were increased significantly in IR-induced HIECs and were subsequently reduced by crocetin treatment (Fig. 3C, D). Silencing of KAT2A enhances the viability and suppresses pyroptosis in IR-induced HIECs

To determine the specific role of KAT2A in IR-induced intestinal injury, we performed loss-of-function assays by silencing KAT2A with specific shRNAs. CCK-8 data showed that KAT2A silencing significantly restored the viability of HIECs following IR (Fig. 4A). Next, IR-induced pyroptosis of HIECs was suppressed by KAT2A silencing (Fig. 4B, C). Additionally, the IR-induced increase in the protein levels of both Caspase-1 and GSDMD-N was decreased following the silencing of KAT2A (Fig. 4D). Similarly, the LDH release induced by IR was inhibited by KAT2A silencing (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the increased levels of the two pyroptosis-related inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β and IL-18, in the IR-induced injury model were reduced by KAT2A silencing (Fig. 4F, G). KAT2A interacts with NLRP3 to mediate the succinylation of NLRP3

NRF2, NLRP3, and ASC are inflammatory factors known to be pyroptosis modulators in various human diseases. We therefore investigated the role that KAT2A plays in modulating their expression. First, we used western blotting to analyze the levels of these three factors in HIECs under different conditions. As shown in Fig. 5A, all three factors were upregulated in HIECs after IR, whereas their expression levels were decreased after KAT2A silencing. Subsequently, we detected the succinylation levels of NRF2, NLRP3, and ASC in the indicated HIECs. It was observed that only the succinylation level of NLRP3 was increased in IR-induced HIECs but was significantly reduced by KAT2A silencing (Fig. 5B). Moreover, IR treatment also increased the NLRP3 protein expression and its succinylation level in intestinal tissues of mice, and crocetin significantly suppressed the NLRP3 protein expression and its succinylation level (Fig. S1). We further explored the relationship between KAT2A and NLRP3. Co-IP assays revealed that KAT2A directly interacted with NLRP3 in HIECs (Fig. 5C). We then predicted the potential sites for NLRP3 succinylation using GPSuc online tool, and the the top three loci with the highest RF scores were selected for further validation (Fig. 5D). To identify the actual functional site, we mutated each of the three sites and then performed succinylation assays. The results shown in Fig. 5E demonstrate that mutation of the K21 site abolished the succinylation of NLRP3, indicating that KAT2A modulates the succinylation of NLRP3 at the K21 site. Finally, we investigated whether KAT2A-mediated succinylation altered the stability of the NLRP3 protein using CHX. The results suggested that KAT2A silencing accelerated the CHX-induced destabilization of the NLRP3 protein (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, mutation of K21 site also accelerated the degradation of NLRP3 protein (Fig. S2). Overexpressing NLRP3 attenuates the effects of KAT2A silencing on the viability and pyroptosis of IR-induced HIECs

Given that our above findings showed a regulatory effect of KAT2A on NLRP3, we next investigated whether KAT2A-mediated NLRP3 expression regulated the viability and pyroptosis of IR-induced HIECs by performing rescue assays. Prior to functional assays, NLRP3 was successfully overexpressed in HIECs by transfecting cells with a pcDNA3.1 vector expressing NLRP3 (Fig. 6A). CCK-8 assays revealed that NLRP3 overexpression attenuated the increase in viability caused by KAT2A silencing in IR-induced HIECs (Fig. 6B). In addition, the suppression of pyroptosis in IR-induced HIECs by KAT2A silencing was reversed by the overexpression of NLRP3 (Fig. 6C, D). Moreover, the reduction in the protein levels of both Caspase-1 and GSDMD-N caused by KAT2A silencing in IR-induced HIECs was rescued by the overexpression of NLRP3 (Fig. 6E). The KAT2A silencing-induced inhibition of LDH release from IR-induced HIECs was reversed after overexpression of NLRP3 (Fig. 6F). Moreover, the reduced levels of the two pyroptosis-related inflammatory cytokines caused by KAT2A silencing were restored by NLRP3 overexpression (Fig. 6G, H). Discussion

Given that the gastrointestinal system is highly sensitive to irradiation, IR-induced intestinal injury often limits the application of radiotherapy and compromises therapeutic efficacy32. Therefore, alleviating IR-induced intestinal toxicity is critical for improving the long-term survival of cancer patients33,34. A growing body of evidence suggests that crocetin exerts therapeutic effects in various human diseases. For instance, crocetin ameliorates non-alcoholic fatty liver disease35; aids in recovery from DSS-induced colitis30; and suppresses human colorectal cancer progression36. In this study, we investigated the effects of crocetin in both in vivo and in vitro models of IR-induced intestinal injury. Our in vivo experimental data indicated that crocetin ameliorated IR-induced intestinal injury, as evidenced by the restoration of intestinal crypt architecture and reduced mucosal edema, a decrease in bacterial colony counts, an increase in villus height, and an increase in the number of crypts. Furthermore, our in vitro experiments supported these findings. Through CCK-8 assays and LDH detection, we determined that crocetin treatment enhanced the viability and reduced cell death in IR-induced HIECs. Radiation can trigger multiple death modalities depending on dose, tissue type, and time-frame examined. Previous studies have reported apoptosis in intestinal epithelial cells exposed to moderate doses or necroptosis in pulmonary tissue after high-dose irradiation37,38. In the present study, we observed caspase-1 cleavage, the generation of GSDMD-N, and the release of IL-1β/IL-18 within 24 h of IR exposure—molecular signatures that are specific for pyroptosis. Pyroptosis appears to be the predominant form of programmed cell death in this study. Moreover, crocetin suppressed the IR-induced pyroptosis of HIECs. Importantly, these protective effects were observed without any overt systemic toxicity, underscoring crocetin’s favorable safety profile. Together, these data demonstrated the therapeutic potential of crocetin in mitigating IR-induced intestinal injury.

KAT2A is a succinyltransferase that regulates the succinylation of its target proteins, thereby influencing the progression of a variety of human diseases. For example, KAT2A acts as a histone H3 succinyltransferase by coupling with the α-KGDH complex to regulate tumor progression22. To our knowledge, our present study is the first to explore the effect of crocetin on KAT2A-mediated succinylation in IR-induced intestinal injury. We found that crocetin treatment could significantly suppress KAT2A expression in both in vivo and in vitro models of IR-induced intestinal injury. Although a proinflammatory role of KAT2A has been reported in some diseases39,40, its specific functional role in IR-induced intestinal injury remains unclear. Here, we demonstrated that silencing of KAT2A enhanced viability and suppressed pyroptosis in IR-induced HIECs.

Previous studies have indicated that NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis aggravates intestinal injury. For example, NLRP3 inflammasome activation promotes mitophagy and pyroptosis during intestinal inflammation41; and NLRP3-induced pyroptosis exacerbates intestinal ischemia–reperfusion injury42. As previously demonstrated, NLRP3 can cooperate with NRF2 and ASC to modulate the inflammatory process43–46. Thus, we used western blotting to analyze the levels of these three factors in HIECs after KAT2A silencing. We found that KAT2A positively regulated the protein levels of all of these three factors, but only mediated the succinylation of NLRP3. Further mechanistic investigation confirmed that KAT2A stabilized the NLRP3 protein by mediating the succinylation of NLRP3 at the K21 site. Finally, we carried out rescue assays, and the results revealed that the protective effects of KAT2A silencing on the viability and pyroptosis of IR-induced HIECs were attenuated by NLRP3 overexpression. Therefore, we conclude that KAT2A exacerbates IR-induced intestinal injury through the succinylation-mediated stabilization of the NLRP3 protein.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted in male C57BL/6 mice; sex differences in radiation sensitivity and crocetin pharmacokinetics remain unaddressed. Second, our analyses focused on the small intestine; accumulating evidence indicates that crocetin exerts broad anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects that may also confer protection in the large bowel30. Future work should therefore systematically examine whether crocetin affords radioprotective effects throughout the whole intestine, including the large bowel. Third, fractionated or lower-dose exposures typical of clinical radiotherapy regimens require validation. Future work should incorporate human intestinal organoids derived from both small and large bowel to test donor-specific responses and to screen for optimal analogues. Ultimately, a phase I safety and pharmacokinetic trial in patients receiving pelvic radiotherapy is warranted to translate the current pre-clinical findings into clinical practice.

Taken together, our results indicate that crocetin can ameliorate IR-induced intestinal injury. Mechanistically, crocetin downregulates KAT2A, which in turn enhances the stability of NLRP3 by mediating its succinylation. Our findings may contribute to the development of novel methods for the mitigation of intestinal injury during radiotherapy.
