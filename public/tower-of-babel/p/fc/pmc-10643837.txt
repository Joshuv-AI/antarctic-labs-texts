Validation of publication pressure questionnaire‐revised into Chinese nurses from a university hospital
Authors: Xinxue Xi, Jing Mao, Tieying Zeng, Dandan Ju
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo translate and validate the Publication Pressure Questionnaire‐revised (PPQ‐r) among the Chinese nurses.DesignA cross‐sectional descriptive study.MethodsThe classical forward/backward translation of the PPQ‐r was conducted first, and then the psychometric values were tested using the data collected in September, 2021 among 924 Chinese nurses from a university hospital in China by calculating its content validity index, construct validity, both convergent and discriminant validity, and internal consistency reliability following the COSMIN guidelines.ResultsThe proposed three‐factor structure of original PPQ‐r cannot be confirmed in present study. With exploratory factor analysis, a new two‐factor structure with 9 items was extracted, accounting for 62.718% of the variance. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated satisfactory goodness of all fit index values: χ 2 = 75.53; df = 23; χ 2/df = 3.28; CFI = 0.973; TLI = 0.958; RMSEA = 0.07 and SRMR = 0.047. Both sub‐scales yielded good internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha being 0.845 and 0.876, respectively. The Chinese version of PPQ‐r could be accepted as a reliable and valid tool to measure the Chinese nurses' publication pressure.Patient or Public ContributionParticipation of nurses in this study was limited to the data provided through participant survey.Keywords: China, nurses, publication pressure, research pressure, validation study

INTRODUCTION

Scientific output has been adopted as a key performance indicator to evaluate the scientific work for both researchers individually and institutes organizationally (Retzer & Jurasinski, 2009). Scholarly papers, as one of the main form of scientific output, are primarily required for individual researcher's career development and international recognition (Wester, 2016). Publication of scholarly papers has been accepted as an integrated component of scientific practices, and is still functioning as main method of knowledge sharing and advanced scientific inquiry via distributing information, encouraging formal discussions among professionals, and so on. However, scholarly papers publication activities could also bring some effects back to the researchers, research institutes, as well as the general publication atmosphere given the ‘publish or perish’ culture influence (Génova & de la Vara, 2018). In particular, some of the effects may be less desired and needed to be intervened properly, such as publication pressure, hyper‐competition in the scientific field (Fink et al., 2023; Kumar, 2018), etc.

Publication pressure refers to the subjective pressure perceived by the paper writers from their feelings of having to publish their research findings (Haven, Bouter, et al., 2019), and it may bring mixed effects on research individuals and institutes. While publication pressure was reported to be a salutary incentive for high production of high quality scientific papers (Siegel et al., 2018; van Dalen & Henkens, 2012) and culture formation of scientific knowledge silos (Obradović, 2019), the findings from other more studies have shown that excessive publication pressure could cause detrimental consequences to both researcher individuals and the publication enterprise (Tijdink et al., 2014). Publication pressure was reported to adversely affect the researchers' character development (Farlin & Majewsky, 2013) and also to be closely related to the researchers' mental health, and the higher publication pressure they perceived, the more likely they would develop burnout (emotional exhaustion) symptoms (Duke et al., 2020). Moreover, the publication pressure was found to be significantly related with scientific misconducts (Moreels, 2018; Quaia & Vernuccio, 2022), exactly treated as strongest predictor for possible misbehaviors during scientific practices (Maggio et al., 2019). In details, publication pressure may drive researchers to selectively publish their research findings (Sharma & Verma, 2019), or have plagiarism inadvertently or not (Bahl & Bahl, 2021). Regarding the general scientific enterprise and the publication atmosphere, publication pressure may encourage haste instead of patience, nurture research lacking of relevance, creativity, and innovation, decrease publications quality and even lead to worthless publications (de Carvalho, 2006; Farlin & Majewsky, 2013; Sarewitz, 2016; Sharma & Verma, 2018). In sum, publication pressure has both positive and negative effects on researchers and scientific enterprise at large.

In nursing discipline, publication pressure is also common just like in many different disciplines including management, humanities, social science, natural science, and biomedicine (Haven, Bouter, et al., 2019; Quaia & Vernuccio, 2022; Sharma & Verma, 2018; van Dalen & Henkens, 2012). The nurses, especially those working in the university hospitals, are experiencing noticeable pressure to publish academic papers (Ness et al., 2014), given the following realities: lack of time, multiple roles (clinician, educator, academician, management staff, etc.), high development requirements of nursing from a vocation to a profession and government's expectation of increasing nursing productivity (Keen, 2007). Moreover, nurses' lack of confidence and insufficient competency of writing and publishing papers is also considered as another significant factor for their high publication pressure in that it may finally lead to a shortfall of nursing papers publication (Keen, 2007; Timmins et al., 2022). Correspondingly, some interventions have been suggested to cope with nurses' publication pressure in western countries through providing nurses academic writing courses (Ansryan et al., 2019), or introducing evidence‐based approaches to increase nurses' publication rates (Fischer‐Cartlidge, 2020). However, the problem of publication pressure among nurses in Asian countries seemingly has not received the same attention as their western counterpart.

It has been the time to think highly of publication pressure for the Chinese nurses thinking of its seemingly becoming much stronger, however, there is a lack of proper tool for accurate measurement of it among the Chinese nurses. Nursing profession and nursing science in China, which has largest volume of nursing staff in Asia, has undergone fast development since its accreditation as one of the top ranked disciplines by the Ministry of Education of People's Republic of China in 2011. With no surprise, scientific output including paper publication, has been referenced as valuable indicator to evaluate the academic development of both nurse individuals and nursing discipline in such circumstances, just like other academic staff group and disciplines. More specifically, scholarly papers publication, as one of the critical components of Chinese nurse performance appraisal index systems (Gao et al., 2015; Li et al., 2014; Rao et al., 2011; Yang & Wu, 2019), was adopted to appraise the performance of nurses from different positions (head nurses or clinical staff), different work settings (inpatient or outpatient), as well as different nursing specialties (general ward or surgery nurse) in last decade, thinking the reality that clinical nurses still role as main force for nursing research practices in mainland China. Despite the precise measurement of their publication pressure would lay solid foundation for further studies for its causes, consequences, and even the interventions, however, the research of publication pressure among the Chinese nurses is still scarce. Thus, a valid and reliable instrument is thought to be urgently needed for accurate investigation of the Chinese nurses' publication pressure.

Publication Pressure Questionnaire (PPQ), regarded as the first quantitative instrument for publication pressure measurement, was initially developed in 2014 (Tijdink et al., 2014) to assess the psychological pressure to publish papers among medical scientists and 14 items were designed covering three domains, namely (a) publication pressure individually experienced; (b) publication pressure in general from the research settings; and (c) publication pressure relevant to individual's academic career development, like tenure, promotion, etc. The statistics of psychometrical properties confirmation indicated that PPQ seems being a valid and reliable tool to measure publication pressure among medical scientists (Tijdink et al., 2014). However, as PPQ has been used in various studies, some limitations gradually came into light and motivated its revision. Firstly, it was initially constructed and validated among bio‐medicine professors and majority of its items were exploring the researchers' attitude towards publication culture and atmosphere rather than their publication pressure, which may distort result interpretation and limit its generalizability. Secondly, its convergent validity is less satisfactory because of its just moderate correlation with relevant sub‐scale scores of Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) based on the theoretical assumption that higher correlation should be observed if burnout is a possible outcome of publication pressure (Tijdink et al., 2014). Lastly, its divergent validity is also arguable in that some possibly relevant constructs of work psychology were not mentioned in the PPQ such as job insecurity, work pressure and work‐home interference (Gillespie et al., 2001; Kinman & Jones, 2008; Tytherleigh et al., 2005). Thus, a revised version of PPQ (PPQ‐r) was developed in 2019 based theoretically on work stress models (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019) and has been testified to be valid and reliable among different researcher populations from all disciplinary fields and academic ranks (Haven, Bouter, et al., 2019).

To our knowledge, despite the publication pressure has dual effects on both individual nurse researchers and nursing profession in general, there has been very limited research conducted on publication pressure among the Chinese nurses. Given the Asian and Western differences of social culture and research atmosphere, we insist the necessity of validating PPQ‐r among the Chinese nurses before its involvement in publication pressure measurement in mainland China. Therefore, the objective of this study was to translate the English version of PPQ‐r into Chinese and calculate its psychometric properties in a Chinese nurse sample from a university hospital in mainland China.

METHODS

Aim

To translate and psychometrically test the PPQ‐r among Chinese nurses.

Methodology

Design

A cross‐sectional, descriptive design was employed for this study. Two phases of research were adopted, including (a) translation and cultural adaptation of the PPQ‐r and (b) validating it among nurses from a university hospital in mainland China. This study was approved ethnically by the research ethics committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China (number: TJ‐IRB20210908) in 3 September 2021.

Subjects/participants

A convenience sampling method was employed and all nurses currently working in a university hospital in mainland China, were invited to participate in this survey. This selected hospital is the largest one in Central China and has around 5000 nursing staff, including 4248 registered nurses. Criteria for inclusion were (a) age over 18 years, (b) registered nurse working over 1 year, (c) be engaged in research for at least 1 day per week, (d) be willing to participate in the survey voluntarily.

Data collection

Data collection occurred between 9 September 2021 and 16 September 2021 in two consecutive stages. Firstly, an online software (https://www.wjx.cn/) was selected by the researcher group and an online survey link was obtained automatically from the website. The software was selected given its characteristics of (a) being free, (b) being open accessible to the public and (c) being capable to ensure participant's information confidentiality in that only the individual participant and the researchers were authorized to access the results of the survey. Secondly, all registered nurses working in this hospital were invited to participate in the survey. Both an introduction letter and the online survey link were send to each of them with the help of hospital‐based information system. The introduction letter was provided to ensure participants' rights of study purpose awareness, individual information confidentiality, voluntary participation and withdrawal without any negative influences or penalties. An informed consent question with yes/no responses was put in the very beginning of the questionnaire before individual participant data collection commencement, and the online survey would just terminate if the participant chose the option of ‘no’. As for the sample size, there is no universal agreement about the minimal number of samples requirement for factor analysis, but some recommendations from the published articles could be referenced, for example, 3–20 times of the number of variables (Mundfrom et al., 2005). It is generally accepted that larger samples are better, so we select the subjects‐to‐variables ratio of 20:1 as the standard for samples calculation in our study. The Publication Pressure Questionnaire‐revised (PPQ‐r) contained 18 items, so the sample size theoretically should not be less than 360. Thinking of possible 20% sample loss, the sample size theoretically should not be less than 432. We conducted both exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis in two different samples for our study to test its construct validity, so the total sample number should not be less than 864. In reality, 961 clinical nurses participated in the survey of our study and 924 samples were finally involved into data analysis with 37 samples being excluded for incomplete information.

Instruments

Socio‐demographic and work/life‐related information

An information sheet was designed to collect nurses' demographic data, including age, gender, marital status, initial education, highest educational, length of employment, working unit, professional qualification, current occupation position. In addition, four other indicators were also included to describe participants' financial, work and life status, namely as current financial burden, current work stress, current work satisfaction, and current life satisfaction.

Chinese version of publication pressure questionnaire‐revised (PPQ‐r‐C)

The English version of revised PPQ‐r contained 18 self‐reported items allocating into three sub‐scales: stress sub‐scale (6 items), attitude sub‐scale (6 items), and resources sub‐scale (6 items) (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019). Response options were grouped on a five‐point Likert scale reporting the extent of respondents' agreement with the statements (1 = totally disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = not sure, 4 = agree, 5 = totally agree). However, for negative statements, they would be scored inversely, e.g. 5 meaning ‘totally disagree’ rather than ‘totally agree’. Each sub‐scale of PPQ‐r not the whole scale could be scored reasonably, and each sub‐scale score was computed by summing the average of all its items. The publication stress sub‐scale calculates the stress experienced by the academician because of their feelings of having to publish papers, and the higher the score is, the higher publication stress the researcher perceives. The publication attitude sub‐scale reflects researchers' attitudes towards publication pressure, and a higher score of this sub‐scale means that the researcher has a more negative attitude towards publication atmosphere and publication pressure. The publication resources sub‐scale includes possible protective factors for publication pressure prevention, and a higher score indicates that the researcher has less resources available to fight against publication stress. Psychometric results from the previous study showed satisfactory reliability for all three sub‐scales, and Cronbach's α coefficients for stress sub‐scale, attitude scale and resource scale are 0.804, 0.777 and 0.754, respectively (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019).

For the present study, a classical forward‐backward translation procedure was involved to ensure proper cross‐cultural adaptation of the original PPQ‐r after obtaining empowerment from its developer (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019). Five steps were conducted to formulate the Chinese version of (PPQ‐r‐C) before its psychometric testing. Step 1, the forward translation, during which 2 bilingual experts translated the PPQ‐r from English to Chinese independently. One of them was Chinese master student majoring in English language and having no knowledge about nursing discipline and nursing research. The other person was a nurse with more than 10 years working experience related to clinical nursing care, nursing teaching, nursing management and nursing research and familiar with the paper publication and publication pressure. Step 2, a research group, consisting of one nursing professor, one senior nursing magazine editor, two PhD nursing students and three nursing research staff with master's degree, worked collaboratively to reach consensus about the Chinese translations after several rounds of discussion and minor modifications to the items. Step 3, backward translation was conducted by two other bilingual experts having no idea about the original PPQ and PPQ‐r. The backward translated version was finally confirmed after discussion and comparison by the same research group as the Step 2. Step 4, the pretest version of PPQ‐r‐C, was confirmed by including all members of the research group of Step 2 and forward and backward translators to compare the back translation with the initial Chinese version and the original English version item by item. Step 5, a pilot study among 20 clinical nurse researchers to ensure its cultural adaptation, and consensus was reached on the final pre‐test version of PPQ‐r‐C.

Data analysis

Two authors of this study worked collaboratively to analyse the data and both SPSS version 23.0 and Mplus 17.0 were applied to this study. Descriptive statistics used to describe respondents' demographic characteristics, work/life related experience and their responses to the PPQ‐r‐C include mean, median, range, standard deviation and frequency.

A seven‐member expert group, consisting of 5 experienced clinical nursing experts, 1 associate professor from a nursing school of university, and 1 senior editor of nursing publication office, was organized to evaluate the content validity of the PPQ‐r‐C. Both scale/sub‐scale content validity indexes (S‐CVI) and item content validity indexes (I‐CVI) were calculated to confirm these indicators. All the experts were invited to rate the relevance of each item of the PPQ‐r‐C on a four point scale (1 = totally irrelevant, 2 = irrelevant, 3 = relevant and 4 = totally relevant). For each item, we summed the total number of experts giving scores of 3 and 4 and then divided this value by seven to get its I‐CVI. And then, we summed all the I‐CVI values of the items within one sub‐scale (or the whole PPQ‐r‐C) and divided it by the total number of items contained in that sub‐scale (or the whole PPQ‐r‐C) to obtain its sub‐scale (or scale) CVI.

For construct validity, four steps were conducted. First, the total samples were randomly assigned into two sub‐samples: Sample 1 and Sample 2. Secondly, CFA was carried out to examine the model fit of the PPQ‐r‐C in relation to previous structure demonstrated in former studies using Sample 1. Good fitness of the model was established based on the following criteria: (a) a χ 2/df ratio less than 5.0 (Bollen, 1989); (b) a comparative fit index (CFI) ≥0.95 (Hu & Bentler, 1999); (c) a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) less than 0.08 (Wu, 2009); and (d) a standardized root‐mean‐square residual (RMSR) less than 0.05 (Wu, 2009). Thirdly, EFA using principal axis factoring analysis with varimax rotation would be employed to explore the structure of the PPQ‐r‐C among Sample 1 if the results of CFA were less satisfied. We would extract the factors with eigenvalue higher than 1 and scree plots were also used to determine the number of factors. Items with loading values higher than 0.4 would be kept and assigned to a specific factor, otherwise, it would be removed (Wu et al., 2018). In addition, no items could load on more than one factor. Fourthly, CFA would be conducted again in Sample 2 to confirm the structure of PPQ‐r‐C obtained from the EFA and the results would be assessed based on the indicators listed above (Wu, 2009).

Meanwhile, both convergent and discriminant validity of PPQ‐r‐C were also explored. Convergent validity, reflected by how the individual items were related to their specific factors, was examined by both composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) (Hair et al., 2010). The convergent validity would be taken to be adequate when its AVE value higher than 0.5 and CR value higher than 0.7 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The divergent validity was assessed by comparing the square root of the AVE of one construct with the value of its inter‐construct correlation, and the divergent validity value would be considered to be acceptable when its square root of the AVE of one construct higher than its correlation with other constructs (Hair et al., 2010).

Given the reliability of the PPQ‐r‐C, internal consistency reliability values were included. Cronbach's α coefficients were calculated to test the internal consistency reliability of the PPQ‐r‐C and its sub‐scales and a coefficient value of higher than 0.7 was taken to be acceptable (Terwee et al., 2007).

Results

Characteristics of the participants

In total, 924 eligible participants were surveyed to explore their socio‐demographics and work/life related characteristics (as shown in Table 1). After being randomly assigned into two sub‐samples, the statistical analysis indicated no difference regarding the data distribution between these two sub‐samples with p values being 0.125 and 0.938. While all the participants aged in their 30s, among both the samples, they averagely had been working in clinic for 8–10 years. Majority of the participants were females and married. Around 70% of them had primary education as bachelors and about 90% had the highest education as bachelors. Over 95% of them were qualified as level 2 or 3 registered nurses, and nearly 90% worked in clinic providing direct care to patients. In addition, all the participants had moderate levels of financial burden, work stress, work satisfaction and life satisfaction.

Content validity

The S‐CVI values of the PPQ‐r‐C and its two domains were 0.968, 0.943, and 1, respectively. As for I‐CVI for the nine items of PPQ‐r‐C, the values ranged from 0.857 to 1, only two items (items no.2, 4) were 0.857 and all other seven items got 1.0. All the data listed above showed that the PPQ‐r‐C had excellent content validity among the Chinese nurse population.

Construct validity

CFA was conducted in one sub‐sample to inspect whether the factor structure of the PPQ‐r demonstrated in previous studies (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019) could be replicated in the present study and the result showed poor fit statistics: χ 2 = 999.560; df = 132; χ 2/df = 7.572; CFI = 0.744; TLI = 0.703; RMSEA = 0.119 and SRMR = 0.126. Therefore, EFA was carried out to explore a new factor structure of PPQ‐r among the Chinese nurses.

Exploratory factor analysis produced a satisfactory Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin Statistics (KMO = 0.798) and Bartlett Test of Sphericity (χ 2 = 1560.238, p < 0.001), indicating that the sample size (n = 462) was adequate for factor analysis. The analysis initially suggested a five‐factor structure, explaining 63.886% of the variance. The results indicated that both item 10 (‘I can combine working on my publications with my other tasks’), and item 11 (‘At home, I do not feel stressed about my publications’) were covered in one factor; the item 12 (‘When working on a publication, many decisions about the content of the paper are outside my control’), and item 13 (‘I cannot cope with all aspects of publishing my papers’) were covered in another factor. So, these four items (item 10, item 11, item 12, and item 13) were deleted in that their corresponding factor contains only two or fewer items. After loading another new matrix, a three‐factor structure was obtained, explaining 59.844% of the variance. Item 14 (‘The current publication climate puts pressure on relationships with fellow‐researchers’), and item 15 (‘I suspect that publication pressure leads some colleagues (whether intentionally or not) to cut corners’) were deleted because they loaded more than 0.4 in two factors. After loading another new matrix again, a new three‐factor structure was obtained, explaining 62.119% of the variance. One of the three factors contained only item 16 (‘My colleagues judge me mainly on the basis of my publications’), and item 17 (‘Publication pressure harms science’), so these two items were eliminated. And then, another new matrix was loaded again, a two–factor structure was obtained, explaining 59.020% of the variance. Item 18 (‘Colleagues maintain their administrative and teaching skills well, despite publication pressure’) was finally deleted given its low communality of 0.348. Thus, the final two‐factor structure was identified based on the extraction criteria mentioned above (Wu et al., 2018), and two factors were named as ‘perceived pressure sub‐scale’ (items no. 1–5) and ‘resource sub‐scale’ (items no. 6–9), as shown in Table 2. The two factors together explained 62.718% of the variance: PPQ‐r‐C factor 1 ‘publication demands sub‐scale’ (33.966%), and PPQ‐r‐C factor 2 ‘publication resources sub‐scale’ (28.751%).

Convergent validity and divergent validity

The convergent validity of PPQ‐r‐C was assessed by both CR and AVE and the results were shown in Table 4. Both ‘publication demands sub‐scale’ and ‘publication resources sub‐scale’ domains had relatively high CR values as 0.85, and 0.86, respectively. DISCUSSION

Given the Asian and Western differences of social cultures and research atmosphere, it was deemed essential to explore and validate the PPQ‐r before its application among the Chinese nurses. The present study was conducted to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Chinese version of PPQ‐r, and the results show that the two‐factor structured Chinese version of PPQ‐r (PPQ‐r‐C) has good validity and reliability among nurses from university hospital setting in mainland China, supporting its transferability to Asian nurse population for their publication pressure measurement.

The three‐factor structure (i.e., stress sub‐scale, attitude sub‐scale, and resource sub‐scale) of the original PPQ‐r (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019) was not confirmed in our samples by using CFAs, and the statistics of the following EFAs and CFAs demonstrated a psychometrically stable two‐factor structure (i.e., publication demands sub‐scale, and publication resources sub‐scale) in the Chinese nurses with satisfactory model fitness indexes, providing explanations of 62.718% of the total variance. The variation of the factor structure of PPQ‐r‐C from PPQ‐r may be explained by the philosophy of work stress models. Previous studies showed that PPQ‐r was developed theoretically based on the work stress models (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019), especially the Job Demands‐Resources model of burnout (JD‐R) with the opinion that one individual would be likely to experience stress and even burnout symptoms once the imbalance between job demands and job resources happened (Demerouti et al., 2001). The stress usually occur when the employee experiences high job demands but has low job resources available. Thus, from the perspectives of the work stress models, it seems much more conceivable to include and cluster all the items related to publication pressure into two domains: the publication demands, and the publication resources domains, just as the factor structure of PPQ‐r‐C shown in our study. By contrast, the ‘attitude sub‐scale’ of the original PPQ‐r may not be so suitable and may be better to be deleted for developing a much more theoretically coherent factor structure for the PPQ‐r‐C. However, the JD‐R model also includes another new component, engagement, compared to the classic stress and coping theory, which describing the willingness of the employee to perform great and to find new positive and constructive challenges within their work (Gerain & Zech, 2019). Moreover, the complex interaction between the job demands, resources, as well as engagement and the effect outcomes (burnout or stress) has been observed in reality. From this view, it may inspire us to borrow the concept of engagement into research and academic publication issues and potentially betterment the construct of publication pressure by developing several items to describe the researchers' willingness to actively participate in the research activities and get positive motivation from the research conducts and academic papers publication.

In terms of the specific items, compared with the PPQ‐r which has 18 items allocating into three domains (6 items for each domain), fewer items are contained in PPQ‐r‐C which just has 9 items clustered in two domains (5 items in ‘publication demands sub‐scale’, 4 items in ‘publication resources sub‐scale’) with deletion of other 9 items (items no. 10–18) from PPQ‐r. In general, the item clusters of the ‘publication resources sub‐scale’ of PPQ‐r‐C is highly similar to that of the ‘resource sub‐scale’ identified by the PPQ‐r developers (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019). However, the items on the other two factors (‘stress sub‐scale’ and ‘attitude sub‐scale’) from PPQ‐r loaded onto one factor, forming the new sub‐scale named as ‘publication demands sub‐scale’ in PPQ‐r‐C. More obviously, only one of the six items (item 5 ‘In my opinion the pressure to publish scientific articles has become too high’) of the ‘attitude sub‐scale’ in PPQ‐r has been maintained to the ‘publication demands sub‐scale’ of PPQ‐r‐C after several rounds of EFAs, with other 5 items (items no. 14–18) deleted. This could not be surprising if we look at the research question closely based on the definition and coverage of concepts of ‘demands and resources’ within work stress theories. In details, within the JD‐R model, all the job characteristics that require sustained efforts or cost input would be called job demands, such as work pressure, blurred roles or role conflict, time insufficiency, work insecurity, etc. Meanwhile, all the job characteristics that could promote work goals achievement, individual staff development, and save the work costs would be defined as job resources, like the family or co‐worker supports, individual career advancement, or work autonomy, and so on (Demerouti et al., 2001). Similarly, for publication pressure measurement, all the items describing the researchers' publication demands and resources should be included, and any other items unrelated, on the contrary, should not be covered. This may provide a full explanation for the deletion of 9 items (items no. 10–18) from PPQ‐r in that all of these 9 items are describing some other related issues rather than publication pressure (Demerouti et al., 2001; Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019). For example, the item 14 (The current publication climate puts pressure on relationships with fellow‐researchers), item15 (I suspect that publication pressure leads some colleagues (whether intentionally or not) to cut corners), and item17 (Publication pressure harms science) are actually describing the possible effects of publication pressure on either the science integrity or interpersonal relationship among academicians, rather than the publication demands or resources.

Although the PPQ‐r‐C instrument was shorter and included fewer domains and items, it is still regarded to be valid and reliable adequately for publication pressure measurement among the Chinese nurses thinking of its satisfactory psychometric properties. The smallest number of its I‐CVI and S‐CVI values was 0.857, and majority of these values are as high as over 0.9, indicating excellent content validity. Cronbach's reliability index of the two sub‐scales and the entire PPQ‐r‐C instrument were all over 0.7, indicating that PPQ‐r‐C had high levels of internal consistency (Terwee et al., 2007). Moreover, the PPQ‐r‐C seems to be more reliable compared to its previous versions including both PPQ and PPQ‐r because of higher reliability indicator values. In details, the Cronbach'sαvalue of PPQ‐r‐C is 0.779 at the scale level, which is higher than that of the PPQ tool with the value being 0.69 (Tijdink et al., 2014). And the Cronbach's α values of PPQ‐r‐C at the two sub‐scale levels of publication demands and resources are 0.845 and 0.876, both of them are higher than that of the stress and resource domains of PPQ‐r, being 0.80 and 0.75 respectively (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019). Finally, all the values of AVE, CR and the inter‐construct correlation showed that both convergent validity and discriminant validity of PPQ‐r‐C was satisfied sufficiently (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hair et al., 2010).

Although the results of the psychometric properties of the PPQ‐r‐C was satisfactory, some limitations of this study still need to be taken into consideration. Firstly, a methodological concern of this research is that sampling all of the participants from one university hospital would restrict the external validity of our findings. Future testing should be conducted within participants from multiple centers. Secondly, test–re‐test reliability was not conducted because of the restriction of cross‐sectional design in our study. Future work may be needed to explore the stability of the PPQ‐r‐C via conducting test–re‐test reliability testing within a proper time interval. Thirdly, criterion‐related validity was not conducted in this study because of the scarcity of a gold‐standard criterion instrument. Although PPQ was thought to be the first quantitative tool for publication pressure measurement, it is not convincing to include PPQ into this study as the criterion‐related measurement tool for PPQ‐r‐C in that PPQ‐r was developed based on PPQ (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019; Tijdink et al., 2014). Finally, as for the convergent validity and discriminant validity, we did not include some other instruments to facilitate these values confirmation. Therefore, the results of this study should be taken with cautions even though we had included the values of AVE, CR, and the inter‐construct correlation to testify its convergent validity and discriminant validity. Future research should be designed to include other tools, such as the MBI or the Work Design Questionnaire (Haven, de Goede, et al., 2019), to explore the convergent validity and discriminant validity from more perspectives.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, a new two‐factor structured PPQ‐r‐C with 9 items has been identified through the results of present study. All of the satisfactory internal consistency, content validity, convergent and discriminant validity indexes indicated that the PPQ‐r‐C is a valid and reliable instrument for publication pressure measurement among Chinese nurses working in university hospitals in mainland China. However, it is still necessary to further address its factor structure in other academician groups from multiple centers even though its two‐factor structure has been verified in an independent sample in present study. With applying PPQ‐r‐C, several potential implications could be recommended in the arena of nursing research, and nursing training. This study to some extent promotes the knowledge development of nursing discipline by enriching the tool options for publication pressure measurement internationally and this may ensure the possibility of publication pressure comparison between researchers from diverse cultural backgrounds. Moreover, in terms of the items covered in this tool, it would also be beneficial for targeted nursing training intervention development to keep nurses' publication pressure within certain range through adjusting publication demands and increasing publication resources development and allocation. Besides that, by publicizing the composition of the publication pressure, nursing educators and trainers may help the nurses to develop healthy attitude towards the publication pressure and finally increase their capability of self‐adjusting and coping with this pressure by designing and providing corresponding learning materials and training courses or lectures, etc.
