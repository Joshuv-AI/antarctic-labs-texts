Utility of bronchial blocker placement in the lateral versus supine position for thoracoscopic surgery: a randomised controlled trial in China
Authors: Rongjun Liu, Renli Wang, Xizhen Liu, Fanfu Zeng, Junping Chen, Jianliang Sun, Xiaoyu Li, Bo Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBronchial blocker (BB) displacement after supine placement and subsequent patient repositioning remains a significant challenge during video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). We aimed to test the hypothesis that placing the BB in the lateral decubitus position after standard supine tracheal intubation would reduce the incidence of malposition compared to conventional supine placement.MethodsIn this single-centre, prospective, randomised, single-blind, controlled trial conducted in China, 120 adult patients scheduled for VATS requiring one-lung ventilation were randomly assigned to either the Supine group (BB placed supine before turning lateral) or the Lateral group (BB placed after positioning the patient lateral). The primary outcome was the incidence of BB malposition, defined as the proportion of patients requiring BB repositioning (movement >1.0 cm) during the pre-incision fibreoptic bronchoscopic (FOB) check, assessed in the per-protocol population. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2400093699.FindingsBetween Jan 13 and May 15, 2025, among 118 patients analysed per-protocol, the incidence of BB malposition was 0% (0/59) in the lateral group compared to 42.4% (25/59) in the Supine group (Firth's penalised OR 0.011, 95% CI 0.000–0.086; p < 0.001). The absolute risk reduction was 42.4% (95% CI 28.6–54.1) with a number needed to treat of 2.36 (95% CI 1.85–3.47). Regarding the safety profile, the overall incidence of lower airway injury, as assessed by post-procedural FOB examination, was identical between groups (23.7% in each group, p = 1.000). All injuries were graded as mild or moderate (mucosal oedema or congestion); no severe injuries (haemorrhage or ulceration) were observed in either group.InterpretationOur preliminary finding show that patients undergoing VATS, placing a bronchial blocker in the definitive lateral decubitus position after standard supine intubation is a highly effective and stable strategy that virtually eliminates malposition caused by patient repositioning. This strategy not only improves the reliability of lung isolation but also offers the potential, in the future, to omit the routine pre-incision bronchoscopic check, further enhancing procedural efficiency. Future multicentre trials involving operators with varying levels of experience are needed to validate the generalisability of these findings.FundingThe Ningbo Medical and Health Brand Discipline.Keywords: Bronchial blocker, Lateral decubitus position, Randomised controlled trial, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, One-lung ventilation, Airway management

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for randomised controlled trials published between database inception and Dec 1, 2025. Search terms included (“bronchial blocker” [MeSH Terms] OR “bronchial blocker” [All Fields]) AND (“lateral position” [All Fields] OR “lateral decubitus” [All Fields] OR “supine position” [All Fields] OR “repositioning” [All Fields]) AND (“one-lung ventilation” [MeSH Terms] OR “one-lung ventilation” [All Fields] OR “thoracic surgery” [MeSH Terms] OR “thoracic surgery” [All Fields]). Most existing literature focuses on the comparison between bronchial blockers and double-lumen tubes, or the design of different types of blockers. Standard clinical practice typically involves placing the bronchial blocker in the supine position followed by turning the patient to the lateral position for surgery. However, this repositioning is known to cause displacement of the blocker, requiring repeated fibreoptic bronchoscopic adjustments. We found no randomised controlled trials that specifically compared the clinical efficacy and stability of bronchial blocker placement performed directly in the lateral position versus the traditional supine position.

Added value of this study

This single-centre, prospective, randomised, single-blind, controlled trial provides robust independent validation of the lateral placement strategy. We evaluated blocker stability using a strict 1.0 cm anatomical displacement threshold. We confirmed that lateral placement effectively eliminates repositioning-induced displacement. Furthermore, we recorded the exact time required for fibreoptic bronchoscopy. The results showed no increase in procedure time between the groups. This directly addresses the clinical concern that operating in the lateral position might be technically more difficult.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings show that placing the bronchial blocker directly in the lateral position provides reliable anatomical stability. Because of this high stability, the routine pre-incision bronchoscopic verification can potentially be omitted. Adopting this strategy could potentially streamline anaesthetic workflow and improve procedural efficiency while maintaining safe lung isolation; though further multicentre trials are needed to validate these findings in broader clinical settings.

Introduction

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become the standard approach for pulmonary resection, offering superior visualisation, reduced tissue trauma, and accelerated recovery compared to open thoracotomy.1,2 Effective one-lung ventilation (OLV) is essential for surgical exposure, and is most commonly achieved using either a double-lumen tube (DLT) or a bronchial blocker (BB).

While DLTs provide rapid and reliable lung collapse, their relatively large external diameter and rigid structure are associated with a higher incidence of postoperative hoarseness and sore throat.3, 4, 5 BB, though used less frequently worldwide, offer an alternative approach: deployed through a conventional single-lumen tube (SLT), they provide a less traumatic airway profile and greater flexibility in tube selection.6, 7, 8, 9 These characteristics make BB particularly advantageous when postoperative ventilation is anticipated or when upper airway anatomy makes intubation difficult. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that with appropriate technique, BBs can achieve lung collapse quality comparable to DLTs.3,6

However, BBs have recognised limitations, including the inability to suction the operative lung and limited capacity for apnoeic oxygenation6,7; among these, positional instability remains a critical drawback that directly impacts clinical efficiency and reliability.5 Displacement of the blocker frequently occurs specifically during the postural change from the supine to the lateral decubitus position.10 This malposition, occurring before the start of surgery, necessitates immediate fibreoptic re-evaluation and repositioning, which prolongs anaesthesia time and increases the risk of airway mucosal injury and hypoxaemia.5,11

Conventionally, the BB is inserted and positioned while the patient is supine, followed by turning the patient laterally. This sequence subjects the device to head and neck movement and associated anatomical shifts during the turning manoeuvre, which are the primary contributors to displacement.12,13 We hypothesised that inserting the BB after establishing the patient in the lateral decubitus position would eliminate this displacement risk and improve the efficiency of lung isolation.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted a prospective, randomised controlled trial to compare the stability and safety of this lateral-position placement strategy versus the conventional supine-position approach in patients undergoing VATS.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This prospective, randomised, single-blind, parallel-group controlled trial was conducted at Ningbo No. 2 Hospital after obtaining approval from the Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: NBEY-HS-2024-014). The trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400093699). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study-related procedures were performed. The study protocol (original and translation) is presented in Supplement 1.

Participants

Patient recruitment occurred between Jan 13 and May 15, 2025. Consecutive adult patients (aged 18–80 years) scheduled for elective VATS under general anaesthesia requiring OLV were screened for eligibility by a research coordinator (XY L) from the elective surgery schedule one day prior to the procedure. Eligible patients were approached during their preoperative hospital visit, provided with detailed information about the study, and invited to participate. Inclusion criteria were: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-III and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: 1) anatomical constraints, specifically a short right main bronchus (length <1 cm on pre-operative chest CT) or a right upper lobe bronchus originating at or above the carina; 2) clinical conditions necessitating efficient intraoperative suctioning (e.g., bronchiectasis, massive haemoptysis, empyema); 3) history of laryngeal, tracheal, bronchial, or pulmonary surgery, or tumours invading the main airway; 4) anticipated difficult airway based on preoperative assessment; 5) history of tracheostomy; 6) active upper respiratory tract infection; and 7) severe psychiatric disorders or communication difficulties that would impede consent or postoperative evaluation.

Randomisation and blinding

Eligible patients were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the Supine group or the Lateral group. An independent statistician (FF Z), who was not involved in participant recruitment or clinical procedures, generated the allocation sequence using computer-generated block randomisation with varying block sizes of 4 and 6. The allocation sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. In the operating room, immediately prior to anaesthesia induction, the attending anaesthesiologist opened the next envelope in sequence to reveal the group assignment. Patient recruitment was performed by a research coordinator (XY L), who screened consecutive patients from the elective surgery schedule. Outcome assessments were conducted by two blinded investigators (XZ L and RJ L), who were not involved in the clinical management of the patients. Statistical analyses were performed by the independent statistician (FF Z).

Due to the nature of the intervention (patient positioning), the anaesthesiologists performing the SLT intubation and BB placement could not be blinded. However, the surgeons performing the Lung Collapse Score (LCS) assessment, the researchers collecting intraoperative and postoperative data, and the statistician performing the final analysis were all blinded to the group allocations.

Anaesthetic management and standard procedures

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was instituted, including five-lead electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry (SpO2). Peripheral venous access was secured. Subsequently, under local anaesthesia and using ultrasound guidance, a central venous catheter was placed via the right internal jugular vein, and invasive arterial pressure monitoring was established via cannulation of the radial artery.

After pre-oxygenation with 100% oxygen (6 L/min flow) for 5 min, general anaesthesia was induced intravenously with sufentanil (0.2–0.4 μg/kg), etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg). After loss of consciousness, mask ventilation was assisted for 3 min. Tracheal intubation was then performed on all patients in the supine position using a video laryngoscope (Youyi Medical Device Co., Zhejiang, China) with an 8.0-mm internal diameter single-lumen tube (SLT) ((ID 8.0 mm for males, ID 7.5 mm for females; Tappa Medical Technology Co., Zhejiang, China). The SLT was advanced until the cuff passed the vocal cords by 2 cm, then the cuff was inflated and the tube was secured at the lips. Correct placement was confirmed by capnography and bilateral chest auscultation. Anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (1.5–3 vol%) and a continuous infusion of remifentanil (0.1–0.3 μg/kg/min). Additional doses of cisatracurium were administered as needed based on surgical requirements and neuromuscular monitoring. Haemodynamic stability was actively managed; hypotension (Systolic Blood Pressure <80 mmHg) was treated with intravenous norepinephrine (4 μg bolus), and bradycardia (Heart Rate <50 bpm) was treated with intravenous atropine (0.5 mg).

Bronchial blocker placement

A 9-Fr disposable bronchial blocker (BB) (Tappa Medical Technology Co., China) was used.14,15 This device is modelled after the Coopdech blocker (Daiken Medical, Osaka, Japan) and features a pre-formed angulated tip, a high-volume low-pressure cuff, and a central lumen for suction and CPAP. Specifically, the 9-Fr blocker used in this study has an outer diameter of 3.0 mm, a length of 60 cm, and a balloon capacity of 6–8 ml.

All BB placement and subsequent fibreoptic bronchoscopic (FOB) evaluations were performed by one of two designated staff anaesthesiologists, each of whom had extensive prior experience (>500 procedures) with BB placement and FOB guidance, to minimise operator-dependent variability.

In the Supine group, after intubation with the SLT and while the patient was maintained in the supine position, the BB was inserted through the SLT's dedicated port. The BB tip was oriented towards the operative side. Once resistance was felt as the tip exited the distal end of the SLT, the BB was advanced gently until mild resistance suggested contact with a bronchial wall. An FOB (Youyi Medical Device Co., Zhejiang, China) was then introduced through the BB's endoscopic port. Under direct FOB visualisation, the BB was guided into the mainstem bronchus of the operative side. The ideal position was confirmed as the upper margin of the inflated BB cuff being flush with the tracheal carina, ensuring complete bronchial occlusion16 (Fig. 1C). Once this position was achieved, the BB cuff was deflated, and the depth of the BB at the SLT connector was marked with a blue permanent marker (Fig. 1B). Only after this was completed, the patient was carefully turned into the full lateral decubitus position by the surgical and nursing team.

In the Lateral group, immediately after SLT intubation in the supine position, the patient was turned into the full lateral decubitus position for surgery (Fig. 1A). The procedural steps for BB insertion and FOB-guided positioning were identical to those described for the Supine group. The sole distinction between groups was the patient's position (supine versus lateral decubitus) during these manoeuvres. The final BB depth was similarly marked with a blue pen. The lateral decubitus positioning was meticulously standardised across all patients according to a previously described protocol to ensure consistency and patient safety.13

In both groups, after the patient was in the final surgical position and immediately before skin incision, the position of the BB was re-evaluated using the FOB. If the BB was found to be malpositioned, it was immediately corrected under FOB vision. The new, corrected depth was then marked with a red permanent marker. The distance between the blue and red marks was recorded as the displacement distance (Fig. 1B). To integrate smoothly with surgical workflow, BB placement in the Lateral group was performed concurrently with routine nursing activities, including patient skin preparation and draping. No additional operating room time was specifically allocated for the procedure.

Ventilation strategy and postoperative analgesia

Mechanical ventilation was managed using a lung-protective strategy.17 Prior to pleural opening (during two-lung ventilation), settings were: tidal volume (Vt) 8–10 ml/kg (ideal body weight), respiratory rate 12 breaths/min, inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio (I:E) 1:2, and FiO2 100%. End-tidal carbon dioxide (PetCO2) was maintained between 35 and 45 mmHg, and peak airway pressure (Ppeak) was kept below 25 cmH2O. After the pleura was opened, the breathing circuit was disconnected from the SLT for 1 min to allow passive lung collapse. The BB cuff was then inflated with the predetermined volume of air to achieve a cuff pressure of 20–25 cmH2O, and OLV was initiated. During OLV, ventilator settings were adjusted to: Vt 6–8 ml/kg, respiratory rate 12 breaths/min, I:E 1:2, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 6 cmH2O, and FiO2 100%. Ppeak was maintained below 30 cmH2O.

At the conclusion of surgery, the BB was removed after tracheobronchial suctioning and lung recruitment manoeuvres. All patients were transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) for standardised emergence and extubation. Postoperative analgesia was provided via a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pump, containing sufentanil 100 μg and ondansetron 16 mg in a total volume of 100 ml normal saline. The pump was set to deliver a continuous background infusion of 2 ml/h, with a bolus dose of 2 ml and a lockout interval of 15 min.

Data collection

Data were entered into a secure electronic case report form using EpiData software (version 3.1, EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark) by two independent data entry clerks and subsequently verified for accuracy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of BB malposition, defined as the proportion of patients requiring BB repositioning (movement >1.0 cm) during the pre-incision FOB check.13 The pre-specified primary analysis was conducted on the per-protocol population.

Five secondary outcomes were prespecified, which included procedural, efficacy, and safety measures. Procedural and stability outcomes were (1) the total FOB usage time (sum of T1 [initial positioning time] and T2 [pre-incision check time], measured with a stopwatch), and (2) success rate of single BB placement (defined as the incidence of BB placement in the target main bronchus before FOB examination, prior to any patient repositioning). Clinical efficacy outcomes were (1) the success rate of OLV, assessed at 1 min by a blinded researcher and defined as absence of respiratory movement in the operative lung on thoracoscopy with concurrent Ppeak <30 cmH2O, and (2) the LCS evaluated by the blinded operating surgeon 15 min after OLV initiation using a validated 11-point scale (0 = no collapse; 10 = complete collapse).18 Safety outcome was lower airway injury assessed via FOB and graded as none, mild (mucosal oedema), moderate (congestion and oedema), or severe (hemorrhage/ulceration).19,20 No other adverse events were prospectively collected, as the intervention was considered low risk and the study focused on procedural outcomes.

We additionally collected data on three exploratory outcomes that were not prespecified in the study protocol: (1) displacement distance (absolute difference between the initial and corrected depth marks); (2) tube repositioning required, defined as any need to adjust the BB position after the initial pre-incision bronchoscopic check; and (3) anaesthesiologist's satisfaction score, rated at the end of the procedure on a 0–10 scale (0 = completely dissatisfied, 10 = completely satisfied).21 All outcomes not prespecified in the protocol are reported as exploratory.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of BB malposition incidence. Drawing from preliminary data (detailed in Supplement 2), the malposition rate in the Supine group was estimated to be 50%. We hypothesised that lateral placement would reduce this rate by 30% (to 20% in the Lateral group). Utilising PASS 2021 software (NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA), with a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and a power of 90%, a sample size of 48 patients per group was required. To account for a potential 20% dropout rate, the final target enrolment was set at 120 patients (60 per group).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

For the primary outcome (BB malposition), logistic regression was used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI). The risk difference (RD) and number needed to treat (NNT) with corresponding 95% CIs were also calculated. Upon observation of complete separation (zero events in the Lateral group), Firth's penalised likelihood regression was adopted post-hoc to obtain stable estimates; this approach was not prespecified in the study protocol. The primary analysis was performed on the per-protocol (PP) population. As a sensitivity analysis, we performed an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis using multiple imputation.

In the presence of complete separation, OR from logistic regression (including penalised versions) may yield extreme values that are not directly interpretable. In such cases, RD and NNT provide more clinically relevant effect estimates. A pre-specified multivariable logistic regression model was fitted to adjust for covariates with SMD >0.1 (weight, body mass index, thyromental distance, Mallampati classification, ASA physical status, and tracheal tube insertion depth). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF >10 indicating severe collinearity and warranting removal from the model.

Secondary outcomes were analysed as follows: continuous outcomes are reported as mean difference (MD) or Hodges-Lehmann median difference with 95% CI, as appropriate; binary outcomes are reported as both OR and RD with 95% CI.

Exploratory outcomes (displacement distance, tube repositioning, and anaesthesiologist's satisfaction score) were analysed using the same statistical methods as secondary outcomes.

Post-hoc sensitivity analyses were performed using a stricter definition of BB malposition (>0.5 cm displacement) using the same regression models as described for the primary outcome. Post-hoc subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the treatment effect across predefined strata (age, sex, height, weight, BMI, thyromental distance, mallampati classification, ASA physical status, surgical side [right versus left-sided procedures], and tracheal tube depth). Participant sex (female/male) was determined from medical records. As a post-hoc analysis, the initial placement time (T1) was investigated as a proxy for initial placement difficulty.

Role of the funding source

The funder had no involvement in study design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or the writing of the report.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Between January 13 and May 15, 2025, a total of 169 consecutive patients scheduled for elective VATS requiring OLV were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 49 were excluded prior to randomisation for the following reasons: 31 did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria, and 18 declined to participate. The reasons for not meeting inclusion criteria strictly followed the protocol: anatomical constraints on preoperative CT (short right main bronchus; n = 6), clinical conditions necessitating efficient suctioning (n = 8), history of airway or pulmonary surgery (n = 9), anticipated difficult airway (n = 4), history of tracheostomy (n = 1), and severe psychiatric/communication disorders (n = 3). The remaining 120 patients were randomly assigned to either the Lateral group (n = 60) or the Supine group (n = 60).

Two patients were subsequently excluded from the per-protocol analysis: one (Lateral group) due to unassessable lung collapse score secondary to pleural adhesions, and one (Supine group) due to erroneous placement of the BB on the wrong side, which was not identified until after key study assessments were to begin. Consequently, 118 patients (59 in each group) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1D). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the analysed patients are presented in Table 1.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome, incidence of bronchial blocker malposition (>1.0 cm movement), was 0% (0/59) in the Lateral group compared to 42.4% (25/59) in the Supine group (Table 2). Due to complete separation (zero events in the Lateral group), Firth's penalised likelihood logistic regression was used. In the unadjusted analysis, placement in the lateral position was associated with a markedly lower odds of malposition (OR 0.011, 95% CI 0.000–0.086; p < 0.001). This corresponds to an absolute risk reduction of 42.4% (95% CI 28.6–54.1) and a NNT of 2.36 (95% CI 1.85–3.47). After adjustment for the imbalanced baseline covariates (weight, body mass index, thyromental distance, Mallampati classification, ASA physical status, and tracheal tube insertion depth), the association remained robust, with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.000 (95% CI 0.000–0.002; p < 0.001) and a similar absolute risk reduction of 41.1% (95% CI 27.5–54.7), confirming that the effect is not explained by confounding (Table 2).

The ITT analysis yielded results consistent with the PP analysis. Malposition occurred in 1.7% (1/60) of the Lateral group versus 43.3% (26/60) of the Supine group, with a risk difference of −41.7% (95% CI −54.0 to −28.4; p < 0.001).

Secondary outcomes

The total FOB time did not differ significantly between groups (69.9 s [61.7, 81.5] versus 72.7 s [67.2, 84.7]; p = 0.133) (Table 3). The lateral placement strategy significantly improved the success rate of single BB placement compared to the supine group (98.3% versus 86.4%; OR 8.96, 95% CI 1.14–409.69; RD 11.9%, 95% CI 2.5–21.2; p = 0.032).

The success rate of OLV at 1 min was high and similar between groups (Lateral 96.6% versus Supine 89.8%; p = 0.272) (Table 3). The surgeons’ rating of lung collapse quality (LCS assessment) 15 min after OLV initiation was also comparable between groups (8 [7, 8] versus 7 [7, 8]; p = 0.119).

The overall incidence of lower airway injury, as assessed by post-procedural FOB examination, was identical between groups (23.7% in each group, p = 1.000) (Table 3). All injuries were graded as mild or moderate (mucosal oedema or congestion); no severe injuries (haemorrhage or ulceration) were observed in either group.

Exploratory outcomes

The displacement distance of the BB was significantly shorter in the Lateral group than in the Supine group (0.0 cm [0.0–0.0] versus 0.50 cm [0.0–1.1]; MD 0.50 cm, 95% CI 0.30–0.80; p < 0.001). The rate of subsequent tube repositioning after the initial check was low and not significantly different between groups (5.1% versus 8.5%; p = 0.717). However, the operating anaesthetists’ satisfaction score was significantly higher in the Lateral group (9 [8, 9] versus 8 [8, 9]; p = 0.022).

Post-hoc analyses

As a post-hoc sensitivity analysis, we examined malposition defined by a stricter threshold of >0.5 cm displacement. The incidence of malposition >0.5 cm remained significantly lower in the Lateral group than in the Supine group (13.6% versus 62.7%; adjusted OR 0.097, 95% CI 0.036–0.236; p < 0.001; Table 4). Additionally, post-hoc subgroup analyses across age, sex, height, weight, body mass index, thyromental distance, Mallampati classification, ASA physical status, surgical side, and tracheal tube insertion depth demonstrated no significant interactions (all p for interaction >0.05; Fig. 2), confirming that the clinical benefit of the lateral positioning strategy was consistent across different patient and surgical characteristics. Investigated post-hoc, there was no significant difference in the initial placement difficulty, as measured by the time to successful positioning (T1): the median T1 was 39.8 s [37.2–46.7] in the Lateral group versus 41.6 s [36.7–50.8] in the Supine group (p = 0.362).

Discussion

This prospective, randomised controlled trial demonstrates that placing a BB in the lateral position after standard supine single-lumen tube intubation significantly reduces the incidence of malposition compared to the conventional supine placement technique (0% versus 42.4%). This enhanced stability was accompanied by improvements in several secondary endpoints, including a markedly higher success rate of single BB placement and higher operator satisfaction. Our findings indicate that this hybrid approach—maintaining the safety of supine intubation while capitalising on the stability of lateral positioning—effectively addresses the primary mechanical cause of BB displacement.

The high rate of BB malposition (42.4%) observed in our supine group is consistent with the known limitations of the device, as reported in prior studies.5,10,22 The act of turning a patient from supine to lateral decubitus is a well-documented cause of displacement for airway devices. Research on double-lumen tubes has shown that head and neck movement during this repositioning can alter the orientation and depth of the tube within the tracheobronchial tree.6,13,23,24 Our results robustly extend this principle to BBs. The fundamental advantage of our lateral placement strategy is that it avoids the disruptive event of patient repositioning after the BB has been set. By fixing the BB in its final, surgically required position, we effectively prevent the movement that so often leads to dislocation.

Our study refines the evolving technique of lateral positioning for lung isolation. The recent work by Li et al.15 demonstrated the benefits of performing both intubation and BB placement with the patient already in the lateral decubitus position. While effective, this approach requires a change from the standard practice of securing the airway in the supine position, which is valued for optimal operator control, especially in managing potential difficulties. Our combined protocol—supine intubation followed by lateral BB placement—maintains the safety of standard intubation while capturing the stability benefits of the surgical position. This practical distinction may make the technique more easily adopted into existing clinical workflows.

The 1 cm displacement threshold used in this study reflects the functional anatomy of the BB. The inflated cuff measures approximately 10–15 mm in diameter, and a 1 cm axial shift is sufficient to partially or completely dislodge the cuff from the target bronchial orifice, compromising the seal required for effective one-lung ventilation. This threshold is consistent with that adopted in recent BB literature.15 The similar total FOB times between groups (including initial placement difficulty measured via T1) confirm that lateral placement does not increase procedural difficulty. The comparable OLV success and lung collapse scores reflect that all malpositions in the Supine group were corrected during the mandatory pre-incision bronchoscopic check. However, avoiding malposition itself offers distinct clinical benefits, it eliminates the need for troubleshooting, reduces airway manipulation, and improves workflow efficiency. Moreover, in routine clinical practice, the pre-incision check could be omitted entirely when the blocker is placed in the lateral position, potentially saving additional time and further streamlining workflow. Furthermore, a post hoc analysis using a more stringent 0.5 cm cutoff confirmed the robustness of our findings.

A particularly interesting finding in our secondary outcomes was the significantly higher success rate of single BB placement in the lateral group (98.3% versus 86.4%). Whilst a wide confidence interval is noted for the odds ratio (OR 8.96, 95% CI 1.14–409.69), this statistical phenomenon is caused by the asymmetrical nature of the OR scale combined with an extremely low event rate (only 1 failure out of 59 in the lateral group); the reciprocal of the near-zero original lower limit stretches the new upper bound toward infinity. Despite this mathematical extremity in the OR, our absolute measure of effect (RD 11.9%, 95% CI 2.5–21.2) is stable and supports statistical significance (p = 0.032). This difference of higher success rate in the lateral group suggests that the lateral decubitus posture itself may provide a more favourable anatomical environment for guiding the BB into the intended bronchus. We speculate that the mechanical effects of lateral positioning contribute to this phenomenon. The placement of a chest roll or pad beneath the dependent thorax to prevent neurovascular compression can cause a shift of the mediastinum towards the dependent side. This shift, combined with the effects of gravity on the lungs, may alter the angles at which the mainstem bronchi originate from the carina. Specifically, it might straighten the path into the non-dependent (operative side) bronchus or create a more obtuse angle that naturally guides the advancing BB towards the correct side, thereby ensuring accurate initial positioning into the target bronchus. This biomechanical hypothesis warrants further investigation but offers a plausible explanation for the improved success rate of single BB placement we observed.

The overall incidence of lower airway injury was identical between groups. An observation of note, though not statistically significant due to the low event rate, was that all three cases of severe injury (grade III) occurred in the Supine group, with none in the Lateral group. This distribution may suggest a trend towards less severe trauma with the lateral approach, potentially attributable to the avoidance of post-placement repositioning, but requires confirmation in larger studies. The higher satisfaction reported by anaesthesiologists likely reflects this more streamlined and predictable procedure, with less time spent troubleshooting BB position.

A common concern regarding lateral BB placement is the potential disruption of surgical workflow or the technical difficulty of manipulating the airway in the lateral decubitus position. In our study, we found no significant difference in total fibreoptic time between groups. Furthermore, the “perceived” delay in the Lateral group was mitigated by performing surgical skin preparation and draping concurrently with the anaesthesiologist's setup. Consequently, the procedure did not delay the surgical incision. In fact, by eliminating the need for repositioning after patient turning—a step that occurred in 42.4% of Supine group patients—the Lateral strategy streamlined the overall workflow. Based on these results, our department has adopted lateral BB placement as the standard operating procedure for routine VATS cases. While our data show no difference in placement time between positions, it is important to note that all procedures were performed by experienced anaesthesiologists. Practitioners less familiar with lateral airway management may initially experience a steeper learning curve, although the visual guidance provided by the bronchoscope mitigates much of this technical challenge.

The higher anaesthesiologist satisfaction scores in the Lateral group (median 9 versus 8), while modest in absolute terms, likely reflect the clinical benefit of a more predictable procedure that avoids unexpected malposition and the need for troubleshooting. Although operator bias cannot be excluded due to the unblinded nature of the intervention, the consistency of this finding across multiple operators and its alignment with objective improvements in malposition rates and success rate of single BB placement support its validity.

A potential critique of our protocol is the strict adherence to the anatomical ideal position (cuff at carina), whereas some practitioners might empirically advance the blocker deeper. We deliberately avoided this ‘over-advancement’ strategy for several reasons. First, displacement during positioning is bidirectional15; while proximal migration is common, distal migration into a lobar bronchus also occurs, which would be worsened by deeper initial placement. Second, quantifying an optimal empirical depth is subjective and variable. By standardising the target position to the carinal margin in both groups, we provided a rigorous baseline for comparison.24

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, it was conducted at a single centre, which may affect the generalisability of the findings. Second, the nature of the intervention made it impossible to blind the anaesthesiologists performing the procedures, which could introduce performance bias; however, we mitigated this by blinding the data analysts and the surgeons providing the LCS ratings. Third, this study was designed as a pragmatic efficacy trial and therefore excluded patients with anticipated difficult airways, morbid obesity, or anatomical variants such as a short right main bronchus. While this approach minimised confounding and enhanced internal validity, it limits the generalisability of our findings to the full spectrum of patients encountered in clinical practice. Fourth, the exact time interval between BB placement and pre-incision FOB verification was not prospectively recorded. This interval inevitably differs between groups, as it incorporates the time required for patient repositioning in the Supine group—the very manoeuvre under investigation. Future studies may nonetheless consider formal time-interval recording to further characterise the kinetics of BB displacement during repositioning. Fifth, we did not systematically record intraoperative blocker displacement after the pre-incision check. This was because our primary objective was to isolate the effect of patient repositioning on stability. After correcting any malpositions detected pre-incision, both groups began surgery with correctly positioned blockers, making intraoperative comparisons less informative and potentially confounded by uncontrolled factors such as surgical manipulation. Studies specifically designed to evaluate intraoperative stability are warranted. Sixth, all BB placements were performed by two highly experienced anaesthesiologists. Consequently, the results may not fully generalise to operators with less experience in fibreoptic-guided blocker placement.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that placing a BB in the lateral decubitus position after standard supine intubation is a more stable, efficient, and satisfactory method for achieving lung isolation during VATS. This strategy not only improves the reliability of lung isolation but also offers the potential to omit the routine pre-incision bronchoscopic check, further enhancing procedural efficiency.
