Introduction

Behçet's disease (BD) is an inflammatory vasculitis affecting multiple organs, its origins dating back millennia along the Silk Road from the Mediterranean to East Asia [1]. Described in modern medicine by Adamantiades [2] and further delineated by Hulusi Behçet [3] in 1937, the disease is characterized by significant vascular involvement, including thrombosis of the vena cava, which markedly affects prognosis.

We present a case of angio-Behçet's disease with abdominal collateral venous circulation (CVC) in a patient involved in a traffic accident. This underscores the complexity and critical nature of managing such cases promptly and comprehensively.

This case report was written in line with the SCARE guidelines [4].

Case presentation

A 30-year-old man with BD, under oral anticoagulation since 2017, was involved in a traffic accident. The collision occurred between a motorcycle and a car, predominantly affecting his abdomen, pelvis, and lower limbs, with no immediate loss of consciousness. He was admitted to the emergency department one hour after the accident in a state of hemorrhagic shock. The patient presented conscious with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15 out of 15, hypotensive at 90/53 mmHg, tachycardic at 120 bpm, and slightly pale conjunctivae. His abdomen was distended and painful, without guarding or rigidity. Laboratory tests showed a hemoglobin level of 9 g/dL compared to 13 g/dL at admission, hematocrit at 45.8 %, white blood cell count of 15,700/μL, normal prothrombin time, and other biochemical parameters within normal limits. A contrast-enhanced CT trauma series revealed diffuse CVC with contrast leakage around perigastric varices and a subhepatic hematoma filling the right paracolic gutter (Fig. 1). The patient was urgently taken to the operating room. Exploratory laparotomy confirmed a moderate amount of hemorrhagic peritoneal fluid, which was aspirated, and diffuse peri-gastric and right peri-colonic venous collaterals, along with a moderate-sized and extended mesenteric and mesocolic hematoma (Fig. 2). Hemostasis was achieved after tamponade, and the non-actively bleeding hematoma was left untouched, followed by placement of drainage under the liver and in the Douglas pouch. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with drainage decreasing to 200 cc of sero-hematic fluid by day 1 and steadily reducing. The patient was discharged on day 5 and followed up in outpatient care after 15 days in good general condition.

Discussion

BD is a rare multisystemic vasculitis that affects arteries and veins of various sizes [5]. The veins are the most commonly affected vessels, particularly the superior vena cava and inferior vena cava [6]. Vascular diseases manifestations in BD include venous thrombotic occlusions, arterial occlusions, pseudoaneurysm, and so on [7]. Abdominal manifestations can include gastrointestinal involvement, vascular aneurysms, and thrombosis, which increase the risk of hemorrhage and vascular complications following trauma. In cases of blunt abdominal trauma in a BD patient, the presence of collateral abdominal circulation can complicate both the diagnosis and management. CVC refers to the development of alternative pathways for blood flow, often due to occlusion or damage to primary blood vessels.

Our patient likely presented with signs of hemorrhagic shock, indicating significant internal bleeding. Initial management focused on stabilizing the patient's hemodynamic status through fluid resuscitation and blood transfusions. Diagnostic imaging, such as CT scans, played a crucial role in assessing the extent of injuries and identifying complications such as hematoma formation and vascular abnormalities.

Surgical intervention, specifically exploratory laparotomy as described in the case, was necessary to address life-threatening bleeding and to evaluate the extent of abdominal injuries. During surgery, careful evaluation and management of collateral circulation and mesenteric hematomas were essential. Techniques such as tamponade and surgical repair were employed to control bleeding and restore vascular integrity. Postoperatively, meticulous monitoring for complications related to both the abdominal trauma and underlying BD was crucial. This included surveillance for recurrent bleeding, thrombotic events, or complications associated with anticoagulation therapy, which is frequently used in BD to manage vascular involvement.

Conclusion

Managing abdominal trauma in patients with BD demands a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach. Close monitoring and customized treatment plans are critical for achieving favorable outcomes and reducing the risk of disease exacerbation and vascular complications.
