Efficacy of gut-brain neuromodulators in functional esophageal disorders: a systematic review
Authors: Zimeng Wang, Zhi Zheng, Xicheng Wei, Xueqi Li, Chenglin Xin, Dan Tian, Haiqiao Zhang, Xiaoye Liu, Lingyue Dong, Anning Li, Yu Fu, Zhongtao Zhang, Jie Yin, Gang Wang, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with functional esophageal disorders exhibit symptoms such as chest pain, heartburn, dysphagia, globus sensation, or reflux hypersensitivity in the absence of structural abnormalities. This is characterized by dysregulated gut-brain interactions and visceral hypersensitivity.MethodsA systematic search of the MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science and the Cochrane central register of controlled trials databases was performed to December 31, 2025. Relevant randomized controlled trials (RCT) reporting the effects of gut–brain neuromodulator (GBN) therapy on functional chest pain (FCP), functional heartburn (FH), reflux hypersensitivity (RH), functional dysphagia (FD), and globus were analyzed.Key ResultsAmong 2538 screened records, 29 RCTs were included. Neuromodulators provided symptom relief in 18–67% of patients with FCP, with 52–71% achieving ≥ 50% symptom reduction. Moreover, 46–80% of patients experienced a globus reduction of more than 50%. In contrast, the number of included studies for RH and FH was small, and the evidence was inconsistent. In healthy individuals, human experimental models indicated that central or local sensory-modulatory pathways can mitigate acid-induced hyperalgesia, thereby offering a promising therapeutic strategy for RH. Convincing evidence to support the use of GBN in the treatment of FD is lacking. However, its effects on normal human esophageal motility also provides strategies for drug selection, although these outcome indicators vary substantially.Conclusions and InferencesGBNs can alleviate FCP, improve globus, and to some extent regulate esophageal sensation. However, their effectiveness for FD, FH, and RH remains controversial. Accordingly, future well-designed and phenotype-specific RCTs are required to establish the role of GBNs in clinical management.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05089-6.

Introduction

In the absence of structural abnormalities, inflammatory pathology, or major motility disorders, functional esophageal disorders (FED) are characterized by typical esophageal symptoms presumed to originate from the esophagus [1]. According to the Rome IV criteria, FEDs encompass five distinct entities: functional chest pain (FCP), functional heartburn (FH), reflux hypersensitivity (RH), globus, and functional dysphagia (FD) [2]. High-quality epidemiologic studies based on objective diagnostic measures remain limited, and the narrow range of available therapeutic options continues to pose critical clinical challenges in terms of management.

A bidirectional association exists between psychological distress and FEDs, with each preceding the other in approximately half of reported cases. While this bidirectionality has been rigorously documented in non-esophageal functional gastrointestinal disorders (such as irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia), its generalization to FEDs represents a plausible clinical extrapolation based on shared brain-gut pathophysiological pathways [3, 4]. This interplay is mediated via central nervous system processing, as confirmed in neuroimaging studies that demonstrate altered brain activity related to visceral hypersensitivity, anxiety, and depression in affected individuals [5–7].

These observations support the hypothesis that the primary pathophysiological process in a subgroup of patients may originate in the gastrointestinal tract and subsequently drive systemic manifestations. Psychological dysfunction is a central component of these systemic manifestations, and the entire process relies on the gut–brain axis. This is a fundamental biological link. Specifically, the brain and gut develop from the same embryonic tissue and remain in constant communication via nerves and hormones. This continuous communication system is the basic framework that renders such disorders possible [8]. Gut–brain neuromodulators (GBN) are often used for pharmacological treatment of FEDs. These include common drug classes, such as antidepressants, antipsychotics, and serotonin-modulating agents [1, 9]. GBNs are believed to operate by simultaneously modifying visceral sensitivity and central pain processing pathways [10]. This dual action aligns with the concept of the gut-brain axis, where psychological and gastrointestinal functions continuously interact.

Although understanding of the gut–brain interactions has improved, and diagnostic criteria have been refined under Rome IV, strong evidence to guide drug treatment for FEDs remains limited. Considering this knowledge gap, in this study, a systematic review of all relevant research on GBNs for FEDs was performed. The study aimed to assess the efficacy of these drugs and to propose a practical and evidence-based approach for their use in clinical practice.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive search of MEDLINE (1966–2025), EMBASE (1978–2025), Web of Science (1964–2025), and the Cochrane central register of controlled trials databases (1988–2025) was conducted until December 31, 2025, to identify relevant articles in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses framework (Fig. 1) [11]. This was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42025116330). Search terms combined text words and Medical Subject Headings, where applicable, covering key concepts of FEDs and commonly prescribed GBNs, including tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), tetracyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI), serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRI), serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitor, norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor, adenosine antagonists, benzodiazepines, and atypical antipsychotics. Additional relevant publications were identified by manually searching the reference lists of included articles. Studies published in languages other than English were excluded (Supplement 1).

Study selection

All randomized clinical trials (RCTs) evaluating GBNs in adults with FEDs were eligible for inclusion. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) RCTs examining the effect of GBNs versus placebo in patients with FEDs; (2) adult patients diagnosed with FEDs based on Rome criteria or healthy individuals with experimentally induced esophageal sensation (e.g., after balloon distension or acid perfusion); and (3) studies analyzing the effect of GBNs on esophageal function, such as the intensity or frequency of heartburn, chest pain, dysphagia, or globus. The programmatic inclusion of healthy volunteer experimental models is methodologically justified as it establishes a pristine, confounder-free physiological baseline. This controlled paradigm isolates the direct sensory-modulatory capacities of GBNs on visceral afferent signaling and central pain processing, entirely unconfounded by the psychological comorbidities or heterogenous clinical treatments typical of patient cohorts. To avoid confusing these two different groups, we kept the data for healthy volunteers completely separate from the clinical patient data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-English language studies; (2) non-RCTs; and (3) abstracts, conference proceedings, and letters. Abstracts retrieved from the initial search were screened for relevance to the study question. Potentially eligible full-text articles were obtained and evaluated in detail. Two investigators (Zm.W and Xc.W) independently performed the literature search and subsequently assessed the eligibility of all retrieved studies. Any disagreements regarding inclusion were resolved through consensus.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form that was adapted from the Cochrane Consumers and Communication Review Group template (https://cccrg.cochrane.org/cochrane-author-resources) was used to collect information. Two reviewers (Z.Z and Xq.L) independently evaluated the risk of bias in each included RCT using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool [12]. Bias was evaluated across five distinct domains, with signaling questions leading to judgments of “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.” The judgments within each domain contributed to an overall risk-of-bias assessment for the results (Figs. 2 and 3). Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion. The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was evaluated using the 8-item modified Jadad scale proposed by Oremus et al. [13], which builds upon the foundational quality assessment framework established by Jadad et al. [14]. This modified scale ranges from 0 to 8 points with high scores (≥ 5) thereby reflecting superior reporting quality (Supplement 2). To comprehensively synthesize the available high-quality evidence, studies with diverse clinical characteristics were included. However, substantial heterogeneity across trials in patient definitions, interventions, and outcome assessments precluded a quantitative meta-analysis. Given these limitations, we conducted a structured narrative synthesis adhering strictly to the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline and the PRISMA 2020 statement (Supplement 3) [11, 15]. Additionally, the overall certainty of evidence for each critical outcome was formally appraised using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework (Supplement 4) [16].

Results

Eligible studies

The initial search identified 3562 records. After screening, 29 RCTs met the eligibility criteria and were included for data extraction. The included studies exhibited highly heterogeneous methodological quality, with modified Jadad scores ranging from 4 to 8. Although the vast majority of the studies (28/29) achieved a reporting-centric modified Jadad score of ≥ 5, their deeper operational designs were confounded by critical risks of bias, such as unblinded dropouts and unclear allocation concealment. Crucially, when evaluated under the objective GRADE framework across all consolidated functional esophageal disease sections, the overall certainty of evidence was uniformly graded as very low for all synthesized clinical outcomes. The clinical evaluation and diagnostic criteria for FEDs are detailed in Supplement 5. It is worth noting that while 29 independent studies were included in total, Limsrivilai 2016 reported data for both FH and RH subgroups.

FCP findings

FCP describes recurring pain behind the breastbone that is thought to originate from the esophagus. This pain is distinct from heartburn and not caused by reflux, motility disorder, or mucosal pathology in the esophagus [1]. This condition is common and affects 19–33% of people [17, 18]. Non-cardiac chest pain is often linked to psychological conditions. In such cases, disorders such as anxiety, depression, and somatization occur frequently in up to 75% of patients [19]. This psychological burden considerably reduces the quality-of-life of a patient and is also linked to frequent episodes of chest pain and a poor response to established medical therapies [20].

Among the 11 RCTs that investigated GBNs for FCPs [21–31] five explicitly excluded patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [21, 22, 24, 26, 31], In contrast, only one study performed esophageal biopsies to rule out eosinophilic esophagitis and fully adhered to the Rome IV diagnostic criteria [21]. Eight trials reported positive outcomes (Table 1). Specifically, oral treatment with venlafaxine [24], sertraline [28], or imipramine [30] demonstrated efficacy in lowering chest pain burden compared to placebo, although the specific therapeutic metrics and evaluated effect sizes varied widely across trials.

Mechanistically, Rao et al. [26] demonstrated that intravenous theophylline increased esophageal pain thresholds and wall distensibility. Similarly, Malik et al. [21] found that a four-week course of dronabinol not only elevated visceral pain thresholds but also reduced the frequency and intensity of chest pain. In a crossover trial by Cox et al. [29], chest pain episodes under imipramine (50 mg) treatment were markedly lower than that under the placebo treatment, but overall quality-of-life was not improved. However, owing to the small sample size, the lack of intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, and unclear allocation concealment, the conclusions of these studies require validation in a large-scale trial. Park et al. [22] reported that patients receiving low-dose amitriptyline (10 mg at bedtime) plus standard-dose rabeprazole achieved > 50% global symptom improvement more often than those receiving double-dose rabeprazole (20 mg twice daily). However, the open-label trial may have resulted in an overestimation of the therapeutic effect.

In another study, Keefe et al. [23] observed that sertraline, coping skills therapy, and their combination all substantially reduced pain intensity over 34 weeks, despite an initial placebo response that was not sustained. In contrast, two trials found that paroxetine was no more effective than the placebo was [25, 27]. Regarding trazodone, Clouse et al. [31] observed that while both the treatment and placebo groups improved substantially due to a high placebo response, trazodone demonstrated a statistically significant advantage over placebo, yielding greater global improvement (P = 0.02) and less residual symptom distress (P = 0.03). These clinical trials, however, are fundamentally downgraded to a very low GRADE certainty due to the ubiquitous presence of small sample sizes, brief follow-up durations, and poorly defined allocation concealment schemes across the contributing trials.

FH findings

FH is characterized by retrosternal burning pain that persists despite optimal antisecretory therapy. This occurs in the absence of GERD, mucosal injury, major motility disorders, or structural abnormalities [1]. FH is reported in 10–21% of patients presenting with heartburn [32, 33], thus accounting for approximately 50% of proton pump inhibitor (PPI) non-responders and 25% of PPI responders [34]. Experimental stress models indicate enhanced visceral sensitivity to acid in GERD. Clinically, patients with FH exhibit higher scores for anxiety and somatization, and report poorer social support than those with objective reflux. This highlights the importance of psychosocial assessment in these individuals [35].

Two studies examined the impact of GBNs on FH (Table 2). Limsrivilai et al. [36] evaluated 83 patients with standard PPI-refractory non-erosive reflux disease, among whom 31 (37%) were classified as having FH. In the specific FH subgroup analysis, oral treatment with low-dose imipramine (25 mg) failed to show any therapeutic advantage over placebo. By ITT analysis, the symptom response rates (defined as a 50% reduction in the total GERD score) were entirely comparable between the imipramine and placebo arms (41.2% vs. 42.9%; P = 0.92). This lack of efficacy was further mirrored in the per-protocol (PP) analysis for the FH cohort (50.0% vs. 54.5%; P = 0.83). Low-dose imipramine showed potential quality-of-life benefits. However, Ostovaneh et al. [37] found that fluoxetine outperformed omeprazole in terms of reducing symptom frequency, as reflected by a higher median improvement in the percentage of heartburn-free days(35.7% vs. 7.14%). Crucially, stratified subgroup analysis demonstrated that this therapeutic benefit was entirely driven by the subpopulation with normal esophageal acid exposure (pH-negative subgroup). Within this normal pH stratification, fluoxetine yielded a significantly higher median improvement in the percentage of heartburn-free days (57.1%, IQR: 35.7–57.1%) compared to both omeprazole (13.9%, IQR: 0–45.6%) and placebo (7.14%, IQR: 0–23.8%; P < 0.001). Conversely, no statistically significant treatment differences among the three arms were observed in patients with pathological acid reflux (pH-positive subgroup). Both studies demonstrated a relatively reliable level of methodological rigor in terms of randomization, concealed allocation, ITT analysis, and double-blind design. However, certain risks of selective reporting in terms of the interpretation and presentation of secondary endpoints and their clinical importance should be interpreted with caution.

RH findings

RH is characterized by typical physiological reflux-triggered esophageal symptoms (heartburn or chest pain) in the absence of endoscopic lesions or pathological acid exposure [1]. RH is estimated to occur in 10–36% of patients with non-erosive reflux disease [38, 39]. Notably, psychological stress is recognized as a key modulator of esophageal sensory perception. Centrally, this factor alters autonomic tone and spinal nociceptive transmission, whereas peripherally, this factor increases mucosal permeability via mast cell activation [40]. Corticotropin-releasing hormone, which is a central neuroendocrine mediator, is implicated in this process [41]. Consequently, psychological factors are regarded as a core pathophysiological feature of RH, which is consistent with their role in other FEDs.

Three studies investigating the effect of GBNs in patients with RH were identified and showed inconsistent results (Table 3) [36, 42, 43]. In a crossover trial by Broekaert et al. [43], the acute effects of citalopram was evaluated in 10 healthy volunteers with diagnosed esophageal hypersensitivity. However, citalopram did not affect esophageal motility but substantially increased visceral sensory thresholds during balloon distension. Citalopram also reduced the incidence of thoracic sensation during edrophonium provocation (from 7/10 to 2/10 subjects; P = 0.02). Considering that the study was a mechanistic study with a small sample size, the conclusions should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to clinical practice. In a six-month trial by Viazis et al. [42], citalopram (20 mg daily) led to significantly higher symptom resolution than that under the placebo (61.5% vs. 33.3%; P = 0.021). In contrast, Limsrivilai et al. [36] evaluated 83 patients with RH and FH in their primary ITT analysis, revealing that imipramine (25 mg at bedtime for eight weeks) was not superior to placebo in providing satisfactory symptom relief (37.2% vs. 37.5%; OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.41–2.41). When turning to the secondary per-protocol (PP) subgroup analysis across the pooled cohorts, the response rates shifted to 45.5% versus 41.2% (OR: 1.19, 95% CI: 0.45–3.13), which remained statistically non-significant. Similarly, dedicated subgroup analyses restricted purely to patients with RH yielded negative results (37.5% vs. 40.0%; P = 0.87). Furthermore, a quality-of-life benefit was observed only in the per-protocol analysis and constipation occurred significantly more frequently with imipramine (51.2% vs. 22.5%; P = 0.01) than with other drugs. Although the findings of these two studies are inconsistent [36, 42], both investigations demonstrate potential selection bias, weakens the overall GRADE certainty for the RH subpopulation.

Four clinical trials tested the efficacy of GBNs against experimentally induced esophageal pain. These were controlled experiments on healthy individuals who did not have anxiety or depression. The tested drugs included TCAs, gamma-aminobutyric acid analogs, and a selective acid-sensing ion channel 1a (ASIC1a) antagonist (Table 4) [44–47]. One study by Olesen et al. [44] applied moderate mechanical pressure to the esophagus. In this setup, the ASIC1a antagonist drug PPC-5650 allowed participants to tolerate a significantly larger volume of pressure than that of the placebo. The measured difference was 13.5 (95% CI 0.58–26.47; P = 0.04). However, this had no measurable effect on pain thresholds from other types of stimuli, such as heat, electricity, or chemical infusion. Moreover, the drug did not change the pattern of referred pain areas, and all comparisons for other outcomes were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Crucially, this study represents limited evidence due to missing data for several stimulations, the small sample size, the artificial nature of acute healthy volunteer models, and the lack of consistent multi-modal sensitization.

In a double-blind study by Gorelick et al. [47], amitriptyline (50 mg) treatment for 21 days markedly increased current thresholds for cutaneous sensation, moderate discomfort, and moderate pain relative to the baseline, whereas the placebo showed no effect. However, amitriptyline did not alter the perception of esophageal distension or affect luminal compliance. [45] demonstrated that pregabalin markedly attenuated the development of acid-induced secondary esophageal hypersensitivity in the proximal esophagus, without affecting baseline pain thresholds. Pregabalin also reduced subjective pain scores during acid infusion. However, considering that these studies are exploratory mechanism studies with small sample sizes, their findings warrant caution in terms of clinical extrapolation.

Globus findings

Globus is defined as a persistent or intermittent non-painful feeling of a lump or foreign body in the throat. It is reported by up to 46% of healthy individuals [1, 48]. Symptoms are often episodic, may improve with eating, and are typically not associated with dysphagia or odynophagia. The diagnosis of this condition requires initial ruling out of other clear causes. Doctors must exclude structural issues, tissue abnormalities, GERD, and major problems with esophageal movement [1]. Clinically, symptoms often begin after stressful life events, which suggests that stress may act as a contributing factor. Furthermore, a vast majority of patients (up to 96%) report that their symptoms worsen during times of emotional distress [49]. Although psychological conditions are frequently observed in these patients, strong scientific proof directly linking them to the disorder remains limited.

Six studies investigated the efficacy of GBNs in treating globus (Table 5). In particular, You et al. [50], compared two drugs over four weeks. Patients taking 25 mg of amitriptyline daily showed better results than those taking 40 mg of pantoprazole daily. Specifically, the amitriptyline group had a higher response rate (75% compared to 36%) and greater improvement in their symptoms, sleep, and quality-of-life than those of the pantoprazole group. Moreover, Chen et al. [51] followed patients for six weeks and they found that both paroxetine and amitriptyline were much more effective than lansoprazole was. Specifically, the response rates were 71.7% for paroxetine and 46.2% for amitriptyline, but only 14.0% for lansoprazole. Further supporting these findings, Zhou et al. [52] noted that the symptom relief from both paroxetine and amitriptyline was linked to a measurable drop in upper esophageal sphincter pressure. This finding suggests a physical mechanism for the improvement. Additionally, their data showed that patients taking paroxetine were significantly less likely than those on amitriptyline to leave the study due to symptom relapse (P = 0.001). Correspondingly, Poovipirom et al. [53] demonstrated that cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and GBNs (flupenthixol/melitracen) were similarly effective and superior to PPIs in terms of reducing globus symptoms after four weeks. Although improvements in reflux symptoms, anxiety/depression, and quality-of-life were comparable across all three groups, treatment with neuromodulators was associated with an increased incidence of adverse events.

In contrast, several studies raise concerns regarding tolerability and comparative efficacy. Specifically, Deary et al. [54] reported that 9 of 12 patients discontinued amitriptyline treatment, thereby highlighting substantial tolerability issue with this TCA. Moreover, Takahashi et al. [55] found comparable response rates after four weeks between a potassium-competitive acid blocker (vonoprazan) and an SNRI (milnacipran), with 14 and 10 responders (and 17 vs. 11 non-responders), respectively. Notably, SNRI responders had low pretreatment symptom scores. Most studies employed rigorous randomization methods [51–53], and some included sample size estimation to enhance comparability and statistical power. Nevertheless, the overall methodological quality was compromised by unclear allocation concealment in most studies, as well as the absence of ITT analysis in over half of the studies (You et al., Chen et al., and Deary et al.) [50, 51, 54], which may overestimate treatment effects and introduce selection bias. Additionally, Deary et al. [54] and Takahashi et al. [55] were further limited by small sample sizes or high dropout rates. Consequently, although antidepressants or cognitive behavioral therapy may offer benefits for globus sensation, the robustness and generalizability of the evidence remain constrained by inadequate bias control across studies.

FD findings

FD is characterized by a sensation of impaired bolus transit through the esophagus in the absence of identifiable structural, mucosal, or major motility disorders. The condition is prevalent in approximately 7–8% of healthy individuals [1, 56]. Evidence supporting psychological distress as a core feature remains limited [57]. Specifically, insights from acute stress models do not fully explain the chronicity and clinical presentation of the condition [58]. Furthermore, the association with anxiety or depression is less established than that in other functional disorders [59]. Only one study involved patients with FD, whereas three other studies conducted in healthy volunteers were related to the mechanisms of FD and its treatment (Table 6).

The sole double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study by Aggarwal et al. [60], evaluated the effects of buspirone (10 mg three times a day) in 10 patients with concurrent FD and ineffective esophageal motility (IEM). Following two weeks of treatment, buspirone showed no considerable benefit over the placebo in any symptom measure, including frequency, severity, or overall presence. Manometric parameters also did not differ substantially between the buspirone and placebo groups. However, the study conclusions are limited by the failure to definitively rule out GERD and further constrained by the small sample size, short treatment duration, and low dosage, which subsequently lowers its outcome-specific GRADE certainty and restricts its clinical applicability.

Studies involving healthy volunteers provide useful clues about the possible mechanisms of GBNs in dysphagia disorders (Table 7) [61–63]. In particular, alprazolam was administered to ten subjects and substantially reduced resting pressure in the upper esophageal sphincter (UES) and impaired the ability of the esophagus to clear acid at night [63]. However, the drug did not affect the lower esophageal sphincter or overall esophageal movement [63]. Another trial tested single doses of three different drugs : buspirone (20 mg), pyridostygmine (60 mg), and bethanechol (25 mg). Each drug strengthened contractions in the lower esophagus and increased residual pressure in the lower esophageal sphincter. Specifically, pyridostigmine decelerated these contractions and increased the total time for liquid passage. This suggests that these agents could potentially modify esophageal motility patterns [62]. A third study examined citalopram and observed a dual effect. In particular, the medication increased resting and post-swallow pressures at both the upper and lower esophageal sphincters, while simultaneously reducing distal esophageal contractile vigor, transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations, and reflux events. Notably, citalopram induced globus sensation in some participants, which correlated with the degree of UES pressure elevation [61]. Among them, Manolakis et al. [61] demonstrated the rigorous methodology, with randomization and allocation concealment, a relatively large sample size, and comprehensive reporting of drug-related adverse events. Furthermore, Blonski et al. [62] implemented effective blinding but was limited by a small sample size, absence of sample size estimation, and lack of correction for multiple comparisons. This raised some concerns about the robustness of the findings. Correspondingly, Singh et al. [63] provided unclear descriptions of randomization and allocation concealment and failed to appropriately address a case of data loss. Additionally, all three studies employed a crossover design. Considering that these studies were conducted in healthy individuals, their findings warrant cautious interpretation.

Adverse events and safety profiles

To ensure a balanced evaluation of GBNs therapies across FEDs, a structured synthesis of treatment-emergent adverse events was performed. Given the clinical and pharmacological diversity of the included agents, a dedicated synthesis of the specific types and reported incidences of adverse events is comprehensively summarized in Supplement 6. While mild to moderate central nervous system and gastrointestinal side effects (such as drowsiness, dry mouth, and constipation) were frequently documented, they rarely culminated in severe clinical sequelae or treatment withdrawal across the consolidated cohorts.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to gather and evaluate all available evidence on the use of GBNs for the full range of FEDs, as currently defined by the Rome IV criteria. Overall, the review found that the effectiveness of this treatment approach varies considerably depending on the specific diagnosis. Specifically, the high-quality evidence supports using GBNs for FCP and globus sensation. However, the reported benefits for disorders, such as RH and FH, are less uniform than those for other disorders. Moreover, the substantial lack of research indicates the minimal availability of evidence to guide the use of GBNs for FD. GBNs are considered a fundamental treatment for disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI). These drugs target pathways in both the brain and the gut that are involved in heightening the sensitivity of internal organs [64, 65].

This mechanism is particularly relevant for FEDs. A key reason is the recognized role of esophageal hypervigilance, where patients become overly focused on esophageal sensations, and symptom-related anxiety in shaping their experience of illness. Furthermore, a consistent finding across studies is the major influence of psychological and cognitive factors in patients with disrupted gut–brain communication. Considering this, GBNs appear to be a theoretically well-suited treatment option for FEDs. However, compiling definitive proof of their effectiveness for these conditions is difficult. Existing studies vary greatly in terms of several key areas. These include changes in diagnostic standards over time, different definitions of successful treatment response, choices of specific drugs and doses, and inconsistent recording of accompanying psychological conditions. This variability renders it difficult to combine results in a rigorous meta-analysis and to fairly compare outcomes from different trials. Despite these challenges, the collective evidence reinforces the view of FEDs as classic DGBIs. Furthermore, the data support a model where symptoms stem from faulty signaling along the gut-brain axis. This dysregulation is driven by a combination of psychological distress, altered processing in the central nervous system, and impaired sensory or motor function in the esophagus itself. Within this framework, GBNs remain a treatment approach with considerable potential for managing FEDs.

Current management for FCP primarily targets pain modulation via the GBA. FCP is characterized by visceral hypersensitivity and central hypervigilance. Neuromodulators are believed to attenuate visceral afferent signaling, modulate central pain processing, and regulate attentional bias [8]. First-line pharmacotherapy includes various antidepressants (TCAs, SSRIs, SNRIs), which demonstrate meaningful efficacy but are constrained by side effects and variable patient acceptance. This has triggered an interest in alternatives, such as gabapentinoids and theophylline. Systematic evidence indicates a broad treatment response to GBNs, with symptom relief rates ranging from 18 to 67% and 50% response rates between 52% and 71% across studies [21–31]. Low-dose TCAs effectively alleviate symptoms; however their impact on quality-of-life remains unclear [22, 29, 30]. Crucially, as supported by the newly integrated evidence from a retrospective study by Voulgaris et al., both citalopram and amitriptyline show comparable efficacy, thereby improving symptoms in approximately 50% of patients [66]. Furthermore, combining amitriptyline with a PPI may offer additional quality-of-life benefits [22]. Therapeutic efficacy extends to other agents, including venlafaxine, sertraline, dronabinol, and theophylline [21, 23, 24, 26, 28]. Notably, early evidence from Clouse et al. [31] demonstrates that both groups improved substantially due to a high placebo response, but low-dose trazodone yields a statistically significant advantage over placebo in overall global improvement and the reduction of residual esophageal distress. A key methodological limitation is the inadequate exclusion of GERD, which confounds interpretation owing to its different pathophysiology from FCP [67]. Although neuromodulators represent a validated strategy for FCP, their use requires careful risk-benefit evaluation.

Although GBNs are empirically used, their efficacy in PPI-resistant heartburn is inconsistent [68]. Hence, the evolving Rome IV diagnostic distinction between RH and FH is crucial for interpreting neuromodulator trials. Furthermore, assessment of treatment efficacy is complicated by heterogeneous patient cohorts enrolled under broad criteria, alongside considerable inter-individual variability and complex underlying mechanisms. Moreover, SSRIs such as fluoxetine demonstrate efficacy in patients with PPI-refractory GERD with normal acid exposure [37]. Although citalopram effectively alleviated symptoms in RH [42], a similar study also demonstrated the effectiveness of SSRIs in treating RH [43]. In contrast, the TCA showed no notable benefit over the placebo in rigorously defined FH or RH [36]. This discrepancy may be a consequence of differing mechanisms. Specifically, considering that SSRIs primarily modulate serotonergic signaling, the drugs may hold increased potential for addressing central sensitization.

The biological rationale for GBNs is supported by experimental models. These suggest that certain agents, such as ASIC1a antagonists, may specifically elevate the perceptual threshold to esophageal mechanical distension without altering wall compliance. However, evidence for TCAs, such as imipramine, is inconsistent [44–47]. These findings collectively indicate that their primary benefit likely stems from the modulation of perception rather than a change in esophageal biomechanics. Considering the established link between psychological comorbidity, gut-brain axis dysregulation, and heightened visceral perception in RH [69], this observation is highly relevant and suggests that visceral hypersensitivity may be driven by diverse, targetable molecular pathways. Accordingly, future therapeutic trials must employ stringent phenotype-specific (Rome IV) diagnostic criteria to resolve past inconsistencies. Another conceptual consideration in our synthesis is the programmatic inclusion of healthy volunteers exposed to experimentally induced esophageal hypersensitivity alongside clinical cohorts. From a methodological standpoint, combining these distinct populations was an intentional approach rooted in a translational pharmacology perspective. While healthy individuals undergoing standardized mechanosensory or chemosensory challenges (such as acid perfusion or barostat distension) are clinically distinct from patients with naturally occurring RH or FD, they provide a pristine, confounder-free physiological matrix. This controlled paradigm allows researchers to isolate the baseline sensory-modulatory capacities of GBNs without the confounding variables typically present in clinical populations. For refractory heartburn, proactive subtyping based on reflux monitoring is warranted. Moreover, routine assessment of anxiety, depression, and stress should be integrated into clinical practice. Therefore, future management must adopt a precise and individualized strategy to combine neuromodulators with psychosocial interventions to improve outcomes in these challenging patients.

Globus represents a compelling indication for GBNs among FEDs. Systematic review evidence confirms the efficacy of agents, such as TCAs and SSRIs, a finding robustly supported by a meta-analysis demonstrating a pooled odds ratio of 6.30 for treatment response compared with that of PPIs [70]. The therapeutic success is attributed to a dual mechanism of action. First, globus is recognized as a classic disorder of the gut–brain interaction that involves visceral hypersensitivity and central nervous system dysregulation, which is frequently accompanied by anxiety. Low-dose TCAs (e.g., amitriptyline) and SSRIs (e.g., paroxetine) address this core pathophysiology by attenuating the central perception of the aberrant sensation, thereby reducing symptom intensity [50–52, 54]. Second, a distinct peripheral effect is evident: successful treatment with these agents has been associated with a measurable reduction in UES pressure [51, 52], which suggests that they also mitigate a peripheral component of the disorder. Recent advances have refined management towards a personalized multimodal paradigm, and pre-treatment symptom profiles can predict therapeutic response. Furthermore, specific symptoms appear to influence patient responses to medication. In particular, those reporting severe pain or swelling tend to achieve good outcomes with a potassium-competitive acid blocker. In contrast, patients whose primary symptom is globus sensation often respond more favorably to SNRI antidepressants [55]. Furthermore, individuals with the S/S genotype of the serotonin transporter gene (SLC6A4) are not only more susceptible to these disorders but also show a stronger therapeutic response to antidepressant medication. This suggests a potential biomarker for personalizing treatment plans [71]. Beyond pharmacology, CBT is a highly effective intervention. The therapy efficacy is comparable with that of GBNs and is superior to PPI therapy in several studies [53, 72].

The therapeutic rationale for neuromodulators in FD is not strongly supported by clinical evidence. A pivotal randomized trial of buspirone in patients with FD overlapping IEM demonstrated no pronounced improvement in symptoms or manometric parameters [60]. Pharmacological studies in healthy volunteers reveal agent-specific and occasionally paradoxical effects on esophageal motility. In contrast, prokinetic agents, such as buspirone, typically enhance contractile vigor, whereas others, such as pyridostigmine, may decelerate peristaltic velocity, thereby potentially worsening dysphagia [62] Notably, anxiolytics (e.g., alprazolam) can compromise protective reflux-clearing mechanisms [63], and SSRIs such as citalopram may simultaneously increase lower esophageal sphincter tone while reducing distal esophageal contractility [61]. These findings emphasize that drug selection must account for precise physiological effects beyond symptom indication alone. Considering the generally benign natural history of FD, first-line management should prioritize patient reassurance and non-pharmacologic interventions, including dietary and behavioral modifications.

A notable methodological observation in our study is the apparent divergence between the modified Jadad scores and the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool ratings for certain trials. The modified Jadad scale is a reporting-centric index that heavily weights the explicit mention and description of randomization, blinding, and withdrawals. Conversely, the RoB tool drills deeper into the actual execution of the trial, scrutinizing specific operational domains where bias can actively compromise internal validity, such as deviations from intended interventions or selective reporting of secondary outcomes. This variation in final outputs underscores exactly why a dual-scale appraisal was necessary in our review; relying on a single tool would risk masking these critical operational nuances.

Several limitations must be acknowledged in this synthesis. First, our search strategy was subject to an English-only language restriction, which may have excluded relevant non-English literature and introduced potential geographic bias. Second, the included mechanistic and clinical trials are characterized by notably small sample sizes, which severely limits statistical power. Third, significant heterogeneity exists across the diagnostic criteria employed, rendering a pooled quantitative meta-analysis unfeasible and potentially introducing publication bias that is difficult to formally test. Consequently, our findings rely on a qualitative narration, and the resulting very low GRADE certainty ratings underscore that the broader clinical generalizability of these psychotropic and neuromodulatory interventions should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that the efficacy of pharmacologic neuromodulators varies considerably across specific FEDs. The most consistent available evidence supports their use in FCP and globus, where they modulate central perception and visceral sensitivity. In contrast, benefits are inconsistent in FH and RH, and evidence is lacking for FD. Correspondingly, critical methodological limitations, including heterogeneous diagnostic criteria and patient phenotypes, have historically obscured clear therapeutic signals. Therefore, future management must adopt a precision approach that aligns treatment with the underlying pathophysiology of each disorder. This requires rigorously designed phenotype-specific clinical trials and the integration of neuromodulators into a broader therapeutic strategy that includes behavioral interventions and personalized care pathways.
