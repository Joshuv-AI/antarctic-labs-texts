Fourteen-year bridge to cure in occult ectopic ACTH syndrome: resection of a 3-mm pulmonary carcinoid
Authors: Nobuyuki Koriyama, Takahiko Obo, Kazuma Ogiso, Mitsuharu Nomoto, Koichi Sakasegawa, Yoshihiko Nishio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEctopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome (EAS) often involves occult microtumors, making localization challenging. We report a 68-year-old woman with severe ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism in whom the primary tumor remained occult despite extensive imaging, including 68Ga-tetraazacyclododecanetetraacetic acid-D-Phe(1)-Tyr(3)-octreotide positron emission tomography/computed tomography. Based on a positive octreotide challenge test, she received long-acting release octreotide for over a decade, achieving sustained biochemical stability. In 2020, she developed breast cancer, with the tumor being ACTH-negative on immunohistochemistry. In 2023, during resection of pulmonary metastases of the breast cancer, a 3-mm nodule was incidentally discovered in the adjacent lung tissue. Through close interdisciplinary coordination, the nodule was removed and was confirmed as an ACTH- and somatostatin receptor (SSTR) 2-positive pulmonary carcinoid. Thereafter, the EAS resolved completely with prompt recovery of adrenal function. In this case, intensive medical stabilization in occult EAS probably served as a strategic “bridge to surgery.” Importantly, long-term biochemical control using somatostatin analogs facilitated prompt recovery of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis immediately after tumor resection. This may also have preserved SSTR2 expression, potentially by mitigating cortisol-induced receptor downregulation, facilitating its eventual localization. This report shows that meticulous multidisciplinary communication during unrelated surgical procedures is indispensable for identifying radiologically occult lesions.

Introduction

Ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome (EAS) accounts for 15% of ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome [1, 2]. However, since causative tumors are typically only a few millimeters, localization remains extremely difficult in 12-19% of cases despite extensive imaging [1, 3]. Diagnosing occult EAS is a significant challenge due to the limitations of current imaging modalities. Consequently, physicians must manage hypercortisolism long-term while continuing surveillance. The incidence of Cushing syndrome from nonmalignant conditions or carcinoids is ∼2.4 cases/million/year [4]. Thus, the incidence of occult EAS is estimated at 3 cases per 10 million/year. Being an extremely rare condition, few reports of long-term follow-up exist [5]. Additionally, the optimal management strategy for occult EAS remains unclear, particularly regarding whether sustained medical therapy can facilitate eventual curative surgery. We previously reported the initial presentation and treatment of occult EAS over 4 years in this patient [6]. Here, we describe an additional 10-year follow-up, focusing on the period during which the patient achieved a cure following the incidental discovery and resection of a primary pulmonary carcinoid.

Case presentation

In 2009, a 68-year-old woman presented with severe ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, accompanied by systemic complications including lumbar compression fractures, proximal myopathy, hypokalemia, hypertension, and hyperglycemia.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory investigations revealed a complete loss of diurnal cortisol rhythm. Plasma ACTH and serum cortisol levels were markedly elevated at 23:00 hours (122 pg/mL [SI: 27.1 pmol/L] (reference range, 7.2-63.3 pg/mL [SI: 1.59-13.94 pmol/L]) and 28.4 μg/dL [SI: 784 nmol/L] (reference range, 6.24-18.0 μg/dL [SI: 172.2-496.6 nmol/L]), respectively), with morning peaks reaching 209.0 pg/mL (SI: 46.8 pmol/L) and 43.9 μg/dL (SI: 1212 nmol/L), respectively. The hypercortisolemia was not suppressible by an 8-mg high-dose dexamethasone suppression test, and a blunted ACTH response to corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) stimulation was observed. Regarding interpetrosal sinus sampling, technical difficulties required atypical sampling near the right superior petrosal sinus and in the left jugular vein. Despite these technical limitations, no significant responsiveness to CRH stimulation was observed. These findings, combined with the absence of pituitary adenoma on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were highly suggestive of EAS, although multiple imaging modalities, including 68Ga-tetraazacyclododecanetetraacetic acid -D-Phe(1)-Tyr(3)-octreotide (DOTATOC) positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT), failed to localize the primary tumor (Fig. 1). An octreotide challenge test confirmed high somatostatin receptor sensitivity (>50% suppression of ACTH). Her plasma dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) level was 512 μg/dL (SI: 13.9 μmol/L) (reference range, 12-133 μg/dL [SI: 0.33-3.61 μmol/L]), and 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) was 3.6 mg/day (reference range, 1.0-6.0 mg/day).

Treatment

Following stabilization with octreotide long-acting release (LAR), a somatostatin analog (SSA), at doses of 10-20 mg, the patient's hypokalemia, proximal myopathy, hypertension and hyperglycemia improved substantially. However, she electively discontinued treatment in 2011. Remarkably, she experienced prolonged treatment-free remission lasting 3.8 years. After a biochemical recurrence in 2014, the disease was again successfully managed with octreotide LAR, with subsequent improvement in these clinical manifestations. During the intervening 9-year follow-up period, she maintained sustained clinical remission with continuation of octreotide LAR (Fig. 1). In 2018, a follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scan from the neck to the pelvis revealed no suspicious findings; the primary ACTH-producing lesion remained radiologically occult at that time (Fig. 2A).

In 2020, following a diagnosis of breast cancer at another institution, the patient underwent surgical resection, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Although the specific protocols for these initial treatments are unavailable, she has since been maintained on a combination of palbociclib (75 mg/day), a CDK4/6 inhibitor, and letrozole (2.5 mg/day), an aromatase inhibitor. This treatment regimen is ongoing and managed at her previous institution. Although ACTH-producing breast cancer is exceedingly rare (reported in <1% of cases) [7], we proactively performed immunohistochemistry of the surgical specimen of the primary breast tumor to exclude it as the source of EAS; the results were negative for ACTH (Fig. 2B).

In 2023, during thoracic surgery for pulmonary metastasis of the breast cancer (Fig. 3A), a well-circumscribed but irregular margined 8-mm solid nodule in the lateral segment of the right middle lobe (S4) was resected due to its slow increase in size on preoperative CT. Notably, a 3-mm nodule was incidentally discovered in the immediate vicinity of this 8-mm lesion and was simultaneously resected. Postoperative pathological examination identified this 3-mm nodule as the ACTH-producing tumor. Even upon retrospective review of the thin-section CT images by expert radiologists, this specific 3-mm tumor had remained radiologically occult, hidden by or indistinguishable from the adjacent 8-mm nodule. Throughout the patient's 14-year clinical course, we maintained close communication with the surgical teams, requesting the provision of all resected tissues to help definitively identify the occult primary lesion of the EAS. Pathological examination of the 3-mm nodule revealed a pulmonary carcinoid. Subsequent immunohistochemical analysis confirmed strong positivity for both ACTH (Fig. 3B) and somatostatin receptor (SSTR) 2 (Fig. 3C), with a Ki-67 labeling index of <1%. This enabled definitive identification of the pulmonary carcinoid as the primary lesion responsible for the EAS. Postoperatively, octreotide LAR was discontinued.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient has thereafter remained recurrence-free for over 2 years, with complete clinical and biochemical resolution of the EAS (Fig. 1).

Discussion

We report here a rare case of occult EAS. The management strategy, centered on long-term octreotide LAR, led to stable maintenance of the patient's condition for more than a decade. Ultimately, the incidental discovery and excision of a pulmonary carcinoid 14 years after the initial diagnosis led to complete recovery. The incidental detection highlights the importance of surgical awareness even during procedures unrelated to EAS. This case highlights a critical management pearl: in patients with occult EAS where the source lesion is not immediately localized, long-term medical suppression of hypercortisolism serves as a vital bridge to definitive surgery. Prioritizing reversible treatments rather than irreversible bilateral adrenalectomy allows preservation of the potential for full adrenal recovery and a superior long-term prognosis once the primary tumor is identified. Our strategy focusing on rigorous hormonal control not only avoided the complications of hypercortisolism, but may also have contributed to preservation of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis responsiveness, leading to an immediate postoperative restoration of normal adrenal function.

In managing this case of occult EAS, we prioritized biochemical control of hypercortisolism over repeated localized imaging. This strategic decision was based on several factors: the initial failure to identify the primary lesion despite high-sensitivity imaging, including 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT, which is reportedly superior to 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-PET [8] and more efficient than OctreoScanTM [9] for neuroendocrine tumor (NET) detection. While SSTR2 expression is a hallmark of NETs that cause EAS, its diagnostic sensitivity can be significantly impaired by hypercortisolism-induced downregulation. Excess glucocorticoids diminish SSTR2 density, potentially leading to false-negative results on SSTR-targeted imaging. While not available during this patient's initial evaluation, “pharmacological priming” with selective glucocorticoid receptor (GR) antagonists, such as relacorilant, has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these diagnostic hurdles. By upregulating SSTR2 density on NETs, this approach can potentially convert a negative 68Ga-SSTR PET/CT into a positive one [10]. In our case, the initial negative 68Ga-tetraazacyclododecanetetraacetic acid-D-Phe(1)-Tyr(3)-octreotate PET/CT might have been influenced by this biochemical suppression; thus, our “biochemical-first” strategy served as a deliberate measure to break the vicious cycle of SSTR downregulation. Moreover, the 3-mm lesion likely fell below the detection threshold of current imaging modalities. Given the sustained biochemical response to SSAs, we prioritized clinical stability over low-yield diagnostic procedures, minimizing radiation exposure. Our experience suggests that in select occult cases, rigorous hormonal control provides a viable alternative to immediate, aggressive intervention, provided there is sustained biochemical control and high-quality imaging follow-up. While our patient achieved biochemical stability through long-term SSA therapy, which may have similarly preserved SSTR2 expression, the proactive use of GR antagonists might further optimize localization of the source of EAS in future cases. Furthermore, for lesions falling below the current 3-mm detection threshold, next-generation molecular imaging, including 68Ga-Pentixafor for C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4-targeted imaging [11] or the use of SSTR-antagonists [12], may offer the requisite sensitivity to identify radiologically elusive sources of EAS.

Although the majority of patients with EAS express SSTRs, the long-term utility of octreotide remains insufficiently established [13]. This limited durability is primarily attributed to therapeutic escape, which may arise from: (1) SSTR downregulation, often exacerbated by hypercortisolism; (2) expansion of SSTR-negative clonal populations; or (3) development of antioctreotide antibodies [14, 15]. Despite these general limitations, several factors likely contributed to the sustained therapeutic efficacy observed in the present case. First, hypercortisolism is known to suppress SSTR expression, particularly SSTR2, through both transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms. Glucocorticoids directly act on the SSTR2 promoter to diminish its transcriptional activity, while also impairing receptor trafficking by promoting internalization and inhibiting recycling to the cell membrane. This downregulation creates a “vicious cycle,” in which escalating cortisol levels progressively blunt SSA responsiveness [15]. In our patient, however, the prompt and stable biochemical control of cortisol levels may have prevented this suppressive effect, thereby preserving SSTR density on the tumor cell surface. Pasireotide, a SSA, generally demonstrates superior efficacy in controlling hypercortisolism because of its higher affinity for SSTR5. However, in the present case, a robust biochemical response to octreotide was predicted by the positive octreotide suppression test, and was subsequently supported by strong SSTR2 immunoreactivity of the tumor. Second, the primary tumor was a well-differentiated NET with indolent biological behavior and a low Ki-67 index. In well-differentiated NETs, ACTH secretory capacity is governed by proopiomelanocortin gene transcription and post-translational processing, distinct from tumor proliferative activity [16]. This difference explains why severe endocrine symptoms can persist even in the presence of minimal tumor burden. This characteristic likely minimized the risk of clonal evolution toward SSTR-negative populations, ensuring maintained sensitivity to octreotide over the long-term [17]. Notably, the tumor showed no evidence of measurable growth, local invasion, or metastasis over a 14-year period. This indolent clinical course may be attributable to both the tumor's inherently favorable biological characteristics, as evidenced by a low Ki-67 index (<1%), and the potential suppressive effects of long-term SSA therapy.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that long-term hormonal stabilization in occult EAS not only facilitates definitive surgical resection, but also contributes to prompt recovery of the HPA axis postoperatively. This successful outcome likely resulted from a synergy between the maintenance of SSTR expression through sustained biochemical control, and the inherent indolent nature of the tumor. Our experience underscores the potential for intensive medical therapy for EAS to serve as a strategic “bridge to surgery” rather than mere palliation. Furthermore, it highlights the importance of proactively sharing diagnostic uncertainty across disciplines, showing that such multidisciplinary communication during unrelated surgical procedures is indispensable for identification and localization of radiologically occult lesions. Ultimately, in select patients with occult EAS, time can be leveraged as a therapeutic advantage rather than a diagnostic limitation to ensure sustained biochemical control.
