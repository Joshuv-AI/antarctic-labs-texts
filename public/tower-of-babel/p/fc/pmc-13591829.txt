Transforming oncology nursing navigation: impact of online training on holistic care and therapeutic relationship skills
Authors: Faith Liao, Hsiu Yun Li, Pei-Lan Kuo, Hui-Fen Fang, Jeng-Fong Chiou, Chun‐Chao Chang, Kuei-Ru Chou, Su-Ru Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOncology nurse navigators play a critical role in addressing patients' physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs. However, gaps in existing training programmes often limit the effective integration of holistic care principles and therapeutic relationship skills into clinical practice. Online education offers potential flexibility and scalability, yet evidence regarding its effectiveness for developing these competencies remains limited.MethodsThis study employed a quasi-experimental design comprising two sequential phases of online training aimed at bridging theoretical foundations and practical application in oncology nursing navigation. Healthcare professionals across Taiwan participated in Phase One, which consisted of online dialogic seminars focusing on foundational concepts of holistic care. Phase Two involved situation-based simulations and structured reflective discussions to strengthen applied skills. Holistic care competence and therapeutic relationship skills were assessed at baseline (T1), mid-training (T2), and post-training (T3) using validated self-report instruments. Data were analysed using paired t-tests and repeated measures ANOVA.ResultsA total of 337 healthcare professionals completed Phase One, of whom 34 oncology nursing professionals met eligibility criteria and participated in Phase Two. Statistically significant improvements were observed in holistic care competence, with paired t-tests indicating significant gains from T1 to T2 across all dimensions (t = − 9.65 to − 13.00, all p < .001). In Phase Two (n = 34), repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed significant improvement in holistic care competence across T1, T2, and T3 (F(1.50, 49.47) = 14.83, p < .001, η2p = .310, Greenhouse–Geisser corrected). Therapeutic relationship skills also improved significantly (F(1.35, 44.56) = 12.18, p < .001, η2p = .270), with the majority of improvement occurring from T1 to T2 (d = 0.59, p = .002). Effect sizes from T1 to T3 ranged from medium to large across holistic care dimensions (Cohen's d = 0.81–1.07).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that an interactive online training programme incorporating scenario-based learning and reflective discussion was associated with enhanced holistic care competence and therapeutic relationship skills among oncology nursing professionals. Baseline therapeutic relationship skills predicted learning gains across all holistic care dimensions, indicating that relational competence may function as a foundational infrastructure for holistic care learning; accordingly, positioning relational skills training as an early component of oncology nursing curricula may optimise learning outcomes across all dimensions of holistic care. While the results are context-specific, this approach demonstrates potential as a feasible and scalable model for advancing professional education in oncology and palliative care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09800-9.Keywords: Oncology nursing, Patient navigation, Holistic care, Therapeutic relationship, Person-centred care, Online education, Scenario-based simulation

Introduction

Cancer continues to rank as one of the leading causes of mortality in Taiwan, accounting for 28% of all deaths [1, 2]. Over the past decade, oncology care has increasingly adopted a holistic approach. Over the past decade, oncology care has increasingly adopted a holistic approach that addresses not only the physical but also the emotional, social, and spiritual needs of patients. This shift aligns with the WHO’s (2018) emphasis on person-centred, integrated care [3]. In particular, Oncology Nursing Navigation has gained prominence as a coordinated care model that ensures comprehensive support from diagnosis through treatment and palliation [4]. By integrating input from multiple professional teams, oncology nurse navigators facilitate communication, streamline access to services, and help patients and their families navigate a complex healthcare environment [5–7].

Despite recognising holistic care as one of the best-practice standards, several gaps persist in current oncology training programmes. For instance, many existing resources focus more on delivering theoretical lectures than on practical applications, limiting the extent to which emotional competence, scenario-based care, and reflective thinking are meaningfully integrated into practice [8–10]. Furthermore, shortcomings in on-site clinical training—including time constraints, limited interactive sessions, and uneven mentorship—have underscored the need for robust online approaches that can supplement or even replace face-to-face formats when necessary [11, 12].

This study draws on several complementary theoretical frameworks, informed by the concept of emotional intelligence (EI)—the ability to perceive, understand, manage, and use emotions effectively in clinical interactions [13–15]. Carl Rogers' (1957) person-centred approach provides the primary theoretical foundation, positing that empathy, genuineness, and unconditional positive regard are necessary and sufficient conditions for facilitating therapeutic change and growth [16, 17]. The Barrett-Lennard Relationship Inventory (B-LRI), used in this study, directly operationalises these Rogerian constructs as measurable interpersonal competencies [18, 19]. Complementing this relational framework, Kolb’s (1984) experiential learning theory informs the pedagogical design of the intervention: the two-phase structure mirrors the experiential learning cycle of concrete experience (simulation), reflective observation (reflective discussion), abstract conceptualisation (dialogic seminars), and active experimentation (applied practice) [20]. Schön’s (1983) reflection-in-action framework further grounds the reflective discussion component, which encourages professionals to critically examine their clinical reasoning during and after simulated encounters [21]. Finally, the simulation-based components align with the INACSL Standards of Best Practice for simulation-based education, which emphasise facilitated debriefing as a mechanism for transforming experience into learning.

Together, these three frameworks form an integrated conceptual model (Fig. 1). Rogers' person-centred approach provides the relational foundation—the 'why' of therapeutic engagement—by establishing empathy, genuineness, and unconditional positive regard as the necessary conditions for facilitating growth. Kolb's experiential learning cycle provides the pedagogical process—the 'how' of learning—by structuring the intervention as a cycle of concrete experience (simulation), reflective observation (debriefing), abstract conceptualisation (seminars), and active experimentation (applied practice). Schön's reflection-in-action provides the deepening mechanism—the 'when' of insight—by enabling professionals to critically examine their reasoning during and after clinical encounters. This study proposes that relational competence, as operationalised by the B-LRI:mini, functions as a mediating educational mechanism—the relational foundation through which experiential learning and reflective practice become effective vehicles for holistic care competence development.

Conceptual Framework: from relational foundation to holistic care learning

Furthermore, the transition from in-person to online training represents not merely a temporary pandemic response, but a structural transformation in healthcare professional education [11]. The scalability and flexibility of online platforms make them particularly suited for multi-site, interprofessional training in oncology nursing navigation [12, 22–25]. This model carries broader relevance for international oncology nursing education and continuing professional development, as the challenges of integrating holistic care into clinical training are shared across healthcare systems worldwide.

Building on these considerations, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a two-phase online educational intervention designed to enhance holistic care competence and the ability to build a positive therapeutic relationship among oncology nursing professionals. Through a combination of dialogic seminars, situation-based simulations, and structured reflective discussions, the intervention aligns with the realities of multi-site clinical practice and responds to the increasing demand for adaptable, high-impact oncology training [10, 12, 22]. The study specifically examined: (a) changes in holistic care competence and therapeutic relationship skills across training phases; (b) the role of professional experience and profession type in baseline competence and learning gains; and (c) the predictive relationship between therapeutic relationship skills and holistic care learning. In line with our aim to quantitatively assess changes in self-reported competence during the online training, this study employed a quasi-experimental design with repeated measures.

Research methodology

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental, pre-, post- and follow-up design using repeated measures (T1, T2, and T3) to quantitatively assess how an online educational intervention influenced participants’ self-reported holistic care competence and therapeutic relationship skills among oncology nursing professionals in Taiwan. The study comprised two sequential phases: Phase One consisted of online dialogic seminars open to all healthcare professionals, and Phase Two involved situation-based simulations with reflective discussion, restricted to a subset of eligible Phase One completers. The online situation-based simulation, incorporating dialogic practices and reflective discussion in Phase Two, emphasised practical application throughout the learning process.

Participants and recruitment

Healthcare professionals across Taiwan were recruited using purposive sampling for Phase One and snowball sampling for Phase Two. The two phases of participant recruitment are illustrated in the TREND-compliant flow diagram (Fig. 2). In Phase One, potential participants were primarily alerted to the study through digital flyers and email announcements distributed via hospital intranet systems, professional networks, and official nursing association channels. Each announcement provided an overview of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and the voluntary nature of participation. Interested individuals were then directed to an online form containing more detailed information about the study and a digital consent statement. By reviewing this information and proceeding to the pre-training survey, participants indicated that they had given informed consent.

Recruitment and study flow diagram (TREND)

In Phase Two, only individuals who had successfully completed Phase One, were currently working in oncology, and expressed interest in more interactive training were recruited from the pool of Phase One participants via snowball sampling. Phase Two was designed as a small-group, simulation-based workshop requiring an e-facilitator-to-participant ratio of approximately 1:4 (with facilitators working in pairs per group of 7–8 participants), capping workshop capacity at approximately 30–35 participants. They received a targeted email invitation reiterating the study’s aims and the voluntary nature of enrolment before the study. As in Phase One, participants confirmed their informed consent through an online agreement step prior to taking part in the situation-based simulation sessions.

To ensure a consistent understanding of the training content, the inclusion criteria were: 1) Demonstrating proficiency in Mandarin, 2) holding a current registered license in healthcare in Taiwan, and 3) consenting to participate in the study, including attending the online dialogic seminars and the online situation-based simulation with dialogic practices and reflective discussion. Participants were required to complete either the first phase or both phases to be included in the study’s final analysis. Participants who did not provide consent for research use of their data were excluded from analysis, except that all Phase Two participants were treated as consented given their active enrolment in the extended programme.

Educational intervention

The design of this educational intervention (Fig. 2), adopted American psychologist Carl Rogers’ Person-Centred Approach [12, 22, 26], was divided into two phases. Phase One (online dialogic seminars) lasted four hours in total, while Phase Two (situation-based simulations) comprised a single five-hour workshop, during which participants engaged in multiple simulated scenarios. Phase One was conducted once for all eligible participants, whereas Phase Two was also conducted once, but each participant in that phase experienced four distinct simulation scenarios within the single five-hour session.

Firstly, the four-hour online dialogic seminars were given including the topics: Oncology Holistic Healthcare, Patient Navigation: history and rationale, Team Workflow: oncology patient navigation in action, Chang Your Core Beliefs, Change Your Perception for Life, and Discussion. The theories and overviews of navigational care and holistic care were given to provide comprehensive navigational care for oncology patients, such as theories of holistic care for cancer, reasons for the development of navigational care, coverage of care, timely diagnosis and treatment, communication between doctors and patients, provision of information about the disease process, emotional support, shared decision-making and treatment planning, continuity of care, and financial and workplace support.

Secondly, the five-hour workshop conducted in the form of online situation-based simulations and reflective discussion. The situation-based simulations were about patients’ unmet needs. A story of a 38-year-old female with breast cancer was created as the simulated patient who experienced receiving the bad news about breast cancer, facing treatment decisions, and life, emotional, spiritual, and social dilemmas. The story included: 1) Physical dimension (communication and decision making): when a cancer patient is encouraged to make her own treatment options, the doctor-patient shared decision-making management should be performed. 2) Emotional dimension: How can we communicate with a patient right after being confirmed with a diagnosis to help the patient cope with it? 3) Social/supporting dimension: How can we help strengthen patients’ support systems? 4) Spiritual dimension: How to help patients maintain their spiritual health and thrive in life.

Finally, wrapping up with facilitating reflective discussions with e-facilitators would allow participants to learn and demonstrate how they would perform person-centred, holistic care in groups in various given situation-based simulations. For the success of the simulations in groups, e-facilitators, including clinical oncologists, psychologists, social workers, and nurse navigators, facilitated the simulations and reflective discussions. All the e-facilitators, working in pairs, received a 3-h practical training programme one week prior to the simulations to achieve the purpose of training in co-teaching. Every participant was required to complete four situation-based simulations in groups. Each group consisted of 7 or 8 participants.

Instruments

Two validated self-report instruments, both administered in Traditional Chinese (Mandarin), were used to evaluate learning outcomes.

The Holistic Care Competence Inventory (HCCI), developed by Liao (2021), was used to assess the self-reported competence of healthcare professionals to provide proactive care to patients (Supplementary File 1). There are 12 questions, including four subscales: physical, emotional, spiritual, and social dimensions. Unlike a purely knowledge-based questionnaire, the HCCI encompasses four distinct domains that collectively reflect perceived holistic care competence. Each statement is scored on a rating scale of one to six. One means “not available or not very available”, and six means “always available”. The higher the score, the better the self-reported active caregiving ability. The scale demonstrates high internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.954, an ICC (intraclass correlation coefficient) of 0.955, and an exploratory factor analysis KMO (Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin) value of 0.947 [27]. The high inter-subdomain correlations (r = 0.777–0.903) suggest substantial overlap among the four subscales, which is discussed as a limitation below.

The Barrett-Lennard Relationship Inventory mini version (B-LRI:mini), developed by Barrett-Lennard (1981) [18], was adopted and adapted into a Chinese version by Chen et al. (2022) [28]. It is designed to assess the establishment of therapeutic relationships between health professionals and patients. The B-LRI:mini operationalises Rogers’ core therapeutic conditions—empathy, congruence (genuineness), unconditional positive regard, and level of regard—as measurable interpersonal competencies. There are 12 questions, including positive and negative ones, with a six-point scale ranging from − 3 to + 3. Item U7 is reverse-scored prior to summation. The total score ranges from − 36 to 36. A score of 25 means that the therapeutic relationship is excellent, 19–24 means that the therapeutic relationship is good, 13–18 means that the therapeutic relationship is acceptable but needs to be strengthened, and ≤ 12 means that the therapeutic relationship is poor [18, 29]. The scale Cronbach’s α value was 0.839 in this sample, retest reliability r = 0.87, and confirmatory factor analysis CFI = 0.952, χ2 = 388.12, RMSEA = 0.051 [19, 29].

Data collection

Participants in both phases completed the demographic information, HCCI, and B-LRI:mini pre-test (T1) before the educational intervention. Both instruments were then administered again at the end of Phase One (T2). Participants who met the Phase Two eligibility criteria and agreed to participate were assigned into four groups for the online situation-based simulations, followed by reflective discussion. The HCCI and B-LRI:mini were administered again at the end of Phase Two (T3).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (SciPy 1.x) with verification against SPSS 22.0. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and percentage) were used to characterise the sample and scale scores. For Phase One (N = 337), paired t-tests were used to evaluate changes between T1 and T2. For Phase Two (n = 34), repeated-measures one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate changes across T1, T2, and T3.

Prior to conducting inferential tests, statistical assumptions were examined. Normality of difference scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; most variables met the normality assumption (p > 0.05), though some T2–T1 difference scores for the emotional and spiritual dimensions showed mild departures from normality. Sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test; as all variables violated the sphericity assumption (all p < 0.05), Greenhouse–Geisser corrected F-values and degrees of freedom are reported for all repeated-measures analyses. Homogeneity of variance between Phase One-only and Phase Two participants at baseline was confirmed using Levene’s test (all p > 0.23).

Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d, calculated using the pooled standard deviation of pre- and post-scores, with positive values indicating improvement (post > pre). Ninety-five percent confidence intervals are reported for all effect sizes. Post-hoc power analysis was conducted because the Phase Two sample size (n = 34) was determined by workshop capacity constraints rather than a priori power calculations, following recommendations for quasi-experimental educational research where sample sizes are often dictated by practical rather than statistical considerations. Achieved power exceeded 0.98 for all variables, indicating sufficient statistical power despite the small sample. To examine selection bias, independent-samples t-tests were used to compare baseline scores between Phase One-only and Phase Two participants. Regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of learning gains, with baseline scores, years of experience, and profession type as predictors.

Results

Demography and characteristics of participants

A total of 337 participants completed Phase One, which consisted of online dialogic seminars (Table 1). Of these, 34 met the additional eligibility criteria for Phase Two—namely, (1) active employment in an oncology setting, (2) completion of Phase One, and (3) a stated interest in more interactive training. Phase Two’s workshop capacity was capped at approximately 30–35 participants due to the e-facilitator-to-participant ratio required for simulation-based learning. No participants who began Phase Two dropped out; all 34 completed the situation-based simulation and reflective discussion in its entirety.

Scores and correlations in phase one

All participants who remained in the study—337 in Phase One and 34 in Phase Two—completed the HCCI and B-LRI:mini as self-report measures at each applicable time point. Although self-report data might also be subject to social desirability bias or inaccuracies in self-assessment, the self-report format captured perceived competence and reflections on holistic care and therapeutic relationships.

At T1, holistic care competence displayed a total mean score of 57.54 (SD = 9.93). The subscales for the physical, emotional, spiritual, and social dimensions were 13.80 (SD = 2.94), 14.71 (SD = 2.52), 14.49 (SD = 2.51), and 14.53 (SD = 2.60), respectively. Therapeutic relationship skills showed a mean score of 15.47 (SD = 8.05). Given the high inter-subdomain correlations (r = 0.777–0.903; Table 2), these subdimension scores should be interpreted with caution, as the HCCI may be capturing a unified construct of general holistic care competence rather than four empirically distinct domains in this population.

Effect sizes (Table 6; Fig. 3) indicated medium to large improvements from T1 to T3 across all holistic care dimensions, with the largest effect observed for physical care (d = 1.07, 95% CI [0.65, 1.49]) and the smallest for spiritual support (d = 0.82, 95% CI [0.43, 1.20]). All effect sizes were positive, indicating improvement across all dimensions. Therapeutic relationship skills showed a medium effect from T1 to T3 (d = 0.64, 95% CI [0.27, 1.01]), with the majority of improvement occurring between T1 and T2 (d = 0.59, p = 0.002); the T2-to-T3 change was not statistically significant (d = 0.07, p = 0.405). In practical terms, the T1-to-T3 improvements represent a shift from moderate to high holistic care competence, with participants' mean scores approaching the upper range of the scale across all dimensions by T3. As noted above, the high inter-subdomain correlations suggest these dimension-specific effect sizes may reflect variation within a single underlying construct rather than independent domain-specific changes.

To examine potential selection bias, baseline HCCI and B-LRI:mini scores were compared between Phase One-only participants (n = 303) and Phase Two participants (n = 34). No significant differences were found on any measure (all p > 0.57, all d < 0.11), suggesting that the Phase Two subsample was not substantially different from the larger cohort at entry. Demographic comparison indicated that Phase Two participants had a slightly higher proportion of oncology case managers (35.3%) compared with Phase One-only participants (7.9%), consistent with the eligibility requirement of active oncology employment. No significant differences were observed in years of experience distribution between the two groups.

Regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of learning gains (T2–T1) for each holistic care dimension. Across all four dimensions, baseline competence scores were the strongest predictor (B = − 0.624 to − 0.671, all p < 0.0001), explaining approximately 40–43% of variance in learning gains. Baseline therapeutic relationship skills (B-LRI:mini) also significantly predicted gains across all dimensions (B = 0.037–0.043, all p < 0.01). Years of experience and profession type were non-significant after controlling for baseline (all p > 0.39).

Discussion

Baseline holistic care competence and the role of professional experience

Baseline holistic care competence was moderate, whereas therapeutic relationship skills required strengthening (Tables 3 and 4). Both measures differed significantly by years of experience, with the > 20-year group scoring highest. These findings are consistent with previous evidence on the role of professional maturity in clinical competence development.

However, a critical distinction emerged between baseline competence levels and learning gains. Although senior professionals started higher, their learning gains were not significantly greater—in fact, the pattern was reversed. Regression analyses confirmed that baseline competence—not years of experience—was the strongest predictor of learning gains across all dimensions (R2 = 0.40–0.43). This indicates that the apparent experience-related differences in learning were attributable to a ceiling effect: professionals who already scored near the scale maximum had limited room for measurable improvement [30, 31]. Among participants with more than 20 years of experience, 33% scored ≥ 17 out of 18 on the spiritual dimension at baseline, and 37% on the social dimension, constraining the magnitude of observable gains.

These findings underscore that experienced healthcare professionals still demonstrated significant improvement (all p < 0.05), aligning with Hojat et al.'s (2003) emphasis on continuous professional development [32–34]. The present findings extend this argument by demonstrating that structured online training benefits even those with substantial prior competence, though ceiling effects may attenuate measurable gains.

Therapeutic relationship competence as a catalyst for holistic care learning

The most significant finding of this study was that baseline therapeutic relationship skills (B-LRI:mini) predicted learning gains across all four dimensions of holistic care competence (B = 0.037–0.043, all p < 0.01), even after controlling for baseline HCCI scores and professional experience. This finding positions relational competence not as a secondary outcome, but as the foundational infrastructure upon which holistic care learning is built (Fig. 1).

This finding is theoretically consistent with Rogers’ (1957) person-centred framework, which posits that empathy, genuineness, and unconditional positive regard are the primary mechanisms through which therapeutic change and learning occur. The present study extends Rogers' framework from its traditional application in therapeutic settings to the domain of professional education, demonstrating that the same relational capacities that facilitate patient healing also facilitate professional learning. This represents a conceptual shift from 'content delivery' models of education to 'relationally enabled learning systems'. When healthcare professionals possess stronger relational competence, they may be more attuned to patients’ multidimensional needs—including emotional, spiritual, and social dimensions—enabling them to translate theoretical foundations into practice more effectively. Recent empirical evidence further supports this interpretation: Li et al. (2025) proposed an “empathy–emotional connection–spiritual care” action model, demonstrating that nurses with higher empathy more accurately identify patients’ spiritual needs [35]. Similarly, Polat and Özdemir (2022) found that compassion explained 31% of the variance in oncology nurses’ spiritual care provision [36]. These converging findings suggest that relational qualities may function as a meta-competence—a capacity that enables the acquisition and application of domain-specific holistic care skills.

These findings carry practical implications for the design of holistic care training programmes: strengthening therapeutic relationship skills may serve not merely as a discrete educational objective, but as an enabling condition that amplifies learning across all dimensions of holistic care. This perspective is consistent with the theoretical underpinnings of the B-LRI [18, 28]. This differential role of relational competence is further evidenced by the contrasting trajectories of the two outcome measures across training phases. Holistic care competence (HCCI) showed cumulative improvement across both phases (Table 6; Fig. 3A), consistent with the interpretation that the scenario-based simulation in Phase Two provided additional value beyond the dialogic seminars. By contrast, therapeutic relationship skills (B-LRI:mini) improved only during Phase One, with no additional gains following Phase Two (Fig. 3B). This pattern suggests that relational competence may develop more readily through conceptual and dialogic engagement than through simulation-based practice, or alternatively, that a ceiling was reached after Phase One as scores already exceeded the 'good' threshold [19–25] on the B-LRI:mini. Reflective discussions, widely recognised as a mechanism for deepening clinical reasoning [37], were embedded in Phase Two; however, the study design does not permit attribution of specific outcomes to individual training components. Future training designs might consider embedding advanced relational skills exercises within the simulation phase to sustain relational growth beyond foundational gains.

The spiritual dimension: experiential accumulation and ceiling effects

Among the four dimensions of holistic care, the spiritual dimension exhibited the most pronounced experience-related gradient in learning gains. Professionals with more than 20 years of experience demonstrated significantly lower spiritual gains compared with less experienced colleagues (d = − 0.23, p = 0.044), despite showing significant improvement from baseline. By contrast, the social dimension showed the weakest experience gradient (Spearman r = − 0.051, p = 0.349), and the emotional and physical dimensions fell between these extremes.

These differential patterns may reflect distinct developmental trajectories. Spiritual care competence—encompassing the ability to recognise and respond to patients’ existential concerns, self-image, and meaning-making—appears to be the dimension most deeply internalised through cumulative clinical experience. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that prolonged exposure to patients’ suffering and end-of-life concerns fosters intuitive spiritual sensitivity [38, 39]. In the Taiwanese oncology context, Hsieh et al. (2020) similarly identified clinical experience as a significant factor associated with spiritual care competence [40–42].

Conversely, social care competence appeared more closely tied to specific professional training, as evidenced by social workers scoring highest at baseline (16.00/18) regardless of years of experience. The implications of these differential trajectories for training design are discussed in the Implications section below.

Participants who completed both phases showed progressively greater improvements in holistic care competence. Holistic care competence showed cumulative improvement across both phases (Table 6; Fig. 3). Notably, therapeutic relationship skills improved only during Phase One, with no additional gains following Phase Two (Fig. 3B). This may reflect the differing foci of the two phases: Phase One directly addressed relational foundations, whereas Phase Two focused on applied holistic care in specific clinical scenarios. Alternatively, a ceiling effect may have contributed, as T2 scores already exceeded the ‘good’ threshold [18, 19, 26–29]. Reflective discussions, widely recognised as a mechanism for deepening clinical reasoning [37], were embedded in Phase Two; however, the study design does not permit attribution of specific outcomes to individual training components.

Strengths and limitations

One notable strength of this study was its two-phase design, which sequentially integrated online seminars with situation-based simulation and reflective practice. However, several limitations must be acknowledged.

Methodological and validaity constraints

Firstly, the study employed a single-group, quasi-experimental approach without a control group, limiting the ability to establish causal inferences. A controlled design was unfeasible because the training programme was implemented as a service-level educational initiative within the national oncology nursing network. A controlled design was unfeasible because the training programme was implemented as a service-level educational initiative. Consequently, threats to internal validity cannot be entirely ruled out. Specifically, maturation effects may have contributed to observed improvements through ongoing clinical practice, while testing effects from repeated administration of the instruments at three time points may have inflated subsequent scores. Additionally, social desirability bias may have influenced self-reported competence, given that participants may have felt motivated to demonstrate growth within a professional development framework.

Sampling and generalisability

Secondly, the sample size decreased from N = 337 to n = 34 in Phase Two due to institutional workshop capacity constraints. Phase Two participants were recruited via snowball sampling, introducing potential self-selection bias. Although baseline comparisons revealed no significant differences in measured competence scores between the two phases, unmeasured characteristics—such as intrinsic motivation, prior exposure to simulation, or institutional support—may have differed. Furthermore, volunteer bias may have contributed to higher baseline motivation among the study sample compared with the broader oncology nursing population, as participation was entirely voluntary. Future studies should consider reporting demographic and attitudinal characteristics of eligible non-participants to better contextualise attrition patterns. Participants voluntarily enrolling in the intensive simulation phase may have been more receptive to experiential learning, potentially inflating the observed effect sizes and limiting the generalisability of the Phase Two findings to the broader oncology nursing population. This generalisability is further constrained by the specific context of oncology care in Taiwan and cultural specificity. Taiwanese healthcare culture, characterised by collectivist values and hierarchical professional structures, may shape both baseline competence levels and responsiveness to training in ways that differ from Western or other Asian contexts. The HCCI and B-LRI:mini, while validated in Chinese, were originally developed within Western theoretical frameworks, and their construct validity across diverse cultural settings warrants further examination.

Measurement and psychometric psychometric properties

Thirdly, the reliance on self-report measures introduces common-method variance and social desirability bias, as self-reported competence may not fully translate into actual behavioural change [43]. Psychometrically, given the high inter-subdomain correlations (r = 0.777–0.903), the Holistic Care Competence Instrument (HCCI) may capture a single latent construct of general holistic care competence rather than four empirically distinct domains in this population. While the four-dimensional framework (physical, emotional, spiritual, and social) remains a useful heuristic for educational design, the present data cannot confirm that these subdimensions are statistically separable. Furthermore, ceiling effects were observed among highly experienced professionals (up to 37% at or near the scale maximum at baseline), potentially leading to an underestimation of the intervention’s effectiveness for this subgroup [30].

Recommendations for future research

To address these limitations, future research should adopt randomised controlled designs, waitlist-control designs, stepped-wedge cluster randomisation across hospital sites, or matched-comparison designs using propensity score matching, where ethically permissible, thereby strengthening causal inferences. Methodologically, it is crucial to incorporate objective outcome measures—such as standardised patient assessments or clinical observation checklists—to supplement self-reported data and better evaluate actual behavioural changes. Furthermore, longitudinal designs incorporating delayed post-tests at 6 and 12 months are warranted to assess the long-term sustainability of the training effects beyond the immediate intervention period. Finally, future psychometric evaluations should employ confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) paired with strict discriminant validity testing, such as the Fornell-Larcker criterion and the HTMT ratio, to rigorously verify whether the subdimensions of the HCCI represent distinct constructs among Taiwanese healthcare professionals.

Implications

Curriculum design

The finding that baseline therapeutic relationship skills predicted learning gains across all holistic care dimensions suggests that relational competence training should be positioned as a foundational prerequisite in holistic care curricula, sequenced before content-specific modules. Training programmes may benefit from beginning with relational skills development—including empathy training, person-centred communication, and reflective listening—before introducing domain-specific content in physical, emotional, spiritual, and social care.

Training delivery

The differential ceiling effects across experience levels indicate that a one-size-fits-all approach may not optimally serve all learners. For less experienced professionals, foundational scenario-based training demonstrates strong effectiveness. For experienced professionals who have internalised substantial holistic care competence, advanced pedagogical approaches may be more effective: specialised masterclasses focusing on complex ethical dilemmas in oncology care, peer-led clinical supervision sessions, structured contemplative practices for spiritual self-awareness, and interprofessional mentorship programmes pairing experienced navigators with colleagues from social work, psychology, and chaplaincy.

Institutional policy

The demonstrated feasibility and effectiveness of the online training model suggest opportunities for integration into institutional and national frameworks. Oncology nurse navigation certification programmes could incorporate relational competence assessment (e.g., B-LRI:mini) as a standard component. National continuing professional development (CPD) frameworks could adopt scalable online simulation modules. At the institutional level, hospitals could use baseline relational competence assessment to identify professionals who may benefit from targeted support prior to entering holistic care training. Based on the present findings, we propose that future oncology nurse navigation competency frameworks incorporate three tiers: (1) foundational relational competence, assessed via instruments such as the B-LRI:mini; (2) domain-specific holistic care competence across physical, emotional, spiritual, and social dimensions; and (3) advanced integrative competence developed through supervised clinical practice and peer-led reflection. Such a tiered framework could guide curriculum development, credentialing standards, and continuing professional development requirements at both institutional and national levels.

Future research should investigate sustained follow-up to assess long-term effects and consider implementing similar models in diverse healthcare settings. Adopting control or comparison groups in subsequent studies would provide more robust evidence regarding effectiveness.

Conclusions

The findings of this quasi-experimental study indicated that an interactive online educational intervention was associated with meaningful improvements in both self-reported holistic care competence and therapeutic relationship skills among oncology nursing professionals. The two-phase design—combining dialogic seminars with scenario-based simulation and reflective discussion—demonstrated cumulative benefits for holistic care competence, with medium to large effect sizes (d = 0.81–1.07) across the physical, emotional, spiritual, and social dimensions. Therapeutic relationship skills improved primarily during Phase One (d = 0.59), with no additional significant change following Phase Two.

Several findings carry theoretical and practical significance. Firstly, baseline therapeutic relationship skills emerged as a significant predictor of learning gains across all holistic care dimensions, suggesting that relational competence may function as a foundational enabler—or ‘infrastructure’—for holistic care learning. This is consistent with Rogers’ (1957) person-centred theory and recent empirical evidence linking empathy to spiritual care competence [35]. Secondly, the spiritual dimension showed the most pronounced ceiling effect among experienced professionals, suggesting that spiritual care competence is deeply internalised through cumulative clinical practice and may require advanced-level training to further develop. Lastly, the intervention was effective across all experience levels, though ceiling effects constrained measurable gains among senior professionals.

These findings suggest that well-structured online training in oncology nursing navigation can enhance participants’ self-reported holistic care competence, and that strengthening therapeutic relationship skills may amplify learning across all holistic care dimensions. The identification of relational competence as a foundational infrastructure for holistic care learning represents the primary theoretical contribution of this study, extending Rogers' (1957) person-centred framework from therapeutic settings to professional education. Future training designs may benefit from differentiated curriculum tracks, advanced relational and reflective modules for senior professionals, and instruments with wider scoring ranges to more sensitively capture competence changes. Importantly, the present findings reflect changes in self-reported perceived competence, which may not directly correspond to changes in clinical behaviour at the bedside. Whether the observed improvements translate into measurable changes in patient care quality and patient-reported outcomes remains a critical question for future longitudinal research incorporating objective performance measures. Caution is advised in generalising these results beyond similar professional settings, and future studies incorporating control groups, objective outcome measures, and longer-term follow-up are recommended to establish more definitive evidence of effectiveness.
