Changes in publicly insured general practitioner consultations before and after workplace injury: interrupted time series by consultation duration and care plans in Australia
Authors: Preeti Maharjan, Michael Di Donato, Daniel Griffiths, Danielle Mazza, Alex Collie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral practitioners (GPs) are central to the treatment and management of injured workers. Australian workers’ compensation funds GP consultations for work-related injuries. Almost all other GP consultations are subsidised by public healthcare insurance (Medicare Benefit Scheme). This study aimed to examine changes in publicly-insured GP consultations before and after injury for injured workers with long-duration claims, focusing on consultation duration types and GP-provided mental health and chronic diseases management plans.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study using interrupted time series analysis included 3,755 injured workers and 10,113 community individuals with injury/index date between 2006 and 2016. We compared monthly changes in rates (per 1000 workers) of six GP consultation types for 12-month before and 24-months after injury. Acute and long-term changes were examined both overall (combining all consultation types) and separately by consultation type. Consultation types include four duration-based levels: A (0–6 min), B (6–20 min), C (20–40 min), and D (40–60 min), and two types describing mental health and chronic disease management plans.ResultsAfter injury, injured workers received an additional 1,600 Medicare-subsidised GP consultations per 1,000 workers per year compared to the community group. Post-injury, the largest increase in monthly consultation rates was observed for Level D consultations (IRR: 2.37, 95% CI: 1.35, 4.20), while the smallest increment was observed for Level B consultations (IRR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.32, 1.59). Monthly GP mental health care and chronic disease development plan rates increased by 84% (IRR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.93) and 20% (IRR: 1.20, 95% CI: 0.78, 1.86) post injury, respectively; however, the monthly rates of these consultations were small (maximum 25/1000 workers) Following these initial increases immediately post-injury, monthly consultation rates decreased by 3% to 9% and by 24-months post-injury had returned to levels observed in the comparison group.ConclusionAll six types of publicly insured GP consultations increased temporarily in the initial months following injury, regardless of worker’s compensation status. These consultations remained elevated above pre-injury levels for over 12 months, likely reflecting delayed claim acceptance or increased care needs. These findings highlight the need of a comprehensive, whole-person approach to recovery that addresses both immediate and broader healthcare needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03477-1.Keywords: General practitioner consultation, Physician visits, Workers’ compensation, Healthcare funding, Workplace injury, Health service use

Introduction

Work-related injury increases healthcare needs, including general practitioner (GP) care. In Australia, GP consultations are typically covered through the Medicare Benefits Scheme (Medicare), a publicly-funded universal healthcare insurance scheme [1]. However, if a person is injured at work, GP consultations for injury are funded through employer-funded workers’ compensation schemes [2]. Beyond medical treatment and care coordination, GPs have administrative and regulatory responsibilities within the workers’ compensation scheme. These include monitoring progress, assessing work capacity, and coordinating with key system stakeholders such as employers and insurers [3, 4]. During their compensation claim, injured workers may also seek GP care funded by Medicare for other, non-compensable health concerns. These may include pre-existing conditions, new health issues, or complications of their injury. Thus, injured workers can be required to navigate a complex healthcare landscape, accessing GP consultations simultaneously through two distinct funding systems [2, 5, 6]. This dual healthcare funding structure is not unique to Australia; countries such as Canada, the United States, and South Korea also operate workers’ compensation schemes that fund work-related healthcare separately from their mainstream universal health insurance systems [7–9]. Our previous study found that workers use Medicare-subsidised GP consultations at a similar annual rate before and after a workplace injury, in addition to a substantial increase in GP consultations post-injury funded by workers’ compensation [10]. However, we did not examine whether the monthly rate of Medicare-subsidised consultations changed post-injury, nor whether the length or type of consultations differed.

A concurrent work-related injury may influence the use of Medicare-subsidised GP consultations in several ways. Physical limitations and stress from injury may exacerbate pre-existing chronic conditions or contribute to the development of new chronic conditions, requiring more intensive management by GPs [11, 12]. Additionally, workplace injuries may lead to mental health issues such as stress, anxiety, and depression, further increasing the need for mental health services [13, 14]. Managing both work-related and non-work-related conditions may require longer consultations, as GPs address multiple health concerns within a single visit. This is further influenced by the central role of GPs in Australia as coordinators of healthcare, where they prepare and review management plans and make referrals to specialists for complex conditions [4, 15]. This multifaceted role is particularly important when managing workplace injuries that involve complex conditions such as mental health and chronic disease [4]. There is limited evidence on how the characteristics of Medicare-subsidised GP consultations, such as consultation durations, mental health and chronic disease management, change following a work-related injury. Existing studies primarily focused on compensation-funded GP consultations, which are restricted to consultations deemed reasonably necessary for managing work-related injury, thereby overlooking the Medicare-subsidised services that workers access concurrently [16, 17]. Understanding this gap is important because it reveals injured workers’ needs beyond compensation coverage, the additional burden placed on GPs, and whether the compensation system’s restricted coverage is displacing costs onto Medicare. Workers with long-duration claims often have persistent healthcare needs [18] and engage with both healthcare funding systems. Thus, this study examined six types of GP consultations based on consultation duration (Level A: 0–6 min, Level B: 6–20 min, Level C: 20–40 min, and Level D: 40–60 min) and care type (mental health care and chronic disease management plans) among injured workers receiving income support for at least 2 years. Using a linked dataset, we conducted an Interrupted time series (ITS) analysis to examine how work-related injury claims impact the duration and type of Medicare-subsidised GP consultations, before and after injury, focusing on both acute and long-term changes.

Method

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study using the Transition study dataset which linked workers’ compensation claims data from the Australian state of New South Wales (NSW) with their Medicare records.

Study setting

Australia has a publicly funded national health insurance scheme, the Medicare Benefits Scheme, that provides subsidised (and in some cases, free) healthcare, including GP care, to all Australians [1]. In 2022/23, there were 166 million Medicare-subsidised GP consultations costing AUD $8.7 billion [15, 19]. To support those injured at work, there are 11 workers’ compensation schemes operated by state, territory, and federal governments, covering 94% of workers [20]. Workers’ compensation schemes cover reasonable and necessary GP care for compensable conditions in injured workers with accepted workers’ compensation claims [2]. This study used workers’ compensation claims data from the state of New South Wales (NSW), which had an estimated residential population of 8.2 million in 2023 [21]. In 2022-23, there were approximately 111,000 accepted workers’ compensation claims in NSW [22].

Study sample

This study analysed data from the Transitions study, which examined the health service use of workers with long-term workers’ compensation claims [23]. The original sample included three groups: (i) injured workers whose income support ceased due to the 260-week legislative limit, (ii) injured workers who received income support for at least 2 years but whose claims ended independent of 260-week rule, (iii) community individuals without a compensation claim matched on age, gender and residential area to the first group.

For this study, the two injured workers groups were combined to form a single cohort of injured workers who received income support for at least 104 weeks (i.e. 2 years) and the original community control group was retained. Participants were included if their recorded injury date or matched indexed date were between 01 July 2006 and 30 June 2016. Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 or older than 65 years at the time of injury or index date.Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in supplementary Fig. 1.

Pre- and post- interruption period

For each participant, the data inception period spans 12 months before and 24 months after injury, with the injury date (day 0) set as an index date. The index date for injured workers was recorded from workers’ compensation claims data. For the community comparison group, which does not have an injury date, an index date was created to define the data inception period. The index date was assigned using a repeated matching process without replacement. Each community comparison participant was matched with an injured worker with the closest birth year. Once the match was found, the injured workers’ injury date was assigned as an index date to the comparator cohort.

GP consultations eligibility

GP consultations during the 12-month period prior to the index date, and 24 months post-the index date were included, to assess the long-term impact of an injury on GP consultations compared to pre-injury levels. Six mutually exclusive consultation types, categorised by duration and care type, were included as described in Table 1 below. These consist of four consultation types (Level A, B, C, and D) based on consultation duration, and two consultation types based on care type: mental health and chronic disease management plans. These consultation types were selected based on the available data on GP consultations from Medicare records and grouped according to the details provided in MBS [24] as listed in Table 1 below. A detailed list of MBS codes used for grouping consultations is available in supplementary Table 1. If an individual had records of multiple GP consultations of the same category on a single day, only one GP consultation was included. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for GP consultations are outlined in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Outcomes

Outcomes were the monthly rates of Level A, Level B, Level C, Level D consultations, and chronic disease management plans, and mental health plans per 1000 persons.

Analytical approach

Descriptive analyses were conducted for the pre-index and post-index periods for each outcome, using both mean with standard deviation (sd) and the median with interquartile range (IQR) consultations per 1000 workers.

Interrupted time series (ITS) analyses were conducted to assess both level and trend changes in GP consultations following injury. Separate negative binomial regression models were used for each outcome, comparing pre-injury with post-injury periods. Plotted results showed non-linear patterns post-injury, so we added a squared post-interruption time term to adjust the model. Negative binomial regression was preferred due to the overdispersion of the GP consultation rate data. The distribution of monthly GP consultation rates was assessed for overdispersion using a regression-based test (graph plot of count data to check the overdispersion) with a DHARMa package in R [25]. The models were adjusted for autocorrelation. For each analysis, all possible models were compared on Akaike Information Criterion values. Seasonality adjustments were not applied as the injury dates (intervention for ITS) were evenly distributed across different months and years between 2006 and 2016. Analyses were conducted in R using RStudio, with the MASS [26] and nlme packages [27]. The fitted model incorporated baseline level and trend, immediate level change after injury, and post-injury consultation trend. The fitted model for each GP consultation type was plotted, and a counterfactual line was included to illustrate the expected trend if the event had not occurred. The community comparison group was included to control for secular trends unrelated to injury. Separate analyses were conducted for injured workers and community controls across each of the six GP types and were combined into a single graph for visual interpretation, but do not represent a combined model.

Results

Changes in overall GP consultations before and after work-related injury

A cohort of 3,755 injured workers and 10,113 individuals from the community comparison groups used a total of 94,345 and 242,200 GP consultations, respectively, within the study period. Among injured workers, 26,118 consultations occurred pre-injury, while 68,227 were recorded post-injury. Similarly, within the community comparator group, 77,070 consultations occurred pre-index date (12 months before), and 165,121 consultations were post-index (i.e., 24 months after). Compared to the pre-injury period, injured workers had a 30.6% increase in GP consultations for all consultation types combined, and the community comparator had a 7.1% increase, indicating that there was a 23.5% increase in injured workers’ GP visits potentially attributable to the work-related injury. This corresponds to an additional 1600 GP consultations per 1000 injured workers annually. The monthly rate of all combined GP consultations immediately increased by 50%, followed by a 4% monthly decrease in slope for the injured workers compared to the counterfactual (Fig. 1). While the volume of consultations increased post-injury for injured workers, the proportional composition of all six consultation types remained similar compared to the pre-injury level (supplementary Table 2).

Changes in the duration of GP consultations before and after work-related injury

Among the four duration-based GP consultation types, Level B consultations (6 to 20 min) accounted for more than 80% of total consultations for both injured workers and the community comparison group. While the proportion remained the same, the volume of these consultation types increased significantly post-injury for injured workers compared to the community comparator. The mean(sd) monthly rate of Level B consultations increased from 477.6(20.5) in the pre-injury period to 608.8(59.1) post-injury. Similarly, the mean number of Levels A, C, and D also increased after injury compared to the pre-injury level (Table 2). Changes in GP-provided mental health care and chronic disease management plan before and after work-related injury

Mental health care and chronic disease management plans accounted for 1.4% and 2.0% of all consultations before the injury and 2.4% and 2.6% post-injury. The monthly mean(sd) rates of GP-provided mental health management plans increased from 8.1(2.6) at pre-injury to 19.9(2.7) post-injury (Table 2). The monthly rate of GP consultations for mental health management plans exhibited an 84% (IRR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.93) immediate increase following a work-related injury. Similarly, the monthly mean (sd) rates of chronic disease management plan increased from and 11.6(2.1) pre-injury to 18.2(3.0) post- injury. The monthly rate of the chronic disease management plan showed a smaller non-significant increment of 20% (IRR: 1.20, 95% CI: 0.78, 1.86) immediately following the workplace injury. The post-interruption trend slightly declined by 3% to 4% (Table 3). In the community comparator group, GP- provided mental health and chronic disease management plans increased gradually following the index date compared with the period before the index date (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study examined changes in Medicare-subsidised GP consultations by injured workers with long-duration workers’ compensation claims before and after a work-related injury. Monthly rates of GP consultations increased significantly during the initial months following workplace injury, and this increment was greater than the pattern observed in the community comparison group. While workers’ compensation systems cover reasonably necessary GP care for work-related conditions, this study demonstrates that following workplace injury, workers also used Medicare-subsidised GP consultations at a level above pre-injury. This increase in GP consultations is beyond that we reported in our prior study, which described a 3.6-fold rise in GP consultations during the first-year post-injury when workers’ compensation-funded consultations are also included. This increase might be explained by the delays in claim processing and acceptance, which means workers must access GP consultations through the Medicare scheme. The injury or claim may also lead to either new onset health conditions or an exacerbation of existing health conditions, increasing healthcare needs. Importantly, the observed increment in Medicare-subsidised GP consultations post-injury suggests a cost-shifting dynamic, whereby costs arising from workplace injury are borne not solely by workers’ compensation schemes but also by universal healthcare systems. This aligns with the emerging evidence demonstrating that injured workers rely on other healthcare funding following injury, where claims are disputed, delayed or coverage gaps restrict the care access [28, 29]. This pattern suggests a structural feature of the dual healthcare funding interface, with implications for how healthcare services are utilised and funded across systems.

The rapid rise in GP consultations during the initial months after injury may be due to several factors. The process of applying for workers’ compensation can take several weeks following injury, during which workers have acute healthcare needs. For example, in NSW, workers have up to 6 months from the injury date to lodge a claim, and insurers may take up to 21 days to determine liability [30]. Our findings of a large acute increase in GP consultations post-injury suggest that at least some of this very early post-injury healthcare is being subsidised by Medicare. In addition to seeking treatment and management of work-related injury, injured workers may also need to visit a GP to gather necessary documents in order to apply for workers’ compensation [30]. The significant increase in longer consultations may reflect the need to address multiple health concerns, including chronic conditions, workplace injuries, and the coordination of care across multidisciplinary teams. The use of mental health and chronic disease development plans rose by 84% and 20%, respectively, reflecting the wider impact of workplace injury on the workers’ overall well-being. The significant rise in mental health care plans may reflect both new onset mental health conditions that are the reason for the workers’ compensation claim, or the development of mental health issues subsequent to a physical workplace injury [13, 14, 31]. In either case, the GP mental health plan plays a key role in facilitating access to Medicare-funded other healthcare services, such as psychologists, that a GP may consider beneficial for injured workers [32]. Though the immediate increase in chronic disease management plans was smaller, it indicates that some workers required additional care for their pre-existing health conditions or the injury [12, 33].

After the initial post-injury surge in consultations, the monthly rate of GP consultations remained above the counterfactual but gradually reduced over time. The decline in longer consultations may indicate a shift from acute care to routine monitoring and recovery, as the immediate healthcare needs decreased, or a shift in billing from Medicare-subsidised to workers’ compensation funded care. The continued use of chronic disease management plans is likely due to continuous care needs for pre-existing conditions or the development of new ones [12, 34]. Similarly, the sustained need for mental health care suggests long-term emotional or psychological challenges triggered or worsened by injury [13, 35]. However, the number of these consultations was relatively small, and they represent the services provided by GPs only. Injured workers may be receiving care from healthcare providers other than GP which are not included in this study. GP consultations may also be influenced by workers’ compensation policies and administrative burden. Workers’ compensation typically covers only reasonable and necessary GP consultations for accepted claims as approved or requested by the insurer [2]. Those consultations that fall outside these criteria may have been billed under Medicare, though they are related to workplace injury. GPs may be reluctant to treat compensable injuries due to the significant time and financial burdens, clinical complexities, and administrative demands involved in managing long-term injuries [36, 37]. Payment delays, insufficient reimbursement, combined with these burdens, often discourage GPs from engaging in workers’ compensation cases [36, 37]. GPs and injured workers dealing with the complex claims process may experience emotional exhaustion [14, 37, 38]. Where there is a choice, GP consultations may be more likely to be billed under Medicare due to fewer administrative demands.

The novel linkage of the workers’ compensation with their Medicare records enabled us to examine the Medicare-subsidised GP care potentially attributed to work-related injuries. The inclusion of pre-injury baseline data and a community comparison group strengthens the reliability of the study. The study benefits from a large sample size of 3,755 injured workers with long-duration claims, covering injury dates between 2006 and 2016. However, there are some limitations to consider while generalising the findings. The study included data from New South Wales only, which may limit the broader applicability of the findings due to differences in policies across various workers’ compensation jurisdictions that impact GP consultations [16, 20]. MBS codes such as 2700, 2701, 2715, and 2717 related to mental health care, and 2126, 2143, and 2195 related to chronic disease development plans were introduced in 2011. Over these study periods, the number of consultations provided under the new mental health codes ranged from 43 to 117 consultations, while the chronic disease codes summed up to a total of 13 consultations. The number of consultations was minimal, thus, the consultations captured under these codes were included. As with all interrupted time series studies, confounding from co-occurring changes in the primary care policy of Medicare rebate structures during the period cannot be fully excluded. It is important to consider that the included injury dates are between 2006 and 2016, which may not reflect current trends and practices. Furthermore, the study included injured workers who received income support for at least two years, which represents a small and atypical subset of the broader injured workers population, thus findings may not be generalisable to all injured workers. However, this group warrants particular attention as they represent a disproportionate burden on the healthcare system due to their persistent healthcare needs. As the study used secondary data from a pre-existing study with specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, the findings may be limited by the constraints of the original study design. Another limitation of the study is that we did not have information on nature of injury or disease in the community control group, as a result, it was not possible to compare the specific work-related injuries to individuals with similar types of injuries in a non-work context. Additionally, injury severity was not available for injured workers. As the community comparison group did not sustain a workplace injury and was assigned a random index date, the community comparator served as a visual reference for secular trends only and was not formally integrated into the interrupted time series model, which may limit the strength of this comparison. Furthermore, the community comparison group was not matched on employment status. Given that the Australian labour force participation was approximately 65% in 2016 [39], a proportion of the community group may not have been in paid employment. This is a key distinction between community control and the injured workers who were working and healthy in the pre-injury year. This difference may have influenced the visual comparisons in healthcare utilisation between groups. Lastly, information about the reason for GP consultation was not available in MBS data. So, while the increase in GP consultations directly coincides with the work-related injury, the worker may have attended for a number of potential conditions.

The study has important implications for injured workers, GPs, the healthcare system, and workers’ compensation schemes. Injured workers often have broader acute and long-term healthcare needs, including mental health care and chronic disease management. Even with accepted workers’ compensation claims, many still rely on Medicare-subsidised GP consultations. These additional consultations could be viewed as potentially shifting a financial burden from workers’ compensation to Medicare. At a 2023 rate of AUD$ 42.85 per Level B consultation, this would incur Medicare an additional cost of AUD $68,560 per 1000 injured workers per year [24]. Injured workers may pay out-of-pocket costs for Medicare-subsidised GP consultations. At a rate of an average out-of-pocket cost of AUD $43 (inflation-adjusted) per Level B GP consultation in 2016 [40], these additional GP consultations would incur an estimated maximum out-of-pocket cost of AUD $68,800 per 1000 injured workers per year. To illustrate the potential impact, we estimated the indicative costs, which should be interpreted with caution. Future research could incorporate direct payment records to more accurately quantification of costs shifting from workers’ compensation and injured workers. Additionally, comparing GP service use between injured workers with accepted and rejected workers’ compensation claims could provide further insights into the post-injury healthcare utilisation patterns. The current workers’ compensation system may consider addressing the comprehensive healthcare needs, including pre-existing conditions and new health issues that arise after injury, particularly in long-duration claims. Providing early support for mental health and chronic disease could improve recovery outcomes for work-related injuries and reduce long-term health problems. Notably, some Australian jurisdictions, such as Queensland and Victoria, have more recently introduced early access to GP and allied health services, including psychologists and psychiatrists, before formal claim acceptance. However, GP awareness of these services may vary, and targeted training could further support improved outcomes. Although workers’ compensation and Medicare operate as separate funding systems, this study demonstrates that following injury, injured workers use Medicare-subsidised GP consultations above pre-injury levels and the community comparator group. Stronger collaboration between Medicare and workers’ compensation could provide integrated care and support for injured workers and reduce fragmentation in service delivery. At a systems level, policymakers should consider mechanisms to better coordinate healthcare utilisation across these two systems to ensure workers’ needs are met without disproportionate cost-shifting to the public healthcare system. Future research may consider exploring how injured workers navigate these two separate healthcare funding systems and what coordination mechanisms might be feasible to support their healthcare needs.

Conclusion

Workers’ compensation, in principle, is a social insurance system that is designed to cover reasonably necessary GP consultations for work injuries, however, there are additional GP consultations funded by a separately operated public healthcare scheme that are associated with work-related injuries. This indicates that the injured workers with long-duration claims have greater healthcare needs following injury, which are not fully covered by the workers’ compensation system. Workers’ compensation may consider to address the comprehensive needs of injured workers, facilitating recovery and return-to-work. Future research may examine how injured workers navigate these two separate systems in practice and mechanisms to coordinate between workers’ compensation and Medicare to better support injured workers.
