Medicaid funds direct care for one in four dual-eligible older adults nationally, but state variation is 4-fold
Authors: Karen Shen, Yang Yang, Jennifer L Wolff, Jennifer M Reckrey, Amanda Kreider, Katherine E M Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMedicaid spent over 129 billion dollars on home- and community-based services (HCBS) in 2022. However, complex financing structures obscure which services are used, who uses them, and how use varies across states.MethodsUsing 100% of national Medicaid claims data from 2021, we characterize HCBS use among dual-eligible adults aged 65 and older, distinguishing between direct care services (services that provide hands-on assistance with functional needs) and non-direct care services (eg, transportation and case management), which are often grouped together in the literature.ResultsWe find that 38% of older duals used any Medicaid-funded HCBS in 2021, and 26% used direct care, with direct care users being older, sicker, and more likely to have dementia than non-users. We also find that the share of older duals using direct care varies more than 4-fold between the lowest and highest utilization states, with states that finance personal care as a State Plan benefit, rather than exclusively through waiver programs, generally having greater utilization.ConclusionIn light of potential funding cuts, our findings highlight the large but uneven role that Medicaid plays in financing direct care for a vulnerable population of older adults, which can be critical for them to remain in their homes and communities.Keywords: Medicaid, direct care, long-term services and supports

Introduction

More than 6 million older adults qualify for Medicaid in addition to Medicare due to having low income and limited assets. These older “dual-eligibles,” or duals, often have complex health and social needs; eg, nearly half require assistance with activities of daily living like bathing and eating.1 Though they comprise just 13% of Medicare and 9% of Medicaid beneficiaries, older duals account for 23% and 17% of total Medicare and Medicaid spending, respectively.1

While Medicare is the primary payer for duals' health care services, Medicaid covers Medicare premiums, cost-sharing, and services not covered by Medicare. Long-term services and supports (LTSS) are a notable non-covered service, accounting for roughly 59% of total Medicaid expenditures on older adults in 2021.2 LTSS encompasses nonmedical institutional services (eg, nursing home care), which states are required to cover, and home- and community-based services (HCBS), which are optional, for people with functional limitations. In recent decades, Medicaid investment in HCBS has grown substantially. In 2022, Medicaid spent $129 billion on HCBS, and this spending comprised 65% of total LTSS spending, compared to 27% in 2000.3

HCBS includes a diverse array of services, including home modifications, transportation, and mobility equipment. One important HCBS category is “direct care,” or hands-on help with functional needs, which can be offered in a range of settings, including a person's home, adult day care, or assisted living. Direct care requires ongoing, human-to-human care and is therefore not easily substituted with technology or devices.

Despite its essential role in helping older duals live in the community, Medicaid coverage of HCBS varies widely across states, with states electing which HCBS services to cover and how to cover them.4,5 For example, HCBS can be financed as a State Plan benefit, to which all eligible Medicaid beneficiaries are entitled, or through waiver programs such as the 1915(c) program. Unlike State Plan benefits, waivers allow states to cap enrollment or spending and offer tailored benefits to specific subgroups. In recent years, an increasing number of states have also shifted LTSS benefits into managed care programs, known as managed LTSS (MLTSS), in which private plans are paid a capitated rate to cover beneficiaries' care needs. These design decisions may directly shape access to HCBS. For example, if waiver programs impose binding enrollment constraints, covering HCBS as a State Plan benefit may increase access. Consistent with this, a recent study finds that Medicaid-eligible older adults are more likely to report receiving paid care when residing in states with State Plan personal care benefits.6 The impact of MLTSS is more theoretically and empirically ambiguous. On the one hand, MLTSS plans may face incentives to increase access to HCBS in an effort to lower spending on institutional care, and recent quasi-experimental evidence has found MLTSS implementation to be associated with increased home care use7,8 and lower rates of nursing home residence.9 On the other hand, MLTSS plans may also face incentives to ration care, particularly if the costs of doing so are likely to fall onto families or Medicare. Recent work has also found that MLTSS is associated with decreased beneficiary satisfaction and care hours.8,10,11 These mixed findings reflect broader uncertainty about the impact of managed care in Medicaid,12 with previous studies of the impact on the disabled population finding both positive and negative effects on health and health care access.13,14

Because of the complex coverage and financing environment of Medicaid HCBS, meaningfully summarizing state variation in HCBS use is challenging. Much of the available data describe enrollment in, or spending on, HCBS programs rather than actual use of particular services.15 As a result, much of the knowledge to date has examined cross-state HCBS coverage variation using global spending-based measures, such as the percent of LTSS spending in a state that goes toward HCBS (ie, a state's “rebalancing” percentage),16-18 or HCBS spending per capita.17,19-21 These measures may conflate variation in access with variation in costs, and can be difficult to interpret because they are often not disaggregated by population (eg, older adults vs younger Medicaid beneficiaries) or type of service (eg, direct care vs non-direct care services).22

Amid Medicaid cuts and growing reliance on HCBS,3,23,24 service-specific measures of HCBS utilization that are comparable across states are critical to informing policy. Using 2021 Medicaid and Medicare claims and enrollment data, we first describe types of HCBS used by older duals, comparing direct care users to other HCBS users and older duals overall. Second, we examine how the share of older duals receiving direct care services varies across states and by 2 key state financing policies of interest: having a State Plan personal care benefit and use of MLTSS. Finally, we examine whether state variation in direct care use is correlated with the rebalancing percentage, a commonly used spending-based measure of HCBS generosity.

Study data and methods

Data

Our primary data source is the 2021 Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System (T-MSIS) Analytic Files (TAF), which contain fee-for-service claims and managed care encounter records for all services paid for by Medicaid nationally. We link these data to the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF), which provides Medicare enrollment information for all Medicare beneficiaries and chronic condition information for individuals enrolled in Traditional Medicare (TM).

Study sample

Our study sample includes individuals aged 65 and over who had at least one month of full dual enrollment (ie, were enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid) in 2021, based on the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File. We follow previous literature in excluding individuals in Health Homes (HH) and Programs of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly (PACE) because these programs may bill HCBS services differently (eg, PACE does not submit encounter data except for 2 states) and therefore may be systematically missing service-specific claims in the Other Services (OT) TAF files, which are critical to identifying HCBS service types.25 We also exclude the small number of enrollees in Money Follows the Person (MFP) due to similar concerns.15 We also exclude 5 states for data quality reasons: Mississippi due to unusable data linkages of claims to beneficiaries in 2021, and Nebraska, Kentucky, Michigan, and Ohio, due to data quality concerns related to identifying HCBS services revealed by our own analysis (more details provided in Appendix A). These 5 states represented 8.4% of the older duals population in 2021.

Outcomes of interest

To identify and categorize HCBS services, we follow a modified version of the methodology from a 2023 CMS methodology brief.25 Specifically, the CMS methodology identifies 13 categories of HCBS services, informed by a commonly used taxonomy of HCBS services,26 and we modify this methodology to instead identify 21 service categories (see Appendix Table S1 for details). The additional categories allow us to: (1) distinguish direct care services from other services in the home-based, round-the-clock, and day services categories, and (2) add specificity regarding types of equipment and home modifications, rather than grouping all equipment and home modifications into one category.

We then use this categorization to define our primary outcomes of interest: (1) individual use of any HCBS service in 2021, and (2) individual use of any direct care service. We define direct care HCBS as personal care, respite, or homemaker services provided in the home, residential care, or adult day care settings. Non-direct care HCBS includes all other services (eg, transportation or case management), which do not involve hands-on personal or attendant care. We focus on the use of “any direct care,” rather than each direct care service individually, as our key outcome of interest, because of the potential for substitution across direct care services.

Individual-level characteristics

Other individual-level variables include demographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity); geographic characteristics (rurality, Census Region); and receipt of any Medicaid-paid institutional long-term care claim in 2021. For the subset of beneficiaries with sufficient Traditional Medicare (TM) claims history to be included in the Chronic Conditions Warehouse (CCW) Chronic Conditions File, we also examine their total number of chronic conditions and having a diagnosis of Alzheimer's and Related Dementias (ADRD).

State-level variables

We examine 2 key characteristics of how state Medicaid programs finance their HCBS programs: (1) coverage authority, ie, whether a state was one of 34 states with a State Plan benefit for personal care, which we identify from KFF reports,27 and (2) delivery model, whether a state had mandatory enrollment for older duals in managed long-term services and supports (MLTSS), which we obtain from CMS reports.28 We also examine a commonly used metric to capture state HCBS generosity: the “rebalancing percentage”, or the share of state LTSS spending on older adults that went toward HCBS, which we obtain from CMS reports.3

Empirical strategy

We first calculate the share of older duals nationally that used any HCBS and any direct care HCBS in 2021, as well as stratifying across our 21 categories of HCBS. Next, we compare the demographic, health, and utilization characteristics of older duals who use direct care services, compared to all HCBS users, and compared to all older duals. We then construct measures at the state level of the share of older duals who use direct care HCBS, which we compare across states with and without (1) a State Plan personal care benefit and (2) mandatory MLTSS. Finally, we estimate the Spearman rank correlation coefficient of this measure with the state rebalancing measure to assess how the 2 measures differ in their ranking of states.

Sensitivity analyses

We conduct several sensitivity analyses. First, we consider a more expansive definition of HCBS that includes skilled Medicaid-funded home care (defined as skilled home health, therapy, or nursing services) as a direct care HCBS service. Second, because some HCBS services (eg, transportation and case management) may be delivered to nursing home residents, we examine the sensitivity of our results to restricting the sample to individuals who did not incur institutional long-term care claims to focus on people who were community-dwelling for the entirety of the year. Third, we restrict our sample to patients who were continuously enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid for the entire year. Fourth, we consider how our results change if we focus exclusively on the most commonly used direct care service, personal or homemaker care.

Finally, our primary state-level measure calculates the share of older duals who received direct care. However, states may also differ in the generosity of eligibility for Medicaid benefits for older adults, as well as in the share of their older adult population with a disability requiring direct care assistance. These differences may complicate the interpretation of our measure. Thus, we also consider how our measure changes using 2 alternative denominators calculated from the American Community Survey: (1) the number of older adults in a state with incomes below 100% FPL (“low-income older adults”), which would be unaffected by differences in Medicaid eligibility policy and (2) the number of low-income older adults in a state with a self-care or independent living disability (“low-income older adults with disability”), which we use as a proxy for need for direct care assistance. To further account for our inability to measure functional need, we conduct 2 additional analyses where we limit the population of interest to those with 3+ chronic conditions and those with a dementia diagnosis in the CCW subsample.

Limitations

Our study is subject to limitations. First, Medicaid claims data quality may vary by state, which may lead to undercounting of HCBS use and potential measurement error in service definitions, and this may be exacerbated in states with MLTSS programs if encounter data submission is incomplete. Second, because we cannot distinguish the purpose for which a given service was provided (eg, transportation provided to a person due to disability-related needs vs social-related needs or to a person living in a nursing home), our definition of HCBS may be broader than definitions from other reports, which may restrict such services to only those provided through HCBS programs. Third, we do not have measures of function in the data that would enable us to identify the population “needing” direct care. Finally, we exclude several states from the analysis due to data quality concerns and are limited to a single year of data, 2021, which occurred during the midst of the COVID-19 pandemic, both of which may limit generalizability. On the one hand, HCBS utilization may have increased during the pandemic due to greater Medicaid enrollment as a result of the continuous enrollment provision, the increased federal match on many HCBS services passed in the American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA), and avoidance of congregate care settings. On the other, the HCBS sector faced a variety of challenges, including workforce shortages and closures, which may have reduced utilization.

Results

Characteristics of duals using direct care and non-direct care HCBS

Of the 5.3 million older duals residing in 46 sample states and DC in 2021, we find that 2.0 million (38.1%) used any HCBS service, including 1.4 million (26.2%) who used direct care (Figure 1). Among direct care services, personal care or homemaker services were most widely used, by 61.5% of all HCBS users. Use of direct care in the form of residential care, adult day care, and respite care was much less common, with each being used by less than 7% of HCBS users. The most common non-direct care HCBS services were transportation, case management, and personal emergency response systems, used by 30.6%, 21.4%, and 15.1% of all HCBS users, respectively.

Compared to the older duals not using HCBS, those who used HCBS were older (average age 78.5 compared to 74.4) and more likely to be female (66.7% compared to 60.7%), Asian (13.9% compared to 12.0%), and living in the West (34.3% compared to 33.2%) or Northeast (28.6% compared to 22.8%), and less likely to be living in rural counties (6.4% compared to 7.6%) (Table 1). HCBS users were less likely to have used institutional care in the same year (13.7% compared to 17.3%), and had more chronic conditions (6.7 compared to 4.6) and higher rates of dementia (31.4% compared to 26.0%) than non-users.

Comparing within HCBS users, older duals using (vs not using) direct care were older (average age 78.5 compared to 74.1), more likely to be female (69.0% compared to 60.7%), much more likely to be Asian (17.6% compared to 5.1%), more likely to live in the West (37.6% compared to 27.6%), and less likely to live in rural counties (5.3% compared to 9.1%). Just 8.0% of older duals receiving direct care also used institutional care in the same year, compared to 26.1% using non-direct care HCBS. Older duals using direct care HCBS had slightly higher numbers of chronic conditions (6.9 compared to 6.4) but were less likely to have dementia (30.9% compared to 32.7%). However, among the subgroup of HCBS users who were community-dwelling for the entire year (78% of the sample), direct care users had nearly twice as many chronic conditions (6.6 compared to 3.6) and were 4 times as likely to have diagnosed dementia (28.2% compared to 8.3%) (Appendix Table S2).

Variation across states in direct care HCBS use

We find wide state variation in the share of older duals who used direct care HCBS (Figure 2). In 7 states, more than 30% of older duals received Medicaid-funded direct care, including 44% of older duals in Minnesota. In the 7 states with the lowest share of older duals using direct care, the rate was less than 15%, including a low of 9% in Louisiana. The State Plan benefit for personal care was present in all 14 states, with the highest share of older duals receiving direct care HCBS. By contrast, we do not observe a clear relationship between direct care use and mandatory MLTSS.

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses, we find that the percentage of older duals who used direct care is very similar when we include skilled home health care as direct care, as well as when we restrict the sample to either community-dwelling or continuously enrolled beneficiaries (Appendix Table S3). Accordingly, these changes have only minor impacts on the ranking of states by percent of older duals using direct care, with rank correlations with our primary measure between 0.94 and 1 (Appendix Table S4). Ranking using only personal care utilization instead of direct care utilization also results in fairly similar rankings (rank correlation 0.80), and similar demographic findings (Appendix Table S5). By contrast, we find that scaling the number of older duals using direct care by the number of low-income older adults or the number of low-income older adults with disability, rather than the number of older duals, has a more significant impact on state rankings, with rank correlations with our primary measure of 0.77 and 0.71, respectively (Appendix Table S6). However, the relationship between the State Plan personal care benefit and utilization of direct care HCBS remains robust when using these alternative denominators (Appendix Figure S1). Finally, we find that rankings are similar when evaluated using the population with 3+ chronic conditions (rank correlation 0.91) but diverge significantly when evaluated using the population with dementia (rank correlation 0.55) (Appendix Table S7).

Discussion

In this study of 5.3 million dually eligible older adults, we find that 38% received at least one Medicaid-funded HCBS service in 2021, of which 7 in 10 received direct care. Our findings suggest that direct care—-particularly personal care and homemaker services provided in the home—-is a core component of care for older duals. We also find that older duals who use direct care services have significant health needs and disability: they are older than the general population of older duals and have high rates of chronic conditions and dementia.

Importantly, we find wide variation across states in the share of older duals utilizing direct care, with 4-fold variation between the highest- and lowest-utilization states. This variation suggests that the optional and patchwork nature of HCBS coverage has resulted in significant geographic disparities in utilization, with the ability of lower-income older adults to age in place varying substantially based on the state in which they live.29,30 Our analysis suggests that whether a state covers personal care as a State Plan benefit available to all Medicaid beneficiaries, rather than only through waiver programs, which allow states to impose caps on enrollment, is associated with greater utilization of direct care. While this finding is in some ways unsurprising, the role of the State Plan personal care benefit in enabling access has been underemphasized in both academic and policy discussions, which have tended to focus on waiver programs. By contrast, we find no clear pattern in direct care utilization with respect to whether states deliver HCBS through fee-for-service or managed care financing, indicating that either delivery model can potentially achieve high rates of HCBS use. These findings match similarly mixed findings from other quasi-experimental work on MLTSS implementation.7-9

Importantly, both policy analyses are purely descriptive and cross-sectional, and obscure substantial heterogeneity across states in how each of these policies is implemented. For example, even within states with a State Plan benefit, states may differ in how they determine clinical eligibility for the benefit, and how many hours of care beneficiaries are eligible for, and the rates paid to personal care providers. MLTSS states may differ in how they structure the capitated payments, and whether MLTSS plans are required to be integrated with Medicare Advantage plans serving duals. Fully understanding this heterogeneity is beyond the scope of this paper but is an important area for future research to help inform how different state policy levers impact HCBS access. Future research could also investigate whether the demographic differences that we document in who among older duals receives direct care (eg, that Asians are particularly likely to receive direct care) represent disparities in access, after accounting for wide state variation in direct care utilization.

Finally, we also demonstrate that while the percentage of older duals receiving direct care correlates with a state's “rebalancing percentage,” a commonly used performance measure for evaluating access to Medicaid HCBS, the measures show important deviations in how they assess and rank state performance. These measures may differ for several reasons: eg, states may rank highly in rebalancing but low in our measure due to serving fewer beneficiaries at higher intensity, due to spending heavily on non-direct care services, or due to limiting utilization of institutional care. Importantly, our measure is also limited by our inability to measure functional need, and may also reflect differences in Medicaid eligibility policies and take-up. However, our finding that having a State Plan personal care benefit is associated with greater utilization of direct care is consistent across measures using alternative denominators to account for differences in Medicaid eligibility policy and functional need across states. Our results thus underscore the continued need for research to develop better metrics of state HCBS generosity across multiple dimensions, including measures that incorporate person-reported perspectives.31,32

Our findings take on added urgency given 2 converging pressures. First, rapid aging of the US population will increase demand for direct care in the coming years. Second, because HCBS comprise over half of optional state Medicaid spending,33 states facing budget pressures from Medicaid funding cuts and the expiration of ongoing policy developments (supplemental HCBS matching funds from the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021) may reduce HCBS coverage.23,24,34 Taken together, these pressures may increase the unmet need for direct care. Given that the unmet need for direct care has been associated with increased acute health care utilization and greater rates of institutionalization, such cuts may have the unintended consequence of increasing health care spending in other domains.35-37 Alternatively, people with unmet care needs may need to rely more heavily on unpaid family and friends, with potential consequences for these caregivers' health, finances, and labor market participation.38-40 Our findings highlight the substantial share of the older dual population that currently relies on Medicaid-financed direct care to remain in their communities.
