Technology-Dependent Children Living at Home in Korea: A Population-Based Analysis Using NHIS Claims Data
Authors: Ji Weon Lee, Cho Hee Kim, Nam Gu Lim, Min Sun Kim, Sun Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in medical technology have improved the survival of critically ill children, resulting in a growing population of technology-dependent children (TDC). Given their substantial healthcare needs and often complex care, understanding the prevalence and healthcare utilization of TDC is crucial for informed decision-making for healthcare professionals and policymakers. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence, incidence, and healthcare utilization of TDC in Republic of Korea (South Korea).MethodsThis retrospective, population-level study analyzed the National Insurance Service claims data in Korea from 2018 to 2019. Children and adolescents aged 0–18 years living at home with technology dependence for respiratory (invasive/non-invasive ventilation, tracheostomy, or oxygen therapy) or nutritional support (nasoenteric or gastrostomy tube feeding) were included. We estimated 2-year prevalence, 1-year incidence, and healthcare utilization, including subgroup comparisons by complex chronic condition (CCC) status.ResultsIn total, 3,306 children were identified as TDC: 1,889 required respiratory support (21.5 per 100,000), and 2,036 required tube feeding (23.2 per 100,000). The mean age was 4.4 ± 5.4 years for respiratory-dependent and 6.2 ± 5.8 years for nutritional-dependent children. Most of the children had ≥ 1 CCC. Compared with non-CCC counterparts, children with CCC had longer hospital stays (125 vs. 35.8 days), more intensive care unit days (74.6 vs. 35.8), and higher annual healthcare costs per patient ($49,870 vs. $10,430). The overall 1-year mortality was 17.0%, with higher rates among patients with CCC.ConclusionTDC represent a relatively small but medically complex population with high healthcare needs and utilization. These findings highlight the importance of coordinated and multidisciplinary home-based care. Further studies are warranted to guide interventions and policies that support improved outcomes for TDC and their families.

INTRODUCTION

Recent advances in medical technology and healthcare systems have substantially improved survival rates for children with life-threatening conditions.123 However, this has led to an increase in technology-dependent children (TDC), who rely on medical devices such as ventilators, feeding tubes, or oxygen therapy for daily survival.345 Although these advances offer hope, they also present substantial caregiving and healthcare challenges, including unmet medical needs, financial strain, and fragmented care.6789 TDC frequently experience hospital readmissions and place ongoing demands on families and health systems.1011

In Korea, the centralized National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) provides near-universal coverage, and pediatric survival rates have improved markedly.10111213 However, the system remains hospital-centric and focused on acute care, limiting the development of community-based services such as home care and long-term care coordination.14 These limitations are especially evident in the care for TDC.15

Despite the increasing numbers of TDC cases, TDC are underrepresented in national data and policy. There is an urgent need for evidence to support planning and care delivery. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence, incidence, and healthcare utilization of children living at home with respiratory or nutritional technology dependence in Korea. Using NHIS claims data, we analyzed demographic characteristics, device use, and healthcare utilization patterns. This is the first nationwide analysis in Korea to include both respiratory- and nutrition-dependent children.

METHODS

Study design and settings

We conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the NHIS, which covers approximately 97% of the Korean population. The dataset included demographic characteristics, diagnostic codes based on the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision, and inpatient/outpatient claims.16 Mortality was determined by NHIS disqualification due to death.

Study population and definitions

The study included pediatric patients aged 0–18 years who met the operational definition of technology dependence based on NHIS claims for medical device use between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2020.

Operational definitions

Children and adolescents aged 0–18 years were included if they required home-based medical devices for respiratory or nutritional support. Technology dependence was defined as the need for such devices to sustain life or essential physiological functions. Other technology-dependent groups, such as those requiring parenteral nutrition, urinary catheterization, or suction devices, were not captured in this study.

Respiratory support at home included three modalities: home ventilator use, identified through ventilator prescription records; home oxygen therapy, defined by the corresponding prescription claims; and tracheostomy, identified either through relevant procedure codes during hospitalization or claims for tracheostomy tube supplies in outpatient or home nursing care settings.

Nutritional support at home was defined as enteral feeding via either a nasoenteric (nasogastric or nasojejunal) tube or a gastrostomy tube. Nasoenteric tube feeding was identified by outpatient or home nursing care claims related to tube exchange or supply. Gastrostomy tube feeding was defined by the presence of gastrostomy procedure codes during hospitalization or by related supply claims in outpatient or home nursing care settings.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Children were included if they had documented use of either respiratory or nutritional support at home, as defined above. We excluded patients who spent ≥ 50 weeks per year in the hospital and those with only one nasoenteric tube claim, in order to focus on community-based, long-term dependence. For patients with multiple claims, the earliest event during the period was used as the index event.

Case identification

We first extracted data on all patients prescribed home ventilators or oxygen therapy from National Health Insurance medical care cost data between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2020, and identified those aged 0–18 years. To account for data entry delays, we selected patients with prescriptions between March 1, 2018, and February 28, 2020, for inclusion. Tracheostomy-related procedures or supply claims were identified from medical benefits data between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2019. We linked the two datasets at the individual level using patient identifiers for further analysis. Given that ventilator prescriptions are typically issued biennially and NHIS coverage began in 2016, we used a 2-year observation window (2017–2018) as a washout period to exclude pre-existing cases and focus on newly documented device use during 2019.

Respiratory support was categorized into four mutually exclusive groups using a hierarchical classification: 1) tracheostomy with a home ventilator (TIV), 2) non-invasive ventilation (NIV) without tracheostomy, 3) tracheostomy without a ventilator, and 4) oxygen therapy without other respiratory devices. Nutritional support was identified via material cost codes for nasoenteric or gastrostomy tubes between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2019. If both types were recorded, the case was classified as gastrostomy. Variables and healthcare utilization

Children were grouped by age (< 1, 1–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–18 years), region (Seoul vs. non-Seoul), and income level (high, medium, low) based on NHIS premiums. Disability status, registered in the National Disability Registration System, was categorized as none, mild, or severe.17 Diagnoses were classified using the complex chronic conditions (CCCs) framework, with diagnostic complexity based on the number of CCC categories per patient.18 Duplicates within the same category were excluded; overlaps across categories were allowed. Additional information on classifications for geographical regions, disability types, diagnostic codes, and claim codes is presented in Supplementary Data 1.

Healthcare utilization was assessed by the number of inpatient days (including intensive care unit [ICU] stays) and total medical costs, including NHIS-covered expenses and out-of-pocket payments. For patients who died within 1 year of the index date, only costs incurred prior to death were included.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations; categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Group differences by CCC status were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. Prevalence was calculated per 100,000 using mid-year population estimates. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University College of Medicine (No. 2210-114-1372) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. NHIS provided anonymized data, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective, non-interventional nature of the study.

RESULTS

Prevalence and demographics of TDC

A total of 1,889 children (21.5 per 100,000) aged 0–18 years required respiratory support at home between 2018 and 2019 (Table 1). Among them, 6.7 per 100,000 used home ventilators (TIV or NIV), and 6.6 per 100,000 had tracheostomies (TIV or tracheostomy alone). The cohort comprised 55.4% male, with a mean age of 4.4 ± 5.4 years. Infants (< 1 year) had the highest prevalence (155.1 per 100,000), mostly requiring oxygen therapy.

The prevalence was comparable in Seoul (24.0) and non-Seoul areas (21.0 per 100,000). Income levels were distributed as follows: high income (1st–25th percentile) accounted for 34.8%, medium income (26th–75th percentile) for 46.7%, and low income (76th–100th percentile) for 18.5%. Incidence of TDC

In 2019, 722 children (8.4 per 100,000) newly required home respiratory support (Supplementary Table 1). The incidence was highest among infants, particularly for oxygen therapy (80.6 per 100,000), TIV (3.9 per 100,000), and NIV (2.3 per 100,000), with rates declining with age (Fig. 2). Tracheostomy without ventilator peaked in the 1–4-year age group (0.4 per 100,000) and showed a secondary rise in adolescents.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study to provide an integrated analysis of TDC at home, encompassing both respiratory and nutritional support needs. We identified a total of 3,306 children aged 0–18 years requiring respiratory or nutritional support after adjusting for overlapping cases, with 1,889 relying on respiratory support (2-year prevalence of 21.5 and annual incidence of 8.4 per 100,000) and 2,036 receiving tube feeding for nutritional support (2-year prevalence of 23.2 and annual incidence of 6.5 per 100,000).

The 2-year prevalence of ventilator use at home (6.7 per 100,000) is comparable to a previous Korean study based on 2015–2017 data, which reported a 2-year prevalence of 6.3 per 100,000 among children under age 15.19 In contrast, U.S. data show tracheostomy rates of 6.8 and 6.0 per 100,000 child-years in 2000 and 2012, respectively, which aligns with our findings on tracheostomy (6.6 per 100,000).20

Age-related patterns were distinct, with approximately 60% of children requiring respiratory support at home under age 4 years, and the highest prevalence observed in infants. Oxygen therapy at home accounted for a substantial portion of support in this age group. However, when excluding those using only oxygen, invasive devices such as ventilators and tracheostomy tubes remained concentrated in children aged < 4 years. These trends highlight the importance of early care coordination and sustained family support. When devices such as tracheostomy or feeding tubes are introduced—particularly at a young age—families face challenges that require education, hands-on training, emotional preparation, and guidance in complex decision-making.212223 In addition, qualitative studies have shown that caregivers often experience distress at technology initiation, feel overwhelmed during the transition home, and face sustained psychological, emotional, and financial burdens in daily management.21 From diagnosis to discharge and transition to home, seamless and well-coordinated care is essential to ensure safety and sustainability, especially for first-time parents navigating the dual roles of caregiving and parenthood.2324 These findings emphasize the need for age-appropriate home care and ongoing care coordination.2224

Given these needs, access to appropriate home-based services is important. In Korea, however, only four hospitals provide child-specific home care, with three located in Seoul, and community-based services remain scarce nationwidely. Considering our study’s findings of no significant difference in TDC prevalence between Seoul and non-Seoul areas, children outside the capital likely face limited access to home-based care and insufficient services, including timely emergency support and multidisciplinary care to meet their complex needs.

Children requiring respiratory support were more concentrated in the high-income group. Although previous studies have reported that caring for children with medical complexity imposes a substantial financial burden, it is difficult to infer a causal relationship based on our findings.1525 Further studies are needed to investigate contributing factors, including whether financial status influences decisions about technology dependence in chronic care or whether prolonged caregiving affects financial status.

Approximately 70% of children using ventilator or tracheostomy tube were registered under the disability system. Despite their complex medical needs, children with disabilities often face access barriers.2627 Limitations in mobility and transportation may further hinder timely outpatient visits or access to emergency care, particularly during episodes of acute clinical deterioration.28

Caregiving is further complicated by the high diagnostic complexity, with 70% of TDC classified under three or more CCC categories. This means that families must coordinate care across multiple specialties, manage frequent hospital visits, and navigate a wide range of medical information and decisions. Previous studies have highlighted the need for multidisciplinary care to address overlapping medical, developmental, and psychosocial needs in TDC.61521 Developing coordinated and tailored care models remains essential to effectively meet these children’s complex needs.

The 2-year prevalence of home tube feeding in our study was 23.2 per 100,000 children. Although methodological differences limit direct comparisons, the prevalence of pediatric tube feeding at home in Korea appears lower than in other countries. For instance, the Netherlands reported an annual prevalence of 83–92 per 100,000 children for pediatric home tube feeding (including nasogastric and gastrostomy tubes) between 2010 and 2014.29 In Western Australia, the prevalence of gastrostomy was approximately 6.7 per 10,000 live births over a 32-year period.30 Cultural attitudes toward tube feeding may partly explain these differences. Previous studies have noted that gastrostomy is less preferred in many Asian countries.313233 In clinical practice, families in these contexts may prioritize prolonged oral feeding, even with aspiration risk.

In our study, gastrostomy accounted for 57.5% of home tube feeding cases and was more common in older children, consistent with global trends in which nasoenteric tubes are typically used for temporary support in younger children and gastrostomy for long-term needs.29 However, in Korea, nasoenteric tubes are often replaced at home using privately purchased supplies that are not reimbursed by the NHIS, potentially under-capturing nasoenteric feeding cases in our analysis and suggesting that actual nasoenteric tube use could be comparable to gastrostomy feeding.

TDC demonstrated high healthcare utilization and costs compared to the general pediatric population. In this study, TDC incurred nearly 70 times the annual healthcare expenditure of their peers ($687.8 per child), with a mean of 123.0 inpatient days per year versus 1.0 days in the general population.3435 In addition, within the technology-dependent cohort, children with CCC showed higher utilization and costs than those without CCC. Previous studies have reported that TDC are frequently hospitalized for common illnesses such as pneumonia or gastroenteritis, which typically do not require inpatient care in the general pediatric population.3637

The 1-year mortality rate among TDC was 17.0%, with higher mortality observed in those with CCC. This notable rate reflects the clinical severity in this population and underscores the need for clear communication about prognosis, complications, and long-term care expectations. Shared decision-making is crucial to help families navigate complex choices regarding device use and care planning.3839 Given this high mortality risk, decisions about long-term technological support should be based on medical prognosis, anticipated quality of life, and family values.40 Furthermore, the trend toward centralization of care—evident in the U.S. study where 35% of hospitals never treat TDC—highlights the need for regional planning to ensure children and families access appropriate services.40

Furthermore, our definition of TDC did not include children dependent on other medical devices such as suction devices, parenteral nutrition, or urinary catheterization. These subgroups could not be captured in our analysis because of the inherent limitations of using health insurance claims data for device identification. Previous international studies have reported these groups, generally representing a relatively small proportion of the overall TDC population.14142 Ongoing efforts to establish patient cohorts and ensure systematic registration of device use in insurance databases will be essential to enable continuous monitoring of the prevalence and incidence of these children, as has been undertaken in other countries.

Although this study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and healthcare utilization of TDC living at home in Korea, it has some limitations. First, this study did not assess longitudinal trends in TDC epidemiology by tracking prevalence and incidence over multiple years. Given that reimbursement for home ventilators began after 2016, and the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic could have introduced inaccuracies, we focused on a 2018–2019 snapshot to enhance data accuracy and reduce potential biases. This approach provides a clear and unbiased baseline for evaluating TDC prevalence and incidence, serving as a valuable reference point for future longitudinal studies.

Second, as previously discussed, the study relied on data from the NHIS, which may not capture the full spectrum of TDC, particularly those using medical devices not covered by the reimbursement system. Consequently, children using these devices may not be represented in claims data, potentially leading to an underestimation of TDC.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a valuable national overview of TDC living at home in Korea, offering foundational data on their demographics and healthcare utilization. Future studies should investigate the clinical contexts and trajectories that lead to technological dependence and explore outcomes such as device weaning, complications, and long-term survival. Establishing a nationwide TDC registry would enhance data accuracy, support continuity of care, and inform evidence-based policies to improve outcomes for this population.
