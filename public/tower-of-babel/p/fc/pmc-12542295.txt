Comprehensive genomic landscape of metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer in the Chinese population
Authors: Bo Yang, Jie Yang, Wei Xia, Qi Zhang, Panwang Zhang, Jing Jin, Hua Gong, Sheng Cheng, Xiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProstate cancer is the most common cancer in urology. While the genomic profiles and prognostic biomarkers of metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) have been well studied, the genomic characteristics of Chinese metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) and their association with clinical features remain incompletely characterized. This study aimed to characterize the genomic landscape of mHSPC in a Chinese cohort and explore correlations with clinical characteristics, with comparisons to data from the Stand Up to Cancer–Prostate Cancer Foundation (SU2C-PCF) cohort.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 52 patients diagnosed with mHSPC were enrolled, and tumor samples were subjected to targeted sequencing. Clinical, demographic, and pathological data were collected. The association between genomic alterations and clinical features was analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared test tests. Comparative analysis was performed using the SU2C-PCF cohort.ResultsCompared to the SU2C-PCF cohort, Chinese patients with mHSPC had significantly higher mutation frequencies in FOXA1 (36.54% vs. 12.0%, P < 0.001), KDM6A (15.4% vs. 4.0%, P < 0.01), PIK3CA (15.4% vs. 4.7%, P = 0.01), and CTNNB1 (11.5% vs. 4.0%, P = 0.047), while AR mutations were significantly more frequent in the SU2C-PCF cohort (1.9% vs. 22.7%, P < 0.001). Homologous recombination repair gene mutations were detected in 36.54% of patients. PIK3CA mutations were associated with low PSA levels (50% vs. 11%, P = 0.044), TP53 mutations were significantly enriched in patients with high Gleason scores (43% vs. 0%, P < 0.001), and KDM6A (7% vs. 30%, P = 0.046) and SPOP (3% vs. 30%, P = 0.012) mutations were significantly enriched in patients with low Gleason scores.ConclusionsChinese patients with mHSPC have a distinct genomic landscape and show high genomic heterogeneity, TP53 mutation was significantly enriched in patients with mHSPC with high Gleason scores.Trial registrationNot applicable. This study is a retrospective observational analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04049-w.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common diagnosed cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. In 2020, there were 1,414,259 newly diagnosed cases of prostate cancer and 375,304 deaths attributed to the disease [1]. Although the incidence of prostate cancer is lower in China compared to global average, it has significantly increased due to economic growth and the adoption of the Western lifestyles. In 2022, more than 130,000 new cases of prostate cancer were diagnosed in China, ranking it as the sixth most common type of male malignant tumor [2]. Due to the insufficient implementation of early screening programs for prostate cancer, approximately 30% of new cases in China are diagnosed at an advanced stage with distant metastasis [3]. The most common metastatic sites for prostate cancer are the lymph nodes and bones, although rare cases of visceral metastasis, including the liver and the lungs, have also been reported.

The management of metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) has significantly improved in the past five years. Numerous studies have shown that the addition of taxane chemotherapy or an androgen receptor signaling inhibitor to androgen deprivation therapy significantly improves progression-free survival and overall survival [4–6]. Additionally, recent studies have demonstrated that docetaxel combined with abiraterone or darolutamide improves survival outcomes for patients with high-volume mHSPC [7, 8]. Despite these therapies improving survival and quality of life for patients with advanced prostate cancer, long-term durable responses remain limited, and the majority of patients will eventually progress to metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC).

Precision medicine and large-scale integrative genomics have elucidated the genetic landscape and molecular subtypes of prostate cancer. Five driver pathways are involved in prostate cancer, including the androgen receptor (AR) pathway, DNA damage repair (DDR) pathway, PI3K pathway, WNT pathway, and the cell cycle pathway (involving TP53 and RB1) [9]. AR signaling plays a crucial role in the proliferation of prostate cancer cells, and blocking AR signaling could impede the growth of these cells. However, mutations or amplifications in the AR gene can lead to resistance against therapies [10, 11]. The DDR genes play an important role in maintaining genomic stability and repairing DNA damage in cells. In prostate cancer, DDR genes are frequently mutated, including both somatic and germline mutations, particularly in homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes. Deficiencies in HRR genes can increase tumor sensitivity to poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and platinum-based chemotherapy [12]. Several PARP inhibitors have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of mCRPC with HRR gene deficiencies, significantly improving survival rates for these patients. Studies have revealed that mutations in PIK3CA and the loss of PTEN function accelerate tumorigenesis and the progression to CRPC [13]. Alterations in the PI3K/AKT pathway are associated with a poor prognosis in prostate cancer. Recently, several AKT inhibitors have been evaluated as monotherapy or in combination with other therapies have shown benefits for managing mCRPC. While phase III clinical trials for mHSPC are still ongoing [14]. Genetic testing in the diagnosis and treatment of prostate cancer has become increasingly common, however, studies on mHSPC, especially in the Chinese population, remain limited.

In this retrospective study, tumor samples from 52 patients with mHSPC were collected from multiple hospitals in China. Using next-generation sequencing (NGS), we analyzed the genomic landscape of mHSPC in Chinese patients and explored the correlation between gene mutations and clinical factors. The obtained data provide valuable insights into the genomic landscape of mHSPC within the Chinese population, guiding precise therapy for prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

Patients

Eligible patients were individuals at least 18 years old with newly diagnosed and untreated, pathologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma or adenocarcinoma mixed with another subtype. All patients had confirmed distant metastasis through either emission computed tomography or positron emission tomography-computed tomography. Demographic data, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, Gleason scores (GS), and sites of metastasis at diagnosis were obtained from medical records.

Next generation sequencing

Tumor tissue from biopsies was collected and sent to HaploX Biotechnology for genomic analysis using a 1326-gene panel (Table S1). DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples using the Maxwell RSC FFPE plus DNA Kit (Promega, Beijing, China). The extracted DNA was then sheared to an average size of 200 bp using the Covaris E210 system (Covaris, Inc.). The KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Roche, Shanghai) and Agilent SureSelect XT kit (Agilent, Beijing, China) were used to construct the library for NGS. The Illumina Nova Seq 6000 platform (USA) was used for sequencing. Raw sequencing reads were filtered by fastp v 0.18.0 and aligned to the hg19 genome (GRch37) using the Burrows‑Wheeler Aligner version 0.7.15‑r1140. Duplicated reads were removed using Gencore v0.12.0 [15]. SAMtools v0.1.19 was used to generate pileup files of properly paired reads with a mapping quality ≥ 60. Somatic mutation calls were annotated using ANNOVAR20. CNVkit version 0.9.3 (https://github.com/etal/cnvkit) for copy number variation detection and GeneFuse version v0.6.1 (https://github.com/OpenGene/GeneFuse) for structural variation detection [15].

A total of 150 mCRPC whole exome sequencing datasets generated by the international Stand Up To Cancer-Prostate Cancer Foundation (SU2C-PCF) Prostate Cancer Dream Team were downloaded [9], The comparative analysis between our mHSPC cohort and the SU2C-PCF cohort was conducted, this approach permitted a comprehensive examination of the genomic disparities between our mHSPC cohort and Caucasian populations with metastatic prostate cancer.

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics such as age, PSA, and GS were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The R package “maftools” was used to conduct mutation analysis and visualize the results of the variant analysis. HRR gene mutation analysis and Pearson’s chi-squared test was used for clinical risk analysis by GraphPad Prism 9.0. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.6.3. The reported p-values were two-tailed test with a P-value<0.05 being defined as statistically significant.

Results

Patient summary

A total of 52 patients diagnosed with mHSPC were enrolled from multiple centers between January 1, 2022, and October 1, 2022. The median age of the patients was 70 years; 44 (84.6%) patients were pathologically confirmed to have prostate adenocarcinoma, while 8 (15.4%) patients were diagnosed with a mixed type, which included 4 cases of adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation, 3 cases with ductal adenocarcinoma, and 1 case with intraductal carcinoma. All 52 patients had bone metastasis, with 4 patients also presenting with visceral metastases, including 2 with lung metastases and 2 with liver metastases. Regarding Gleason score (GS) distribution, 4 (7.7%) patients had a score of 7, 16 (30.8%) had a score of 8, 19 (36.5%) had a score of 9, and 11 (21.1%) had a score of 10; data were missing for 2 patients. At diagnosis, 6 (11.5%) patients had a PSA level < 20 ng/mL, 17 (32.7%) patients had a PSA level between 20 and 100 ng/mL, and 27 (51.9%) patients had a PSA level > 100 ng/mL, data were missing for two patients. The characteristics of all the patients are summarized in Table 1 (Table S2).

Somatic mutations in mHSPC

To explore somatic mutations and mutation patterns in mHSPC, all 52 samples were subjected to NGS, in 2 patients no mutations were detected, most likely because of DNA degradation. The remaining 50 samples yielded positive results, giving a successful detection rate of 96.15%. A total of 287 nonsynonymous protein-coding mutations were identified across the 50 patients, with 1 to 30 mutations per patient. The most frequently mutated genes in mHSPC were FOXA1 (36.54%), TP53 (26.92%), APC (17.31%), KMT2D (17.31%), KDM6A (15.4%), and PIK3CA (15.4%) (Fig. 1A, Table S3). Copy number variations were rare in mHSPC, only 4 patients (7.7%) exhibiting copy number variation (Table S3). We compared the genomic alterations in our prostate cancer cohort with those in the SU2C-PCF database. The alteration frequencies of FOXA1 (36.5% vs. 12.0%, P < 0.001), KDM6A (15.4% vs. 4.0%, P < 0.01), PIK3CA (15.4% vs. 4.7%, P = 0.01), POLE (9.6% vs. 1.3%, P < 0.01) and CTNNB1 (11.5% vs. 4.0%, P = 0.047) were significantly higher in our cohort than in the SU2C-PCF cohort. Conversely, AR (1.9% vs. 22.7%, P < 0.001) was significantly more prevalent in the SU2C-PCF cohort (Fig. 1B). These results indicated that the genomic landscape of mHSPC in Chinese patients may be distinct from that of Western patients.

HRR gene mutations in mHSPC

Homologous recombination repair (HRR) gene deficiency has been established as a biomarker for PARP inhibitors, such as Olaparib, in the treatment of prostate cancer. We analyzed the mutation landscape of 14 HRR gene mutations in patients with mHSPC. Our findings revealed that 36.5% (19/52) of patients exhibited mutations in HRR genes (Fig. 2A). Among these mutations, BRCA2 (9.6%, 5/52) mutations were the most frequent. Other mutations included CDK12 (5.8%, 3/52), RAD51C (3.8%, 2/52), and PALB2 (3.8%, 2/52) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we analyzed the HRR mutation in SU2C-PCF cohort and found that 36 out of 150 patients carried HRR mutations (24%). Our results are relatively higher than those reported in the SU2C-PCF database (Fig. 2C). Overall, HRR gene mutations were present in 36.54% of the patients with mHSPC.

Clinical factors and genetic mutations

We analyzed the correlation between genomic mutations and baseline PSA levels, pathological GS, and age to explore the relationship between somatic mutations and baseline patient characteristics. Among the 52 patients in our cohort, the rate of PIK3CA mutation was significantly higher in patients with PSA levels ≤ 20 ng/mL (50.0% vs. 16.7%, P = 0.044) (Fig. 3A). FOXA1 mutation rate was slightly higher in patients with PSA > 20 ng/mL, but the difference was not statistically significant. In the GS analysis, TP53 mutations were significantly more prevalent in patients with high GS (46.7% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). However, KDM6A (6.7% vs. 30.0%, P = 0.046) and SPOP (3.3% vs. 30.0%, P = 0.012) mutations were more prevalent in patients with low GS (Fig. 3B). The age analysis revealed no gene mutations significantly associated with patient age; however, mutations in APC and FOXA1 were more prevalent in older patients, although this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3C, Table S4). These results indicate that prostate cancer is a highly genomically heterogeneous disease, and that the genomic characteristics are correlated with different clinical features.

In the CC category, the MLL3/4 protein complex and the PI3K complex are the main cellular components enriched by these mutations.

Discussion

Prostate cancer is the most common cancer in urology. Studies indicate that prostate cancer in Chinese populations presents a higher risk compared to Western populations. Approximately 32% of patients in China exhibit metastasis at the time of diagnosis compared to only 19% in the United States. The 5-year survival rate in China is 69.2%, significantly lower than the rate of over 90% in the United States [3]. These may be attributed to different lifestyles, such as a high-fat diet, as well as differences in living environments. Additionally, the genome is also likely a significant factor. In our small cohort study on the genomic characteristics of prostate cancer, we found that FOXA1 was the most frequently mutated gene (36.54%), consistent with previous reports [16]. FOXA1 is a pioneer factor that targets AR and promotes the development of prostate cancer. A large Chinese cohort study reported that 41% of prostate cancers had FOXA1 mutations, which were associated with worse prognosis and shorter survival rates [16, 17]. Besides FOXA1, TP53 is also a commonly mutated gene in mHSPC. TP53 is a key tumor suppressor gene that regulates genomic stability, as well as transcription and translation processes. TP53 mutations have been reported in 24% of mHSPC cases [18], consistent with our findings. TP53 status has been closely associated with the clinical prognosis in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), and acts as a biomarker for poor response to novel hormonal therapies (abiraterone and enzalutamide) [19]; therefore, patients with mHSPC with TP53 mutations may show less favorable outcomes when treated with these novel therapies. The WNT signaling is a cellular pathway involved in embryogenesis, development, and neoplasia. Activation of this pathway may promote androgen-independent growth and resistance to antiandrogens in prostate cancer. It has been reported that 10% to 20% of advanced prostate cancers harbor mutations in genes associated with the WNT pathway. Our results indicate that mHSPC cases had mutations in APC and CTNNB1, which may affect the response to abiraterone and enzalutamide, especially compared to patients without alterations in the WNT pathway [20]. AR is a critical gene for the progression of prostate cancer, in our mHSPC cohort, the AR gene mutation is a rare occurrence, yet it is prevalent in the SU2C-PCF cohort, this may be attributed to the fact that the SU2C-PCF cohort comprises mCRPC patients. SU2C-PCF cohort is a widely recognized and extensively utilized database for prostate cancer research. By comparing our study data with the SU2C-PCF data, we discovered significant differences in specific genes between Chinese patients and Western populations, including FOXA1, PIK3CA, CTNNB1, POLE, and AR. Several studies have demonstrated that the mutational landscape of prostate cancer differs between Western and Eastern cohorts [3, 11]. Our findings align are similar with these previous reports, suggesting that the observed discrepancies in mutation frequencies are likely attributable to racial and genetic background differences. Moreover, Chinese prostate cancer carries more genes associated with poor prognosis, such as APC, CTNNB1 and PIK3CA, which may be a reason for the poorer prognosis of prostate cancer patients. This finding suggests that the patients in our cohort may need combination therapies or a triplet therapy (Darolutamide/abiraterone Plus Androgen-Deprivation Therapy and Docetaxel) to improve patient outcomes. In conclusion, our results suggest that mHSPC has high genomic heterogeneity, which may help us to make therapeutic decisions for patients with mHSPC.

The HRR pathway is a crucial molecular mechanism for repairing DNA double-strand breaks and plays a significant role in the development and progression of prostate cancer. It has been reported that HRR gene mutations in mCRPC can reach 27%. Our results were slightly higher than previous reports, this discrepancy is likely due to the fact that our data are based on a small sample size and include mutations of uncertain significance. The results would likely be lower if only pathogenic or suspected mutations were considered. Patients with HRR gene mutations in prostate cancer have a poor prognosis, particularly those with BRCA2 and CDK12 mutations [21]. Patients with CDK12 mutations exhibit a shorter time to progression or to CRPC and have lower overall survival [22, 23]. PARP inhibitors, such as olaparib and niraparib, have been approved for the treatment of HRR-deficient mCRPC. Clinical trials exploring the use of PARP inhibitors in HRR-mutated mHSPC are ongoing [24]. Therefore, the presence of HRR gene mutations at diagnosis may influence treatment decisions and may indicate a potential benefit from PARP inhibitors.

Prognostic stratification is essential for managing mHSPC management. Guidelines of the European Association of Urology (EAU) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommend using tumor stage, PSA levels, and GS for prognostic stratification. A GS > 7 or a PSA level > 20 ng/mL are indicating high-risk prostate cancer, which is associated with a poor prognosis. We correlated mutations with baseline PSA and GS and found that the PIK3CA mutation was associated with lower PSA levels. TP53 mutations were linked to higher GS, while SPOP and KDM6A mutations were associated with lower GS. PIK3CA is a critical gene in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and is generally associated with a poor prognosis in prostate cancer. However, our data indicate that the PIK3CA mutation is significantly correlated with low PSA levels, which may stratify it as low or intermediate risk. This discrepancy may be attributed to the small sample size of our study. TP53 is a commonly mutated gene in prostate cancer and is significantly enriched in metastatic cases, indicating a poor prognosis [25]. It has been reported that TP53 alterations in hormone-naïve prostate cancer in the Chinese population exhibit a relatively higher percentage of alterations in the WNT signaling pathway, consistent with findings from previous studies.

SPOP is part of an E3-ubiquitin ligase complex that is involved in controlling the stability of the androgen receptor. Patients with prostate cancer who harbor mutations in the SPOP gene tend to have a more favorable survival prognosis [26, 27]. Our results showed that SPOP mutations are significantly prevalent among patients with low GS, suggesting that individuals with these mutations may have a better prognosis. In addition, early-onset prostate cancer presents a distinct molecular landscape, characterized by lower mutation rates of mutations in genes such as APC, CTNNB1, and RB1 [28]. Our cohort showed that early-onset cases had fewer APC and FOXA1 mutations, similar to previous reports, although these differences did not reach statistical significantly. In conclusion, our data revealed that TP53 mutations are more prevalent in patients with high-risk prostate cancer, while mutations in SPOP, KDM6A, and PIK3CA are more prevalent in patients with low- or intermediate-risk cancer. These mutations may have important prognostic implications for the management of mHSPC.

Fibroblasts, one of the most prevalent cell types in the stromal component of both primary and metastatic prostate cancer, play an important role in disease progression. The apoptotic process of fibroblasts has been associated with the advancement of the disease. It has been reported that senescent fibroblasts in the prostatic stroma can exhibit a senescence-associated secretory phenotype in the tumor microenvironment, which can contribute to increased local inflammation, thereby promoting the development of prostate cancer, its progression, and resistance to therapy [29]. Therefore, enrichment of the fibroblast apoptotic process pathway in mHSPC may indicate a specialized tumor environment, which leads to a poor prognosis. The MLL protein is a component of a large SET1-like histone methyltransferase (HMT) complex that possesses inherent histone 3 lysine 4 (H3K4) methyltransferase activity. The MLL–HMT complex consists of highly conserved core proteins, which are essential for its enzymatic activity. It has been reported that the AR directly interacts with the MLL complex via the menin–MLL subunit, which is a mechanism contributing to castration resistance [30]. Together, these results indicate that mHSPC patients may show distinct genomic pathway activations, potentially leading to a more rapid progression to mCRPC.

Our study describes the genomic characteristics of mHSPC in the Chinese population and reveals the correlation between genomic alterations and clinical manifestations. However, it has several limitations. Firstly, the analysis was conducted on a small cohort of Chinese patients, which may potentially introduce statistical biases when compared to larger databases, such as the SU2C-PCF. Secondly, the use of a specific gene panel could inadvertently cause selection bias. Finally, the duration of follow‑up time was insufficient to examine the relationship between gene mutations and the biochemical recurrence of prostate cancer. To address these limitations and enhance the robustness of our study, we will continue to increase patient enrollment and collect more comprehensive follow-up information from patients with mHSPC. In addition, we aim to construct a predictive model based on gene mutations to more accurately guide the prognosis of prostate cancer.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Chinese patients with mHSPC had a distinct genomic landscape and high genomic heterogeneity. TP53 mutation was significantly enriched in high Gleason score mHSPC, while SPOP and KDM6A mutations were significantly enriched in low Gleason score mHSPC, which may help to predict the prognosis and make clinical decisions.
