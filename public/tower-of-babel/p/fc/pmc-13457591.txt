Complementary nonlinear optics for polarimetric computing in tellurium
Authors: Hao Liu, Guokai Bai, Chuanqi Tang, Zhihan Jin, Fanhao Meng, Zhaozhao Tang, Feifan Xu, Tianci Huang, Yan Zhang, Chee Leong Tan, Xiang Wan, Shancheng Yan, Yi Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polarization serves as a high-dimensional information carrier, yet current polarization-sensitive optical systems typically separate sensing from processing, generating massive redundancy and latency. Here, we demonstrate an intelligent paradigm based on the complementary integration of low-dimensional tellurium nanowires and nanosheets, leveraging their intrinsic optical anisotropy and opposite bias-modulated second-harmonic generation behaviors. We systematically exploit these mechanisms across two key domains. In information security, optical anisotropy serves as an entropy source for physical unclonable functions, while integrating tellurium nanowires and nanosheets constructs robust encryption units. In polarized image processing, anisotropy facilitates optical filtering of interference, and, crucially, the complementary bias modulation empowers the system to synergistically perform background denoising and edge extraction. This strategy effectively executes complex information encoding and image pre-processing tasks directly at the detection stage, offering a compact solution for next-generation intelligent optical systems.

Introduction

The polarization state of light serves as a high-dimensional information carrier that transcends the limitations of conventional intensity-based capability1–3. In the realm of information security, the modulation of polarization states enables robust encryption protocols, effectively converting optical channels into secure mediums that resist unauthorized interception4–7. Simultaneously, in machine vision and remote sensing, polarization-based selective filtering allows for the rigorous extraction of target features from complex interference, a critical requirement for autonomous navigation and biological diagnostics8–11. To translate these optical phenomena into compact, on-chip polarization-sensitive systems, low-dimensional (LD) materials have emerged as ideal platforms12,13. Unlike bulky conventional optical components, materials such as one-dimensional (1D) nanowires and two-dimensional (2D) layered structures possess intrinsic geometric and crystalline anisotropies that naturally align with the polarization vectors of incident light14,15. Recent advances have demonstrated that integrating these anisotropic LD materials with functional electronic circuits can significantly amplify polarization sensitivity, enabling digital-level discrimination of optical information16–18. While linear or circular dichroism19,20 in these materials has been extensively explored, the nonlinear optical (NLO) response, particularly second-harmonic generation (SHG), offers a more powerful dimension for optical manipulation. SHG is a coherent process with polarization sensitivity to crystal symmetry breaking and local fields, making it an exquisite probe for polarization orientations21–23. Furthermore, electrically switchable SHG allows for the dynamic modulation of optical signals, facilitating logic operations such as exclusive-OR (XOR) gates directly within the optical domain for secure communication24–27.

However, a critical bottleneck persists in the current polarimetric computing paradigm. Traditional approaches typically treat the polarization-sensitive material solely as an isolated unit that passively converts light, delegating the heavy lifting of information processing (such as noise filtering and feature extraction) to external, back-end computational units28–31. This physical separation between the sensing front-end and the processing back-end generates massive redundant data traffic, imposing severe bandwidth limitations and latency, which are detrimental to real-time applications. There is an urgent need for hardware-level intelligence in which the material network itself performs pre-processing tasks directly at the physical layer of light-matter interaction32.

In this work, we present an intelligent paradigm driven by the complementary integration of tellurium (Te) nanowires and nanosheets. As an elemental semiconductor characterized by a unique chiral helical chain structure, Te exhibits pronounced 1D anisotropy in its nanowire form and strong in-plane anisotropy in its 2D nanosheet form33–35. Crucially, Te possesses an extremely high second-order nonlinear susceptibility and a topological electronic structure that supports broad optical responses36,37. Based on these material attributes, we identify distinct electrical modulation mechanisms in which nanowires and nanosheets exhibit opposite bias-modulated SHG behaviors. We systematically exploit these mechanisms to bridge material physics with system-level applications. For security applications, we utilize the optical anisotropy as an entropy source for physical unclonable functions (PUFs) and employ the complementary integration of the nanostructures to form encryption units. We also develop a polarized image processing strategy where the anisotropy inherently filters optical interference, while the opposite bias-modulated SHG synergistically eliminates background noise and extracts feature edges. By offloading the complex tasks to the sensing front-end, our work establishes a compact and efficient pathway for next-generation intelligent optical systems.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of tellurium nanostructures

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 1a) revealed two sharp characteristic peaks at 573 eV and 583.4 eV for the Te nanomaterials, corresponding to the Te 3d5/2 and Te 3d3/2 orbitals, respectively. The calculated spin-orbit splitting energy of the Te 3d orbitals was 10.4 eV, confirming that Te exists in the elemental zero-valent state (Te0) in the products. The presence of only these two sharp peaks indicates the absence of surface oxidation and impurities in both Te nanowires and nanosheets. Trigonal tellurium exhibits intrinsic spontaneous polarization, leading to pronounced second-harmonic generation (SHG). The polarized SHG patterns of both tellurium nanowires and nanosheets display a spindle shape (two-fold symmetry) (Fig. 2a, d and Supplementary Note 1), directly confirming their uniaxial anisotropic nonlinear optical response. By rotating the samples, we found that the SHG response reached its maximum when the polarization direction was perpendicular to the c-axis (represented as a 0° polarization angle in this work). Furthermore, we confirmed that this SHG response is an intrinsic optical characteristic of tellurium nanomaterials (Supplementary Fig. 8). To investigate the electrical tunability of the SHG response in tellurium nanomaterials, two-terminal devices based on individual nanowires and nanosheets were fabricated and characterized via current-voltage (I–V) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 9). The SHG intensities of the nanowires and nanosheets were systematically measured under different applied voltages (VB). The experimental results show that for tellurium nanowires (Fig. 2b), the SHG intensity gradually decreases with increasing VB amplitude (the curves in the figure represent the fitted SHG signals; the fitting model and its associated parameters are provided in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Table 1, respectively). Under VB of equal amplitude but opposite polarity, the SHG intensities were approximately equivalent. The maximum SHG intensity, at a polarization angle of 0°, follows an inverted V-shaped curve as a function of applied voltage (Fig. 2c). In contrast, the SHG intensity of tellurium nanosheets gradually increases with increasing VB amplitude (Fig. 2e, the curve represents the fitted result, with fitting parameters provided in Supplementary Table 2). Consequently, the maximum SHG intensity exhibits a characteristic V-shaped dependence on the applied voltage (Fig. 2f). Device-to-device measurements further show nearly overlapping two-fold polarization-dependent SHG responses for multiple Te nanowires and nanosheets (Supplementary Fig. 10). The opposite bias-modulated SHG behavior observed in tellurium nanowires and nanosheets originates from their morphological structures with different dimensionalities, which determine their polarization characteristics and subsequent responses to the applied electric field. The lattice chirality of tellurium nanowires (Supplementary Fig. 12) leads to their polarization direction being radial41 and rotating along the c-axis (Supplementary Fig. 7). In contrast, the polarization direction of tellurium nanosheets is always parallel to the short-side direction42 along the b-axis (Supplementary Fig. 7). Upon the application of bias voltage VB, the external electric field couples with the polarization vector of the nanosheets, accompanied by atomic displacement (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3). The changed electronic structure of tellurium nanosheets that couples with the external field leads to an electric field-induced second harmonic (EFISH43) effect, which would enhance the effective second-order nonlinear susceptibility \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi }_{{eff}}^{2}$$\end{document}χeff2 (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 14). Consequently, the SHG intensity increases linearly with the rise of |VB|. When an electric field is applied along the c-axis of Te, displacement of partial Te atoms is observed via quasi-in-situ aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM (Supplementary Fig. 15), which is consistent with the evolutionary tendency from DFT calculations. For one-dimensional chiral Te nanowires, the existence of Eshelby torsion endows their structure with lower stability compared with perfect crystals. The application of an electric field parallel to the c-axis degrades the polar order of nanowires (Supplementary Fig. 16), which serves as the direct reason for the attenuation of SHG intensity in Te nanowires under electric field stimulation. The degree of disruption to the polarization order is only correlated with the electric field magnitude, while the influence of electric field direction is negligible. As a result, the SHG intensity of Te nanowires decreases as the value of |VB| increases.

Tellurium nanostructures for information security

Based on the anisotropic nonlinear SHG response confirmed in Te nanowires or nanosheets, we demonstrate optical PUFs44. We simulated a large-scale array in which each unit consists of a single Te nanowire or nanosheet (Note: The simulated results for Te nanosheet-based PUFs are provided in Supplementary Fig. 17). The orientation angle of each unit relative to the linearly polarized light is assigned a random value, emulating stochastic assembly. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, when the linearly polarized light illuminates the array of randomly oriented nanowires, the SHG intensity of each unit is dictated by the specific alignment between the polarization vector and the nanowire’s c-axis. Due to the anisotropic nonlinear response of Te nanowires, random spatial orientations result in a spatially random distribution of SHG intensities, serving as a unique optical entropy source. The modeling of this process is grounded in the experimental polarized SHG response obtained at 0 V bias (detailed in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Table 1). To extract a digital PUF-generated key, the analog SHG intensity was digitized using the ideal mean SHG intensity as a threshold. Units exhibiting SHG intensity above the threshold were encoded as binary “1”, while those below were encoded as “0”. The bit uniformity of a single PUF was assessed by analyzing the distribution of 0 and 1 states (detailed in Supplementary Note 3). As shown in Fig. 3b, the occurrence probability of logical “1” in binary bits extracted from different randomly simulated PUFs tightly clusters around the ideal value of 0.5, indicating an excellent entropy source essential for resisting prediction attacks. To verify the uniqueness of the device fingerprints, we calculated the inter-Hamming distance (Inter-HD) by comparing keys generated from different randomly simulated PUFs (detailed in Supplementary Note 3). The resulting Inter-HD distribution (Fig. 3c) is perfectly fitted by a Gaussian curve centered at an ideal value of 0.50. This confirms that the keys generated from different PUFs are statistically uncorrelated, demonstrating the robustness and uniqueness. To further demonstrate the practical secure transmission of information, we constructed a hardware-level encryption unit based on the complementary integration of Te nanostructures. As illustrated in Fig. 3d, a Te nanowire and a nanosheet are connected in parallel and aligned at an identical orientation angle to form the encryption unit, with the incident laser at a polarization angle of 0° to maximize the SHG response. The signals of the plaintext image (VImg) and the PUF-generated key (VKey) are applied to the two input terminals of the unit, where logic “0” and “1” are defined as 0 V and 5 V potentials, respectively. Leveraging the opposing bias-modulated SHG behaviors, this unit realizes complementary logic operations. When the input bits are identical (both logic “0” or “1”), the voltage drop across each nanostructure approaches zero. In this state, the nanowire exhibits maximal SHG intensity (q, logic “1”) due to its inverted V-shaped response, whereas the nanosheet remains at a low SHG state (q̅, logic “0”). Conversely, when the inputs differ, the resulting voltage bias suppresses the nanowire’s SHG signal (q, logic “0”) while enhancing the nanosheet’s response (q̅, logic “1”). Consequently, the Te nanowire effectively functions as an exclusive-NOR (XNOR) gate, while the nanosheet operates as a complementary exclusive-OR (XOR) gate. Detailed logic truth tables for both nanostructures are provided in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6. The parallel connection exposes the Te nanowire and nanosheet to the same pair of electrical inputs while reading out two opposite bias-dependent SHG transfer functions. The two optical outputs are complementary because any given input pair drives both branches with the same voltage condition but produces opposite SHG states owing to their inverted bias dependences. During encryption, the PUF-generated key bit mixes with the plaintext bit through this XOR/XNOR pair, yielding dual-rail encrypted states. By utilizing this encryption unit driven by keys generated from the Te nanowire-based PUF, we encrypted the plaintext image (Fig. 3e, left) into a disordered noise pattern (Fig. 3e, middle) that effectively conceals the information. The original image can be restored only by decrypting it with the same encryption unit and the same PUF-generated key (Fig. 3e, right). The effect of SHG readout fluctuations on decryption robustness is quantified in Supplementary Fig. 19.

Tellurium nanostructures for image processing

The integration of Te nanowires and nanosheets enables intelligent, hardware-level polarized image processing. The processing hardware is simulated as a network driven by a front-end photodetector array (Fig. 4a), which converts the optical image into electrical potentials (5 V for illuminated pixels, 0 V for dark ones). These potentials serve as inputs to the network, where each processing unit consists of a Te nanowire and a nanosheet connected in parallel at one end, with their other ends interconnected via coupling resistors to adjacent row and column branches. All the Te nanowires and nanosheets are aligned at the same orientation angle. More detailed image processing is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 20. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the input polarized image consists of a complex scene containing “planes” as the target body patterns and “birds” as interference objects. We assigned specific polarization angle distributions to these objects: the planes possess a polarization angle range of 170°–190° (centered at ~180°), while the birds are set orthogonal to the targets at 80°–100° (centered at ~90°). Additionally, to test the system’s robustness, 10% random background noise with the same polarization angle as the targets (170°–190°) was added to the scene. The polarized image processing mechanism relies on the synergistic combination of optical anisotropy and the opposing bias-modulated SHG behaviors of the two nanostructures (modeled in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). First, optical filtering is rigorously enforced by the alignment of Te nanowires and nanosheets. The orthogonal interference objects (“birds” at ~90°) fail to induce efficient nonlinear polarization, effectively rendering them invisible in the SHG domain. Second, optoelectrical spatial processing is governed by the collective potential distribution within the network (detailed in Supplementary Fig. 21). For continuous regions such as plane bodies, the clustered activation (5 V) of adjacent pixels creates an equipotential zone, minimizing the voltage drop across the two parallel-connected nanostructures. Conversely, at the light-dark boundaries (edges between 5 V and 0 V) or in isolated light spots (noises at 5 V), the lack of equipotential neighbors induces a significant voltage gradient across the nanostructures. The two distinct nanostructures then exploit this spatial voltage differentiation. Leveraging the inverted V-shaped voltage dependence of Te nanowires (maximal SHG at zero bias), the Te nanowire array preserves the SHG of continuous body interiors due to the low voltage while suppressing the isolated noises due to the high voltage (Fig. 4d, left). In stark contrast, the Te nanosheet array utilizes its V-shaped modulation characteristic (enhanced SHG at high bias). In the SHG domain, the continuous body interiors are suppressed by the low-biased nanosheet. Consequently, the light-dark boundaries and isolated noises are selectively preserved by the high-biased nanosheet (Fig. 4e, left). A 1 × 3 network experimentally validates this complementary SHG response of the two branches (Supplementary Fig. 22). These two SHG intensity maps are digitized via thresholding to generate binary representations. For the nanowire array (Fig. 4d, right), the resulting binary image presents a clean, solid silhouette of the target fleet. This successfully rejects both discrete background noise and bird interference. However, it retains the redundant information of the plane interiors. Conversely, for the nanosheet array (Fig. 4e, right), the binarization effectively extracts the skeletal outlines of the planes, hollowing out the redundant interiors. However, a critical trade-off arises: because isolated background noises induce high-biased nanosheets similar to light-dark boundaries, they are inevitably retained. To quantitatively evaluate the complementary functions of the two branches, we further used peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) to assess the denoising capability of the Te nanowire branch under different random noise densities (Supplementary Fig. 23), and Pratt’s Figure of Merit (FOM) to assess the structural fidelity of the edge-extraction result from the Te nanosheet branch (Supplementary Fig. 24). To reconcile these complementary attributes, a logical “AND” operation is applied to the two binary images, as shown in Fig. 4f. The nanowire array effectively serves as a “denoising mask” (logic “0” at noise positions) that filters the noisy edge map generated by the nanosheet array. Simultaneously, the nanosheet array serves as an “outline mask” (logic “0” at body interiors) that carves out the redundant body interiors from the nanowire array. Consequently, the final output exclusively preserves the clean, continuous edges of the target planes. The robustness of this system is further evaluated under varying noise levels (detailed in Supplementary Fig. 25). This hardware-level polarized image processing strategy not only effectively eliminates interference objects and background noise but also inherently discards redundant morphological information directly at the physical layer, significantly reducing the data bandwidth and computational overhead required for downstream processing.

Polarimetric computing exploits polarization as an optical degree of freedom beyond intensity, enabling richer information encoding and processing. State-of-the-art polarization-sensitive photodetectors have achieved efficient readout, high sensitivity, and in-sensor processing by converting polarization-dependent optical responses into electrical signals45–49. However, the electrical readout route inevitably places the subsequent processing outside the optical domain, limiting direct exploitation of optical propagation speed, parallelism, and multiple degrees of freedom. Optical-domain polarization platforms based on diffractive networks or metasurfaces preserve optical propagation, but their polarization-dependent outputs are generally carried in the same-frequency channel. The Te platform developed here provides a different physical route by producing electrically tunable, polarization-sensitive SHG at 2ω, which keeps the processed output in the optical domain while spectrally separating it from the fundamental background. At the present proof-of-concept stage, SHG readout is more experimentally demanding than direct linear photocurrent readout, which is currently more mature from an engineering perspective. Nevertheless, it provides a frequency-converted nonlinear optical channel in which polarization, morphology, and electric bias jointly define the processing function. The opposite bias-modulated SHG transfer functions of nanowires and nanosheets further convert this nonlinear readout into complementary processing channels for information security and image processing.

Discussion

We have successfully realized an intelligent paradigm by synergistically integrating the complementary nonlinear optical attributes of Te nanowires and nanosheets. Unlike traditional architectures that rely heavily on digital post-processing, our strategy fundamentally exploits the distinct, opposite-bias-modulated SHG behaviors, coupled with intrinsic optical anisotropy, to execute logic and pre-processing tasks directly at the physical layer. We demonstrated the versatility of this complementary integration by constructing encryption units for secure communication and implementing a computing network that simultaneously denoises and extracts edges from complex visual scenes. By merging high-dimensional sensing and computing into a single, compact platform, this work effectively overcomes the bandwidth and latency bottlenecks inherent in conventional optical systems. These findings not only underscore the unique potential of low-dimensional Te nanostructures in polarimetric computing but also pave the way for future high-speed, energy-efficient edge computing solutions.

Methods

Synthesis of Te nanowires and nanosheets

Tellurium nanowires and nanosheets were synthesized via a facile solvothermal route. Specifically, 25 mL of ethylene glycol (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) was poured into the polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) liner of a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. Subsequently, 1.3 g of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, K30, Mw = 40,000, Aladdin) was added to the solvent. The mixture was magnetically stirred at 500 rpm and room temperature for 30 min, until the PVP was dissolved entirely, yielding a transparent, homogeneous solution. Next, 0.221 g of sodium tellurite (Na2TeO3) was incorporated, followed by stirring for an additional 20 min to form a clear solution. Thereafter, 0.04 g of potassium hydroxide (KOH, ≥99.95% on a metals basis, Aladdin) was added, and stirring was continued for 10 minutes. The sealed PTFE liner was then placed into the stainless-steel autoclave, which was tightly sealed and transferred into an electric oven. The temperature was ramped to 180 °C at 5 °C min−1 and held for 3 h (for nanowires) or 24 h (for nanosheets) to allow the solvothermal reaction to proceed. After the reaction, the autoclave was naturally cooled to room temperature (approximately 2 h). The resulting black precipitate was collected and alternately washed with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol for 3–5 cycles via centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min per cycle) to remove residual impurities. Finally, the washed product was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 2 h, yielding black powdered tellurium nanowire and nanosheet samples.

Characterizations

The XRD patterns were collected on a Bruker D2 PHASER diffractometer (Nanjing University). The SEM images and corresponding EDS mappings were captured using a JEOL JSM-IT800. The ZDYF-1 scanning probe microscope system collected AFM images. Raman spectra and polarized SHG were measured using the Mstarter 100 Microspectral Scanning Test System (Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications, manufactured by Nanjing Metatest Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The samples used for scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB) on a Helios G4 UC dual-beam scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Atomic-level HAADF-STEM images of nanosheets were obtained using a FEI Titan3 Themis G3 60-300.

Theoretical calculation

The density functional theory was used to simulate the nonlinear optical properties of Te crystal, which was performed with the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package. Before calculating the nonlinear susceptibility, the crystal model of Te is fully relaxed through geometry optimization according to system energy, force, and stress with the Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof functional. In the geometry optimization process, the external electric field was applied on the Te atoms with the values of −0.02, −0.15, −0.10, −0.05, 0, 0.05, 0.15, and 0.02 V/Å/atom, respectively. To improve the simulating accuracy, the All bands electron minimization method is used, and the norm conserving pseudopotentials are applied to describe the ionic cores with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 250 eV. The k-point sampling grids are 4 × 4 × 4.
