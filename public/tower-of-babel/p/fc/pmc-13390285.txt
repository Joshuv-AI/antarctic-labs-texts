Protecting Europe’s prevention infrastructure in the next EU budget
Authors: Benedetta Armocida, Knut-Inge Klepp, Hanna Tolonen
Published: 2026

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) remain the leading cause of premature mortality and disability across the European Union (EU) [1]. Yet prevention continues to receive only a small share of health spending despite its strategic importance for resilience, productivity, and equity. As the EU negotiates its 2028–34 Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF), there is a growing concern that simplifying the funding architecture around competitiveness may unintentionally dilute the dedicated governance infrastructure that has enabled coordinated action on health promotion and disease prevention across Member States (MSs).

The European Commission’s proposal of 16 July 2025 for the 2028–34 MFF consolidates 14 existing funding programmes into the newly created European Competitiveness Fund (ECF) [2]. This restructuring would subsume EU4Health, the Union’s largest dedicated health programme, under a thematic window on ‘Health, Biotech, Agriculture and Bioeconomy’.

While Recital 32 of the ECF regulation proposal (2025/555) explicitly acknowledges the need to address communicable diseases and NCDs through prevention and investment in the pharmaceutical and medical devices sector, the current proposal contains no dedicated Article for public health actions, and no explicit, ring-fenced financial envelope for health promotion and disease prevention [3]. Unless specific safeguards are introduced, prevention may no longer benefit from the dedicated programme objectives, budget lines, and performance indicators that make it possible to track investments, implementation, and outcomes. This would reduce the visibility and accountability of public health action within a broader competitiveness framework, weakening one of the key governance foundations of the European Health Union, even if public health actions continue under the new architecture.

The Commission’s stated rationale is administrative simplification and strategic coherence, based on the assumption that the functions of EU4Health can be preserved within a broader competitiveness framework. This assumption merits careful consideration from a public health governance perspective.

EU4Health (2021–27) was not only the EU’s largest health programme in financial terms, it established a dedicated governance architecture for public health prevention and health system collaboration. This included specific programme objectives, clearly delineated eligible actions, national focal points across MSs, and a regulatory requirement that at least 20% of programme expenditure support health promotion and disease prevention [4]. Importantly, these governance features created not only financial support but also accountability. Dedicated programme objectives, earmarked expenditure, and measurable indicators enabled transparent monitoring of investments and outcomes across MSs, thereby strengthening both democratic oversight and evidence-informed policymaking. Together, these features created the governance conditions under which cross-border, equity-oriented NCD prevention becomes operationally viable. The programme’s interim evaluation (November 2025) confirmed both its continued policy relevance and its strong performance record [5]. Notably, two long-term key performance indicators had already been exceeded by 2024: the target for the number of MSs implementing best practices in health promotion, disease prevention, and the reduction of health inequalities, and the target for patients diagnosed and treated through European Reference Networks [3, 5]. The evaluation further concludes that the structural challenges EU4Health was designed to address, including ageing populations, the rising NCD burden, and persistent health inequalities, remain acute and unresolved, underscoring the continued need for dedicated EU investment in prevention.

A central implementation instrument within EU4Health is the Joint Action (JA), through which MSs and European institutions collaboratively address shared health priorities, with activities co-funded by the EU and participating organizations. By pooling expertise from public health institutes and other organizations across the EU, JAs have generated unique EU added value that extends beyond individual projects. JAs provide a structured governance mechanism that enables MSs to address shared public health challenges through coordinated implementation rather than isolated interventions [6]. Their added value lies in fostering long-term institutional collaboration, mutual accountability, and collective responses to issues that cannot be effectively tackled by individual countries alone [6]. This model has supported action across key areas of European public health, including pandemic preparedness, antimicrobial resistance, cancer, rare diseases, and the prevention and management of NCDs.

Recent JAs, including the Joint Action on Cardiovascular Diseases (CVD) and Diabetes (JACARDI) and the Joint Action on Cancer and Other NCD Prevention (JA PreventNCD) are examples of how this broader governance model operates in practice. JACARDI involves 21 European countries and addresses CVD and type 2 diabetes as interrelated conditions sharing common risk factors, care pathways, and overlapping patient populations, spanning high-risk population screening, integrated care pathways, self-management, health literacy, and health data quality [7]. Equity is embedded structurally within its governance design through a dedicated framework and harmonized methodological approach [8]. JA PreventNCD includes 25 European countries and focuses on cancer and other NCDs. It simultaneously targets the structural drivers of disease at a societal level while implementing behavioural change strategies for high-risk individuals [9]. In both initiatives, pilot activities are designed as scalable and transferable interventions that can be integrated into national health systems rather than remaining isolated demonstrations of good practice. These examples demonstrate how JAs function as operational platforms for implementing European health priorities while strengthening institutional capacity, cross-country collaboration, and policy learning across MSs. Similar governance arrangements underpin implementation in many other areas of EU public health.

The inter-institutional process on the 2028–2034 MFF is now well advanced. The Commission proposal (COM(2025)571) sets out a €1.763 trillion budget (1.26% of EU GNI) and proposes a substantial simplification of the EU funding architecture, with the stated objective of increasing flexibility and aligning expenditure more closely with competitiveness and security priorities [2, 10]. While the Commission has indicated that current EU4Health activities are expected to continue within the new framework, the governance arrangements through which these activities would be financed, coordinated, and monitored have yet to be clearly defined. In particular, JAs are not explicitly retained as a dedicated implementation instrument in the current regulatory proposal, and their future role therefore remains uncertain. The European Parliament has advocated for a larger MFF budget [10] and emphasized the need to preserve transparency, democratic scrutiny, and expenditure traceability. It has also expressed concerns that reduced programme specificity and a more streamlined budget structure may weaken expenditure traceability and transparency, and could imply a greater role for Commission work programmes in implementation, with potential implications for parliamentary scrutiny. Within this evolving negotiation process, the operational continuity of EU4Health governance mechanisms, including JAs and other instruments supporting coordinated implementation, remains undecided.

From a public health standpoint, safeguarding the governance infrastructure for NCD prevention in the future MFF architecture requires three concrete conditions. First, the establishment of a dedicated and adequately protected financial envelope for health promotion and disease prevention within the ECF would help maintain the visibility of prevention and mitigate the risk of dilution within a broader competitiveness-oriented framework. Maintaining a reference level consistent with the minimum allocation established under EU4Health would provide continuity while recognizing prevention as a strategic investment rather than a discretionary expenditure. Second, maintaining a clear legal basis for JAs, or a functionally equivalent cross-border implementation mechanism, appears essential to ensure the continued operationalization of EU-level health strategies, including the EU Safe Hearts Plan and Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan. Without a dedicated implementation mechanism, the EU may retain ambitious health policy objectives while losing the institutional capacity needed to implement them consistently across MSs. Third, the continuation of a health-specific monitoring framework, with clearly defined objectives and measurable performance indicators, is necessary to support accountability, transparency, and evidence-based policy learning, as demonstrated by EU4Health’s own interim evaluation [5].

These governance features are essential to uphold Article 168 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU, which mandates a high level of human health protection across all Union policies.

The next MFF should recognize health promotion and disease prevention as strategic investments in Europe’s resilience, economic sustainability, and social cohesion, rather than treating them as discretionary expenditures within a broader competitiveness agenda. The central policy question is therefore not whether public health remains part of the future EU budget. Rather, it is whether the governance architecture that enables prevention to be financed, implemented, monitored, and evaluated will continue to exist. Protecting a ring-fenced budget for prevention, ensuring the continuation of JAs, or an equivalent cross-border implementation mechanism, and maintaining health-specific governance, monitoring, and accountability should be explicit priorities in the final MFF agreement. Without these safeguards, prevention may remain a stated political priority while progressively losing the visibility, traceability, and implementation capacity necessary to deliver measurable improvements in population health across MSs.
