Evaluating Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency testing across Europe to ensure diagnostic accuracy: an EARCO multicentre project
Authors: Joanna Chorostowska-Wynimko, Stefania Ottaviani, Magdalena Pelc, Malika Balduyck, Tomás P Carroll, Timm Greulich, N Gerard McElvaney, Marc Miravitlles, Francisco Rodríguez-Frías, Marie-Françoise Odou, Adriana Rozy, Susana Seixas, Gerard Orriols, Martina Veith, Farid Zerimech, Angelo Guido Corsico, Maria Cristina Monti, Marion Wencker, Ilaria Ferrarotti

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency (AATD) is a rare, underdiagnosed genetic disorder caused by pathogenic variants of the SERPINA1 gene, which can lead to reduced levels and/or dysfunctional forms of the Alpha-1 Antitrypsin (AAT) protein. Accurate AATD diagnosis is critical due to its implications for pulmonary and hepatic health. However, diagnostic practices vary across laboratories, particularly in the detection and reporting of rare variants. This study aimed to evaluate the consistency and reliability of AATD diagnostic testing across European laboratories through a non-commercial external quality assessment (EQA)-like initiative.MethodsSeven European laboratories participated in six assessment rounds between 2019 and 2021. Each round involved blinded analysis of serum and dried blood spot (DBS) samples using local standard operating procedures. Tests included AAT and C-reactive protein quantification, isoelectric focusing for AAT phenotyping, and genotyping/sequencing for common and rare SERPINA1 variants. Interrater agreement was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa statistic. Allele error rates were calculated for each variant and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare AAT concentrations across laboratories.ResultsFor common variants (M [normal], S [p.E288V], and Z [p.E366K]), interlaboratory concordance was high, with Fleiss’ kappa values indicating almost perfect agreement (0.85–1.00). Genotyping and sequencing error rates for these variants were zero. However, detection of rare variants (e.g., MProcida [p.L65P], MHeerlen [p.P393L], and MWurzburg [p.P393S]) was inconsistent, with error rates ranging from 0.14 to 0.29. Sequencing was more effective than phenotyping or simple genotyping for rare variant identification. AAT concentration measurements were generally consistent across laboratories, though DBS samples showed more variability. Reporting nomenclature also varied widely, highlighting the need for standardisation.ConclusionsThis initiative demonstrated strong interlaboratory agreement in detecting common AATD variants but revealed challenges in identifying and reporting rare variants. The findings support the need for SERPINA1 sequencing in AATD diagnostics and the adoption of standardised nomenclature. Background

Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency (AATD) is an autosomal codominant disorder caused by pathogenic alterations in the SERPINA1 gene encoding the Alpha-1 Antitrypsin (AAT) protein [1, 2], the main protease inhibitor (PI) acting in the pulmonary environment, where it protects lung tissue from degradation by serine proteases, especially neutrophil elastase (NE). AAT is also an acute-phase protein and it has been identified as one of the key regulators of immune and inflammatory responses in the lungs [3]. Pathogenic SERPINA1 variants result in reduced serum levels and/or functional deficiency of AAT, giving rise to a wide range of genotypes and clinical phenotypes, where the disease spectrum depends on the molecular pathomechanism(s) of each specific SERPINA1 variant [2, 4]. The most common SERPINA1 deficiency variants according to the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) nomenclature are: NM_000295.5:c.863 A > T, p.Glu288Val (PI*S); and NM_000295.5:c.1096G > A, p.Glu366Lys (PI*Z) [5]. However, many other less frequent (rare) pathogenic variants have also been reported in relation to AATD [2, 6], owing to the evolving methods and accurate genetic testing [7, 8], as well as improved computational tools of pathogenicity prediction [9–11].

AATD is frequently under- or misdiagnosed due to difficult clinical diagnostics, variable phenotypic expression, a symptomatic spectrum shared with other lung diseases, late-onset of pulmonary manifestation, and lack of routine laboratory diagnostics in patients with obstructive pulmonary disease [12–14]. While the prevalence is high and estimated at 1/1600–1/5000 (ORPHA:60) in Western Europe and the United States (US) [15], over 90% of individuals with AATD remain unrecognised [12, 16, 17]. Diagnosing AATD currently involves both quantitative and qualitative analyses, mainly relying on the identification of low serum AAT levels measured by nephelometry or immunoturbidimetry methods [18], and either detection of an AAT deficient variant by isoelectric focusing (IEF; protein phenotyping) [19] and/or biallelic pathogenic variant genotyping using a variety of molecular methods [20–22]. These comprise real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based [23, 24] or Luminex-based assays [25, 26], targeting either the most common (PI*S and PI*Z) or other known genetic variants. The sequencing-based analyses of the whole coding and non-coding DNA sequence of SERPINA1, utilising Sanger or Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) technology [8, 10, 11, 27], is not yet as widely used as expected.

As laboratory test results direct important clinical decisions, they must be accurate, reliable, and presented in a clear and precise, but comprehensive manner. This is ensured by implementing a good quality management system, including standard operating procedures (SOPs), test validations, and internal quality control. Participation in an external quality assessment (EQA) scheme is important to ensure accuracy, reliability, and clear presentation of laboratory results. EQAs enable objective evaluation and improvement of the quality of routine laboratory practice based on the performance result [28]. Moreover, EQAs have educational value, not only contributing to the incorporation of current guidelines and modern techniques in clinical diagnostics, but also in effecting their continuous expansion and refinement. Based on EQA outcome, a laboratory may minimise error rates by eliminating some pitfalls, such as systematic and technical flaws from testing methods or incorrect data analysis processes, and can also learn and improve their methodology and/or important aspects of result interpretation [29]. However, rare disease testing does not align with traditional EQA frameworks.

In this study, we conducted a non-commercial EQA-like initiative across seven European laboratories that regularly perform diagnostic assessments for AATD. Laboratories performed test diagnoses on a series of samples, following their local SOPs and diagnostic algorithms, and the concordance of results between sites was assessed to understand the consistency and reliability of diagnostic practices across the laboratories. The objective was to investigate the detection of AAT variants across multiple laboratories and identify whether improvements in diagnostic methods, as well as the interpretation and presentation of the molecular results, are required.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a non-commercial EQA-like initiative within the European Alpha‑1 Antitrypsin Research Collaboration (EARCO; a clinical research collaboration of the European Respiratory Society) [16]. Given the rare-disease context, this exercise was organised as a collaborative, educational initiative among specialist reference laboratories, with EQA‑like methodology that incorporated interlaboratory comparison principles, rather than as a formal accredited EQA programme delivered by an external provider. The objective was to assess interlaboratory consistency under routine diagnostic conditions and to identify areas for methodological and reporting harmonisation in AATD diagnostics.

Seven laboratories (Labs A–G) from centres participating in EARCO, each from different European countries, took part in this initiative to determine the level of consistency among laboratories that routinely perform diagnostic assessments for AATD. Laboratory identities are anonymised to maintain focus on the consistency of results rather than the relative performance of each centre. A questionnaire, concerning the general activities of each laboratory, including quantitative tests, qualitative tests, and general laboratory organisation, was originally conducted in 2018, and updated in 2022, to summarise laboratory activities between 2013 and 2021 (Table 1).

Pilot validations (Rounds 1 and 2) involved Lab A and Lab B alternately acting as the unblinded referral centre responsible for sample collection, shipping, and report verification. A fully independent third‑party validation of results was not feasible, as no external referral centre or certified reference panel currently exists that encompasses the full AATD diagnostic pathway, particularly for rare SERPINA1 variants. In Round 1 (January 2019), samples were shipped from Lab A to Lab B, while Round 2 (May 2019) was from Lab B to Lab A. Subsequent Rounds 3–6 (August 2019–November 2021) involved up to seven participating laboratories (Lab G taking part in Rounds 5–6, only), performing analyses in accordance with their routine SOPs and diagnostic algorithms. Participating laboratories reported results for verification to the allocated reference laboratory for that round within 6 weeks from samples being received.

Sample homogeneity and stability were assessed during the pilot validation rounds. Complete concordance for phenotyping and genotyping, together with highly comparable serum AAT concentrations, demonstrated adequate sample homogeneity and stability under the storage and transport conditions used. Nevertheless, the concordant results observed across subsequent rounds further support the suitability of the sample materials for this study.

In keeping with the validation algorithm, each laboratory was provided with a report of each of round (for Rounds 3–6), including individual outcomes and the blinded results of other participating laboratories. Positive feedback and some specific individual requirements were noted for the improved organisation of subsequent rounds. In Rounds 5 and 6, samples carrying rare variants were included.

It should be noted that SOPs and diagnostic algorithms varied between laboratories. As different diagnostic algorithms are employed by the participating laboratories, reflecting routine clinical practice in rare disease diagnostics, some laboratories may not report results for techniques not included in their routine diagnostic algorithm. Assessment methods

Each assessment round consisted of five (Rounds 3–5), or six (Round 6), clearly labelled samples collected as both 1 mL serum samples (stored and shipped at − 80 °C) and dried blood spot (DBS) samples (stored and shipped at room temperature). Samples were pre-analysed by the laboratory of collection (Lab A or B), and analysis consisted of AAT and C-reactive protein (CRP) quantification, phenotyping by IEF, and genotyping for PI*S and PI*Z alleles and/or a multiple allele AAT assay (Luminex) [25, 26], or SERPINA1 sequencing. Analyses were performed both in serum and DBS samples. Here, we have utilised standard HGVS nomenclature at the first mention of each variant. Patients from Labs A and B provided their approval by written informed consent for the use of anonymised materials for research.

Statistical analysis

Although the primary purpose of this non-commercial EQA-like initiative was the assessment of individual laboratory results against expected findings, measures of interlaboratory agreement were applied descriptively as a secondary outcome to characterise overall consistency in AATD diagnostics, particularly for rare variants. Quantitative variables, measures of AAT and CRP concentration, were described by medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while for categorical variables, phenotyping and genotyping, absolute and relative frequencies were calculated. Plots were used to compare, among laboratories: (a) the relative frequency distribution of each phenotype/genotype identified in the samples analysed, and (b) the distribution of AAT or CRP sample concentrations. Comparisons were performed separately for the pilot validation rounds (Rounds 1 and 2) and subsequent rounds (Rounds 3–6), considering each round separately or all samples globally. Individual samples are reported as ‘Round.Sample’ (i.e., the second sample in Round 3 is reported as sample ‘3.2’).

The Fleiss kappa statistic measure of interrater agreement was applied to qualitative AATD phenotyping/genotyping analyses [30]. Kappa was scaled to be 0 when the amount of agreement is what would be expected if observed by chance; 1 was perfect agreement. For intermediate values, the following interpretations were used: <0.00, poor; 0.01–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; 0.81–0.99, almost perfect agreement. The test statistic Z related to the kappa coefficient shows how different the expected proportion and the observed proportion of agreement used to calculate the kappa statistic are; it could only be applied when there were the same number of raters for each sample of a round.

In addition, for the AATD qualitative analyses, the allele error rate was calculated for each specific allele where there was genotyping or sequencing data, on a per round basis. Allele error rate was defined as the ratio between the number of samples in which a different allele was observed, with respect to the allele that was expected, divided by the total number of alleles that were expected. The expected allele was determined using Lab A and Lab B allele results as reference.

For AAT sample concentrations, to assess the differences between laboratories, Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed. The significance threshold for these analyses was p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 17; StataCorp LP, College Station, TX).

Pilot validation: Rounds 1 and 2

Serum AAT concentrations, IEF phenotyping, and genotyping were performed by both Lab A and Lab B for all five samples in Rounds 1 and 2 (Fig. 1). Serum AAT concentrations were similar between the two laboratories; however, the differences in concentrations were greater when concentrations were above 120 mg/dL (Fig. 1a). One sample for Lab B, sample 2.5, was below the detectable range. Serum AAT concentrations were generally similar to DBS concentrations (Additional Table 1); however, in most samples, the recorded concentration in DBS was lower than the concentration recorded in serum. For both phenotyping and genotyping, there was 100% concordance between the two reference laboratories (Fig. 1b).

Assessment: rounds 3–6

AAT concentrations

Serum AAT concentration measurements were performed by Labs A–F according to their SOPs. Lab G, which was only included in Rounds 5 and 6, does not report AAT concentrations and therefore, no data are included. Serum AAT concentrations were plotted per laboratory for each round (Fig. 2), and median and IQR were compared (Additional Table 2). The distribution of serum AAT concentrations was not significantly different by laboratory for each round (p = 0.65, p = 0.98, p = 0.69, p = 0.90, respectively). Measured concentrations were closely clustered between laboratories, with the exception of data from Lab F in samples 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 (Additional Fig. 2). In general, the results were consistent with reported ranges of AAT concentrations for respective genotypes (Additional Fig. 2).

Phenotyping

All laboratories used IEF for protein phenotyping to detect AAT variants (Fig. 3). In general, for the common variants M (normal), S (p.E288V), and Z (p.E366K), reported phenotypes were consistent between laboratories and with the sample genotype. For rare variants, phenotyping was less successful. For example, the MProcida (p.L65P) variant in sample 5.5 was not detected by any laboratory; it was detected as the M variant, according to electrophoretic mobility. In sample 6.5, the MHeerlen (p.P393L) variant was only detected as a ‘rare variant’ by one laboratory. Similarly, for sample 6.6, the MWurzburg (p.P393S) variant was only correctly identified by Lab A.

Genotyping and sequencing

Detection of AAT variants by molecular genotyping and sequencing methods were performed as required by the SOP of the respective laboratory (Table 1). For these analyses, testing could identify separate alleles. In accordance with the phenotyping analyses, the molecular methods were consistent between laboratories for identification of the common PI*M, PI*S, and PI*Z alleles (Fig. 4). Detection of rare variants was more successful with the molecular genotyping and sequencing methods than protein phenotyping. According to testing methods, the PI*MProcida and PI*MWurzburg alleles were each only detected by one laboratory using genotyping methods; sequencing was required by the other laboratories to detect these variants. Sequencing methods were only used in Rounds 5 and 6, and by a subset of laboratories, where required by their SOP (Tables 1 and 2). All sequencing results are summarised in Additional Table 5.

When all phenotyping, genotyping, and sequencing analyses were combined (Additional Fig. 3), we obtained perfect agreement among laboratories regarding common genotypes. Sample 3.1 (PI*SZ) was identified as PI*MZ by Lab D due to a non-conformity regarding sample identity prior to shipment. By contrast, the following rare genotypes were not identified correctly: PI*M/MProcida (Sample 5.5, Labs D and G); PI*M/F (Sample 6.2, Lab C); PI*Z/MHeerlen (Sample 6.5, Lab D); PI*S/MWurzburg (Sample 6.6, Labs C and D).

Reporting nomenclature for variants identified by genotype and sequencing methods varied by laboratory. There was substantial variation in the terminology used, making comparison between results challenging. An example of the variation in reporting nomenclature is highlighted in Additional Table 6.

Concordance analysis for phenotyping, genotyping, and sequencing

The reliability of agreement for phenotyping, genotyping, and sequencing between laboratories, calculated as the level of interrater agreement (Table 3), was perfect in Rounds 3–5 (Fleiss k coefficient = 1), but decreased to substantial agreement for Round 6 (Fleiss k coefficient, ranges 0.61–0.77) due to difficulties in the interpretation of rare genotypes. In Round 6, three of the genotypes included rare variants; F (p.R247C), MHeerlen and MWurzburg. Note, although interrater agreement between laboratories may be 1.00, this does not necessarily indicate the sample was correctly identified; in Sample 5.5, no laboratories identified the MProcida variant via phenotyping, but as all laboratories reported the same result, the interrater agreement for phenotyping of Round 5 is still 1.00.

To understand the rate at which different alleles were misidentified, the genotyping/sequencing error rate was also calculated (Table 4). When genotyping or sequencing were performed, the error rate for the common alleles (M, S, Z) was zero; for the rare variants (F, MHeerlen, MWurzburg, MProcida), the error rate was low, but not zero (range 0.14–0.29), indicating that in most circumstances the allele was correctly identified.

A non-conformity was reported on sample 3.1 sent to Lab D, so this sample was eliminated from the analysis. With this elimination, the overall non-conformity rate regarding the samples sent in this assessment was 0.64%.

Discussion

As laboratory results directly influence clinical decisions, this study was conducted to assess the consistency and concordance between laboratories that routinely test AATD samples. The initial verification rounds between Lab A and Lab B provided confidence that samples could be successfully transported between sites for assessment without affecting sample quality. There was 100% concordance between laboratories with regards to phenotyping and genotyping analyses, indicating that the received samples were stable and that analysis methods were accurate and appropriate. The serum AAT concentrations were similar, albeit not identical, between laboratories. Variations between samples were similar to the variability experienced in the subsequent rounds of the assessment. DBS AAT concentrations were generally similar to serum sample concentrations, indicating DBS samples were also stable. In general, DBS samples are considered stable within 14 days from collection [31].

The main outcome from the EQA-like assessment in Rounds 3–6 was that for common AAT variants, both phenotyping and genotyping were successful. For rare AAT variants, there were differing levels of success in identification using phenotyping and genotyping or sequencing. For the F and MHeerlen variants, 4 out of 5 of the non-reference laboratories successfully identified these with phenotyping. Whereas, for MWurzburg and MProcida, phenotyping was either mostly, or wholly, unsuccessful, and sequencing was the principal method for successful identification; for which the allele error rate has been previously shown to be low [7]. This was also reflected in the concordance analysis, where in Round 6, the Fleiss k coefficient decreased to substantial agreement (ranges 0.61–0.77), due to the inclusion of three rare variants; F, MHeerlen, and MWurzburg. These challenges in detecting rare variants need to be addressed. For the most reliable identification of rare variants, sequencing of the SERPINA1 gene is strongly recommended. Simple allele-specific genotyping is limited, and in the case of a heterozygous result, it does not exclude the possibility that another rare pathogenic variant is present. However, it should be noted that there were still two errors when sequencing was performed; in both cases using Sanger sequencing with ‘DNA’. Therefore, the methods and/or interpretation of sequencing may also be relevant. The interrater agreement analysis also highlights the importance of genotyping and sequencing methods.

For the measures of serum AAT concentration in Rounds 3–6, measures were generally consistent between laboratories for any given sample and the distribution of serum AAT concentrations, by round, were not significantly different. For common variants, most values were consistent with previously described ranges [32, 33]. The main exceptions to this were samples 3.1, 3.2, and 3.3 for Lab F, the first three samples tested.

Where variations were observed, it is important to identify the methodological sources and consequences of these variations to improve consistency. Some laboratory SOPs depend on a decisional cut-off of AAT concentrations for the next step in the diagnostic pathway. At present, there are samples that may be on different sides of this value, depending on the laboratory performing the analysis. For instance, if a 1.2 g/L concentration was applied to the rare genotype Pi*MF in sample 6.2, in four laboratories the sample would be above the threshold, and in two laboratories it would be below the threshold. Note, the F variant is a clinically relevant, dysfunctional variant of AAT, which results in reduced inhibitory activity of the protein, but does not cause serum AAT deficiency [34, 35].

For DBS concentration measures, there were a number of samples where levels of AAT could not be detected. Variability in DBS concentrations were greater than for serum samples. Assessment of DBS AAT concentration would benefit from further monitoring, and potentially the need to refine/standardise the sample handling protocol and/or methodology applied in some of the participating laboratories [36]. The failures in these circumstances may be preanalytical, as DBS samples require rapid referral with measurement within 14 days of sample collection [31]. As there are no universally validated DBS-specific diagnostic cut-offs and results can vary depending on assay standardisation across laboratories, DBS AAT concentrations should ideally overlap those established for plasma AAT concentrations and be interpreted primarily as a screening measure rather than used for definitive clinical decision-making. Measures of serum CRP concentration were also broadly consistent, where detected. However, there were too few samples in which successful measurements were made to perform statistical measures of consistency. Only a subset of laboratories measured CRP, despite AATD guidelines stating that CRP levels should be measured with serum AAT to exclude falsely increased AAT due to inflammation [37]. Without concurrent inflammatory markers such as CRP, serum AAT concentrations cannot be accurately interpreted, as inflammation may mask underlying deficiency [38].

In parallel with biochemical assessments, the interpretation of genetic findings in AATD also requires standardisation. Commonly used nomenclature for SERPINA1 variants was introduced before the molecular characterisation of the gene, based on differentiating the isoelectric point of AAT protein variants (using IEF), where the PI* prefix describes the protein inhibitor system instead of the gene name [19]. However, utilising molecular methods for detecting pathogenic variants currently requires standardised genetic nomenclature established by the HGVS [5], as well as variant characterisation according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology criteria [39, 40]. This would ensure worldwide consistency of genetic reports and avoidance of confusion and redundancy caused by legacy descriptions of particular variants (e.g., reporting only amino acid changes, despite different possible nucleotide substitutions, using multiple historical names for the same variant) [6, 41].

For the genotyping and sequencing results, the reporting nomenclature mostly followed the non-conventional or historic nomenclature, and the standardised HGVS guidelines for molecular variant nomenclature were rarely followed. This highlights the need to introduce current unified reporting standards into AATD diagnostic reports, in order to increase precision and informativeness, and harmonise reporting in Europe. It is recommended that for AATD genotype reporting, the current unified variant nomenclature guidelines should be followed. On the other hand, it needs to be considered that interpretation of the genetic language used by the HGVS may be challenging for pulmonologists and general practitioners, while the medical consequences of a diagnosis must be readily understood. The correct diagnosis is of paramount importance to patients with genetic diseases, as it has significant impact on treatment and lifestyle choices. The compromise in presenting genetic results may comprise reporting of both unified and customary nomenclature for all detected variants.

The EQA process is an important tool to assess the quality of routine diagnostic methods; however, no EQA scheme currently exists for AATD diagnostics. Consequently, independent external validation was not possible. Therefore, this study adopted a non-commercial EQA-like approach as a pilot exercise, with voluntary participation. The inclusion of five laboratories, in addition to the two original reference laboratories, provides a breadth of diagnostic environments in which to assess the consistency and concordance of AAT detection in Europe. Consistent with recognised distinctions between formal, provider‑based EQA schemes and other quality‑assessment formats, this initiative was not intended to replicate a full EQA programme. Given the rarity of AATD, the limited availability of well‑characterised samples, the small number of expert laboratories, and the absence of accredited AATD‑specific EQA programmes, key elements of large‑scale EQA or interlaboratory comparison frameworks, such as target‑value assignment, formal scoring, and accreditation‑linked performance evaluation, were not applicable to this study. Nevertheless, this EQA‑like approach provides valuable insight into interlaboratory consistency and identifies important opportunities for methodological and reporting harmonisation in rare‑disease diagnostics. The combination of independent testing by the two coordinating laboratories, complete concordance during the pilot validation rounds, long-term successful participation in AATD testing, and comprehensive pre-distribution sample characterisation provided the most robust and practical approach currently available in this setting.

The initial survey outcomes, and the outcomes of the assessment rounds, highlight the diversity of techniques used by participating laboratories. The study involved a limited number of participating laboratories with small sample sizes per round, and several laboratories employed heterogeneous SOPs. In some cases, laboratories outsourced specific analytical steps without full methodological transparency. The study was not designed to attribute discordant results to individual techniques or laboratory workflows but rather to reflect the performance of complete diagnostic pathways as currently implemented in routine clinical practice in AATD. The outcomes could be enhanced by the inclusion of further laboratories and further testing samples; however, the seven laboratories that participated did provide an informative overview of AATD diagnostics across Europe. Participating laboratories were experienced in AATD testing and these data highlight differences in outcomes, particularly where there are rare variants. In general, however, where common variants were evaluated, the consistency of the outcomes between laboratories was very encouraging.

Conclusions

In summary, these results showed an exemplary interlaboratory consistency and concordance in the detection of common AAT variants; however, identification and reporting of rare variants is complex, and even specialist laboratories do not achieve a correct diagnosis for all samples. Incorporation of SERPINA1 sequencing into the diagnostic pathway is therefore recommended to enable robust detection of rare molecular variants and an accurate assessment of genotype. Results suggest that AATD diagnostics should be performed by dedicated and validated reference laboratories, in a centralised form, ensuring proper handling of samples and accurate reporting. Additionally, AATD genotype reporting may benefit from adopting a dual-reporting approach, presenting customary nomenclature alongside standardised HGVS guidelines. This offers a practical compromise that may support the accurate interpretation of genetic findings and their clinical implications, ultimately enhancing patient care. Taken together, these findings highlight how differences in routine diagnostic pathways, rather than individual laboratory performance alone, can influence rare variant detection and reporting in AATD.
