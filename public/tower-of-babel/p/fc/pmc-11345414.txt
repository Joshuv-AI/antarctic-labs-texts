The impact of cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math and science anxieties with or without controlling general anxiety
Authors: Ahmed M Megreya, Ahmed A Al-Emadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is well-established that general anxiety associates with the lower use of adaptive emotion regulation and the higher use of maladaptive emotion regulation. However, no study has previously investigated the impact of cognitive emotion regulation on academic anxieties. Using a sample of secondary school students (N = 391), this study examined the impact of cognitive emotion regulation on math and science anxieties. Math anxiety showed stronger correlations with adaptive than maladaptive emotion regulation, whereas general anxiety showed stronger correlations with maladaptive than adaptive emotion regulation. Hierarchical regression analyses showed that math anxiety was associated with the high uses of acceptance, rumination and other-blame and the low uses of positive reappraisal and putting into perspective. However, with controlling science and general anxieties, math anxiety was associated with the high use of rumination and the low use of positive reappraisal. In contrast, science anxiety was associated with the high uses of acceptance and other-blame and the low use of positive reappraisal. Importantly, however, with controlling math and general anxieties, those science anxiety associations did not remain. Accordingly, these results might provide important insights for the specificity, etiology, and intervention of math anxiety.Keywords: Cognitive emotion regulation, Positive reappraisal, Rumination, Math anxiety, Science anxiety, General anxietySubject terms: Human behaviour, Scientific data

Introduction

Emotion regulation stands at the core of contemporary literatures in several fields of psychology and neurophysiology1. It refers to the attempts, processes, or strategies that an individual makes to influence “which emotions one has, when one has them, and how one experiences and expresses these emotions”2,p5, regardless of whether they are cognitive or behavioral, deliberate or automatic, explicit or implicit, antecedent-focused or response-focused, and adaptive or maladaptive3. Adaptive emotion regulation is linked with physical and psychosocial well-being whereas maladaptive emotion regulation is associated with physical and psychosocial ill-being and difficulties in daily life behaviors4. The present study sought to explore the impact of a range of adaptive and maladaptive emotion regulation strategies on math and science anxieties.

Emotion regulation

A range of different models have theorized how individuals regulate their positive and negative emotions2. One of the most accepted frameworks is the information-processing model of emotion regulation5,6. This framework was built on the modal model of emotion generation that describes four sequential stages of triggering an emotion including encountering an emotional situation, attending to it, giving it an appraisal, and eliciting an experiential, behavioral, or neurophysiological response2. According to this information-processing model, each emotion generation stage is a potential target for regulation using five different families of emotion regulation processes or strategies. These include situation selection (e.g., approaching or avoiding a situation based on its anticipated emotional effects), situation modification (e.g., trying to change the situation), attentional deployment (e.g., deploying the attention toward or away from the situation), cognitive change (e.g., re-evaluating or re-interpreting the value of the situation), and response modulation (e.g., adjusting the emotions associated with the situation)2. In addition, Gross (1998) identified two broad classes of emotion regulation strategies, which are antecedent-focused (regulation before an emotion is generated) or response-focused (the inhibition of emotional response tendencies once the emotion has been already triggered)5. Cognitive reappraisal and behavioral suppression are among the most investigated antecedent-focused versus response-focused strategies7. Cognitive reappraisal refers to the reinterpretation of an emotion-evoking situation to change its emotional impacts whereas behavioral suppression refers to the inhabitation of ongoing emotion-expressive behaviors5.

Another well-established framework focused “purely” on cognitive emotion regulation, which refers to the general cognitive styles or thoughts that individuals use in order to manage and control their emotions during or after experiencing a stressful situation8. Namely, Garnefski et al. (2001) identified nine conceptually-independent cognitive emotion regulation strategies, including self-blame (i.e., the thoughts of blaming oneself for what has been experienced), acceptance (i.e., the thoughts of resigning what has happened), rumination (i.e., constantly thinking about the feelings and thoughts associated with a negative event), positive refocusing (i.e., thinking of other pleasant matters instead of the actual event), refocus on planning (i.e., thinking on potential steps to deal with negative events), positive reappraisal (i.e., thinking of attaching a positive meaning to the event in terms of personal growth), putting Into perspective (i.e., the thoughts of playing down the seriousness of a negative event as compared to other events), catastrophizing (i.e., over-emphasizing the negativity of the events), and other-blame (i.e., the thoughts of blaming others for what one has experienced)8. These nine strategies could be collapsed into two broader factors, which include adaptive versus maladaptive emotion regulation8. Females were found to score higher than males in rumination, positive refocusing, and catastrophizing9.

Math anxiety

Math anxiety is a world-wide academic anxiety that might affect students’ mental health and career choices. It refers to the “feelings of tension and anxiety that interfere with the manipulation of numbers and the solving of mathematical problems in a wide variety of ordinary life and academic situations”10,p.551. A negative relationship between math anxiety and math performance is well-documented but its causal direction is debatable11. On the one hand, math anxiety is assumed to deteriorate math performance12. On the other hand, poor math performance is thought to trigger math anxiety13. A reciprocal interaction between math anxiety and math performance was also proposed11. A range of different reasons are associated with math anxiety including biological (e.g., heredity), educational (e.g., inappropriate teaching or scholastic experiences), cognitive (e.g., working memory deficits), and neurological (e.g., less activity in the frontal and parietal brain areas) factors14. Based on the ninth quartile, high math-anxious students are defined as those who score above “30” on the Modified-Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale15. Using this cut-off score, a large-scale study of the prevalence of math anxiety in Qatar found that 20% of students in government schools from Grade 7 to Grade 12 were highly math-anxious, with a higher proportion of females than males16. Indeed, females are more math-anxious than males17–19 and this gender difference might be explained by gender stereotyping, in which math is traditionally viewed as a male domain20.

Science anxiety

Science anxiety refers to “a debilitating combination of fearful negative emotion and cognition in the context of science learning21,p.432. Mallow (2006) discussed different factors that might explain science anxiety such as negative myth and stereotypes about science and the deficiency of analytical thinking22. In addition, students with high levels of science anxiety reported inappropriate science experiences in high schools23. Science anxiety was also associated with low self-efficacy toward science24. Consistently, a negative correlation between science anxiety and science performance was reported25. Similar to the association between math anxiety and math performance, the direction of the link between science anxiety and science performance was not clear as poor science performance might elicit science anxiety; science anxiety might lead to poor science achievement; or science anxiety and science achievement might reciprocally interact with each other25. Females showed higher levels of science anxiety than males24–26 and this gender difference might be explained by gender stereotyping where science is considered as a male domain22,27.

Specificity of math and science anxieties

A range of different evidence indicates that math and science anxieties are distinct constructs. For math anxiety, some studies reported stronger positive correlations between different math anxiety scales than between math anxiety and general or test anxiety14. In addition, math anxiety was negatively associated with math achievement even when general and test anxieties were controlled, whereas general and test anxieties did not associate with math achievement when math anxiety was controlled19. Furthermore, math anxiety was associated with the activation of some brain areas that linked with emotion and arithmetic processing28. The specificity of science anxiety has received little attention. For example, it was negatively associated with science achievement even when general and test were controlled whereas general and test anxieties did not associate with science achievement when science anxiety was controlled25. In addition, confirmatory factor analysis showed that science learning anxiety was associated with science achievement but not with general and test anxieties25.

Nevertheless, shared variances among math, science and general anxieties were noticed. For example, many studies consistently reported moderate positive correlations between math and science anxieties (r = 0.54,29), between math and general anxieties (e.g., r = 0.35,30), and between science and general anxieties (e.g., r = 0.45,25). In addition, the relationship between math anxiety and math performance weakened when general anxiety was controlled18,19. Further, math anxiety was influenced by the genetic and nonshared environmental factors that were associated with general anxiety31. Therefore, the present study has taken into consideration these shared variances among math, science, and general anxieties.

Current study

The present study aimed to examine the impact of cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math and science anxieties with or without controlling general anxiety. A large body of studies have previously investigated the associations between emotion regulation and general anxiety4. For example, meta-analysis studies showed that general anxiety was associated with the lower use of some adaptive emotion regulation strategies (problem solving, cognitive reappraisal, and acceptance) and the higher use of some maladaptive emotion regulation strategies (suppression, avoidance, and rumination) in adolescents32, with larger effect sizes for maladaptive than adaptive strategies4. Studies examining the associations between cognitive emotion regulation strategies and general anxiety have reported that general anxiety was associated with the higher use of self-blame, rumination, and catastrophizing and the lower use of positive reappraisal33–35. In addition, some studies have found that general anxiety was associated with the higher use of acceptance36, other-blame35–37 and the lower use of refocus on planning35,37, positive refocusing35,38, and putting into perspective35. Importantly, however, the associations between emotion regulation and math/science anxieties have received little attention, and the results of the few existing studies were mixed. For example, a negative association between stress reappraisal and math anxiety was reported39. However, math anxiety did not correlate with cognitive reappraisal, expressive suppression, or emotion regulation difficulties40. No study has previously examined the influence of cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math and science anxieties41. However, keeping in mind the associations between general anxiety and emotion regulation (for meta-analyses see4,32) and the shared variances between general anxiety and math/science anxiety19,25, it was expected that math and science anxieties might be negatively associated with adaptive emotion regulation whereas they might be positively associated with maladaptive emotion regulation. In addition, it was expected that these associations might be modified with controlling the shared variances among math, science, and general anxieties.

Method

Participants

A total of 391 students (187 females and 204 males) in eight government secondary schools (four for females and four for males) in Qatar volunteered to participate in this study. All participants were students in Grade 10 and their ages ranged from 15 to 17 (with a mean of 16 years and 4 months SD). This sample represented 23.1% of the total student population in grade 10 in those eight schools (N = 1695). The Ministry of Education and Higher Education in Qatar, along with Qatar University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) committee, which required written consents from all participants, approved data collection.

Questionnaires

(1) The Modified-Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale17

This 9-item math-anxiety questionnaire consisted of two subscales measuring Learning Math Anxiety, which consists of five items (e.g., “Listening to the teacher talk for a long time in math”), and Math Evaluation Anxiety, which consists of four items (e.g., “Taking a math test”). In addition, a total math anxiety score represents the summation of these two subscales. Using a 5-point Likert ranking scale ranging from 1 (low anxiety) to 5 (high anxiety), students were asked to rate how anxious they would feel during a range of different situations of math learning and evaluation. Confirmatory factor analyses confirmed the two-factor structure of this scale, with good-to-adequate internal reliabilities for the three math anxiety scores that ranged from 0.77 to 0.93 across different samples of students17,19.

(2) The Abbreviated Science Anxiety Scale25

This scale has been adjusted from the Modified-Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale17 by replacing “math” environments with “science” contexts. It measures learning science anxiety using five items (e.g., “Listening to the teacher talk for a long time in science”) and science evaluation anxiety using four items (e.g., “Taking a science test”). A total science anxiety score represents the summation of all items. Using a 5-point Likert ranking scale ranging from 1 (low anxiety) to 5 (high anxiety), participants were asked to rate how anxious they would feel during specific situations of science learning and evaluation. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the two-factor structure of this scale, with good-to-adequate internal reliabilities for the three science anxiety scores that ranged from 0.77 to 0.89 across different samples of students25.

(3) General Anxiety (Personality Inventory for DSM-542).

General anxiety was measured by the Anxiousness subscale of the Personality Inventory for DSM-542,43 that refers to the “feelings of nervousness, tenseness, or panic in reaction to diverse situations; frequent worry about the negative effects of past unpleasant experiences and future negative possibilities; feeling fearful and apprehensive about uncertainty; expecting the worst to happen”44,p.481. This subscale consists of nine items (e.g., “I worry a lot about terrible things that might happen”). Each item requires participants to describe themselves using a 4-point Likert-type scale (0 “very false or often false,” 1 “Sometimes or Somewhat false,” 2 “Sometimes or Somewhat True,” and 3 “very true or often true”). Anxiousness is scored by averaging participants’ responses across all items. Notably, this trait-level general anxiety scale has been previously utilized in the original studies, where the Arabic versions of the Modified-Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale19 and the Abbreviated Science Anxiety Scale25 were validated. In addition, good-to-adequate internal reliabilities were reported for this subscale (0.85 and 0.87) using the same population of students19,25 that was used in the present study.

(4) Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire: Short version45

This 18-item scale was developed from the original version of the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire8,46, which consists of 36 items measuring nine cognitive emotion regulation strategies. These strategies include self-blame (e.g., “I feel that I am the one who is responsible for what has happened”), acceptance (e.g., “I think that I have to accept the situation”), rumination (e.g., “I am preoccupied with what I think and feel about what I have experienced”), positive refocusing (e.g., “I think of something nice instead of what has happened”), refocus on planning (e.g., “I think about a plan of what I can do best”), positive reappraisal (e.g., “I think that I can become a stronger person as a result of what has happened”), putting into perspective (e.g., “I tell myself that there are worse things in life”), catastrophizing (e.g., “I keep thinking about how terrible it is what I have experienced”), and other-blame (e.g., “I feel that others are responsible for what has happened”)8. Each strategy was measured using four items, which require participants to describe how they cope with negative events using a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Garnefski and Kraaij (2006) developed a short version of this questionnaire by reducing the number of items in each strategy from four to two, keeping all of the nine strategies45. Confirmatory factor analyses confirmed the nine-factor structure of the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, with good-to-adequate internal reliabilities for the nine strategies that ranged from 0.68 to 0.85 across different samples45,47.

Procedure

Eight government secondary schools (four for females and four for males) were randomly selected in four geographically-different cities in Qatar. In each school, two classes in Grade 10 were randomly selected. The questionnaires were administered in groups during class attendance, and administration lasted for approximately 30 min. All methods were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of Qatar University IRB committee and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) along with 95% confidence intervals were reported. A series of independent sample t-tests were conducted to examine gender differences in all variables, reporting Cohen’s d effect sizes for each comparison. In addition, a series of Pearson Coefficients tests were used to examine the correlations between the three types of anxiety and the cognitive emotion regulation strategies for females and males separately. The correlations between the three types of anxiety and adaptive vs. maladaptive emotion regulation combined factors were examined for females and males separately. Adaptive emotion regulation factor refers to the summation of four adaptive strategies (positive refocusing, planning, positive reappraisal, and putting into perspective). Acceptance was excluded as it showed positive correlations with the three types of anxiety. In contrast, maladaptive emotion regulation combined factor refers to the summation of the four maladaptive strategies (self-blame, rumination, catastrophizing, and other-blame). Furthermore, a total of 8 hierarchical multiple regression analyses were performed to investigate the impacts of cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math and science anxieties. These hierarchical models included gender in the first step, controlling variables (science anxiety, general anxiety, or both) in the second step, and the nine cognitive emotion regulation strategies in the third step. In order to establish the baseline of associations, two hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted using math or science anxiety as a dependent variable while gender and cognitive emotion regulation strategies were used as independent variables (method = enter). Then, to control the shared variance between math and science anxieties, two hierarchical multiple regression analyses were carried on one form of topic anxiety (math or science anxiety) as a dependent variable while gender, the other form of topic anxiety, and cognitive emotion regulation strategies were used as independent variables (method = enter). After that, to control the shared variances between math and general anxieties and between science and general anxieties, two hierarchical multiple regression analyses were applied on math or science anxiety as a dependent variable while gender, general anxiety, and cognitive emotion regulation strategies were used as independent variables (method = enter). Finally, to control the shared variances among math, science, and general anxiety, two hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted on one form of topic anxiety as a dependent variable while gender, the other form of academic anxiety, general anxiety, and cognitive emotion regulation strategies were used as independent variables (method = enter). For each multiple regression analysis, we report the model (F, df, and p-value), R-squared (R2), standardized coefficients (Beta, β), t-score with p-value, and partial correlations (rpartial). Notably, these analyses converged with the models used by Garnefski and Kraaij (2018) to control the shared variance between anxiety and depression36.

Results

Gender differences

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics and gender differences in all variables. Females were more math-anxious than males, with stronger magnitudes for evaluation math anxiety (Cohen’s d = 0.43) than leaning math anxiety (Cohen’s d = 0.27). Similarly, females were more science-anxious than males, but this gender difference was only noticed for evaluation science anxiety (Cohen’s d = 0.38). Therefore, the gender differences in math and science anxieties were more apparent in evaluation, rather than learning, situations. In addition, females had higher scores on general anxiety than males (Cohen’s d = 0.29). For the cognitive emotion regulation strategies, males had higher scores on self-blame (Cohen’s d = 0.29) and planning (Cohen’s d = 0.25), whereas females had higher scores on acceptance (Cohen’s d = 0.33), rumination (Cohen’s d = 0.22), and catastrophizing (Cohen’s d = 0.37). No gender differences were noted for positive refocusing, positive reappraisal, putting into perspective, and other-blame. In addition, there were no gender differences in adaptive and maladaptive factors.

Correlations between the three types of anxiety and the cognitive emotion regulation strategies

Table 2 presents correlations between the three types of anxiety and cognitive emotion regulation strategies in females and males separately. On one hand, math, science, and general anxieties correlated negatively with four adaptive strategies (positive refocusing, planning, positive reappraisal, and putting into perspective) in female and male students. Although all of these correlations were small (rs ranged from − 0.27 to − 0.45), they were stronger for math anxiety (mean r = − 0.44) than science anxiety (mean r = − 0.31) and general anxiety (mean r = − 0.27). Although acceptance is considered an adaptive strategy, there were small positive correlations between acceptance and the three types of anxiety (means rs = 0.30, 0.21, and 0.32 for math, science, and general anxieties, respectively). On the other hand, math, science, and general anxieties correlated positively with three maladaptive strategies (rumination, catastrophizing, and other-blame) in female and male students, except a close-to-zero correlation between rumination and science anxiety in male students. Although self-blame did not correlate with any type of these anxieties in female students, there was small negative correlations between this strategy and the three types of anxiety in male students. On the one hand, the three types of anxiety correlated negatively with adaptive emotion regulation combined factor in females and males, with stronger magnitudes for math anxiety (mean r = − 0.44) than science anxiety (mean r = − 0.31) and general anxiety (mean r = − 0.23). On the other hand, the three types of anxiety correlated positively with maladaptive emotion regulation factor in females and males, with stronger magnitude for general anxiety (mean r = 0.38) than math anxiety (mean r = 0.30) and science anxiety (mean r = 0.18). Regression models

Table 3 presents the results of the final steps of the four hierarchical multiple regression analyses examining the impacts of cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math anxiety with or without controlling science anxiety and/or general anxiety (the results of prior hierarchical regression steps can be found in the supplementary file). Math anxiety was associated with the high uses of rumination and other-blame and the low uses of positive reappraisal and putting into perspective when science and general anxieties were not controlled. However, with controlling science anxiety, math anxiety was associated with the high uses of rumination and other-blame and the low uses of positive reappraisal and putting into perspective. In addition, with controlling general anxiety, math anxiety was associated with the high use of rumination and the low uses of positive reappraisal and putting into perspective. Furthermore, with controlling both science and general anxieties, math anxiety was associated with the high use of rumination and the low use of positive reappraisal.

Discussion

Using a sample of secondary school students in an Arabic-speaking Middle-Eastern country, Qatar, the present study examined the impacts of adaptive (acceptance, positive refocusing, refocus on planning, positive reappraisal, putting into perspective) and maladaptive (self-blame, rumination, catastrophizing, and other-blame) cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math anxiety (with or without controlling science and/or general anxieties) and science anxiety (with or without controlling math anxiety and/or general anxiety). The results replicated gender differences in math, science, and general anxieties. In addition, the three types of anxiety correlated negatively with the adaptive strategies (except acceptance), whereas they correlated positively all maladaptive strategies (except a close to zero correlation between science anxiety and rumination). More importantly, multiple regression analyses showed that math anxiety was associated with the high uses of acceptance, rumination and other-blame and the low uses of positive reappraisal and putting into perspective. However, with controlling science and general anxieties, math anxiety was associated with the high use of rumination and the low use of positive reappraisal. In contrast, science anxiety was associated with the high uses of acceptance and other-blame and the low use of positive reappraisal. Importantly, however, with controlling math and general anxieties, these associations did not remain.

Consistent with previous studies18,25,48, the present study found that females have higher levels of math, science, and general anxieties than males. Gender differences in math and science anxieties have been attributed to socio-cultural stereotyping, in which math20 and science27 are traditionally viewed as male domains. In addition, as math and science anxieties share some features of general anxiety19,25, gender differences in these academic anxieties might be parts of the well-established gender differences in general anxiety, where females are twice as likely to experience anxiety than males48.

It has been previously found that general anxiety had stronger associations with maladaptive than adaptive emotion regulation strategies in adolescents4. Consistently, the present study found that general anxiety correlated more strongly with maladaptive than adaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategies in adolescents. In a stark contrast, math anxiety correlated more strongly with adaptive than maladaptive emotion regulation strategies (see Fig. 1). Together, these results might provide support for the specificity of math anxiety. In fact, one might expect a negative correlation between acceptance and anxiety as acceptance has been conceptualized as an adaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategy. For example, Ford et al. (2018) found that acceptance was associated with better mental health even when other emotion regulation strategies (reappraisal, rumination, and mindfulness) were controlled49. However, these benefits were associated with accepting negative emotions, but not accepting stressful situations49. Consistently, some previous studies found no correlation between accepting stressful situations and anxiety33–35. However, Garnefski and Kraaij (2018) found a positive correlation between this type of acceptance and general anxiety (r = 0.31)36. The present study replicated this positive correlation between cognitive acceptance for stressful events and the three types of anxiety (Table 2). Therefore, the conceptualization of acceptance might need a revision.

Multiple regression analyses provided another evidence for the specificity of math anxiety. Namely, math anxiety was associated with the high use of rumination and the low use of cognitive reappraisal. These associations robustly persisted regardless of whether science and/or general anxieties were controlled or not, suggesting that math anxiety was the primary factor for these associations (see Table 3). In contrast, science anxiety was associated with the high use of acceptance and other-blame and the low use of cognitive reappraisal when math and general anxieties were not controlled. Importantly, however, these associations did not remain when math and general anxieties were controlled, suggesting that science anxiety was not the primary factor for these associations (Table 4). Together, these results indicate that some characteristics of science anxiety might be primary features of math anxiety. Consistently, it was previously reported a moderate positive correlation between math and science anxieties (r = 0.54,29). This shared variance between math and science anxieties might due to the fact that science anxiety was measured in secondary schools on the contexts of biology, chemistry, and physics. Indeed, physics is categorized as a science but it heavily relies on math equations. To a lesser extent, chemistry also relies on math equations to understand chemical reactions. Therefore, we suggest that measuring science anxiety in secondary school students should selectively focus on the context of biology and this might reduce the shared variance between math and science anxieties. Future studies need to examine this suggestion.

The present study significantly improved our understanding of the etiology of math anxiety. As mentioned above, math anxiety was strongly associated with the high use of rumination and the low use of positive reappraisal. Indeed, rumination has been thought to play a vital role in the development of math anxiety in students50. For example, rumination was found to disrupt working memory resources, which are critical for retrieving cognitive information required for math-problem solving12. Therefore, rumination moderated the association between math performance and executive functioning that was noticed in high, but not in low, ruminators51. Furthermore, mindfulness was found to improve math performance by reducing test and math anxieties that might give a more space for working memory resources52,53. One proposed explanation for this beneficiary effect was that mindfulness breaks rumination54. Therefore, the strong positive association between math anxiety and rumination in the present study might support this explanation.

Ramirez, Shaw, and Maloney (2018) proposed an Interpretation Account framework, which posits that math anxiety may be developed from the maladaptive interpretations of previous and ongoing negative math-related experiences and outcomes41. The present study might provide a strong support for this framework as math anxiety was robustly associated with the low use of positive reappraisal. Consistently, some studies have previously found that the positive reappraisal of arousal associated with math assessment improved students’ math-related feelings and performances55. In addition, it was found that resource appraisal (perceiving stress and arousal as functional, adaptive and coping tools) correlated negatively with evaluation MA, whereas demand appraisal (perceiving stress and arousal as dysfunctional, maladaptive and failing tools) correlated positively with both learning and evaluation math anxiety39. Further, cognitive reappraisal moderated the association between physiological arousal and math performance in highly math-anxious individuals56.

There are a number of limitations in the present study. First, although controlling co-variates via multiple regression analyses is a well-established procedure in psychology literature, this procedure may increase type 1 error rates (false positives), which can be magnified when a sample size is large and measurement reliability is moderate57. Second, as the present results based on regression designs, the causal relationships between cognitive emotion regulation strategies and math anxiety are still not clear. Future clinical studies need to investigate the effects of improving positive reappraisal and reducing rumination on math anxiety using an appropriate experimental design. Third, cognitive emotion regulation strategies and math anxiety were measured by self-report questionnaires. Future studies need to replicate the present findings using behavioral or physiological measures. Fourth, the present study focused on only one form of math-related emotions (i.e., anxiety). It is important to investigate how emotion regulation might influence different positive and negative math-related emotions58. In spite of these limitations, this is the first study that examined the influence of cognitive emotion regulation strategies on math and science anxiety. The results showed that math anxiety was associated with the high use of rumination and the low use of cognitive reappraisal, even when science and/or general anxiety were controlled. All told, we conclude that cognitive emotion regulation might play a vital role in the development of math anxiety in students and it could be a successful avenue for counseling students with high math anxiety by reducing rumination and improving positive reappraisal.
