Study on deformation control technology for deep loose and fractured roadways in Yunjialing Mine
Authors: Dongming Guo, Jin Chen, Qinyuan Lu, Deguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates severe deformation and instability in deep roadways within loose and fractured rock, using the 12,810 transportation roadway of the Yunjialing Coal Mine as a case study. Field investigation, theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and in-situ monitoring were integrated to systematically reveal the deformation mechanisms and instability characteristics of fractured surrounding rock. Based on the Hoek-Brown criterion, a reinforced bearing arch model was established to describe the interaction between the support system and the surrounding rock. Furthermore, a novel combined support scheme was proposed and implemented, consisting of densely spaced anchor cables in the roof and sidewalls, pressure-relief grooves in the floor, and floor cable reinforcement. Numerical and field results demonstrate that the proposed support system effectively suppresses roadway convergence, controls floor heave, and maintains the overall stability of the surrounding rock. Compared with conventional schemes, the proposed method not only controls deformation more efficiently but also provides a theoretical framework and engineering reference for the stability control of deep soft rock roadways under similar complex geological conditions.Keywords: Loose and fractured rock, Hoek-Brown criterion, Combined support, Surrounding rock controlSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of underground engineering, the support of deep, fractured, and weak rock roadways has become increasingly prominent, emerging as one of the key factors restricting the safe and efficient mining of underground resources1–3. The deformation and failure of surrounding rock in such deep fractured soft rock roadways are influenced by multiple factors, including the mechanical properties of the rock mass, geological structures, and mining-induced disturbances4–6. The surrounding rock is typically characterized by low strength, large deformation, and poor stability7. Traditional support techniques fail to effectively control the deformation and failure of the surrounding rock, resulting in increased support costs, greater maintenance difficulties, and even disruption of normal engineering operations8,9. Therefore, the development of effective control techniques for surrounding rock in deep fractured soft rock roadways is imperative.

Extensive research has been conducted on deformation control in deep fractured soft rock roadways. The primary approaches for surrounding rock control include optimized support parameter design, grouting reinforcement, stress regulation techniques, and combined support systems10–13. Liao and Feng14 proposed a layered grouting reinforcement method to address severe roof damage in fractured roadways, effectively controlling roadway failure. Yu et al.15 addressed instability in ultra-thick coal seams by optimizing roadway layout and support parameters.Huang et al.16, based on in-situ stress measurements, employed theoretical analysis and numerical simulation to investigate the shape and extent of plastic zones in fractured surrounding rock roadways under non-uniform loads, and proposed a combined support scheme with optimized parameters.Xie et al.17 studied the failure mechanisms of roadways in “three-soft” coal seams under high horizontal stress, and proposed a combined support system consisting of hollow grouted cable bolts and high-strength rock bolts, which performed well in field applications. Shan et al.18 addressed the challenge of supporting fractured roadways in semi-coal and rock strata by analyzing the failure mechanism and developing a novel C-shaped tubular anchorage structure, which effectively controlled shear-slip failure. Cui et al.19 analyzed the deformation characteristics and failure mechanisms of compound roof surrounding rock in soft and weak coal roadways, and mechanically compared the advantages of prestressed truss cable bolts over conventional cable bolts. Field monitoring indicated that the truss cable bolt support system effectively controlled roadway deformation. Wu et al.20 addressed the challenge of deformation control in soft coal-rock roadways of high-gas mines, emphasizing that forming a pressure arch between bolts and surrounding rock is key to successful support, and conducted an in-depth study on the arch formation mechanism.Wang et al.21 responded to the difficulty of supporting cracked and spalling surrounding rock in large cross-section soft rock roadways of kilometer-deep shafts by proposing a “grouting-anchoring-casting” synergistic control scheme.Pei22 investigated large deformation of soft rock in fractured roadways beneath normal faults under combined mining-induced and fault structural stress. Through theoretical analysis, the energy-driven mechanism of large deformation was revealed, and a “pressure relief–reinforcement” control method was proposed to effectively limit deformation. Tan et al.23 developed a composite support system composed of U-shaped steel and a foam concrete damping layer, in which the foam concrete absorbs significant energy and effectively reduces the contraction deformation of the U-shaped steel.Yin et al.24 proposed differentiated control techniques for roadways with large deformation under close-range irregular goafs and remnant coal pillars, including asymmetric support beneath goafs, combined support beneath coal pillars, and pressure relief through borehole drilling.Chu et al.25 addressed the challenge of large deformation in soft rock at kilometer depths by proposing a multi-level support technique.Ming, C., et al.26 found that roof directional pre-splitting blasting can effectively reduce the dynamic pressure induced by thick coal seam mining and decrease roadway deformation.Yang et al.27 proposed a combined blasting method integrating shaped charge and non-directional blasting, which promotes roof caving in the goaf and enhances the bulking effect of surrounding rock. As a result, the coal stress in front of the working face and on the coal pillar side of the roadway is reduced, which helps maintain roadway stability. Liu, Y., et al.28 addressed the problem of large deformation in water-bearing soft rock roadways under strong mining disturbance. They proposed a comprehensive control method of surrounding rock, combining “roof shaped charge blasting + high-strength support with constant-resistance large-deformation rock bolts. ” This method effectively controlled the large deformation of the surrounding rock.

Current control technologies for loose and fractured surrounding rock can be broadly categorized into active support methods (e.g., rock bolts and cable bolts), passive support systems (e.g., steel sets and lining structures), grouting techniques, and pressure-relief methods such as deep-hole blasting and hydraulic fracturing.These methods have solved many problems in engineering practice. However, most studies are still limited to individual engineering cases. However, a comprehensive and systematic theoretical framework for loose and fractured roadways remains insufficiently developed. In particular, research on control technology under complex conditions-such as high in-situ stress, highly fractured rock mass, and large floor heave-is still insufficient. Therefore, it is necessary to carry out more targeted theoretical and engineering studies. This study investigates the 12,810 transportation roadway of the Yunjialing Coal Mine as a representative case. Based on the Hoek-Brown criterion, an “anchored bearing arch” model suitable for loose and fractured surrounding rock is established. A combined support system is proposed, consisting of densely arranged cable bolts in the roof and sidewalls, floor pressure-relief slots, and floor cable bolt reinforcement.

The aim of this study is to reveal the deformation mechanism of deep fractured surrounding rock, to establish a compression bearing arch model suitable for deep loose and fractured roadways, and to provide theoretical guidance and engineering reference for stability control of soft rock roadways under similar geological conditions.

Engineering overview

Geological conditions of the roadway

The 12,810 transportation roadway of the Yunjialing Mine is situated at a burial depth of approximately 800 m. The original cross-sectional design of the roadway is 4.8 m in width and 3.5 m in height. Functioning primarily to facilitate coal extraction, material transportation, and ventilation for the 12,810 working face, the roadway is designed with an anticipated service life of approximately 2.5 years. Due to geological influences, the surrounding rock of the roadway is extremely fractured. According to site data (Fig. 1), the local false roof of the working face is composed of dark gray mudstone with a flat fracture surface, containing clastic fossils. The rock core is fragmented with visible fractures filled with calcite. The thickness ranges from 0 to 1.72 m, with an average of 0.35 m.The immediate roof consists of dark gray siltstone with fragmented cores and irregularly developed fractures filled with argillaceous material. The thickness ranges from 6.70 m to 8.49 m, with an average of 7.6 m. The main roof is composed of dark gray blocky mudstone, containing plant root fossils in the upper part and fragmented structures in the lower part, filled with argillaceous materials and calcite. Its thickness ranges from 4.48 m to 4.58 m, with an average of 4.53 m.The immediate floor is dark gray siltstone, locally rich in fragmented plant fossils, exhibiting fractures filled with calcite. The thickness ranges from 4.55 m to 4.70 m, with an average of 4.62 m.

Stratigraphic distribution of roof and floor rock layers in the coal seam.

The in-situ stress was measured on site using the hollow inclusion method. The vertical stress at Yunjialing Mine is 20.1 MPa. The maximum horizontal principal stress is 24.2 MPa, and the minimum horizontal principal stress is 15.6 MPa. The angle between the direction of the maximum horizontal principal stress and the axis of the 12,810 transportation roadway is θ = 0°.

According to the classification criteria and recommendations for surrounding rock stability of coal mine roadways in China, the 12,810 transportation roadway is classified as a Class IV unstable roadway.

Characteristics of large deformation failure of the original support structure

The roadway cross-section adopts a trapezoidal shape, with a designed cross-sectional size of 4.8 m in width and 3.5 m in height. A combined support system of “prestressed rock bolts + prestressed anchor cables for reinforcement” is employed, as shown in Fig. 2. During roadway excavation, instability and failure of the surrounding rock occurred, accompanied by failure of bolts and anchor cables (Fig. 3). The specific manifestations are as follows:

Original Support Scheme.

Deformation of the 12,810 Transportation Roadway. (a) Roof Deformation and Failure Condition, (b) Sidewall Deformation and Failure Condition, (c) Floor Deformation and Failure Condition.

①The roof strata of the roadway are extremely fractured, with a prominent “net pocket” phenomenon. The W-shaped steel straps on the roof exhibit stepped deformation, and the anchor cables show sequential failure. The overall integrity of the support structure is poor.

②Significant deformation occurs in both sidewalls of the roadway, with the lower part of the sidewalls often exhibiting greater deformation than the upper part. The sidewall rock is severely fractured, with some long anchor cables pulled into the surrounding rock. In some cases, rock bolts located in the fractured zones cause the bolt and the bearing plate to bulge outward together with the coal wall, or even result in complete bolt pull-out.

③In the original support scheme for the Yunjialing roadway, no support design was implemented for the floor, while the floor surrounding rock was severely fractured and loose. As a result, under high in-situ stress conditions, significant deformation occurred in the floor, making it a “breakthrough point” for stress release. According to field monitoring data from the Yunjialing Mine, the maximum floor heave reached 1350 mm and continued to exhibit a tendency for further development. The floor of the 12,810 working face transportation roadway generally underwent 2 to 3 rounds of floor trimming, with some sections requiring 5 to 6 rounds. Although repeated floor trimming helped to some extent in controlling floor deformation, it effectively increased the “working height” of the roadway, adversely affecting the stability of the sidewalls and roof, compromising the overall stability of the support structure, and increasing construction burdens.

Displacement monitoring was carried out for the 12,810 transportation roadway under the original support conditions, with the surrounding rock deformation at one monitoring point shown in Fig. 4. As illustrated in the figure, within less than 80 days of monitoring, the roof displacement of the roadway reached approximately 200 mm. The total inward convergence of the two sidewalls was about 900 mm, with the deformation of the left sidewall being greater than that of the right. The floor exhibited the largest deformation, and even after two floor excavations, the deformation had not ceased.

Analysis of roadway instability causes

Based on geological investigation and monitoring analysis of the transportation roadway in the 12,810 working face of the Yunjialing Mine, it is considered that the instability and failure of the roadway resulted from the combined effects of fractured surrounding rock, high in-situ stress, and inadequate support. The failure mechanism is as follows:

①The mining depth is considerable, leading to high in-situ stress. The 12,810 working face is buried approximately 800 m below the surface, falling under the category of deep mining under high stress conditions.

②The lithological characteristics of the surrounding rock are poor. While the immediate and main roofs in the 12,810 roadway consist of relatively strong rock blocks, the coal seam and floor strata exhibit low bearing capacity. The surrounding rock is highly jointed, and the rock cores are fragmented, with local zones containing large amounts of plant fossil debris. As a result, the overall bearing capacity of the surrounding rock is low, ground pressure manifestations are intense, and deformation is significant.

③The support parameters are not properly designed. The roadway is primarily supported by a combination of anchor cables, rock bolts, W-shaped steel straps, and mesh reinforcement. However, due to the loose and fragmented nature of the surrounding rock, the bearing capacity is low and the failure zone is extensive. The support resistance provided by the bolts and anchor cables is insufficient to form a stable anchorage body with the surrounding rock, resulting in large deformation and damage. Additionally, the existing support system mainly focuses on controlling deformation in the sidewalls and roof, while the floor is left unsupported. Under high stress conditions, severe floor heave occurs, becoming the weakest part of the entire support system. Therefore, controlling floor deformation, enhancing its load-bearing capacity, and reducing the extent of damage are essential. A complete support system should be formed by integrating the support of the floor with that of the sidewalls and roof, which is the key approach to achieving effective control of roadway surrounding rock stability.

Based on the above support strategy, a coupled surrounding rock control technique combining “densely arranged anchor cables in the sidewall and roof” with “floor pressure-relief anchorage” was adopted for roadway support. First, the roadway cross-section was changed from a trapezoidal shape to a straight-wall circular-arch shape to reduce stress concentration at corners and fully utilize the bearing capacity of the surrounding rock. Second, high-preload anchor cables were installed densely and in a staggered arrangement in the sidewall and roof area to form an integrated anchored bearing arch structure. Finally, a pressure-relief groove was excavated in the floor and floor anchor cables were installed to control floor deformation.

Integrated control technology for roadways in loosely consolidated and fragmented surrounding rock

Coupled support technology with densely arranged anchor cables in roof and sidewalls

To fully utilize the load-bearing capacity of the roadway cross-section, the original trapezoidal profile is modified to a circular-arch shape. Meanwhile, a coupled support technology employing densely distributed anchor cables in the roof and sidewalls is proposed. In this approach, conventional rock bolts with limited bearing capacity are replaced by high-capacity anchor cables, which can effectively mobilize the strength of the deeper surrounding rock and reduce deformation.

After roadway excavation in deep surrounding rock, a plastic failure zone typically develops around the opening due to stress redistribution. When rock bolts or anchor cables are installed, they exert compressive reinforcement on the fractured surrounding rock. If the spacing between the bolts (or anchor cables) is sufficiently small, the compressive stress diffusion zones around each anchor will overlap, forming a compressed anchorage body within the rock mass, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Within this anchorage body, the rock is confined both by the installed bolts (or anchor cables) and the adjacent surrounding rock, significantly improving the stress distribution. This results in a triaxial compression state that greatly enhances the load-bearing capacity of the rock mass.

Mechanical model of load-bearing arch formed by anchored compressive zone.

Due to the severely fractured nature of the surrounding rock in the 12,810 transportation roadway of the Yunjialing Mine, the Hoek–Brown failure criterion, which is more suitable for loosely consolidated and fragmented rock masses, is adopted for the theoretical analysis of the compressive anchorage arch model.

Based on systematic studies involving laboratory experiments and in-situ tests, Hoek and Brown proposed several versions of empirical failure criteria for rock masses. Considering field applicability and parameter availability, the 1980 version of the Hoek–Brown failure criterion is selected for this study. This criterion treats jointed and fragmented rock masses as homogeneous and continuous media, with empirical parameters introduced to account for the material and structural characteristics of the rock mass.

Based on the characteristics of the roadway in the loosely consolidated and fragmented surrounding rock, the following assumptions are made:

①The surrounding rock is idealized as an equivalent isotropic and continuous medium for analytical purposes.

②After excavation and support with bolts (or anchor cables), a uniformly distributed arch-shaped anchorage body is formed.

③The failure of the supported rock mass follows the Hoek–Brown failure criterion.

④The support system consists of densely arranged bolts (or anchor cables).

⑤The support resistance of the bolts (or anchor cables) is uniformly distributed along the circumferential fractured zone.

⑥The anchorage body formed after support can be approximated as a semicircular shape, and the stress state of the roadway is assumed to resemble that of a semicircle under boundary loading along its diameter.

Due to current technical limitations and cost constraints, the support strength provided by bolts (or anchor cables) on the roadway surface is limited, generally less than 1 MPa, making it difficult to control the surrounding rock solely by increasing support resistance. The thickness of the anchorage body is related to the length and spacing of the bolts (or anchor cables); therefore, increasing the thickness of the anchorage body to enhance its overall bearing capacity is relatively more feasible. In weak and fractured roadways, the surrounding rock is significantly influenced by geological structures and excavation-induced disturbances, leading to the development of fractures and a substantial reduction in bearing capacity. Consequently, for the support of weak and fractured roadways, priority should be given to grouting or similar techniques to fill fractures and improve the integrity of the surrounding rock. This should be followed by optimizing the spacing and length of bolts (or anchor cables) to enlarge the anchorage support zone. Finally, increasing the diameter of bolts (or anchor cables) or reducing their spacing can enhance support strength and effectively control surrounding rock deformation.

For the transportation roadway 12,810 in the Yunjialing Mine, the depth of the disturbed zone on the sidewalls exceeds 4 m, with a selected value of 4.3 m. The total thickness of the immediate roof and the pseudo-roof is approximately 5.0 m. Therefore, to maintain the stability of the fractured surrounding rock, the support system must provide a support resistance greater than 1.25 MPa. The uniaxial compressive strength of the coal seam is 25 MPa, and the roadway diameter is 4.4 m. Considering that the surrounding rock is relatively fractured, parameters m = 0.2 and s = 0.0001 are adopted. As the 12,810 transportation roadway is a mining roadway, the use of grouting would reduce coal quality and economic efficiency. Taking all factors into consideration, it was decided to adopt an all-cable bolt support system for the roadway.If Φ17.8 anchor cables (with a designed load capacity of 355 kN) are used at a spacing of 0.8 m×0.8 m, the resulting support resistance is P ≈ 0.55MPa . The typical length of anchor cables should not be less than 4 m, leading to a calculated bearing capacity of the compressive anchorage arch of q = 1.73MPa . In practical support design, to ensure safety, the selected support parameters should not be lower than those specified in this scheme.

Floor stress-relief and anchorage support technology

To further validate the theoretical rationality of the aforementioned reinforced bearing arch model and quantitatively evaluate the specific impact of key parameters (especially the relief groove depth) in the Floor Stress-Relief and Anchorage Support scheme on surrounding rock stability, this study employs numerical simulation for refined analysis. Based on the mechanical model and theoretical derivations established, the critical role of support intensity and surrounding rock parameters on the stability of the bearing arch has been clarified. However, for the key measure of Floor Stress-Relief and Anchorage Support, its core control parameter—the geometric dimension of the relief groove (particularly its depth)-requires quantitative analysis to determine the optimal value, thereby achieving the best balance between “stress relief” and “maintaining the overall strength of the surrounding rock.” Therefore, this study establishes a three-dimensional numerical model to systematically simulate the stress distribution and deformation patterns of the roadway surrounding rock under different relief groove depths. This aims to reveal the mechanism of floor heave control and provide a direct basis for parameter optimization in subsequent engineering schemes.

Mechanism of floor stress-relief and anchorage support technology

For the control of roadway floor heave, in addition to support techniques such as rock bolt reinforcement, inverted arch construction, and grouting reinforcement, the excavation of pressure-relief grooves is also an effective method. Deep rock masses are typically subjected to a triaxial stress state and exhibit high strength. After excavating the pressure-relief groove, the shallow stress in the roadway floor is transferred to the deeper part of the rock mass. On one hand, a self-bearing structure is formed in the deep high-stress zone of the floor to resist concentrated roadway stress, allowing the shallow floor rock mass to remain stable due to stress relief. On the other hand, the shallow surrounding rock of the floor still retains a certain residual strength, which provides constraint to the deeper rock mass, thereby enhancing the overall stability of the surrounding rock.

Considering the loose and fractured condition of the floor in the 12,810 transportation roadway of the Yunjialing Mine, which results in low bearing capacity, the control effect on floor deformation is poor after excavating the stress-relief groove. To address this, two rows of anchor cables are installed on each side of the groove following excavation, forming a “stress relief–support” coupled control scheme for floor heave. This approach implements a “yield first, then resist” mechanism, significantly improving the effectiveness of floor heave control. Based on the above analysis, the rationality of the stress-relief groove excavation parameters will directly determine the success or failure of floor control. Therefore, this study conducts a simulation-based investigation on the excavation parameters of the stress-relief groove.

(1) As the depth of the pressure-relief groove increases, the heave of the roadway floor exhibits a trend of first decreasing and then increasing. When the groove depths are 0.5 m, 1.0 m, 1.5 m, and 2.0 m, the corresponding vertical deformation values of the floor are 294 mm, 237 mm, 315 mm, and 347 mm, respectively. Among the four schemes, the roof subsidence values are 188 mm, 203 mm, 223 mm, and 269 mm, respectively, while the sidewall deformations are 154 mm, 164.0 mm, 176.5 mm, and 210.5 mm, respectively.

Therefore, after a comprehensive evaluation, a groove depth of H = 1 m is identified as the optimal design.

Engineering application

Combined support scheme for roadways in loosely fragmented surrounding rock

Based on theoretical calculations, numerical simulations, and engineering analogy, a combined support scheme was ultimately developed, as shown in Fig. 11.

Combined support scheme.

The short anchor cables in the roof are Φ18.9 × 4300 mm, installed with two four-hole W steel straps measuring 2.75 × 320 × 2600 mm, with the central holes overlapped. The row and spacing between bolts are 800 × 1280 mm. The long roof anchor cables are Φ22 × 7300 mm and are arranged in groups of six per set, with the same spacing of 800 × 1280 mm. The two sidewalls of the roadway are supported with Φ18.9 × 4300 mm short anchor cables. Six anchor cables are installed on each sidewall, matched with two W steel straps measuring 2.75 × 320 × 1900 mm, with the central holes overlapped. The row and spacing between bolts are 800 × 1280 mm.

A pressure-relief groove with a width of 1 m and a depth of 1 m was excavated at the center of the roadway floor. Anchor cables with a diameter of Φ18.9 mm and a length of 4300 mm were installed in the floor. Two anchor cables were installed on each side of the pressure-relief groove, each matched with one W-steel strap measuring 2.75 × 320 × 1700 mm. The row and column spacing was set at 800 × 640 mm.

Discussion

The theoretical model uses a semicircular shape for easy calculation. However, actual roadways often have trapezoidal, rectangular or arched shapes. The model also does not consider geological structures or rock anisotropy. Therefore, when using this model in practice, users should calibrate it with field measurements.

Although the proposed support scheme requires additional investment in dense cable support, floor pressure-relief slot excavation, and floor cable reinforcement, it can effectively control large deformation in loose and fractured surrounding rock, especially in roadways with severe floor heave and poor overall stability. However, this method is not a universally applicable support form, but a targeted control technology that is sensitive to geological and mechanical conditions. Its applicability depends on burial depth, in-situ stress, lithology, fracture development, deformation characteristics, and service requirements. Therefore, the main value of this study lies not in providing a fixed parameter set for direct application, but in proposing a coordinated control concept for the roof, sidewalls, and floor in loose and fractured roadways. This control idea can provide a useful reference for similar engineering conditions, but the key support parameters should be adjusted according to the specific geological and engineering conditions.

Conclusions

(1) The 12,810 transportation roadway in Yunjialing Mine is a deep roadway excavated in fractured soft rock. High in-situ stress, loose and fragmented surrounding rock, and inadequate support parameters are the primary causes of large deformation in the surrounding rock. In response, a targeted coupled surrounding rock control technology system was proposed, consisting of “densely arranged anchor cables for sidewalls and roof + pressure-relief anchorage for the floor.”

(2) Based on the Hoek-Brown criterion, a compressive anchorage arch model suitable for loosely fragmented surrounding rock was established. An analytical expression was derived to describe the relationship between the bearing capacity of the anchorage structure and factors such as the spacing of bolts (or cables) and the strength of the surrounding rock.

(3) A floor pressure-relief and anchorage support technique was proposed: a pressure-relief groove is excavated at the center of the floor to fully release the stress, enabling the transfer of shallow floor stress to deeper levels. Anchor cabless are installed on both sides of the groove to control floor deformation through a “yield first, resist later” mechanism.

(4) The support parameters for the all-cable bolt support system in the sidewall and roof, the floor pressure-relief groove, and the floor cable bolts were determined.Engineering practice has demonstrated that this support technique can effectively control large deformations in loosely fragmented surrounding rock, providing a valuable reference for the surrounding rock control of similar roadways.
