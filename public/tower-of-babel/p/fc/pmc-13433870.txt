Analysis of prognostic factors and construction of a nomogram for patients with brain metastases from cancer of unknown primary based on the SEER database
Authors: Xiaolu Ma, Chuanxia Zhang, Xiaoxi Wan, Heng Lu, Mengyuan Kang, Qianhao Meng, Guangru Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCancer of unknown primary with brain metastases (CUP-BM) represents a distinct and challenging disease entity characterized by an extremely poor prognosis. Currently, there is a lack of systematic research on its prognostic factors. This study aimed to analyze the prognostic factors associated with CUP-BM.MethodsPatients diagnosed with CUP-BM between 2010 and 2022 were identified from the SEER database. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were utilized to analyze survival differences among various histological subtypes. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were employed to identify prognostic factors and construct a prognostic nomogram model. Subgroup analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of age as a prognostic factor and explore potential interaction effects with other variables.ResultsA total of 700 eligible CUP-BM patients were included. The neuroendocrine subtype demonstrated the most favorable prognosis. Multivariate analysis revealed that histological type, chemotherapy, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, bone metastasis, and age were independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS). The constructed nomogram exhibited satisfactory predictive performance (bias-corrected C-index = 0.663). Subgroup analysis showed that age remained a significant prognostic factor in most clinical strata, although its effect varied across subgroups defined by metastatic burden and histological subtype.ConclusionNeuroendocrine histology and chemotherapy were associated with improved survival, while adenocarcinoma histology, age greater than 60 years, and the presence of liver, lung, or bone metastasis were identified as poor prognostic factors for CUP-BM. The nomogram exhibited good calibration and discrimination capability for prognostic prediction in CUP-BM patients. Notably, the prognostic impact of age was substantially attenuated in patients with extensive metastatic burden, highlighting the importance of individualized risk assessment.

Introduction

Cancer of Unknown Primary (CUP) is defined as a histologically confirmed metastatic malignancy whose primary site cannot be identified despite comprehensive and systematic imaging evaluation [1]. As a highly heterogeneous disease [2, 3], CUP accounts for approximately 3–5% of all malignant tumors [4, 5]. Characterized by early distant metastasis, rapid disease progression, and an elusive primary site, CUP is associated with a generally poor prognosis and has a median overall survival (OS) of only 6 to 9 months [6].

In recent years, advancements in precision medicine have enabled the identification of the tissue of origin in a subset of CUP cases through specialized diagnostic techniques applied to metastatic lesions. This allows for the implementation of targeted, “site-specific” treatment strategies, which have, to some extent, improved patient outcomes [7, 8]. However, CUP with brain metastases (CUP-BM) represents a particularly challenging subtype. The difficulty in sampling intracranial lesions and the complexities in pathological identification often preclude the determination of their tissue origin. Consequently, management remains largely reliant on empirical regimens and symptomatic support, posing significant challenges in clinical decision-making. Furthermore, patients with CUP-BM frequently suffer from severe neurological complications, such as cerebral edema, neurological deficits, and seizures, secondary to the mass effect of intracranial lesions. These complications not only severely impair patients’ quality of daily life but also further shorten their expected survival time [9]. While existing studies have focused on the clinical features and prognosis of CUP with common metastatic patterns such as liver or bone involvement [10, 11], the prognostic factors specific to CUP-BM remain inadequately explored in large-scale, systematic studies. Moreover, there is a notable lack of reliable tools for individualized prognosis assessment in this unique patient population.

This study was conducted based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, through which clinical characteristics and survival data of patients with CUP-BM were systematically collected. A comprehensive analysis of prognostic factors was performed, with survival differences among histological subtypes specifically examined from the perspective of histology. Based on the results of multivariate analysis, a visual and clinically applicable nomogram model was developed to enable quantitative prediction of individual patient prognosis. Furthermore, age-stratified analysis confirmed the relative robustness of age as a prognostic factor, while also revealing significant heterogeneity within specific subgroups. This finding provides an important basis for formulating individualized treatment strategies for different age groups.

Materials and methods

Data source

The data for this study were obtained from the SEER 17 Registries Database (2000–2022), which covers approximately 26.5% of the U.S. population. After obtaining approval for database access, we utilized SEER*Stat software (version 9.0.40) for data extraction. As detailed information on brain, bone, liver, and lung metastases has been systematically collected only since 2010, this study included patients diagnosed with CUP between 2010 and 2022. Since the SEER database is publicly available and contains de-identified patient information, the requirement for institutional review board ethics approval and informed consent was waived for this study. The data extraction flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) primary site classified as “Unknown Primary Site” (ICD-O-3 code C80.9); (2) availability of complete follow-up data; (3) histologically confirmed malignancy; (4) confirmed presence of brain metastasis; and (5) CUP as the first primary cancer diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with a survival time of 0 months; (2) CUP as non-first primary malignancy; and (3) cases with unclear documentation regarding the presence or absence of bone, liver, or lung metastases.

Data collection

The extracted variables of interest included sex, age, race, marital status, metastatic status, histological type, rural-urban residence, and radiotherapy/chemotherapy information. Based on marital status, patients were categorized as married, unknown, or unmarried (including single, divorced, separated, widowed, unmarried, or domestic partner) [12]. Histological types were classified according to ICD-O-3 codes as adenocarcinoma (ACC) (8140–8389), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (8050–8089), neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) (8240, 8241, 8243, 8244, 8245, 8246, and 8249), and carcinoma not otherwise specified (NOS) (8010–8049) [13]. Consistent with prior studies [14], rural-urban classification was based on the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Rural-Urban Continuum Codes. Urban areas were defined as counties within metropolitan areas with a population of 250,000 or more, while rural areas encompassed non-metropolitan counties that did not meet this criterion. OS served as the primary outcome, defined as the time interval from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and R software (version 4.2.0), with visualization graphics generated in R. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Survival data were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, and the log-rank test was employed to assess statistically significant differences. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate prognostic factors. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the subsequent multivariate Cox model. Hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were utilized to calculate relative risk. Kaplan-Meier analysis and visualization were conducted using the “survival” and “survminer” packages in R. A nomogram was constructed based on the independent prognostic factors identified in the multivariate Cox analysis, primarily utilizing the “rms” package. The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was quantified using the concordance index (C-index), and calibration was assessed by calibration curves comparing the predicted and observed survival probabilities. To account for potential overfitting, internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations, yielding bias-corrected C-index and optimism-corrected calibration curves. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to further visualize the discriminatory ability of the model at specific time points. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the net clinical benefit of the nomogram across a range of threshold probabilities. Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed using stratified Cox regression, and the “forestplot” package was used to generate forest plots for these analyses. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Analysis of patient characteristics

A total of 700 CUP-BM patients were identified in this study (Fig. 1). As summarized in Table 1, the cohort comprised slightly more female patients (364, 52.0%) than male patients (336, 48.0%). The majority of patients were of White race (560, 80.0%). Patients aged > 60 years (495, 70.7%) outnumbered those aged ≤ 60 years (205, 29.3%). Regarding treatment history, 325 patients (46.4%) had received radiotherapy, while 172 (24.6%) had undergone chemotherapy. The prevalence of concurrent bone, liver, and lung metastases was 36.4%, 30.3%, and 38.4%, respectively. Among the study cohort, 318 patients (45.4%) were married, and most resided in urban areas (584, 83.4%). Stratified by histological type, NOS was the most common subtype (64.3%), followed by ACC (22.1%), while NEC (10.0%) and SCC (3.6%) were less frequent.

Survival analysis by histological subtype

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for different histological subtypes are presented in Fig. 2. Significant survival differences were observed among the subtypes (P = 0.00069). The NEC subtype demonstrated the longest median OS of 6 months (95% CI: 4–10). In contrast, other subtypes showed relatively poorer outcomes. The median OS was 2 months (95% CI: 1–6) for SCC, 2 months (95% CI: 2–3) for NOS, and 2 months (95% CI: 2–3) for ACC. The 1-year OS rate for the entire cohort was 19.29%. By subtype, the 1-year OS rates were 32.86% for NEC, 24.00% for SCC, 20.22% for NOS, and 9.68% for ACC.

Prognostic factors for OS in CUP-BM patients

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify factors influencing OS in CUP-BM patients. Univariable analysis indicated that age, bone metastasis, lung metastasis, liver metastasis, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and histological type were all significant prognostic factors. Multivariable Cox analysis confirmed NEC subtype (vs. ACC; HR = 0.656; 95% CI: (0.478–0.901); P = 0.009), history of chemotherapy (vs. No/Unknown; HR = 0.638; 95% CI: (0.518–0.785); P < 0.001) as the protective factors for CUP-BM. Conversely, age > 60 (vs. age ≤ 60; HR = 1.393; 95% CI: (1.155–1.680); P = 0.001) and the presence of bone (vs. no; HR = 1.238; 95% CI: (1.026–1.495); P = 0.026), lung (vs. no; HR = 1.283; 95% CI: (1.059–1.555); P = 0.011), or liver metastases (vs. no; HR = 1.249; 95% CI: (1.019–1.531); P = 0.033) were identified as independent risk factors (Table 2).

Prognostic model construction and assessment

A nomogram for predicting patient prognosis was constructed based on the results of the multivariate regression analysis (Fig. 3). The model identified age as the most influential prognostic factor, followed by history of chemotherapy. Patients with concurrent lung, liver, or bone metastases also demonstrated poorer prognoses. Initial evaluation of the nomogram yielded a concordance index (C-index) of 0.671 (95% CI: 0.645–0.697). Rigorous internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples confirmed the model’s stability, yielding a bias-corrected C-index of 0.663 (95% CI: 0.637–0.689). These results demonstrate that the model possesses satisfactory discrimination and promising clinical utility. To assess the accuracy of the nomogram, we generated calibration curves for 6-, 9-, and 12-month OS using the same bootstrap approach (1,000 resamples). As shown in Fig. 4A–C, the calibration plots revealed a high degree of agreement between the predicted probabilities and the actual survival rates, suggesting that the model is well-calibrated and does not significantly over- or underestimate the risk. In addition to calibration, the discriminatory power of the nomogram was evaluated via ROC analysis. Figure 4D illustrates the ROC curves for the three time points. The model achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.782 for the 6-month prediction, 0.782 for the 9-month prediction, and 0.775 for the 12-month prediction. These AUC values confirm that the nomogram has robust sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing between patients with different survival outcomes. Furthermore, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was employed to assess the clinical utility of the model. As shown in Fig. 5A–C, the nomogram demonstrated superior net benefit compared to the treat-all or treat-none schemes across a broad range of threshold probabilities, suggesting high potential for clinical application.

Exploratory subgroup analysis based on age

A forest plot (Fig. 6) was utilized to illustrate the impact of age on CUP-BM across different subgroups. These analyses were exploratory, and formal tests for interaction were performed to assess effect modification. Statistically significant interactions were identified between age and liver metastases (P for interaction = 0.007), bone metastases (P for interaction < 0.001), and histological subtype (P for interaction = 0.003). In subgroups with concurrent liver metastases (P = 0.558), or bone metastases (P = 0.719), age did not demonstrate a significant prognostic impact. Conversely, among patients without liver, or bone metastases, those aged > 60 years exhibited a significantly higher risk of mortality. For lung metastases, although the interaction did not reach statistical significance (P for interaction = 0.088), a similar pattern was observed, with attenuated age effects in the presence of lung metastases (P = 0.020 vs. P < 0.001). These findings collectively suggest that the prognostic impact of age diminishes when heavy disease burden is present. Regarding histology, age > 60 years was a significant risk factor for NOS (P < 0.001), ACC (P = 0.016), but not for SCC (P = 0.619) or NEC (P = 0.215). No significant interactions were observed for sex, radiation, chemotherapy, residence, or marital status, indicating that age > 60 years remained a consistent risk factor regardless of these variables.

Discussion

CUP-BM represents a rare but aggressive disease. The inability to identify the primary tumor restricts treatment options, leading to persistently grim survival outcomes. Despite this, data regarding prognostic factors for these patients are still scarce. Our study utilized large-scale data from the SEER database to systematically analyze the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of patients with CUP accompanied by brain metastases. The final nomogram prediction model was based on seven predictors: histology, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, liver metastases, lung metastases, bone metastases, and age. It demonstrated a bias-corrected C-index of 0.663 and exhibited satisfactory discriminatory ability, suggesting its potential utility for clinical risk stratification and individualized prognosis assessment.

Although whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) is one of the most common treatment modalities for brain metastases [15], some previous retrospective studies have reported limited improvement in overall survival [16, 17]. Our findings indicate that patients who did not receive chemotherapy or radiotherapy had a relatively poorer prognosis. Multivariate analysis further revealed that chemotherapy was associated with superior survival compared to radiotherapy. However, these modalities play distinct yet complementary roles. As a systemic treatment, chemotherapy acts on potential micrometastases and the occult primary tumor itself [18, 19]. Equally important, precise radiotherapy is indispensable for rapidly controlling intracranial lesions, alleviating neurological symptoms, and creating conditions for systemic therapy. Therefore, while systemic treatment serves as the core strategy for prolonging survival, radiotherapy remains essential for local control and symptom management.

Furthermore, our results corroborate the significant negative impact of multi-organ metastasis on the prognosis of CUP patients with brain metastases [20]. Multivariate analysis identified liver, lung, and bone metastases as independent risk factors, consistent with the general biological behavior of advanced cancer, where a higher metastatic burden correlates with greater difficulty in disease control and poorer prognosis. The underlying mechanisms for this phenomenon are multifaceted. On one hand, multi-organ metastasis is closely associated with higher tumor mutational burden and increased invasiveness. Previous studies have shown that extensive metastasis often correlates with high positivity rates in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) detection [21], reflecting the tumor’s sustained disseminative capacity within the body. On the other hand, multi-organ metastasis may indicate that tumor cells possess enhanced stem cell-like properties—including self-renewal, differentiation, and resistance to conventional therapies—making the disease more challenging to eradicate [22]. Several small-scale studies have confirmed the utility of ctDNA and circulating tumor cell (CTC) detection in monitoring cancer patient prognosis and guiding treatment decisions [23, 24]. Given the difficulty in determining the tissue of origin via metastatic lesion biopsy in CUP-BM, liquid biopsy technology presents a promising new avenue for guiding precise treatment [25].

Age was also identified as a key independent risk factor for prognosis. This association can be attributed to multiple biological and clinical factors. Physiologically, immunosenescence associated with aging leads to a decline in immune surveillance against tumors [26]. Specifically, this process is characterized by a shift from naive to memory T-cell compartments, decreased TCR (T-cell receptor) repertoire diversity, and an expansion of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, which limits the effective recognition and elimination of malignant cells [27–29]. Concurrently, older patients often have reduced functional reserve in vital organs, resulting in generally poorer tolerance to aggressive treatments like radiotherapy and chemotherapy, as well as an increased risk of treatment-related toxicities. From a comorbidity perspective, multimorbidity is highly prevalent in the elderly population. These concurrent chronic conditions can not only limit the intensity of antitumor therapy but also competitively influence the patient’s ultimate survival outcome [30–32]. Additionally, in the diagnostic process, clinical presentations in older adults are often atypical or easily mistaken for other age-related conditions. Combined with the inherently occult nature of the primary site, this frequently leads to diagnostic delays and missed potential windows for optimal treatment. Therefore, the clinical management of older CUP-BM patients urgently requires more proactive early screening awareness and a comprehensive geriatric assessment-based approach. This entails carefully balancing treatment benefits and risks while pursuing efficacy to develop individualized treatment strategies.

Regarding histological type, consistent with previous evidence on CUP with liver or bone metastases [10, 11], our study also confirmed that adenocarcinoma histology is an indicator of poor prognosis, likely due to its inherently more aggressive and metastatic potential. In contrast, patients with the neuroendocrine subtype exhibited a relatively favorable prognosis. This is biologically plausible, as neuroendocrine neoplasms tend to have slower growth kinetics and a more indolent disease course compared with poorly differentiated carcinomas [33]. Furthermore, NEC has been recognized as a favorable subset of CUP, with demonstrated responsiveness to platinum-based chemotherapy [34]. Nevertheless, this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of neuroendocrine cases in our cohort. However, unlike the aforementioned studies, the most frequent histological type in our CUP-BM cohort was not adenocarcinoma, but rather “carcinoma not otherwise specified (NOS).” This distribution highlights the unique challenges in the pathological diagnosis of this specific CUP-BM population. Due to the difficulty and high risk associated with intracranial metastatic lesion biopsies, the obtained tissue samples are often limited in quantity and quality, making it challenging for pathologists to obtain sufficient morphological and immunohistochemical evidence for precise classification. This situation underscores the difficulty of performing molecular profiling based on histology for this special disease entity. It further argues for the urgency of developing alternative classification methods and prognostic assessment tools, such as those utilizing liquid biopsy and other novel technologies [35].

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to the retrospective nature of the study, inherent selection bias is inevitable. Treatment decisions were guided by clinical judgment rather than randomized assignment, potentially leading to confounding by indication. Second, the SEER database lacks granular clinical information, including specific chemotherapy regimens, details on targeted therapy or immunotherapy, and patient comorbidities. Crucially, data on performance status—a key prognostic factor in patients with brain metastases—were unavailable, which precluded us from adjusting for this vital covariate in the multivariate analysis. Finally, our findings are based on a single population-based database without external validation. Although external validation with an independent cohort was precluded by the inherent rarity of CUP-BM, rigorous internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples confirmed the stability and reliability of our nomogram. Future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to verify our prognostic model and define the optimal treatment strategies for this specific population. Despite these constraints, in the absence of prospective data for this rare malignancy, our findings provide a necessary reference for clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This SEER database-based analysis identified age > 60 years, the presence of multi-organ metastasis, and adenocarcinoma histology as poor prognostic factors for patients with CUP-BM. Chemotherapy and neuroendocrine histology were associated with superior survival. The nomogram developed based on these variables demonstrated satisfactory predictive value and may assist clinicians in risk assessment and individualized management. Exploratory analyses revealed that while older age is generally an adverse factor, its prognostic influence was substantially attenuated in patients with extensive metastatic burden and varied by histological subtype, underscoring the complexity of prognostication in this heterogeneous population.
