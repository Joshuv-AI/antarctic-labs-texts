Ionic liquid as a structure-directing agent for Frank-Kasper phase formation in block copolymers
Authors: Aditya Sahare, Sumana Bandyopadhyay, Yu-Hsuan Lin, Chun-Yu Chen, Hsin-Lung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accessing Frank-Kasper (FK) phases in block copolymers (BCPs) traditionally requires complex molecular designs or polymer blending strategies. In this work, we demonstrate that incorporating the ionic liquid (IL), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EMIM][TFSI]), into a cylinder-forming poly(ethylene oxide)-block-poly(1,2-butadiene) (PEO-b-PB) provides a facile and tunable alternative that significantly expands the phase space for complex spherical micelle packings. A diverse array of structures, including σ, A15, and Laves C15 phases, emerges over broad composition and temperature ranges. Notably, we observe thermotropic A15 → σ → C15 and lyotropic σ → A15 → C15 phase transition sequences. Analysis reveals a dual role for the IL: it enhances the effective Flory-Huggins χ parameter through thermally responsive hydrogen bonding with PEO and, at elevated temperatures, redistributes to induce partially dry-brush segregation and micellar size dispersity. These synergistic effects stabilize the FK lattices, enabling reconfigurable complex spherical assemblies in a simple diblock system.

Introduction

Block copolymers (BCPs) are archetypal self-assembling materials capable of forming diverse periodic nanostructures with potential applications in nanolithography, energy storage, and photonics1–7. This morphological diversity arises from microphase separation of chemically distinct blocks, governed by a balance of interfacial interactions, chain stretching, and packing frustration. Under high compositional asymmetry, BCP melts typically form spherical micelles, where the minority block constitutes the core and the majority block forms the corona. While these micelles commonly adopt body-centered cubic (BCC) packing, more complex arrangements, notably Frank-Kasper (FK) phases, such as σ, A15, and Laves C14/C15, have drawn increasing attention due to their topological complexity and potential for emergent functionality8–16.

In BCP melts, the equilibrium packing of spherical micelles is dictated by the minimization of the total free energy under the constraint of uniform segmental density. Because spheres cannot fill space without voids, space filling necessitates micelle deformation toward the shapes of their local Voronoi cells within the lattice12,17–19. Such deformation introduces multiple free energy penalties, including increases in the elastic free energy associated with coronal chain deformation, core-corona interfacial free energy if the core deforms cooperatively with the corona, and the intermicellar free energy arising from the interdigitation or overlap of coronal chains between neighboring micelles20,21. The thermodynamically stable lattice corresponds to the configuration that minimizes the total free energy penalty. Typically, BCC lattice minimizes the conformational entropy loss of the coronal chains by maintaining a more uniform corona thickness, whereas FK phases achieve lower interfacial and intermicellar overlap free energies through a reduction in interfacial area and locally heterogeneous packing geometry17,18,21,22.

Theoretical studies predict that FK phases become favorable at higher core volume fractions (fc), where the core deformation toward the geometry of their Voronoi cells effectively leads to a more uniform corona thickness12,13,17,22. This cooperative deformation alleviates the elastic free energy penalty of the coronal chains and shifts the dominant free energy contribution toward interfacial interactions, ultimately favoring FK over BCC packing. However, BCPs with inherently high fc often favor structures such as hexagonally packed cylinders (HEX) or network phases17,21,22. This presents a key challenge: how to stabilize the spherical phase at elevated fc to access FK phases. To meet this challenge, strategies have been developed such as introducing pronounced architectural or conformational asymmetry in the constituent chains9,12,23–26 to promote interfacial bending toward the minority-block domain. Other approaches include multicomponent blending, such as mixing BCPs of different molecular weights and compositions14,27–30 or incorporating homopolymers31–36, as well as chemical modification of the block chains37,38. Collectively, these strategies enable tuning of micelle size and size dispersity, interfacial curvature, and packing frustration, thereby facilitating access to FK phases. More recently, studies on monodisperse, discrete BCPs with precisely controlled sequence, architecture, and stereochemistry have demonstrated the emergence of FK phases even in the absence of molecular weight dispersity39–41. These findings highlight that molecular weight polydispersity is not a prerequisite for accessing FK phases, and instead point to the importance of molecular design and local packing constraints in governing their stability.

Here, we show a strategy based on manipulating the relative free energies of the ordered and disordered states. In HEX-forming BCP with elevated fc, the free energy of the spherical phase remains higher than that of the disordered state, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). As a result, upon heating, the system undergoes a direct order-disorder transition (ODT) from HEX to disordered phase, bypassing the spherical morphology entirely. However, if the free energy of the disordered state can be selectively increased, more so than that of the ordered phases, through a targeted strategy, it may open a thermodynamic window for the spherical phase to emerge at elevated temperatures, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). Under such conditions, the system would first transition from the HEX phase to a spherical phase with increasing temperature, rather than directly to the disordered state. Thus, increasing χ provides an effective means for raising the free energy of the disordered state. Our previous study demonstrated that incorporating metal salts that preferentially interact with the polar poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) block while being strongly incompatible with the nonpolar poly(1,2-butadiene) (PB) block in a HEX-forming PEO-b-PB diblock copolymer effectively increased the χ parameter and elevated the ODT temperature (TODT), thereby enabling the formation of the FK σ phase42. Under specific thermal conditions, this approach also gave rise to a metastable Laves C14 phase.

In addition to this thermodynamic pathway, incorporating salts into the micellar core may introduce size dispersity among spherical domains. Although this effect has not been explicitly examined for salt-containing systems, self-consistent field theory (SCFT) studies of BCP/homopolymer blends have shown that additives can increase micelle size dispersity, thereby stabilizing FK phases32. Therefore, these two effects, i.e., modification of the free energy landscape and dispersity-induced stabilization of micellar packing, are expected to operate synergistically to promote the formation of FK phases in BCP/salt blends.

In this work, we introduce an ionic liquid (IL) as a versatile and tunable structure-directing agent that simultaneously modulates both the thermodynamic landscape and micellar characteristics. Specifically, incorporating 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EMIM][TFSI]) into the PEO core of PEO-b-PB simultaneously increases χ and the effective core volume fraction, thereby opening a broad composition window in which spherical micelles assemble into a full sequence of FK phases, including σ, A15, and C15. The stabilization of C15 as a thermodynamic phase highlights the distinct capability of ILs to reshape the free-energy landscape of micellar packing. Unlike the metal salt additives, ILs are nonvolatile, thermally robust, and strongly interactive with polyethers43–57; moreover, the temperature-dependent hydrogen bonding between [EMIM][TFSI] and PEO58–60 enables thermally regulated IL redistribution and responsive changes in micelle geometry. This dual functionality substantially expands the accessible phase space for complex spherical packings and positions IL incorporation as a scalable strategy for accessing FK phases in simple diblock copolymers.

Results and discussion

The chemical structures of the PEO-b-PB and the ionic liquid, [EMIM][TFSI], are provided in Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Information (SI). The PEO-b-PB sample used in this study had a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 4200 g/mol and a narrow polydispersity index (PDI = 1.04). The Mn values of the PEO and PB blocks were 1300 and 2900 g/mol, respectively, corresponding to a PEO volume fraction (fPEO) of 0.273. Blends of PEO-b-PB with [EMIM][TFSI] were prepared by solvent casting. The IL concentration in the blend was defined as the molar ratio of [EMIM][TFSI] to ethylene oxide (EO) repeating unit, γ = nIL/nEO. The blends with γ values ranging from 0.01 to 0.15 were prepared here.

According to our previous studies, the neat PEO-b-PB formed a HEX phase, in which the cylindrical micelles with PEO cores and PB coronas arranged in a two-dimensional hexagonal lattice42. Upon heating to approximately 125 °C, this HEX phase underwent an ODT without passing through a spherical phase. The present study investigated how the incorporation of [EMIM][TFSI] affected the self-assembly behavior of this PEO-b-PB. The IL was expected to selectively partition into the PEO core primarily through hydrogen-bonding interactions58–60, as schematically illustrated in Fig. S2 of the SI. This partitioning increased the effective core volume fraction, defined as the combined volume fractions of PEO and [EMIM][TFSI], i.e., fc = fEO + fIL. As summarized in Table S1 of the SI, even a small molar addition of IL resulted in a substantial increase in fc, due to the relatively high molecular weight (=391.3 g/mol) of [EMIM][TFSI], demonstrating its strong capacity to modulate micelle dimensions.

The self-assembled structures of PEO-b-PB/[EMIM][TFSI] blends were investigated as a function of temperature and IL concentration. The temperature-dependent small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles of the blend with γ = 0.02 (Fig. 2a) revealed a series of morphological transitions during heating. From 30 to 150 °C, the blend exhibited a HEX phase. Upon heating to 160 °C, new scattering peaks appeared, which further developed at 170 °C into a well-defined diffraction pattern characteristic of the FK σ phase8. The peaks could be indexed to the P4₂/mnm space group with tetragonal unit cell parameters a = 58.90 nm and c = 30.92 nm (c/a = 0.525). (Fig. S3 in SI). The σ phase remained stable up to 180 °C, beyond which an ODT was observed. Further increasing the IL content led to more intricate phase transitions. At γ = 0.04, a HEX phase was observed between 30 and 170 °C. Upon heating to 190 °C, a well-defined scattering pattern corresponding to the FK A15 phase emerged (Fig. 2b). Notably, the A15 phase underwent a direct order–order transition (OOT) to the σ phase at 210 °C, followed by an ODT at 230 °C upon further heating. Such an A15-to-σ transition has not been observed in BCP systems, although SCFT calculations have predicted its feasibility13,22.

The A15 phase represents a complex micelle packing that is generally more difficult to access than the σ phase in conventional linear coil-coil BCPs. SCFT studies have shown that A15 stability can be enhanced by substantially increasing conformational asymmetry or by incorporating branched molecular architectures, such as ABn-type copolymers9,13,18. The A15 phase has also been experimentally observed in blends of asymmetric and symmetric BCPs61. The spontaneous formation of the A15 phase and its subsequent transition to the σ phase in our system highlight the ability of the IL to effectively modulate both the thermodynamic landscape and the kinetic pathways governing FK-phase formation.

At an IL concentration of γ = 0.05, the HEX morphology persisted from 30 °C to 190 °C, followed by a transition to the A15 phase at 200 °C (Fig. 2c). Further heating induced a transformation to the σ phase at 230 °C, and upon reaching 240 °C, a distinct diffraction pattern indexed to the Laves C15 phase (Fd\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{3}$$\end{document}3¯m, a = 53.08 nm; Fig. S5 in SI) emerged.

As the IL concentration was further increased to γ = 0.07, the system exhibited a lamellar (LAM) morphology at 30 °C (Fig. 2d). This behavior arose from the increased core fraction (fc = 0.354) combined with strong segregation strength, both of which disfavored the formation of highly curved interfaces and instead stabilized planar lamellae that minimized the interfacial free energy at low temperatures. Upon heating, the LAM phase transitioned to a HEX structure and then directly to the Laves C15 phase at 210 °C, notably bypassing the A15 and σ phases observed at lower IL loadings. This sequential LAM → HEX → C15 transition highlights a distinct thermotropic pathway in which the system directly accessed the C15 lattice without passing through intermediate FK structures.

Laves phases are typically associated with packings of particles that exhibit a greater size disparity than those forming σ or A15 lattices11,12,62. In neat BCP systems, the Laves C15 and C14 phases are generally regarded as metastable and tend to appear only after specific thermal treatments that generate micelles with a relatively broad size distribution11,63,64. Such polydispersity introduces the size asymmetry required for binary packing, but it often reflects nonequilibrium states induced by processing history rather than equilibrium structures. In contrast, thermodynamically stable Laves phases can emerge in the blends of BCPs with homopolymers or with BCPs of different chain lengths29,32,65, where the micelles inherently develop size heterogeneity. This intrinsic disparity provides a robust driving force for the formation of C14 and C15 lattices under equilibrium conditions.

This study demonstrates that incorporating the ionic liquid [EMIM][TFSI] into a cylinder-forming PEO-b-PB system substantially broadened the accessible phase space, stabilizing a rich set of complex FK morphologies over wide composition and temperature ranges, as evidenced by the morphological diagram derived from the SAXS results (Fig. 3). Notably, all types of FK phases previously reported in BCP systems, including the σ, A15, and Laves structures, appeared even at relatively low IL concentrations (γ < 0.1). Another key finding of this work was the thermotropic A15-to-σ transition observed upon heating, as evidenced by the temperature-dependent SAXS profiles in Fig. 2b. This observation provides experimental support for earlier SCFT predictions of this transition in BCP systems9,13,22. Its absence in conventional BCPs can be attributed to two factors. First, the A15 phase is only stable in the systems with significant conformational asymmetry and therefore occupies a very limited region of the phase diagram9,13,18. Second, although SCFT predicts an A15-to-σ transition upon decreasing χN, thermal fluctuations may destabilize the σ phase near the ODT, leading instead to a direct A15-to-disordered phase transition13. In the present BCP/IL system, the IL incorporation enhanced the segregation strength and partially suppressed fluctuation effects, thereby enabling the σ phase to emerge within an experimentally accessible temperature window. This shift in the balance between mean-field thermodynamics and fluctuation effects allowed the A15-to-σ transition to be realized.

Moreover, at a higher IL concentration (γ = 0.05), the A15-to-σ transition was followed by an additional transformation into the C15 phase, as shown in Fig. 2c. This sequence of transitions constituted an unusual OOT pathway within the spherical phase regime of BCP.

With respect to the lyotropic sequence driven by increasing fc, the micelle packing was found to evolve from σ to A15 and subsequently to C15 with increasing IL content, as illustrated in Fig. 4. While the σ-to-A15 transition has been predicted by SCFT13,22, the observed lyotropic A15-to-C15 transition further expands the range of experimentally accessible FK transition pathways in BCP systems. A closer examination of the data, however, revealed that above approximately 100 °C the rate of decrease became substantially weaker. For the γ/0.05 blend, the temperature dependence of Rc even reversed: Rc began to increase with temperature once the system reached approximately 160 °C, even though it remained in the HEX phase regime (Fig. 5b). Importantly, this upturn in Rc could not be ascribed to a reversal of the temperature dependence of χPB-EO/IL, because the system still underwent an ODT upon heating. The observed behavior must therefore originate from an additional mechanism beyond the χ-driven softening of polar-nonpolar segregation.

A striking feature of the results was the pronounced swelling of both Rm and Rc at the cylinder-to-sphere transition, where these parameters underwent a nearly discontinuous and substantial increase. In contrast, the PB corona thickness experienced only a slight and similarly abrupt decrease across the OOT. Such a sudden enlargement of the micelle core is highly atypical for BCP melts, in which domain sizes generally decrease or vary smoothly with increasing temperature. Even during OOT that involves geometric reconstruction, such as the conventional HEX → BCC transition, the accompanying changes in domain dimensions are usually modest. The large, step-like expansion observed here therefore suggested that additional physical mechanisms contributed to the structural reorganization across the transition.

One possible factor contributing to the swelling of the PEO/IL core at elevated temperatures was the temperature-dependent interaction between the IL and the PEO block. At lower temperatures, the IL molecules formed extensive hydrogen bonds with the PEO chains58–60, effectively anchoring the IL within the PEO-rich domains. These enthalpically favorable interactions restricted the available free volume and suppressed the translational entropy of the IL molecules. As the temperature increased, the hydrogen bonds progressively weakened, and the system developed a stronger thermodynamic drive to restore the translational entropy of the IL. Under such conditions, a fraction of the IL may segregate from the PEO chains and accumulate toward the interior of the PEO/IL domain, producing a partially dry-brush-like distribution. IL molecules located in this segregated region, however, were subject to confinement within the finite core volume, and the associated entropic penalty grew with increasing temperature if the confining volume remained fixed. This created a thermodynamic incentive for the PEO/IL core to expand to provide additional free volume for the IL. Nevertheless, the magnitude of this IL-driven expansion appeared to be modest, as evidenced by the only weak temperature-induced increases in Rc observed in both the HEX phase and in the spherical phase above ~190 °C. Consequently, the nearly discontinuous and pronounced increase in Rc at the cylinder-to-sphere transition could not be accounted for solely by the weakening of IL-PEO interactions or by the gradual relief of IL confinement. This indicates that another mechanism, beyond IL thermodynamics, was responsible for the abrupt change in core size.

The discontinuity must instead originate from the structural reorganization associated with the transition from cylinders to spheres and, more importantly, from the selection of FK lattices for packing the spherical micelles. The relationship implies that the corona becomes thinner than the core once <fc> ~0.13, a regime in which FK phases commonly arise. Under these conditions, the coronal chains lack sufficient radial space and are therefore compelled to spread tangentially along the core-corona interface, resulting in enhanced compression in the radial direction. Such compression imposes a confinement effect on the corona chains and leads to a loss of conformational entropy when the chains are restricted within a narrowly defined corona layer66.

In the space-filled melt, neighboring micelles are in direct contact, which permits portions of the corona chains to extend beyond their own intermicellar boundaries and enter the corona layers of adjacent micelles. Such intrusion partially alleviates radial confinement and produces interdigitated or overlapping regions. However, this interdigitation introduces an opposing entropic penalty: the overlapping chain segments must tilt and reorient within the shared corona region, leading to a reduction in orientational entropy67.

The FK lattices appearing in this system, such as σ, A15, and Laves C15, possess Voronoi cells with smaller average surface areas per micelle than the BCC lattice typically observed in BCP melts12,16,20. The reduced interfacial area implies less intermicellar contact, which correspondingly lowers the extent of corona interdigitation between neighboring micelles. A reduced degree of interdigitation alleviates the orientational entropy penalty. However, limiting interdigitation also increases the conformational free energy of the coronal chains. The stability of the FK phases thus reflects a balance between these competing effects, and the fact that the system forms FK structures indicates that the reduction of intermicellar contact (and the associated decrease in orientational entropy penalty) dominates over the conformational cost incurred by restricting corona interdigitation.

To further reduce the intermicellar contact area, the micelles packed in FK lattices tend to increase their size, thereby decreasing the total interfacial area within the space-filling constraint of the lattice. In other words, the thermodynamic drive to minimize the intermicellar surface free energy cost associated with filling space favors both the adoption of FK lattices and the enlargement of the micelles within those lattices. This interplay gave rise to the nearly discontinuous and pronounced swelling of the micelles observed across the HEX → FK phase transition66.

Across the transition, the PB corona thickness lcn decreased slightly rather than increasing. This behavior reflected the asymmetric manner in which the core and corona responded to the change in micelle geometry. Although FK packing enabled a sudden enlargement of the PEO/IL core, the PB chains nonetheless encountered greater interfacial curvature and a larger effective interfacial area per junction point when the morphology transformed from cylinders to spheres. These geometric constraints promoted a slight contraction of the corona layer, and consequently the PB corona thickness decreased mildly even as the core underwent a pronounced expansion.

In the FK phase regime, the core radius exceeded 7 nm and even surpassed 8 nm for the γ/0.07 blend (Fig. 5). This dimension approached the fully extended contour length of the PEO block (approximately 8 nm for Mn = 1300 g/mol). Because nearly complete stretching of the PEO chains would impose a prohibitive conformational entropy penalty, the PEO chains should not be uniformly extended throughout the entire core. Instead, the results indicated that the core adopted a compositionally structured configuration in which the IL preferentially accumulated near the center of the domain, while the PEO chains resided predominantly in a shell surrounding this IL-rich region. Although this PEO shell may remain partially swollen by IL, such a segregated core architecture alleviated the severe stretching penalty that would arise if PEO and IL were fully mixed and the PEO chains were required to extend uniformly to fill the entire core. This interpretation was consistent with a partially dry-brush configuration generated by local demixing between PEO and IL within the core domain, as discussed earlier.

This dry-brush-like mixing behavior in the PEO-b-PB/IL system resembled the well-established dry-brush state in FK-forming blends of block copolymers with homopolymers whose molecular weight is comparable to that of the core-forming block and that preferentially partition into the central region of the micelle core34,65,68,69. In such BCP/homopolymer blends, the homopolymer-rich domain is enveloped by a brush-like layer of the core-forming block chains. This architecture is known to stabilize spherical packings even at unusually high core volume fractions and to promote the formation of FK phases.

From a theoretical perspective, SCFT studies have shown that the incorporation of homopolymers into micellar cores leads to nonuniform domain swelling and enhanced size dispersity32,34. Such size differentiation plays a crucial role in stabilizing FK phases, as these structures can accommodate unequal domain volumes and geometries, thereby reducing the overall interfacial free energy relative to classical sphere packings. Importantly, this theoretical framework clarifies that the emergence of spherical and FK phases in the PEO-b-PB/IL system cannot be attributed to an increase in χ alone. While increasing χ elevates TODT and enabled the stabilization of spherical phases at elevated temperatures, the nonuniform distribution of IL among micellar domains, which generated domain size dispersity, played a crucial role in stabilizing FK phases.

Indeed, SCFT calculations and experimental studies have shown that dry-brush-type BCP/homopolymer systems can exhibit a sequence of FK packings, including σ and Laves C14/C15 phases32,34,65,66,68,69. In the AB4 miktoarm copolymer/A-homopolymer system, where the neat conformationally asymmetric copolymer stabilizes the A15 phase, the addition of homopolymer preferentially promotes the emergence of the more strongly size-nonuniform Laves phases by inducing a nonuniform redistribution of homopolymer among distinct domains32.

Our observation of a similarly broad C15 stability window in the present PEO-b-PB/IL blends was consistent with these theoretical predictions, indicating that the emergence of FK phases arose from a mechanism closely analogous to that in classical dry-brush BCP/homopolymer systems. In this context, the ionic liquid played a role analogous to that of the homopolymer by selectively partitioning into the micelle core and inducing heterogeneous swelling, thereby generating domain size dispersity.

The key distinction, however, is that in the IL-containing system, the extent of dry-brush behavior and core segregation was thermally regulated through temperature-dependent IL-PEO interactions, whereas in conventional BCP/homopolymer blends the dry-brush state was governed primarily by homopolymer molecular weight. Moreover, our system started from a cylinder-forming linear diblock and accessed a broad thermotropic and lyotropic sequence of FK phases through IL incorporation.

It is noteworthy that the A15 phase has not been predicted or observed in linear BCP/homopolymer dry-brush blends. In prior SCFT studies, A15 appeared only when the neat copolymer already possessed strong conformational asymmetry (e.g., AB4 miktoarm copolymers) and intrinsically supported A15 packing, such that the blend inherited this structural motif32. In contrast, the present PEO-b-PB/IL system, based on a linear diblock that did not exhibit A15 in its neat state, was able to access the A15 phase upon IL incorporation. This distinction suggests that the ionic liquid acted as a more versatile structure-directing agent, enabling access to a wider range of complex micellar packings.

Overall, this work demonstrates that incorporating the ionic liquid [EMIM][TFSI] into a cylinder-forming PEO-b-PB provides a versatile and tunable platform for accessing FK phases that are rarely achievable in simple linear architectures. IL incorporation simultaneously increases χ and the effective core volume fraction, enabling spherical micelles to become thermodynamically competitive at elevated temperatures and giving rise to a broad window of FK packings. Within this window, we observed the complete sequence of σ, A15, and Laves C15 structures, together with the thermotropic A15 → σ → C15 and lyotropic σ → A15 → C15 transitions.

Temperature-dependent structural analysis revealed that the IL reshaped the free energy landscape not only by enhancing the segregation strength but also by inducing a partially dry-brush core through thermally regulated PEO-IL demixing. This process led to nonuniform IL partitioning within micellar cores, resulting in heterogeneous core swelling and enhanced domain-size dispersity, which is a key factor in stabilizing FK phases. The associated, nearly discontinuous expansion of the micelle core at the cylinder-to-sphere transition reflected the cooperative interplay among IL redistribution, micelle geometry, and the lowered intermicellar surface free energy cost associated with FK lattice selection. The IL-regulated core-corona asymmetry thus provides a thermodynamic route for stabilizing FK phases that parallels, but is more versatile than, the classical dry-brush mechanism in BCP/homopolymer blends. A schematic summary of this synergistic mechanism, where χ enhancement enables access to spherical phases and IL-induced dispersity stabilizes FK packings, is provided in Fig. S8 of the SI.

Collectively, these findings identify ionic-liquid incorporation as a scalable, synthesis-free strategy for directing micellar packing into a wide spectrum of FK phases and for enabling reconfigurable spherical assemblies in BCPs. By coupling χ enhancement with temperature-responsive core restructuring, this approach opens alternative pathways for bottom-up fabrication of ordered soft materials and expands the design principles for accessing geometrically complex sphere packings in polymer systems.

Methods

Materials and blend preparation

The PEO-b-PB diblock copolymer used in this study was obtained from Polymer Source, Inc. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis revealed a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 4200 g/mol and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.04. Further characterization using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy determined the molecular weights of the PEO and PB blocks to be 2900 g/mol and 1300 g/mol, respectively, corresponding to a PEO volume fraction (fPEO) of 0.273. NMR analysis also confirmed the PB block to consist of 90.2 mol% 1,2-addition and 9.8 mol% 1,4-addition units.

The ionic liquid [EMIM][TFSI] (99% purity), a clear, faint yellow liquid, was purchased from UniRegion Biotech. Blends of PEO-b-PB with [EMIM][TFSI] were prepared via a solvent casting method. A predetermined amount of PEO-b-PB was dissolved with [EMIM] [TFSI] in tetrahydrofuran (THF), sealed in a vial, and stirred for 24 h to ensure thorough mixing. The resulting solution was dried under ambient conditions in a fume hood for 24 h to remove most of the THF, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C for four hours to yield the final blend. Synchrotron small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements were conducted at the TPS 25A1 beamline at the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC), Hsinchu, Taiwan. A monochromatic X-ray beam (15 keV) was used to collect two-dimensional scattering patterns on an EIGER 16 M detector. Scattering intensity was recorded as a function of the scattering vector magnitude q, defined as q = (4π/λ)sin(θ/2), where λ is the X-ray wavelength and θ is the scattering angle.

For temperature-dependent SAXS, samples were heated at ~5 °C/min, and data were collected at 10 °C intervals. Each temperature point was equilibrated for five minutes before acquisition, with a typical exposure time of 0.3 s. For clarity, we displayed the SAXS profiles at selected temperatures in the main text.
