Cardiovascular Safety of T Cell Engagers
Authors: Qun Shao, Tong Liu, Yue Li, Zhi-Ren Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

T cell engager (TCE) therapies are rapidly becoming an important cornerstone of cancer therapy. Several agents, such as blinatumomab, teclistamab, and glofitamab, have been routinely used to treat hematologic malignancies, while other TCEs have expanded to treat solid tumors.1, 2, 3 As these therapies expand their antineoplastic indications, there is a growing attention on their safety profile, including potential cardiovascular toxicities.

However, from a cardio-oncology perspective, the currently available data on TCE-associated cardiovascular toxicity remain limited. While pharmacovigilance datasets may be helpful for recognizing TCE-associated toxicities, these datasets cannot estimate true incidence or infer causality due to the absence of reliable exposure “denominators” and systematic follow-up.4 In this context, real-world clinical cohort data are particularly valuable for defining the spectrum, frequency, and clinical relevance of TCE therapy-associated cardiovascular adverse events.

In this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, a 2-center retrospective cohort study by Ben-Zadok et al5 systematically evaluated the incidence rate and types of adverse events, as well as the prognostic implications of cardiovascular events (CVEs) in 567 patients treated with TCEs. This study provides timely and clinically relevant real-world evidence. This study suggests that the cumulative incidence of CVEs was 10.4%, and cardiovascular death was rare at 0.4%.5 The most common adverse events were left ventricular systolic dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, and acute coronary syndrome. To further inform the epidemiology of cardiovascular disease with TCE, in this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, a research letter by Grunblatt et al6 detailed the single-center experience of 140 patients who received bispecific T-cell redirecting antibody therapies, and suggested that the cumulative incidence for any CVE was 12.3% (95% CI: 7.2%-18.5%). This included moderate-to-severe cardiac dysfunction (6.6%; 95% CI: 2.9%-11.6%), atrial fibrillation (5.4%; 95% CI: 2.2%-9.9%), and myocardial infarction (0.8%; 95% CI: 0.0004%-3.1%).

Overall, these findings suggest that although cardiovascular toxicity is not negligible, in our view, it is not sufficiently common or severe to justify indiscriminate, high-intensity surveillance for all patients receiving TCEs.

However, at the same time, an approximately 10% to 12% adverse event rate is clinically meaningful in real-world practice, particularly because as demonstrated in both studies, the events appeared to occur early in the treatment course. As TCEs are increasingly used in older and more medically complex patients, the practical consequences of this toxicity profile may become even more important.

Ben-Zadok et al5 demonstrated that cardiovascular risk was not evenly distributed across patients. Baseline coronary artery disease emerged as the only independent predictor of CVEs, with a subdistribution HR of 4.06, suggesting the importance of careful assessment for patients with prevalent ischemic heart disease. In patients with known coronary artery disease, guideline-directed management and optimization of cardiovascular risk factors and disease should be emphasized. Although the study did not test whether such measures reduce CVEs, the observed risk pattern provides a clinical rationale for this approach.

In addition, development of grade ≥2 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome during treatment was associated with a significantly higher risk of CVEs (HR: 2.27). These findings reinforce the need to move beyond a “one-size-fits-all” view of TCE usage safety and instead identify those patients most likely to benefit from closer cardiovascular attention and surveillance.

Both studies have implications for clinical practice. Cardiovascular management in patients receiving TCEs should place greater emphasis on risk stratification. Patients with atherosclerotic risk factors or known coronary artery disease may warrant more structured cardiovascular evaluation before treatment initiation, along with optimization of modifiable risk factors. During the early TCE treatment period, particularly in patients who develop CRS or immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome, a lower threshold for obtaining electrocardiograms, cardiac biomarkers, or echocardiography may be appropriate.

The association between grade ≥2 CRS and CVEs that was observed in the larger study by Ben Zadok et al5 also raises an important mechanistic and therapeutic question: does proactive use of cytokine blockade, such as tocilizumab, reduce the downstream cardiovascular burden associated with inflammatory toxicity in patients at higher risk? At present, available evidence does not support the conclusion that intensified anticytokine treatment reduces CVE rates; therefore, this should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Nevertheless, if inflammatory syndromes such as CRS are indeed a major mediator of TCE-associated cardiovascular toxicity, then earlier or more effective control of inflammation could be clinically important. This question deserves further evaluation in our view.

Another clinically important observation is the association between CVEs and survival. Ben-Zadok et al5 found that the occurrence of CVEs was associated with a significantly increased risk of subsequent all-cause mortality, which is consistent with prior work by Sayed et al.4 Nevertheless, these data support the idea that cardiovascular toxicity during TCE therapy is not merely an isolated adverse event, but also may have broader implications for patient outcomes. From a clinical standpoint, this finding underscores the possibility that prevention and timely management of CVEs may have the potential to reduce mortality risk in patients receiving TCE therapy.

There are a few limitations that may temper interpretation of these findings. Both studies were retrospective, without uniform cardiovascular monitoring, and follow-up time was relatively short. In addition, the cohorts did not include patients treated with talquetamab or patients with solid tumors, limiting its generalizability across the expanded landscape of TCE therapy. Future investigations should focus on larger prospective studies that can better define cardiovascular risk across newer TCE agents and in broader populations, including patients with solid tumors. It will also be important to identify which patients are most likely to develop high-grade CRS and to clarify the mechanistic links between inflammatory toxicities and cardiovascular complications. In this regard, prospective studies could test whether preventive use of cytokine-blocking strategies in high-risk patients is feasible and whether it reduces severe immune toxicity and secondary CVEs.7

Overall, these studies are an important step forward in defining the cardiovascular safety profile of TCE therapy. The available evidence suggests that TCE therapy is associated with an overall reassuring cardiovascular safety profile but that risk is not uniform across patients. Identifying high-risk individuals and applying targeted monitoring and management strategies will likely be central to optimizing the safe delivery of TCE therapy. As TCE therapy continues to expand across malignancies and treatment settings, predicting and recognizing TCE therapy–associated cardiovascular toxicities will become an increasingly important priority.
