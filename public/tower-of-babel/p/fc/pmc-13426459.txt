IL-22 synergizes with IL-17 to promote mucosal inflammation and bone loss
Authors: Xiaofei Li, Xiaolan Ye, Xiang Yu, Shuai Liu, Hui Wang, Jonathan M Korostoff, Gundappa Saha, Jong-Hyung Lim, George Hajishengallis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterleukin (IL)-22 mediates immune cell communication with nonhematopoietic cells and was shown to exert protective or destructive effects in different disease contexts. In the oral mucosal disease periodontitis, IL-22 has been associated with increased tissue destruction, although cause-and-effect evidence and the underlying mechanisms are lacking. Here, we showed that endogenous IL-22 was required for experimental periodontitis in mice, whereas local administration of exogenous IL-22 exacerbated periodontal inflammation and bone loss. Importantly, the ability of IL-22 to induce expression of inflammatory cytokines and tissue-degrading metalloproteinases as well as cause bone loss required intact IL-17 function, suggesting a potential cooperation between the two cytokines. As human fibroblasts prominently coexpress IL-22 and IL-17 receptors in the periodontal tissue and play a role in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, we examined them in vitro as potential targets of a destructive IL-22–IL-17 interplay. IL-22 synergized with IL-17 for enhanced nuclear factor κB–mediated inflammatory responses (IL-6, matrix metalloproteinase-1) in a STAT3-dependent manner. Analysis of cytosolic and nuclear extracts revealed that IL-22 enhanced nuclear factor κB p65 phosphorylation and translocation to the nucleus of IL-17–stimulated fibroblasts. In conclusion, our study provides causal evidence for IL-22 involvement in periodontitis and describes a hitherto unknown IL-22–IL-17 synergy in inflammatory bone loss.Keywords: bone loss, IL-17, IL-22, mucosal inflammation, periodontitis

Introduction

Interleukin (IL)-22, a member of the IL-10 cytokine family, is produced by innate and adaptive immune cells (e.g. γδ T cells, group 3 innate lymphoid cells, and CD4+ T cells) and acts in a unidirectional manner on nonhematopoietic cells, including epithelial and stromal cells.1–4 This one-way communication is due to the fact that the IL-22 receptor (IL-22R) complex (heterodimer of IL-22Rα1 and IL-10Rβ2) is restrictively expressed by nonhematopoietic cells.3,5 The binding of IL-22 to IL-22R is antagonized by the IL-22 binding protein (IL-22BP), a secreted single-chain receptor (IL-22Rα2) that binds IL 22 with high affinity.6 IL-22–induced activation of IL-22R leads to phosphorylation of the JAK1 and TYK2 kinases followed by phosphorylation of STAT3 and other transcription factors that control genes involved in important homeostatic functions: tight and gap junction formation, antimicrobial immunity (e.g. induction of the production of antimicrobial molecules S100A8/A9 and Reg3β/3γ), and stem cell survival and proliferation. Collectively, these processes promote maintenance of mucosal barrier integrity or tissue repair when needed.3–5,7–12

Despite its role in promoting homeostatic immunity and tissue regeneration,8,11,12 IL-22 is also implicated in certain inflammatory disorders, and often shows an ambiguous dual nature (detrimental versus protective) that has confounded its potential as a therapeutic target.2,5,9,13 The conditions under which IL-22 can be protective or destructive are incompletely understood.3,13–19 In psoriasis, uncontrolled expression of IL-22 contributes to chronic inflammation, tissue damage and epidermal hyperplasia.9,15–17 In rheumatoid arthritis, IL-22 is elevated in synovial tissues and promotes inflammation and destruction of bone, in part by inducing expression of RANKL.20–22 After onset of collagen-induced arthritis in mice, antibody-mediated neutralization of endogenous IL-22 decreased the severity of arthritis; however, neutralization of endogenous IL-22 prior to the onset of collagen-induced arthritis increased the severity of arthritis,23 suggesting that IL-22 plays a context-dependent role in the disease. In line with suggestive clinical evidence that IL-22 is associated with protection against human inflammatory bowel disease,24–29 IL-22 was shown to play a protective role in different mouse models of colitis by promoting innate immunity and/or mucosal wound healing.8,30–32 Consistently, we showed that colitis associated with leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1 is attributed to defective production of IL-22.33 However, in a model of colitis that mimics colitis in children with IL-10R deficiencies, IL-22 induces epithelial chemokine expression and harmful recruitment of neutrophils that drive gut pathology.14

Periodontitis is a prevalent chronic mucosal disease associated with increased risk of systemic comorbidities and poses a significant economic and public health burden.34–37 The disease is initiated by dysbiosis of the oral microbiome and is mediated by a complement–T helper 17 (Th17)/IL-17 inflammatory axis, which causes destructive inflammation of the gingival mucosa and loss of the underlying alveolar bone.38–43 Genetic ablation or antibody-mediated neutralization of IL-17 (i.e. IL-17A) blocks inflammatory bone loss.39,42,43 Complement acts upstream of IL-17 and is required for maximal Th17/IL-17 responses in experimental periodontitis,40 while local complement inhibition (with the C3-targeted drug AMY-101) in human patients leads to significant and sustained resolution of periodontal inflammation.44 In contrast to the well-defined role of IL-17, the role of IL-22 in inflammatory periodontal bone loss remains poorly defined.

Three independent studies of human subjects showed that gingival crevicular fluid collected from periodontitis-affected sites exhibited higher concentrations of IL-22 relative to samples obtained from periodontally healthy control subjects.45–47 One of the studies also reported that higher IL-22 concentrations correlated with increased severity of periodontal tissue destruction.46 IL-22 concentrations in the gingival crevicular fluid were also positively associated with those of human β defensin-2.45 Similar to the findings in human periodontitis patients, IL-22 expression is significantly elevated in the gingival tissue of mice and rats subjected to experimental periodontitis.48,49 The significance of these findings must be interpreted with caution since cytokines that are upregulated during an inflammatory disease may not necessarily have a proinflammatory/destructive role but may alternatively exert anti-inflammatory and/or antimicrobial effects to mitigate ongoing destructive activity.

Together, the previously described correlative studies indicate that IL-22 plays a role in periodontitis, although understanding its precise influence (protective or destructive) would require cause-and-effect experiments. To address this knowledge gap, in the present work we performed genetic and interventional experiments in mice, as well as mechanistic studies using in vitro culture models. We showed that IL-22 is detrimental in experimental periodontitis by engaging in destructive synergy with IL-17. Thus, whereas IL-22–IL-17 synergy may be beneficial in certain infections,4,50 the IL-22–IL-17 axis drives mucosal inflammation and bone loss in periodontitis.

Methods

Human subjects and gingival tissue collection

Collection of human periodontal tissue samples was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania. Informed consent was obtained to procure normally discarded gingival tissue from individuals undergoing periodontal surgery in the postgraduate clinic of the Department of Periodontics, University of Pennsylvania School of Dental Medicine. All subjects were 18 yr of age or older with no serious medical illness contraindicative of surgical treatment. Diseased tissue was obtained from 7 patients with stage II or III periodontitis (defined by probing pocket depth ≥5 mm, clinical attachment loss ≥4 mm, bleeding on probing, and radiographic evidence of bone loss) who underwent pocket-reduction surgery after scaling and root planing. Control tissue specimens were obtained from 4 clinically healthy subjects (probing pocket depth ≤3 mm, no clinical attachment loss, absence of bleeding on probing, and normal radiographic bone levels) who required crown-lengthening surgery. The mean demographic and clinical parameters of the two groups are shown in Table 1.

Mice

Il22 −/− mice on the C57BL/6 background were generated by Genentech and have been described previously.15Il22−/− mice were crossed with WT C57BL/6 (The Jackson Laboratory) mice to generate Il22−/− and Il22+/+ (wild-type [WT]) littermates. Local microinjection of IL22−Fc, Fc control, neutralizing anti-IL17A monoclonal antibody (mAb), or IgG1 isotype control was applied to randomly assigned mice. Sex- and age-matched mice were used for experiments at 8 to 10 wk of age, and were kept in individually ventilated cages under specific pathogen–free conditions. Because there were no significant differences in the results obtained with male and female mice, the respective data were pooled per treatment group. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pennsylvania and were performed in compliance with institutional, state, and federal guidelines.

Ligature-induced periodontitis model and gingival microinjections

Ligature-induced periodontitis was performed in mice as previously described.51 Briefly, a 5-0 silk ligature was tied around the maxillary left second molar, whereas the contralateral molar tooth in each mouse was left unligated (baseline control). Mice were euthanized on day 5 or day 10 (as specified in figure legends), and gingival tissues around the area of ligature placement and the contralateral unligated (control) area were harvested for analysis. Periodontal bone loss in defleshed maxillae was assessed morphometrically under a dissecting microscope (×40) (Nikon Instruments) as previously described.51 The distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) on 6 predetermined points on the ligated second molar and the affected adjacent regions was measured. Bone loss was calculated by subtracting the 6-site total CEJ–ABC distance of the ligated side of each mouse from the 6-site total CEJ–ABC distance of the contralateral unligated side. In interventional experiments, IL-22-Fc, anti-IL-17, or their respective control reagents were administered by local microinjection into the palatal gingiva between the first and second maxillary molars using a 33‑gauge stainless steel needle attached to a Hamilton microsyringe (Fisher Scientific; catalog no. 7633‑01). The needle was inserted at a 45° angle, and a volume of 3 µL was delivered per site.

Proteins and inhibitors

IL-22-Fc protein was kindly provided by Genentech (IL-22–mIgG2a fusion protein, PRO312045), and Fc protein control was purchased from R&D Systems. For in vitro stimulation of human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (hPDLFs) and human gingival fibroblasts (hGFs), recombinant human IL-22 (catalog #782-IL-010/CF) and IL-17A (catalog #317-ILB-050) were purchased from R&D Systems. Nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) inhibitor (BAY11-7082, catalog #S2913) and STAT3 signaling inhibitor (Stattic, catalog #S7024) were from Selleck Chemicals.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total cellular RNA was isolated from mouse and human tissues using TRIzol (Life Technologies). For real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), total RNA was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA using the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Applied Biosystems), and real-time PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Applied Biosystems). TaqMan probes and gene-specific primers for detection and quantification of murine and human genes were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Table S1). Data were analyzed using the comparative (ΔΔCt) method and were normalized to mouse Gapdh or human GAPDH messenger RNA (mRNA).

Oral mucosal cell atlas single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

The human oral mucosa cell atlas-associated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data52 were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information with Gene Expression Omnibus number GSE164241. The scRNA-seq data were processed as described in https://github.com/williamsdrake/oralAtlas with slight modifications. Briefly, the scRNA-seq data processed with Cell Ranger v2.0.0 (10x Genomics) were utilized as input for the Seurat R package.53 Eight periodontal disease (PD) gingival mucosa samples were merged into a PD Seurat project. After normalization and identification of variable features, the PD Seurat projects underwent batch correction to generate a new Integrated Seurat project called oralIntegrated_HvP. Dimensionality reduction and cell clustering were performed, followed by identification of significant markers for each cluster. Clusters were classified into 5 cell types: endothelial, fibroblast, epithelial, immune, and other, based on their markers, with red blood cells filtered out. The average expression of IL22RA1, IL17RA, MMP1, IL6, and TNFSF11 genes among the aforementioned 5 cell types in PD samples was calculated and displayed as a heatmap using the Doheatmap function. Additionally, the SingleR package was used to annotate cell subtypes of immune cells in PD samples, and the expression of IL22, IL17A and IL22RA2 in these immune cell subsets was visualized as a heatmap.

Cell culture

hPDLFs were purchased from Lonza and human gingival fibroblasts were isolated from gingiva of healthy human subjects as previously described.54 Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 4.00 mM L-glutamine, 4,500 mg/L glucose, and 100 units/mL of penicillin-streptomycin, and were maintained in 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C.

Immunoassays

The concentrations of human IL-6 and matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP1) in culture supernatant of hPDLF and hGF cells were determined using Human IL-6 DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems; catalog DY206-05) and Human MMP1 DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems; catalog DY901B-05) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunoblotting

hPDLFs were seeded (3 × 105 cells/2 mL) in 6-well plates overnight and stimulated with human IL-17A (10 ng/mL) alone or in combination with human IL-22 (10 ng/mL) for up to 60 min. Cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and harvested. Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions (Boster), and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Briefly, equal amounts of protein in loading buffer were subject to SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (Millipore) and blocked with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) buffer (Beyotime) containing 5% nonfat milk (Sangon Biotech). Membranes were incubated on the shaker overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in TBST buffer containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Blots were then washed in TBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with corresponding secondary horseradish peroxidase–conjugated antibodies, diluted in TBST buffer containing 5% BSA, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Blots were washed and developed using the Hypersensitive ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (Proteintech). Rabbit mAb to STAT3 (catalog #9139 T), Phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705, catalog #9145), NF-κB p65 (catalog #8242), and phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536, catalog #3033) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Rabbit polyclonal antibodies to GAPDH (catalog #10494-1-AP) and to proliferating cell nuclear antigen (catalog #10205-2-AP) as well as horseradish peroxidase–conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) were purchased from Proteintech (catalog #SA00001-2).

Immunofluorescence histochemistry

Mouse gingival tissue blocks were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in Immunocal decalcifier solution (Decal) for 15 d, and embedded in OCT compound. Serial sections (8 µm thick) of the teeth and gingiva were stained with rat anti-mouse IL-22RA1 (1:100, clone 496514, catalog #MAB42941; R&D Systems) and corresponding secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rat IgG (1:500; Invitrogen; catalog #A-11007). hPDLFs were seeded (4 × 104 cells/2 mL) in a 20-mm glass bottom cell culture dish and cultured overnight and then stimulated with recombinant human IL-22 (10 ng/mL) and IL-17A (10 ng/mL) for 30 min. The cells were washed and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde fix solution for 20 min and permeabilized with Triton X-100 solution (0.2% Triton X-100 and 0.2% BSA in PBS) for 10 min at 4 °C. The cells were then blocked with blocking solution (5% BSA and 0.02% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were stained with mouse mAb to phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) (1:200, clone M9C6; Cell Signaling Technology; catalog #4113) and corresponding secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:2,000; Invitrogen; catalog #A-11001), or phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (1:2,000; Cell Signaling Technology; catalog no. 3033), and corresponding secondary antibody APC goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:250; Invitrogen; catalog #A-10931). The specificity of staining was confirmed by using primary isotype control antibodies with corresponding secondary antibodies. The tissue sections and cells were counterstained with DAPI and images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E automated fluorescent microscope and NIS-Elements software (Nikon Instruments).

Statistical analysis

After testing for normality, data were analyzed by 2-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests (2-group comparisons) or by one-way analysis of variance (multiple-group comparisons) followed by a multiple comparison test (Dunnett’s or Tukey’s as appropriate). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.4.3; GraphPad Software). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Cellular sources and upregulation of IL-22 in human periodontal tissues

In line with previous investigations,42,46,49,55 we observed a significant elevation in the expression levels of both Il17A and Il22 in human gingival tissue of periodontitis-affected sites (Fig. 1A), which consistently also displayed increased probing pocket depth compared with samples from healthy donors (Fig. 1B). To dissect the cellular source of IL-22 within periodontal tissues, we conducted a comprehensive reanalysis of scRNA-seq data sourced from the Human Oral Mucosa Cell Atlas (NCT01568697).52 UMAP visualization showed immune cell subpopulations from human periodontal tissues divided spatially into 5 main categories (Fig. 1C). Upon closer examination of the cellular sources of IL-22, we identified IL-17–secreting CD4+ cells (Th17) cells as the primary producers of both IL-22 and IL-17 in human periodontitis-involved gingival tissues. Additional IL-22–producing cells included γδ T cells, terminal CD8+ T cells, and mucosal-associated invariant T cells (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, myeloid dendritic cells were the predominant cellular source of IL-22Rα2 (also referred to as IL-22BP), a secreted single-chain receptor that binds IL-22 with high affinity, among all immune cell populations (Fig. 1D). These findings highlight the complex interplay of immune cells and cytokine signaling pathways underlying periodontal disease pathogenesis and also reveal the coexpression of IL-22 and IL-17 by multiple types of immune cells. Based on these considerations, we set out to dissect the role of IL-22, and its potential functional connection with IL-17, in a mouse model of experimental periodontitis.

Endogenous IL-22 is required for induction of periodontal inflammation and bone loss

As outlined previously, previous studies on the role of IL-22 in human periodontitis have been correlative,45–47 and thus the precise role (protective or destructive) of IL-22 has remained uncertain. To address this question by means of cause-and-effect experiments, we used the ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) model in mice. Ligature placement in molar teeth simulates human periodontitis, as it fosters the development of a disease-provoking microbiota leading to inflammatory bone loss in conventional (but not germ-free) mice or other animals.39,43,56–61 The mouse LIP model has implicated elements of both innate and adaptive immunity in destructive periodontal inflammation, such as complement, Th17 cells and IL-17, which have been confirmed in human studies.39,41,43,44 In line with the human condition (Fig. 1), ligature placement significantly upregulated the expression of Il22 mRNA both at 5 d and 10 d postligation, as compared with the unligated contralateral (control) sites (Fig. 2A, left). Moreover, at the same time points, the expression of Il22ra2—which encodes for IL-22BP that antagonizes IL-226—was significantly downregulated (Fig. 2A right), suggesting that IL-22 might act with minimal antagonism during periodontitis. These data validate our model as appropriate for investigating the role of IL-22 in periodontal disease pathogenesis.

To this end, we first determined the role of endogenous IL-22 in LIP-subjected IL-22 gene-deficient (Il22−/−) mice and WT (Il22+/+) littermate control mice. LIP-subjected IL-22−/− mice developed significantly less bone loss than did IL-22+/+ control mice (Fig. 2B). The inhibition of bone loss due to IL-22 deficiency was >70% (Fig. 2B), indicating that IL-22 plays a critical role in the pathogenic process. Consistent with this, Il22−/− mice displayed a pronounced decrease in the expression of proinflammatory and pro-osteoclastogenic genes (Il1b, Il6, and Tnfsf11 [Rankl]) relative to Il22+/+ control mice. Intriguingly, however, the expression of Il17a was comparable between the two genotypes (Fig. 2C). Given that IL-17 is typically causally linked to ligature-induced periodontal bone loss (and human periodontitis),39 this result was surprising. In other words, we expected that the diminished bone loss in Il22−/− mice would be associated with decreased Il17a mRNA expression relative to Il22+/+ littermate control mice. This paradox was addressed in the following experiments.

The destructive effect of IL-22 in experimental periodontitis requires intact IL-17 function

Although LIP-subjected Il22+/+ and Il22−/− mice had similar IL-17 expression, the lack of IL-22 in the latter group might have prevented a potential destructive cooperation or synergy between the two cytokines. We addressed the hypothesis that IL-22 and IL-17 cooperate to mediate enhanced periodontal inflammation and bone loss.

First, we determined whether exogenous IL-22 can enhance ligature-induced bone loss in WT mice in an IL-17–dependent manner. To this end, two groups of WT mice were subjected to LIP for 5 d, with or without gingival microinjection of 5 μg IL-22-Fc or Fc protein control given on days −1, 1, and 3 (Fig. 3A, B). Two additional groups of LIP-subjected WT mice were similarly microinjected with IL-22-Fc in the presence of 10 μg neutralizing anti-IL-17A mAb or IgG1 isotype control (Fig. 3C, D). Mice treated with IL-22-Fc developed significantly more bone loss than Fc control–treated mice (Fig. 3A), thus independently corroborating the earlier finding (obtained using Il22−/− mice) (Fig. 2B) that endogenous IL-22 contributes to bone loss. Importantly, moreover, the ability of IL-22-Fc to enhance bone loss was abrogated in mice that were coadministered anti-IL-17A, whereas IL-22-Fc maintained its enhancing effect in mice coadministered IgG1 isotype control (Fig. 3C).

We also analyzed gingival mRNA expression of proinflammatory and proosteoclastogenic cytokines as well as MMPs, which are involved in and serve as indicators of periodontal tissue destruction.62 Specifically, we analyzed the expression of Mmp1 (collagenase), Mmp3 (stromelysin), and Mmp9 (gelatinase), all of which have human orthologues.63 Although IL-22-Fc did not upregulate the mRNA expression of IL1b and Il17a, it did enhance the expression of Il6 and Rankl and two of the tested MMPs, Mmp1 and Mmp3 (Fig. 3B). The upregulation of these molecules (IL-6, RANKL, MMP1, MMP3) by IL-22-Fc was reversed by anti-IL-17A mAb; moreover, anti-IL-17A inhibited the expression of IL1b and Il17a itself (presumably due to the overall reduction of inflammation), relative to the IgG1 isotype control (Fig. 3D). Thus, as long as endogenous IL-17 is not neutralized by antibody treatment, exogenous IL-22 enhances ligature-induced bone loss associated with increased expression of indicators of connective tissue destruction (MMP1 and MMP3) and osteoclastic bone resorption (RANKL). The data from Figure 2 and 3 collectively suggest that both IL-22 and IL-17 are required for maximal ligature-induced bone loss, as bone loss was diminished in IL-22 deficiency or after anti-IL-17A neutralization. The observation that IL-22-Fc enhanced bone loss without quantitatively changing the expression of Il17a (Fig. 3B) implies a functional cooperation between these two key cytokines. Moreover, locally microinjected anti-IL-17A mAb failed to suppress periodontal inflammation and bone loss in Il22−/− mice, in contrast to its potent inhibitory effects in IL-22–sufficient WT mice (Fig. S1),39,42 indicating that, under physiological conditions, IL-17–driven periodontal pathology requires the presence of IL-22, and that IL-17 alone is insufficient to induce bone loss in the absence of IL-22.

Taken together, our in vivo findings suggest that IL-22 and IL-17 cooperate for induction of inflammatory bone loss in a manner consistent with a synergy between the two cytokines.

IL-22 and IL-17 synergize to activate fibroblast

Given that IL-22 acts selectively on nonhematopoietic cells,2 a likely explanation of the previously discussed in vivo findings is that IL-22 and IL-17 may synergize in stromal cells to induce production of mediators of destructive inflammation. Our reanalysis of scRNA-seq data from the Human Oral Mucosa Cell Atlas52 showed that, in periodontitis-involved human gingival tissues, IL22RA1 mRNA is expressed in fibroblasts and to a greater extent in epithelial cells (Fig. 4A). Consistently, in LIP-subjected mice, the receptor for IL-22 (IL-22Rα1) is readily detectable via immunohistochemistry in the epithelium as well as in the periodontal ligament (PDL) and gingival connective tissue, where fibroblasts reside (Fig. S2). The same scRNA-seq analysis showed that IL17RA mRNA expression is significantly higher in endothelial cells and to a lesser extent in fibroblasts, relative to other cell populations (Fig. 4A). Thus, fibroblasts express IL-22RA1 and IL-17RA, rendering them capable of responding to both IL-22 and IL-17. Therefore, we used fibroblasts to test the hypothesized synergy between IL-22 and IL-17. As in arthritis, where fibroblasts promote destructive inflammation and bone erosion,64–67 in periodontitis, PDLFs or fibroblasts in the gingival connective tissue become activated by inflammatory stimuli (including IL-17) and release proinflammatory cytokines and MMPs that mediate connective tissue destruction.68–73

However, whether IL-17 and IL-22 synergize to induce proinflammatory cytokines and tissue-degrading enzymes in fibroblasts has not been previously investigated. To address this possibility, we used an in vitro activation model with primary hPDLFs exposed to IL-17 and/or IL-22 for 24 h. The release of IL-6 and MMP1 in the culture supernatants was taken as a readout for cell activation. Whereas IL-17 alone upregulated the production of both IL-6 and MMP1 (Fig. 4B, left and right, respectively) in a dose-dependent manner, IL-22 alone failed to upregulate production of either molecule (in fact, it exerted a modest inhibitory effect) (Fig. 4C). However, IL-22 significantly and dose-dependently upregulated the ability of IL-17 to induce the release of IL-6 and MMP1 by hPDLFs (Fig. 4D, left and right, respectively). Thus, in the presence of IL-17, IL-22 acquires the ability to exert a proinflammatory effect indicating that this IL-22–IL-17 cooperation represents a synergistic rather than an additive interaction. Similar to our in vivo observations, maximal fibroblast responses required both cytokines. Consistently, fibroblasts in human periodontal tissues express IL6 and MMP1 mRNA at significantly higher levels compared with other cell populations, as revealed by scRNA-seq reanalysis (Fig. 4A).

The IL-22–IL-17 synergistic effect in fibroblasts depends on NF-κB and STAT3

We next set out to dissect the mechanism of the previously described synergistic effect between IL-22 and IL-17 in hPDLFs. IL-17 and IL-22 can activate NF-κB and STAT3, respectively.2,74 Whereas IL-17–induced NF-κB activation upregulates the production of proinflammatory cytokines/chemokines and MMPs,70,75–78 it is not known whether and how the STAT3-activating capacity of IL-22 potentiates IL-17–induced NF-κB-dependent responses. To address this possibility, we stimulated hPDLFs with IL-17, IL-22, or both IL-17 and IL-22, in the presence of the NF-κB inhibitor BAY11-68,79,80 and/or the STAT3 inhibitor Stattic.81 BAY11-7082, but not Stattic, inhibited the ability of IL-17 (used at its maximum concentration, 40 ng/mL, as tested in Fig. 4B) to upregulate the release of IL-6 (Fig. 5A) or MMP1 (Fig. 5B). In contrast, neither of the inhibitors affected the IL-6 or MMP1 concentrations in hPDLF cultures exposed to 40 ng/mL of IL-22 (Fig. 5A, B). As shown in Fig. 4D, a synergy between 40 ng/mL IL-22 and 5 ng/mL IL-17 was established with regard to IL-6 and MMP1 induction in hPDLFs. Strikingly, this IL-22–IL-17 synergistic effect was abrogated not only by BAY11-7082 (which was expected because this inhibitor completely blocked IL-17–induced IL-6 and MMP1), but also by Stattic, which had no effect on IL-17–induced IL-6 or MMP1 release in the absence of IL-22 (Fig. 5A, B). The combination of BAY11-7082 and Stattic did not significantly further reduce the levels of IL-6 and MMP1 relative to each inhibitor alone (Fig. 5A, B).

We confirmed these hPDLF findings through independent experiments using hGFs. Stimulation with IL-17 alone upregulated dose-dependently IL-6 release, which was reduced to background levels when IL-17 (at its highest concentration; 40 ng/mL) was tested in the presence of the BAY11-7082 (Fig. S3A). Stimulation of hGFs with IL-22 alone failed to upregulate IL-6 even at the highest concentration (40 ng/mL) used (Fig. S3B). IL-22 significantly and dose-dependently upregulated the ability of IL-17 to induce IL-6 release, although this synergistic effect was abrogated in the presence of Stattic as well as by BAY11-7082 (Fig. S3C). Taken together, these data indicate that the ability of IL-22 to synergize with IL-17 in fibroblast activation requires STAT3 signaling.

IL-22 promotes NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and translocation in IL-17–stimulated fibroblasts

To better understand why the NF-κB–dependent ability of IL-17 to activate fibroblasts is enhanced by IL-22 in a STAT3-dependent manner, we analyzed the time course of NF-κB and STAT3 activation in hPDLFs stimulated by IL-17 or IL-17 plus IL-22. To this end, nuclear and cytoplasmic extracts were prepared from hPDLFs and levels of STAT3, phospho-STAT3 (p-STAT3), NF-κB p65 (RelA) and phospho-p65 (p-p65) proteins were determined by Western blot. IL-17 treatment increased the abundance of p-p65 in the cytoplasm of hPDLFs, as well as the nuclear translocation of p65 (Fig. 6A). The additional treatment with IL-22 (i.e. IL-17 + IL-22) increased the abundance of p-STAT3 in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus of hPDLFs, especially at 10 min post-stimulation (Fig. 6A). More importantly, the addition of IL-22 also enhanced (relative to IL-17 alone) the abundance of p-p65 in the cytoplasm at all time points tested, as well as the nuclear translocation of p65, most notably at 30 min following stimulation (Fig. 6A). To confirm the latter finding with an independent approach, we utilized immunofluorescent staining and confocal microscopy to detect p-STAT3 and p65 in hPDLFs stimulated with IL-17 or both IL-17 and IL-22 for 30 mins. In line with the immunoblotting results, we observed that the addition of IL-22 significantly increased the nuclear translocation of p65 in IL-17–stimulated hPDLFs (Fig. 6B). These data suggest that the capacity of IL-22 to enhance IL-17–induced fibroblast activation may, at least in part, involve the ability of IL-22 to enhance NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and translocation to the nucleus of IL-17–stimulated fibroblasts.

Discussion

IL-22 plays a complex role in immune-driven diseases, in which it can function in either a protective or destructive manner.2,13,15,17,19–23,25,31–33 Our present findings in the mouse LIP model suggest that IL-22, which is upregulated in human and experimental animal periodontitis (and this study),45–49 drives inflammatory periodontal bone loss in synergy with IL-17. Indeed, maximal bone loss required the presence of endogenous IL-22, whereas exogenously administered IL-22-Fc could further promote inflammation and bone loss. However, IL-22-Fc could not exacerbate periodontitis when endogenous IL-17 was neutralized by anti-IL-17A mAb, suggesting that the presence of functionally active IL-17 is required for the destructive effects of IL-22. As described previously, Th17 cells prominently coexpress IL-22 and IL-17 in periodontitis-involved human gingival tissue, and fibroblasts coexpress IL-22RA1 and IL-17RA, thus representing a major target of potential IL-22–IL-17 synergy. In this regard, IL-22 by itself could not induce the production of a proinflammatory cytokine or MMP (IL-6 and MMP1, respectively) by human fibroblasts. However, IL-22 dose-dependently upregulated IL-17–induced release of IL-6 and MMP1, suggesting a bone fide synergistic effect between IL-22 and IL-17.

Under homeostatic conditions, fibroblasts in the PDL and the gingiva maintain tissue structure and integrity (by producing and remodeling the extracellular matrix) and contribute to immune surveillance.64,69 However, excessive activation of fibroblasts can contribute to the destruction of both the connective and bone tissue.64,68,69,82,83 Moreover, a single-cell transcriptome atlas of human gingival tissue in periodontally healthy and diseased individuals clearly revealed a fibroblast gene expression profile consistent with activation of inflammatory pathways and potential for recruitment of inflammatory cells to periodontal disease lesions.52 Our present study suggests a hitherto unknown mechanism of fibroblast overactivation, namely the coactivation of fibroblasts by IL-22 and IL-17, both of which are highly upregulated in human periodontitis (and present study).39,46 Specifically, by inducing STAT3 signaling, IL-22 could synergize with IL-17 to enhance NF-κB–dependent inflammatory responses in fibroblasts. It was recently shown that pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 in the LIP model leads to significant inhibition of periodontal bone loss.80 Although STAT3 is activated in multiple cell types besides fibroblasts in periodontal tissue,80 the protective mechanism(s) of STAT3 inhibitors may include the abrogation of the IL-22–IL-17 synergy impacting fibroblasts in the PDL and gingival connective tissue.

Our analysis of cytosolic and nuclear protein extracts of hPDLFs revealed that IL-22 promotes NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and translocation of p65 to the nucleus of IL-17–stimulated fibroblasts. In a related context, an earlier study showed that sustained NF-κB activity in tumors depends on STAT3, which inhibits the export of NF-κB from the nucleus.84 These are not the only mechanisms of STAT3 and NF-κB cooperation, which may depend on the cell type and stimuli involved, among other potential factors. In this regard, these two transcription factors were shown to also physically interact and the resulting complex could bind to unique DNA sequences where neither factor could bind by itself.85 Moreover, STAT3 and NF-κB may cooperate without physical interaction, as they may bind to different sites at the same promoter, leading to synergistic activation of gene transcription.85

The difference in effect size between IL-22–Fc treatment and global Il22 deficiency likely reflects the fundamentally distinct nature of these two modes of pathway manipulation. Il22−/− mice lack IL-22 entirely, constituting profound pathway disruption, whereas IL-22–Fc treatment represents a transient, modest, and local augmentation superimposed on an otherwise IL-22–replete background, yielding a comparatively attenuated proinflammatory effect. Thus, the stronger protective phenotype observed in IL-22–deficient mice relative to the degree of exacerbation induced by IL-22–Fc does not call into question the proinflammatory role of IL‑22, but instead underscores how differences in both the magnitude and duration of IL-22 signaling critically shape disease outcomes.

A similar principle explains the differential impact of anti–IL-17A treatment on Il17a mRNA expression in IL-22–Fc–treated WT versus Il22−/− mice. In WT mice, IL-22–Fc amplifies inflammation, thereby promoting IL-17–driven responses; under these conditions, IL-17A neutralization suppresses Il17a transcription via negative feedback. In contrast, Il22−/− mice lack IL-22–dependent inflammatory amplification and display attenuated IL-17 functional activity, such that IL-17A neutralization does not elicit comparable transcriptional feedback. Collectively, these observations indicate that IL-22–IL-17 interactions are highly context dependent, with the extent of pathway engagement critically dictating both phenotypic severity and transcriptional responses.

The role of the IL-23–IL-17 axis and of Th17 cells in inflammatory periodontal bone loss, which was first established in mice,39,42,43,86 was subsequently validated in human studies.39,87 Specifically, individuals with genetically defective Th17 cell development have diminished periodontal inflammation and bone loss relative to age- and sex-matched individuals with normal Th17 development.39 Moreover, anti-IL-23 therapy (via ustekinumab, which inhibits the common IL-12/IL-23 p40 subunit) suppresses gingival IL-17 expression and resolves inflammatory lesions.87 Our preclinical findings that IL-22 is required for the destructive inflammatory effects of IL-17 suggest that IL-22 blockade could similarly inhibit periodontal disease in humans. Although this notion would require empirical confirmation, it should be noted that the IL-22 protein is highly homologous between mice and humans, moreover, the structure of the IL-22R gene is strikingly comparable between the two species.2,88,89 Furthermore, human and mouse IL-22 are similarly regulated during periodontitis (and the present study).45–49

In contrast to the destructive IL-22–IL-17 synergy in inflammatory bone loss in the present study, IL-22 and IL-17 may also engage in beneficial synergistic interactions that promote resistance to infection (e.g. by cooperatively enhancing the expression of chemokines and antimicrobial peptides).4,50 Moreover, IL-22 and IL-17 cooperate for protection against oropharyngeal candidiasis because IL-22–induced STAT3 signaling in the basal epithelial layer promotes the proliferation of IL-17R–expressing suprabasal epithelial cells, hence sustaining IL-17–mediated antifungal responses for immune clearance of Candida albicans.90 Thus, it appears that the combined action of IL-22 and IL-17 may be advantageous in the context of infection but potentially detrimental in the context of inflammatory disease.

In summary, our study has provided causal evidence for the involvement of IL-22 in periodontal inflammatory bone loss. Moreover, the present study has shown for the first time that IL-22 and IL-17 synergistically promote inflammatory tissue destruction in vivo; at the cellular level, the IL-22–IL-17 synergy requires intact STAT3 and NF-κB function in fibroblasts. These findings enrich our understanding of the complex interactions that drive destructive mucosal inflammation in periodontal disease and offer new targets for host-modulation therapies.
