Laparoscopic management of a heterotopic cornual pregnancy following first-trimester miscarriage – A case report and literature review
Authors: Vishal Bahall, Reiaz Mohammed, Lance De Barry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeterotopic pregnancies, defined by the simultaneous occurrence of an intrauterine and ectopic pregnancy, present a rare but potentially life-threatening diagnostic and surgical challenge. Heterotopic pregnancy is rare in spontaneously conceived pregnancies; however, a dramatic rise in the incidence is observed in patients undergoing assisted reproductive technology procedures. Moreover, cornual localization comprises only a minority of ectopic cases yet bears the highest maternal mortality rate due to the risk of catastrophic hemorrhage.This report presents the case of a 33-year-old woman with a confirmed first-trimester miscarriage who remained asymptomatic but demonstrated persistently elevated β-hCG levels. Transvaginal ultrasonography identified a right cornual ectopic pregnancy. Laparoscopic wedge resection was performed with the preservation of uterine anatomy.This case illustrates a critical vulnerability in the follow-up of early pregnancy loss, where residual or ectopic trophoblastic tissue may be misattributed to a slowly resolving miscarriage. It reinforces the value of repeat imaging and surveillance of β-hCG when clinical resolution is incomplete. This report presents one of the first documented cases of asymptomatic, spontaneously conceived heterotopic cornual pregnancy managed laparoscopically in the Caribbean; it contributes to the evolving literature on heterotopic pregnancies in low-risk women and supports early surgical intervention to preserve reproductive potential.Keywords: Heterotopic pregnancy, Cornual ectopic pregnancy, Laparoscopic surgery, Post-miscarriage surveillance, Β-hCG monitoring, Fertility preservation

Introduction

Heterotopic pregnancy is a rare and often misdiagnosed clinical condition. It is defined as the simultaneous development of an intrauterine and ectopic pregnancy [1]. Its incidence is estimated at 1 in 30,000 spontaneous pregnancies but this may rise to 1 in 100 with the use of assisted reproductive technology (ART), where ovulation induction and embryo transfer significantly increase the risk of multiple implantations [1]. The diagnostic complexity of heterotopic pregnancy is frequently compounded by the false reassurance of a documented intrauterine gestation, which may delay the recognition of a coexisting ectopic focus.

Among ectopic variants, cornual pregnancy, defined by implantation in the proximal intramyometrial portion of the Fallopian tube, comprises 2–4 % of all ectopic gestations [2]. However, it contributes disproportionately to maternal morbidity and mortality owing to its location in a highly vascularized area of the uterus [3]. Rupture may result in profound hemorrhage, and mortality rates are reported to be as high as 2.5 %, significantly exceeding those of other ectopic sites [3]. Moreover, cornual ectopic pregnancies often progress asymptomatically and may reach advanced gestational ages before detection, increasing the risk of rupture and diagnostic delay [4].

Transvaginal ultrasonography remains the cornerstone of early diagnosis, but its sensitivity for cornual gestations is limited in the presence of a concurrent intrauterine pregnancy [5]. The classic sonographic criteria may be obscured or overlooked, particularly after miscarriage. Therefore, heterotopic cornual pregnancies are often diagnosed only after rupture or through persistent β-hCG elevation following apparent pregnancy resolution.

This report concerns a rare case of an asymptomatic cornual heterotopic pregnancy diagnosed incidentally three weeks after a confirmed first-trimester miscarriage. The patient underwent successful laparoscopic wedge resection with preservation of uterine anatomy. This is among the first such cases reported in the Caribbean. This report underscores the critical importance of post-miscarriage surveillance and highlights the role of minimally invasive surgery in managing rare ectopic variants while preserving reproductive potential.

Case Presentation

A 33-year-old woman, gravida 2 para 1, with no known medical conditions and a recent history of a lower-segment cesarean section, presented to the gynaecology clinic for a follow-up three weeks after a confirmed first-trimester missed miscarriage. The pregnancy had been conceived spontaneously, and there was no use of ART. The miscarriage was managed medically with misoprostol 800μg following ultrasonographic confirmation of a non-viable intrauterine pregnancy at 6 weeks and 5 days of gestation (Fig. 1). The patient remained clinically well, with no ongoing symptoms.

Transvaginal ultrasound image showing intrauterine gestation with fetal pole and no cardiac activity at 6 weeks and 5 days of gestation.

At follow-up three weeks after the miscarriage, a urinary pregnancy test remained positive, and serum β-hCG levels were elevated at 799.63 mIU/ml. The patient reported no abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, dizziness or syncopal episodes. Clinical examination was unremarkable, with stable vital signs and no abdominal tenderness or peritoneal signs. A repeat transvaginal ultrasound revealed a well-circumscribed gestational sac measuring 3.97 cm in diameter in the right cornual region of the uterus, surrounded by a thin myometrial mantle (Fig. 2). The endometrial cavity was empty, and there was a moderate volume of free fluid in the pouch of Douglas, suggestive of hemoperitoneum. These findings raised strong suspicion for a contained rupture of a cornual heterotopic pregnancy.

Transvaginal ultrasound image showing a right cornual ectopic gestation consistent with a heterotopic pregnancy.

The patient underwent an urgent diagnostic laparoscopy. Intraoperatively, approximately 500 ml of hemoperitoneum was evacuated. A distended right cornual mass with thinning of the overlying myometrium was visualized, with no evidence of active bleeding (Fig. 3A). To minimize intraoperative blood loss, diluted adrenaline was injected circumferentially around the cornual base. Laparoscopic wedge resection of the cornual pregnancy was performed and the myometrial defect was repaired in two layers using a delayed-absorbable barbed suture (Fig. 3B). Hemostasis was secured, and the specimen was removed in an endoscopic retrieval bag to avoid tissue spillage.

(A) Laparoscopic view of right cornual heterotopic pregnancy. (B) Myometrium closed in two layers following cornual wedge resection.

The estimated intraoperative blood loss was 100 ml. The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged on postoperative day 1. Postoperatively, β-hCG levels had declined to 394.97 mIU/ml on day 4, and a urinary pregnancy test three weeks later was negative. Histopathology confirmed a right cornual ectopic pregnancy with no molar change or trophoblastic disease. The patient remained asymptomatic at follow-up with preserved uterine integrity.

Discussion

Heterotopic pregnancy is exceedingly rare in spontaneous conceptions, with an incidence estimated at 1 in 30,000 pregnancies [1]. However, this risk markedly increases to approximately 1 in 100 pregnancies with the use of ART, such as in-vitro fertilization and ovulation induction protocols [1]. Cornual heterotopic pregnancy, a variant where the ectopic component implants within the cornual or interstitial portion of the Fallopian tube, accounts for only 2–4 % of all ectopic pregnancies [2]. Most reported cases occur in women of reproductive age with known risk factors for ectopic pregnancies. These include previous ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease, previous tubal surgery, and smoking [6].

Cornual pregnancies result from implantation of the blastocyst within the intramural segment of the Fallopian tube, traversing the myometrial wall [7]. This anatomic site is richly vascularized by branches of the uterine and ovarian arteries, permitting delayed rupture and significant hemorrhage [3]. In the case of heterotopic pregnancy, simultaneous intrauterine implantation occurs, likely due to multiple ovulations or asynchronous embryo migration. While ART procedures may explain such occurrences, spontaneous heterotopic pregnancies may reflect subtle alterations in tubal motility, embryo transport or uterine receptivity [8].

The clinical presentation of cornual heterotopic pregnancy is notoriously variable and may closely mimic complications of an early isolated intrauterine pregnancy. Abdominal pain, adnexal tenderness, and peritoneal signs may develop insidiously and are often misattributed to corpus luteal cyst rupture or gastrointestinal disturbances in early pregnancy. Vaginal bleeding, a hallmark of ectopic pregnancy, is frequently absent in cornual heterotopic presentations. The presence of an intrauterine gestation can obscure suspicion of a concurrent ectopic focus and thus contribute to diagnostic delay. Remarkably, a small subset of patients may be completely asymptomatic, and heterotopic pregnancy may be diagnosed incidentally or in cases where rupture occurs, leading to hemodynamic compromise.

High-resolution transvaginal ultrasonography remains the first-line imaging modality [9]. However, its sensitivity in detecting the ectopic component of heterotopic pregnancy, mainly before 6 weeks of gestation, is limited to approximately 56 % [10]. The ‘interstitial line sign’, a thin echogenic line extending from the endometrial cavity to the cornual gestation, is a highly specific marker for cornual pregnancy [10]. However, its identification requires expertise and a high level of clinical suspicion. A gestational sac seen separate from the endometrial stripe, surrounded by less than 5 mm of myometrial tissue, should also raise concern [10]. Colour Doppler improves diagnostic confidence by demonstrating a “ring of fire” pattern, which reflects high-velocity, low-resistance flow consistent with trophoblastic activity [9].

Furthermore, serial serum β-hCG measurements are of limited use in heterotopic pregnancy. In isolated ectopic pregnancies, non-viable trophoblastic tissue typically results in suboptimal rises or plateaus in β-hCG levels, whereas heterotopic pregnancies often demonstrate physiologic trajectories reflective of the intrauterine component and obscure diagnostic interpretation [11,12]. This limitation is further complicated in cases following spontaneous miscarriage of the intrauterine pregnancy, where residual or persistent β-hCG production from the extrauterine trophoblast may be erroneously attributed to delayed hormonal regression [13]. Consequently, in any post-miscarriage clinical context marked by persistent or evolving pelvic symptoms, continued β-hCG elevation must not be dismissed as benign post-abortion hormonal lag but should prompt expedited imaging to exclude a concealed ectopic component, as demonstrated in this case [13].

Historically, cornual pregnancies were managed by laparotomy and cornual resection or hysterectomy due to the risk of rupture and uncontrolled hemorrhage [5]. However, advances in minimally invasive surgery have made laparoscopy the preferred approach in hemodynamically stable patients [14]. Laparoscopic cornual wedge resection or cornuostomy, performed with particular attention to hemostasis, is an effective surgical treatment associated with a faster recovery [14]. Local vasopressin injection, bipolar coagulation and layered myometrial suturing are essential to minimize bleeding [15]. Non-surgical options, such as systemic methotrexate, are contraindicated in heterotopic gestations due to potential harm to the intrauterine fetus, and local injection of potassium chloride may not be feasible in cornual pregnancies with thick overlying myometrium [1].

Cornual heterotopic pregnancies carry a high risk of uterine rupture between 7 and 12 weeks of gestation [16]. Early diagnosis and surgical management, as achieved in this case, dramatically reduce these risks. Future fertility outcomes are generally favourable with laparoscopic treatment, although patients should be counselled about the risk of uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancies. Elective cesarean delivery may be considered in select cases based on the extent of myometrial involvement and repair technique [16].

In conclusion, cornual heterotopic pregnancy remains a rare and potentially life-threatening diagnostic challenge, particularly in spontaneously conceived pregnancies. Its atypical presentation often delays diagnostic recognition. This underscores the importance of a high clinical suspicion and the use of transvaginal ultrasonography when β-hCG levels fail to decline during follow-up of early pregnancy loss. Timely surgical intervention, preferably by laparoscopy, is critical to optimize maternal outcomes while preserving fertility. This case reinforces the need for vigilance in post-miscarriage clinical contexts with elevated β-hCG, where a concealed ectopic pregnancy may be overlooked.
