MASQ: A multiplex qPCR platform for quantitative alternative splicing analysis in clinical specimens
Authors: Hyejeong Jeong, Namjoon Cho, Jong-Il Park, Heon Jong Yoo, Kee K Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlternative splicing quantification is critical for understanding disease mechanisms and developing precision medicine approaches. Conventional gel-based methods suffer from poor resolution and limited quantitative precision, especially for similar-sized exons. Here, we present multiplex alternative splicing quantification (MASQ), a dual TaqMan probe qPCR platform enabling precise quantification of alternative splicing events. MASQ employs FAM-labeled probes targeting the alternative exon and HEX-labeled probes for constitutive exon normalization, providing internal controls and eliminating variability. Using PBRM1 exon 27 as a model system, where inclusion promotes immune checkpoint resistance through enhanced PBAF complex recruitment to the PD-L1 promoter, we demonstrate superior analytical performance. MASQ exhibits exceptional linearity (R2 > 0.98), high precision (intra-assay CV < 9%), and femtogram-level sensitivity (LOD 195 fg). CRISPR-engineered cellular validation confirmed probe specificity, while analysis of uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma specimens revealed significantly elevated exon27 inclusion in cancer tissues (61.2% ± 14.5%) compared to normal endometrium (12.7% ± 4.0%, p < 0.01). Platform generalizability was demonstrated through adaptation to HTRA2 exon 7 quantification and monitoring splice-switching oligonucleotide effects (44.6% reduction at 24 h, p < 0.0001). MASQ represents a broadly applicable platform for alternative splicing analysis, enabling applications including biomarker discovery, diagnostic development, and monitoring.Keywords: MT, Oligonucleotides, Diagnostics and Biosensors, alternative splicing, multiplex qPCR, PBRM1, precision medicine, biomarker, HTRA2, MASQ, therapeutic monitoring

Introduction

Alternative pre-mRNA splicing is a fundamental post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism that enables a single gene to generate multiple mRNA variants through the differential inclusion or exclusion of exonic sequences.1 This process dramatically expands the functional diversity of the human proteome, with over 95% of multi-exon genes producing multiple transcripts through alternative splicing events.2,3,4 The resulting protein isoforms can exhibit distinct structural and functional properties, providing essential regulatory flexibility for normal cellular function.5,6,7 However, dysregulation of alternative splicing has emerged as a critical factor in numerous human diseases,8,9,10 including cancer,11,12,13,14,15 neurological disorders,16,17,18 and genetic syndromes,19,20 with aberrant splicing events both driving disease pathogenesis and representing promising therapeutic targets.21,22 Consequently, accurate detection and quantification of alternative splicing events is essential for understanding gene function, elucidating disease mechanisms, and developing splicing-targeted diagnostics and therapeutics.23,24,25,26,27

Despite the critical importance of alternative splicing in human health and disease, current methodological approaches face significant limitations in accurately detecting and quantifying these events.28,29,30 Current approaches for alternative splicing analysis fall into two main categories: targeted PCR-based methods and transcriptome-wide sequencing approaches. Traditional RT-PCR using primers flanking alternative exons, followed by agarose gel electrophoresis, offers cost-effectiveness and simplicity but suffers from limited quantitative accuracy and reproducibility concerns that limit diagnostic applications with human tissue samples.31,32 While quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using junction-specific primers can distinguish individual variants, this approach becomes impractical for genes with complex alternative splicing patterns requiring multiple separate reactions.33,34 Transcriptome-wide approaches provide comprehensive splicing profiles but have distinct limitations. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) struggles with accurate quantification of short exons, transcripts with multiple simultaneous splicing events, and regions where adjacent exons undergo alternative splicing.35,36,37,38,39 Although long-read sequencing (LR-seq) addresses some of these limitations by providing full-transcript context,40,41,42 it remains hindered by relatively low read depth per transcript and high per-sample costs that limit its application in clinical and mechanistic studies requiring large sample sizes.43,44,45

Recent advances in alternative splicing research have increasingly highlighted its importance in precision medicine. Growing evidence demonstrates that aberrant splicing events contribute to the pathogenesis of several human diseases.46,47,48,49 The clinical translation potential of splicing-based therapeutics has been demonstrated through FDA-approved splice-switching oligonucleotide (SSO) therapies, such as Nusinersen for spinal muscular atrophy and Eteplirsen for Duchenne muscular dystrophy.50,51,52,53,54 Most notably, a patient-specific SSO, Milasen, was designed to correct MFSD8 splicing defects and administered to a single child with a rare neurodegenerative disorder under compassionate use authorization.55 This landmark clinical case exemplifies the potential for individualized splicing-targeted interventions and emphasizes the critical need for diagnostic platforms capable of precisely characterizing alternative splicing patterns in patient-derived tissues to guide personalized therapeutic strategies.56,57,58

To address these methodological gaps, we developed multiplex alternative splicing quantification (MASQ), a novel qPCR-based platform that enables precise, quantitative measurement of alternative splicing events from single-tube reactions. We demonstrated the utility of MASQ by quantifying exon 27 (E27) inclusion in Polybromo-1 (PBRM1) mRNA, a potential biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitor response in cancer patients, and validated its broad applicability by measuring exon 7 (E7) inclusion in HTRA2 mRNA.33 Through comprehensive validation using plasmid DNA controls, CRISPR-engineered cell lines, and human tissue samples from patients with uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), we established a robust, quantitative method for detecting functionally relevant splicing alterations in clinical samples. This approach can be readily expanded into a standardized PCR-based assay for predicting therapeutic response based on alternative splicing signatures, thereby advancing precision medicine applications.

Results

Technical limitations of conventional alternative splicing quantification methods

Alternative splicing of PBRM1 E27 generates functionally distinct protein isoforms with differential roles in chromatin remodeling and immune checkpoint regulation (Figure 1A). The full-length (F.L.) isoform containing E27 enhances PBAF (polybromo-associated BAF) complex recruitment to the PD-L1 promoter, leading to increased PD-L1 expression and resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in cancer patients.33 Given this clinical significance, we sought to develop robust quantitative methods for measuring E27 inclusion ratios in clinical specimens.

(B) RT-PCR analysis of PBRM1 E27 splice variants across 10 human cancer cell lines using primers flanking exons E25 and E28. Products separated on 1.5% agarose gel show four splice variants: E25-E26-E27-E28, E25-E26-E28, E25-E27-E28, and E25-E28. M, DNA ladder. (C) MASQ platform workflow. Dual TaqMan probe strategy enables simultaneous quantification of alternative exon inclusion and total gene expression in a single qPCR reaction.

Conventional RT-PCR analysis of PBRM1 alternative splicing using primers flanking constitutive exons E25 and E28 revealed complex splicing patterns across 10 human cancer cell lines (Figure 1B). Four distinct splice variants were detected: E25-E26-E27-E28 (F.L.), E25-E27-E28 (E26 exclusion), E25-E26-E28 (E27 exclusion), and E25-E28 (dual exclusion). However, accurate quantification proved technically challenging owing to the minimal size difference between E26 (165 nucleotides) and E27 (156 nucleotides). This 9-nucleotide difference resulted in insufficient electrophoretic separation on 1.5% agarose gels, making densitometric quantification unreliable with coefficients of variation exceeding 25% across technical replicates. To address these limitations, we developed the MASQ platform, employing dual TaqMan probe chemistry for simultaneous quantification of alternative and constitutive exons within a single qPCR reaction (Figure 1C). This approach eliminates the size-dependent separation limitations of gel electrophoresis while providing inherent normalization through internal reference signals.

MASQ platform design and analytical performance validation

The MASQ platform employs a dual-probe detection strategy with primers designed to amplify all PBRM1 splice variants (Figure 2A). The HEX (hexachloro-fluorescein)-labeled probe targets constitutive exon, E25 (5ʹ-TCAGCCGCCTGGTGGGGACAGAATGG-BHQ1-3ʹ) for normalization, whereas the FAM (6-carboxyfluorescein)-labeled probe specifically detects alternative exon, E27 (5ʹ-TGGCCTTGTTAGCATGGGCAGCATGC-BHQ1-3ʹ). Both probes incorporate 3ʹ black hole quencher modifications to minimize background fluorescence and maximize signal to noise ratios.59 Probe specificity was rigorously validated using engineered plasmids containing F.L. or E27-deleted (ΔE27) sequences (Figure 2B). The PBRM1 F.L. plasmid generated robust signals in both HEX and FAM channels (cycle threshold [Ct] values: HEX = 11.1 ± 0.18, FAM = 12.0 ± 0.24), confirming co-amplification of target sequences. Critically, the PBRM1 ΔE27 plasmid produced HEX signal exclusively (Ct = 11.1 ± 0.17) with complete absence of FAM signal (Ct > 40), demonstrating absolute specificity of the E27 probe with no cross-reactivity. Analytical performance was assessed using serial dilution analysis spanning 400 pg to 195 femtogram (fg) of PBRM1 F.L. plasmid DNA, covering >103 concentration range (Figure 2C). Both detection channels exhibited exceptional linearity: R2 = 0.9969 for E27-FAM and R2 = 0.9804 for E25-HEX. Amplification efficiencies were 59.8% (FAM) and 65.1% (HEX). The limit of detection was determined as 195 fg for both channels, corresponding to approximately 20,000 copies of plasmid template. Intra-assay precision showed coefficients of variation <9% across all dilution points. The mathematical framework for exon inclusion quantification employs a modified ΔΔCt approach: % exon inclusion = {2ˆ[−((Ct_FAM − Ct_HEX)_sample − (Ct_FAM − Ct_HEX)_reference)]} × 100 (Figure 2D). This dual-probe normalization strategy accounts for variations in RNA input, reverse transcription efficiency, and PCR amplification, providing absolute quantification relative to the F.L. reference standard representing 100% exon inclusion.

Cellular validation using CRISPR-engineered models

To validate MASQ performance in complex cellular backgrounds, we generated PBRM1 ΔE27 HeLa cells using CRISPR-Cas9 targeting the E27 3ʹ splice site (Figure 3A). A single guide RNA (5ʹ-GTGTGTCCTTCAAGGCATGAT-3ʹ) induced small insertions/deletions that disrupted the splice acceptor site, resulting in constitutive E27 exclusion. CRISPR efficiency was >95% based on next-generation sequencing analysis, with no off target effects detected at predicted sites. Real-time PCR validation confirmed the intended splicing modification (Figure 3B). Wild-type (WT) HeLa cells exhibited multiple PBRM1 splice variants including E27-containing transcripts, whereas ΔE27 cells showed complete elimination of all E27-containing variants with exclusive detection of E27-exclusion products. Importantly, total PBRM1 protein expression remained unchanged in ΔE27 cells (western blot analysis, data not shown), confirming that the editing specifically affected splicing without altering overall gene expression. MASQ analysis of these cellular models provided definitive validation of platform specificity (Figures 3C and S1). WT HeLa cells generated robust signals in both detection channels (HEX Ct = 30.4 ± 0.19, FAM Ct = 28.3 ± 0.21), while ΔE27 cells produced HEX signal exclusively (Ct = 30.6 ± 0.03) with complete absence of FAM signal (Ct > 40). Quantitative analysis revealed E27 inclusion of 27.0% ± 0.44% in WT cells versus no detectable inclusion in ΔE27 cells (Figure 3D), validating the precision and accuracy of the quantification algorithm for cellular cDNA samples. To further demonstrate the platform’s quantitative capabilities, we applied MASQ to diverse human cell lines (Figure 3E). MASQ successfully captured the wide dynamic range of E27 inclusion across all cell lines tested. The platform quantified inclusion frequencies ranging from extremely low levels in A549 (5.34% ± 0.7%) and U2OS (5.96% ± 1.1%) cells to high abundance in HCT116 (76.0% ± 13.1%) and Jurkat (67.9% ± 6.1%) cells. These results demonstrate that MASQ provides consistent and reproducible quantification of endogenous splicing patterns across diverse biological backgrounds, complementing the qualitative resolution offered by conventional gel-based approaches.

Validation using patient tissue specimens

To establish clinical applicability, we analyzed PBRM1 E27 inclusion in UCEC specimens, including five cancer tissues and three matched normal endometrial samples (Figure 4A). All tissue specimens met stringent quality criteria: RNA integrity numbers >7.0, A260/A280 ratios 1.8–2.0, and successful amplification of housekeeping genes with Ct values < 30. Conventional RT-PCR revealed differential splicing patterns between tissue types, with cancer specimens displaying more prominent E27-containing bands compared to normal endometrium. However, quantitative assessment by densitometry showed high variability (CV [coefficient of variation] >20%) and overlapping confidence intervals between groups, highlighting the limitations of gel-based quantification for clinical applications. MASQ analysis provided superior quantitative resolution (Figure 4B). HEX channel detection showed consistent amplification patterns across all samples (mean Ct = 26.8 ± 1.2), confirming uniform PBRM1 expression and successful normalization. In contrast, FAM channel signals exhibited striking tissue-dependent differences, with cancer samples showing substantially lower Ct values (higher E27 inclusion) compared to normal endometrium. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly elevated E27 inclusion in UCEC tissues (61.2% ± 14.5%, n = 5) compared to normal endometrium (12.7% ± 4.0%, n = 3, p values <0.01, unpaired t test) (Figure 4C). Individual cancer samples showed substantial heterogeneity (range: 48%–85%), potentially reflecting tumor subtype differences or disease progression stages. These findings demonstrated substantially higher E27 inclusion in cancer tissues compared to normal endometrium (61.2% ± 14.5% vs. 12.7% ± 4.0%, respectively) with minimal overlap, suggesting the potential utility of E27 inclusion quantification as a quantitative biomarker in future large-scale clinical studies. Importantly, our results were consistent with independent RNA-seq analysis of UCEC samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, providing orthogonal validation of the observed splicing differences.

Platform generalizability and therapeutic monitoring applications

To demonstrate broader applicability beyond PBRM1, we adapted MASQ for quantifying HTRA2 E7 alternative splicing (Figure 5A). HTRA2 represents a functionally distinct gene encoding, a mitochondrial serine protease, demonstrating the platform’s versatility across different biological pathways and splicing mechanisms. Probe design followed identical principles: FAM-labeled probe targeting alternative exon, E7 (5ʹ-GTGTGCAGGGGAGCCCAGGATGACTT-BHQ1-3ʹ) and HEX-labeled probe for constitutive exon, E6 normalization (5ʹ-GCGGCGCTACATTGGGGTGATGATGCT-BHQ1-3ʹ). Specificity validation using HTRA2 plasmids confirmed absolute probe selectivity (Figure 5B). The HTRA2 F.L. construct generated signals in both channels (HEX Ct = 12.2 ± 0.27 and FAM Ct = 12.7 ± 0.55), while the ΔE7 construct produced HEX channel signal exclusively (Ct = 12.3 ± 0.57) with no FAM detection, validating the transferability of the dual-probe design strategy. To assess therapeutic monitoring capabilities, we employed SSOs to modulate HTRA2 E7 inclusion (Figure 5C). Target SSO (5ʹ-GGATGCTGAAAGTACACAGATCA-3ʹ) designed to block the E7 3ʹ splice site was compared against a scrambled control sequence. HeLa cells were transfected with 100 nM SSO and analyzed at 24 and 48 h time points to assess temporal dynamics of splicing modulation.

Conventional RT-PCR analysis showed time-dependent reduction in E7-containing transcripts following target SSO treatment (Figure 5D). Densitometric quantification indicated decreased E7 inclusion from 69% (Con.) to 24% (24 h) and 33% (48 h), suggesting partial recovery over time likely due to SSO degradation or cellular dilution effects. MASQ analysis provided more precise quantification of SSO-mediated splicing modulation (Figure 5E). At 24 h post-transfection, target SSO significantly reduced E7 inclusion from 68.8% ± 6.2% (Con.) to 24.2% ± 5.8% (T.), representing a 44.6% reduction. At 48 h, E7 inclusion decreased from 60.9% ± 10.8% (Con.) to 34.8% ± 12.6% (T.), representing a 26.1% reduction. The quantitative precision of MASQ (CV <10%) enabled detection of subtle recovery kinetics that were difficult to assess accurately using conventional gel-based methods.

These results establish MASQ as a versatile platform for alternative splicing quantification with broad applicability across functionally distinct genes. The successful application to both PBRM1 (chromatin remodeling) and HTRA2 (mitochondrial protease) demonstrates platform generalizability.

Discussion

The MASQ platform represents a practical and accessible adaptation of multiplex qPCR methodology for alternative splicing quantification, addressing the limitation of conventional PCR-based analysis, which requires agarose gel electrophoresis to quantitatively measure exon inclusion rates. This study demonstrates that dual TaqMan probe chemistry within a single multiplex reaction offers a straightforward solution to size-dependent separation limitations of gel electrophoresis, while providing consistent and reproducible quantification of alternative splicing events without requiring specialized instrumentation.

The core innovation of MASQ lies in its dual-probe normalization strategy, which eliminates the primary sources of variability inherent in conventional quantification methods. Traditional RT-PCR followed by gel electrophoresis suffers from size-dependent separation artifacts, non-linear band intensity relationships, and substantial inter-gel variability that typically results in coefficients of variation exceeding 25%.60,61,62,63 While advanced techniques such as capillary electrophoresis offer substantially higher resolution and quantification precision than standard agarose gels, they require specialized instrumentation and additional post-PCR processing steps that may limit their accessibility in routine clinical settings. In contrast, a practical advantage of MASQ is its reliance on widely accessible and cost-effective standard qPCR instrumentation, which directly converts real-time amplification data into quantitative digital values without additional post-processing. Our demonstration that PBRM1 exons E26 and E27 differ by only nine nucleotides exemplifies a widespread challenge, where functionally critical exon pairs exhibit minimal size differences that preclude reliable gel-based quantification.33 MASQ addresses these limitations through sequence-specific TaqMan probes that eliminate size-dependent separation requirements, dual-probe design that provides inherent internal normalization, and single-tube format that eliminates inter-reaction variability. Our analytical validation demonstrates exceptional performance characteristics: linearity (R2 > 0.98) across more than three orders of magnitude, femtogram-level sensitivity, and intra-assay precision <9%. These metrics establish MASQ as a superior approach for alternative splicing quantification compared to conventional gel-based methods and even advanced techniques such as digital PCR.63

Furthermore, conventional qPCR-based alternative splicing assays relying on exon-exon junction primers may exhibit reduced accuracy in complex human tissue samples due to variability in primer binding and amplification efficiency between isoforms. In addition, when exon inclusion isoforms are expressed at low basal levels, small absolute changes can appear as disproportionately large fold changes, potentially complicating clinical interpretation. MASQ addresses these concerns by employing target-specific probes directed at both the constitutive and alternative exons, and normalizing their Ct values against a reference F.L. plasmid to calculate a calibrated relative percentage. This ratio-based approach may reduce the interpretive ambiguity associated with fold-change calculations when quantifying low-abundance transcripts. This distinction is particularly relevant in clinical applications, where consistent and reproducible quantification of alternative splicing events across samples is essential for accurate biomarker assessment and therapeutic monitoring.

Validation of MASQ analysis using UCEC patient specimens demonstrates that alternative exon inclusion can be quantitatively assessed in clinical samples. The observed difference in E27 inclusion between cancer and normal tissues (61.2% vs. 12.7%) provides a substantial diagnostic window, whereas the detection of significant inter-tumor heterogeneity (48%–85%) underscores the inadequacy of binary classification approaches and highlights the need for precise quantitative methods in personalized medicine. The clinical significance extends beyond diagnostic applications through the established connection between E27 inclusion and immune checkpoint resistance. Our previous work demonstrated that E27 inclusion enhances PBAF complex recruitment to the PD-L1 promoter, leading to increased PD-L1 expression and resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.33 The precise quantification capability of MASQ therefore enables stratification of cancer patients for immunotherapy selection and monitoring of treatment response, directly supporting precision oncology applications. The consistency of our findings with independent TCGA RNA-seq data provides orthogonal validation and demonstrates that MASQ can accurately quantify splice variants identified through large-scale genomic studies.33,64

The successful adaptation of MASQ to HTRA2 E7 quantification demonstrates broad applicability across functionally distinct genes and biological pathways. HTRA2 encodes a mitochondrial serine protease involved in apoptosis regulation, representing a markedly different biological context from the chromatin remodeling functions of PBRM1.65 The identical probe design principles and analytical performance achieved across these diverse targets establish the transferability of the MASQ approach to virtually any alternative splicing event of interest. The demonstration of precise SSO monitoring represents significant advancement for therapeutic development in the emerging field of splicing-targeted medicine. The ability to quantify SSO-mediated splicing modulation enables robust pharmacodynamic monitoring essential for clinical development. This capability has particular relevance given the growing pipeline of antisense oligonucleotide and small molecule splicing modulators entering clinical trials, where MASQ provides the quantitative precision necessary for dose escalation studies, proof of concept trials, and efficacy assessments.66

MASQ addresses a critical gap in the precision medicine toolkit by providing quantitative methods for the functional genomics layer that bridges DNA sequence variation and phenotypic outcomes. While genomic sequencing has revealed extensive alternative splicing alterations in human disease, the lack of practical quantification methods has limited translation of these discoveries into clinical applications. MASQ represents a step toward routine clinical assessment of post-transcriptional regulation, complementing existing genomic and proteomic diagnostic approaches with superior speed (4 h vs. 2–3 weeks for RNA-seq) and cost-effectiveness (10- to 20-fold lower per sample). The applicability of the platform extends beyond oncology to neurological disorders, autoimmune diseases, and rare genetic conditions that exhibit characteristic alternative splicing patterns.67 As our understanding of the role of alternative splicing in human disease continues to expand, particularly with the emergence of RNA-targeted therapeutics, robust quantification methods such as MASQ will be essential for translating these insights into improved patient outcomes. The demonstrated analytical performance, clinical validation, and therapeutic monitoring capabilities position MASQ as a valuable addition to the precision medicine toolkit for the era of personalized RNA medicine.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). HeLa, U-2 OS, HCT116, HT29, 293A, HepG2, SH-SY5Y, and SK-N-SH cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco #12483–020, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Welgene). BeWo cells were cultured in F-12K medium (Kaighn’s modification of Ham’s F-12 medium; ATCC #30–2004) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. A549, K562, and Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Corning Life Sciences #10-040-CV, Corning, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. NK-92 cells were cultured in alpha-MEM (Gibco #12561–056) supplemented with 12.5% FBS, 12.5% horse serum (Gibco #26050–088), 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco #21985–023), 200 μM inositol (Sigma-Aldrich #I7508, St. Louis, MO, USA), 20 μM folic acid (Sigma-Aldrich #F8758), 100 U/mL recombinant human IL-2 (PeproTech #200–02, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing

PBRM1 ΔE27 HeLa cells were generated as previously described.33 Briefly, a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the 3ʹsplice site of PBRM1 E27 was designed using the CRISPOR tool (http://crispor.tefor.net/). Complementary oligonucleotides were synthesized as follows: top strand, 5ʹ-CACCGTGTGTCCTTCAAGGCATGAT-3ʹ and bottom strand, 5ʹ-AAACATCATGCCTTGAAGGACACAC-3ʹ. The oligonucleotides were annealed and cloned into the pX330 vector (Addgene #42230, Watertown, MA, USA), then transfected into HeLa cells using jetPEI reagent (Polyplus, Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Clinical tissue specimens

UCEC tissue samples (n = 5) and matched adjacent normal endometrial tissue samples (n = 3) were obtained from patients undergoing surgery at Chungnam National University Hospital. Normal tissues were histologically confirmed to be free of malignancy. All tissue samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction. The samples were fully de-identified, and the study was exempted from full ethical review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Chungnam National University Hospital (IRB approval number: CNUH202312018-HE003). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissue samples using the Hybrid-R RNA Extraction kit (GeneAll 305–101, Seoul, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis (1% gel in TAE buffer) and concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Only RNA samples with A260/A280 ratios ≥1.8 and A260/A230 ratios ≥2.0 were used for subsequent analyses. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed in a 20 μL reaction volume containing random hexamers (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega #M170B), and RNase inhibitor (Enzynomics #M007S, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The reverse transcription reaction was performed at 50°C for 60 min. The synthesized cDNA was diluted 1:5 with nuclease-free water and stored at −20°C until use. RT-PCR was performed using AccuPower Taq PCR Master Mix (Bioneer #K-2610, Daejeon, Republic of Korea), followed by agarose gel electrophoresis. For quantification of splice variants, DNA band intensities were measured using ImageJ software. To calculate exon inclusion percentages, PCR band intensities were normalized to the size of the PCR products.

Plasmid construction

PBRM1 plasmids containing E27 inclusion (PBRM1 F.L.) or exclusion (PBRM1 ΔE27) variants were generated by amplifying PBRM1 fragments spanning exons E25-E28 or E25-E27-E28 from HeLa cell cDNA and cloning into the pCR2.1-TOPO vector (Invitrogen #K450001, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Similarly, HTRA2 plasmids containing E7 inclusion (HTRA2 F.L.) or exclusion (HTRA2 ΔE7) were constructed. All constructs were verified using Sanger sequencing (Macrogen, Seoul, Republic of Korea).

TaqMan probe and primer design

The primers used in MASQ were for PBRM1, the forward primer (5ʹ-GATTAAGGCCCAACACCCAGAC-3ʹ) and reverse primer (5ʹ-GCTGGAGTCCCTACCATAGG-3ʹ) flanked exon E27. A FAM-labeled probe (5ʹ-TGGCCTTGTTAGCATGGGCAGCATGC-BHQ1-3ʹ) targeted E27, and a HEX-labeled probe (5ʹ-TCAGCCGCCTGGTGGGGACAGAATGG-BHQ1-3ʹ) targeted E25 for normalization. For HTRA2, the forward primer (5ʹ-ATTCCTCCTCCGGAATCAGTGG-3ʹ) and reverse primer (5ʹ-GCCAAAATCACATCACCAGGC-3ʹ) flanked exon E7. A FAM-labeled probe (5ʹ-GTGTGCAGGGGAGCCCAGGATGACTT-BHQ1-3ʹ) targeted E7, and a HEX-labeled probe (5ʹ-GCGGCGCTACATTGGGGTGATGATGCT-BHQ1-3ʹ) targeted E6. All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Macrogen (Seoul, Republic of Korea. All primers and dual-labeled TaqMan probes were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool. Probes were designed with a melting temperature (Tm) approximately 10°C higher than that of their corresponding primers, and probe lengths were restricted to under 30 bp to ensure efficient quenching and fluorophore release. The synthesized oligonucleotides were diluted to working concentrations of 10 pmol/μL for primers and 5 pmol/μL for probes. Each 20 μL reaction contained 10 pmol of each primer and 5 pmol of each TaqMan probe, consistent with the multiplex qPCR assay conditions described previously.

MASQ analysis

MASQ analysis was performed using the QuantStudio 1 real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with HS Prime qPCR Premix for TaqMan Probe (GENETBIO #Q-4000, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Each 20 μL reaction contained 10 μL of 2× qPCR premix, 5 pmol of each TaqMan probe (FAM and HEX), 10 pmol of each primer, and diluted cDNA template. The thermal cycling conditions used are initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Fluorescence signals were acquired at the end of each extension step. Ct values were determined using QuantStudio Design and Analysis software v.1.5.1 and Diomni Design and Analysis software v.3.0.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The Ct values obtained from MASQ analysis are listed in Table S1.

Calculation of exon inclusion percentage

The percentage of exon inclusion was calculated using a modified ΔΔCt method based on dual-probe normalization. The formula used was % exon inclusion = {2ˆ[−((Ct_FAM − Ct_HEX)_sample − (Ct_FAM − Ct_HEX)_reference)]} × 100, where Ct (cDNA, target) is the Ct value obtained from the cDNA sample using the E27-specific FAM probe; Ct (cDNA, constitutive) is the Ct value from the cDNA sample using the target exon-specific HEX probe; Ct (F.L., target) is the Ct value from the F.L. plasmid DNA (100% inclusion control) using the target exon-specific FAM probe; and Ct (F.L., constitutive) is the Ct value from the F.L. plasmid DNA using the constitutive exon-specific HEX probe. The F.L. plasmid serves as a reference standard representing 100% exon inclusion. This calculation method normalizes the target exon signal to the constitutive exon signal in both the sample and reference, allowing accurate quantification of alternative splicing ratios. It should be noted that the exon inclusion percentage reported by MASQ represents a calibrated relative measure normalized to the F.L. plasmid reference standard, rather than an absolute quantitative percentage. Since the F.L. plasmid contains, the target and constitutive exons at equimolar ratios, the reference ΔCt (Ct_FAM − Ct_HEX) inherently accounts for any differences in binding and amplification efficiency between the FAM and HEX probes. By subtracting this reference ΔCt from the sample ΔCt in the ΔΔCt calculation, the impact of probe efficiency differences on the final exon inclusion value is minimized.

SSO treatment

All SSOs were synthesized as chemically modified RNA oligonucleotides with a phosphorothioate backbone and 2ʹ-O-methyl modifications at the 2ʹ-sugar positions. For HTRA2 splicing modulation, a target SSO (5ʹ-GGAUGCUGAAAGUACACAGAUCA-3ʹ) was designed to induce E7 exclusion, and a control SSO (5ʹ-UGCAUUCGCCCUCUUAAUGGGGA-3ʹ) was designed as a non-targeting control. HeLa cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were transfected with 1 μg of SSO using Lipofectamine stem transfection reagent (Invitrogen #STEM00015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested at 24 and 48 h post-transfection for RNA extraction and subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. For qPCR analysis, technical triplicates were performed for each sample. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired Student’s t test and Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p values <0.05.
