Machine learning model for predicting hepatitis C seroconversion in methadone maintenance patients in China
Authors: Xinyu Lu, Qing Yue, He Jing, Gangliang Zhong, Zhen Ning, Jiang Du, Min Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is a substantial public health concern, particularly among individuals with opioid addiction. The methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) programmes serve as a harm reduction strategy to mitigate HIV disease spread, yet the risk of HCV infection remains high within these settings. Accurate risk prediction for HCV seroconversion is therefore crucial for improving patient outcomes.MethodsWe collected data from 1547 individuals with opioid use disorder who entered the MMT programme from May 2005 to October 2023 in Shanghai, China, and 283 individuals from July 2006 to October 2023 in Mianyang, China, whose HCV infection status was monitored. Shanghai data were divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio, with 70% of the Shanghai samples used for model training and the remaining 30% reserved for internal validation. Additionally, the Mianyang dataset was employed as an independent external validation cohort to assess the model’s generalisability. Four machine learning models were developed. We then validated the predictive performance of the model using C-index, receiver-operating characteristic curves, calibration plots and decision curve analysis.Results13 predictive factors, including sex, age, ethnicity, education, occupation status, marriage status, living status, financial resource, drug use method in the past half year, injected drug last month, condom use, average methadone dosage and positive rate of drug urine tests, were all incorporated into the predictive model. The eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model exhibited superior performance in both discrimination and calibration compared with the other three models. Specifically, it achieved C-indices of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.771 to 0.813) in the training cohort, 0.744 (0.709 to 0.779) in the internal validation cohort and 0.756 (0.712 to 0.799) in the external validation cohort for predicting HCV seroconversion. A publicly accessible web tool was generated for the model.ConclusionsThe developed XGBoost model has the potential to accurately predict individuals on MMT programmes at high risk of HCV seroconversion.Keywords: Disease Transmission, Infectious; Public Health; Sexually Transmitted Diseases

Introduction

WHO estimates that 58 million people worldwide were infected with the hepatitis C virus (HCV) in 2019 and that HCV infection-related diseases cause 700 000 deaths each year. HCV infection can lead to chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis and ultimately liver cancer1 and has been a dominant cause of liver cancer in almost all regions of the world.2 In China, viral hepatitis is the largest risk factor for liver cancer, regardless of gender and region.3 China has experienced a concerning increase in HCV disease burden from 1990 to 2019, particularly among high-risk populations such as people who inject drugs. Because of its population size, the absolute number of people living with HCV is still substantial, estimated at 9.5 million.4

Injection drug use is the primary risk factor for HCV infection, followed by sexual transmission.5 Other high-risk factors include transfusion of contaminated blood or blood products, but this has gradually diminished in China with the establishment of a blood donor safety system.6 The main illicit drug in China in the early 21st century was opioids.7 The methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) programme was established in 2004 as a form of harm reduction. The MMT programme is a cornerstone intervention for opioid use disorder that provides daily supervised methadone dosing alongside psychosocial support. This has become the largest MMT project globally. As of 2011, it has provided services to 300 000 individuals with opioid use disorders.8 A review encompassing 90 relevant studies in China indicated that the prevalence of HCV stands at 60.1% in this population8 compared with only 1.6% in the general population. The incidence of HCV among MMT clinic users varies by region, with the highest reaching 78.1%.9 In Shanghai, the incidence among MMT users is approximately 66.4%,6 9 which is similar to the national average. Given the exceptionally high HCV prevalence in MMT populations and the similarly alarming seroconversion rates among initially HCV-negative subgroups, monitoring seroconversion becomes particularly critical. Despite the success of China’s MMT programme, this population remains at elevated risk for HCV seroconversion due to multiple interrelated factors. A subset of patients continues to engage in relapse behaviours and unsafe injection practices (particularly needle sharing), perpetuating HCV transmission chains. The high baseline HCV prevalence among MMT patients is further compounded by their increased likelihood of engaging in high-risk behaviours. Additionally, psychosocial challenges, including family dysfunction, personal instability and social marginalisation, contribute to mental health comorbidities that may predispose individuals to risk-taking behaviours.10,12 These complex, mutually reinforcing factors create a perfect storm for sustained HCV transmission within this vulnerable population. Despite the extensive coverage of MMT programmes, significant gaps remain in routine HCV screening and treatment implementation.13 Compounding this challenge is the fact that HCV infection often remains asymptomatic in its early stages, making timely detection particularly difficult. Undiagnosed seroconverters during the acute, highly infectious phase serve as key drivers of ongoing HCV transmission within MMT populations. Clinically, delayed identification allows progression to chronic infection, significantly diminishing treatment efficacy while escalating long-term healthcare burdens.14 This underscores the critical need for a simple yet practical clinical tool that can effectively identify high-risk individuals within MMT populations. Such a tool may enable healthcare providers to implement targeted interventions, ultimately helping to reduce the persistently high HCV seroconversion rates observed in this vulnerable group. Identifying high-risk individuals for prevention within this project is of the utmost importance.

Over the past 20 years, numerous studies have reported risk factors associated with HCV infection in MMT programmes. However, there has been limited research on HCV seroconversion. Only two relevant studies have been identified: a sample from 14 clinics in Guangdong Province showed an HCV incidence rate of 20.54 (95% CI: 18.62 to 22.46) per 100 person-years between 2006 and 2014.15 Risk factors included positive urine drug tests, unmarried status, recent injection drug use within the past 30 days, history of sexual activity and higher daily methadone doses. Another study in Wuhan, China, from 2006 to 2011, found that approximately 46.3% (555/1200) of patients experienced HCV seroconversion during MMT.16 Predictive factors included recent injection drug use within the past 30 days and positive urine tests for opioid substances during MMT. Despite these important findings, three critical research gaps remain: (1) while these studies have identified key risk factors, they primarily used conventional statistical approaches that have limited capacity to generate individualised, clinically useful predictions—a gap that could be addressed by contemporary machine learning (ML) techniques as demonstrated in recent healthcare applications 17,19; (2) the ≤8-year observation periods (2006–2014) fail to capture evolving risk patterns in China’s current MMT system and (3) risk factors were treated as static variables rather than dynamic indicators measurable during routine clinical visits.

Patients in the MMT clinics have a high probability of HCV seroconversion, and clinicians need to pay more attention to high-risk groups. In other studies investigating the risk factors for HCV infection among MMT patients, additional factors have been proposed, including prolonged history of drug use,9 female gender,9 minority nationality,9 living alone20 and reliance on social welfare for financial support.20 Current research has been limited to risk factor analysis, without proposing the use of reliable predictive models to quantify the risk of HCV seroconversion, enabling clinicians to rapidly identify high-risk populations. Furthermore, the total duration of the only two studies on HCV seroconversion does not exceed 10 years,15 16 thus lacking insight into long-term maintenance treatment for patients.

ML is a methodology that uses artificial intelligence to more effectively generate predictive models by unveiling latent patterns within complex datasets. An increasing amount of research is using ML techniques to predict the progression of chronic viral hepatitis,21 22 thereby establishing ML-based models as valuable tools in clinical practice. This study aims to establish an ML-based diagnostic model for predicting HCV seroconversion in patients attending MMT outpatient clinics, which assists clinical practitioners in identifying high-risk populations and enables early detection of hepatic inflammation in these patients.

Methods

Study design

Participants of this multicentre, retrospective study came from 14 MMT treatment clinics in Shanghai between May 2005 and October 2023 and the Mianyang MMT treatment clinic in Sichuan, China, between July 2006 and October 2023. The completely independent Mianyang cohort was specifically collected to perform rigorous external validation—a crucial step recommended by TRIPOD guidelines for assessing model generalisability.23 Study participants from Shanghai were randomly grouped into a training cohort (n=1082) and an internal validation cohort (n=465) at a ratio of 7:3, and participants from Mianyang were used as an external validation cohort (n=283). To ensure the comparability of baseline characteristics between the training and validation sets, we conducted a thorough assessment of data balance across all variables, including continuous and categorical features. Clinicians at these MMT clinics used a questionnaire developed by the China Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), to collect baseline information on all patients at the entry of the MMT programme. Data collected also included information on methadone dosages dispensed at each follow-up visit during treatment, laboratory testing for HCV and drug use urine testing.

This study involved a rigorous selection of research participants. For the measurement of HCV antibody seroconversion that occurred during MMT, we excluded patients who tested seropositive for HCV at entry and those who had only one HCV test at enrolment. Of the remaining patients who tested seronegative at entry and had records of at least one follow-up test, we also excluded patients without methadone dispense records and those who did not undergo baseline enrolment surveys. The study participants’ flow chart is displayed in online supplemental figure S1. HCV tests were performed using ELISA test, with patients in maintenance treatment at the MMT clinic being tested at least every 6 months. The HCV ELISA test demonstrates high sensitivity (>95%) and specificity (>95%), making it reliable for HCV antibody detection in screening programmes. Its simple operation requires minimal training and no specialised equipment, enabling widespread use in primary care settings.

Data collection

Data collection was divided into two parts, the first part being the self-reported survey information from the questionnaire at entry, and the second part being the data collected during follow-up. The first part included detailed demographic characteristics, including age, sex, ethnicity, education, employment, marriage status, living situation and financial resources; it also included the main drug use method in the past half year, the history of injecting drug use last month and sexual behaviour. Condom use was then regrouped according to consistent condom use and recorded as no sex, yes and no. The second part included average methadone dosage and the proportion of positive drug urine tests during follow-up. Average methadone dosage was measured by dividing the total dose of methadone dispensed by the total number of days taking methadone. The MMT clinics dispense methadone daily, but most patients do not come to the clinic every day.24 The positive rate of positive drug urine tests was calculated by dividing the number of positive urine tests by the total number of urine tests performed. Urine tests for morphine metabolites were performed once a month in MMT patients.16 The data collection for the first part also involved several other variables, including the initiation time of drug use, needle sharing, primary drug currently in use, number of sexual partners, and so on. However, due to a missing data rate exceeding 30% in this section, these variables were not included in the model construction. The collected data did not exhibit any missing variables; hence, imputation was unnecessary.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were presented as mean and SD and compared by t-test or Mann-Whitney U tests when necessary between the training and validation datasets. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test. The Kaplan-Meier method and the reverse Kaplan-Meier method were used to calculate the median HCV seroconversion time and the median follow-up time.

In our study, we developed and compared four models aimed at predicting HCV seroconversion among patients in MMT outpatient clinics, with particular emphasis on the four time points of 1, 3, 5 and 10 years of MMT maintenance treatment. These models include the Cox proportional hazards model (Coxph), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Random Survival Forest (RSF) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost).25 26 We deliberately evaluated both individual algorithms (Coxph, LASSO) and ensemble techniques (Random Forest, XGBoost) to provide a comprehensive comparison. Ensemble-of-ensembles (eg, stacking XGBoost+RF) risks overfitting given our sample size (n=1547).

LASSO regression, as a regularisation method, introduces an L1 regularisation term into the regression model. This process shrinks specific feature coefficients to zero,27 effectively selecting the most relevant features. The RSF, an ensemble learning method, comprises multiple decision trees, each trained on distinct subsets of the training data formed by random sampling with replacement.28 In the construction of each decision tree, the Random Forest enhances model diversity by the random selection of features.29 XGBoost, an ensemble learning model based on the gradient boosting algorithm, constructs a robust classifier through iterative training of multiple weak classifiers and optimisation of the loss function using gradient descent.21 Notably, XGBoost excels in handling structured data and large-scale datasets, demonstrating both efficiency and accuracy.30 31 The hyperparameter tuning process was conducted using the ‘caret’ package within a nested validation framework.32 Specifically, a fivefold cross-validation approach was employed on the training set, combined with a comprehensive grid search evaluating 50 distinct parameter configurations for each ML algorithm. For the XGBoost model, key hyperparameters were meticulously optimised, such as the number of boosting rounds (nrounds), maximum tree depth (max_depth), step size shrinkage (eta) and regularisation parameters (gamma). Similarly, the Random Forest model underwent parallel optimisation, with adjustments made to the number of randomly selected predictors per split (mtry), splitting criteria (splitrule) and the minimum observations required in terminal nodes (min.node.size)(online supplemental figure S2)

The training cohort encompassed 13 variables, including sex, age, ethnicity, education, occupation status, marriage status, living status, financial resources, drug use method in the past half year, injected drug last month, condom use, average methadone dosage and positive rate of drug urine tests. These variables were incorporated into four ML algorithms.

The predictive performance of the ml-based model was then quantified using the concordance index (C-index) and the area under the curve (AUC) of the time-dependent receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and the diagnostic performance was then quantified by calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA) based on the training and validation sets, respectively.33 The time-dependent AUC and C-index quantified discrimination—the model’s ability to distinguish seroconverters from non-converters over time. Calibration curves assessed agreement between predicted and observed risks, ensuring clinical utility. DCA evaluated net benefit across risk thresholds, guiding clinical decision-making. Together, these metrics address distinct aspects of predictive performance: discrimination (AUC/C-index), accuracy (calibration) and practicality (DCA). A robust predictive model must simultaneously satisfy three key criteria: (1) strong discrimination (C-index>0.7), (2) accurate calibration and (3) clinical utility (positive net benefit on DCA across relevant risk thresholds). The C-index and AUC values are within the range from 0.5 to 1.0, where 0.5 represents the random probability, and a value of >0.7 is deemed reasonable.34 The 95% CIs of C-index and AUC were calculated through 1000 bootstrapped resamplings.31 All participants were categorised into low-risk and high-risk groups by the median of the predicted individual risks, and the risk stratification was compared.

All statistical analyses were performed using R V.3.4.3. Statistical tests were two-sided, and p values<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The characteristics of the training cohort (n=1082), internal validation cohort (n=465) and external validation cohort (n=283) are presented in table 1, with similar characteristics between the three groups. In the training cohort, the mean age was 52.9 years (SD 9.1), 76.2% being male, 78.9% unemployed, 38.7% injected drug in the last 6 months and 42.0% injected last month. In the internal validation cohort, the mean age was 52.9 years (SD 8.6), 77.2% being male, 77.8% unemployed, 38.5% injected drug in the last 6 months and 38.1% injected last month. In the external validation cohort, the mean age was 53.3 years (SD 8.6), 78.8% being male, 76.7% unemployed, 42.8% injected drug in the last 6 months and 47.7% injected last month.

Characteristics of the training set and validation cohort

The incidence of HCV seroconversion during follow-up was 108.9 cases per 1000 person-years in the training cohort, 104.4 cases per 1000 person-years in the internal validation cohort and 105.0 cases per 1000 person-years in the external validation cohort. The median time to HCV seroconversion was 82.9 months (95% CI: 71.1 to 105.0), 98.4 months (95% CI: 66.2 to 140.0) and 91.2 months (95% CI: 65.3 to 127.0) at training set, internal and external validation cohort, respectively. The median follow-up time was 53.0 months (95% CI: 46.0 to 57.8), 57.5 months (95% CI: 51.5 to 65.2) and 53.7 months (95% CI: 47.5 to 66.6) at training set, internal and external validation cohort, respectively.

Performance of predicting HCV seroconversion for four models

Four models were based on 13 variables: sex, age, ethnicity, education, occupation status, marriage status, living status, financial resources, drug use method in the past half year, injected drug last month, condom use, average methadone dosage and positive rate of drug urine tests. A publicly accessible web tool was generated for the model, available at https://wahahacode.shinyapps.io/MMTxgboost/. Prediction using this tool is clinically practical. For instance, for a 25-year-old employed man with family responsibilities and low educational attainment, whose baseline assessment reveals pre-enrolment drug use, a urine positivity rate of 0.3 and a mean MMT dose of 35 mg, the model projects his HCV-negative probability as 0.72 (1 year), 0.40 (3 years), 0.26 (5 years) and 0.11 (10 years).

Table 2 displays the discriminative accuracy of four models in the training and validation cohort. In the training cohort, the C-index value was 0.723 (95% CI: 0.697 to 0.748) for the Coxph model, 0.709 (95% CI: 0.682 to 0.736) for the LASSO model, 0.817 (95% CI: 0.798 to 0.833) for RSF model and 0.793 (95% CI: 0.771 to 0.813) for XGBoost model. The C-index of the RSF and XGBoost models in the training cohort was approximately 0.8, with AUC values exceeding 0.8 at four different time points, suggesting that the models had good discriminatory power. Discussion

HCV infection stands as a leading cause of chronic liver disease and liver cancer,35 36 resulting in substantial mortality each year. Over 20 years, the MMT project in China has revealed that patients undergoing treatment within this programme bear a considerable burden of HCV serum-related diseases.9 Notably, prolonged treatment within this programme has shown a relatively frequent occurrence of HCV seroconversion.15 16 Despite this, a predictive model for HCV seroconversion among MMT outpatient populations is currently lacking. Leveraging patient data from MMT clinics in Shanghai spanning from May 2005 to October 2023, we developed an ML-based predictive model for HCV seroconversion and verified in Mianyang MMT patient data between July 2006 and October 2023. This study integrates an XBGoost model, enabling personalised prediction37 and facilitating the assessment of patient conversion risk for clinical use.38

Our XGBoost prediction model is applicable for assessing and predicting the risk of HCV seroconversion in patients enrolled in the MMT programme. It demonstrated good discriminative abilities in both the training and validation cohorts, with a C-index of 0.793, 0.744 and 0.756, respectively. The ROC curve and calibration plot exhibited a strong concordance between predicted and actual outcomes.

Previous studies have only involved a small number of investigations into HCV seroconversion within MMT programmes,15 16 while other research has focused on the risk factors for HCV infection.9 Our study found that the cumulative seroconversion rate among patients in Shanghai’s MMT programme was 10. 9 per 100 person-years. This value is lower than the rates reported in related studies in Wuhan, which stood at 34.5 per 100 person-years,16 and in Guangdong Province, which reported 20.5 per 100 person-years.15 The primary reason for this difference is the gradual decline in HCV seroconversion rates15 and the relatively short duration of the two aforementioned studies, which may not fully reflect long-term changes. The median seroconversion time for patients in Wuhan was 36 months, whereas our study observed a median time of 82.9 months. Several similar risk factors were identified in the aforementioned studies, such as the use of injected drugs and positive results in opioid testing. These findings align with the predictive factors in our study. Specifically, our research revealed two factors associated with drug injection: the primary method of drug use in the past 6 months and whether drug injection occurred in the previous month. Both of these factors exhibited significant correlations with HCV seroconversion, thus confirming the commonality of drug injection as the most prevalent route of HCV transmission.36 This study also revealed that the positive rate of drug urine tests is a crucial predictive factor. Previous research implemented linear methods which could not fully capture the non-linear association of urine test positivity with HCV seroconversion.16 The higher the proportion of positive urine tests, the greater the frequency of drug use. Among drug injectors, the practice of sharing injection equipment is a highly perilous factor for HCV transmission.8 39 The predictive model’s key feature importance is visually presented in figure 3, allowing clinicians to immediately identify which variables most significantly impact outcomes. Designed for seamless integration into routine clinical practice, this tool enables healthcare providers to simply input a patient’s recent urine test results, baseline drug use characteristics and other relevant variables to quickly generate intuitive HCV risk predictions (1 year, 3 years, 5 years and 10 years)—a streamlined process requiring just 2 min using only routinely collected clinical data. This predictive model demonstrates multiple clinical utilities in MMT clinics. For newly enrolled patients, it enables rapid HCV risk assessment by inputting routine clinical data such as drug use history, allowing clinicians to promptly identify high-risk individuals for prioritised intensive interventions. In resource allocation, the model’s risk stratification guides priority sequencing for limited medication distribution. For patient education, enhanced counselling targeting high-risk populations significantly improves treatment adherence and behaviour modification. Additionally, the model’s real-time risk monitoring capability facilitates timely adjustment of prevention measures when detecting elevated risk levels, effectively preventing clustered transmission. All applications are integrated within routine clinical workflows without requiring additional testing.

When compared with published literature, apart from drug-relative points, the study in Guangdong also highlighted unmarried status, sexual behaviour and daily high-dose methadone use as risk factors. Unmarried individuals may experience unstable family relationships, potentially leading to a higher risk of transmission through increased sexual contact.40 Patients taking daily high doses of methadone often represent a population with prolonged drug use and more severe addiction.41 Within this group, there is a greater likelihood of engaging in high-risk behaviours, thus increasing susceptibility to infection.42 In this study, it was found that not using condoms is an independent risk factor. Although the efficiency of HCV transmission through sexual intercourse is low,43 during long-term follow-ups, the cumulative increase in the number of sexual partners and risky encounters may elevate the overall risk of sexual behaviour. Previous research also revealed that 10% of patients in Shanghai’s MMT programme had multiple sexual partners and low condom usage,6 even in the context of commercial sex. This study also identified low educational attainment and reliance on social support for financial expenses as predictive factors. Low educational attainment may lead to a lack of HCV-related knowledge and a disregard for risky behaviours. Individuals receiving social support often lack stable employment and family relationships. Additionally, female drug users, without stable income, are more likely to engage in commercial sex.43 In such circumstances, having multiple partners and engaging in unprotected sexual activity are common occurrences.

In other cross-sectional studies on the risk factors for HCV infection among patients in MMT programmes, several additional risk factors have been identified. Researchers from the Chinese CDC conducted a study on nearly 300 000 patients enrolled in MMT programmes nationwide.9 They found that longer drug use history, female gender and minority ethnicity were risk factors. A longer history of drug use is a reliable indicator of the individual’s drug use status. However, during data extraction for this study, it was observed that there was a significant amount of missing data in the variable of self-reported duration of drug use. As a result, this variable was not included. This issue is also quite common in other studies, indicating that the practical utility of this data may be limited,16 and its absence is unlikely to significantly impact the model’s predictions. In this study, female gender and minority ethnicity were not predictive factors. This could be attributed to regional differences. Furthermore, being female did not emerge as a risk factor, a finding consistent with similar research conducted in Wuhan city.16

Long-term HCV seroconversion among patients undergoing MMT treatment was investigated in this study, and a predictive model was established to address the gaps in seroconversion data for these patients. This enables clinical practitioners to swiftly assess a patient’s risk of HCV seroconversion using readily available variables, allowing for early intervention to reduce new HCV infections. Since the number of new patients entering MMT programmes has been decreasing annually after 2011,44 studying long-term HCV seroconversion in this context becomes more meaningful. The ML-based model developed in this study demonstrated the highest AUC value in predicting 10-year HCV seroconversion, indicating the stability of the XGBoost model in forecasting long-term HCV seroconversion. While self-reported behavioural data (eg, condom use) may vary over time and repeated measurements could theoretically improve prediction accuracy, our model achieved sustained 10-year predictive performance (AUC=0.826) by leveraging more stable indicators. Two factors contributed to this finding: (1) the dominant predictive value of objectively monitored treatment data (eg, long-term urine test positivity rates) and baseline drug use patterns in our XGBoost model, and (2) the characteristically low behavioural variability in this population, as shown by persistently low condom use rates among long-term MMT patients.45 This suggests that while time-varying behavioural data may offer additional value, reliable risk prediction can be achieved using routinely collected treatment indicators—a practical advantage for clinical implementation.

Our study possesses several significant strengths. First, it addresses the gap in predicting HCV seroconversion within MMT outpatient settings. Additionally, our study maintains a prolonged follow-up period, enabling the observation of patients undergoing long-term maintenance treatment. Our model incorporates both patient self-reported data and systematically recorded testing and medication records, demonstrating strong predictive performance, with AUCs exceeding 0.8 for short-term and long-term HCV seroconversion in external validation cohorts. Furthermore, we enhance the interpretability of our ML model by visualising feature importance. The present study does have certain limitations. First, our analysis was based on self-reported questionnaire data collected at the time of entry, which may be susceptible to recall bias, leading to potential errors. Second, there was substantial missing data in the self-reported records, such as the duration of drug use. It is possible that patients were unwilling to disclose this information or their lack of specific awareness regarding the details, leading to missing data. Third, a key limitation is our reliance on baseline behavioural characteristics (eg, drug use, condom use) without regular longitudinal updates. Although these factors may change over time, our model’s performance (AUC>0.80 across validation sets) suggests baseline measures retain predictive value. Future iterations should incorporate periodic behavioural reassessments to enhance temporal accuracy.

Conclusion

We developed an XGBoost model to predict the incidence of HCV seroconversion in patients of the MMT programme with good discriminative ability. This may be useful in facilitating precise prevention and personalised management of high-risk populations.
