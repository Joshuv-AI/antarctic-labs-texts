Real‐world implementation of lecanemab in Korean patients at a tertiary memory clinic: Safety, ARIA risk, and early treatment patterns
Authors: Jaemin Shin, Sang‐Il Suh, Yu Jeong Park, Seungyun Lee, Jimin Kang, Eun Seong Lee, Hye Na Jung, Inseon Ryoo, Seongin Lee, Jae Seon Eo, Kyungmi Oh, Seong‐Beom Koh, Sung Hoon Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLecanemab has demonstrated clinical efficacy in early Alzheimer's disease (AD), but real‐world data remain limited, particularly in Asian populations.METHODSWe prospectively enrolled 127 patients across the AD continuum who initiated lecanemab at a tertiary memory clinic in Korea. Infusion‐related reactions (IRRs), amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs), and cognitive outcomes were evaluated through clinical assessments, serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and longitudinal Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) follow‐up.RESULTSAmong 127 patients, 102 completed at least five infusions with follow‐up MRI. IRRs occurred in 27 (21.3%) patients, primarily during the first infusion. ARIAs occurred in 9 of 102 (8.8%), including ARIAs with edema in 4 (3.9%) and ARIA with hemorrhage in 7 (6.9%); most events were mild or asymptomatic. Higher microbleed burden and white matter hyperintensity were associated with increased ARIA risk. MMSE scores remained stable over 6 months.DISCUSSIONIn this prospective Korean cohort, lecanemab was generally well tolerated. Longer term controlled studies are needed to clarify cognitive outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, leading to progressive synaptic dysfunction, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline. 1 , 2 , 3 Growing evidence suggests that soluble amyloid aggregates, particularly protofibrils, play a key role in the early stages of AD pathogenesis and may contribute to downstream tau pathology and neuronal injury. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Lecanemab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that preferentially targets soluble amyloid protofibrils while also facilitating the clearance of insoluble amyloid plaques, supporting disease‐modifying treatment in early AD. 8 , 9

In the phase 3 CLARITY‐AD trial, lecanemab slowed cognitive and functional decline while reducing cerebral amyloid burden. 7 Emerging real‐world studies have supported the safety and effectiveness of lecanemab in routine clinical practice. 10 , 11 , 12 In addition, a large real‐world study from the United States demonstrated that lecanemab could be implemented safely in clinical practice, although amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs) and infusion‐related reactions (IRRs) remained important safety considerations. 13

Despite these encouraging findings, translating lecanemab treatment into routine clinical practice remains challenging. Participants in pivotal trials were highly selected, closely monitored, and predominantly recruited from Western populations, potentially limiting generalizability. 7 , 14 In real‐world settings, treatment‐related adverse events, particularly IRRs and ARIAs, represent major safety concerns and may vary according to patient characteristics, monitoring strategies, and population‐specific factors, including genetic background and cerebrovascular profiles. 7 , 15

Emerging East Asian studies suggest that the incidence and clinical impact of IRRs and ARIAs may differ from those observed in Western cohorts. 16 , 17 Furthermore, data on patients who fall outside strict trial eligibility criteria remain particularly limited. Beyond safety, early cognitive trajectories after anti‐amyloid therapy appear heterogeneous. 17 Cognitive response is increasingly recognized to be influenced by baseline disease severity, downstream tau pathology, and genetic factors such as apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotype. 18 , 19

In this context, we aimed to characterize the real‐world implementation of lecanemab in a tertiary memory clinic, focusing on safety, ARIA risk, and treatment characteristics in patients within and beyond phase 3 trial eligibility criteria.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This study was conducted as part of the K‐LEARN (Korean Lecanemab Real‐world Evidence and Response Network) cohort, a prospective real‐world registry of patients receiving lecanemab at a tertiary memory clinic at Korea University Guro Hospital, Seoul, Korea. We prospectively recruited 127 patients across the AD continuum, including patients with early AD (mild cognitive impairment [MCI] due to AD and mild AD dementia) and individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD), who initiated lecanemab treatment at this clinic between December 2024 and October 2025. Detailed diagnostic criteria are provided in Supplementary Methods in supporting information. Although SCD is not a standard indication in all appropriate use recommendation (AUR) 20 , 21 frameworks, selected SCD patients with confirmed amyloid pathology were included based on clinical judgment in a real‐world setting.

At baseline, all patients underwent neurologic examination, routine laboratory tests, and comprehensive neuropsychological assessment using the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery (SNSB). 22 , 23 Detailed test components are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Additional assessments included the Korean version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination, Second Edition (MMSE), 24 brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), and APOE genotyping, along with a comprehensive assessment of past medical history and current medications, to assess eligibility and clinical suitability for lecanemab treatment. In addition, blood samples were collected for the preparation of research‐ready samples and for the analysis of AD‐related blood biomarkers, including plasma phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), 25 as described in detail in the Supplementary Methods. Patients with the following conditions, which were considered to be clinically inappropriate for lecanemab administration, were generally excluded based on criteria informed by the CLARITY‐AD trial and published appropriate use recommendations: 7 , 20 , 21 (1) a history of stroke, transient ischemic attack, bleeding disorders, or seizures within the previous 12 months; (2) current use of anticoagulant or thrombolytic therapy (including warfarin, heparin, or acute thrombolytics) or the presence of clinically significant coagulation disorders; (3) intolerance or contraindication to MRI; (4) MRI evidence of clinically significant hemorrhagic lesions, including prior symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage or other hemorrhagic findings judged by the treating physicians to represent an unacceptable safety risk for lecanemab treatment; and (5) the presence of an unstable psychiatric disorder. However, in real‐world clinical practice, a small number of patients receiving non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) were included based on individualized risk–benefit assessments, reflecting pragmatic treatment decisions beyond strict trial‐based eligibility criteria. The institutional review board of the Korea University Guro Hospital approved the study protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients.

Lecanemab treatment protocol

Lecanemab was administered intravenously every 2 weeks at dedicated infusion centers. The drug was diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride and infused at a weight‐adjusted dose of 10 mg/kg, in accordance with the US Food and Drug Administration label 26 and the AUR. 20 Each infusion lasted ≈ 1 hour and was followed by a post‐infusion observation period of 6 hours after the first infusion, 2 hours after the second and third infusions, and 1 hour after the fourth and subsequent infusions to monitor for potential IRRs.

APOE genotyping

APOE genotyping was performed at baseline, and patients were classified as ε4 non‐carriers, ε4 heterozygotes, or ε4 homozygotes for subsequent analyses. Detailed genotyping procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

MRI acquisition

All patients underwent baseline MRI within 2 months prior to lecanemab initiation to assess treatment eligibility and safety. Follow‐up MRI scans were performed at 2, 3, and 6 months after treatment initiation and are planned at 12 and 18 months, with additional scans obtained if clinically indicated, in accordance with AUR. ARIAs with edema or effusions (ARIA‐E) and ARIAs with hemorrhage (ARIA‐H) were assessed on follow‐up MRI. Detailed MRI acquisition parameters, image review procedures, and ARIA grading criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Amyloid PET acquisition and quantification

Amyloid PET imaging using 18F‐florbetaben was performed prior to lecanemab initiation to confirm amyloid positivity. 20 Follow‐up 18F‐florbetaben PET is planned at 18 months after the initiation of lecanemab treatment to assess longitudinal changes in cerebral amyloid burden. PET images were visually assessed by two independent readers, with discrepancies resolved by consensus, in accordance with established appropriate use criteria. 27 , 28 Centiloid (CL) quantification was performed using standardized methods. Detailed PET acquisition parameters and analysis procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Measurement of cognitive trajectory

All patients underwent comprehensive cognitive assessments administered by experienced neuropsychologists at baseline (the day before lecanemab initiation) and at 3 and 6 months after treatment initiation, with additional assessments at 12 and 18 months planned. The assessments included the MMSE, the CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), and standardized comprehensive neuropsychological testing using the SNSB at baseline, with repeat comprehensive assessments planned at 12 months, while the MMSE and CDR‐SB were administered at 3 and 6 months and are planned at 18 months. Using a baseline CDR‐SB cutoff score of 4, patients were stratified into milder and more severe symptom groups for subsequent analyzes.

IRR assessment

IRRs were assessed through structured monitoring and follow‐up. After the first infusion, patients were observed on site for 6 hours, during which the occurrence of any IRR symptoms was actively monitored. Thereafter, IRRs were additionally ascertained by (1) patient‐initiated telephone contact if symptoms suggestive of IRRs occurred after discharge and (2) systematic symptom inquiry using a predefined checklist at routine outpatient visits at 2 and 3 months after lecanemab initiation. IRRs were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. 29 Definitions of CTCAE grades are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and frequency (%). To estimate the incidence of ARIAs, cumulative incidence curves were constructed, with time since lecanemab initiation as the time scale and the first occurrence of ARIA‐E or ARIA‐H as the event of interest. To identify factors associated with ARIA risk, univariable logistic regression analyses using the Firth penalized likelihood method were performed to reduce small‐sample bias and address potential separation issues. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each variable. To visualize longitudinal changes in cognitive performance across the entire cohort, spaghetti plots were constructed for MMSE scores. To evaluate differences in longitudinal changes in cognitive performance according to baseline cognitive severity, linear mixed‐effects models were used to examine changes in MMSE scores over time. Fixed effects included time, baseline cognitive severity subgroup defined by CDR‐SB (≤ 4 vs. > 4), the interaction between time and subgroup, age, sex, and years of education. Participant ID was specified as the random‐effects grouping variable, with random intercepts and random slopes for time. Similar linear mixed‐effects models were constructed to assess longitudinal changes in MMSE scores according to APOE ε4 status (non‐carriers, heterozygous carriers, and homozygous carriers), substituting APOE ε4 status for the baseline cognitive severity subgroup. Fixed effects included time, APOE ε4 status, the interaction between time and APOE ε4 status, age, sex, and years of education. Participant ID was specified as the random‐effects grouping variable, with both random intercepts and random slopes for time. When a significant interaction between time and APOE ε4 status was identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons of time‐related slopes were performed using Tukey adjustment to account for multiple comparisons.

All reported P values were two‐sided and the significance level was set at 0.05. Missing data were not imputed and were excluded from analyses as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 127 patients initiated lecanemab treatment and constituted the safety population. Of these, 102 patients completed at least five infusion sessions and had follow‐up MRI available for ARIA evaluation. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 72.0 ± 8.0 years, and 77 patients (60.6%) were female. The mean years of education was 11.7 ± 4.7 years. Ninety‐one out of 127 patients (71.7%) had a global CDR score of 0.5. With respect to cognitive impairment severity, 62 patients (48.8%) met criteria for MCI, 52 patients (40.9%) for mild dementia, and 13 individuals (10.2%) for SCD. The mean CDR‐SB score was 3.21 ± 2.03, and the mean MMSE score was 23.3 ± 3.7. APOE ε4 carriers accounted for 55.1% of the cohort (70 of 127 patients), including 46.5% heterozygotes (59 patients) and 8.7% homozygotes (11 patients). The mean CL value indicating amyloid burden was 71.45 ± 32.69. Baseline plasma p‐tau217 levels were 1.26 ± 0.60. Most patients (93.7%) were receiving acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and/or memantine. Vascular comorbidities included hypertension (34.6%), diabetes (18.9%), and hyperlipidemia (40.2%). Antiplatelet agents were used in 19.7% of patients, and NOACs were used in 3.1%. Compared to the CLARITY‐AD trial population, this cohort included a broader clinical spectrum, including SCD patients, individuals with more than four baseline microbleeds, those with severe white matter hyperintensities (WMHs; Fazekas score 3), and patients receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy.

IRRs

IRRs occurred in 27 of 127 patients (21.3%) during lecanemab treatment. Most IRRs developed during or shortly after the first infusion, with progressively fewer events observed during subsequent infusions. Specifically, IRRs were observed in 24 patients during the first infusion, 11 during the second, and 9 during the third infusion, whereas only 5 patients experienced IRRs after the third infusion. Among the 27 patients who developed IRRs, 15 (55.6%) experienced a single IRR episode, whereas 12 (44.4%) experienced recurrent IRRs across multiple infusion periods. Treatment discontinuation due to IRRs occurred in 3 patients. In patients who experienced IRRs, intravenous dexamethasone was administered as premedication for subsequent infusions. Among the 27 patients who developed IRRs, 24 received intravenous dexamethasone as premedication for subsequent infusions. Of these, 11 patients experienced additional IRRs during subsequent infusion periods, whereas 13 did not. Fever was the most common IRR‐related symptom, observed in 16 patients (12.6%), followed by general weakness in 11 patients (8.7%) and headache in 8 patients (6.3%). Rash was reported in 4 patients (3.1%), while myalgia and angioedema were each observed in 3 patients (2.4%). Dizziness, nausea, and sweating were each reported in 2 patients (1.6%). Other symptoms, including hypotension, itching sensation, chest discomfort, and facial flushing, were infrequently observed. Most IRRs were graded as grade 1 or grade 2 in severity.

ARIAs

ARIAs occurred in 9 of 102 patients (8.8%) who had received at least five lecanemab infusions and underwent at least one follow‐up MRI during lecanemab treatment. ARIA‐E was identified in four patients (3.9%), of whom three were asymptomatic and one was symptomatic; two cases were classified as mild and two as moderate. The symptomatic ARIA‐E case presented with transient visual change, which improved after corticosteroid treatment. ARIA‐H occurred in seven patients (6.9%), all of whom were asymptomatic and classified as mild. Five patients (4.9%) exhibited isolated ARIA‐H without concurrent ARIA‐E. The cumulative incidence of ARIAs is shown in Figure 1. Most events occurred within 3 months of treatment initiation, with similar early‐onset patterns observed for ARIA‐E (Figure 1A) and ARIA‐H (Figure 1B). Detailed clinical and radiological information on ARIA cases, including timing, imaging features, and clinical outcomes, is summarized in Table 2. Among ARIA cases, several patients had baseline characteristics beyond typical trial eligibility, including higher microbleed counts and non‐APOE ε4 genotypes, highlighting the heterogeneity of ARIA risk in real‐world clinical practice and the need for individualized risk stratification.

To further assess factors associated with ARIAs, univariable Firth logistic regression analyses were conducted (Figure 2 and Table S1 in supporting information). For ARIA‐H, APOE ε4 homozygosity was significantly associated with increased risk (OR, 5.56; 95% CI, 1.04–28.98; P = 0.045). In addition, a microbleed count > 4 (OR, 27.00; 95% CI, 3.08–275.61; P = 0.004) was significantly associated with increased ARIA‐H risk. Higher WMH burden (OR, 9.00; 95% CI, 2.02–40.94; P = 0.005) was also significantly associated with ARIA‐H. For ARIA‐E, a higher microbleed burden was significantly associated with increased risk, with increased risk observed in both the 3 to 4 and > 4 microbleed groups (OR, 19.57; 95% CI, 1.34–298.28; P = 0.032 for two groups). Higher WMH burden was also significantly associated with ARIA‐E (OR, 19.67; 95% CI, 2.92–217.94; P = 0.003).

Treatment discontinuation during lecanemab therapy

A total of 12 patients discontinued lecanemab treatment early. The primary reasons for discontinuation included ARIAs (n = 1), IRRs (n = 3), and patient preference or other clinical considerations (n = 8). Most discontinuations occurred during the early treatment phase, particularly within the first few months after treatment initiation.

Short‐term cognitive outcomes during lecanemab treatment

Among 90 patients with available cognitive follow‐up at 3 and 6 months, no significant decline in MMSE scores was observed during the study period (Figure 3). However, given the short duration of follow‐up and the open‐label nature of the study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment efficacy, as potential practice effects and placebo effects cannot be excluded. Exploratory subgroup analyses adjusted for age, sex, and years of education suggested that patients with a baseline CDR‐SB score ≤ 4 showed a numerically more favorable trend in MMSE change compared to those with a baseline CDR‐SB score > 4 (Figure S1A in supporting information; P = 0.049) after controlling for age, sex, and education years. Similarly, exploratory analyses according to APOE ε4 genotype suggested differences in MMSE trajectories among ε4 non‐carriers, ε4 heterozygotes, and ε4 homozygotes (Figure S1B; P = 0.027) after controlling for age, sex, and education years. In post hoc analyses, ε4 homozygotes showed a steeper decline than ε4 non‐carriers (Tukey‐adjusted P = 0.026), whereas the difference between homozygotes and heterozygotes did not reach statistical significance after multiple‐comparison adjustment (Tukey‐adjusted P = 0.104). Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective real‐world study, we characterized the clinical application of lecanemab in a tertiary memory clinic setting, focusing on safety, ARIA risk, and treatment patterns. The major findings were: (1) IRRs occurred in approximately one fifth of patients and were mostly mild to moderate, occurring predominantly during the first infusion; (2) ARIA events were infrequent, generally mild or asymptomatic, and occurred mainly within the first 3 months of treatment; and (3) treatment was implemented across a broader clinical spectrum than that typically included in phase 3 trials, including patients with SCD, higher microbleed burden, severe WMHs, and concomitant anticoagulant use.

From a safety perspective, lecanemab was generally well tolerated in this real‐world cohort. IRRs occurred in approximately one fifth of patients, most frequently during the first infusion, and were predominantly mild to moderate in severity. Most IRRs were manageable with supportive care, and lecanemab discontinuation due to IRRs was rare. The incidence and clinical characteristics of IRRs were broadly consistent with those reported in both clinical trials and real‐world studies. 7 , 16 , 17 , 30

With respect to ARIAs, the incidence and clinical characteristics of ARIAs observed in this real‐world cohort were generally favorable and comparable to, or lower than, those reported in clinical trials. 7 ARIA events were infrequent and predominantly mild or asymptomatic, and were mostly detected within 3 months after treatment initiation through scheduled MRI surveillance. ARIA‐E was rare and generally manageable with temporary treatment interruption and monitoring, while ARIA‐H events were mostly asymptomatic and mild, underscoring the importance of structured imaging follow‐up. Compared to the CLARITY‐AD trial, in which ARIA‐E occurred in 12.6% 7 of treated patients and was more frequent among APOE ε4 carriers, particularly ε4 homozygotes, 7 the lower incidence observed in our cohort may reflect differences in patient characteristics and real‐world clinical practice. In our study, APOE ε4 homozygotes accounted for a relatively small proportion of participants (8.7%) compared to the lecanemab group in the CLARITY‐AD trial (15.8%). 7 Similarly, although ARIA‐H was frequently reported in clinical trials, 7 , 31 real‐world studies from East Asia have described predominantly mild and clinically silent ARIA‐H events, 11 , 16 , 17 suggesting a lower clinical impact in routine practice.

Importantly, this cohort included patients who would have been excluded from phase 3 clinical trials, such as individuals with SCD, higher microbleed burden, advanced WMHs (Fazekas score 3), and those receiving anticoagulant therapy. In our clinical practice, treatment eligibility beyond phase 3 trial criteria was determined through individualized risk–benefit assessment incorporating clinical, imaging, and patient‐related factors, followed by shared decision making with patients and caregivers. Consistent with this real‐world heterogeneity, our analysis further demonstrated that imaging‐related factors, particularly microbleed burden and WMHs, were more strongly associated with ARIA risk than demographic or clinical variables. Notably, increased ARIA risk was observed not only in patients with > 4 microbleeds but also in those with 3 to 4 microbleeds, suggesting that risk may extend beyond traditionally defined high‐risk thresholds. These findings underscore the importance of detailed baseline MRI assessment for individualized ARIA risk stratification in clinical practice.

Regarding cognitive outcomes, no significant decline in MMSE scores was observed over the first 6 months of treatment. However, given the short follow‐up duration and the open‐label design, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment efficacy, as potential practice and placebo effects cannot be excluded. The inclusion of patients at very early clinical stages, including those with SCD, may also have influenced these observations. Importantly, the therapeutic goal of lecanemab is to slow cognitive and functional decline rather than to produce clinical improvement. Therefore, the absence of measurable cognitive improvement in this study should not be interpreted as evidence against a disease‐modifying effect. Because this study was not designed to evaluate efficacy and lacked an untreated control group, larger controlled studies with longer follow‐up are needed. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested possible differences in short‐term MMSE trajectories according to baseline cognitive severity and APOE ε4 genotype. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature.

The strengths of this study include its prospective real‐world design and the comprehensive evaluation of safety, ARIA risk, and treatment patterns in patients with early AD treated with lecanemab. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‐center study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other clinical settings with different patient characteristics, monitoring protocols, or practice patterns. Second, the sample size was modest and the duration of cognitive follow‐up was relatively short, limiting statistical power and precluding conclusions regarding treatment effects or long‐term outcomes. Third, subgroup analyses by baseline CDR‐SB and APOE ε4 genotype were exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Although age, sex, and years of education were included as covariates in the linear mixed‐effects models, the absence of an untreated control group limits interpretation of the observed cognitive trajectories. In addition, repeated MMSE assessments may have introduced practice effects, particularly among cognitively less impaired patients, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, enlarged perivascular spaces, a potential imaging marker associated with ARIA risk, 32 were not systematically assessed in this study. Finally, the inclusion of patients at very early clinical stages, including SCD, may have influenced short‐term cognitive findings. Despite these limitations, this study provides important real‐world evidence on the safety of lecanemab in early AD. Importantly, this study adds real‐world data from an Asian cohort, an underrepresented population in pivotal anti‐amyloid trials, highlighting the value of population‐specific evidence to inform clinical decision making.

In conclusion, this prospective real‐world study shows that lecanemab was generally well tolerated in patients with early AD. These findings highlight the importance of structured safety monitoring and may help inform individualized treatment strategies in real‐world clinical practice.
