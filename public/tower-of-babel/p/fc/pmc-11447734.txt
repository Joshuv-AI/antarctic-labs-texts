Enhancing Ultradeep Hydrodesulfurization of Coker Diesel through Adsorptive Predenitrogenation
Authors: Narjes Ghaloum, Muhieddine A Safa, Hamza Albazzaz, Mohammed S Alshemali, Shibu Jose, Xiaoliang Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe petroleum refining industry places significant challenge in the production of ultralow-sulfur diesel (ULSD) from various middle distillates with high nitrogen concentration in an energy-efficient and cost-effective way to meet strict environmental regulations as coexisting nitrogen compounds significantly inhibit the ultradeep hydrodesulfurization (HDS). Among all of the approaches reported in the literature for this challenge, a combination of adsorptive denitrogenation (ADN) and HDS has attracted great attention. This study focuses on ultradeep HDS of coker diesel (CD) through a synergistic approach combining ADN over a carbon-based adsorbent and the current HDS process. The study found that the predenitrogenation significantly enhanced the HDS reactivity of CD and greatly reduced the start of run temperature for producing ULSD by even 20 °C, depending on the denitrogenation depth. It results in dominantly decreasing energy and H2 consumption in the HDS process, and increasing the lifetime of the catalyst. Furthermore, the predenitrogenation prior to HDS significantly improved the quality of the hydrodesulfurized product by decreasing aromatic content and density, increasing the cetane index, and improving the color and stability of the product as the HDS process for producing ULSD can be conducted at a much lower temperature. The study demonstrated the combination of ADN and HDS for producing ULSD from CD on a pilot unit scale, and the advantages and disadvantages of this combination were discussed in comparison with the conventional HDS process. In addition, it was found that there is a good linear relationship between the logarithm of the product sulfur concentration and the HDS reaction temperature, which can be used conveniently to predict the start-of-run temperature to achieve different sulfur levels.

Introduction

Catalytic hydrotreating is an important petroleum refining process that handles effectively the removal of various impurities such as sulfur, nitrogen, and metals from various hydrocarbon streams.1 Hydrodesulfurization (HDS) has been an integral part of petroleum refineries worldwide for many years, ensuring the consistent production of clean fuels.2−6 The growing environmental awareness has resulted in shifting the focus of researchers and scientists to ultradeep HDS technologies of middle distillates, aimed at producing ultralow-sulfur diesel (ULSD, S < 10 μg/g). Sulfur is a significant contributor to air pollution because it is converted into sulfur oxides during combustion in internal combustion engines, leading to the unwanted consequences of acid rain formation, and also poisoning of the catalytic converter for exhaust emission treatment.6,7 Consequently, environmental regulations in many countries mandate an ultralow sulfur level of 10 μg/g in diesel fuel to reduce harmful emissions from diesel engines and enhance air quality. Hence, ultradeep HDS of middle distillates plays a crucial role in the production of ULSD.

Nowadays, it is a great challenge to produce ULSD in an energy-efficient and cost-effective manner, while simultaneously maximizing the utilization of various hydrocarbon feedstocks.4 The significant surge in the global demand for diesel fuel in recent years has led to the processing of not only straight-run gas oil (SRGO) but also other middle distillates, such as light cycle oil (LCO) from fluid catalytic cracking, coker diesel (CD) from coking of vacuum residue, and others, to sustain the diesel fuel pool.8,9 However, LCO and CD are characterized by significantly higher levels of nitrogen, and aromatic compounds compared to SRGO, as well-known.10 The nitrogen concentrations in CD and LCO can be higher than 600 μg/g.10−13 These nitrogen-containing compounds have been identified as strong inhibitors toward the deep HDS due to their competitive adsorption on the active sites of the HDS catalyst.14−16

Conventional hydrotreating technologies with the current advanced catalysts are effective for removing the major sulfur compounds such as thiophenes, benzothiophenes, and a part of dibenzothiophenes in middle distillates.1,4−6 However, these technologies are less effective for removing the refractory sulfur compounds (alkyl dibenzothiophenes with alkyl substitutes at the 4- and/or 6-positions), especially when there are a large amount of the coexisting nitrogen compounds. It is because the HDS of the refractory sulfur compounds takes place dominantly through the hydrogenation route (HY), instead of the direct desulfurization route (DDS),17,18 and the nitrogen compounds are the major inhibitors toward the hydrogenation route. Consequently, removal of the nitrogen compounds from middle distillates is necessary for ultradeep HDS to produce ULSD. The conventional method in a petroleum refinery for removing nitrogen compounds from middle distillates is through catalytic hydrodenitrogenation (HDN).19,20 However, compared to sulfur analogues, the nitrogen compounds in middle distillates exhibit lower reactivity and necessitate more stringent operating conditions for removal, as the aromatic ring prehydrogenation is mandatory prior to hydrogenolysis of the heterocyclic C–N bond in them.20,21 Consequently, hydrodenitrogenation for the removal of the nitrogen compounds is costly and energy-intensive, particularly challenging for CD and LCO due to their high concentrations of nitrogen compounds and aromatic hydrocarbons, which result in substantial hydrogen gas consumption and stringent operating conditions. Therefore, many nonhydrotreating approaches have been reported for removing nitrogen compounds from the various middle distillates, including extractive denitrogenation, adsorptive denitrogenation (ADN), and oxidative denitrogenation (ODN).22 ADN has emerged as an attractive method for removing nitrogen compounds from fossil fuels.20,23−27 This process is a favorable pathway for nitrogen reduction, as it can operate effectively under very mild conditions without consuming hydrogen gas. In addition, ADN is easier than adsorptive desulfurization (ADS), as the nitrogen compounds have higher adsorption affinity than the sulfur compounds due to their higher polarity, and the weight percentage of the nitrogen compounds in the petroleum middle distillates is usually 1–2 orders of magnitude less than the sulfur compounds, resulting in much less loss of hydrocarbons within the process. These features enable the ADN of middle distillates to be conducted more economically, environmentally friendly, and energy-efficiently.28 Consequently, the predenitrogenation by selective adsorption has attracted a lot of attention in improving the reactivity of feedstock for the subsequent HDS processes and enhancing the performance of the existing hydrotreating units, which has been reviewed by Laredo et al.29 and Prado et al.,19 respectively.

Many studies in the effect of predenitrogenation on the HDS of fuels used a mixture of model compounds as feed. Some studies using real feeds of SRGO and LCO have been reviewed in the literature.20,29 However, these studies did not focus on the production of ULSD. Zeuthen et al. investigated the predenitrogenation by adsorption over silica gel and its impact on the HDS of an SRGO-LCO mixture.15 They found that a decrease of N concentration in the mixture from 300 to 5 ppm resulted in decreasing sulfur concentration in the hydrotreated product from 780 to 90 ppm under the same HDS conditions in a down-flow reactor on a commercial CoMo catalyst. Lee et al. reported a SK HDS pretreatment technology for ULSD production.30 They found that predenitrogenation of a blend of LCO, CGO, and 40% SRGO from 720 to 220 ppmw over an undisclosed adsorbent was able to decrease sulfur concentration in the HDS product from 232 to 68 ppmw over a CoMo catalyst at LHSV of 2 h–1, and H2 partial pressure of 40 bar, and decrease sulfur concentration in the product from 60 to 18 ppmw over a mixture of CoMo and NiMo catalysts at LHSV of 1 h–1, H2 partial pressure of 50 bar. Their study indicates that predenitrogenation by selective adsorption can significantly reduce the start of run (SOR) temperature. Very recently, Meneses-Ruiz et al. studied the predenitrogenation of SRGO and a mixture of SRGO-LCO/CGO over MIL-101 (Cr) and its impact on ULSD production by hydrotreating in a fixed-bed up-flow reactor over a commercial NiMo/Al2O3 catalyst.26 They found that the predenitrogenation by the adsorption could reduce the SOR temperature by about 15–20 °C. Consequently, the combination of the predenitrogenation process with the current HDS process is potentially a highly promising technology for producing ULSD.

Activated carbon is a favorable adsorbent for ADN due to its unique properties, including high specific surface area, superior adsorption capacity, easy modification of its surface chemistry, and availability from a wide range of sources.31−33 These types of porous materials have shown higher adsorptive capacity and selectivity for removing the nitrogen compounds, making them highly suitable for applications in effective ADN.34 Mochida’s group has reported the adsorptive removal of nitrogen species from SRGO over activated carbons and its impact on deep HDS in a batch reactor.35 They found that some activated carbon (Maxsorb-II with a surface area of 2972 m2/g) was able to reduce the nitrogen concentration in SRGO to a very low level, depending on the treated amount of oil (mL of oil per gram of activated carbon), and simultaneously remove a part of the refractory sulfur compounds. When the nitrogen concentration in the feedstock was reduced from 250 ppm in the original SRGO to 60 and 20 ppm in the treated SRGO, the total sulfur concentration in the HDS products decreased from 193 to 11 and 8 ppm, respectively. Almarri et al. studied the adsorptive pretreatment of LCO by selective adsorption over activated carbons and its effect on subsequent HDS in a batch reactor over a commercial catalyst CoMo/Al2O3(Cr447).36 They found that there was a clear relationship between the HDS conversion and nitrogen concentration in the pretreated feeds. When the nitrogen concentration was reduced from 598 ppmw in the original SRGO to 7 ppmw in the treated feed, the corresponding HDS conversion increased from 52 to 84%, indicating that the predenitrogenation by adsorption over activated carbon can significantly improve the HDS reactivity of LCO.36 In spite of those, studies in the predenitrogenation of real middle distillates with high nitrogen concentration, such as LCO and CD, by selective adsorption over the carbon-based adsorbents are very scarce, and studies on the effect of pre-ADN on HDS in the batch reactor have less guiding significance toward the real HDS processing for producing ULSD. Enhancing ultradeep HDS of real CD in a fixed-bad pilot unit through selective adsorption over carbon-based adsorbent for producing ULSD has not been reported in the literature, to the best of our knowledge.

The overall aim of this study is to enhance ultradeep HDS of CD through selective adsorption to remove nitrogen compounds for producing ULSD. A graphical mechanism for this study is shown in Figure 1. In one of our previous papers, the selection of the carbon-based ADN adsorbents, their adsorption performance and regenerability, and the preparation method of denitrogenated CD have been reported.13 The objective of this paper is to report the subsequent study on impact of the predenitrogenation on the ultradeep HDS reactivity of CD in detail. The approaches in this study include (1) predenitrogenating the real CDs with different nitrogen concentrations by selective adsorption over the selected carbon-based adsorbent13 and (2) evaluating and comparing the HDS reactivity of the original and predenitrogenated CDs over a commercial NiMo/Al2O3 catalyst in a fixed-bed pilot unit at the conditions applied currently in a Kuwait refinery. As a result of this study, an effective method for enhancing ultradeep HDS of CD through the adsorptive predenitrogenation to produce ULSD was developed and demonstrated. Furthermore, the study provided a better understanding of the effect of predenitrogenation of CD on the subsequent HDS processes. The advantages and some potential challenges in the combination of predenitrogenation and hydrotreating for producing ULSD were also discussed.

Physical and Chemical Properties of CD

A commercially available carbon-based adsorbent (AC–CC) was used in this study for preparation of a denitrogenated coker diesel (ADN-CD) through selective adsorption. AC–CC was made from coal and was purchased from Skyrun International China. It was found to be an excellent carbon-based adsorbent for ADN of CD on the basis of the previous work.13 AC–CC has a surface area of 1091 m2/g, with pore volume and pore diameter of 1.307 cm3/g and 2.20 nm, respectively. The characterization and analysis of AC–CC and its ADN performance have been reported in detail before.13

Preparation of Denitrogenated CD

The preparation of denitrogenated CD was carried out using a packed-bed adsorption system. AC–CC was loaded into the adsorbent bed. The CD feed was passed through the AC–CC adsorbent bed at ambient temperature and under vacuum to accelerate the adsorption rate. Two denitrogenated CDs (ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2) with different nitrogen concentrations were collected at different oil-to-adsorbent weight ratios for the hydrotreating test.

Hydrotreating of CD and Predenitrogenated CD

HDS of the original and predenitrogenated CDs was conducted in a fixed-bed pilot unit over a commercial NiMo/Al2O3 catalyst. About 15 mL of the catalyst was loaded into the reactor with an internal diameter of 16 mm and a thermocouple tube (OD: 6 mm) in the center. The catalyst was diluted with an inert material (medium carborundum) in a ratio of 1:2 v/v. The feedstock and fresh H2 were mixed, then sent to the top of the reactor, and flowed down through the catalyst bed. The catalyst was presulfurized using a feed with 1.3 wt % of dimethyl disulfide in an SRGO. The total sulfur concentration in the presulfiding feed was 2.5 wt %. The presulfiding temperature was raised from room temperature to 150 °C at a ramp rate of 20 °C/h at 40 bar and 1.5 h–1 LHSV. After holding at 150 °C for 5 h, the temperature was continuously raised from 150 to 315 °C at a ramp rate of 25 °C/h and held at it for 5 h. After the presulfurization was completed, the reactor temperature was reduced to 200 °C, and then the feed was shifted to the test feed. The temperature was raised to 320 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/h and held at 320 °C for 80 h for catalyst stabilization at a total pressure of 55 bar, LHSV of 0.8 h–1, and H2/oil ratio of 320 v/v. Collection of the hydrotreated product samples was started after catalyst stabilization. After sampling at 320 °C, the temperature of the catalyst bed was raised by 5 °C and kept at it for 5 days and sampling, and so on, until the temperature increased to 370 °C. The hydrotreated products were collected from the bottom of the liquid–gas separator once the hydrotreating reaction at the desired conditions became stable. Analysis of Feedstocks and Products

The physical and chemical properties of CD, ADN-CD1, and ADN-CD2 as well as their hydrotreated products were determined according to suitable international standard methods published by the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM), as also shown in Table 1. Total sulfur and nitrogen in the feedstock and products were determined using a Mitsubishi Chemical TS-100 V trace sulfur and nitrogen analyzer, according to the standardized methods. The instrument was calibrated at a range of 1–1000 μg/mL. The relative error for analyses of sulfur and nitrogen contents in the oils is less than 3%.

An Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph equipped with a Nitrogen Chemiluminescence Detector (GC-NCD) was used to analyze the various nitrogen compounds in the feeds and the products. The column temperature for GC-NCD was gradually increased from 150 to 300 °C for 2 °C/min. The injector temperature was set at 280 °C, and the injection volume was 1 μL. An HP-5 capillary column (30 m length, 0.32 mm internal diameter, and 0.25 μm film thickness) was used.

Identification and quantification of sulfur compounds in CD, ADN-CD1, and ADN-CD2 were determined using a gas chromatograph configured with a pulsed flame photometric detector (GC-PFPD) and an CP-Sil 5CP capillary column (length: 30 m, ID: 0.32 mm, and 0.25 μm film thickness). The GC-PFPD analysis conditions were set at a split ratio of 100:1, with injector and detector temperatures of 325 and 300 °C, respectively. A temperature program for GC-PFPD analysis was used with the initial column temperature at 35 °C for 3 min after that increased at a rate of 10 °C/min from 35 to 300 °C, and then kept at 300 °C for 5 min. The identification of the major sulfur compounds was performed by using some standard samples, such as DBT, 4-methyldibenzothiophene (4-MDBT), and 4,6-dimethyl-dibenzothiophene (4,6-DMDBT) and comparing the relative retention times of the peaks with those from the literature.16 The sulfur compounds were further separated into four groups on the basis of their structure and GC-PFPD chromatograms according to our previous study.16 Group 1 includes benzothiophene and its alkyl derivatives. Group 2 includes dibenzothiophene and its alkyl derivatives without any alkyl substitute at the 4- or 6-positions. Group 3 includes alkyl dibenzothiophenes with only one alkyl substitute at the 4- or 6-positions. Group 4 includes the alkyl dibenzothiophenes with alkyl substitutes at both the 4- and 6-positions and the alkyl dibenzothiophenes with alkyl carbon numbers of 3 and more than 3. The quantification of the sulfur species was determined by using the normalization method. The relative error for the conducted GC-PFPD analyses of all sulfur-containing hydrocarbons is less than 4%.

Results and Discussion

Predenitrogenation and Its Effect on Composition and Properties of CD

Predenitrogenation of the CD by adsorption over AC–CC was conducted in a fixed-bed device to prepare the adsorptive denitrogenated coker diesel (ADN-CD). The ADN of CD over AC–CC has been reported in detail in one of our previous papers,13 which was able to remove the nitrogen concentration in CD from 794 μg/g to less than 5 μg/g, depending on the treated amount (g) of CD per gram of AC–CC. Two denitrogenated CDs (ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2) with nitrogen concentrations of 19.0 and 9.0 μg/g, respectively, were prepared for this study. The GC-NCD chromatograms of CD, ADN-CD1, and ADN-CD2 are shown in Figure 2, indicating that the major nitrogen compounds are alkyl anilines, alkyl indoles, carbazole, and its alkyl derivatives, and about 97.6 and 98.9% of nitrogen were removed from CD for ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2, respectively, as shown in Table 3.

Composition and properties of ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2 in comparison with those of the original CD are listed in Table 3. It shows clearly that the ADN changed not only the total nitrogen concentration but also the density, total sulfur concentration, as well as distribution of the sulfur compounds. After the ADN, the density of CD decreased from the original 0.8702 g/cm3 to 0.8568 and 0.8556 g/cm3, and the total sulfur concentration (ST) decreased from the original 8537 to 6695 and 5548 μg/g, respectively, for ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2. The results indicate that the ADN removed not only 97.6 and 98.9% of the total nitrogen concentration but also 21.6 and 35.0% of the total sulfur concentration for ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2, respectively. The further quantitative analysis based on the GC-PFPD chromatograms, as shown in Figure 3, indicates that the ADN also changed the distribution of the sulfur compounds in the ADN-CDs. For example, it removed 48.8 and 46.9% of sulfur compounds in groups 3 and 4, respectively, for ADN-CD1, and 56.4 and 53.9% of sulfur compounds in groups 3 and 4, respectively, for ADN-CD2, but almost did not change the concentration of group 1. It means that the ADN removed selectively a part of the refractory sulfur compounds (groups 3 and 4). The decrease in the oil density can be attributed to the removal of the heavier nitrogen/sulfur-containing molecules and a part of poly aromatic hydrocarbons from CD during the ADN process.

Effect of Adsorptive Predenitrogenation on HDS Reactivity of CD

In order to compare the reactivities for ultradeep HDS, hydrotreating of CD, ADN-CD1, and ADN-CD2 was conducted in the fixed-bed reactor system over the commercial catalyst at the typical commercial HDS conditions (0.80 h–1 of LHSV, 320 of H2/oil volume ratio, and 55 bar of H2 partial pressure). The SOR temperature to achieve the target sulfur level of 10 μg/mL was used as an HDS reactivity index for different feeds. The weighted average bed temperature (WABT) and the sulfur and nitrogen concentrations as a function of the stream of run for hydrotreating of CD are shown in Figure 4, indicating that both sulfur and nitrogen concentrations decreased with increasing WABT. The change of sulfur concentrations as a function of WABT for the three feeds is shown in Figure 5. These three feeds showed quite different HDS reactivities. The hydrotreating at 340 °C was only able to remove the sulfur in CD to around 150 μg/mL, while the hydrotreating at the same conditions was able to remove the sulfur in ADN-CD1 to around 20 μg/mL and the sulfur in ADN-CD2 to even 10 μg/mL. If the target sulfur level in the product is 10 μg/mL, the required HDS temperature is 362, 350, and 342 °C for CD, ADN-CD1, and ADN-CD2, respectively. It implies that the HDS reactivity increases significantly after the adsorptive predenitrogenation.

It should be pointed out that when the reaction temperature is higher than 360 °C, a continuous increase in the hydrotreating temperature results in increasing (instead of decreasing) the sulfur concentration in the product, as shown clearly in Figure 5. At this temperature range, the remaining sulfur compounds are the alkyl dibenzothiophenes with alkyl groups at the 4 and/or 6 positions. As well-known, removal of these refractory sulfur compounds is dominantly through the hydrogenation route.39 In this case, the thermodynamics of hydrogenation becomes a major factor in controlling the HDS reaction. Consequently, the reaction temperature higher than 360 °C is thermodynamically unfavorable for hydrogenation of the refractory sulfur compounds, resulting in increasing the sulfur concentration in the product.

Simultaneous Hydrodenitrogenation of CD

During the HDS of CD, hydrodenitrogenation (HDN) also took place simultaneously. The concentration and conversion of total nitrogen as a function of reaction temperature in comparison with those of total sulfur are shown in Figure 7. The total nitrogen concentration in the product decreased from 106 to 1.5 μg/mL with increasing reaction temperature from 320 to 355 °C. However, when continuously increasing the reaction temperature, the total nitrogen concentration in the product increased, similar to that for the total sulfur. It can also be attributed to the thermodynamic effect as the HDN requires the hydrogenation of the nitrogen-containing aromatic ring. In comparison of sulfur and nitrogen conversions, it is found that the conversion of nitrogen was lower than that of sulfur at a temperature below 350 °C, indicating that most sulfur compounds, such as those in Group 1 and Group 2, are easier to remove than the nitrogen compounds. When the reaction temperature is higher than 350 °C, the conversion of nitrogen increases significantly to reach a conversion similar to that of sulfur, implying that the HDN reaction is more sensitive to the change in reaction temperature.

Effect of Predenitrogenation on Composition and Properties of the Hydrotreated Products

Composition and properties of the hydrotreated products from CD and ADN-CD1, respectively, under the reaction conditions of 0.8 LHSV, 55 bar of hydrogen partial pressure, and 320 v/v H2/oil ratio, and at the reaction temperature of 362 and 350 °C, respectively, to achieve the same target sulfur level (S = 10 μg/mL) are listed in Table 4. Although the total sulfur concentration is almost the same, the hydrotreated product from ADN-CD1 gave lower density, lower viscosity, lower nitrogen concentration, higher aniline point, and higher cetane index than those from CD. This is because the hydrotreating of ADN-CD1 was conducted at a relatively lower temperature to achieve the same target sulfur level. The lower density, higher aniline point, and higher cetane index indicate that the hydrotreated product from ADN-CD1 contains fewer aromatic hydrocarbons. The lower concentration of aromatic hydrocarbons and lower nitrogen concentration of the hydrotreated product from ADN-CD1 also benefit the oil stability and color. All of these results imply that the deep hydrotreating of ADN-CD can produce a better quality of ULSD than that of CD.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Adsorptive Predenitrogenation

As shown above, the adsorptive predenitrogenation of CD gave some significant benefits for the ultradeep HDS for producing ULSD: (1) the adsorptive predenitrogenation decreases the SOR temperature from 362 °C for original CD to 350 °C by 12 °C for ADN-CD1, and to 342 °C by 20 °C for ADN-CD2 for producing ULSD at LHSV of 0.8 h–1. The adsorptive predenitrogenation lowers dominantly the hydrotreating severity, resulting in a significant reduction of the energy and hydrogen consumption in the hydrotreating process. (2) The adsorptive predenitrogenation significantly increases the lifetime of the catalyst. As well-known, a deactivation rate is around 1 °C per month for a typical HDS process,40 or around 0.9 °C for typical HDS of light gasoil for producing ULSD.30 Thus, a reduction of the SOR temperature by 12 or 20 °C implies an increase of the catalyst lifetime of about 12 or 20 months, respectively, when using ADN-CD1 and ADN-CD2. If considering that most nitrogen compounds and a part of poly aromatic hydrocarbons, which are poisonous materials, have been removed from the feed in the adsorptive predenitrogenation, the deactivation rate should be even lower. (3) Since the target sulfur level can be achieved at the lower hydrotreating temperature, the ULSD from the hydrotreating of ADN-CD can give better quality with lower aromatic content, higher aniline point, higher cetane index, lighter product color, and better stability.

It should be pointed out that many studies on the effect of adsorptive predenitrogenation on HDS of middle distillates focused only on how the adsorptive predenitrogenation improves the HDS reactivity of feedstock, few of the studies considered the effect of the adsorptive predenitrogenation on the oil loss in the process. In one of our previous studies,13 a relationship between the nitrogen concentration of the predenitrogenated oil and the oil yield for the ADN of CD over the same adsorbent has been reported. The oil yield in the ADN process and the SOR temperature to achieve 10 μg/mL sulfur as a function of nitrogen concentration in the ADN-CD are shown in Figure 8. It can be found that the SOR temperature decreases with decreasing nitrogen concentration in feed. However, the oil yield also decreases simultaneously. For example, when the nitrogen concentration in feed decreases from 691 to 16.3 and 7.7 μg/mL, the SOR temperature decreases from 362 to 350 and 342 °C, respectively, while the oil yield decreases to 82.5 and 76.0%, respectively. It is because the adsorptive predenitrogenation over the carbon-based adsorbent removes not only the nitrogen compounds but also a part of the refractory sulfur compounds and poly aromatic hydrocarbons, especially at the higher nitrogen removal percentage. Consequently, there is an optimal nitrogen removal percentage for maximizing the profit of refinery by considering both the reaction temperature gain and the oil yield. The removed nitrogen fraction in the predenitrogenation process may be recovered and used as a blend to mix with atmospheric residue to improve the reactivity of atmospheric residue in the atmospheric residue hydrodesulfurization (ARDS) process. Due to containing a lot of polar nitrogen compounds and poly aromatic hydrocarbons, the adsorbate fraction can be an excellent solvent to disperse the asphaltenes and provide the hydrogen carrier in the ARDS process.41−43 More approaches in recovery and utilization of the nitrogen fraction should be conducted for the adoption of the predenitrogenation process in the refinery.

Conclusions

In this study, the combination of the predenitrogenation of CD by selective adsorption over a commercial carbon-based adsorbent and the hydrotreating of the predenitrogenated CD at the current commercial conditions for producing ULSD was conducted and evaluated. The effect of the predenitrogenation on the composition and properties of the feeds, their HDS reactivity, and HDS product properties was studied in detail. The adsorptive predenitrogenation over the carbon-based adsorbent is able to remove most of the coexisting nitrogen compounds in CD. The predenitrogenation also removes a part of the refractory sulfur compounds, changes the concentration and distribution of sulfur compounds, and reduces the density of CD, depending on the nitrogen removal percentage. The predenitrogenation significantly enhances the HDS reactivity of CD. When the nitrogen concentration in CD is reduced from 794 μg/g in the original CD to 19 and 9 μg/g in the pretreated CD, the SOR temperature decreases from 362 to 350 and 342 °C, respectively, resulting in significantly reducing energy and H2 consumptions in the subsequent hydrotreating process and increasing the lifetime of the catalyst. The predenitrogenation also improves the quality of the hydrotreated product by reducing the aromatic content and density, increasing the aniline point and cetane index, and improving product color and stability since the same target sulfur level can be achieved at a lower hydrotreating temperature. In addition, the present study found that there is a good linear relationship between the logarithm of the product sulfur concentration and the HDS reaction temperature, which can be used conveniently to predict the SOR temperature for achieving different sulfur levels.
