Novel LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion in PDGFRA-rearranged myeloproliferative neoplasm with eosinophilia: a case report
Authors: Jing Zhou, Hanbing Yao, Jiarong Ren, Ke Sun, Dongxue Chen, Rui Zhang, Gangfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement are a group of haematologic tumors, in which the clonal proliferation of precursor eosinophils leads to persistent eosinophilic hyperplasia, and varying degrees of involvement of the liver, spleen and lymph nodes. While PDGFRA rearrangements are frequently observed in mpn-eo, the co-occurrence of t(15;16)(q14;p13) translocations leading to LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion genes has not been previously documented. Here we present a 30-year-old male patient with leukocytosis and generalized lymphadenopathy. Bone marrow cytology revealed eosinophilia with abnormal and atypical granulocytes showing dysplastic cytoplasmic granules. Cytogenetic analysis identified t(15;16)(q14;p13), while RNA sequencing confirmed the novel LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene and co-detected FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion gene. The patient achieved stable disease imatinib (100 mg/day) treatment. This first report of dual PDGFRA/NUTM1 fusions expands the molecular spectrum of MPN-Eo and highlights the therapeutic implications of novel fusion detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04298-3.

Introduction

Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement, as defined by the 5th WHO classification (2022), are clonal hematopoietic disorders marked by excessive proliferation of differentiated bone marrow lineages [1]. Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasmharboring recurrent rearrangements involves not only PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, and PCM1::JAK2 fusions, but also encompasses additional tyrosine kinase gene fusions including BCR::JAK2, ETV6::JAK2, ETV6::ABL1, FLT3 rearrangements (such as ETV6::FLT3, TRIP11::FLT3), and other emerging fusion partners, collectively classified as myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions (MLN-TK) in the updated WHO and International Consensus Classification [2]. Clinically, patients often present with multilineage hematologic abnormalities, hepatosplenomegaly, and thrombohemorrhagic events. A unique aspect of this case is its unprecedented genetic profile: coexisting FIP1L1::PDGFRA and novel LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion genes, accompanied by marked eosinophilic dysplasia—a combination unreported in existing literature.

The del(4) (q12) deletion drives FIP1L1::PDGFRA (F/P) fusion, activating constitutive tyrosine kinase signaling [3–5]. Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, effectively targets PDGFRA kinase activity, achieving high remission rates in F/P-positive cases [3, 5–10]. Notably, early intervention with imatinib during the fusion-driven disease phase significantly reduces mortality [11]. Diagnostic criteria per 2016 WHO guidelines require molecular confirmation (e.g., PDGFRA rearrangement) alongside peripheral/bone marrow eosinophilia. This case breaks new ground by identifying LUC7L::NUTM1—a previously undocumented fusion gene—in an myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement, expanding our understanding of disease genetics. While imatinib remains the cornerstone therapy for myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement [11], the functional implications of LUC7L::NUTM1 warrant further investigation to optimize therapeutic strategies for such complex genetic profiles.

Case report

Patient information

The reporting of this study conforms to CARE guidelines [12]. Patient was admitted to the hospital due to splenomegaly lasting for 2 years and leukocytosis lasting for 1 week, accompanied by progressive systemic symptoms including irregular fever, night sweats, and obvious sternal tenderness. He had previously received symptomatic and supportive treatment with drugs such as hydroxyurea, allopurinol, sodium bicarbonate, and furosemide (see Table 1), but the therapeutic effect was poor. No obvious cause was identified through physical examination. After a detailed inquiry, the patient was transferred to the Department of Hematology in a hematological disease hospital for further treatment. Physical examination revealed multiple lymphadenopathies (cervical: 2.4 × 0.9 cm; axillary: 2.2 × 0.7 cm; inguinal: 1.3 × 0.6 cm), diffuse bone pain, and pitting edema. The lower edge of the spleen was flat, and the umbilical region was swollen to grade III, with a hard texture and no tenderness. We have de identified all patient information. This study has obtained the patient’s signed treatment consent form and publication consent form.

Diagnostic hallmarks

The patient presented with significant peripheral blood abnormalities: a white blood cell count of 137.35 × 10⁹/L (normal range 3.5–9.5), an eosinophil percentage of 9.49% (normal range 0.5–5%), a hemoglobin level of 110 g/L (normal range 115–150), and a platelet count of 80 × 10⁹/L (normal range 125–350). Bone marrow aspiration indicated bone marrow hyperplasia, accompanied by atypical granulocytes and increased pathological eosinophils (24.40%, normal range 0.7–7.82%; Fig. 1A). Bone marrow biopsy showed granulocytic hyperplasia with eosinophilic infiltration (Fig. 1B), and HE staining revealed immature nuclei and excessive eosinophilic granules in eosinophils (Fig. 1C). Flow cytometry detected abnormal granulocyte antigens (abnormal CD13/CD11B expression, 94.6% granulocytes; Fig. S2), and immunohistochemistry showed weak positivity for CD34/CD117 and Ki-67 (15%+) (Fig. S1). Molecular complexity

Chromosome karyotype analysis revealed t (15;16)(q14; p13) (Fig. 1D), and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) confirmed the PDGFRA break (Fig. 1E). RNA sequencing (PRJNA1032454) and quantitative PCR detected the FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion and the first discovery of the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion (with a 43.36% positive rate by digital PCR; Fig. S3). JAK2/MPL/CALR and BCR/ABL were negative. The comprehensive diagnosis is myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement.

Therapeutic response and long-term follow-up

After obtaining informed consent, imatinib (100 mg/day) was initiated but discontinued on day 4 due to severe cytopenia (platelets 17 × 10⁹/L; leukocytes 2.06 × 10⁹/L), requiring transfusions and supportive care (Table 2). Reintroduction at 100 mg/day after hematopoietic recovery led to transient cytopenia recurrence, necessitating a second brief interruption. Stabilization was achieved through stepwise dose escalation combined with electrolyte correction and hepatoprotective agents.

Discussion

Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with PDGFRA rearrangement constitute a category of disorders characterized by eosinophilia [2].The incidence of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement is low. The key features of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement include the formation of fusion genes and the abnormal expression of tyrosine kinases [13], which are key to diagnosis; however, the most common feature is rearranged PDGFRA [14–17]. In the present case, an absolute eosinophil value of 9 × 109/l was reported, in addition to the detection of the FIP1L1::PDGFRA gene fusion. Thus, the present case was classified as myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement. Notably, this disease simultaneously induces specific differentiation and clonal proliferation of hematopoietic precursor cells into eosinophil/mast cell lines [18].

In this study, the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion transcript, comprising exons 1–9 of LUC7L and exons 5–8 of NUTM1. NUTM1, located at chromosome 15q14, is a protein-coding gene primarily rearranged in NUT midline carcinoma and other tumors such as B lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, central nervous system embryonic tumors, and eosinophilic myeloid tumors [19–22]. Mammals have three homologs of Luc7p: LUC7L, LUC7L2, and LUC7L3. LUC7L, located at chromosome 16p13.3, is thought to encode a mammalian heterochromatin gene, but its precise function remains unclear. To explore their evolutionary relationship, we conducted an analysis of the LUC7 family using MEGA 11 software [23, 24], showing that LUC7L, LUC7L2, and LUC7L3 genes are closely related in the evolutionary tree. A domain analysis using the Evolview database [25–27] further revealed that the functional domains of these genes range from 5 to 292 nucleotides and are highly conserved, suggesting similar functional roles in disease progression (Fig. 2C). The cDNA transformation protein sequence of the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene was analyzed using the EMBOSS Transeq database [28], and the conserved sequence analysis of the fusion gene and the three LUC7 family genes was carried out using the WebLogo3 database [29, 30] (Fig. 3D). Results showed that the fusion gene shares high sequence conservation with LUC7L, LUC7L2, and LUC7L3. While studies on the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene are rare, existing evidence suggests that it exhibits biological functions similar to Luc7p homologs. LUC7L2, for instance, co-localizes with U1 small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNP) in the nucleus and is involved in the expression of splicing factors that regulate glycolysis-related genes, which could contribute to the development of bone marrow tumors [31, 32]. The patient also exhibited clinical features similar to those described in cases of FIP1L1::PDGFRA-associated myeloid tumors with eosinophilia, including bone marrow mesenchymal cell proliferation and eosinophilia. A previous study reported a rare NUTM1 rearrangement, the NAP1L4/NUTM1 fusion, in MPN-Eo generated by t (11;15) (p15; q12) translocation. This patient responded well to imatinib and achieved hematologic and molecular remission [21]. Although the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene in this study is similar to the NAP1L4::NUTM1 fusion, it may affect different chromatin regulatory domains. We hypothesize that important functional regions of the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene, such as the transcription start site, 3′-UTR, or 5′-UTR, may regulate gene transcription, translation, and protein modification. Although the specific functional regions remain unclear, this hypothesis can provide directions for future research. Notably, when the patient is positive for FIP1L1::PDGFRA, the expression of LUC7L::NUTM1 is also positive; however, when the expression of FIP1L1::PDGFRA is lost, the expression of the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene becomes negative, and the typical manifestations of PDGFRA rearrangement usually appear.

The case exhibited characteristic features including leukocytosis, superficial lymphadenopathy, and marked imatinib sensitivity. Eosinophils were significantly elevated and morphologically abnormal, suggesting involvement of the t(15;16)(q14;p13) translocation. We hypothesize that positive PDGFRA expression activates both upstream and downstream pathways within the LUC7L::NUTM1 functional domain, contributing to disease pathogenesis. The genetic instability of FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion may predispose to secondary t(15;16) translocation, generating the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion. These findings provide novel insights into myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement pathogenesis and suggest that LUC7L::NUTM1 may play a critical role in disease development and therapeutic response.

However, this study has several limitations, including the small sample size and lack of detailed molecular mechanistic studies regarding LUC7L::NUTM1 function. Future investigations should focus on expanding patient cohorts, elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms underlying LUC7L::NUTM1-mediated imatinib sensitivity, evaluating long-term patient outcomes, and exploring alternative therapeutic targets. These efforts will advance our understanding of this rare fusion gene and improve clinical management strategies for patients with similar genetic alterations.

Conclusion

This study presents the first reported case of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with eosinophilia harboring both a PDGFRA fusion gene and the novel LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion gene. Our findings demonstrate that the LUC7L::NUTM1 fusion is associated with high leukemic cell levels and exhibits sensitivity to imatinib treatment. Bioinformatics analyses suggest potential involvement of this fusion gene in myeloma pathogenesis.
