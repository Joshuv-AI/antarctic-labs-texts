Atypical presentation of long-standing hand neurofibroma: A case report
Authors: Suhail Bayomi, Momen Mohamed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceA solitary neurofibroma of the hand is an uncommon condition that typically lacks distinct symptoms, often resulting in misdiagnosis and prolonged morbidity. While it primarily affects younger individuals, it can also present in older adults. This article discusses an extraordinary case of a long-standing neurofibroma in the hand.Case presentationA 70-year-old right-handed individual, known to have hypertension and a pacemaker, presented with a mass on the palm of his left hand. This mass was associated with tingling and mild pain, particularly after excessive use of the hand. Recently, he had experienced partial limitations in hand movement due to the mass's pressure. Following a thorough clinical assessment, he underwent surgical excision of the mass under local anesthesia. Histopathological findings confirmed the diagnosis of neurofibroma. There are no particular radiological approaches that can definitively diagnose this condition; however, histopathological findings have proven effective in confirming the diagnosis.ConclusionLong-term hand neurofibroma is an infrequent disorder that typically lacks prominent symptoms, highlighting the critical need for medical assessment. Surgery is the only effective treatment for hand cases, providing both a definitive diagnosis and the possibility of being performed with regional anesthesia.

Introduction

The hands are fundamental and intricate components of the human body, exhibiting a sophisticated structure and diverse functions. When pathology arises in the hand, it poses significant challenges to hand surgeons, who must navigate treatment while maintaining the hands' sensitivity, appearance, and functionality. Among the various conditions that can affect the hand, tumors are particularly concerning, often resulting in considerable morbidity and, in extreme instances, leading to functional loss or amputation. Neurofibroma, a benign tumor of neural components, accounts for about 5 % of soft tissue tumors [1] [2].

Neurofibromas can manifest as either solitary tumors or as part of multiple neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). This condition is characterized by a mass that enlarges slowly and a late onset of symptoms. Nevertheless, due to the infrequency of soft tissue masses in the hand, the symptoms may appear earlier than masses at different locations on the body. Young adults are the primary demographic affected by this condition, with cases becoming increasingly rare in older individuals, and it is uncommon for symptoms to emerge after a prolonged period [3]. In the context of hand tumors, compression symptoms often present as numbness or tingling, resulting from the infiltration of neural structures. This similarity in presentation can make diagnosis difficult, as numerous hand tumors share these symptoms while lacking any external skin manifestations [4].

Surgical resection is still considered the most effective approach for alleviating symptoms; however, if the nerve is not successfully separated from the tumor, it may result in hand disability, affecting both mobility and sensation. Preserving these functions is essential for maintaining a functional hand.

This article describes the management of long-lasting solitary neurofibroma of the hand.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old right-handed man of African descent has been diagnosed with hypertension for the past four years and is currently on regular medication. His medical history includes the insertion of a pacemaker in 2007. He reports experiencing swelling in his left hand for the past nine years. The mass has exhibited a slow growth pattern during this time and is associated with discomfort following prolonged hand use, yet it does not limit his daily functioning. With the growth and protrusion of the mass, the patient reported a tingling sensation in the medial region of the index finger, which has gradually worsened over the last two years. He also experienced difficulty fully extending his thumb and index finger, while maintaining normal movement capabilities in his hand. Sensation in the affected hand was largely intact, except for the index finger. The postponement in presenting for medical evaluation is linked to the upheaval caused by the war, which compelled him to move frequently and limited his access to specialized medical care. He disclaimed the existence of any masses on multiple parts of his body. Moreover, he indicated that there were no similarities among his family members. Upon clinical evaluation, a mass is noted at the base of the second metacarpal bone, spreading into the thenar area (Fig. 1). The skin over the mass is shiny, thin, and moist, showing no discoloration or ulceration. The index finger and thumb exhibit partial flexion, particularly at the distal interphalangeal joints (DIP), but can be extended with assistance from the other hand, resulting in numbness. The mass is characterized by its firmness, lack of mobility, and absence of attachment to the overlying skin, with no tenderness observed. An X-ray of the hand revealed no bony irregularities. Regrettably, a CT scan and MRI were not accessible. The thorough examination did not reveal any comparable masses or skin lesions. In the operating room, maintaining aseptic conditions, a regional block was administered at the wrist, followed by the placement of a tourniquet. An oblique incision measuring approximately 12 cm was made along the proximal palm crease. The incision was then followed by dissection through the subcutaneous tissue. The thenar muscles were carefully identified and retracted to reveal the mass located within the wound. Examination revealed that the flexor digitorum profundus of the index finger was being stretched by the mass, which simulated a partial flexion of the distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ). Notably, the tendon itself was not affected by the tumor. Additionally, the branches of the median nerve and the digital arteries were located and retracted to safeguard them from potential damage. It was observed that the mass was encircling and infiltrating the second digital nerve. Dissection continued to separate the mass from the surrounding tissue, which was ultimately found to be attached to the second digital nerve (Fig. 2). The mass measured approximately 5 * 4 cm (Fig. 3). An en bloc excision of both the nerve and the mass was carried out (Fig. 4). Neurotization of the nerve to the adjacent digital nerve was performed to restore sensation in the finger. Following careful hemostasis, hand movement was assessed, as the procedure was conducted under regional anesthesia, and no dynamic deficits were noted. The specimen was analyzed histopathologically, and the results showed a haphazardly arranged spindle cell within the collagenous stroma, exhibiting hyperchromatic, wavy nuclei and moderate nuclear atypia. These observations confirmed the diagnosis of neurofibroma. The follow-up period was largely uneventful, with only mild numbness reported, while hand functions remained unaffected. Discussion

Neurofibroma is identified as a hamartoma that arises from neural and mesenchymal cell populations. It is categorized as a benign peripheral nerve tumor, which includes schwannomas. Although neurofibromas are more frequently encountered than malignant peripheral nerve tumors, they constitute only about 5 % of the total soft tissue tumors. Neurofibromas typically occur in younger demographics and are not influenced by gender. They can manifest as either sporadic (solitary) tumors or as part of a syndrome, such as neurofibromatosis. Notably, sporadic neurofibromas in the hand constitute around 1 % of all sporadic neurofibromas, highlighting their rarity [2] [3].

Neurofibromas of the hand typically do not exhibit distinctive features and often resemble other hand tumors, presenting as a slowly enlarging mass that is generally painless and somewhat mobile. However, there are instances where patients may experience significant pain. Sensory deficits are relatively rare, as patients tend to describe a tingling sensation, even in cases where the tumor has grown significantly. Moreover, the confined nature of the hand influenced the development of symptoms, prompting the patient to pursue medical care promptly. In light of these similarities of neurofibroma and other hand tumors, it is crucial to consider various differential diagnoses during the evaluation of a patient presenting with a hand mass. In our patient, even though the pathology persisted for nearly a decade, the tingling sensation emerged as the most distressing symptom, with the mass being the next most significant concern. Furthermore, despite the mass's considerable size, the patient did not experience the severe pain often described in various literature [3] [6].

Numerous diagnostic modalities are accessible; however, none are specifically designed for the identification of neurofibroma. While ultrasound may offer restricted insights into neurofibroma, particularly in the context of the hand, it is proficient in outlining the anatomical location and certain morphological attributes. In contrast, a CT scan can furnish additional information, though it is not as adept at assessing soft tissue masses. In contrast, MRI is regarded as the most comprehensive diagnostic tool for soft tissue lesions, offering detailed insights into the anatomical relationships between the tumor and adjacent structures, in addition to the vascular status of the area. The MRI findings showed a hyperintense rim with a central low-signal area, potentially resembling a target sign on T2 images; however, this characteristic is not uniquely indicative of a neurofibroma. While MRI offers several advantages, it does not specifically confirm the presence of neurofibromas. In our case, the lack of CT availability and the incompatibility of MRI with pacemaker devices presented obstacles to accessing these imaging techniques. Consequently, an X-ray of the hand was utilized to evaluate for bony involvement, it clarified the situation [3] [7] [8].

Histological evaluation continues to be the sole diagnostic method with exceptional accuracy in differentiating conditions. Microscopic examination revealed well-defined nodules characterized by low to moderate cellularity within the spindle cell population, which exhibited wavy nuclei. The spindle cells are arranged irregularly and are interspersed with stromal structures [9] [10]. In our case, the diagnosis made by professionals was substantiated by histological evaluation and immunohistochemistry, both of which tested positive for S100 and CD, specific markers associated with neurofibroma.

The management of solitary neurofibromas typically involves either a conservative approach or surgical intervention. The conservative method is suitable for small tumors that cause minimal symptoms and do not disrupt daily activities. Surgical treatment is deemed curative, effectively alleviating symptoms and providing tissue biopsy for precise diagnosis. However, it carries risks, including potential structural damage. In more complex situations where the tumor has significantly infiltrated the nerve, sacrificing the nerve may be necessary to manage the disease and reduce the likelihood of recurrence. Following this, nerve reconstruction is essential to restore normal function and sensation in the hand. In our situation, we experienced the benefits of surgery; however, the digital nerve was removed along with the mass, necessitating nerve reconstruction. While the tingling sensation showed some improvement, it persists, consistent with findings from other studies [2] [11].

Conclusion

Long-standing hand neurofibroma is a rare disorder that typically does not show significant symptoms, highlighting the importance of obtaining medical advice. The only effective treatment for hand cases is surgery, which not only facilitates an accurate diagnosis but can also be performed with regional anesthesia. Unfortunately, patients may continue to experience tingling sensations for an extended period.
