Task-based interpretation flexibility predicts real-world emotion regulation variability
Authors: Wisteria Deng, Yutong Zhu, Mark Shuquan Chen, Sofie Glatt, Tyrone D. Cannon, Jutta Joormann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adapting to daily emotional challenges requires flexibility including flexible interpretations of ambiguous situations and dynamic use of emotion regulation (ER) strategies. Understanding whether flexibility is a domain-general capacity that spans both cognition and emotion regulation has important implications. This study investigated whether baseline interpretation flexibility predicts day-to-day ER variability and affective outcomes using a multimethod design. Participants completed a lab-based Interpretation Inflexibility Task (IIT) to assess flexibility in revising interpretations of ambiguous social scenarios, followed by a 14-day tracking of daily emotions and ER strategy use. Multilevel modeling revealed that individuals with higher interpretation flexibility demonstrated greater ER variability and reported less intense negative and positive affect. Interpretation flexibility also accounted for unique variance in ER variability beyond self-reported ER difficulties. These findings show overlap between interpretation flexibility and healthy ER, suggesting that targeting interpretation flexibility may have transfer effects for improving ER.

Introduction

Daily life is inherently dynamic and often ambiguous, requiring individuals to continuously interpret evolving situations and adjust their emotional responses accordingly. The way individuals interpret these situations plays a central role in shaping their emotions and subsequent behaviors1,2. Because situational information unfolds over time, adaptive functioning depends not only on initial interpretations, but also on the ability to revise those interpretations as new information emerges1,3. This capacity to update interpretations is referred to as interpretation flexibility4. Interpretation flexibility is crucial for psychological well-being, while inflexibility is associated with various psychiatric disorders and symptoms4. Difficulty revising interpretations has been associated with paranoia5, depression6, social anxiety4, and broader impairments in social functioning7.

Interpretation flexibility may influence mental health and well-being not only by reducing biased or rigid appraisals, but also by shaping the ability of individuals to regulate their emotions8. Emotion regulation (ER) ability refers to the ability to deploy strategies that modify the intensity or duration of emotional responses in accordance with situational demands and personal goals9. Effective ER may relate to the ability to flexibly interpret ambiguous and evolving situations. For instance, if individuals can flexibly interpret new information, they may be better able to select and implement ER strategies that are contextually appropriate. Conversely, interpretation inflexibility may constrain ER ability. Consistent with this conceptual link, research shows that interpretation inflexibility moderates the relationship between impaired ER ability and anxiety10, and that both ER ability and interpretation flexibility jointly buffer against psychopathology in individuals with psychotic experiences11. Together, these findings suggest an interdependence of interpretative and emotion regulation processes in shaping well-being.

Since interpretative and emotion regulation processes unfold in evolving, dynamic environments, emerging research has shifted from static assessments of overall ER ability to measurements that reflect the contextual flexibility of regulatory behavior. Critically, although ER strategies have traditionally been dichotomized as adaptive and maladaptive12, contemporary models of ER recognize that contextual appropriateness of a strategy is equally, if not more, important than its static classification (e.g13.,). The extent to which individuals adjust their use of ER strategies in response to evolving situational demands encountered in daily life, termed ER variability, is a hallmark of this adaptive and contextual emotional functioning.

ER variability is typically conceptualized in two forms14. Within-strategy ER variability captures how individuals modify the intensity or application of a single strategy over time, and between-strategy ER variability reflects individuals’ selection of different ER strategies in response to varying emotional contexts14. Of these, between-strategy variability is particularly related to positive adjustment and lower levels of psychopathology, as it reflects differential use of strategies15,16. This flexible implementation suggests a more sophisticated and differential approach to emotion regulation, rather than rigid or habitual strategy use. Overall, understanding ER variability, particularly at the between-strategy level, provides important insight into the ER mechanisms as protective against affective disorders.

Despite the theoretical interdependence of interpretative and emotion regulation processes in shaping psychopathological outcomes, their direct relationship remains largely unexplored. Both processes involve ongoing adjustments to changing situational information, suggesting that shared underlying mechanisms such as belief updating may link them. However, studying this relationship requires approaches that can capture how these processes unfold in real-world contexts. Accordingly, a growing body of research has used intensive longitudinal methods to examine patterns of emotional and regulatory change across time and situations17.

To date, however, ER variability is typically examined in naturalistic settings1415;, whereas interpretative flexibility has primarily been assessed through cross-sectional, lab-based tasks2. Bridging these approaches is therefore critical for determining whether lab-based measures of interpretative flexibility capture processes that generalize to real-world emotional functioning. The Interpretation Inflexibility Task (IIT3;); see Methods for full task description) is particularly well-suited to this purpose: its progressive unblurring design directly operationalizes belief updating by requiring participants to revise interpretations as new situational information is revealed, and its separate positive and negative flexibility indices mirror the valence-differentiated nature of ER variability in daily life. Crucially, the IIT has been validated in populations characterized by both interpretative rigidity and emotion regulation difficulties including anxiety and depression3, lending further support to its use as a bridge between lab-based and naturalistic approaches.

The present study uses this approach to directly examine the relationship between interpretative flexibility and ER variability, combining the IIT with a 14-day daily diary study assessing affect and ER strategy use. Based on prior findings linking interpretation flexibility with less ER difficulties18,3, we hypothesize that individuals with greater baseline interpretation flexibility will demonstrate greater ER variability across the two-week period. We further predict that higher ER variability and greater interpretation flexibility will be associated with more favorable affective outcomes, specifically lower negative affect and higher positive affect. By integrating a lab-based cognitive measure with longitudinal assessments, this study aims to examine how interpretation flexibility may support dynamic emotional responding and more adaptive affective experiences over time.

Methods

Participants

To ensure sufficient data density for reliable estimates of within-person variability and temporal dynamics, only participants who completed at least 50% of the daily diary surveys were included in the final analyses. This threshold minimizes bias due to incomplete data and enhances the stability of individual-level estimates19,20. The study had a final sample of 90 participants (compliance rate: 80.93%; see Supplemental Table 1 for detailed demographics). To assess potential bias due to exclusion, retained participants (n = 90) and excluded ones (n = 10) were compared on demographic and baseline variables. Retained participants had a more balanced gender distribution (64.4% female) than excluded participants (100% female, p = .028). No significant differences emerged in age, race, education, or mental health characteristics (all ps > 0.05), suggesting minimal exclusion bias.

Participants ranged in age from 18 to 70 years (M = 34, SD = 14). Over half of the participants identified as female (64%, n = 58). Regarding racial and ethnic composition, less than half of the sample identified as Caucasian (47%, n = 42), followed by Black/African American (21%, n = 19) and Asian (18%, n = 16). This was a highly educated sample, with 38% (n = 34) holding a bachelor’s degree and 12% (n = 11) holding a master’s degree (see Supplemental Table 1).

Tasks

Baseline assessment

At baseline, participants underwent a behavioral task to measure interpretation flexibility, followed by self-report questionnaires assessing general ER difficulties, within-strategy ER variability, and levels of psychiatric symptoms, as detailed below.

Interpretation flexibility. The Interpretation Inflexibility Task (IIT) is a validated, picture-based assessment designed to measure interpretation bias and inflexibility in social and emotional scenarios3. The task presents 24 interpersonal situations using progressively unblurred photographs—12 leading to positive and 12 to negative resolutions. Each scenario is displayed three times, with 80%, 20%, and 0% of the image blurred, gradually revealing more contextual information. At each stage, participants are presented with four interpretations of the scenario (one True interpretation, two Lure interpretations, and one Absurd interpretation) and are asked to rate the plausibility of each interpretation. An interpretation bias score is calculated at each stage by dividing the plausibility rating of the True interpretation by the average plausibility rating of the two Lure interpretations. Higher bias scores indicate relatively greater endorsement of the interpretation consistent with the scenario’s resolution compared to the alternatives. Bias scores are computed separately at Stages 1, 2, and 3 and averaged across scenarios, separately for positive- and negative-resolution scenarios.

The IIT generates two key indices, both calculated using the same method: (1) the Positive Flexibility Index that measures an individual’s ability to revise interpretations in scenarios that resolve in positive outcomes, and (2) the Negative Flexibility Index that measures an individual’s ability to revise interpretations in scenarios that resolve in negative outcomes. Higher RMSSD values indicate greater stage-to-stage variability in interpretation bias (i.e., greater flexibility in revising interpretations), whereas lower values indicate reduced variability and greater interpretation inflexibility. By distinguishing between positive and negative interpretation flexibility, the IIT allows for a comprehensive assessment of how individuals adapt their interpretations based on emotional valence. The IIT has been used to examine interpretation flexibility in populations with anxiety, depression, and delusion-like ideations, providing insights into the role of interpretation flexibility in emotional processing3.

Emotion regulation difficulties. Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS21;) was used to measure emotion regulation difficulties. The DERS assesses various aspects of emotion regulation, including awareness, clarity, acceptance of emotions, impulse control, and the ability to access effective regulation strategies. Lower scores on the DERS indicate less emotion regulation difficulties, characterized by greater emotional insight, effective strategy use, and fewer difficulties in managing emotions. DERS demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the present sample, Cronbach’s α = 0.96.

Daily diaries

Each participant completed an orientation meeting with trained research assistants before their first daily diary survey. During the orientation meeting, the research assistant explained the daily diary procedure and instructed the participant to complete a practice survey that was identical to the daily diary survey. This allowed participants an opportunity to ask questions about the daily diary procedure. During the daily diary phase of the study, participants were prompted by email to complete daily diary surveys once per day at 9 PM (to allow participants to reflect on their emotional experiences throughout the entire day) over the course of 14 days. All daily diary surveys were identical, assessing the most emotional situation in the past 24 h, followed by measures of positive and negative affect and use of various emotion regulation strategies. All questions are detailed below in the order in which they were presented in each daily diary survey.

The Emotional Situation. Each daily diary survey started with asking the participant to recall the most emotionally salient situation in the past 24 h. The most emotionally salient situation in the past 24 h was specifically probed to ensure that the ER strategies participants endorsed reflected meaningful regulatory demands rather than low-intensity or neutral experiences, which allowed us to capture instances in which ER is likely to be engaged and variable. Similar to previous studies22,23, participants were asked to write a response covering the following information: Where were you? Who was with you? What were you (and others) doing? What was happening? These open-ended descriptions were used to anchor participants’ reports of ER strategy use to a concrete emotional episode. The prompts were included to situate strategy reports within meaningful contexts and to reduce decontextualized responding; they were not analyzed in the present study.

Emotion Regulation Variability. Participants were asked to rate, on a scale of 1 to 7, how much they used a series of emotion regulation strategies in response to the daily emotional situation. Participants were instructed to select all strategies that applied to them in the situation. These strategies include reappraisal (i.e., I changed how I thought about the event), suppression (i.e., I made sure to hide how I feel), rumination (i.e., I repeatedly thought about the event and could not stop), worry (i.e., I worried about what may happen), distraction (i.e., I did something else to take my mind off it), problem solving (i.e., I tried to solve problems or resolve the events), savoring (i.e., I took time to savor or enjoy what happened), planning (i.e., I made plans for the next steps), positive refocusing (i.e., I focused on the positive aspect of the event), soothing (i.e., I looked to others for comfort), and perspective seeking (i.e., I tried to find out what others would do if they were in my situation). These strategies were selected to represent a broad cross-section of commonly studied ER approaches spanning cognitive (e.g., reappraisal, rumination, positive refocusing), behavioral (e.g., distraction, problem solving, planning), and interpersonal (e.g., soothing, perspective seeking) domains, which was consistent with our goal of capturing strategy diversity and relative use to quantify ER variability.

Emotion regulation variability was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV) for ER strategy use at a given time point15. where σ represents the standard deviation of ER strategy use at a given time point, and µ represents the mean ER strategy use at the same time point. A higher CV indicates greater variability in ER strategy use, reflecting greater differential use of emotion regulation, whereas a lower CV suggests more stable ER use24.

Affective Outcomes. The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS25;) is a widely used self-report measure that captures momentary emotional states. It consists of two subscales: (1) positive affect, which measures the degree to which individuals experience emotions such as interest, enthusiasm, and attentiveness, and (2) negative affect, which captures distressing emotions like nervousness, irritability, and fear. Participants rate each affective state on a Likert scale from 1 (“Very slightly or not at all”) to 5 (“Extremely”), reflecting their experiences within a given time frame. Higher scores on each subscale indicate stronger positive or negative affect, respectively. The PANAS is particularly valuable in our daily diary design, as it offers a reliable assessment of affective fluctuations. To estimate multilevel reliability, we excluded participants who had fewer than two measurement occasions or showed no within-person variability on one or more PANAS items, as such cases cannot contribute to within-person reliability estimates. Multilevel reliability analyses showed low within-person but high between-person consistency for positive affect (ωwithin = 0.18, 95% CI [0.10, 0.26]; ωbetween = 0.84, 95% CI [0.76, 0.92]), and moderate within-person and high between-person consistency for negative affect (ωwithin = 0.41, 95% CI [0.33, 0.50]; ωbetween = 0.88, 95% CI [0.82, 0.94]).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations were computed for all study variables (Table 1). To examine the relationship between ER (ability and variability) and interpretation flexibility, we conducted a hierarchical regression analysis. In the first step, ER ability was entered as the predictor of ER variability. In the second step, the interpretation flexibility index was added to assess their unique contributions beyond ER ability. Model fit improvement was assessed using ΔR² and F-tests, and standardized regression coefficients were reported for interpretability.

To further explore how interpretation flexibility and ER variability relate to daily affect, we employed multilevel modeling using maximum likelihood estimation. The models accounted for the nested structure of the data (daily observations within individuals) by including random intercepts for participants. Interpretation flexibility was positively correlated with ER variability (r = .46 for negative flexibility and r = .47 for positive flexibility, both ps < 0.001), indicating that greater flexibility in interpreting social information is linked to more variability in daily ER strategy use (Fig. 1). Additionally, greater ER variability was associated with better emotion regulation (i.e., lower DERS scores; r = − .27, p = .011). Linking interpretation flexibility with ER difficulty and variability

We conducted a hierarchical regression analysis to test interpretation flexibility as a hypothesized predictor of ER variability. The models aimed to confirm the relationship between ER difficulties and variability, then to assess the amount of variance in ER variability explained by interpretation flexibility over and above that explained by the ER ability. In the first model, fewer ER difficulties significantly predicted ER variability (β = − 0.27, t(88) = -2.61, p = .011), indicating that individuals with better ER (i.e., lower DERS scores) tend to show greater variability in their ER strategies in daily life.

In the second model, we included the flexibility indices in endorsing positive and negative new information to evaluate their unique contributions to the association between ER difficulties and variability. The addition of these variables significantly improved the model fit (ΔR² = 0.25, F(2, 86) = 15.52, p < .001), suggesting that interpretation flexibility plays a crucial role in explaining ER variability beyond what is explained by ER difficulties alone. Specifically, both flexibility in endorsing positive information (β = 0.30, t(86) = 2.95, p = .004) and negative information (β = 0.30, t(86) = 2.97, p = .004) were positively linked with ER variability. In other words, individuals who are more flexible in interpreting social information, regardless of the valence of the information, tend to employ ER strategies more variably in daily life. Furthermore, when interpretation flexibility was included in the model, the association between ER difficulties and ER variability was reduced to non-significant levels (β = − 0.14, t(86) = -1.48, p = .143). This suggests that the ability to integrate novel social information may relate to more varied use of ER strategies.

Linking interpretation flexibility and ER variability with positive and negative affect

In addition to examining the associations among trait-like variables, we conducted multilevel analyses to investigate the association between baseline interpretation flexibility and daily affect. Both positive and negative flexibility were linked to lower momentary negative affect (positive: β = − 0.22, t(87) = -2.93, p = .004 and negative: β = − 0.20, t(87) = -2.66, p = .009). The relationship between flexibility and positive affect was less consistent: only flexibility in endorsing negative (not positive) new information was associated with less intense positive affect (β = − 0.19, t(87) = -2.34, p = .022). These findings suggest that interpretation flexibility relates to momentary emotional experiences, particularly in regulating negative affect.

Moreover, we conducted multilevel analyses to examine the association between daily fluctuations in ER variability and affect. The findings revealed that higher daily ER variability was linked to reduced negative affect (β = − 0.08, t(1035) = -3.32, p < .001) but was not associated with positive affect (β = 0.02, t(1035) = 0.83, p = .409) on the day level. In other words, on days with greater variability in ER strategy use, participants reported less negative affect.

At the between-person level (i.e., averaged across the 14 days), greater ER variability was associated with less positive affect (β = − 0.22, t(88) = -2.75, p = .007) and negative affect (β = − 0.30, t(88) = -4.16, p < .001), indicating that participants who displayed more ER variability on average reported lower intensity of affect.

Discussion

Interpretation flexibility and ER variability are central to adaptive affective outcomes and overall psychological wellbeing3,13. This multimethod study examined the relationship between baseline lab-based interpretation flexibility, daily affect, and ER variability. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate how interpretation flexibility predicts emotions and ER variability using both lab-based assessments and real-time ecological methods. Findings revealed that baseline interpretation flexibility predicted ER variability. Both ER variability and interpretation flexibility are also related to lower affect intensity (i.e., reduced mean levels of both positive and negative affect).

Our findings relating interpretation flexibility with ER variability expand upon prior work linking interpretation flexibility with less ER difficulties18,3 . Past work finds that interpretation (in)flexibility moderates the relationships between ER difficulties and anxiety10, underlining the impact of interpretation flexibility on ER and psychological outcomes. Current results further the understanding of interpretation flexibility and its clinical significance by directly confirming the hypothesis that interpretation flexibility is linked with the dynamic use of ER strategies across daily contexts. Indeed, both interpretation flexibility and ER variability involve similar cognitive mechanisms: Interpretation flexibility taps into the capacity to integrate new information and reassess current circumstances3. This cognitive process of revisioning is also invoked in ER variability27. Just as rigid interpretations of social information can be maladaptive, deploying the same ER strategies across all situations reflects inflexibility. In contrast, the ability to dynamically adjust ER strategies based on situational demands (i.e., ER variability) implicates more adaptive affective outcomes and psychological wellbeing28. Increasing evidence connecting flexibility and ER underscores the importance of studying interpretation flexibility as a key factor in understanding adaptive ER processes.

Importantly, we examined positive and negative interpretation flexibility separately. Although these indices are derived using the same approach, their modest relationship and somewhat distinct patterns of associations with affective outcomes suggest that they may not reflect a single flexibility construct. Instead, flexibility in updating toward positive versus negative interpretations may relate to partially distinct processes, such as openness to positive disambiguating information versus sensitivity to negative disambiguating information. This distinction aligns with affective science models that suggest valence-specific processing systems2. Accordingly, the separate positive and negative flexibility indices may provide a granular characterization of interpretation revisioning.

It is worth noting that both ER variability and interpretation flexibility are linked with lower affect intensity (in both positive and negative affect) over time. This finding diverges slightly from the initial hypothesis, which predicted that higher ER variability and greater interpretation flexibility would be linked to higher positive affect and lower negative affect. However, the pattern of lower affect intensity aligns with prior work on ER effectiveness. Existing research suggests that individuals with more effective ER and greater flexibility may engage in more adaptive, context-sensitive responses to emotional experiences, allowing for greater emotional stability14,27. Whereas rigid ER strategies and interpretation styles are often linked to heightened emotional reactivity, increasing vulnerability to mood disturbances and psychological distress29,30. Thus, rather than amplifying positive affect, interpretation flexibility and ER variability may contribute to more moderate emotions over time. This finding underscores the importance of conceptualizing affective coping not only in terms of maximizing positive affect and minimizing negative affect, but also in fostering emotional stability and regulatory balance.

In examining ER variability alongside affective outcomes and flexibility, the current study focuses on within-strategy ER variability, which reflects how individuals adjust the intensity or application of the same ER strategies across days14. This form of variability captures the dynamic regulation of emotions within a given strategy – for instance, a person using cognitive reappraisal might sometimes reframe a negative situation extensively and at other times less so, depending on the emotional context and perceived control over the situation. Within-strategy ER variability contrasts with between-strategy ER variability, where individuals select different ER strategies (e.g., using reappraisal in one situation and distraction in another) based on contextual demands14. Between-strategy variability has been linked to greater psychological flexibility and resilience, as it allows individuals to select the most context-appropriate regulation strategy31,13. However, within-strategy variability may be just as critical, as it reflects an individual’s ability to fine-tune their emotional responses without completely shifting regulatory approaches32. Our work connects greater within-strategy variability with lower affect intensity and greater interpretation flexibility. This is in line with prior work showing that rigid use of any strategy (even those traditionally deemed as adaptive) can be less than effective. For example, individuals who consistently use high levels of reappraisal even when situations are highly aversive and uncontrollable may be more prone to maladaptive outcomes30,33. By investigating within-strategy variability in relation to affective outcomes and interpretation flexibility, the present study provides insights into how individuals dynamically regulate emotions in everyday life. Understanding how people fine-tune the intensity of their ER strategy use, rather than solely focusing on strategy selection, may enhance our knowledge of adaptive emotion regulation processes and provide novel targets for interventions.

Importantly, interpretation flexibility is conceptually distinct from the use of any specific ER strategy, including those that potentially involve flexibility. Interpretation flexibility, as operationalized here, reflects the updating of interpretations in response to newly available external information prior to any regulatory intent. This flexibility precedes and potentially scaffolds ER strategies involving effortful reappraisal rather than overlapping with reappraisal itself. Indeed, correlational analyses in the present study found modest relationships between interpretation flexibility and daily reappraisal use, indicating that these constructs are relatively distinct.

These findings should be interpreted in consideration of several limitations. First, the present study focused on interpretation flexibility, whereas the revisioning process exists at many other stages of the information integration process (e.g., visual attention, impression formation). Additionally, although the IIT reflects stage-to-stage revision of interpretations in response to newly revealed information, it is possible that task performance also reflects individual differences in cautious or reserved responding under ambiguity. Participants may learn that contextual information unfolds gradually and therefore withhold strong initial endorsements. This potential cautious responding is conceptually related to flexibility but not identical to the process of updating prior interpretations. Importantly, the IIT flexibility index (RMSSD of bias scores) captures variability across stages. However, future work should disentangle tolerance of ambiguity, initial response caution, and dynamic belief revision as potentially separable components of interpretation flexibility. Future work should expand the assessment of flexibility to other stages of information integration, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how information integration and updating ability may relate to affective coping and ER.

Relatedly, because we focused only on the most emotionally salient event each day, the present design emphasizes ER under high-intensity emotional demand rather than capturing the full distribution of daily emotional experiences. Similarly, although the open-ended descriptions of daily emotional situations were not analyzed quantitatively in the present study, they are conceptually important for interpreting ER variability. Variability in strategy use may reflect adaptive, context-sensitive responding, but it could also reflect inconsistent or situation-irrelevant strategy deployment. Future work could leverage these narrative data to distinguish context-appropriate flexibility from spontaneous or maladaptive variability. Integrating contextual features of emotional episodes with strategy use represents an important next step in clarifying when ER variability is adaptive.

Second, while we used longitudinal data, our analytic models do not establish causality. More work is needed to probe the temporal dynamics and evaluate if ER variability is an antecedent or a downstream outcome of daily affect changes. Third, our sample size limits more fine-grained analysis of ER patterns by investigating the specific ER strategies individuals implement. Studies with larger samples and/or more extensive experience sampling methods (e.g., collecting several assessments per day) will allow for more stringent statistical methods (e.g., longitudinal trajectory analysis) to uncover specific ER patterns in response to day-to-day stressors.

Conclusion

Taken together, our findings suggest that lab-based interpretation flexibility predicts higher ER variability and less intense affect. Such associations highlight the importance of targeting interpretation flexibility in interventions as a possible means to promote ER variability across situations and improve affective outcomes. Methodologically, this study departs from the typical assessment of ER through a trait-level self-report questionnaire. Combining daily diary methods with lab-based tasks offers a novel approach to understanding how cognitive factors influence real-world ER. Future research should consider such multimethod designs to better capture the dynamic processes of ER and interpretation revisioning across diverse contexts34.
