Differences in hospital staff nurses’ and nurse leaders’ assessments of the work environment and patient safety are associated with staff nurse burnout
Authors: Hyunmin Yu, Linda H Aiken, Lori J Grischott, Carolyn L Davidson, Kathleen Vnenchak, Christine Pearl, Dale Beatty, Daniela Golinelli, Karen B Lasater, the US Clinician Wellbeing Study Consortium
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Nurse burnout remains a significant concern, with modifiable factors playing a crucial role in its mitigation or exacerbation.Purpose:We examined the association between nurse burnout and assessment gaps in work environment and patient safety culture between nurse leaders and staff nurses, and whether these associations varied by Magnet status.Methods:Data from the U.S. Clinician Wellbeing Study and RN4CAST-NY/IL Study (13,381 staff nurses, 1,722 nurse leaders) in 2021 were analyzed using multiple linear regression.Discussion:Nurse leaders rated work environments and patient safety culture more favorably than staff nurses. A one-point gap in work environments was linked to a 13% increase in burnout (b = 12.99, p = .001). A one-point gap in patient safety culture was associated with a 2% increase (b = 1.75, p = .015). Associations were weaker in Magnet hospitals (b = −4.63, p = .007).Conclusion:Addressing assessment gaps between leaders and staff nurses is vital for reducing burnout.Keywords: Nurse leader, Nurse burnout, Work environment, Patient safety culture

Introduction

Nurse burnout is associated with system-level factors such as poor work environments and a poor culture of patient safety (Lake et al., 2024; Schlak et al., 2021). Key components of the nurse work environment include leadership support, staffing, shared governance, and nurse–physician collaboration (Lake et al., 2024). Although many studies have assessed the association between the nurse work environment and burnout (Copanitsanou et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2018), in this paper, we evaluate a novel question about whether alignment between nurse leaders’ and staff nurses’ assessments of their work environment and patient safety is associated with staff nurse burnout.

Prior research has documented discrepancies in how nurse leaders and staff nurses assess their work environments. A study conducted in two Midwestern hospitals found significant differences between nurse managers and staff nurses in their evaluations of the work environment, with managers consistently rating the environment more positively than staff nurses (Gormley, 2011). Similarly, the 2021 American Association of Critical-Care Nurses National Nurse Work Environment Study revealed a persistent gap, with nurse managers reporting healthier work environments compared with direct care nurses (Ulrich et al., 2024). While the existence of this assessment gap is well-established, its implications for nurse job outcomes, particularly burnout, have not been thoroughly investigated.

Discrepancies in assessments between leadership and frontline staff may signal a lack of shared understanding and communication within an organization, which can contribute to feelings of disempowerment and disengagement among nurses (Hall et al., 2022). When nurse leaders rate the work environment more positively than direct care nurses, it may lead to unmet expectations, inadequate support, and a perceived lack of responsiveness to frontline concerns. These factors have been linked to emotional exhaustion and burnout (Aiken, Lasater, et al., 2023). In this context, understanding whether alignment in assessments between nurse leaders and staff is associated with burnout, and examining the role of organizational factors such as Magnet status in this relationship, is a critical step toward identifying actionable strategies to improve nurse well-being.

Assessment gaps between nurse leaders and staff nurses and their impact on staff nurse burnout may differ by a hospital’s Magnet status. In Magnet hospitals, the senior nurse leadership team plays a pivotal role in shaping the vision for the future, establishing the systems, policies, and environment necessary to achieve that vision (Fischer & Nichols, 2019). These leaders are committed to clearly communicating the rationale behind organizational changes and involving all departments in the process of achieving these goals. This approach aligns closely with the principles of transformational leadership, where leaders not only drive change but also actively engage with staff at all levels to ensure alignment with the organization’s goals (Fischer & Nichols, 2019). As a result, Magnet hospitals may foster greater alignment between nurse leaders’ and staff nurses’ assessments of the work environment, potentially reducing burnout.

This study had two aims: (a) to investigate the association between high burnout among staff nurses and assessment gaps of the work environment and patient safety culture between nurse leaders and staff nurses, and (b) to explore whether these associations differ by hospitals’ Magnet status. Specifically, we hypothesized that hospitals with larger gaps in assessments of the work environment and patient safety culture between nurse leaders and staff nurses would have higher proportions of staff nurse burnout, with potential moderation by Magnet status.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This cross-sectional observational study combined data from three sources: (a) the U.S. Clinician Wellbeing Study, (b) the RN4CAST-NY/IL Study, and (c) the American Hospital Association (AHA) Annual Survey.

The U.S. Clinician Wellbeing Study was an extensive, multisite collaborative research initiative that evaluated the job outcomes (e.g., burnout, job satisfaction) and work environments of more than 21,000 physicians and nurses across 60 Magnet hospitals using electronic surveys conducted in 2021 (Aiken, Lasater, et al., 2023). The RN4CAST-NY/IL Study, also conducted in 2021, utilized a comparable survey to collect data from registered nurses licensed in New York and Illinois on work environments, job outcomes, and perceptions of care quality and patient safety (Aiken, Sloane, et al., 2023). Data about hospitals’ Magnet designation were obtained from the American Nurses Credentialing Center’s regularly updated and publicly available online list of Magnet hospitals (American Nurses Credentialing Center, 2025). Data on hospital characteristics used in risk-adjustment were obtained from the 2021 AHA Annual Survey, a nationwide survey of hospitals (American Hospital Association, 2022).

Study Sample

We drew Magnet hospital data from the 2021 U.S. Clinician Wellbeing Study and non-Magnet, as well as nonoverlapping Magnet hospital data, from the 2021 RN4CAST-NY/IL Study. To investigate the association between staff nurse burnout and assessment gaps of the work environment and patient safety culture between nurse leaders and staff nurses, we established the following inclusion criteria: hospitals with at least 10 survey respondents identifying as nurse leaders (e.g., nurse managers, nurse directors, chief nursing officers [CNO], or chief nurse executives) and at least 10 staff nurses (e.g., direct patient care) from either the RN4CAST-NY/IL or the U.S. Clinician Wellbeing Study. This criterion was set to establish a statistically robust foundation for calculating hospital-level aggregated measures while minimizing the risk of skewed or unrepresentative results.

Initially, there were a total of 30,724 nurse respondents across 528 hospitals. We excluded 15,621 respondents from 448 hospitals that had fewer than 10 nurse leader respondents. The final analytic sample included 15,103 respondents from 80 hospitals (13,381 staff nurses and 1,722 nurse leaders), including 58 Magnet hospitals and 22 non-Magnet hospitals. The number of staff nurses per hospital ranged from 16 to 563, with an average of 167, while the number of nurse leaders per hospital ranged from 10 to 42, with an average of 22.

Study Variables

Dependent Variable: Percentage of Staff Nurses With High Burnout

Burnout was assessed using the validated 9-item Emotional Exhaustion Subscale from the Maslach Burnout Inventory (Maslach & Jackson, 1981; Maslach et al., 2001). Respondents rated each item on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 = “never” to 6 = “every day.” Sum scores were calculated, with a total possible range from 0 to 54. The Cronbach’s alpha for our analytic sample was 0.95. A score of 27 or higher was categorized as high burnout (Maslach et al., 1997). We focused on high burnout because it represents a clinically and practically meaningful cutoff and facilitates clearer interpretation. To calculate hospital-level measures of staff nurse burnout, we computed the percentage of staff nurses in each hospital whose scores met or exceeded this threshold.

Independent Variable I: Assessment Gap of the Work Environment Between Nurse Leaders and Staff Nurses

The nurse work environment was assessed using a validated short form of the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES-5) (Lake et al., 2024). This five-item scale was rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = “strongly agree” to 4 = “strongly disagree,” with the prompt: Please indicate the extent to which you agree that each of the following features is present in your current practice environment. The responses were reverse-coded so that higher values reflected more positive work environments. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.81 in our analytic sample. Each respondent’s individual responses to the PES-5 were averaged to generate a continuous score that reflected their overall assessment of the work environment. Individual scores were aggregated to calculate hospital-level mean scores, separately for staff nurses and nurse leaders. To assess differences in nurse work environment assessments between the two groups, we calculated a mean difference (MD) score for each hospital.

Independent Variable II: Assessment Gap of Patient Safety Culture Between Nurse Leaders and Staff Nurses

Patient safety culture was evaluated using six items from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture. Given the length of the nurse survey and the need to minimize respondent burden, the investigators selected these six items that had been used in prior research (Olds et al., 2017) and included in both the U.S. Clinician Wellbeing Study and the RN4CAST-NY/IL Study, reflecting a range of safety features within hospitals. Participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale, where 1 = “strongly agree” and 5 = “strongly disagree,” with the prompt: Please indicate whether you agree or disagree with the following statements about patient safety in your hospital (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2024). To ensure consistency, four of the positively worded items were reverse-coded. A total score, ranging from 6 to 30, was calculated, with higher scores indicating a more favorable patient safety culture. The Cronbach’s alpha for our analytic sample was 0.75. Similarly, we calculated a MD score for this measure at the hospital level. The intraclass correlation coefficient (1, k), which reflects the degree of agreement among respondents within hospitals compared with between hospitals for each independent and dependent variable, was greater than 0.80 for both the nurse leader group and the staff nurse group in our sample. A value above 0.60 is considered satisfactory for aggregation (Glick, 1985).

Covariates: Hospital Characteristics

Hospital-level covariates included teaching status, size, and specialized service capacity. Teaching status was measured by the ratio of resident physicians and fellows to hospital beds, categorizing hospitals as nonteaching, minor teaching (≤1:4 ratio), or major teaching (> 1:4 ratio) (Ayanian et al., 1998). Specialized service capacity, such as major transplant or open-heart surgery capabilities, classified hospitals as high capacity (Hartz et al., 1989). Hospital size, representing the total number of beds in each hospital, was treated as a continuous measure. For this study, hospitals with Magnet designation as of 2021 were classified as Magnet hospitals.

Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1. Descriptive analyses were used to examine variation across hospitals in staff nurse burnout as well as differences between staff nurses’ and nurse leaders’ assessments of the nurse work environment, patient safety culture scores, and their individual items.

For our primary analyses examining the association between nurse burnout and assessment gaps of the nurse work environment and patient safety culture, we used multiple linear regression analyses. The dependent variable was the percentage of staff nurses with high burnout, and the independent variable was the assessment gap. We included the staff nurse mean work environment (or patient safety culture) score, teaching status, hospital size, and specialized service capacity as covariates. Including the staff nurse mean scores was essential for accounting for the overall quality of the work environment and patient safety culture as experienced by staff nurses, ensuring an accurate estimation of the impact of assessment gaps on burnout.

For our secondary analyses aimed at assessing the differential impact of assessment gaps on staff nurse burnout by hospitals’ Magnet status, we used multiple linear regression analyses with the percentage of staff nurses with high burnout as the dependent variable. In these models, we excluded the staff nurse mean work environment and mean patient safety culture scores as covariates due to their high correlation with Magnet status (> 0.7). Assessment gaps and Magnet status were included as an interaction term, with teaching status, hospital size, and specialized service capacity as covariates. Prior to fitting each model, we calculated generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF) to assess multicollinearity among all independent variables. All GVIF values were low, indicating no issues with multicollinearity. After fitting each model, we checked regression diagnostics, which indicated no violations of the normality and heteroscedasticity assumptions.

There were four hospitals with missing values for hospital characteristics (5%) from the AHA Annual Survey. Although the exact mechanisms driving these missing values were not identifiable, there was no apparent systematic pattern linking missingness to observed data. Therefore, we assumed that the data were missing at random. Under this assumption, we employed multiple imputation using chained equations (White et al., 2011).

Results

Burnout Prevalence and Assessment Gaps by Magnet Status

The percentage of staff nurses with high burnout was higher in non-Magnet hospitals compared with Magnet hospitals, though the difference was not statistically significant (52.1% vs. 47.6%, p = .077) (Table 1). In both Magnet and non-Magnet hospitals, nurse leaders rated the work environment and patient safety culture more favorably than staff nurses. Magnet hospitals had significantly higher ratings for both the work environment and patient safety culture from nurse leaders and staff nurses compared with non-Magnet hospitals. However, the MD score (assessment gaps) for the work environment between nurse leaders and staff nurses (MD = 0.4, SD = 0.2 vs. MD = 0.4, SD = 0.3; p = .441) and for patient safety culture between nurse leaders and staff nurses (MD = 2.4, SD = 1.2 vs. MD = 2.6, SD = 1.6; p = .500) was not significantly different between Magnet and non-Magnet hospitals. No significant differences were found in hospital characteristics between the two hospital groups.

Associations Between Assessment Gaps and Staff Nurse Burnout

Table 3 presents the regression results for our primary and secondary analyses. Each one-point work environment assessment gap between nurse leaders and staff nurses was associated with a 13% higher percentage of staff nurses with high burnout (b = 12.99, p = .001). For example, if the mean gap were to increase from 0.4 to 1.4 in the PES-5 score, this would be associated with an approximate 13% increase in the proportion of staff nurses experiencing high burnout. Higher staff nurse work environment scores were significantly associated with a lower percentage of staff nurses experiencing high burnout (b = −27.72, p < .001). No significant interaction was found between Magnet status and work environment assessment gaps (b = 2.90, p = .797).

Association Between High Nurse Burnout and Assessment Gaps in Nurse Work Environment and Patient Safety Culture

For patient safety culture, each one-point assessment gap was associated with a 2% higher percentage of staff nurses with high burnout (b = 1.75, p = .015). For example, if the mean patient safety culture gap were to increase from 2.4 to 3.4, this would be associated with an approximate 2% increase in the proportion of staff nurses experiencing high burnout. Higher staff patient safety culture scores were significantly associated with a lower percentage of staff nurses experiencing high burnout (b = −3.25, p < .001). A significant interaction was observed between Magnet status and patient safety culture assessment gaps (b = −4.63, p = .007). Specifically, each one-point patient safety culture gap was linked to a 0.5% higher percentage of staff nurses with high burnout in Magnet hospitals (b = 0.54, SE = 2.1) and a 5% higher percentage in non-Magnet hospitals (b = 5.17, SE = 0.5).

Discussion

Among the 80 hospitals in our study, nurse leaders on average had more favorable assessments of nurse work environments and patient safety than direct care staff nurses. Differences between how nurse leaders and staff nurses assess the work environment and patient safety culture were significantly associated with greater proportion of staff nurses with high burnout. These gaps may arise due to leadership disconnects, lack of communication, or insufficient support, all of which are known to exacerbate burnout (Hall et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2020). The emotional exhaustion central to burnout (Maslach et al., 1997) is likely exacerbated when nurses perceive that their leaders are out of touch with their experiences in the workplace. The association between staff nurse burnout and assessment gaps of patient safety culture was significantly weaker in Magnet hospitals. This suggests that Magnet recognition may serve as a protective factor, potentially mitigating some of the negative consequences associated with these assessment gaps.

Significant differences in the baseline work environment and the quality of leadership may explain the varying burnout proportions across hospitals. In our study, while Magnet hospitals received higher overall ratings from both leaders and staff nurses, the magnitude of the assessment gaps was not significantly different from those in non-Magnet hospitals. This finding highlights that stronger overall environments do not necessarily eliminate assessment differences between leaders and staff nurses. The significantly weaker association between assessment gaps and burnout in Magnet hospitals may be attributed to their more positive overall work environment, as rated by staff nurses. Consistent with prior studies (Brooks Carthon et al., 2021; Nikpour & Brooks Carthon, 2023), we found that higher staff nurse-rated work environment scores were significantly associated with lower burnout. Staff nurses in Magnet hospitals gave significantly more favorable work environment scores compared with those in non-Magnet hospitals. These higher scores may potentially buffer the negative effects of assessment gaps. Additionally, Magnet hospitals’ emphasis on transformational leadership and staff engagement, both associated with lower nurse burnout (Lewis & Cunningham, 2016; Vargas-Benítez et al., 2023), highlights transparent communication, staff empowerment, and shared decision-making. These factors may have mitigated burnout by enhancing nurses’ sense of support and engagement.

The findings underscore the importance of addressing assessment gaps of the work environment and patient safety culture. Leadership behavior is a key determinant of both employee voice and silence (Morrison, 2023). Employee voice, defined as the informal and voluntary communication of concerns, ideas, suggestions, or opinions regarding work-related issues with the intent to foster improvement or change (Morrison, 2023), is critical to addressing these gaps. In contrast, employee silence occurs when individuals withhold information about problems, ideas, or opinions related to work (Knoll & Van Dick, 2013). Silence can significantly undermine organizational learning and contribute to unresolved systemic issues (Morrison, 2023). Nurse leaders should prioritize regular engagement with staff nurses through structured forums such as unit-based councils and staff huddles (Hebb et al., 2024). They should also implement closed-loop communication strategies, which involve confirming receipt and understanding of messages, to ensure clarity and mutual accountability (Salas et al., 2005). These approaches not only promote a culture of transparency and shared decision-making but also help surface frontline concerns related to the work environment and safety culture, ultimately supporting nurse well-being and patient outcomes.

Improving psychological safety in the workplace will be beneficial in this context. Psychological safety, within the context of team dynamics, is defined as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking such as speaking up (Edmondson, 1999). This construct emphasizes the importance of an environment where individuals feel free to express their concerns, ideas, and mistakes without fear of judgment or negative consequences. Additionally, psychological safety has been associated with lower nurse burnout (de Lisser et al., 2024; Kerrissey et al., 2022). To foster psychological safety and open communication, nurse leaders can implement strategies grounded in professional governance, such as CNO town halls (Riesch et al., 2023) and employee resource groups (Seegmiller Renner et al., 2022). These mechanisms promote transparency, empower frontline staff, and create structured opportunities for speaking up. When supported by responsive leadership, these practices help bridge assessment gaps, enhance the work environment, and ultimately improve both staff well-being and patient safety.

Nurse leaders should also foster open communication when developing strategies for mitigating critical nurse burnout. The findings from our study of Magnet hospitals revealed significant discrepancies in the perceived importance of certain strategies. For example, staff nurses placed greater importance on interventions such as increasing individual control over scheduling or the right to decline reassignment to other clinical units, whereas nurse leaders prioritized interventions such as resilience training. This indicates that even in Magnet hospitals, nurse leaders should prioritize engaging with staff nurses regularly, ensuring that communication channels remain open, and actively seeking feedback about work conditions and safety culture. Given the high levels of burnout observed in both Magnet and non-Magnet hospitals, with no statistically significant difference despite prior research suggesting otherwise (Rodriguez-Garcia et al., 2020), which may have been partially attributable to the additional strain of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2021, nurse leaders should actively engage staff nurses in participatory problem-solving and self-directed workplace improvements.

Leadership style plays a key role in fostering staff–management collaboration and reducing gaps in work environment assessments. Transformational and authentic leadership, which emphasize transparency, shared decision-making, and empowerment, are linked to greater nurse engagement, lower burnout, and improved communication (Wei et al., 2020). An example of a promising intervention aimed at fostering collaboration between clinicians and managers is the Nursing Workplace Environment and Staffing Council Program (NWESC), developed by the Organization of Nurse Leaders of New Jersey Advocacy Committee (Organization of Nurse Leaders of New Jersey, 2025). The program aims to provide staff nurses with a respected voice in human resource allocation through a structured, collaborative process with management. The Council consists of 51% bedside nurses, with at least half of their work time dedicated to direct patient care. It is co-chaired by the CNO and a direct care nurse, and the number of staff nurses is sufficient to represent all nursing care within the hospital. The Council is involved in developing nurse staffing plans and creating a positive work environment, with the finalized plan submitted to the CNO for budget planning. In addition to formal councils, leaders can use organizational survey results and conduct regular leadership rounding to gather frontline feedback, model approachability to strengthen trust, and bridge practice-management assessment gaps (Blake & Bacon, 2020).

Leadership development training could potentially reduce the gap between nurse managers’ and clinical nurses’ perceptions of the quality of the nurse practice environment. When high-performing clinical nurses are promoted to leadership positions, they often receive little to no formal leadership training. Nurse manager-specific training on best practices for daily clinical operations, people management, care quality, data-driven decision-making, and financial management may equip new leaders with the skills needed to better support their clinical nurses’ aspirations. However, further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of existing leadership programs in bridging the workplace assessment gaps between staff nurses and managers. Future research should also examine the underlying mechanisms that link assessment gaps between nurse leaders and staff nurses to burnout. Identifying which of these mechanisms are most influential could help inform targeted interventions.

While the study provides valuable insights into the role of assessment gaps and Magnet status in nurse burnout, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design of the study limits our ability to infer causality. Second, we excluded hospitals with fewer than 10 nurse leader responses to ensure adequate representation for hospital-level aggregation, which resulted in omitting approximately 85% of hospitals and likely biased the sample toward larger institutions. As a result, findings may not generalize to smaller hospitals with different structures, resources, and leadership dynamics. Future studies should explore whether similar patterns hold in smaller or rural settings and consider methods such as descriptive comparisons or sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of this exclusion. Third, treating Magnet status as a binary variable may obscure important variation among Magnet hospitals (e.g., newly designated vs. long-standing). Additionally, due to high collinearity between Magnet status and staff nurse work environment and safety scores, these covariates were excluded from the secondary analysis. This limits comparability between models and makes it more difficult to isolate the independent effect of Magnet designation. Future research may benefit from using approaches like ridge regression to address multicollinearity while retaining key predictors. Finally, our model did not account for potential differences in nurse workload and work environments across individual units or assess matched leader–staff dyads, which may be associated with burnout.

Conclusions

While Magnet hospitals have more positive work environments than non-Magnet hospitals, the proportion of nurses with burnout remains high. Differences in assessments of the work environment between clinicians and leaders documented in this study is a modifiable and actionable issue that, if effectively addressed, could help reduce clinician burnout. Even with the emphasis in Magnet hospitals on structural empowerment of clinicians, more needs to be done to foster open communication between clinicians and leaders. The differences found in assessments of the work environment and patient safety culture between nurse leaders and staff nurses in Magnet hospitals should serve as an impetus to Magnet hospital leaders to reprioritize their efforts to achieve unity of purpose across clinicians and leaders in the pursuit of excellence in clinician and patient well-being.
