Identification of a novel nonsense mutation (c.1369 C > T) in the WAS gene in a neonate: a case report and literature review
Authors: Shuyue Deng, Xinyi Liu, Siyu Chen, LinXiao Wan, Wenbin Dong, Lan Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWiskott-Aldrich syndrome (WAS) is an X-linked recessive primary immunodeficiency disorder characterized by the classic triad of microthrombocytopenia, eczema, and recurrent infections. However, neonatal onset often presents atypically, making early diagnosis challenging.Case reportWe report a male neonate presenting with spontaneous petechiae, intractable thrombocytopenia, anemia, and leukopenia on the first day of life. The patient was initially misdiagnosed with immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and showed refractoriness to conventional first-line (IVIG, corticosteroids) and second-line (recombinant human thrombopoietin) therapies. Trio-whole exome sequencing (Trio-WES) identified a novel hemizygous nonsense mutation, c.1369 C > T (p.Q457X), in exon 11 of the WAS gene. Bioinformatic analysis suggests this mutation leads to the loss of the critical C-terminal VCA domain of the WASP protein.ConclusionThis case expands the mutational spectrum of WAS. It highlights that for male neonates with unexplained thrombocytopenia—particularly those with reduced mean platelet volume (MPV)—early genetic testing is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis and inappropriate immunosuppressive treatment.

Introduction

Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (WAS) is a rare X-linked recessive immunodeficiency caused by mutations in the WAS gene located on Xp11.22-Xp11.23. The gene encodes the Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein (WASP), a 502-amino acid intracellular protein exclusively expressed in hematopoietic cells [1–3]. WASP acts as a pivotal distinct cytoskeletal regulator. Structurally, it contains an N-terminal EVH1 domain, a GTPase-binding domain (GBD), and a critical C-terminal VCA (verprolin, cofilin, and acidic) domain. Through these domains, WASP regulates actin polymerization via the Arp2/3 complex, which is essential for immune synapse formation, cell migration, and platelet biogenesis [4]. Loss-of-function mutations typically lead to the classic WAS phenotype: thrombocytopenia with small platelets (microthrombocytopenia), eczema, and recurrent infections. However, the clinical spectrum is broad. Genotype-phenotype correlations suggest that missense mutations often result in milder X-linked thrombocytopenia (XLT), whereas nonsense mutations, frameshifts, and large deletions are associated with the severe classic WAS phenotype and a higher risk of autoimmunity and malignancy [5–7].

Here, we report a male neonate with severe, refractory thrombocytopenia caused by a novel nonsense mutation (c.1369 C > T/p.Q457X) that was not previously recorded in major genetic databases. We discuss the diagnostic challenges in the neonatal period and the molecular implications of this specific mutation.

Case presentation

Clinical history

A 1-day-old male infant was admitted due to the spontaneous onset of diffuse skin petechiae. The mother reported a history of unexplained intra-abdominal hemorrhage, but her platelet count during pregnancy was normal. The family history was significant for X-linked inheritance patterns (Fig. 1). The proband was the fourth conception (G4P3). First pregnancy resulted in the birth of a female infant who is in good health. The second pregnancy ended in spontaneous abortion due to unexplained fetal death. The third pregnancy (a male brother) was diagnosed with thrombocytopenia at 1 month of age and died at 1 year due to bleeding complications.

Physical and laboratory findings

Physical examination revealed dense pinhead-sized petechiae and ecchymosis on the trunk and limbs. Notably, no eczema or hepatosplenomegaly was palpable. During the hospitalization of the patient, intensive laboratory monitoring was conducted. Some key data are summarized as follows (Table 1). Initial laboratory tests showed severe thrombocytopenia (PLT 26 × 10^9/L) with a significantly reduced mean platelet volume (MPV: 6.6–10 fl.; normal range 9.4–12.5 fl.). Coagulation profiles showed mild prolongation of PT and APTT, but DIC screening was otherwise inconclusive. On the 6th day of hospitalization (the 7th day after birth), the infant underwent a bone marrow puncture while being treated with IVIG and prednisone. Bone marrow aspiration revealed active proliferation but a complete absence of megakaryocytes (Fig. 2), suggesting a defect in platelet production rather than peripheral destruction. Because megakaryocyte evaluation depends on sampling and specimen type, an apparent absence of megakaryocytes on aspirate smears may reflect sampling limitation and should be interpreted cautiously; repeat assessment (and/or biopsy if feasible) may be considered when clinically indicated.

Treatment process and clinical outcome

The patient was initially suspected of having Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP) or sepsis. ITP was initially suspected and treated with IVIG and corticosteroids. However, the presence of thrombocytopenia from day 1 of life, persistently low MPV (microplatelets), and poor/absent platelet response to IVIG/corticosteroids argued against typical ITP and suggested an inherited thrombocytopenia.

Comprehensive treatment was administered, including:

Efficacy evaluation

The child patient showed a completely refractory state to the above-mentioned conventional treatment. The platelet count continued to fluctuate at a low level of (12–22)×10⁹/L. On the 10th day after discharge (the 25th day after birth), the patient’s condition deteriorated, with bloody stools and signs of infection. New bleeding points appeared on the face and upper palate. The test results showed HGB at 97 g/L and PLT at 12 × 10⁹/L. Although the platelet count briefly rose to 84 × 10⁹/L after strong anti-infection treatment (ceftriaxone) and blood transfusion, it rapidly dropped back to 11 × 10⁹/L within a week. Genetic analysis

Given the resistance to ITP therapy, low MPV, and family history, Trio-Whole Exome Sequencing (Trio-WES) was performed. Analysis identified a hemizygous mutation in exon 11 of the WAS gene: c.1369 C > T (NM_000377.3).

Discussion

The p.Q457X mutation identified in this case is located at position 457 of the WASp protein. The WASp protein is 502 amino acids in length, and the VCA domain at its C-terminus approximately begins around position 460 (the exact boundaries vary slightly among different literature sources, but position 457 is definitely before the core sequence of the VCA) [8]. Due to the p.Q457X mutation, the entire VCA domain (including the crucial acidic tail A) is lost. Even if the truncated WASp protein exists, it completely loses the ability to recruit and activate the Arp2/3 complex. Without the activation of Arp2/3, actin cannot form branched structures, and the cytoskeletal reorganization is completely paralyzed [9–11]. More seriously, as the mutation occurs in exon 11 (out of a total of 12 exons) and is more than 50–55 nucleotides away from the last exon-intron junction, this premature termination codon (PTC) is highly likely to trigger the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) mechanism in cells. This implies that truncated proteins may not be produced within the cells at all, but rather manifest as a complete absence of WASp protein (WASp-negative) [12–14]. Previous studies have suggested an association between residual WASp expression and clinical severity, and truncating variants are more frequently reported in classic WAS than in XLT. However, genotype–phenotype relationships show considerable variability and should not be interpreted deterministically. In our patient, the congenital onset of thrombocytopenia and early clinical manifestations are consistent with a severe phenotype described in association with truncating WAS variants, but these clinical observations do not by themselves establish the functional consequence of the variant [7, 15, 16].

Given that p.Q457X is a nonsense variant, it is expected to result in a truncated WASp protein and a putative loss-of-function mechanism. Nevertheless, we did not have residual samples to assess WASp protein expression (e.g., flow cytometry or Western blot) or to perform functional assays. Therefore, the functional impact of this variant remains inferred rather than experimentally confirmed, which we acknowledge as a limitation.

The differential diagnosis of neonatal thrombocytopenia is extremely complex. The most profound lesson from this case is that it was initially misdiagnosed as ITP and received ineffective treatment. We have summarized the key differentiating points between WAS and ITP (Table 2) for clinical reference. In general, ITP is associated with increased megakaryocytes in bone marrow as a compensatory response, whereas most WAS/XLT patients have a normal megakaryocyte mass with variable dysmegakaryopoiesis; therefore, an apparent absence of megakaryocytes on aspirate smears should prompt consideration of sampling limitations and repeat assessment if clinically feasible.

We also note that microthrombocytopenia, although typical, is not universally present in WAS/XLT. Rare cases with normal or even increased platelet size (macrothrombocytopenia) have been reported [19], which can further mimic ITP and delay diagnosis.

Regarding the mildly prolonged PT/APTT observed early in life, we interpret this as a nonspecific finding in sick neonates, potentially related to physiologic hepatic immaturity, vitamin K status, inflammatory consumption, or blood loss, rather than a defining feature of WAS itself.

In neonatal practice, if a male infant presents with thrombocytopenia accompanied by a low MPV and shows no response to IVIG and corticosteroids, WAS/XLT should be considered early rather than escalating therapy under an assumption of refractory ITP. Some third-line agents used for refractory ITP (e.g., hypomethylating agents such as decitabine) can cause myelosuppression and increase infection risk; in an infant with a suspected inborn error of immunity, such immunosuppressive exposure may be particularly hazardous. We therefore recommend performing genetic testing before initiating such therapies in male infants with atypical or treatment-refractory thrombocytopenia. In this case, decitabine was not used.

Therefore, this report strongly recommends that before initiating novel second- or third-line treatments such as decitabine for refractory thrombocytopenia in children, especially male infants, genetic testing must be conducted to completely rule out congenital bone marrow failure diseases such as WAS.

Clinical management recommendations and summary

For children diagnosed with WAS, the treatment strategy should shift from “immunosuppression” to “immunoreconstitution” and “supportive treatment”.

Supportive treatment

Anti-infection prophylaxis: Compound sulfamethoxazole (for prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia), acyclovir (for prevention of herpes virus). IVIG replacement therapy: Maintain plasma IgG levels and reduce bacterial infections.

Platelet transfusion

Only used in cases of active bleeding or before surgery to avoid the production of alloantibodies.

TPO

Evidence suggests that TPO receptor agonists (e.g., eltrombopag/romiplostim) can increase platelet counts in a subset of WAS/XLT patients, but they do not restore platelet activation/function and responses are variable [20, 21]. Therefore, TPO may serve as a temporary bridging/supportive approach rather than definitive therapy in WAS.

Prohibited

Aspirin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (interfere with platelet function); avoid contact sports (risk of splenic rupture).

Radical treatment

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (Allo-HSCT): This is currently the only proven method to cure WAS. If HLA-matched transplantation is performed at an early age (< 2 years old), the survival rate can reach over 90% [22, 23].

Gene therapy

For patients without suitable donors, gene therapy using lentiviral vectors to transduce autologous hematopoietic stem cells has achieved remarkable results in clinical trials, effectively restoring platelet counts and immune function, and demonstrating superior safety compared to earlier retroviral vectors [24].

Unfortunately, following our recommendation for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, the child’s guardians elected to discharge the patient for personal reasons and were subsequently lost to follow-up.

Through in-depth analysis of this case, we hope to enhance the ability of pediatricians and hematologists to identify atypical WAS in the neonatal period, achieve precise diagnosis and individualized treatment, and thereby improve the quality of life of these rare and critically ill children.
