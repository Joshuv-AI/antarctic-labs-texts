Barriers and facilitators to the implementation of a complex nursing intervention: a human-in-the-loop LLM-Assisted longitudinal and Cross-Case analysis across twelve cardiac surgery centres
Authors: Yvonne J Jordens, Lilian Lechner, Myrna Pelgrum-Keurhorst, Roelof G A Ettema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImplementation of complex nursing interventions remains challenging, with success varying substantially across healthcare settings. Most implementation research assesses barriers and facilitators at single timepoints, limiting understanding of how these determinants evolve. Therefore, we aimed to understand how barriers and facilitators evolve over time during different implementation phases across three categories of implementation outcomes.MethodsFollowing the “Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment” (EPIS) framework, this longitudinal qualitative study examined barriers and facilitators during implementation of the PREDOCS nursing intervention across twelve Dutch cardiothoracic centers from 2016 to 2019. We conducted 252 structured interviews across eight measurement waves with diverse healthcare professionals. Data were systematically coded for barriers and facilitators using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model through a hybrid approach combining Large Language Model (LLM)-assisted analysis with rigorous human verification. Hospitals were categorized into three implementation outcome groups: Implementation (n = 4), Delayed-Implementation (n = 5), and No-Implementation (n = 3).ResultsBarriers and facilitators evolved systematically across EPIS phases. Motivation remained universally high across all outcome groups throughout all phases. Fundamental knowledge gaps about intervention components during the Exploration phase were present in all three No-Implementation hospitals and absent in the Implementation group. Opportunity-domain factors, specifically the presence or absence of organizational infrastructure, resources, and management support, differed systematically between outcome groups across phases. As implementation progressed, the Implementation group reported a shift in the Capability domain from training barriers to available training and competent staff, a pattern absent in the Delayed and No-Implementation groups. Successful hospitals reported compensatory opportunity facilitators alongside persistent barriers, whereas hospitals that discontinued implementation reported sustained belief in intervention value in the absence of enabling organizational conditions.ConclusionsImplementation success depends less on motivation-building than on developing organizational infrastructure to translate intention into practice. Background

Implementing complex interventions in clinical practice is a challenging process that requires careful alignment of multiple interdependent components with the specific context and needs of various stakeholders [1]. This complexity increases when interventions encompass different settings and target diverse populations and behaviors, as is often the case in nursing interventions [2, 3]. The more components and interacting elements an intervention includes, the more complex it becomes and the more challenging it is to implement [4, 5]. Central to this implementation challenge process is behavior change among healthcare professionals. The COM-B model provides a framework for understanding such change by examining three key factors that influence behaviour, both as barriers or facilitators: capability (knowledge and skills), opportunity (environmental factors), and motivation (reflective and automatic processes) [6]. Understanding these barriers and facilitators is crucial for successful implementation, which requires careful attention to how interventions are delivered and adapted in practice.

Successful implementation requires that an intervention is both delivered correctly (intervention fidelity) and adapted to the local context (implementation fidelity) [7]. As intervention complexity increases, maintaining high fidelity becomes increasingly difficult [4, 8], which may diminish effectiveness [9]. The dynamic interplay between intervention components, contexts, and implementation processes creates various factors that can influence implementation outcomes, leading to variations in both intervention and implementation fidelity. Therefore, measuring barriers and facilitators is essential to understand which contextual factors influence implementation outcomes [10].

The PREDOCS (PREvention of Decline in Older Cardiac Surgery patients) consultation exemplifies such a complex intervention requiring systematic implementation across multiple healthcare contexts. This evidence-based multicomponent nursing intervention was designed to prepare older patients for cardiac surgery to prevent postoperative complications including delirium, depression, pressure ulcers, and infections [11]. From 2016 to 2019, the PREDOCS consultation was implemented across seven top clinical hospitals and five university hospitals, geographically distributed throughout the Netherlands.

Understanding barriers and facilitators to implementation has become a central focus in implementation science, with extensive research identifying factors that influence intervention uptake across healthcare settings [12]. The Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework emphasizes that implementation is a dynamic, non-linear process that unfolds in distinct phases, where different factors become important at different stages, and where multi-level context determines which factors are most influential [13, 14]. However, two methodological limitations constrain our understanding of how implementation determinants operate in practice. First, barriers and facilitators are typically assessed at a single point in time, such as before implementation begins or after completion, which assumes these determinants remain static throughout the implementation process and limits our ability to capture the dynamic changes that occur as implementation unfolds [15, 16]. Second, determinants are often examined within specific local contexts, making it difficult to identify patterns that might be generalizable across different settings. Given that implementation trajectories and outcomes vary substantially across contexts [17], both longitudinal tracking of determinants and cross-case comparative analyses are essential.

A unique opportunity is given by the PREDOCS implementation study which examines implementation dynamics longitudinally across multiple contexts [18]. Over a four-year period, twelve hospitals implemented the PREDOCS intervention. Preliminary observations indicate varying degrees of implementation outcomes across participating hospitals despite similar support and resources. Each hospital followed a distinct implementation trajectory, defined as the pathway hospitals follow over time, characterized by their progression through implementation phases and the outcomes achieved at each phase. Understanding how barriers and facilitators evolve across these trajectories can provide crucial insights for predicting and supporting successful implementation of complex nursing interventions in healthcare settings. By systematically mapping barriers and facilitators across phases and trajectories, this study provides actionable insights for healthcare leaders and implementation teams to anticipate and address implementation challenges proactively.

The aim of this study was to understand how barriers and facilitators evolve over time during different implementation phases across three categories of implementation outcomes (implementation, delayed implementation and no implementation).

Method

Study design

This qualitative multicenter study used a longitudinal multiple case design to examine barriers and facilitators in PREDOCS implementation. It was conducted as part of a larger implementation research project in which the PREDOCS consultation was implemented across twelve cardiac surgery centers in the Netherlands between 2016 and 2019 [18]. We conducted a longitudinal analysis to track how barriers and facilitators evolved over time during different implementation phases, and cross-case analysis to compare patterns across three categories of implementation success.

The PREDOCS consultation

The PREDOCS consultation is an evidence-based multi-component nursing intervention aimed at preparing older patients for elective cardiac surgery to reduce the risk of postoperative complications such as delirium, depression, pressure ulcers, and infections [11]. The consultation is built on three principles: 1) using the time waiting for hospital admission as an opportunity for preparation, 2) conducting a risk assessment directly after the decision for surgery for potential postoperative complications, and 3) promoting self-management by offering targeted preventive measures during the preparation phase (Additional file 1).

Implementation approach

Participating hospitals were tasked with implementing the PREDOCS consultation respecting the three main principles, while allowing for flexibility in implementing and execution of the intervention. Hospitals were encouraged to adapt the intervention to their specific contexts, ensuring that essential components, such as risk assessment, preparation during the waiting period, and promoting self-management, remained intact [17]. To support the implementation process, a project structure was established focusing on both the general program level, which addressed all hospitals, and the local project level within each specific hospital (Additional file 2).

The overall implementation approach consisted of three interconnected components: Learning Community meetings, measurements and feedback. This was combined with local tailored approaches to enhance the fit between the intervention and its unique implementation context [18]. For monitoring, three implementation outcomes were measured: 1. barriers and facilitators of implementation, 2. behaviours of healthcare professionals involved and 3. the fidelity of executing the intervention.

Measuring barriers and facilitators of implementation

Semi-annual evaluations of barriers and facilitators were an integrated component of our implementation approach, designed to provide implementation teams with regular insights into both local and common factors that might affect both the degree of implementation and intervention fidelity. For this evaluation, structured interviews were conducted every six months from 2016 to 2019 to assess these factors. All interview data were analysed using the COM-B model as the theoretical framework to systematically categorize barriers and facilitators according to capability, opportunity, and motivation factors. This analysis enabled targeted support by identifying specific areas where hospitals needed additional guidance or resources. The results were systematically fed back to local project leaders through written reports and follow-up discussions, creating a continuous feedback loop that allowed for real-time adjustments to implementation strategies (Fig. 1). Based on these insights, project leaders could modify their local approaches, address identified barriers, and leverage facilitators more effectively. This developmental evaluation method with regular feedback created opportunities for continuous improvement of implementation strategies while simultaneously generating rich longitudinal data on implementation dynamics. As such, the measurement and analysis of barriers and facilitators was not just an evaluation tool, but an active component of the implementation intervention itself, directly influencing implementation success through informed strategy adjustments.

Participants

The study population consisted of healthcare professionals from twelve participating cardiac surgery centers. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling and included a diverse group of professionals involved in the PREDOCS project: project leaders, PREDOCS nurses (nurses who perform the PREDOCS-consultations), managers, general ward nurses, cardiothoracic surgeons, planners responsible for scheduling consultations, and data managers. Convenience sampling was used following from the pragmatic nature of the implementation study and the need to minimize disruption to clinical practice. To ensure diverse perspectives, local project leaders were instructed to include representatives from different professional groups when possible. By including a wide range of involved healthcare professionals, we aimed to gain comprehensive insights into the implementation process across different roles and responsibilities within the cardiac surgery centers.

Data collection

Between 2016 and 2019, groups of two to four final-year students from the Bachelor of Nursing and Bachelor of Medical Assistance educational programs collected data semiannually in each hospital. During each data collection period, students contacted local project leaders at participating hospitals, who selected three to four available in the PREDOCS intervention involved staff members using convenience sampling. Students conducted semi-structured interviews on-site at each hospital. Prior to data collection, students received standardized training in qualitative interviewing techniques and were briefed on the content and context of the PREDOCS intervention to ensure consistency in interview approach across sites and time periods. All interviews were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim for analysis. Transcripts were returned to the interviewees for verification and approval as part of the member check procedure before analysis commenced. Additional implementation data were systematically collected throughout the PREDOCS implementation period. The national project leader maintained a comprehensive logbook documenting all contact moments with local project leaders, including minutes from Learning Community meetings. During these meetings, local project leaders presented dashboards displaying implementation progress (not yet started, ongoing, completed, stagnation) for six core elements: implementation plan, kick-off meeting, baseline measurement, nursing training, initiation of PREDOCS consultations, and evaluation with feedback.

Structured interview Guide

The interview guide was developed by the research team with the focus on creating comprehensive insights into the implementation progress of each hospital. The questions were designed as simple, opinion-based inquiries that would enable project leaders to continuously refine and improve their implementation strategies based on the insights gathered from their responses. The interview guide comprised two sets of questions: one for hospitals that had not yet implemented the PREDOCS consultation and another for those that had already started implementation (Table 1).

Data analysis

In line with the aim of this study, the data analysis was conducted at two levels: examining changes in barriers and facilitators across implementation phases and comparing these patterns between hospitals based on their implementation trajectories (see Key Analytical Concepts at the start of this method section). Given the longitudinal nature of the data (252 interviews across 12 hospitals over 8 time periods), we employed an innovative hybrid analytical approach combining large language model (LLM)-assisted coding with systematic human verification and iterative refinement. This approach enabled comprehensive analysis while maintaining analytical rigor through continuous human oversight and validation (Mosqueira-Rey et al., 2023).

The analytical process comprised three sequential steps [1]: classification of hospitals by implementation phases using the EPIS framework and implementation outcome through researcher-led analysis [2], systematic LLM-assisted coding of interview transcripts and longitudinal analysis per hospital using the COM-B model [3], integrated cross-hospital thematic synthesis examining barriers and facilitators from three complementary analytical perspectives: temporal development per EPIS phase, comparison by implementation outcome, and a cross-comparison matrix combining both dimensions. Each step is detailed below, including data sources, analytical procedures, the role of LLM where applicable, quality assurance measures and human verification.

Step 1: Implementation Phase Classification and Hospital Categorization

EPIS framework application

The Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework (Aarons et al., 2011) was used as the theoretical foundation for categorizing implementation phases. This framework distinguishes four key phases that guide the implementation process and identifies common and unique factors across outer context (system level) and inner context (organizational level), as well as factors that bridge these contexts and characteristics of the innovation being implemented.

Phase assignment procedure

The implementation trajectory of each hospital was segmented into phases according to the EPIS framework. Phase assignment for each hospital and time period (six-month intervals) was conducted by YJ, based on multiple data sources: dashboards from Learning Community meetings documenting implementation progress and documentation from meetings with local project leaders (see Table 2). These dashboards tracked implementation progress each half year. However, as sustainment criteria were not systematically captured in the dashboard format, the transition to the sustainment phase was determined based on real time observations in daily practice, specifically focusing on whether PREDOCS had become standardized in routine care delivery. All phase assignments were subsequently verified by RE through discussion until consensus was reached.

Hospital categorization by implementation outcome

Based on their overall implementation trajectory over the four-year study period, we grouped the twelve hospitals into three implementation categories at the end of 2019:

Implementation: Hospitals that successfully progressed through all EPIS phases within the study period, operationalized as having formally started PREDOCS consultations and embedded the intervention in routine care delivery.

Implementation: Hospitals that successfully progressed through all EPIS phases within the study period, operationalized as having formally started PREDOCS consultations and embedded the intervention in routine care delivery.

Delayed implementation: Hospitals that experienced significant delays or setbacks during the implementation process but ultimately reached the Implementation phase before the end of the study period, operationalized as having progressed through Preparation phase milestones but with documented periods of stagnation or interruption before or during the Implementation phase

No implementation: Hospitals that never progressed to the Implementation phase, remaining in the Exploration or Preparation phase and ultimately discontinuing, operationalized as having made no sustained start with PREDOCS consultations.

This categorization was determined by YJ and RE based on analysis of the complete implementation trajectory for each hospital, including dashboard data documenting implementation progress, documentation from Learning Community meetings, and real-time observations in daily practice. Classifications were discussed and agreed upon jointly within the full author team.

This dual classification, combining phase progression according to the EPIS-phases with implementation category (implementation, delayed or non-implementers), enabled subsequent examination of how barriers and facilitators evolved over time within each hospital’s trajectory, as well as how patterns differed between hospitals with varying implementation outcomes.

Step 2: LLM-Assisted Data Analysis Procedure

For the analysis of the interview transcripts, an innovative hybrid approach was employed combining LLM-assisted analysis with systematic human verification and iterative refinement. Using LLM has the advantages of consistency across such a large dataset (Jalali et al., 2024). This approach enabled efficient processing of the extensive qualitative dataset (252 interviews) while maintaining analytical rigor through continuous human oversight [19, 20].

Development of structured prompts

All LLM-assisted analyses in this study utilized structured prompts developed according to the ACTOR framework for qualitative data analysis [21], which specifies five key prompt components: (A) Actor role definition, (C) Context specification, (T) Task description, (O) Output format, and (R) Rules and constraints. For example, the coding prompt defined Claude (Sonnet 4.5, Anthropic) as a qualitative researcher with COM-B expertise (Actor), situated within the PREDOCS implementation context across 12 Dutch cardiothoracic centers from 2016 to 2019 (Context). This was tasked with systematic coding of barriers and facilitators (Task), producing structured tables with hospital, time period, quotation, speaker, code (s), and explanatory notes (Output), while applying specific coding rules such as distinguishing evaluative statements from neutral descriptions and using the most specific available codes (Rules). This framework structure was consistently applied across all analytical prompts to ensure systematic and transparent LLM-assisted analysis (see Table 3).

Development of the COM-B coding framework (prompt 1)

The coding framework was developed through a phased approach combining inductive and deductive elements. Initially, YJ conducted inductive coding of nine interviews from three hospitals to generate preliminary codes. These inductively generated codes were organized according to the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour) [6] and discussed with the research team. Subsequently, deductive coding was independently carried out by three researchers (YJ, 6 hospitals; MP, 3 hospitals; RE, 3 hospitals), with intentional overlap to allow for reliability testing. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The researcher-developed coding scheme was further refined through LLM-assisted empirical enhancement. The initial code tree was implemented within Claude (Sonnet 4.5, Anthropic), which was applied to interview transcripts to identify gaps and optimization opportunities. Through iterative testing on sample transcripts, this process led to refinement of existing codes and the addition of new codes where appropriate. Comprehensive operational definitions were established for all codes to ensure consistent application and minimize overlap between related codes. The final coding prompt (Prompt 1, PREDOCS Interview Coding Manual v2.0; Additional file 4) integrated these operational definitions within the ACTOR framework structure.

Quality assurance

The coding prompt was validated through complete transcript mapping of three pilot interviews (one hospital, one measurement wave, three nurses). Claude Sonnet 4.5 was instructed to categorize each statement in the transcript (e.g., n = 416 statements for the pilot transcript) as [1]: coded (with assigned code) [2], implementation-relevant but not coded, or [3] irrelevant. YJ and MP systematically reviewed these LLM-generated categorizations statement-by-statement against the original transcript to assess coding comprehensiveness and identify systematic patterns in coding errors. This review revealed that ‘irrelevant’ classifications were consistently accurate and ‘implementation-relevant but not coded’ statements were infrequent. The primary source of error was in the ‘coded’ category: LLM-generated codings were sometimes incomplete because Claude selected small text fragments rather than larger contextually relevant passages, and interpretation errors occurred whereby codes were assigned based on surface-level reading rather than factual content. Disagreements between LLM-generated categorizations and human judgment were resolved by YJ and MP jointly reviewing discrepant cases and determining the correct classification. Identified errors were not corrected case-by-case but were used to refine the prompt, with explicit instructions added to select larger text fragments and to apply codes strictly based on literal content, supported by concrete examples within the prompt. Based on these findings, prompt rules were refined and six additional codes were added to the codebook. YJ and MP subsequently re-verified the refined prompt through complete transcript mapping of the same three interviews, confirming satisfactory coding accuracy and consistency.

Longitudinal analysis per hospital (prompt 2)

A comprehensive longitudinal analysis prompt was developed that integrated the complete COM-B codebook (Prompt 1) with structured instructions for time-series analysis (Prompt 2, Additional file 5). This prompt enabled simultaneous coding and longitudinal pattern analysis within each hospital’s implementation trajectory.

For each hospital, all interview transcripts across eight time-periods (2016–2019, spring and autumn of each year) were analyzed together. Transcripts were organized per six-month period (typically 2–4 interviews per period) and submitted as raw data. The analysis followed a structured four-step procedure [1]: period-specific coding of each interview independently according to the COM-B framework [2]; systematic cross-period comparison of code presence within and across time frames [3]; identification of temporal evolution patterns (e.g. persistent barriers, emerging facilitators, barrier-to-facilitator transitions); and [4] synthesis documenting critical turning points and the overall implementation trajectory. This process was conducted separately for each of the 12 hospitals, resulting in 12 comprehensive longitudinal reports.

Quality assurance

Each LLM-generated longitudinal analysis was reviewed by YJ to verify overall plausibility and completeness. This review assessed whether [1]: all time periods were represented in the analysis [2], identified patterns aligned with the researcher’s overall understanding of each hospital’s implementation trajectory, and [3] the synthesis provided a coherent narrative of implementation development. In addition, RE systematically reviewed the longitudinal output of one hospital, assessing consistency between quotes, codes, and explanatory notes against the source data, and confirming that cited implementation factors were accurately represented. MP independently reviewed longitudinal outputs to assess overall analytical quality. Based on these reviews, targeted prompt refinements were made and analyses were re-run where necessary. Where patterns appeared inconsistent or conclusions required verification, relevant coding series were checked against original transcripts on a spot-check basis. This layered quality assurance built on the detailed transcript-level validation already conducted during Prompt 1 development, in which complete transcript mapping had confirmed that relevant implementation factors were consistently and accurately extracted.

Step 3: Integrated Cross-Hospital Thematic Synthesis

Development of integrated thematization (prompt 3)

An integrated thematization prompt was developed that combined the longitudinal hospital analyses (Step 2) with the EPIS phase classifications and implementation outcome categories (Step 1) to enable multi-perspective thematic analysis (Prompt 3, Additional file 6). This prompt structured the analysis according to the ACTOR framework and specified three complementary analytical perspectives examining the same data through different lenses: temporal development per EPIS phase (Additional file 7), comparison by implementation outcome (Additional file 8), and a cross-comparison matrix combining both dimensions (Table 5).

The prompt employed a hybrid thematization approach that integrated top-down and bottom-up coding strategies. The top-down component organized all themes within the three COM-B domains (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation), providing theoretical structure. The bottom-up component identified substantive themes within each COM-B domain based on patterns emerging from the actual codes in the data (e.g., “Training and knowledge development” within Capability, “Personnel capacity and continuity” within Opportunity). This approach ensured both theoretical grounding and empirical sensitivity to the implementation contexts.

Input and analytical process

The 12 longitudinal hospital analyses were submitted to Claude for cross-hospital thematic synthesis. The LLM was instructed to identify substantive themes by clustering related codes within COM-B domains and to examine these themes from three complementary analytical perspectives. For each perspective, the analysis generated structured tables and narrative summaries presenting identified themes, their constituent codes, frequencies across hospitals, and representative quotes.

Quality assurance: human verification of thematization

To validate the LLM-generated themes, a systematic human verification procedure was conducted. YJ, RE, and MP independently created themes based on consolidated barrier and facilitator data from two EPIS phases (Preparation and Implementation). Specifically, all barriers from Preparation phase and all facilitators from Implementation phase were extracted across all hospitals and provided to each researcher. At this stage, LLM-generated themes were not shared with the researchers. The three researchers independently grouped codes into themes and labeled these themes. Subsequently, the independently generated thematizations were compared and discrepancies discussed until consensus was reached.

The human-generated themes were then compared with LLM-generated themes to assess whether [1]: the LLM identified all relevant themes that human coders identified (completeness), and [2] the LLM correctly grouped codes into appropriate themes (accuracy). Where the LLM missed themes or misclassified codes, the prompt was refined and the analysis re-run to improve thematization quality. Where LLM outputs showed a pragmatic, efficiency-focused approach to the extensive dataset (e.g., remaining at code level rather than thematizing, data extraction errors), prompts were iteratively refined and outputs systematically re-verified against source data until achieving the desired analytical depth.

Results

Sample characteristics

The PREDOCS implementation study generated a comprehensive longitudinal dataset of 252 interviews conducted half-yearly over a four-year period (Spring 2016 to Autumn 2019) across all 12 participating Dutch cardiothoracic centers, covering eight measurement moments. Each hospital contributed an average of 21 interviews (range: 15–26), with interview counts per measurement moment ranging from 27 to 36 (see Additional file 3). Ten centers completed full-time series across all eight data collection periods. Hospital H02 provided a nearly complete series covering seven periods (missing autumn 2017 data collection). Hospital H08 contributed data for five of eight time periods. Data collection was incomplete due to unavailable student transcripts (autumn 2016) and project discontinuation with restricted hospital access (autumn 2018, autumn 2019). Overview of implementation trajectories

Based on longitudinal observation of PREDOCS consultation delivery and sustainability across the study period, hospitals were categorized into three implementation trajectories: implementation (n = 4: H04, H05, H06, H11), delayed implementation (n = 5: H01, H02, H03, H09, H10), and no implementation (n = 3: H07, H08, H12).

Hospital trajectories through EPIS phases diverged substantially over the four-year observation period (Fig. 2). ‘Implementation’ hospitals demonstrated rapid progression, initiating PREDOCS consultations within 6–12 months and achieving sustained integration into standard workflows by 2017–2018. Delayed implementation hospitals showed slower, more variable trajectories. No implementation hospitals remained in preparation and implementation phases throughout the study period; despite initial engagement, these hospitals never established routine consultation delivery.

Evolution of barriers and facilitators across EPIS phases

Exploration phase

Analysis of the Exploration phase reveals three patterns in the distribution of barriers and facilitators across implementation groups. All reported barriers are located in the Opportunity domain (time pressure, workload, patient population suitability), with the exception of one Capability barrier in the No Implementation group. In contrast, nearly all facilitators are located in the Motivation domain, particularly ‘Belief in intervention and prevention’ which is reported by all three implementation groups. “It’s a valuable addition to the existing care, especially since it specifically targets older adults. For nurses, it also adds value because when a patient is screened beforehand, you prevent complications, which reduces the workload.” (Nurse H07, no implementation group). The No Implementation group is the only group reporting a Capability barrier: ‘Knowledge and clarity about intervention’, mentioned by all three hospitals in this group (3/3).

Preparation phase

Analysis of the Preparation phase reveals that no group reports facilitators within the Capability domain. As in the Exploration phase, all barriers remain concentrated in the Opportunity domain, with universal reporting of staff shortage and turnover, time pressure and workload across all three implementation groups. “Staff turnover in the cardiothoracic surgery nursing department is very high, people leave very quickly” (Operational manager H08, No Implementation group). Capability barriers concern requests for training (all groups) and knowledge gaps (only Delayed and No Implementation groups). The Motivation domain shows predominantly facilitators, particularly ‘Belief in intervention and prevention’ reported by all groups. Resistance is only mentioned by the Delayed and No Implementation groups. A crucial distinction is that the No Implementation group reports exclusively Opportunity barriers, without any Opportunity facilitators. The Implementation and Delayed groups, in contrast, also report Opportunity facilitators, particularly regarding integration of the intervention into existing work processes. “We are almost ready … The consultation hours are now being made official, we will start with 4 afternoons” (Project leader H06, Implementation group). The No Implementation group additionally reports unique barriers such as deficient information technology (IT) infrastructure and need for external guidance during execution.

Implementation

Analysis of the Implementation phase reveals a notable shift in the Capability domain within the Implementation group: alongside the persistent request for training (3/4), they now also report availability of training (2/4) and experienced, competent staff (3/4). “We know what we need to do. All conditions are well arranged and we see effect” (Nurse H04, Implementation group). This shift from barrier to facilitator is absent in the Delayed group, where exclusively Capability barriers are reported. The Motivation domain shows only facilitators in the Implementation group, with universal reporting of belief in the intervention (4/4) and positive patient feedback (4/4). “Patients are very enthusiastic. They appreciate the preparation” (Project leader H11, Implementation group). Notably, positive patient feedback is also reported by the Delayed group (3/5) but is completely absent in the No Implementation group, where the intervention is not actually being delivered. Collegial support emerges as a differentiating element: the Implementation group reports support from colleagues and learning from each other as facilitators (both 2/4), while both the Delayed (2/5) and No Implementation groups (2/3) report lack of collegial support as a barrier. “What you notice is that we are trying to get the department on board. And I notice there is still some resistance” (Project leader H10, Delayed group).“Opportunity barriers remain persistent across all groups, staff shortage, time pressure, and logistical challenges. Patient population suitability affects all groups (2/4 Implementation, 3/5 Delayed, 2/3 No Implementation), “Well now the barrier is really the short waiting list, which means we have very few consultations” (Project leader H03, Delayed group). These barriers are compensated in the Implementation group by Opportunity facilitators related to integration and collegial support. The No Implementation group reports notably few themes: they possess competent staff (2/3) and maintain belief in the intervention (2/3), but Opportunity barriers persist without compensating facilitators.

Sustainment

Analysis of the Sustainment phase reveals a sustained implementation pattern in which structural barriers persist but are compensated by a broad range of facilitators. In the Capability domain, three of the four hospitals report experienced, competent staff and ongoing training, although two hospitals still mention knowledge gaps, an indication that capability development remains a continuous process. The most notable pattern is in the Opportunity domain: whereas the Implementation phase showed a mixed picture of barriers and emerging facilitators, all four hospitals now report good integration of the intervention, and three of the four mention active management support, collegial support and adequate structural resources. Simultaneously, the same structural barriers reported in earlier phases persist: staff shortage (3/4), time pressure (2/4) and patient population suitability (2/4). The crucial difference lies not in the absence of barriers, but in the presence of compensating structural facilitators. The Motivation domain shows complete unanimity: all four hospitals report belief in the intervention, team enthusiasm and positive patient feedback (all 4/4), “Everyone is indeed aware of the positive outcomes of PREDOCS” (Manager H04, Implementation group) and “I really see the added value of it, patients also say this, almost exclusively positive stories” (Nurse H06, Implementation group),a level of motivational saturation not achieved in any previous phase.

Discontinuation

Analysis of the Discontinuation phase reveals a distinct organizational pattern. In the Capability domain, no facilitators are reported across any of the discontinued hospitals, an absence contrasting with the sustained capability development seen in the Implementation group. Discontinued hospitals report lack of project leadership as a defining characteristic,: “There has been no leader throughout the entire project” (Team leader H08, No Implementation group), while another respondent articulated the structural consequence: “If you are managing a project leader … you are being paid for that, and you should do your best for it” (Nurse specialist H08, No Implementation group). In the Opportunity domain, all three hospitals universally report staff shortage and turnover, competing priorities, and time pressure, the same barriers present in earlier phases, but now without any compensating facilitators. The structural infrastructure shows marked deterioration: “The senior outpatient clinic is gone, so there is simply no senior outpatient clinic anymore” (Team leader H08, No Implementation group), while the fundamental planning constraints persist: “We have no waiting lists anymore. We have no … the OR program for next week is not even filled” (Team leader H08, No Implementation group). In the Motivation domain, a notable pattern emerges: belief in the intervention actually intensifies in this phase, with all three hospitals reporting conviction of the intervention’s value, “I believe in the content, but the conditions were not there” (Manager H08, No Implementation group) and “Wonderful … I think it is an excellent project and the patients need it” (PREDOCS nurse H12, No Implementation group). Simultaneously, resistance and frustration emerge that were absent in previous phases: “I am not going to beg to be allowed to do a project … Either they support it or they don’t” (PREDOCS nurse H12, No Implementation group). Discontinued hospitals also report organizational rather than motivational reasons for cessation: “We stopped because we could not produce results on paper to show” (PREDOCS nurse H07, No Implementation group). This pattern indicates that in the Discontinuation phase, motivation and belief in the intervention remain intact but no longer align with organizational infrastructure, a disconnect reflected in the resistance and frustration characteristic of this phase.

Discussion

This longitudinal study examined PREDOCS implementation across 12 Dutch cardiothoracic centers over four years, generating 252 interviews across eight measurement waves. Hospitals were categorized into three implementation trajectories: Implementation (n = 4), Delayed (n = 5), and No Implementation (n = 3), which diverged substantially over the observation period. Across all EPIS phases, barriers were concentrated predominantly in the COM-B Opportunity domain (organizational structures, staffing conditions, and management support) while facilitators were concentrated in the Motivation domain. Opportunity barriers persisted across all groups and all phases, including Sustainment, but were compensated in the Implementation group by a broad range of structural facilitators that emerged progressively across phases. The No Implementation group, by contrast, reported fundamental knowledge gaps in the Exploration phase and no Opportunity facilitators in the Preparation phase, and never developed the organizational infrastructure necessary to sustain routine consultation delivery. In the Discontinuation phase, motivation and belief in the intervention intensified among No Implementation hospitals even as organizational infrastructure deteriorated.

We interpret these findings as reflecting three overarching patterns. First, motivation to implement PREDOCS was universally high across all outcome groups and throughout all EPIS phases, including among hospitals that ultimately discontinued the intervention. Although motivational readiness appears to be a prerequisite for engaging in an implementation process, it proved insufficient on its own: the critical differentiator was not motivation level but the presence or absence of opportunity-domain facilitators, specifically organizational structures, resources, and management support. Second, fundamental knowledge gaps during the Exploration and Preparation phases functioned as early signals of implementation failure: all three No Implementation hospitals reported these gaps in the Exploration phase, while the Implementation group reported only training requests, reflecting awareness of learning needs rather than foundational uncertainty. Third, implementation success did not reflect the absence of barriers but rather the development of structural facilitators that compensated for ongoing challenges. Staffing shortages, time pressure, and workload constraints persisted across all groups and all phases and were therefore not differentiators in themselves. What differentiated successful hospitals was the emergence of organizational infrastructure that enabled action despite these constraints, whereas all groups, including those that failed to implement, sustained strong motivation throughout.

Our findings align with established implementation research on critical determinant domains. Geerligs and colleagues [22] identified three main categories of barriers and facilitators: system factors, staff factors, and intervention characteristics, which map meaningfully onto the COM-B framework applied in the present study. System factors correspond primarily to the Opportunity domain, staff skills and capabilities to the Capability domain, and staff attitudes and motivation to the Motivation domain. However, most existing research, including Geerligs’ work, captures these factors at single timepoints. The innovation of our longitudinal design lies in revealing how these determinants evolve across implementation phases. Silver and colleagues [23] demonstrated similar temporal dynamics in pediatric clinic implementation, showing that some themes remain stable while others shift substantially over four years. Petersen and colleagues [24] further showed that healthcare professionals’ perceptions of barriers and facilitators change during implementation, with evolving beliefs influencing intervention uptake and sustainability. Our contribution is empirical specification of when and how: knowledge gaps prove critical in early phases, opportunity facilitators must emerge during Preparation for later success, and compensatory mechanisms rather than barrier elimination characterize successful implementation trajectories.

Methodological considerations

Several methodological aspects of this study warrant discussion. The longitudinal design comprising eight measurement waves over four years provided temporal resolution rarely achieved in implementation research, enabling examination of how barriers and facilitators evolve across phases rather than merely exist at a single point in time. Data was generated from 252 interviews spanning multiple professional roles (nurses, project leaders, team leaders, managers, medical staff, and support personnel) offering comprehensive organizational perspective across all twelve hospitals. The three-way outcome categorization into Implementation, Delayed, and No Implementation groups enabled systematic pattern recognition and strengthened the basis for identifying early predictive indicators. The empirical scale and temporal resolution achieved in this study were made possible in part by the hybrid LLM-assisted analytical approach, which enabled systematic processing of 252 interviews across eight measurement waves at a level that would not have been feasible through manual methods alone. The semi-annual interviews served a dual function: evaluation at the general program level while simultaneously providing implementation support. This created opportunities for local tailoring, as hospitals could use insights from evaluation moments to adapt their implementation strategies to their specific organizational context. Rather than viewing this as a limitation, we consider it a design strength reflecting authentic implementation support processes, where monitoring and facilitation are naturally intertwined.

The choice of COM-B as theoretical framework warrants reflection. PREDOCS implementation fundamentally required sustained behavior change among nursing professionals, making COM-B’s explicit focus on behavior change mechanisms a natural fit. In principle, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) might have been considered as an alternative, given its extensive validation and comprehensive determinant specification across 39 constructs and five domains [17]. However, COM-B’s three clearly delineated domains provided coding consistency across eight waves and multiple student interviewers, a practical necessity that CFIR’s 39 constructs might have compromised, while complementing the EPIS framework effectively: EPIS provided temporal structure for when factors matter, COM-B provided a mechanistic structure for why behavior changes or fails to change. This combination proved analytically productive: it enabled the clear identification that motivation remained universally present while opportunity factors differentiated outcomes, a pattern that might have been obscured within more granular construct specifications. Future studies may nonetheless consider CFIR’s application where coding resources and team expertise allow for its fuller deployment.

This study employed a hybrid methodology combining LLM-assisted analysis with human oversight for methodologic rigor, to process 252 interviews across eight measurement waves. The LLM-driven codebook development identified six additional codes not detected in the original manual coding, demonstrating LLM’s value in pattern recognition within large qualitative datasets. However, developing effective analysis prompts was not without challenges. Initial analyses produced overly generic themes lacking sufficient depth, and when confronted with the extensive dataset of 831 codes, the LLM tended toward efficient solutions that compromised analytical depth, remaining at the code level rather than achieving meaningful thematization. Returning to these 831 codes as primary source data proved essential to deepen and verify the analysis at each iterative step. Multiple iterations were required to refine instructions regarding theme formulation, hierarchical structure, and strict distinctions between COM-B domains. A critical challenge was that the LLM made data extraction errors whereby codes were not correctly linked to EPIS phases and hospitals, necessitating multiple reanalysis. Through continuous output verification and iterative prompt refinement, a methodology was ultimately developed whereby each EPIS phase was analyzed separately with systematic verification against source data, resulting in prompt version 5.0 that produced narrative, contextually rich thematic analyses with clear distinctions between implementation outcome groups. This iterative process illustrates a key insight emerging in recent methodological literature: the use of LLM in qualitative analysis requires careful prompt design, iterative refinement, and continuous human-in-the-loop verification as an essential condition for analytic rigor [25, 26].

This approach warrants reflection considering recent debates about the role of LLM in qualitative research. Jowsey and colleagues [27] argue that generative AI should be rejected for reflexive qualitative approaches, on the grounds that AI is incapable of meaning-making and that qualitative research should remain a distinctly human practice. We partially concur: these concerns are valid for reflexive, phenomenological, and interpretive approaches where subjective interpretation of lived experience is central. Our study, however, employed structured, systematic comparative analysis across implementation sites and temporal phases, fundamentally different from reflexive meaning-making of individual narratives. In such contexts, AI is increasingly conceptualized as a research assistant supporting analytical workflows rather than replacing human judgment [21, 26]. Empirical comparisons further suggest that AI can increase efficiency in large-scale qualitative analysis while still requiring human verification to ensure accuracy and interpretive validity [20]. Our hybrid approach demonstrates that AI can function as a tool, analogous to software packages in quantitative research, rather than as a replacement for human analytical judgment, provided its use is methodologically congruent with research aims and rigorously supervised through continuous human-in-the-loop verification [28, 29].

With regard to the interviews, two issues are noteworthy. First, not all professional groups were represented across all hospitals: medical staff were interviewed in 2 of 12 hospitals, which may have introduced a systematic gap in perspective given the potential role of physician support in shaping organizational implementation conditions. Second, the use of multiple student interviewers across four years introduces potential variability in interview quality, despite standardized training, structured interview guides, and experienced researcher supervision. Although these two factors may have influenced data richness, they do not undermine the consistency of the patterns identified across hospitals and outcome groups.

A methodological choice with implications for interpretation concerns the exclusion of themes reported by only a single hospital within an outcome group. While this threshold ensured that presented patterns reflect collective rather than isolated, hospital-specific experiences, it carries a potential limitation: themes that recurred across multiple EPIS phases but always within a single hospital were systematically excluded. Two themes in particular warrant mentioning in this regard. Lack of project leadership control, a factor identified in implementation literature as central to creating the organizational conditions necessary for sustained behavior change [30], emerged as a potentially decisive case-level factor. Similarly, the degree to which hospitals experienced national support as adequate varied despite all hospitals receiving identical support, consistent with Fixsen’s [31] observation that the effectiveness of external implementation support depends on how organizations integrate it within their own context. These case-specific factors may represent tipping points in individual implementation trajectories that group-level pattern analysis cannot capture. A secondary analysis of n = 1 themes is presented in Additional file 9.

Findings are grounded in the context of cardiothoracic surgery centers within the Dutch healthcare system, which limits direct generalizability to other healthcare settings for implementation. However, the identified mechanisms, particularly the role of organizational context in differentiating outcomes despite uniform external support and the temporal evolution of implementation determinants, are likely relevant beyond this specific setting and may apply more broadly to the implementation of complex healthcare interventions requiring sustained behavior change across multiple organizational levels.

Implications and future recommendations

Motivational readiness is of key importance, but alone it is insufficient as an indicator of implementation potential: all twelve hospitals, including those that never established routine delivery, demonstrated consistently high motivation throughout. What ultimately differentiated outcomes was the presence of organizational infrastructure that enabled motivated professionals to act. Implementation programs should therefore set realistic expectations: barriers such as staffing shortages and time pressure do not disappear over time, and success depends not on eliminating these barriers but on developing compensatory facilitators that enable action despite persistent constraints. The nature of required support also shifts across phases, from building knowledge and organizational conditions early, to reinforcing structural facilitators and sustaining momentum later, and support strategies should be tailored accordingly.

The finding that all twelve hospitals received identical national support yet diverged substantially in outcomes underscores the centrality of local organizational context in implementation. While the CFIR framework [17] positions local context as a moderating factor, our findings suggest that when external support is held constant, local context may function as the primary enabling or disabling condition for implementation success.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs capable of detecting temporal dynamics in implementation determinants, as single-timepoint assessments cannot capture the early warning signals this study has shown to be predictive of later outcomes. Additionally, as LLM-assisted analysis tools continue to develop, hybrid approaches combining LLM pattern recognition with human-in-the-loop interpretive oversight offer promising methodological possibilities for implementation research at scale.

Conclusion

This study examined how implementation determinants evolved across four years and eight measurement waves in twelve Dutch cardiothoracic centers implementing the PREDOCS nursing intervention. The findings demonstrate that although sufficient motivation is a basic prerequisite for embarking on an implementation process, differential implementation outcomes cannot be explained by motivational differences: motivation was universally high across all hospital groups, including those that ultimately discontinued the intervention. What distinguished successful from unsuccessful implementation was the presence or absence of organizational infrastructure, that is, the structural and relational conditions that enable motivated professionals to act on their intentions as evident compensating factors. Early knowledge gaps and the absence of opportunity facilitators in the Exploration and Preparation phases functioned as early signals of later failure, while successful hospitals developed compensatory facilitators rather than eliminating persistent barriers. These findings underscore that implementation determinants are not static but evolve across phases in ways that have direct consequences for outcomes. Anticipating and responding to this temporal dynamic requires longitudinal monitoring as a standard component of implementation practice and research, rather than a one-time assessment at a single point in time.
