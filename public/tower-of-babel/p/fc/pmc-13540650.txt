Endoscopic closure of esophageal perforation in variceal hemorrhage
Authors: Zeid Jarrar, William King, Lehar Khanna, Hannah Zuercher, Hussam Freihat, Bashar Qumseya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsEndoscopy has emerged as a safe and effective treatment for esophageal perforation, but varices have traditionally been considered a contraindication. We aimed to treat an esophageal perforation with endoluminal suturing during an active variceal bleed.MethodsA 55-year-old woman with (previously) compensated alcohol-associated cirrhosis presented with hematemesis, hypotension, and tachycardia. Although endoscopy was being arranged, the care team attempted to place a Sengstaken-Blakemore tube. The gastric balloon was inadvertently inflated in the esophagus; malposition was confirmed on chest radiograph. Bedside EGD revealed esophageal varices and a briskly bleeding, large, linear perforation extending from 24 to 36 cm from the incisors. Repeat CT was consistent with hemopneumomediastinum. She was brought to the interventional endoscopy suite, where an over-the-scope endoscopic suturing device was used both to close the perforation and to achieve hemostasis. No esophageal stent was placed. The esophagus was filled with water-soluble contrast, and a CT scan revealed no extraluminal contrast.ResultsThe patient was weaned off vasopressors and extubated. Her diet was advanced, and she had no residual dysphagia.ConclusionsEndoscopic suturing may be considered as salvage therapy for esophageal perforation in selected patients who are not candidates for surgery.

Introduction

Esophageal perforation is rare. The estimated incidence ranges from 0.3 to 1.1 per 100,000 patient-years.1, 2, 3 It is most commonly iatrogenic, with an estimated 47% of cases occurring during a medical procedure.4 Leaking of esophageal contents can rapidly precipitate mediastinitis and death, resulting in a mortality rate as high as 40%.4, 5, 6, 7

Esophageal rupture was historically managed surgically. Endoscopic therapy, typically with self-expandable endoluminal stents, has emerged as an alternative for patients with smaller defects or for poor surgical candidates.6,8

Case description

A 55-year-old woman presented with large-volume hematemesis. Comorbidities included (previously) compensated alcohol-associated cirrhosis, multifocal dental disease, and unhealthy use of alcohol and cocaine. EGD performed 9 months earlier for melena had revealed portal hypertensive gastropathy but no varices.

She then developed massive hematemesis, hypotension, and tachycardia. She was given ceftriaxone, intravenous pantoprazole, and infusions of octreotide and norepinephrine. After endotracheal intubation, the care team attempted to place a Sengstaken-Blakemore tube, which coiled in the distal esophagus. On the first attempt, the gastric balloon was inadvertently inflated in the esophagus; malposition was confirmed on chest radiograph.

Bedside EGD revealed esophageal varices and a briskly bleeding, large, linear perforation extending from 24 to 36 cm from the incisors. Repeat CT was consistent with hemopneumomediastinum.

She was brought urgently to the interventional endoscopy suite. In the meantime, the critical care and anesthesia teams administered infusions of vasopressin, octreotide, and norepinephrine and transfused 9 units of packed red blood cells, 11 units of platelets, and 2 units of fresh frozen plasma. By the time of the endoscopy, the brisk bleeding had partially subsided (Figs. 1 and 2). Fortunately, the extraluminal air was confined to the mediastinum, and the patient never developed a pneumothorax, allowing for safe insufflation. Using an endoscopic suturing device loaded with 2.0-polypropylene suture, we placed 2 interrupted sutures with a total of 20 bites in an interrupted manner, extending from the distal esophagus toward the proximal esophagus (Figs. 3 and 4; Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org). After cinching the suture, there was excellent tissue approximation and no residual bleeding (Figs. 5 and 6). We therefore elected not to place an endoluminal stent. Furthermore, an esophageal stent would have put tension on the sutures. The esophagus was filled with water-soluble contrast, and a CT scan revealed no extraluminal contrast. The procedure took a total of 44 minutes.

Within 24 hours after the procedure, the patient was weaned off vasopressors and extubated. She spent 2 weeks on total parenteral nutrition. A repeat CT esophagram 2 weeks after the procedure again showed no evidence of contrast extravasation. Her diet was advanced, and she tolerated soft foods. She was discharged on hospital day 18.

She had no residual dysphagia. She had another EGD after 3 months of alcohol cessation and β-blockade. Her varices had regressed to grade 1, and there was no esophageal stenosis.
