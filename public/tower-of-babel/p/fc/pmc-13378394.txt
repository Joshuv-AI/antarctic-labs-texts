Translational resistance to amino acid starvation in breast cancer cells revealed by ribosome profiling
Authors: Fajin Li, Siqiong Zhang, Haoran Duan, Dieter A. Wolf, Xuerui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTranslation elongation is highly sensitive to amino acid availability, with deprivation causing ribosome pausing at cognate codons, suppression of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTORC1) signaling pathway, and GCN2-mediated phosphorylation of eIF2α. However, cell-type heterogeneity in these responses remains unclear. Integrating ribosome profiling datasets across over ten human cell lines and multiple starvation conditions, we uncover translational resistance in breast cancer cells specifically under leucine and glutamine deprivation. Unlike non-breast cancer cells, breast cancer cells maintain ribosome occupancy on mRNAs with a 5′ terminal oligopyrimidine tract (5′TOP mRNAs), indicating sustained mTORC1 activity, and exhibit attenuated codon-specific pausing. GCN2-eIF2α pathway activation varies among breast cancer lines. Downregulation of the cystine/glutamate transporter SLC7A11 correlates with this resistance, and its overexpression restores sensitivity by reducing S6K phosphorylation while enhancing eIF2α phosphorylation. Our findings reveal that breast cancer cells adaptively reshape translation regulation to withstand amino acid starvation, highlighting a potential metabolic vulnerability.

Introduction

Translation elongation is a critical and coordinated process in protein synthesis, mediated by the dynamic interplay of elongation factors, transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and ribosomes.1 Although ribosome translocation generally proceeds codon by codon in a processive manner, the elongation rate is highly heterogeneous, varying substantially across different mRNAs, within distinct regions of the same transcript, and under diverse physiological conditions.2,3 This regulatory complexity arises from both intrinsic and extrinsic determinants. Key intrinsic features, such as codon usage, mRNA secondary structure, polyproline motifs, and clusters of positively charged amino acids, have been established as important modulators of elongation dynamics.3,4,5,6,7,8 Furthermore, the availability of cognate tRNAs, the fidelity of co-translational folding, and the propensity for nascent chain misfolding also contribute to localized variations in elongation speed.3,9,10,11 Extrinsic factors, including nutrient availability, cellular stress, and pharmacological interventions, can also inhibit translation elongation and reduce protein output.9,10,12,13,14 For example, under stress conditions such as heat shock or oxidative stress, ribosomes frequently arrest within the first 50–100 codons downstream of the start codon, a region termed the ribosome “ramp,” which suggests a widespread slowdown of early elongation under these conditions.12,15 Some recent studies including ours have further indicated that the dysregulation of translation factors can, in itself, induce ribosome pausing during early elongation and attenuate protein synthesis.5,16,17,18,19,20,21 Importantly, impaired elongation has been implicated in a spectrum of human pathologies, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders, highlighting its broad physiological significance.22,23 Thus, understanding the precise mechanisms that regulate translation elongation remains a central challenge in the field.

Amino acids are indispensable to numerous processes essential for cell proliferation and homeostasis, serving as key substrates for protein synthesis and as regulators of metabolic reprogramming.24 Deprivation of essential amino acids, a condition frequently encountered in certain physiological and pathological contexts, disrupts the production of functional proteins and consequently suppresses cell growth and proliferation.25,26,27 These observations underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting amino acid metabolism in cancer.24,28,29,30,31,32 Currently, amino acid starvation is known to modulate mRNA translation and protein synthesis primarily through three mechanisms: (1) suppression of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTORC1) signaling pathway,33,34,35 (2) activation of the GCN2-eIF2α stress response,36,37,38 and (3) restriction of mRNA translation elongation rates.39,40,41 Several studies have demonstrated that amino acid starvation can simultaneously impact mRNA translation through both the mTORC1 and GCN2-eIF2α pathways, thereby modulating cellular homeostasis under conditions of amino acid starvation.36,42 It has been reported that amino acid starvation, like other cellular stresses, can inhibit translation elongation, causing a shift of ribosomes toward the 5′ end of the coding sequence (CDS),20,43 despite the fact that these effects may be artifacts inflicted by cycloheximide (CHX).15 Nevertheless, local ribosome pausing and collisions at cognate codons have been widely observed under deprivation of various amino acids, such as leucine (Leu), arginine (Arg), glutamine (Gln), glycine/serine (Gly/Ser), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp).23,39,41,43,44 Notably, such codon-specific pausing has also been documented in multiple human cancer models, such as clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), breast cancer, melanoma, and pancreatic cancer.23,40,41,44 Intriguingly, beyond pausing at codons corresponding to the deprived amino acid, starvation can induce marked ribosome accumulation immediately downstream of these codons, forming a distinct ribosome “bump.” For example, in melanoma cells, deprivation of Trp or Tyr leads to a pronounced ribosome bump downstream of Trp (W) or Tyr (Y) codons, respectively.44 Such collisions increase the frequency of translational frameshifting, leading to the production of aberrant nascent peptides, which can elicit T cell-mediated immune responses.44 Collectively, these findings underscore the pathophysiological significance of translation elongation inhibition during amino acid stress and its relevance to human disease. These findings indicate that although the effects of translation regulation under amino acid starvation have received considerable attention, systematic comparisons of the sensitivity and heterogeneity of these pathways to different amino acid deficiencies across diverse cellular models remain scarce.

In this study, we performed an integrated analysis of publicly available ribosome profiling datasets spanning over 10 human cell lines, encompassing 10+ distinct amino acid deprivation conditions and 9 time points, to systematically reassess translational regulation during amino acid starvation. Our meta-analysis revealed that breast cancer cells maintained mTORC1 activity under Leu and Gln deprivation, in clear contrast to non-breast cancer cells, and exhibited heterogeneous activation of the GCN2-eIF2α pathway. Notably, Leu or Gln starvation induced a marked ribosome accumulation at the 5′ CDS in basal-like breast cancer cell lines (Hs578t and MDA-MB-231), but not in luminal (MCF7 and T47D) or non-breast cancer cells (HEK293T or MCF10A). Moreover, codon-specific ribosome pausing was broadly attenuated across all breast cancer models under these nutrient stresses. Further analysis revealed that the cystine-glutamate transporter SLC7A11 was consistently downregulated in breast cancer cells during Leu or Gln starvation, while it was upregulated in most non-breast cancer lines. This pattern suggests a role for SLC7A11 in shaping the adaptive response of breast cancer cells to nutrient limitation. Biochemical assays confirmed that SLC7A11 overexpression restored sensitivity to Gln starvation in breast cancer cells. Together, these results demonstrate that breast cancer cells display enhanced translational resistance to amino acid deprivation and identify SLC7A11 as a key regulator of cellular responsiveness to nutrient stress.

Results

Differential translational response to amino acid starvation in breast cancer cell lines

To systematically evaluate the effects of translation regulation under various amino acid starvation across multiple cell lines, we compiled a dataset of more than 150 ribosome profiling samples from 18 studies, spanning 16 cell lines, 10+ amino acids, and 9 time points20,23,39,40,41,44,45,46,47,48,49 (Figure S1A; Table S1). Additionally, we incorporated ribosome profiling data from serum starvation conditions to enable comparison with other forms of nutrient stress.23,49,50 Given the established role of amino acid starvation in inhibiting mTORC1 and activating the GCN2-eIF2α pathway,34,35,36,37 we included relevant ribosome profiling datasets as positive controls. These include samples treated with mTORC1 and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway inhibitors (PP242, Torin-1, rapamycin, and MNKi) and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress inducers (tunicamycin, Tm).51,52,53,54,55,56 The transcription factor MYC, which is often dysregulated in tumors, is a known regulator of both essential and non-essential amino acid metabolism.30 Accordingly, a ribosome profiling dataset from MYC-overexpressing U2OS cells was incorporated (Figure S1A). This comprehensive dataset then underwent rigorous quality control to ensure the overall high quality and utility of these datasets for investigating translation regulation under amino acid starvation, including normal length distribution of ribosome-protected fragments (RPFs), obvious 3-nt periodicity, and ribosome enrichment at the main open reading frame (ORF) (Figures S1B–S1E).

To evaluate translational responses in the absence of matched RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data (required to calculate translation efficiency), we used total ribosome density as a proxy for protein synthesis rate, as previously validated.20,57 We acknowledged that ribosome occupancy changes may reflect a combination of transcriptional and translational regulation. However, in datasets with matched RNA-seq, a previous study has reported minimal changes in mRNA abundance under acute amino acid starvation.47 Principal-component analysis (PCA) was performed on both gene-level and longest transcript-level ribosome densities to assess potential batch effects across datasets (Figures S2A and S2B). The results revealed no obvious clustering by dataset, indicating the absence of significant batch effects across treatments (Figures S2A and S2B). The gene-level and transcript-level changes in ribosome densities are highly correlated (0.835 on average) (Figures S2C and S2D). As subsequent analyses are transcript-based and transcript-level log2 fold changes (log2FC) showed no batch effects (Figure S2E), we used the longest transcript-level ribosome density in all downstream analyses unless otherwise noted. Notably, ribosome densities under Leu and Gln starvation were more highly correlated across four breast cancer cell lines than in non-breast cancer cells (Figure S2D), suggesting that breast cancer cells may exhibit a distinct translational profile under amino acid starvation.

To investigate the heterogeneity of translational profiles under Leu and Gln starvation across distinct cell types, we performed differential expression analysis within each individual ribosome profiling dataset, using DESeq258 for datasets with biological replicates and Gfold59 for those without replicates (Figure S3A). This within-study analytical strategy ensures that comparisons are made under consistent experimental conditions and avoids confounding batch effects across datasets. Strikingly, the results showed that Leu starvation (3, 6, and 12 h) suppressed the ribosome occupancy of mRNAs related to cytoplasmic translation and ribosomes in non-breast cancer cells, whereas it enhanced their ribosome loading in SUM159PT breast cancer cells (Figures 1A, S4B, and S4C). In contrast, mRNAs associated with nutrient stress showed increased ribosome occupancy in non-breast cancer cells but decreased in breast cancer cells (Figure 1A). These results may indicate that Leu starvation can induce nutrient stress in non-breast cancer cells but maintain normal cellular homeostasis in breast cancer cells. Interestingly, distinct from that under Leu starvation, the stress response genes were activated in most cell lines under Gln starvation, showing few variances between breast cancer cells and non-breast cancers (Figure 2B). However, similar to Leu starvation, ribosome and translation-related mRNAs were also decreased under Gln starvation in non-breast cancer cells such as HeLa and MCF10A, but either unchanged or even increased in breast cancer cells except for Hs578t cells (Figure 2B). In contrast, serum starvation seemed to consistently inhibit those gene set in both breast cancer cells and non-breast cancer cells (Figure 2C). Discordant mTORC1 and GCN2-eIF2α activities in breast cancer cells under Leu and Gln starvation

It is well-established that mRNAs encoding cytoplasmic translation and ribosomal proteins, which are characteristically enriched with a 5′-terminal oligopyrimidine (TOP) motif,60,61,62,63 are downregulated upon mTORC1 pathway inhibition.53,64 We therefore hypothesized that mTORC1 signaling remains functional in breast cancer cells under Leu or Gln starvation, but not in non-breast cancer cells. To evaluate mTORC1 activity, we compiled 143 TOP and TOP-like mRNAs previously identified as mTORC1 targets and assessed their ribosome occupancy levels (Figure S4A).53,64 Notably, in breast cancer cells, correlation analysis based on the log2 fold change (log2FC) of these mRNAs revealed that several conditions clustered together under Leu, Gln, serum, serine, and glycine starvation (Figure S4B). Further analysis confirmed markedly distinct expression patterns of TOP and TOP-like mRNAs between breast cancer and non-breast cancer cells across all amino acid starvation conditions (Figures 2A and S4C). Specifically, Leu or Gln deprivation increased ribosome loading of TOP and TOP-like mRNAs in five breast cancer cell lines, whereas their ribosome occupancy was suppressed in most non-breast cancer cells, similar to the results obtained for mTORC1 inhibitors (Figure 2A). To rigorously assess the translational response of these TOP and TOP-like mRNAs across datasets, we performed a vote-counting meta-analysis.65 The results showed that most TOP and TOP-like mRNAs are significantly downregulated in a large proportion of non-breast cancer cells under amino acid starvation, whereas they are upregulated in breast cancer cells (Figure S4D). Given the established role of mTORC1 in activating TOP mRNA translation, its suppression typically leads to profound translational downregulation of these transcripts, whether by amino acid withdrawal or direct pharmacological inhibition (e.g., rapamycin, PP242, Torin-1).33,34,53,64 Thus, the observed discordance between breast cancer and non-breast cancer cells may highlight a biologically significant divergence.

Interestingly, as a positive reference, serine and glycine starvation triggered the most pronounced increase in ribosome density on TOP and TOP-like mRNAs in pancreatic PATU-8902 cells (Figure 2A). PATU-8902 is an extracellular serine-dependent line, in which serine deprivation markedly impairs cell growth and proliferation.41 Intriguingly, however, phosphorylation of the canonical mTORC1 targets 4EBP and S6K remains unchanged under such conditions.41 The proteomic analysis further revealed a strong correlation between protein abundance and ribosome loading of TOP and TOP-like mRNAs (Figure S4E), supporting the view that mTORC1 activity is maintained or even enhanced in PATU-8902 cells during serine/glycine starvation, which may account for the pronounced increase in ribosome density observed on these transcripts (Figure 2A). The pattern of ribosome occupancy on TOP and TOP-like mRNAs in breast cancer cells under Leu/Gln deprivation closely mirrors that in PATU-8902 cells under serine/glycine starvation (Figure 2), indicating maintenance of the mTORC1 signal activity in these contexts.

The GCN2-eIF2α pathway has also been reported to sense amino acid deprivation and reprogram translation by phosphorylating eIF2α.38 This imposes global translational suppression through impaired initiation complex assembly, while selectively promoting the translation of stress-responsive transcripts such as ATF4 and CHOP.38,56,66 To determine whether amino acid starvation activates the GCN2-eIF2α pathway in breast cancer cells, we evaluated the ribosome occupancy of its downstream targets (Figures 2B and S4F). As a positive control, Tm, which inhibits N-linked glycosylation and induces ER stress, triggered the unfolded protein response and activated the GCN2-eIF2α pathway, leading to increased translation of ATF4, ATF5, CHOP, GADD34, and other ATF4-CHOP pathway targets.56 This effect was effectively reversed by the integrated stress response inhibitor ISRIB (Figure 2B). Consistent with GCN2-eIF2α activation, starvation for multiple amino acids (e.g., Leu, Arg, and Gln) also enhanced the ribosome occupancy of these target mRNAs in both breast cancer and non-breast cancer cells, with the notable exception of MCF7 breast cancer cells under Gln starvation for 48 h (Figure 2B).

In line with our data analysis results, western blotting confirmed that MCF7 cells maintained S6K phosphorylation and showed no increase in eIF2α phosphorylation during prolonged Leu or Gln starvation (Figure 2C), suggesting pronounced resistance to these nutrient stresses. This maintenance of the mTORC1 signaling pathway under conditions that normally suppress it represents what we termed “initiation-level resistance.” In contrast to the aberrant responses observed in breast cancer cells, HEK293T control cells exhibited the canonical integrated stress response: both Leu and Gln starvation consistently suppressed mTORC1 signaling while activating the GCN2-eIF2α pathway (Figure S4G). Together, these results suggested that mTORC1 and GCN2-eIF2α pathways are relatively insensitive to Leu and Gln starvation in MCF7 cells (Figures 2C and S4G).

Heterogeneity of the ribosome accumulation toward the 5′ of the CDS induced by amino acid starvation

The 5′ ribosome ramp represents a global, early elongation defect that affects ribosome distribution broadly across the transcriptome.5,15,20 To determine whether diverse amino acid deprivation conditions similarly trigger a conserved ribosome ramp in human cells, we systematically compared global translation dynamics using a previously established ribosome profiling strategy.20 Specifically, we evaluated ribosome accumulation by calculating the difference in polarity score (Δpolarity) across CDSs. Transcripts exhibiting a negative Δpolarity score were interpreted as exhibiting ribosome accumulation at their 5′ end of CDS. Hierarchical clustering revealed that most amino acid starvation conditions did not provoke a global shift of ribosomes toward the 5′ end of CDSs, although localized 5′ accumulation was detected in a subset of treatments by Δpolarity analysis (Figure 3A). Metagene analysis further indicated that only a limited number of conditions elicited a conspicuous ribosome ramp, with the response being highly dependent on both cell type and the specific amino acid deprived (Figures 3B–3E and S5). Interestingly, Leu starvation did trigger a pronounced ribosome ramp in the basal-like breast cancer cell line SUM159PT, but failed to induce a ramp in non-breast cancer cell lines such as HEK293T under the same condition (Figures 3B, 3C, and S5). Notably, this effect was abolished under acute Leu deprivation for 24 h (Figures 3A and 3B). Similarly, we observed that Gln starvation elicited a robust ramp in basal-like breast cancer cell lines such as Hs578t and MDA-MB-231 cells but did not induce the ramp in MCF10A and luminal breast cancer cells like MCF7 and T47D cells (Figures 3D and 3E). This pattern is consistent with known metabolic differences among breast cancer subtypes: basal-type cell lines such as MDA-MB-231 and Hs578t are highly sensitive to Gln withdrawal, whereas luminal subtypes (e.g., MCF7 and T47D) are much more resistant, partly through sustained inhibitory phosphorylation of the elongation factor eEF2.23 In addition, we also found that no conspicuous ribosome ramp was detected under deprivation of other amino acids, including Arg, cysteine, glycine, Tyr, Trp, or serine (Figures 3A and S5). Attenuated ribosome stalling at cognate codons in breast cancer cells under amino acid starvation

Beyond the induction of a 5′ ribosome ramp, amino acid starvation has been widely reported to provoke pronounced ribosome pausing at codons cognate to the deprived amino acid.23,39,40,41 To systematically evaluate this effect, we computed changes in ribosome density at individual codons before and after starvation (Figures 4 and S6). As expected, the results showed that deprivation of specific amino acids generally induced marked ribosome pausing at their corresponding codons. For example, branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) and cysteine starvation led to pausing at valine and cysteine codons, respectively; serine starvation increased pausing at serine codons; and Trp or Tyr deprivation induced pausing at Trp and Tyr codons, respectively (Figure S6). However, several exceptions were noted. Methionine starvation in HEK293T cells, even for up to 12 h, did not elicit ribosome stalling at methionine codons (Figure S6A), possibly due to a global reduction in ribosome loading on CDSs when translation initiation is inhibited. Previous work reported that Arg, but not Leu, starvation inhibited translation elongation in HEK293T, HeLa, and HCT116 cells, with pausing observed specifically at CGC and CGT Arg codons.39 By contrast, our data indicated that Leu starvation also induced ribosome pausing at Leu codons, particularly TTA and TTG, albeit to varying degrees across cell lines (Figure S6C). Specifically, in HEK293T and HeLa cells, 3 h of Leu starvation increased pausing at TTA and TTG codons by approximately 20%–30%, while extended starvation (6–12 h) in HEK293T led to more than 50% pausing at the TTA codon (Figures 4A and S6C). Notably, although SUM159PT breast cancer cells developed a pronounced 5′ ribosome ramp after 12 h of Leu starvation (Figure 3C), they displayed only mild ribosome pausing at TTA and TTG codons compared to HEK293T cells (Figure 4A), suggesting that breast cancer cells may exhibit resistance to Leu starvation at the level of codon-specific pausing.Figure 4Amino acid starvation triggers codon-specific ribosome pausing(A) Bar plots depicting changes in ribosome density at each of the 61 sense codons under leucine starvation for 12 h.(B) Ribosome density changes along coding sequences in four breast cancer cell lines after 48 h of glutamine starvation. Similarly, breast cancer cells appeared less susceptible to pausing induced by Gln starvation. Gln deprivation for 48 h in four breast cancer lines (Hs578t, MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and T47D) did not induce substantial ribosome pausing at Gln codons (Figure 4B). By contrast, in MCF10A cells, as little as 30 min of Gln starvation resulted in ∼20%–30% pausing at Gln codons (Figure 4C). Prolonged starvation (4 h in MCF10A and 9 h in HeLa cells) led to extensive pausing, with over 90% of ribosomes arrested at Gln codons (Figures 4C and 4D). A previous study attributed this resistance in breast cancer cells to sustained inhibitory phosphorylation of eEF2 at threonine 56.23 However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Divergent magnitude of ribosome bump induced by amino acid starvation across cell types

In addition to provoking ribosome pausing at cognate codons, amino acid starvation can induce ribosome accumulation immediately downstream of these sites, forming distinctive “bumps” in ribosome profiling data such as “W-bumps” during Trp starvation and “Y-bumps” under Tyr deprivation (Figures S7A and S7B).44 The formation of these bumps appears to depend on both the duration of starvation and the sequence context. For instance, while no significant W-bump was detected after 24 h of Trp starvation, a pronounced bump emerged by 48 h (Figure S7A). Similarly, no ribosome bumps were observed under Arg starvation for a short-time duration, while a pronounced R-bump appeared under starvation for 12 h (Figures S7C and S7D). This pattern correlates with local enrichment of the corresponding amino acid residues upstream of the bump region, suggesting their presence may be a prerequisite for bump formation (Figures S7A–S7D).44

To determine whether ribosome bumps represent a general feature of amino acid starvation and whether breast cancer and non-breast cancer cells differ in their response to this condition, we expanded our analysis to include additional deprivation conditions. Consistently, short-term Leu starvation (3 h) in HEK293T, HCT116, and HeLa cells did not produce detectable L-bumps downstream of Leu codons, while extending Leu starvation to 12 h induced a clear L-bump in HEK293T cells but not in SUM159PT cells (Figures 5A, 5B, and S7E). Although SUM159PT cells exhibited similar upstream Leu enrichment at potential L-bump sites (Figure 5B), no significant bumps formed. Given the attenuated ribosome pausing at Leu codons in SUM159PT cells compared to HEK293T, we hypothesized that theoretical L-bump signals might be obscured by dominant negative regulatory signals. Analysis across all six Leu codons showed that 12 h Leu starvation in HEK293T cells induced L-bumps downstream of four codons (CTC, CTG, CTT, and TTA), whereas SUM159PT cells formed detectable bumps only downstream of two codons (CTC and TTA) (Figure 5C). Notably, L-bump formation did not strongly correlate with pausing intensity, as TTA and TTG—the codons with the most prominent ribosome accumulation (Figure 4C)—displayed divergent bump outcomes.

We next evaluated the effects of Gln starvation across four breast cancer and two non-breast cancer cell lines. Interestingly, we did not observe a detectable Q-bump downstream of the Gln codons under Gln deprivation in non-breast cancer cell lines such as MCF10A and HeLa cells (Figure 5D). Instead, we indeed observed a sharp ribosome “peak” right at the Gln codons (Figure 5D), distinct from the pattern we observed in Leu starvation conditions (Figure 5A). Notably, we did not observe either ribosome peak or ribosome bump in 3 breast cancer cell lines like MDA-MB-231, MCF7 and T47D cells (Figure 5E), indicating those breast cancer cells showed more resistance to Gln starvation compared with non-breast cancer cells.

However, Gln starvation indeed induced a pronounced Q-bump in Hs578t cells (Figure S7F), with detectable enrichment of Gln motifs upstream of the bump sites. This observation raises an interesting possibility that Hs578t cells may lack effective metabolic compensation during Gln limitation, particularly the ability to activate alternative pathways that sustain survival when extracellular Gln is scarce. In eukaryotic cells, GLUL is the key Gln synthetase responsible for catalyzing the conversion of endogenous glutamate into Gln.67 Comparative analysis revealed that GLUL expression is markedly lower in Hs578t cells than in the other three breast cancer cell lines (Figure S7G), suggesting a limited capacity for endogenous Gln synthesis. This deficiency likely underlies the heightened sensitivity of Hs578t cells to Gln deprivation and explains why a prominent Q-bump is observed only in this cell line under Gln starvation.

In summary, systematic analysis of ribosome profiling data reveals that breast cancer cells uniformly exhibit attenuated codon-specific ribosome pausing under Leu and Gln starvation. Notably, even in Hs578t cells, which uniquely developed a pronounced Q-bump downstream of Gln codons, we did not detect significant pausing directly at the cognate codons (Figures 4B and S7F). This dissociation between pausing and bump formation underscores the heterogeneity of elongation dynamics among breast cancer subtypes. Nevertheless, compared with non-breast cancer cell lines, all four breast cancer lines displayed markedly reduced or absent ribosome pausing at cognate codons, and three of the four lines showed no detectable ribosome bumps. We collectively termed these features “elongation-level resistance,” operationally defined by attenuated codon-specific pausing and absence of downstream ribosome bumps under amino acid starvation. This context-dependent reprogramming of elongation dynamics indicates that, despite internal heterogeneity, breast cancer cells share a core translational resistance—encompassing both initiation-level and elongation-level components—that may reflect broader adaptive metabolic mechanisms employed by cancer cells under nutrient stress.

SLC7A11 correlates with the sensitivity of amino acid starvation in breast cancer cells

Beyond its established role in mTORC1 inhibition, amino acid starvation is a well-characterized inducer of autophagy.68,69,70 Previous studies have reported that prolonged serum or Gln deprivation can trigger transient autophagic activation in normal rat kidney cells, leading to the replenishment of intracellular amino acids and subsequent reactivation of mTORC1 signaling.71 This process has been linked to ATF4-mediated upregulation of the amino acid transporter SLC7A5.71 To determine whether autophagy is similarly engaged in breast cancer cells under amino acid stress, we assessed the ribosome occupancy levels of key autophagy-related genes, including p62/SQSTM1, NCOA4, BECN1, MAP1LC3A/B, and ULK1.69,72,73 Our data revealed a mild upregulation of several autophagy reporters, including BECN1, MAP1LC3A/B, NBR1, NCOA4, and SQSTM1, across multiple starvation conditions, indicative of potential autophagy induction (Figure S8A). It is important to note that increased ribosome occupancy on autophagy-related transcripts does not necessarily equate to functional autophagic flux, which is regulated at multiple post-transcriptional and post-translational levels. In addition, although ATF4 expression was elevated to a similar extent in both breast cancer and non-breast cancer cells under these stresses (Figure 2B), the persistent activity of mTORC1 was uniquely maintained in breast cancer lines. This dissociation suggests that neither ATF4 induction nor autophagic activation under amino acid starvation can fully account for the resistance of mTORC1 signaling to amino acid starvation in breast cancer cells.

Gln has been reported to be sufficient for reactivating the mTORC1 pathway under amino acid starvation, a process dependent on autophagy and the intracellular levels of glutamate and other non-essential amino acids derived from Gln.74 Therefore, upregulation of intracellular glutamate is critical for mTORC1 reactivation. Notably, we observed that the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11, which imports extracellular cystine in exchange for intracellular glutamate, was specifically downregulated in breast cancer cells but upregulated in non-breast cancer cells under amino acid starvation (Figure 6A). Since SLC7A11 exports glutamate in exchange for cystine, its downregulation is predicted to reduce glutamate efflux, thereby retaining intracellular glutamate pools that can support mTORC1 signaling.74 Western blot analysis further revealed that Leu starvation reduced SLC7A11 protein levels in MCF7 cells but not in MDA-MB-231 cells (Figures S8B and S8C). In contrast, Gln starvation markedly decreased SLC7A11 expression in both breast cancer cell lines, with the greatest reduction observed after 48 h (Figures S8B and S8C). We therefore focused on investigating the role of SLC7A11 in mediating cellular responses to Gln starvation for 48 h. Given that low SLC7A11 expression has been linked to increased dependency on glucose and Gln in breast cancer cells,75,76 we hypothesized that SLC7A11 downregulation upon amino acid starvation may contribute to maintained mTORC1 activity. To test this, we overexpressed SLC7A11 in MDA-MB-231 cells and assessed the phosphorylation of S6K and eIF2α. Overexpression of SLC7A11 led to a more pronounced reduction in S6K phosphorylation and enhanced eIF2α phosphorylation in response to Gln and Leu starvation (Figure 6D), indicating that restoration of SLC7A11 expression sensitized MDA-MB-231 cells to Gln and Leu deprivation. Overall, these findings suggest that SLC7A11 plays a role in regulating the sensitivity of breast cancer cells to amino acid starvation.

Discussion

While the core mechanisms of translational control in response to amino acid starvation are well-established,33,34,36,37,39,40,41,77 the heterogeneity of these responses across different nutrient conditions and cellular contexts remains poorly characterized. Here, through systematic ribosome profiling, we revealed that breast cancer cells exhibit a distinct pattern of resistance to Leu and Gln deprivation. We operationally define this resistance at two distinct levels. “Initiation-level resistance” is characterized by maintained mTORC1 signaling under starvation, as evidenced by (1) no decrease in ribosome occupancy on TOP and TOP-like mRNAs (median log2 fold change ≥0 relative to non-starved controls) and (2) maintained phosphorylation of the mTORC1 effector S6K. “Elongation-level resistance” is defined by (1) attenuated codon-specific ribosome pausing at cognate codons, with less than 30% increase in ribosome occupancy at Leu or Gln codons, in stark contrast to the >50% accumulation observed in non-breast cancer cells, and (2) absence of detectable ribosome bumps immediately downstream of the deprived amino acid codons. These quantitative criteria provide a framework for comparing translational responses across cell types and nutrient conditions, and they reveal that breast cancer cells have adaptively reshaped their translation regulatory machinery to evade the canonical suppressive effects of amino acid starvation.

A central finding of this study is the paradoxical maintenance of mTORC1 signaling in breast cancer cells under amino acid starvation, whereas it is potently inhibited in non-breast cancer cells. Specifically, ribosome occupancy on 5′ TOP mRNAs, a well-established readout of mTORC1 activity,53,64 was maintained or increased in breast cancer cell lines during Leu or Gln starvation, whereas non-breast cancer cells showed the expected suppression. This phenotype was biochemically validated by sustained S6K phosphorylation in MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 cells. The evasion of canonical mTORC1 suppression likely confers a significant survival advantage under nutrient stress. Previous studies have identified multiple mechanisms by which cancer cells can uncouple mTORC1 activity from amino acid availability34,35,78,79,80,81,82,83 Our data point toward an alternative, metabolism-driven mechanism involving SLC7A11. SLC7A11 (xCT) functions as a cystine/glutamate antiporter, exporting glutamate in exchange for cystine.84 We found that SLC7A11 was specifically downregulated in breast cancer cells under amino acid starvation (Figures 6A and 6B). Its downregulation is predicted to reduce glutamate efflux, thereby retaining intracellular glutamate, a key metabolite that, together with other non-essential amino acids derived from Gln, is sufficient to reactivate mTORC1 during starvation.74 Thus, SLC7A11 downregulation may represent a metabolic adaptation that uncouples mTORC1 activity from extracellular amino acid availability, enabling breast cancer cells to maintain protein synthesis despite nutrient deprivation.

The GCN2-eIF2α-ATF4 axis is a canonical sensor of amino acid deprivation85,86,87 and exhibits more complex, cell line-specific regulation. While Tm treatment and amino acid restriction induced canonical ATF4 and CHOP upregulation across cell types, MCF7 breast cancer cells were a notable exception (Figures 2B and 2C). In MCF7 cells, both Leu and Gln starvation reduced eIF2α phosphorylation, yet they differentially regulated ATF4 protein levels: Leu starvation enhanced ATF4 expression despite reduced eIF2α phosphorylation, whereas Gln starvation suppressed it. This dissociation suggests alternative regulatory mechanisms for stress-responsive gene expression in MCF7 cells, independent of the canonical GCN2-eIF2α signaling pathway, such as the m6A-mediated alternative translation.88 Conversely, MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited expected eIF2α phosphorylation and ATF4 induction under amino acid starvation, resembling non-breast cancer cells (Figure 6C). This heterogeneity in GCN2-eIF2α pathway regulation among breast cancer lines highlights the context-dependent nature of integrated stress response activation in breast cancer cell lines (Figure 6E).

A key advantage of ribosome profiling is its ability to detect local changes in elongation dynamics, most notably ribosome pausing at cognate codons during amino acid limitation.23,39 We found that non-breast cancer cells consistently exhibited robust pausing at Leu and Gln codons under the respective starvations, with >50% increases in ribosome occupancy at TTA codons in HEK293T cells and >90% pausing at Gln codons in MCF10A and HeLa cells. In striking contrast, all four breast cancer lines examined showed markedly attenuated pausing, with <30% increases at cognate codons. Codon-specific pausing is thought to result from limited charging of the cognate tRNA, and recent work has directly correlated ribosome dwell times with tRNA charging levels during BCAA starvation.43 The near-universal attenuation of pausing in breast cancer cells implies that these cells may maintain higher basal tRNA charging efficiency for Leu and Gln, possibly through enhanced amino acid recycling, increased aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase activity, or sustained intracellular amino acid pools via SLC7A11-mediated glutamate retention.

We observed that a 5′ ribosome ramp, characterized by ribosome accumulation toward the start codon, was induced in basal-like breast cancer lines (SUM159PT, Hs578t, MDA-MB-231) under Leu or Gln starvation, but not in luminal lines (MCF7, T47D). This pattern is consistent with previous findings that basal-type breast cancer cells are more sensitive to Gln withdrawal, partly due to differential phosphorylation of the elongation factor eEF2.23 Phosphorylation of eEF2 at Thr56 by eEF2 kinase precludes its binding to the ribosome, resulting in termination of peptide elongation.89 The mechanistic basis for the 5′ ramp remains debated. It may reflect slowed elongation initiation, reduced initiation rates, or early elongation defects. Interestingly, our data reveal a clear dissociation between the 5′ ramp and codon-specific pausing: resistant luminal cells (e.g., MCF7) show neither a ramp nor pausing, while resistant basal cells (e.g., MDA-MB-231) show a ramp but still lack pausing. This uncoupling suggests that the 5′ ramp and cognate-codon pausing are governed by distinct regulatory mechanisms, and that the core resistance phenotype—attenuated pausing and maintained mTORC1 signaling—transcends the presence or absence of the 5′ ribosome ramp.

Our functional experiments demonstrated that SLC7A11 overexpression in MDA-MB-231 cells restores sensitivity to Gln and Leu starvation, as evidenced by reduced S6K phosphorylation and enhanced eIF2α phosphorylation. This finding positions SLC7A11 as a critical modulator of amino acid stress sensitivity. The consequence of SLC7A11 downregulation was predicted to reduce glutamate efflux and increase intracellular glutamate availability,76,84 which provides a plausible mechanism for the maintained mTORC1 activity and attenuated pausing we observed. Elevated intracellular glutamate could support Gln synthesis via Gln synthetase (GLUL),29 thereby buffering cells against extracellular Gln depletion. Notably, Hs578t cells, which uniquely exhibited a Q-bump and low GLUL expression, may lack this compensatory capacity, rendering them partially sensitive despite belonging to the basal subtype. This observation highlights the interplay between transporter expression, biosynthetic enzyme levels, and ultimate metabolic resilience. The regulation of SLC7A11 itself is pretty complex.90 ATF4, NRF2, and ESR1 act as transcriptional activators of SLC7A11 in response to various stresses, whereas p53 and ATF3 serve as transcriptional suppressors.90,91 We observed that Gln starvation reduced SLC7A11 expression in MDA-MB-231 cells despite increased ATF4 levels, suggesting the involvement of alternative regulatory mechanisms such as p53-mediated repression or post-translational control. Deciphering the upstream regulators of SLC7A11 in breast cancer cells under amino acid starvation remains an important direction for future investigation.

In conclusion, this study provides a systematic, quantitative framework for assessing translational resistance to amino acid starvation and reveals that breast cancer cells have adaptively reshaped their translation regulatory machinery to withstand Leu and Gln deprivation. The identification of SLC7A11 as a modulator of this resistance phenotype suggests that targeting amino acid transporter expression or function may represent a therapeutic strategy to selectively sensitize breast cancer cells to metabolic stress.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides systematic insights into translational control under amino acid starvation, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our analysis relies almost entirely on publicly available ribosome profiling datasets. While this enables systematic cross-cell-type comparisons, the heterogeneity in experimental protocols (e.g., CHX pretreatment duration, starvation time, sequencing depth) across studies could introduce technical variation. We mitigated this by performing all differential analyses within individual datasets (using log2 fold change between starved and control conditions), which inherently normalizes for study-specific baselines. The consistency of our key findings across multiple independent datasets supports their robustness. Nonetheless, future prospective studies with standardized protocols would be valuable. Second, for most datasets, matched RNA-seq data were unavailable. Consequently, we used total ribosome density changes as a proxy for translational regulation. However, the effects of transcriptional changes should be noted. Third, although we initially profiled multiple amino acid starvation conditions across various cell lines, our mechanistic investigation ultimately focused on Leu and Gln deprivation in a limited panel of breast cancer cell models. Whether other breast cancer subtypes or additional amino acid deficiencies elicit similar resistance patterns requires further validation in expanded cellular contexts. Technical constraints also precluded comprehensive functional validation across all resistant models. Specifically, while SLC7A11 overexpression effectively rescued Gln sensitivity in MDA-MB-231 cells, we were unable to establish stable SLC7A11-overexpressing MCF7 lines, leaving open the question of whether this rescue effect generalizes across breast cancer subtypes with divergent baseline signaling states. Fourth, the mechanistic link between SLC7A11 and translational resistance remains correlative. Direct measurements of intracellular glutamate/Gln levels, tRNA charging efficiency, and loss-of-function experiments (e.g., short hairpin RNA [shRNA] knockdown or sulfasalazine treatment) in non-breast cancer cells are needed to establish causality. Fifth, the relationship between the 5′ ribosome ramp, codon-specific pausing, and downstream ribosome bumps remains mechanistically unresolved. Our data suggest these phenomena are not strictly coupled and may reflect distinct aspects of elongation dysregulation. Future work using tRNA charging assays, elongation factor profiling, and in vitro reconstitution will be required to disentangle these connections. Sixth, although all four breast cancer lines shared attenuated codon-specific pausing and maintained TOP mRNA occupancy, the observed subtype-specific heterogeneity in GCN2-eIF2α activation and ramp formation underscores the need for larger-scale studies stratified by molecular subtype. Finally, our wet-lab validation is limited to a small cell line panel and relies primarily on western blotting and overexpression, some of which lack biological replications. Future studies employing stable knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of SLC7A11, exogenous supplementation experiments in non-breast cancer cells, and ribosome profiling to directly measure the rescue of pausing will be important to solidify the mechanistic model. In addition, while our combined ribosome profiling and western blot data provide compelling evidence for maintained mTORC1 signaling, direct measurement of mTOR kinase activity or genetic manipulation of pathway components would provide definitive confirmation, and more detailed time-course analyses with multiple biological replicates will be needed to establish the temporal relationship between SLC7A11 downregulation and the maintenance of mTORC1 signaling. Despite these limitations, our core conclusion—that breast cancer cells exhibit a distinct translational resistance phenotype characterized by maintained mTORC1 signaling and attenuated ribosome pausing—remains robust and highlights promising avenues for future investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

HEK293T, MCF7, and MDA-MB-231 cells were obtained from Westlake University (Hangzhou, China) with short tandem repeat (STR) authentication reports provided by the supplier. The cell lines were not independently authenticated after receipt. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based kit (TransGen Biotech, #FM3-11-01) once per month. All tests were negative throughout this study.

Cell culture conditions

HEK293T, MCF7, and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in complete growth medium consisting of DMEM/High Glucose, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin under a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Method details

Overexpression of SLC7A11

For stable overexpression of SLC7A11 in HEK293T, MCF7, and MDA-MB-231 cells, a recombinant lentivirus was constructed by inserting the PCR-amplified cDNA sequence into Xba1 and PspX1 sites of pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro-3xFlag-3xHA. HEK293T cells were transfected with the packaging plasmids pMDLg/pRRE, pVSVG, and pRSV-Rev at a ratio of 0.5: 0.3: 0.2 μg and 1 μg pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro-SLC7A/11-3xHA plasmids with 5 μL Lipofectamine 2000 in opti-MEM. The medium was exchanged for DMEM high glucose medium with 10% FBS after 6 h. Virus supernatant was collected after 48 h, passed through a 0.45 μm filter, and stored at −80°C. 30% confluent HEK293T, MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were infected by virus in 35 mm dishes with 10 μg/ml polybrene. After 24 h, the medium was exchanged for DMEM medium, and cells were selected for 1 week in puromycin (2 μg/ml), and the lysate of polyclonal cultures was tested for SLC7A11 expression by immunoblotting.

Western blotting

Cells were collected by scraping into 0.1 mL of SDS sample buffer (60 mM Tris/Cl, pH 6.8, 5% beta-mercaptoethanol, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.02% bromophenol blue), then heated for 10 min at 95°C. The protein lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat powdered milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing the membranes three times with TBST (10 mM Tris/Cl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Tween 20) for 10 min each, they were incubated with goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were washed three times again as described, followed by chemiluminescence detection using ECL reagents. The preliminary or exploratory experiments to identify optimal treatment durations were performed only once (Figures 2C, S4F, S8B, and S8C). The core validations were performed at least two times (Figures 6B–6D). Quantification was performed using ImageJ.

Collection of ribosome profiling datasets

Eighteen ribosome profiling datasets containing more than 150 samples were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and detailed information on these datasets is provided in Table S1. Specifically, these datasets included 10 kinds of amino acids (Arginine-Arg, Cystine-Cys, Glutamine-Gln, Glycine-Gly, Leucine-Leu, Methionine-Met, Serine-Ser, Tryptophan-Trp, Tyrosine-Tyr, and Branched Chain Amino Acid-BCAA) starvation across 9 different time points (0.5h, 1h, 3h, 6h, 9h, 12h, 24h, and 48h) in 16 distinct cell lines. For comparison with other nutrient starvation and drug treatments, three datasets under serum starvation, four datasets under mTORC1 inhibitors, and one dataset under tunicamycin treatment were also included. Most datasets have already been collected in our previous work.20

Pre-processing of the ribosome profiling data

All the ribosome profiling data were processed with the sample pipeline before.20,98 Specifically, the human reference genome (hg38), non-coding sequences (ncRNA) and annotation file are downloaded from the Ensemble Genome Browser (release 109, https://asia.ensembl.org/index.html).104 Ribosome RNA sequences were downloaded from UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/) with the method we published before.105 FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) was used for quality control, and the adapter sequences were trimmed using the cutadapt program.92 Reads with lengths 15–45 nt were used, but only those with 3-nt periodicity were counted, which was assessed by RiboCode.97,105 Low-quality reads with Phred quality scores lower than 25 (>75% of bases) were removed using the fastx quality filter (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/). The sequence reads originating from rRNAs were identified and discarded by aligning the reads to human rRNA sequences using Bowtie 1.1.293 with no mismatch allowed. The remaining reads were mapped to the genome and transcriptome using STAR with the following parameters: --runThreadN 8 --outFilterType Normal --outWigType wiggle --outWigStrand Strande --outWigNorm RPM --alignEndsType EndToEnd --outFilterMismatchNmax 1 --outFilterMultimapNmax 1 --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate --quantMode TranscriptomeSAM GeneCounts --outSAMattributes All. Then, the BAM format files were sorted and indexed using Samtools (http://www.htslib.org/).

Read counting and differential expression analysis

The P- and A-sites of ribosome footprint (RPF) were determined by RiboCode.97,105 The counts at each codon, coverage of each transcript, and total RPF counts of each gene excluding the first 15 codons and the last 5 codons were obtained with scripts from RiboMiner.98 Differential expression analyses were performed within each dataset independently using DESeq258 for datasets with biological replicates and Gfold59 for datasets without replicates, with thresholds of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and q-value ≤0.05 or |Gfold| > 0. DESeq2 uses Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for p-values. Gfold provides a posterior probability of differential expression. RPKM and TPM values of each gene or transcript were computed by custom R scripts. Sequencing depth and reads distribution for each processing step were calculated by custom Python scripts and presented in Microsoft Excel format (Table S2). Plots of statistics were generated with R4.3 in Rstudio.

Metagene analyses of ribosome profiling data

Metagene analyses of ribosome profiling data were performed by MetageneAnalysis of RiboMiner98 with “-u 100 -d 400 -L 100 -n 10 -m 1” parameters. Specifically, for genes that exhibited more than one transcript, only the longest transcript was used in all analyses. Only the transcripts with lengths over 100 codons and RPKM more than 10 in the whole CDS region were considered. The ribosome densities were normalized to reads per million mapped reads (RPM).

Hierarchical clustering of the translation perturbations

The log2FC of each differentially expressed gene or transcript detected by DESeq2 or Gfold was used for hierarchical clustering. Heatmaps were generated with the ComplexHeatmap package in R, using the following parameters: “cluster_rows = T, cluster_columns = T, clustering_distance_columns = ‘euclidean’, clustering_method_columns = ‘ward.D2’”. Other heatmaps, like the Pearson correlation between gene-level and transcript-level ribosome density, the polarity sore differences (Δpolarity) were generated with the same method.

Dimensionality reduction analysis

The principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with customed R scripts. Specifically, the base function “prcomp” was used for PCA analysis and plots were generated with “ggpubr” and “ggrepel” packages. The uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis was performed with the “umap” package and plots were generated with the same method as PCA analysis.

Functional enrichments

“ClusterProfiler”99 package and online web tool Metascape100 were used for functional enrichments such as gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway, and results generated with ClusterProfiler were presented in this manuscript.

Collections of TOP and TOP-like mRNAs and targets of ATF4-CHOP

To systematically compare the translational effects of amino acid starvation on the mTORC1 pathway, TOP and TOP-like mRNAs were collected from previous reports53,64 and used as a proxy for the status of the mTORC1 signal pathway. The principle was that TOP and TOP-like mRNAs would be inhibited and with fewer ribosomes loaded into the CDS when the mTORC1 signal pathway was inactivated. When the activity of the mTORC1 signal pathway was maintained or enhanced, the translation level of TOP and TOP-like mRNAs would be increased with more ribosomes. Similarly, amino acid starvation would activate the GCN2-eIF2α pathway, increasing the translation level of transcription factors ATF4 and CHOP, which would induce more transcription of their targets.39 Thus, targets of ATF4 and CHOP were used as a proxy for the status of the GCN2-eIF2α pathway. Unlike the previous work,39 log2FC of all targets of ATF4 and CHOP were presented not only for those genes with log2FC > 0.

Finding ribosome “bumps” and identifying amino acid sequences around the “bump” positions

Codon-level specific pausing was analyzed by RiboMiner.98 Ribosome densities around each codon or amino acid were calculated with a customed Python script and ribosome “bumps” were found using the method published previously.44 Specifically, BAM files mapped to human transcriptome were converted to BED using BEDTools106 and later file formats were edited using Python and R scripts. Each gene was divided into 100 windows of equal length, and reads at each window were quantified using BEDTools. The array of reads was normalized between 0 and 1. As for replicates, average values would be taken. Thereafter, peaks were identified in an array of reads per window using the findpeaks (pracma v1.9.9) function in R with (numps = 1, ndown = 1, minpeakheight = 10) parameters. Only peaks called in the treatment condition (amino acid starvation) were identified as treatment peaks, namely the “bumps” and peaks called in the control condition were identified as control peaks, namely the “possible bumps”. The highest point (window with the greatest number of reads) per peak was marked as the reference point. The amino acids were mapped at 30 codons of each side of the reference point and quantified as a sum at every individual position in Python. Line plots were then plotted in Rstudio. The upstream/downstream ratio for every amino acid was quantified as the ratio of the average presence of a particular amino acid 30 codons upstream versus 30 codons downstream. The original scripts and pipeline of this analysis can be found at https://github.com/apataskar/bump_finder_example2.

Sequences enrichments

The amino acid sequences around the reference point (bump position) were extracted and described before. A Python customed script was used for converting the sequences into a fasta format, which was used for sequencing enrichment analysis via Seq2Logo 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/Seq2Logo-2.0/). The position-specific frequency matrixes (PSFM) for amino acid sequences around both treatment and control bumps were downloaded from Seq2Logo 2.0 and the relative weight matrix was calculated as a ratio of treatment PSFM versus control PSFM, which would be further imported into Seq2Logo 2.0 again and generate the sequence logo plots.

Meta-analysis of top mRNAs with vote-counting method

To provide a rigorous, statistically sound meta-analysis that fully utilizes all available data (including those without biological replicates, for which standard errors are not estimable), we adopted a vote-counting meta-analysis framework65 for the ribosome density change of the top mRNAs under amino acid starvation or treatment with mTORC1 inhibitors. This approach relies solely on the direction (sign) of log2 fold change across independent studies, rather than on effect sizes or their standard errors. For each of the top mRNAs, we performed two independent one-sided exact binomial tests.●In breast cancer (BC) cell lines, we tested whether the proportion of studies with up-regulation (log2FC > 0) significantly exceeded 0.5 (the null expectation of random direction). A significant result indicates that the gene is up-regulated in the majority of BC datasets.●In non-breast cancer (non-BC) cell lines, we similarly tested whether the proportion of studies with down-regulation (log2FC < 0) significantly exceeded 0.5.

p-values were obtained from the exact binomial test (binom.test in R) and adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Genes with FDR <0.05 and a proportion ≥0.7 (i.e., consistently regulated in ≥70% of studies) were considered as robustly directional.
