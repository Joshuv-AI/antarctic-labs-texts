Transcranial Pulse Stimulation Enhances Dexterity in Parkinson's Disease: A Randomized Sham‐Controlled Trial
Authors: Eva Matt, Nina Plischek, Michael Mitterwallner, Sonja Radjenovic, Alexandra Weber, Alina Domitner, Gregor Dörl, Anna Zettl, Sarah Osou, Anna Leitgeb, Agnes Santer, Viktoria Winkler, Julia Mandelburger, Heidemarie Zach, Roland Beisteiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound‐based neuromodulation, capable of reaching deep brain areas with high precision, represents cutting‐edge technology in non‐invasive brain stimulation and is investigated as a novel treatment for neurological and psychiatric disorders, including Parkinson's disease (PD).ObjectivesTo evaluate the effects of repeated transcranial pulse stimulation (TPS) applications on motor symptoms, brain structure, and functional activation in PD.MethodsIn this randomized, sham‐controlled, double‐blind, crossover clinical trial, 30 participants with mild PD underwent six sessions of both verum and sham TPS targeting the sensorimotor network. Comprehensive clinical and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessments were administered at baseline, 1 day, and 1 month post‐stimulation for both conditions.ResultsManual dexterity, measured via the coin rotation task, showed significantly larger improvement following verum compared with sham TPS (post‐stimulation vs. baseline: P corr = 0.048, ηp2 = 0.14; 1‐month post‐stimulation vs. baseline: P corr = 0.048, ηp2 = 0.17). Gross motor symptoms, evaluated by the Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale‐Part III (MDS‐UPDRS‐III), improved similarly after both verum and sham TPS. Functional MRI during a coin rotation task revealed increased activation in the sensorimotor network following verum stimulation compared with sham (P < 0.001). Diffusion tensor imaging data indicated enhanced structural integrity in the primary somatosensory white matter following verum TPS compared with sham (P < 0.05 family‐wise error [FWE]‐corrected). A total of 21 adverse events were reported, all mild and transient.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that TPS produces behavioral, neurophysiological, and structural effects within the targeted sensorimotor network, and may represent a safe and effective adjunct to standard therapies in PD. © 2026 The Author(s). Introduction

Current standard therapies for Parkinson's disease (PD), administering levodopa or dopamine agonists, are limited by progressive loss of efficacy, increased motor fluctuations, and adverse effects during the treatment course. 1 The efficacy of levodopa can be enhanced by deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus through surgically implanted leads. 2 Recently, incisionless ablation of these structures using high‐intensity focused ultrasound (HI‐FU) demonstrated improvements in motor function in PD. 3 , 4 Nonetheless, DBS and HI‐FU have been associated with adverse events (AEs), including dyskinesia, speech disturbances, and gait impairments. 2

Non‐invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) presents a promising alternative with a favorable safety profile, mild side effects, and the potential for long‐lasting neuroplastic effects. 5 , 6 However, even successful transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) protocols were not as effective as levodopa, and long‐term benefits remained rather modest. 6 Moreover, these NIBS approaches are limited by coarse targeting and restriction to cortical regions. Transcranial ultrasound enables access to deep brain structures such as the substantia nigra or striatum. Using unfocused low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS), these areas were sonicated in a sham‐controlled trial in 56 PD patients, reporting improvements in motor symptoms, cognition, and mood following 40 sessions. 7

The application of low‐intensity focused ultrasound may further enhance the targeting accuracy of cortical and subcortical regions. 8 , 9 Currently, two main classes of low‐intensity focused ultrasound (LI‐FU) techniques are used: transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS), implemented in various technical systems and typically employing multi‐cycle sinusoidal ultrasound waves at a single fundamental frequency, and transcranial pulse stimulation (TPS), applying single‐cycle, multi‐frequency pulses of microsecond‐scale durations. 8 , 10 Both techniques have been extensively studied in basic research and clinical trials targeting neuropsychiatric disorders, including dementia, psychiatric conditions, and movement disorders. 11 , 12 , 13

In PD, current investigations using tFUS 14 , 15 , 16 or TPS 17 , 18 include only exploratory, small‐scale trials with 10–20 patients. Targeting the primary motor cortex (M1), motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were increased following repeated theta burst tFUS (5 Hz) in PD patients off dopaminergic therapy 14 and after a single application in PD patients on dopaminergic therapy. 15 Sonication of the globus pallidus with 5 and 10 Hz tFUS increased local field potential power, implying target‐specific upregulation of the motor network excitability. 16 In a direct comparison of stimulation sites, theta burst tFUS of the globus pallidus demonstrated greater efficacy in reducing motor symptoms than M1 stimulation. 19 After a single TPS session targeting M1, resting tremor amplitude was significantly reduced compared with sham, 18 although this difference was not significant 24 hours later, suggesting a transient effect of a single TPS session. Improvements in global motor scores, such as the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) or its revision Movement Disorder Society (MDS)‐UPDRS, were observed in open‐label TPS studies in PD, 17 , 20 but comparisons to sham did not reach significance in other small, exploratory trials. 14 , 18

Here, we report the largest prospective, randomized, sham‐controlled crossover study in the field to date, involving 30 PD patients. Functional MRI (fMRI) assessed task‐based motor activation, and white matter integrity was evaluated using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), providing the first MRI evidence of ultrasound neuromodulation in PD.

We hypothesized that verum TPS would outperform sham in reducing global motor symptoms (MDS‐UPDRS‐Part III), and in enhancing manual dexterity (coin rotation task) as co‐primary outcomes. Based on prior imaging findings with TPS in healthy participants and in Alzheimer's disease, 21 , 22 , 23 we anticipated increased task‐based brain activation and improved white matter integrity.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

In this randomized, double‐blind, sham‐controlled, crossover clinical trial, 30 patients with PD were included, with the sample size determined based on feasibility considerations. The study was conducted at the Medical University of Vienna, obtained approval of the local ethics committee and relevant regulatory authorities, and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04333511). Patients were recruited at the Department of Neurology, Medical University of Vienna, or via newspaper advertisements. Following comprehensive eligibility screening by experienced movement disorder specialists (H.Z., A.S.), participants were consecutively enrolled and randomly allocated to the treatment sequence groups Verum‐Sham, receiving verum TPS in the first cycle and sham TPS in the second cycle, or Sham‐Verum (n = 15 each; Fig. 1A). Each cycle began with baseline clinical, neuropsychological, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessments, followed by six verum or sham TPS sessions targeting the sensorimotor network (Fig. 1B,C), administered over 2 weeks (three sessions per week, distributed across 4–5 days). Clinical post‐stimulation evaluations were performed on the following days (median = 1, range 0–10 days), and 1 month afterwards. The 1‐month post‐stimulation assessment of the first treatment cycle served as the baseline for the second cycle, resulting in a washout phase between both treatment conditions of 4 weeks. MRI measurements were conducted at baseline and 1 day after the last stimulation (median = 1, range 0–8 days), leading to four MRI sessions across both cycles.

Participants

To be included, patients required a neurologist‐confirmed diagnosis of PD with clinically stable motor deficits and had to be 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria were relevant intracerebral pathology unrelated to PD (eg, brain tumor), blood clotting disorders, thrombosis, anticoagulation therapy, cortisone treatment within 6 weeks prior to the first stimulation, MRI contraindications, non‐compliance with the protocol, continued participation in other studies, pregnancy, or breastfeeding. Participants continued their individual standard medication and were instructed to keep their medication stable during the study participation. All participants signed written informed consent forms according to the Declaration of Helsinki and received financial compensation.

Transcranial Pulse Stimulation

In total, participants received six verum and six sham TPS interventions using the prototype of the NEUROLITH (modified DUOLITH SD1, Storz Medical AG, Tägerwilen, Switzerland; Fig. 1B). This system has been evaluated regarding safety, feasibility, and efficacy in Alzheimer's disease (AD), 21 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 PD, 17 , 18 , 20 and other neuropsychiatric disorders. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 TPS received the CE‐mark approval for the treatment of AD in 2018 and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval (Investigational Device Exemption). Per session, lasting approximately 30 min, 6000 TPS pulses were delivered to motor network areas, including primary motor cortex (M1), primary somatosensory cortex (S1), and supplementary motor area (SMA). These regions of interest (ROIs) were predefined ellipses that were aligned to individual structural MRI scans (Fig. 1C) by a neurologist and TPS expert (R.B.). Pulses were evenly distributed within the ROIs, ensuring proper targeting by real‐time tracking using an infrared camera monitoring sensors on goggles and the TPS handpiece. The ultrashort TPS pulses (3 μs) with a multi‐frequency band predominantly between 0.05 and 0.45 MHz 22 were applied with a pulse repetition frequency of 4 Hz and 0.25 mJ/mm2 energy flux density (duty cycle = 0.000012). Adjusting for skull attenuation of 80%–90%, 21 typical maximum pulse characteristics were 24 mW/cm2 spatial peak temporal average intensity (ISPTA), 1.6 kW/cm2 spatial peak pulse average intensity (ISPPA), and ambient pressure‐related positive (7.5 MPa) and negative (−6 MPa) peak pressures below the skull. Sham stimulation was achieved by blocking the ultrasound pulses with a cap on the handpiece, looking identical, and producing a sound closely matching the verum condition. Experimenters delivering TPS were informed about the sham or verum condition but were not involved in the outcome evaluations. Patients, the principal investigator, clinical evaluators, and data analysts were blinded. Immediately after each stimulation, patients rated perceived pain and pressure on the scalp using a visual analogue scale (0–10), and assessed whether verum or sham had been applied.

Outcome Variables

The motor examination of the MDS‐UPDRS‐III 33 and the coin rotation task 34 , 35 were the co‐primary outcomes in this trial. The MDS‐UPDRS‐III score was used to assess motor functions such as rigidity, resting and action tremor, bradykinesia, gait, and other, rated from 0 (normal) to 4 (severe impairment) by movement disorder specialists (H.Z., A.S.). Only the total score of the MDS‐UPDRS‐III was analyzed, with no additional analyses of subdomains (eg, tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, axial symptoms). Manual dexterity was assessed with a coin rotation task, 34 in which the patients rotated a plastic €2 coin as quickly as possible for 20 s, to complement the MDS‐UPDRS‐III with a fine motor evaluation. The task was performed alternately with the left and right hand, starting with two training trials per side prior to the experiment. Per side, the highest count from four attempts served as the outcome.

Subjective ratings of motor aspects of daily living, provided by the patient and someone close to them, were recorded using the MDS‐UPDRS‐II, with ratings from 0 (normal) to 4 (severe impairment). For self‐ and external assessment, the sum of the 13 MDS‐UPDRS‐II items was calculated and analyzed as secondary outcomes. Clinical evaluations further included the Keyboard Test, 36 the Nine‐Hole Peg Test, 37 Timed Up and Go Test, 38 Buttoning and Unbuttoning Test, 35 and the PD Quality of Life Questionnaire (PDQ‐39 39 ); data are not presented here.

Neuropsychological assessments included the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; www.mocatest.ch/test/), as well as the self‐report scales Beck Depression Inventory (BDI‐II 40 ), Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐15 41 ), Bayer Activities of Daily Living Scale (B‐ADL, 42 self‐score and external), and Leisure Behavior Questionnaire (LBQ, 43 self‐score and external) (see Supporting Information).

MRI

MRI sessions before and after sham and verum TPS included structural, DTI, and fMRI scans (Supporting Information). The fMRI motor task was adapted from previous work probing basal motor skills like finger tapping and more complex sensorimotor functions using a coin rotation paradigm, 34 in an event‐related design to differentiate movement planning, preparation, and execution. Each fMRI run consisted of four coin rotation and four finger tapping periods, performed with the right hand, with a fixed time for planning (2.8 s) and preparation (2.8 s), followed by motor execution phases (2.8 s, one finger tap or one 180° flip of a €2 plastic coin) that were repeated two to four times. The order of the tasks was pseudo‐randomized, and the time between the tasks varied between 5.6 and 8.4 s. The motor task consisted of eight runs (approximately 3 min each), and task instructions were visually presented.

Four DTI indices were investigated to assess white matter changes on the whole‐brain level. As a measure of directionality of water diffusion, fractional anisotropy (FA) provides insight into white matter integrity. 44 Axial diffusivity (AD) measures water diffusion along the principal axis or the underlying fiber orientation, reflecting axon number and caliber. 45 Radial diffusivity (RD) indicates diffusion perpendicular to the tract, which is sensitive to myelin changes, 46 but is also influenced by axon diameter and density. 44 Mean diffusivity (MD) reflects the average diffusion without directionality. 47 Intact neuronal microstructures, such as axonal cell membranes and myelin sheaths, promote increased levels of FA and reduce MD, AD, and RD. 44 , 47 DTI data were processed and analyzed using FSL 6.0 and its toolboxes (Supporting Information).

Statistical Analysis

Initial behavioral outcome analysis was performed by a contract research organization, applying t‐tests of score changes (data not presented here). Predefined complex data analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., version 29.0) and employed a repeated measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA) with the within‐subject factors Condition (verum/sham) and Session (baseline, post‐stimulation, 1‐month post‐stimulation), testing for the interaction of interest Condition*Session for all behavioral outcomes. Following a significant interaction, post‐hoc comparisons between the follow‐ups and the baseline were calculated and corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni–Holm adjustment. Statistical analysis was performed for the Intention‐to‐Treat sample (n = 30) with missing data being imputed using last‐observation‐carried‐forward. The alpha level for significance (two‐sided) was set at 0.025 for the co‐primary outcomes, MDS‐UPDRS‐III and coin rotation, and at 0.05 for secondary outcomes. As a sensitivity analysis, a Condition*Session*Group rmANOVA was calculated for the primary outcomes to model cycle effects. Partial eta squared (ηp2) was used to measure effect size, with thresholds of 0.01 for a small effect, 0.06 for a medium effect, and 0.14 for a large effect. In the case of substantial outliers or skewed data distribution, variables were logarithmically transformed (LN[X + 1]) to meet the assumptions for the rmANOVA. If this procedure was not successful, the nonparametric Friedman test for paired samples was applied with Bonferroni–Holm adjusted post‐hoc tests. An exploratory correlation analysis between primary outcomes and the remaining behavioral data across all time points was conducted (Pearson's correlation for parametric data or Spearman's rank correlation).

Ratings of pressure and pain after TPS were averaged over the six sessions of each condition and analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test.

Following fMRI data preprocessing and first‐level statistics (Supporting Information), a flexible factorial model with the within‐subject factors Condition (verum/sham), Session (pre/post stimulation), and Subject was applied for the coin rotation and finger tapping tasks separately. To estimate baseline motor activation, the first fMRI session was analyzed using a t‐test against 0 at the group level. Further, an ROI analysis was conducted for target regions (M1, SMA, S1) and an auditory control region (Supporting Information). Mean T‐values within target ROIs were correlated with clinical outcomes for pre‐ and post‐stimulation sessions.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Between December 2019 and August 2022, 43 patients with PD were recruited (Fig. 1 and Supporting Information). The intention‐to‐treat sample (n = 30, 15 Sham‐Verum, 15 Verum‐Sham) comprised 8 female and 22 male participants with a mean age of 66.87 (SD = 7.03) years and 14.30 (SD = 3.11) years of formal education, on average. All patients were diagnosed with mild PD (Hoehn and Yahr Scale 1–2.5) with an average time since diagnosis of 5.30 (SD = 3.35) years. Twenty‐two patients (73.3%) received levodopa, and eight (26.7%) took dopamine agonists (pramipexole, rotigotine, ropinirole) as standard medication.

Improved Manual Dexterity Following Verum TPS

Alongside the MDS‐UPDRS‐III, 33 the coin rotation task 35 was a primary outcome of this trial. Interestingly, this assessment of manual dexterity revealed a substantial difference between right and left hand (Fig. 2A). While the results for the right hand, being dominant in 93.3% of the patients, did not show significant results in the rmANOVA (compare Table 1), the interaction Condition*Session was significant for the left hand (P = 0.024, ηp2 = 0.14). Post‐hoc tests were significant for both follow‐up sessions compared with baseline (post‐stimulation: P corr = 0.048, ηp2 = 0.14; 1‐month post‐stimulation: P corr = 0.048, ηp2 = 0.17), confirming that the number of coin flips was significantly increased following verum compared with sham stimulation over at least 1 month. The interaction of treatment sequence group with Condition*Session was not significant (left hand: P = 0.538, ηp2 = 0.02; right hand: P = 0.198, ηp2 = 0.06), indicating that the treatment effects were independent of whether verum TPS was administered in the first or second cycle. Higher coin rotation performance was associated with lower motor symptom severity (MDS‐UPDRS‐III), fewer self‐ and externally reported difficulties in activities of daily living (B‐ADL, MDS‐UPDRS‐II), higher leisure activity levels, better cognitive performance (MoCA), and lower depression scores (BDI‐II) (see Supporting Information).

(A) Coin rotation (CR) with the left and right hand. Performance for the left hand significantly improved following verum transcranial pulse stimulation (TPS) treatment (blue) compared with sham (green, Condition*Session interaction: P = 0.024, n = 30) indicating an improvement in manual dexterity lasting up to 1 month after the stimulation. (B) Change in the motor part of the Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale‐Part III (MDS‐UPDRS‐III) over time. The mean MDS‐UPDRS‐III scores decreased 4.93 points (SD = 9.23, n = 30) from the first session (Cycle 1, baseline) to the last time point (Cycle 2, 1‐month post‐stimulation), indicating a clinically relevant score change in global motor symptoms over time for both treatment sequence groups (turquoise = Verum‐Sham, violet = Sham‐Verum). No Modulation of Global Motor Scores by Verum TPS

Overall, mean MDS‐UPDRS‐III total scores decreased 4.93 points (SD = 9.23) from the first session (Cycle 1, baseline: mean = 34.10, SD = 13.38) to the last time point (Cycle 2, 1‐month post‐stimulation: mean = 29.17, SD = 14.43; Fig. 2B). Score decreases 1‐month post‐stimulation relative to baseline were comparable in both conditions (verum: −2.63, sham: −2.86), resulting in a significant effect of Session (P = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.19) but no significant effects for Condition (P = 0.475, ηp2 = 0.02) and Condition*Session (P = 0.991, ηp2 = 0.00). Treatment sequence had no significant effect on the interaction of interest (Condition*Session*Group: P = 0.276, ηp2 = 0.04).

The MDS‐UPDRS‐II self‐scores decreased on average 3.67 points (SD = 3.51) during the study, starting with a mean score of 10.40 (SD = 6.65) in session 1 and ending with a mean score of 6.73 (SD = 4.91) in session 6. The external assessment of the MDS‐UPDRS‐II revealed a more subtle decrease: session 1: 8.33 (SD = 6.43), session 6: 6.93 (SD = 6.87). For both external and self‐evaluation, the main effect of Session was significant, but not the main effect of Condition or the interaction Condition*Session (Table 1). However, B‐ADL patient ratings revealed a significant improvement in activities of daily living after verum TPS (Friedman's test: P = 0.004; post‐hoc comparisons: verum baseline vs. post‐stimulation: P corr = 0.028). Additional results on behavioral outcomes are provided in Table 1 and the Supporting Information.

Upregulation of Functional Motor Activation Following Verum TPS

As expected, the coin rotation task at baseline elicited activation in the left primary motor and somatosensory areas, contralateral to the hand moved, and in the bilateral anterior SMA. (Fig. 3A). The interaction Condition*Session for the coin rotation task revealed an upregulation of somatosensory feedback processing, with activation found in the left postcentral gyrus and the left Rolandic operculum, for verum compared with sham TPS, and increased activation in the motor network contralateral to the primarily engaged motor system (right M1, left cerebellum) and in the right ACC (Fig. 3B; Table S3). Negative clusters represent more pronounced deactivations, as found in the baseline fMRI assessment (Fig. 3A), in the verum compared with the sham condition, particularly in the default mode network (precuneus, angular gyrus).

The ROI analysis of the coin rotation task revealed an increased mean T‐value in the verum condition in all ROIs investigated, reaching significance for the interaction of interest (Condition*Session) in the right M1 and S1 (Fig. 3C; Table S4). Performance in the behavioral coin rotation task of the left hand, which was significantly improved by verum compared with sham stimulation, was correlated with mean T‐values in both right and left hemispheric ROIs (Table S5). In contrast, behavioral coin rotation performance of the right hand was correlated only with mean T‐values in left‐hemispheric ROIs.

A control ROI analysis of potential coactivation of the primary auditory cortex showed no significant effects, neither in the coin rotation task (Friedman test: P = 0.440), nor in the finger tapping task (rmANOVA: Condition*Session: P = 0.739). Adverse Events

Ratings of adverse effects immediately after each stimulation demonstrated low levels of perceived pressure (verum: median = 0.83, interquartile range [IQR] = 1.33; sham: median = 0.67, IQR = 1.00; P = 0.519, n = 28) and pain (verum: median = 0.00, IQR = 0.00; sham: median = 0.00, IQR = 0.00; P = 0.587, n = 28), without significant differences between verum and sham condition. Most of the stimulations were judged as ‘real’ (verum: 78.0%, sham: 78.6%), resulting in high sensitivity (78.0%) but low specificity (21.4%) and an accuracy of 49.7%. A total of 21 AEs according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) were recorded during the study, including pain, muscle cramps, depressive symptoms, headache, tremor, or fatigue, but no serious AEs occurred (Supporting Information). Neuroradiological evaluations by an external radiologist (M.S.) revealed no neuropathological changes between baseline and post‐stimulation MRI.

Discussion

In this randomized, sham‐controlled trial in 30 PD patients, the primary outcome analysis revealed a significant improvement in fine motor skills, but no sham‐controlled enhancement of gross motor abilities following verum TPS in PD.

Manual dexterity is essential for activities of daily living. 35 Coin rotation performance improved in the left hand following verum TPS, whereas the right hand showed only limited response, potentially due to a ceiling effect for the skilled dominant hand. However, improved manual dexterity following verum TPS was reflected by significantly reduced self‐reported difficulties in daily living (B‐ADL), and coin rotation performance was significantly correlated with motor symptoms, activities of daily living, and leisure activity level.

fMRI during the right‐hand coin rotation task revealed significantly higher activation following verum TPS compared with sham in the left S1 and Rolandic operculum, indicating enhanced primary and secondary somatosensory processing. 48 Smaller clusters in the ipsilateral (right) M1 and the ACC also showed higher activation following verum TPS. While the ACC has been linked to motor coordination, learning, 49 , 50 and tactile discrimination, 51 the ipsilateral M1 may support the dominant left M1 during coin rotation, as reported for other complex manual tasks. 52 , 53 Interestingly, mean T‐values in left‐ and right‐hemispheric ROIs were correlated with performance in the behavioral coin rotation task of the left hand, suggesting that recruitment of bilateral cortical areas during the lateralized motor task was associated with better left‐hand performance. Verum TPS significantly enhanced both the mean T‐value in the right M1 and left‐hand coin rotation performance, indicating a potential to upregulate non‐dominant motor function at the network and behavioral levels.

Previous imaging studies in PD revealed reduced S1 activation in PD patients on and off dopaminergic medication compared with controls during coin rotation, while M1 was upregulated by dopaminergic drugs. 34 Behavioral coin rotation performance exhibited poor dopaminergic response as well, whereas simple finger tapping improved under medication in a previous study. 54 Here, PD patients were on stable dopaminergic medication, which likely limited motor enhancement and confined it to the non‐dominant function. In contrast, TPS upregulated primary and secondary somatosensory areas, suggesting facilitation of somatosensory processing, typically less responsive to dopaminergic drugs. 34 , 54 , 55

Significant DTI findings also involved somatosensory regions, with elevated FA levels in the right somatosensory gyrus following verum TPS. Generally, decreased FA reflects reduced axonal alignment and demyelination, as observed in several neurodegenerative disorders, whereas increased FA indicates improved white matter integrity and remyelination. 44 , 56 The sham‐controlled FA enhancement in the stimulated sensorimotor system suggests that TPS promotes white matter integrity, potentially by inducing or reactivating compensatory neuroplasticity, as found in early stages of PD. 57 , 58 , 59

These results suggest TPS is particularly effective at alleviating somatosensory dysfunction, a prominent feature of PD 60 contributing to motor disturbances. 61 Nevertheless, motor symptoms such as rigidity, tremor, bradykinesia, and postural instability remain cardinal PD features, and are traditionally assessed using the (MDS)‐UPDRS‐III. 33 The MDS‐UPDRS‐III decreased on average 4.93 points over the study, indicating a clinically relevant benefit, 62 comparable with a retrospective TPS investigation in PD. 17 However, consistent with other sham‐controlled fFUS 14 and TPS 18 studies, no significant difference emerged between verum and sham regarding this global motor score. Although the MDS‐UPDRS remains the most common PD outcome, 63 it is rather coarse and insensitive to subtle motor difficulties and dexterity impairments, 64 especially in early stages 65 or in longitudinal investigations. 66 These limitations may have contributed to the lack of a significant difference in the MDS‐UPDRS between verum and sham TPS in the present study.

Another potential explanation is the placebo effect, particularly pronounced in PD, 67 , 68 as endogenous dopamine release in the striatum due to positive expectations counteracts dopamine loss within the nigrostriatal motor system. 69 , 70 The placebo response is amplified by medical devices, perceived effort, novelty, and positive treatment expectations. 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 Here, patients showed strong belief in receiving verum TPS, with ~78% of sessions rated as ‘real’ regardless of condition, likely contributing to a robust placebo response that, combined with medication, left little room for additional TPS‐related improvement.

Transcranial ultrasound has been associated with increased release of neurotrophic factors 74 , 75 and neurotransmitters, including dopamine, 76 , 77 anti‐inflammatory effects, 75 and the facilitation of neuropathological protein clearance. 78 , 79 Accordingly, TPS application may improve motor symptoms by enhancing dopaminergic signaling and neuroplasticity, delay disease progression by counteracting neurodegenerative processes such as α‐synuclein aggregation and inflammation, and promote neuronal repair and growth through the upregulation of neurotrophic factors, particularly in early stages of PD.

Several limitations must be considered. Despite a 4‐week washout, lower MDS‐UPDRS baseline levels in the second cycle (Fig. 2B) indicate a carryover effect. As both treatment sequence groups were similarly affected and no significant Condition*Session*Group interaction was observed, the overall results are not necessarily compromised. Repeated testing might also have induced training or attrition effects. Regarding disease severity, 90% of the patients were classified as Hohn and Yahr Stage 2. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to other disease stages is limited, as TPS mechanisms of action may vary with disease progression. In earlier stages, TPS may primarily support compensatory neuroplasticity, 58 whereas in later stages, add‐on effects may prevail as efficacy of dopaminergic therapy declines. 1 In addition, PD subtype and age of onset 80 were not considered. To minimize placebo‐related confounds, the sham condition matched verum TPS in appearance, acoustic profile, and sensory feedback. Both produced a repetitive 5 Hz ticking sound, probably inducing habituation to this constant auditory input over the 20‐min stimulation period. Addressing previous concerns regarding auditory biases, 81 the control ROI analysis revealed no significant effect within the auditory cortex during the fMRI motor task. Regarding somatosensory confounds, patients reported no significant differences in perceived pressure or pain between conditions. Furthermore, condition identification did not exceed chance.

Future studies should include patients across different PD stages and subtypes in randomized sham‐controlled parallel‐group trials, with adequate sample sizes. 11 Comprehensive assessments of both gross and fine motor symptoms, and somatosensory function, are essential, preferably monitored over extended periods. The impact of dopaminergic therapy should be systematically examined by including patients both on and off levodopa or de novo patients. Electrophysiological measurements and functional and structural imaging will further elucidate TPS mechanisms. Finally, optimal stimulation parameters, targets, and dosing protocols remain to be established. With further refinement, TPS could serve as an additional therapeutic option to complement standard dopaminergic medications, aiming to enhance sensorimotor function and support activities of daily living in patients with PD.
