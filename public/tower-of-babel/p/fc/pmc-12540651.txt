Box-meter integrated solution for power data imputation through device design and deep learning integration
Authors: Chen Gao, Hua Lin, Yinrong Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn smart metering systems, data loss often occurs due to sensor failures, communication delays, and equipment maintenance, affecting the accuracy of power data analysis. This study proposes an box-meter integrated metering device that supports localized data imputation and combines it with deep learning models for further research. We compared the imputation performance of different deep learning models–including DLinear, TimesNet, and iTransformer–under varying missing rates. Experimental results show that TimesNet achieves optimal imputation performance across diverse missing scenarios. The device is capable of deploying deep learning models and integrates a raw analog signal acquisition interface, thereby reducing data loss at the source and enhancing data continuity and real-time availability. This approach improves data quality and timeliness, providing a solid data foundation for power system tasks such as intelligent scheduling and load forecasting.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Renewable energy

Introduction

In smart meter-based power data monitoring systems, multivariate time-series data is widely used in statistical analysis of time-series power data, time-series prediction, classification of fault modes and other practical testing tasks, and has become a key source of data for decision-making and trend analysis, and the accuracy and high quality of the data is an important factor in promoting data analysis.However, due to various reasons (e.g., sensor failure, communication delay, equipment maintenance, etc.), these power data often have missing values1. Missing data not only affects the accuracy of data analysis, but also may lead to decision errors, especially in scenarios requiring high-precision prediction and real-time scheduling. Therefore, how to effectively fill in the missing data has become an important topic in power system data analysis. Electricity datasets usually exhibit some time dependence. For example, power load data are often affected by cyclical factors such as seasonality, climate change, weekends and weekdays2. In this case, the distribution of missing values not only depends on the randomness of the data itself, but is also closely related to the variation of external factors. These missing values will destroy the temporal characteristics of power data and affect the subsequent analysis results.Therefore, taking the collected smart meter data as the research object, conducting research on the missing data repair method to improve the integrity of smart meter data becomes an important research direction in the field of power system3,4.

The power data imputation scheme is mainly divided into two categories: master station imputation and meter local imputation. The master station imputation scheme relies on the remote centralized monitoring system and uses a global algorithm to estimate the missing data. The granularity of this method is relatively rough, and it can only be supplemented based on historical data or local trends, making it difficult to restore the real power consumption situation on site, and it is often not accurate enough in the face of high-frequency fluctuations or short-term shortages. By deploying a more intelligent local processing system in the field equipment, the Meter Local Imputation Program is able to sense the dynamic changes in the field power load with high frequency, and provide more accurate support for data imputation. Meter Local Imputation can more accurately restore the real power consumption situation at the site, and it can carry out data imputation on the basis of higher frequency, so as to improve the reliability and timeliness of the data.Imputation data and the actual measurement data need to be clearly distinguished to ensure the accuracy of data identification, to avoid affecting the subsequent analysis of the measurement confidence. The fitted data is the data obtained from the algorithmic model to fill in the missing values, while the actual measurement data comes from the real-time measurements of the field equipment. Since the complementary data is based on the results of the existing data projection, there is a certain degree of error, especially in complex power systems, can not fully represent the real power load situation on site.

In smart grid scenarios, there is a growing need to employ algorithms and techniques for imputing missing data. Many prior studies have sought to address the issue of missing values in power-system datasets, demonstrating enhanced imputation accuracy5–8. Existing imputation methods can be grouped into traditional time series imputation techniques, machine learning–based approaches, and deep learning–based models. Early statistical imputation methods have long been widely adopted for handling missing entries. The mean imputation technique9 replaces each missing value with the mean of the observed values, yielding expedient and straightforward results. However, this approach typically fails to capture the complex temporal dependencies and periodic patterns inherent in power-system time series10. With the advancement of deep learning and machine learning techniques, an increasing number of researchers have begun to employ deep neural network–based models for time series imputation, particularly in the power data domain with strong temporal characteristics. In recent years, deep models have been widely applied to time series analysis tasks11, including Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP)12, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)13,Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Transformers14 ,Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)15, Pre-trained Foundation Models (PFMs), and Large Language Models (LLMs)11. MLPs represent a popular architecture for time-series data modeling. They generate forecasts from historical observations and, in imputation tasks, estimate missing values based on the true observed data. LTSF-Linear16 is a basic linear model built on MLPs, which employs weighted-sum operations to directly regress on historical time-series data for future prediction. RNNs,originally designed for sequential data modeling17,18,were among the earliest deep architectures adopted for advanced time series analysis and have since been extended to imputation tasks. Convolutional neural networks are widely used in such complex time-series analysis. TimesNet19 innovatively integrates the Fourier transform to convert a one-dimensional time series into a two-dimensional representation, enabling the CNN to learn both inter-period and intra-period features and thereby enhancing its data-processing capabilities. Since the introduction of the Transformer , Transformer-based models have also emerged as a powerful backbone for temporal data analysis. The self-attention mechanism has been extensively used to model temporal dependencies14, allowing these models to learn long-term relationships and complex multivariate correlations. However, owing to the extreme complexity of real-world temporal dynamics, attention alone may struggle to uncover reliable dependencies across discrete time points. GANs facilitate adversarial learning via a minimax game between generator and discriminator; Luo et al.15 propose GRUI-GAN, a two-stage GAN-based imputation method and the first of its kind for time-series data, though its performance is highly sensitive to data quality. PFMs enhance generalization and adaptability through large-scale pre-training on diverse datasets20, and LLMs–by virtue of their extensive parameterization–demonstrate strong sequence-modeling capabilities via next-token prediction, yielding excellent imputation performance. Machine learning methods

In traditional statistical methods, Khan et al.21 used mean imputation to address missing values in electricity theft detection (ETD) under smart meter environments for handling data imbalance problems. Zhao et al.22proposed an intelligent imputation method based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW): by first classifying missing data and then imputing values using the average or median of dates with similar patterns considering seasonal and holiday factors, they improved accuracy. However, the imputation results still exhibited significant deviations in complex power data scenarios.

Machine learning is widely used in missing data repair. Lv et al.23 repaired missing data by improving random forest model. Zhao et al.24 repaired incomplete information by K-nearest neighbor(KNN). Qian et al.25 repaired missing data by building support vector regression (SVR) model to repair the missing data.Crookston et al.26 proposed KNN imputation, which outperforms most of the covariance structures are able to be preserved between the defined Y and X vectors27, by defining the gain by using a weighted Euclidean Distance, and in order to avoid potentially large matrix assignments, only necessary approximations need to be computed when looking for the nearest neighbor of K and used when needed. Wang et al.28 compared linear interpolation, ARIMA models, and machine learning algorithms (including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Support Vector Regression (SVR)) for addressing missing values in power data, concluding that machine learning methods generally outperform traditional statistical approaches. Hanna et al.29 systematically evaluated four models: MLP, Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT) and Linear Regression (LR) to import missing data from electricity consumption. Their findings indicated that while MLP achieved the highest predictive accuracy, it exhibited notable drawbacks including high computational resource consumption and limited generalization capability.

Based on the idea of matrix decomposition, a new interpolation method was proposed by Hastie et al.30 to decompose the data matrix into a product of two or more low-ranks, and columns were used to interpolate the missing values using these low-rank matrices. Gajawada et al.31 utilized clustering technique to interpolate missing values by dividing the samples into different clusters, and interpolating the missing values using the already observed values within each cluster.

The existing methodologies predominantly rely on stacked machine learning architectures that fail to account for temporal dynamics and inter-temporal correlations within sequential data, consequently demonstrating limited efficacy in addressing missing value imputation for time series.

Deep learning methods

In using deep learning based methods to deal with the task of imputing missing values of multi-source time series data, the model is required to capture the temporal relationship of the data and to understand the temporal dependency between the variables, for this reason, Cao32 proposed BRITS, which uses bi-directional RNN to predict the missing values, and matters the missing values as the variables of the bi-directional RNN graphs to deal with the task of imputing the missing values of multi-source time series data.

In recent years, with the continuous development and application of deep learning in various fields, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have performed well in power data timing prediction.Long Short-Term Memory Neural Networks (LSTMs) have solved the problem of vanishing weights in the backpropagation of RNN over time, and are able to extract the periodic features and patterns in the temporal dimension automatically. Yang et al.33 used convolutional neural network for feature extraction of time-series data, combined with LSTM to repair continuous type missing data. Song et al.34 used LSTM to repair missing data by analyzing the full post-complete sequence of missing data. Lu et al.35 used encoder to optimize clustering to group feature similar data into one class, and then used LSTM to repair missing data.

Luo 15 proposed a Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) model based approach for imputing time series missing values by considering the missing values as data generation.Lian et al.36 proposed a GAN-based imputation method for electricity consumption data that incorporates dummy variables to effectively enhance data completeness, albeit at the cost of high computational complexity and strong dependence on data quality. Miao et al. introduced US-GAN37, which employs a discriminator augmented with a temporal attention matrix to increase the discriminator’s training complexity and, consequently, improve the generator’s performance.

The CNN-based model SCINet38 applies standard convolutions within a hierarchical downsampling–convolution–interaction architecture to capture dynamic temporal dependencies at multiple time resolutions in time-series data. TimeNet19 enhances periodicity modeling by transforming the cyclical variations of a raw one-dimensional time series (1D) into a two-dimensional tensor (2D), thereby more effectively capturing and representing periodic features and improving performance through CNN processing. In CSDI39, the CNN serves as a key feature extractor, bolstering the model’s ability to learn complex temporal patterns in time-series data.

Transformer models with an attention mechanism, originally designed for natural language processing tasks, have demonstrated excellent capabilities when dealing with large-scale data and have been successfully applied to learning time series data. The advantage of these architectures is the ability to selectively focus on different parts of the input sequence to more accurately capture temporal and variable dependencies in the time series.Wu et al40 proposed Autoformer, a model that introduces an auto-correlation mechanism to capture temporal dependencies of sequences through learning cycles.Autoformer employs a deep decomposition architecture to efficiently extract the seasonality and trend components of the input series. FEDformer41 enhances the seasonality and trend decomposition with an expert mixture design and implements a sparse attention mechanism in the frequency domain.

DLinear is a variant of LTSF-Linear that combines a decomposition scheme with linear layers. The method decomposes the raw data into two components: a trend component and a seasonality component. It captures long-term trends and cyclical variations in time series more efficiently by separating trend and seasonality components.

Imputation model based on convolutional neural networks

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) excel in handling tasks with localized features and spatial dependencies, especially in the field of computer vision. However, the properties of time series data are also spatially dependent, especially temporal patterns and trends, which makes CNNs equally suitable for feature extraction and pattern recognition of time series.

TimesNet19 introduces the processing of periodic features based on traditional time series analysis to better capture periodic and multi-scale variations in time series. This process involves the conversion from one-dimensional time series data to a two-dimensional tensor and capturing periodic information at different scales by means of a hierarchical convolutional network (e.g., Inception block), the structure of which is shown in Fig. 4.

TimesNet structure.

1D to 2D conversion:

TimeNet is able to better capture and represent periodic features by transforming periodic changes from a raw one-dimensional time series (1D) to a two-dimensional tensor (2D). In this conversion, intra-periodic variations (i.e., changes in details within each period) are represented as rows of the tensor, while inter-periodic variations (i.e., changes between periods) are represented as columns of the tensor. In this way, TimesNet is able to capture both the subtle changes within cycles and the relationships between different cycles. During this transformation, k is a hyperparameter that determines the choice of the number of cycles, denoting a 1D to 2D transformation through several different cycles. This allows the model to accommodate multiple periodic patterns in the time series, e.g., daily, weekly, annual cycles, etc. When performing the transformations, TimesNet makes the dimensions of the input data match the desired output dimensions through a padding operation. This padding ensures that the periodic features of the input sequence are evenly distributed across the dimensions of the tensor, which in turn helps in the efficient execution of the convolution operation.

Apply the Inception block to capture multi-scale features:

The Inception block is a deep learning module that typically contains multiple parallel convolutional layers that can learn different sized convolutional kernels to capture multi-scale features.The Inception block processes each 2D tensor , and learns both intra-periodic and inter-periodic features through multi-scale convolutional kernels. In TimesNet, the Inception block helps the model learn the multi-scale periodic representation. Convert back to 1D:

After the Inception block processing, the resulting 2D representation is converted back to 1D format 1D and aggregated. This step is actually equivalent to compressing the multi-scale information back to 1D for subsequent tasks such as classification and regression.

TimesNet uses a hierarchical convolutional network, which can further enhance the model’s ability to learn multi-level periodic information. Hierarchical convolution helps the network learn different features at different levels, capturing both low-level short-term patterns as well as recognizing high-level long-term periodic changes.

Transformer-based imputation models

Transformer was initially designed for Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, but due to the power of its self-attention mechanism, it has gradually become an important tool for processing time series data. Its main advantage is that the self-attention mechanism can compute the similarity between any two points in time, so Transformer can effectively capture long-term temporal dependencies, unlike traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) that are limited by the order of time steps. Through parallel computation and global dependency modeling, Transformer can handle complex correlations between multivariate time series. This capability allows Transformer to demonstrate excellent performance in multiple time series tasks such as multivariate prediction, anomaly detection, etc.

Transformer embeds temporal markers, which contain a multivariate representation of each time step. iTransformer42 embeds each sequence independently into the variable markers such that the attention module describes the multivariate correlation and the feedforward network encodes the sequence representation, and its model structure is shown in Fig. 5.

Structure of iTransformer.

In the iTransformer model, future values of a particular variant are predicted based on historical time series data .

First, the input time series is transformed into a tokens representing the time series through the Embedding layer. These tokens will be fed as input to the subsequent Transformer model for processing.The Embedding layer maps the time series of dimension T to tokens of dimension D.

Transformer Block: The embedded tokens are passed into a series of Transformer blocks for processing. These blocks capture the relationships between tokens through self-attention and further process them through a feed-forward network. The tokens interact with each other through the self-attention mechanism and are further processed through a shared feed-forward network.

After processing by the Transformer block, the final tokens are mapped back to the output space through the Projection layer, i.e., the future sequence of a particular variant is predicted.

Design and implementation of the box-meter integrated metering device

Overall architecture

The proposed box-meter integrated metering device provides deployable hardware support for the localized data imputation module. Its overall architecture adopts a modular and layered design to ensure system security, scalability, and maintainability. The core of the system consists of a Metrology Core and an Application Core. The Metrology Core is responsible for legally compliant energy measurement, while the Application Core runs various intelligent edge computing applications, including deep learning–based imputation algorithms.In addition, the system integrates multi-protocol communication modules, electrical protection, and remote control components. It features a high-protection physical enclosure and extensible interfaces, forming an intelligent device that combines data sensing, real-time processing, and security protection. The overall system architecture is illustrated in Fig. 6.

The overall system architecture.

Core functional module design

The metrology module of the device integrates a high-precision metering chip and a wide-range manganin shunt, supporting bidirectional energy metering. Independent measurement channels ensure the legality and compliance of metering data. The management module adopts a dual-core separation architecture and executes the data imputation algorithms proposed in this study. It is connected to voltage and current sensors via a high-speed SPI bus to acquire high-resolution raw waveform data, ensuring accurate and real-time inputs for the imputation algorithms. This module operates independently from the metrology core, ensuring that its computational processes do not interfere with statutory metering accuracy.

The protection and switch control module is equipped with a motor-driven miniature circuit breaker, supporting remote switching, overcurrent and undervoltage protection, leakage protection, and anti-islanding functions. It can also interact with the system status monitoring unit to enable comprehensive active protection. The communication module supports Bluetooth, RS485, HPLC, and 4G/5G, satisfying both local high-speed transmission needs and diverse protocol requirements of the main power system. It also features pluggable communication modules for future expandability. For human–machine interaction, the device is equipped with a color OLED display for intuitive local data visualization and includes a reserved analog signal acquisition interface to facilitate secondary development and advanced AI model training and validation.

Data flow and imputation module deployment

The device performs real-time sampling of voltage and current using high-precision voltage and current transformers. Data is transmitted efficiently and without distortion between the management core and sensors via a high-speed SPI bus, avoiding the signal degradation associated with redundant sampling. The imputation module runs on the management core, leveraging its dedicated computing resources and storage space to recover missing data locally, without interfering with the tasks of the metrology core. This module can operate in synergy with other edge computing functions, forming a flexible and scalable intelligent analytics system. After imputation, the structured data is first stored in the Flash memory of the management core to prevent data loss during power outages. It can be visualized locally via the OLED display or uploaded to the power utility’s central system through multi-protocol communication interfaces, ensuring the continuity and reliability of the data pipeline.

Comparative analysis with traditional smart meters

Compared with traditional smart meters, the proposed box-meter integrated metering device offers significant advantages in functional architecture. It integrates metering, protection, control, and physical enclosure functions within a single unit. Its modular, pluggable communication design allows flexible adaptation to various communication protocols. The device provides IP68-rated protection, far exceeding the passive external protection commonly found in traditional smart metering setups, enabling long-term stable operation in harsh environments.

More importantly, the system supports the execution of deep learning-based imputation algorithms within the management core and provides open access to raw waveform acquisition interfaces, overcoming the “black-box” limitations of conventional smart meters. This facilitates advanced applications such as non-intrusive load monitoring, power quality analysis, and predictive analytics. The device’s lifecycle is aligned with that of grid infrastructure, significantly reducing maintenance frequency and long-term operational costs, and embodying a future-oriented, platform-based, and sustainable design philosophy.

Experiments

Dataset

Due to glitches in the power system, the time series data collected in the power supply system may have missing values. The existence of these missing values can significantly affect the subsequent related tasks such as power time series analysis and prediction model training, therefore, time series imputation becomes one of the key techniques to solve this problem. The goal of time series imputation is to speculate and fill in the missing data points through appropriate imputation methods based on the contextual characteristics of the existing data.

The box-meter integrated metering devices are able to collect power data in real time and carry out preliminary processing by integrating smart sensors and edge computing technologies during the data collection process, and the power data collected by these devices, combined with different imputation strategies, can be used to effectively evaluate the effect of imputation methods under different missing rates, especially in the imputation task for different time periods and power loads in complex power system environments.

In this study, we selected the electricity(ECL) dataset as the evaluation dataset to simulate the data collection results of the meter-integrated metering device to explore the performance of the time series imputation task under different missing rates. Specifically, we simulate different levels of missing rates and apply a variety of model-based imputation methods to conduct experiments to evaluate their effectiveness in imputing time-series data of key metrics of customer electricity consumption. Through this process, we hope to understand the effectiveness of different imputation strategies in real-world applications and how to select the most appropriate imputation method based on the characteristics of the data.

By simulating the data acquisition process of box-meter integrated metering device, we utilize the high-quality ECL dataset to investigate and validate power data imputation methods. In the experiment, the ECL dataset emulates the data collection process of box-meter integrated metering device, providing comprehensive time-series data. Through experimental simulations, we evaluate the performance of different imputation methods across various missing-data scenarios to verify their accuracy and reliability in power data restoration.

The ECL dataset contains 321 users’ power consumption recorded at 60-minute intervals in kilowatt (kW) units from 2012 to 2014. With a total length of 26,304 time points and 321 dimensions, the average load is 2538.792 kW with a standard deviation of 15027.571 kW. The maximum load reaches 764000 kW, the dataset comprises 50.8 MB of complete observational data. To simulate realistic data missing scenarios, we implement a masking mechanism that randomly obscures portions of the original data according to predefined missing ratios, while preserving the dataset’s inherent temporal patterns and statistical characteristics.

Experimental setup and metrics

The experiments in this study were conducted using Python 3.9 and the PyTorch 2.0 framework. Numerical computation and data preprocessing were carried out with Numpy and Scipy, while Matplotlib was used for visualization. Model training and inference were performed on two NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs (48GB each). For deployment on the actual box-meter integrated metering device, a pruned and quantized version of the model will be used. However, in the experimental phase, we first validated the algorithm’s accuracy and feasibility on the aforementioned hardware platform.

In order to compare the model capacity under different missing data proportions, we adopt the following strategy: By randomly masking the ECL dataset, we set the missing data proportions as 12. 5%, 25%, 37. 5%, and 50%, respectively. In each proportion, we randomly select time points for the simulation of missing values to construct different imputation tasks.

In order to compare the scale of sequences with different lengths, temporal dependencies (long-term trends, periodic fluctuations), and the impact of data scale on models, we adopt the following strategy: selecting time series of different lengths as inputs, with chosen input segment lengths of 96 and 128.

In order to investigate the effectiveness of different model architectures in various time series imputation tasks, we conducted comparative experiments using the traditional mean imputation method, state-of-the-art models designed with diverse deep architectures, and a generative adversarial network (GAN)-based model. Specifically, the experiments compared the traditional method based on mean imputation, representative Transformer-based models (iTransformer, Autoformer), the CNN-based model TimesNet, the MLP-based model DLinear, and the generative model US-GAN.

Evaluation Metrics: we employ MAE, MSE, RMSE, and MAPE as the performance indicators. To verify the reliability of the experiments, confidence intervals are incorporated as statistical measures. Specifically, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is defined as the average of the absolute differences between the actual and predicted values; it quantifies the average magnitude of prediction errors. Moreover, the Mean Square Error (MSE) is calculated as the average of the squared differences between actual and predicted values, thereby emphasizing larger discrepancies. The RMSE is derived as the square root of the MSE. Furthermore, the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) assesses the ratio of imputation errors relative to the true values. Notably, the imputation results of the 320th variable were analyzed using a 95% confidence interval to further substantiate the experimental findings.

Analysis and discussion

The D-linear combined decomposition scheme combined with the linear layer, according to Table 3, is slightly inferior on the imputation task in the case of high missing values compared to the transformer-based itransformer and the cnn-based timesnet. The possible reason is that MLP mainly relies on local features and cannot effectively capture global temporal patterns. Therefore, it may perform poorly when dealing with time series with long time dependencies. When the percentage of missing data is high, the performance of MLP usually suffers because it cannot effectively model the contextual information of the missing data.

TimesNet shows more effective performance in the combined aspect of imputation for the power imputation task, which is close, though not as good as itransformer, at 12.5% and 25% imputation, and at 37.5% and 50%, TimesNet shows better performance.Times net can extract temporal local features in the sequence and captures time series features at different scales through multiple convolutional layers, which is especially useful in temporal imputation to deal with variations at different frequencies in the time series.

Transformer is the most powerful architecture in the current time series imputation task due to its powerful global modeling capability and efficient parallel computing advantage, and its variant iTransfomer shows better performance in imputation experiments compared with FEDformer and Informer.

In a high missing rate scenario (imputation step length of 96 and mask rate of 50%), Fig. 7 compares the imputation performance on the ECL dataset’s time series features among TimesNet, iTransformer, and DLinear. Taking the 4th feature as an example, TimesNet achieved precise imputation in the low-frequency oscillatory segment (indices 30–60 with ±0.3 fluctuation) through its frequency domain base frequency detection mechanism, with its core advantage rooted in the fast Fourier transform (FFT) that accurately identifies the global periodic length. In contrast, DLinear maintained a smooth imputation trajectory in the same interval but exhibited systematic bias in the periodic truncation region (indices 50–70, maximum deviation 0.4), revealing the generalization limitations of linear decomposition models for incomplete local periodicity. Meanwhile, iTransformer captured transient spikes using reverse embedding and a channel-independent self-attention mechanism; its attention weights in low signal-to-noise ratio regions (e.g., the noise accumulation segment of the 5th feature at indices 80–100) were prone to interference, resulting in a divergent imputation trajectory and a higher RMSE relative to TimesNet.

DLinear shows limitations under high missing rates, particularly when dealing with power data that exhibit long-term dependencies and complex periodicity, as MLP-based models struggle to capture global temporal patterns effectively. In contrast, the CNN-based TimesNet model achieves superior performance in power data imputation tasks, especially under high missing rates, by effectively capturing local features and multi-scale periodic variations in time series. Transformer-based models, such as iTransformer, exhibit strong capabilities in capturing long-term dependencies and global patterns, making them effective for detecting sudden changes. However, the sensitivity of the self-attention mechanism to outliers may lead to overshooting, causing imputed values to exceed expected ranges.

Figure 7 shows that the imputation trend of TimesNet is consistent with the true data trend. In stable segments, imputation values almost coincide with the true values, indicating high imputation accuracy in these regions. Minor fluctuations are captured promptly by the imputation values, whereas in high-amplitude oscillatory segments the tracking performance is less ideal, and the model demonstrates a limited ability to capture abrupt changes. The imputation method is effective for the overall dataset, although improvements in capturing fine details remain necessary.The visualization results suggest that TimesNet performs better for stable or low-fluctuation time series compared with highly dynamic sequences. TimesNet’s adaptive period recognition mechanism has limitations when local periodic patterns in the input sequence deviate significantly from the global statistics, which may lead its Fourier-based period detection module to produce misjudgments.

This study reveals that the imputation task based on the ECL power dataset effectively demonstrates the adaptability of integrating box-meter integrated metering device with imputation models to the multi-temporal characteristics of power systems. TimesNet exhibits robust performance under steady-state conditions with matched fundamental frequency periods, while iTransformer handles sudden events such as equipment start-ups and shutdowns. DLinear provides a feasible solution for conservative imputation in high-noise environments, whereas US-GAN shows higher dependency on data quality.

The integration of box-meter integrated metering device with the TimesNet model for missing data imputation demonstrates unique trade-off characteristics in processing high-frequency disturbances and weak signals, outperforming traditional methods and certain Transformer variants. However, experiments also highlight potential improvements for TimesNet: its imputation results exhibit lagging phenomena during high-frequency transient intervals, and minor phase drifts suggest the need to enhance dynamic correction capabilities for periodic fundamental frequencies. Additionally, decoupled modeling of high-frequency transient components and steady-state signals remains challenging in imputation experiments.

Conclusion

Missing power data is a common problem in power systems, and the missing values have a significant impact on the accuracy of data analysis and decision-making. In order to solve this problem, this paper adopts the box-meter integrated metering device to make up the power data at a fine-grained level, and explores the application of the combination of it and the imputation method based on deep learning in the actual power data. Through its original analog signal acquisition interface, the box-meter integrated metering device can reduce data missing due to sensor problems, communication delays, or equipment maintenance during the data acquisition process, provide high-quality and continuous data input, ensure the timeliness of the data, and provide a more stable basis for the training and validation of the imputation model.

This study investigates the application of deep learning models in temporal data imputation for power systems, focusing on comparing the performance of MLPs, CNNs, GANs, and Transformer models in power data restoration tasks. Experimental results reveal two core limitations of current research: (1) insufficient capability in processing high-frequency transient data, leading to inadequate imputation accuracy for abrupt high-frequency data variations; (2) constraints in algorithm complexity and hardware costs, where high-parameter algorithms face substantial deployment cost barriers. Future work will prioritize further optimization of the TimesNet model and exploratory experiments with other deep learning approaches (e.g., GNNs and LSTM) for power data imputation. With the increasing adoption of box-meter integrated metering device in power systems, the data collected and reconstructed through this integrated framework will provide more reliable support for power data analysis and smart grid management, advancing the intelligent and digital transformation of power systems.
