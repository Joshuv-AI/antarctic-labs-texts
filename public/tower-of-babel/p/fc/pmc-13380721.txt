Combining MRI criterion and pathology-based microvascular invasion improves prognostic stratification in early HCC
Authors: Xinyuan Jia, Junhan Pan, Yanshu Wang, Riccardo Sartoris, Jules Grégory, Aurélie Beaufrère, Chanel R Varney, Danielle E Kruse, Fangfang Fu, Tianyi Xia, Mengsi Li, Yanyan Zhang, Luna Wang, Ming Kuang, Mustafa R Bashir, Feng Chen, Bin Song, Hanyu Jiang, Maxime Ronot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsMicrovascular invasion (MVI) is an established risk factor for postoperative recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but its assessment relies on histopathologic examination which is subject to sampling errors. Therefore, we aimed to develop and externally validate an integrated MRI-pathology prognostic stratification tool (MRI-Pathology Criterion [MPC]), which combines histopathologic MVI and MRI features to predict the risk of early recurrence.MethodsA total of 1,207 consecutive patients (median age, 56.0 years; 993 men; 666 with cirrhosis) with single BCLC 0/A HCC who underwent curative-intent resection and preoperative contrast-enhanced MRI at 12 hospitals across China, France, and the USA were retrospectively included in the training (n = 656) or testing (Eastern, n = 373; Western, n = 178) datasets. Four peritumoral (e.g., portal venous phase [PVP] peritumoral hypoenhancement) and 15 other previously reported prognostic MRI features were evaluated by two radiologists. Using early recurrence-free survival (eRFS; recurrence within 2 years) as the endpoint, an MPC was developed in the training dataset by combining histopathologic MVI and independently prognostic peritumoral MRI feature(s).ResultsIn the training dataset, PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement (hazard ratio 1.83; p <0.001) and histopathologic MVI (hazard ratio 2.59; p <0.001) were independent predictors of eRFS. Accordingly, MPC was defined using three risk groups: high risk (MVI+ and PVP+), intermediate risk (MVI+ and PVP−), and low risk (MVI−). In both testing datasets, eRFS differed significantly across the three groups (eRFS rates for the high-, intermediate-, and low-risk groups [East or West]: 44.6% or 37.5%; 65.6% or 57.6%; 84.7% or 73.9%; all p <0.001). MPC remained predictive of eRFS after adjusting for known prognostic factors, including α-fetoprotein level, tumor size, and Edmondson-Steiner grade (p values <0.001 to 0.006).ConclusionsThe MPC integrates a peritumoral MRI feature with histopathologic MVI to refine postoperative prognostic stratification in HCC and may guide more individualized postoperative management.Impact and implicationsBased on 1,207 patients who underwent curative-intent resection for a single BCLC 0/A HCC at 12 tertiary hospitals across China, France, and the USA, this study demonstrated that integrating portal venous phase (PVP) peritumoral hypoenhancement on preoperative MRI with histopathologic microvascular invasion (MVI) improves prediction of HCC recurrence, potentially mitigating the limitations of sampling variability associated with histopathologic assessment. Using these two variables, the integrated MRI-Pathology Criterion (MPC) may provide a robust framework for postoperative recurrence-risk stratification and may help inform surveillance and adjuvant treatment strategies (e.g. intensified surveillance and longer durations of adjuvant therapy for patients classified as high risk by MPC). These findings are relevant to clinicians, radiologists, and pathologists involved in HCC management. However, prospective interventional studies, ideally randomized controlled trials, are needed to determine whether MPC-guided management improves clinically meaningful outcomes before MPC can be adopted for treatment allocation.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Magnetic resonance imaging, Microvascular invasion, Prognosis

Introduction

Microvascular invasion (MVI) is a well-established risk factor for postoperative recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1,2 Accordingly, MVI status serves as a key determinant in postoperative management, with MVI-positive patients being considered candidates for adjuvant therapy in major randomized clinical trials,[3], [4], [5], [6] and in clinical practice, such status often informs more intensive surveillance strategies and consideration of adjuvant treatment to mitigate recurrence risk. However, owing to substantial heterogeneity in the severity and location of MVI, as well as in the types of small vessels involved, MVI has variable prognostic implications,[7], [8], [9], [10] which likely contributed, at least in part, to the inconsistent results observed across the aforementioned trials.

Currently, histopathologic examination of the surgical specimen remains the gold standard for assessing and grading MVI.8,9,11 For example, MVI of the portal vein branches has been associated with a higher risk of intrahepatic metastasis compared with MVI involving the tumor capsule or peritumoral areas.9,12 Additionally, the extent of MVI – classified as focal (≤5 vessels involved) or extensive (>5 vessels involved) – has been correlated with distinct postoperative outcomes.13 However, the highly heterogeneous spatial distribution of MVI has rendered its histopathologic evaluation susceptible to sampling errors.14,15 Although multi-site sampling is often required to improve reliability, it is time-consuming and labor-intensive, and its accuracy may decline with increasing tumor size owing to a greater risk of MVI underestimation.7,15 On the other hand, because HCC is susceptible to contamination during slide preparation, artifactual displacement of tumor cells into the vascular lumina may result in overestimation of MVI severity.8 Consequently, histopathologic assessment alone may be insufficient for accurate MVI grading.

Emerging data have shed light on the potential of non-invasive imaging techniques, particularly MRI, to complement histopathology for HCC prognostication.[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21] For the assessment of MVI, MRI enables comprehensive evaluation of the entire tumor and peritumoral liver parenchyma, which may help mitigate the concerns associated with sampling errors and slide contamination. Notably, peritumoral MRI features, such as hepatobiliary phase (HBP) peritumoral hypointensity and portal venous phase (PVP) peritumoral hypoenhancement,[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26] have been reported as particularly important imaging predictors of MVI, likely because the peritumoral liver parenchyma is the first area affected by MVI. In addition to indicating the presence of MVI, peritumoral MRI features may also provide information regarding MVI grade, as an increased number and wider involvement of MVI are also more likely to give rise to peritumoral changes on imaging.27 Therefore, peritumoral MRI features hold promise to improve MVI grading and recurrence risk stratification beyond histopathologic examination. However, this topic remains largely understudied, with little known about the impacts of ethnic background or chronic liver disease etiologies.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop an integrated MVI stratification tool combining preoperative peritumoral MRI feature with histopathologic MVI (i.e. MRI-Pathology Criterion [MPC]) and to investigate its utility in predicting postoperative early recurrence (≤2 years) risk in patients undergoing resection for single Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) 0/A HCC.

Materials and methods

This international multi-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at 12 tertiary-care referral hospitals in China, France, and the USA. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at all participating centers with a waiver of the informed consent.

Patients

Between April 2011 and March 2023, consecutive patients who met the following inclusion criteria were identified: (a) age ≥18 years; (b) Child–Pugh class A liver function; (c) receipt of curative-intent resection;9 (d) contrast-enhanced MRI performed within 3 months before surgery; (e) pathologically confirmed solitary BCLC stage 0/A HCC, as these patients represent optimal candidates for resection and provide a clinically relevant and methodologically reliable population for MVI evaluation, given that tumor multifocality is an established risk factor for MVI and that solitary tumors allow more accurate radiologic-pathologic correlation;1,2,7,28,29 and (f) fulfillment of a stringent multi-site sampling requirement for histopathologic assessment of MVI.

Patients were excluded if they (a) had received any previous treatment for HCC; (b) had a current or prior malignancy other than HCC (c) had incomplete imaging data (e.g. absence of key sequences); or inadequate image quality (e.g. severe motion artifacts) that precluded reliable analysis; (d) had ruptured HCC; (e) received any adjuvant treatment after surgery; or (f) lacked follow-up information.

For the development and validation of MPC, patients from two hospitals in China (i.e. West China Hospital and Shangjin Nanfu Hospital) were assigned in the training dataset, those from another eight hospitals across geographically different areas in China in the Eastern testing dataset, and those from the USA (Duke University Medical Center) and France (Beaujon Hospital) in the Western testing dataset. Image acquisition and analysis

The preoperative contrast-enhanced MRIs were acquired on various 1.5 or 3.0 T MRI scanners. Either extracellular (ECA) or hepatobiliary (HBA) agent was used. Detailed MRI acquisition protocols are summarized in the supplementary methods.

The MRIs of the training and Eastern testing datasets were centrally evaluated by two independent abdominal radiologists with 5 (Y.W.) and 9 (H.J.) years of experience in liver MRI, while those of the Western testing datasets were evaluated by two independent abdominal radiologists with 6-8 years of experience at each center. To standardize image interpretation, all reviewers for the derivation cohort completed a hands-on training session using 50 non-study HCC cases before formal image analysis. The reviewers were informed that all patients had a single HCC, but were to the remaining clinical, histopathologic, and follow-up information. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with senior radiologists with over 20 years of experience in liver MRI.

The presence or absence of four peritumoral MRI features previously associated with MVI was assessed across all datasets:[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26] (a) T2-weighted peritumoral mild-to-moderate hyperintensity;25 (b) arterial phase peritumoral hyperenhancement;[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26],30 (c) PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement;25,31 and (d) HBP peritumoral hypointensity.[21], [22], [23], [24], [25] Notably, HBP peritumoral hypointensity was only assessed for patients who underwent HBA-MRI. Definitions, graphical illustrations, and examples of these peritumoral features are shown in Fig. 2.

Definitions, graphical illustrations, and examples of the assessed peritumoral MRI features.

Additionally, 15 other MRI features which have been widely associated with HCC aggressiveness and prognosis were also evaluated for the training and Eastern testing datasets. These features included tumor size (cm), rim arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE, present or absent), internal artery (present or absent), APHE proportion (>50% or ≤50%), non-peripheral washout (present or absent), “capsule” appearance (complete or incomplete [including absent]), tumor margin (smooth or non-smooth), tumor growth subtype (simple nodular type or others), mosaic architecture (present or absent), blood products in mass (present or absent), infiltrative appearance (present or absent), necrosis or severe ischemia (present or absent), marked diffusion restriction (present or absent), fat in mass, more than liver (present or absent), and the 2018 LI-RADS category, which was assessed for all patients regardless of LI-RADS high-risk status (definitions detailed in Table S2).32 For the Western testing dataset, tumor size was evaluated, but data on the remaining 14 prognostic MRI features were not available.

Histopathologic examination

Information on the MVI status, Edmondson-Steiner grade, and satellite nodules was retrieved from routine histopathology reports. Multi-site sampling procedures were performed to ensure reliable assessment of MVI status (supplementary methods). For the training and Eastern testing datasets, the severity of MVI was graded as M1 (≤5 invaded microvessels in the peritumoral liver parenchyma ≤1.0 cm away from the tumor) and M2 (>5 invaded microvessels in the peritumoral liver parenchyma ≤1.0 cm, or any number of invaded microvessels in the peritumoral liver parenchyma >1.0 cm away from the tumor).7

Follow-up

Patients were regularly followed up after resection at 1 month and every 3 to 6 months thereafter with serum AFP and imaging techniques (ultrasound or contrast-enhanced CT/MRI). All patients were followed until death, loss to follow-up, or December 2024.

The primary study endpoint was early recurrence-free survival (eRFS), defined as tumor recurrence or death from any cause within 2 years after resection.16,33,34 The secondary endpoints included recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS), of which the former was computed from surgery to the first documented recurrence or death of any cause, while the latter from surgery to all-cause death. Notably, eRFS was selected over RFS since eRFS has been more closely linked to metastasis of the primary HCC and is driven predominantly by tumor biology, whereas RFS may be confounded by de novo hepatocarcinogenesis, which is largely influenced by the severity of underlying chronic liver disease.16,33,34 eRFS was selected over OS because the latter may be substantially confounded by post-recurrence treatments, which have evolved considerably over our long study period (>10 years) (1,2,7).

Statistical analysis

Multiple imputations were performed to estimate the missing values, which comprised 2.7%-6.6% of the baseline laboratory data. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed for the multivariable Cox regression analysis, as the study satisfied commonly used ‘rule-of-thumb’ criteria, suggesting adequate statistical power.35

Developing MPC in the training dataset

Uni- and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify peritumoral MRI features which predicted eRFS beyond histopathologic MVI (Table 2). Specifically, using eRFS as the outcome, each peritumoral MRI feature and histopathologic MVI were first assessed individually in the univariable Cox analysis. Thereafter, variables with univariable p <0.05 were included for multivariable Cox analysis using a backward stepwise method.

Thereafter, according to different combinations of histopathologic MVI and the independently prognostic peritumoral MRI feature(s), a risk stratification system was then developed by categorizing patients into one of the following four groups: (a) presence of (any) peritumoral MRI feature(s) as well as histopathologic MVI, (b) presence of (any) peritumoral MRI feature(s) in the absence of histopathologic MVI, (c) absence of (all) peritumoral MRI feature(s) in the presence of histopathologic MVI, and (d) absence of (all) peritumoral MRI feature(s) as well as histopathologic MVI. To simplify the risk stratification system, eRFS outcomes of the four above groups were estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test, and groups with similar eRFS outcomes were combined. If more than two groups exhibited similar eRFS outcomes, the combination strategy with the better prediction performance based on the Cox regression analysis was selected. Following these steps, the MPC was developed as an ordinal risk stratification system (Fig. 1B).

Furthermore, uni- and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to investigate the incremental prognostic value of MPC to other known clinical (e.g. age, serum), imaging (prognostic MRI features described previously), surgical (e.g. resection extent), and histopathologic (e.g. Edmondson-Steiner grade) factors for predicting eRFS.

Validating MPC in the testing datasets

First, the capacities of MPC in stratifying eRFS, RFS, and OS risks were externally validated by comparing survival outcomes of different risk groups for the Eastern and Western testing datasets, respectively. Thereafter, to validate the incremental value of MPC to known clinical, imaging, surgical, and histopathologic prognostic features for predicting eRFS, uni- and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed using the same method as described above (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare the associations between MPC-defined risk groups and eRFS outcomes for different age (>/≤50 years), sex (male/female), chronic HBV infection status (yes/no), cirrhosis (present/absent), tumor size (>5/≤5 cm), field strength (1.5/3.0T), contrast-agent type (HBA/ECA), resection type (major/minor), Edmondson-Steiner grade (G1/2 or G3/4), MVI category (M1/M2), and satellite nodule (present/absent) groups. All datasets were combined to improve statistical power.

Sensitivity analyses for HBA-MRI

To evaluate the robustness of our results, we repeated uni- and multivariable Cox regression analyses for all patients who underwent HBA-MRI to develop an MPCHBA, in which HBP peritumoral hypointensity was counted as an additional peritumoral MRI feature. Thereafter, eRFS outcomes of different (a) MRI and MVI combinations as well as (b) MPCHBA-defined risk groups were compared again as described above.

Interrater agreement was investigated by computing the Cohen's kappa or Gwet’s AC1 values (for highly skewed features, defined as frequencies >90% or <10%) for binary features, weighted kappa values for ordinal categorical features, and the intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous features. To account for the potential impacts of contrast agent types and field strengths on the assessment of several imaging features, frequencies and interrater agreement were evaluated separately.

All statistical analyses were conducted with MedCalc (version 20.112; MedCalc Software) or R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-tailed p <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Developing MPC in the training dataset

Only PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement (hazard ratio [HR] 1.83; p <0.001) predicted eRFS beyond histopathologic MVI (HR 2.59; p <0.001) at the multivariable Cox regression analysis (Table 2). Therefore, based on these two factors, patients were assigned to one of the following four groups: (a) “both+” (n = 75); (b) “MVI+ PVP-” (n = 167); (c) “MVI- PVP+” (n = 35); and (d) “both -” (n = 379). No difference in eRFS was detected between the “MVI- PVP+” and “both -” (p = 0.416) groups or between the “MVI- PVP+” and “MVI+ PVP-” (p = 0.091) groups (Fig. 3A), but the combination of “MVI- PVP+” and “both-” groups demonstrated superior prediction of eRFS (Table S3). Therefore, MPC was simplified comprising the (a) high-risk (i.e. “both+”), (b) intermediate-risk (i.e. “MVI+ PVP-”), and (c) low-risk (i.e. “MVI-”) groups.

Subgroup analyses

In subgroup analyses, eRFS outcomes were compared between the MPC intermediate- and high-risk groups to investigate the incremental value of MPC to histopathologic examination for MVI grading. The MPC high-risk group was consistently associated with worse eRFS outcomes than the intermediate-risk group for larger tumors (>5 cm) as well as across subgroups defined by age, cirrhosis, chronic HBV infection, contrast agent type, magnetic field strength, resection type, Edmondson–Steiner grade, and MVI category (p values ranged from <0.001 to 0.020) (Fig. S4).

Sensitivity analyses for HBA-MRI

Sensitivity analysis results were in line with the primary analysis. Specifically, among all patients who underwent HBA-MRI (totally 364 patients: training, n = 223; Eastern testing, n = 88; Western testing, n = 53), only PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement (HR 1.99; p = 0.002) predicted eRFS beyond histopathologic MVI (HR 2.06; p <0.001) at the multivariable Cox regression analysis (Table S4).

eRFS outcomes of different MVI and PVP combinations for the 364 patients who underwent HBA-MRI are shown in Fig. 3D, and those of MPC in Fig. 3H. The cumulative eRFS rates for the MPC-defined high-, intermediate-, and low-risk groups were 34.5% (n = 42), 65.4% (n = 73), and 75.2% (n = 249) (p <0.001), respectively. The median RFS for these groups was 14.0 months (95% CI 5.1–21.6), 30.0 months (95% CI 24.9–40.8), and 51.6 months (95% CI 44.0–61.8) (p <0.001), respectively (Fig. S2). The median OS for these groups was 66.5 months (95% CI 36.3–66.5), not reached, and not reached (p <0.001), respectively (Fig. S3).

Interrater agreement

Interrater agreement on MPC and the peritumoral and other prognostic MRI features analyzed are detailed in Table S5. In brief, the interrater agreement on MPC was excellent regardless of the type of contrast agent (weighted κ: 0.96 [all patients]; 0.95 [patients receiving ECA-MRI]; 0.98 [patients receiving HBA-MRI]). Similarly, irrespective of the type of contrast agent used or field strength, the interrater agreement on peritumoral MRI features was substantial to excellent (Cohen’s κ values, 0.61-0.88). The interrater agreement on the remaining prognostic MRI features was moderate to excellent (κ/intraclass correlation coefficient/Gwet's AC1 values, 0.40-0.99), and similar between different contrast agent types as well as field strengths.

Discussion

In this international multicenter cohort study of 1,207 patients with single BCLC 0/A HCC from 12 tertiary-care referral centers across China, France, and the USA, we found that PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement on MRI can complement histopathologic MVI for better prediction of postoperative recurrence risk. Based on these factors, we developed an integrated MRI-pathology prognostic tool (i.e. MPC), which was externally validated as an effective, robust, and reproducible tool for early recurrence risk stratification.

Histopathologic assessment of MVI is inherently limited by sampling variability, heterogeneity, and slide contamination.3,[7], [8], [9],14,15,33 Our study demonstrates that peritumoral MRI features can augment histopathologic prognostication without increasing clinical workflow cost or complexity. Broadly, MVI-associated MRI features could be categorized into two groups: those reflecting tumor biology (e.g. rim APHE, non-smooth tumor margin) as well as those directly caused by MVI (e.g. peritumoral alterations).21,22 In this study, we focused exclusively on peritumoral MRI features profiling key perfusional (i.e. arterial phase peritumoral hyperenhancement and PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement) and functional (i.e. T2-weighted peritumoral mild-to-moderate hyperintensity and HBP peritumoral hypointensity) alterations, since these features are more specific to MVI.[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26] Among these features, PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement may indicate decreased portal venous blood flow following minute portal venule obstructions;9,25,31 arterial phase peritumoral hyperenhancement might imply neovascularization and/or increased compensated arterial blood flow following MVI;21,26 T2-weighted peritumoral mild-to-moderate hyperintensity could reflect dilations of sinusoids and tissue edema;25 while HBP peritumoral hypointensity is likely related to decreased uptake of EOB because of impaired hepatocyte function following MVI.[21], [22], [23], [24], [25] Furthermore, these peritumoral alterations may also be associated with key tumor immune microenvironment features (e.g. infiltration of CD8 +T cells, expression of programmed cell death ligand 1).36,37 Interestingly, PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement was consistently the strongest peritumoral MRI predictor of eRFS beyond histopathologic MVI, potentially attributed to the more frequent and biologically aggressive involvement of small portal veins than other vessels (e.g. hepatic veins, arteries, and small bile ducts).9 However, because rigid radiologic–pathologic correlation using spatial sampling and co-registration was not performed, these biological correlates remain largely empirical and require further validation and refinement in future studies.

Substantial differences have been reported in the epidemiological, clinical, histopathologic, and molecular landscapes between patients with HCC from the East and West.1,2,7 For example, whereas chronic HBV infection is the predominant cause of chronic liver disease in Asia,7 MASLD is more frequent in Western countries,1,2,28 as observed in our study. HBV-related and MASLD-related HCCs have been reported to exhibit distinct tumor biology.28,29 Despite these differences, the consistent validation of MPC in both the Eastern and Western testing datasets underscores its generalizability across diverse patient populations and clinical settings. Furthermore, the MPC remained an independent predictor of eRFS after adjusting for several established clinical, imaging, and histopathologic prognostic factors (e.g. serum α-fetoprotein, tumor size, rim APHE, and Edmondson-Steiner grade). Additionally, despite potential impacts of contrast agent on the interpretation of arterial phase peritumoral hyperenhancement and PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement, the interrater agreement for MPC was consistently excellent irrespective of the field strengths (1.5/3.0 T) or the type of contrast agent used (ECA/HBA). The above results highlight the effectiveness and robustness of MPC in predicting postoperative eRFS and underscore its incremental prognostic value to current biomarkers.

Several histopathologic subtypes have been proposed according to the extent and location of MVI (e.g. M1/M2), as well as the number and types of microscopic vessels involved.[7], [8], [9], [10] However, none of these subtypes is resistant to sampling errors or time-consuming and labor-intensive microscopic assessment. Therefore, no universally accepted MVI grading system currently exists. By contrast, the MPC integrates histopathologic and imaging data to overcome these limitations, enabling prognostic stratification of MVI-positive HCC into two distinct risk categories: a particularly high-risk group (PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement present) and an intermediate-risk group (PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement absent), independent of the MVI subtype (i.e. M1/M2) and several other clinical, histopathologic, and surgical factors (e.g. tumor size, Edmondson-Steiner grade, satellite nodules, resection type).7,10 Taken together, our work may be a preliminary proof for the evolving concept that imaging and pathology are temporal (i.e. before and after surgery) and spatial (i.e. granularity and entirety) complements, and patients could benefit from an integrated approach that leverages the unique strengths of these two modalities.21

Collectively, MPC may serve as an easy-to-use prognostic tool for better postoperative recurrence risk stratification and a decision-making tool to inform individualized adjuvant therapy and follow-up. For example, the recent failures of phase III randomized controlled trials investigating adjuvant therapies in HCC were likely in part due to suboptimal patient selection6 and/or inadequate administrating duration of the adjuvant therapies.5 The MPC could potentially improve selection of adjuvant therapy candidates (i.e. by providing more granular prediction of the recurrence risk) and regimens (e.g. by prolonging the duration of adjuvant therapy for the MPC-defined high-risk patients). Furthermore, the MPC may also help establish a hierarchical postoperative monitoring protocol that incorporates the most intensified follow-up strategy (e.g. shorter follow-up interval, use of abdominal MRI over CT/ultrasound, and serum tumor markers) for high-risk patients, a less intensified follow-up strategy for intermediate-risk patients, and the least intensified follow-up strategy for low-risk patients. To enhance clinical utility, we also proposed a structured report which may be easily adopted in clinical practice. However, prospective interventional studies, ideally randomized controlled trials, are required to confirm a clinically relevant survival benefit before MPC can be used for treatment allocation.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias. Second, the differences in MRI scanner, protocols, field strengths and contrast agents across different centers, as well as spanning the long study period, may have led to variability in imaging quality and interpretation. However, the consistent performance of the MPC across diverse datasets suggests applicability of the MPC in a real-world context where MRI equipment and technique is inherently heterogeneous.30 Third, histopathologic sampling methods varied across participating centers, precluding uniform spatial radiologic-pathologic correlations. Moreover, most prognostic MRI features were not available in the Western test dataset, which hampered a granular validation of the MPC and may affect the generalizability of our findings to Western patient populations. Finally, despite the potential of the MPC to inform postoperative adjuvant therapy and follow-up decisions, these roles are largely hypothetical without direct evidence from prospective interventional studies.26,[38], [39], [40] Therefore, future prospective studies are encouraged to investigate the utility of the MPC as a decision-making tool, ideally with standardized imaging acquisition, sampling protocols, treatment protocols, and dedicated radiologic-pathologic co-registrations.

In summary, by analyzing 1,207 patients with single BCLC 0/A HCC, this international multicenter study developed an integrated prognostic tool called “MPC” based on PVP peritumoral hypoenhancement on MRI and histopathologic MVI, which improved stratification of postoperative early recurrence risk. The MPC may serve as an easily accessible, robust, and reproducible decision-making tool.
