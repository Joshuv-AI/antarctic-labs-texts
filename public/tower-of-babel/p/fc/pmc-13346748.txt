Optimizing third-line and beyond therapies for metastatic colorectal cancer in Taiwan: an evidence-based consensus
Authors: Pei-Hung Chang, Meng-Che Hsieh, Hao-Wei Teng, Yi-Hsin Liang, Hong-Hwa Chen, Hung-Chih Hsu, Jeng-Kai Jiang, Feng-Fan Chiang, Johnson Lin, Been-Ren Lin, Yao-Yu Hsieh, Tao-Wei Ke, Feng-Che Kuan, Wen-Shih Huang, Bo-Wen Lin, Yu-Yao Chang, Yu-Li Su, William Tzu-Liang Chen, Chien-Kuo Liu, Jaw-Yuan Wang

ABSTRACT
Background:Metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) is a major public health burden in Taiwan. Despite multiple later-line therapies, outcomes remain suboptimal. International guidelines provide valuable direction, but are difficult to apply locally due to differences in drug accessibility, reimbursement policies, and practice patterns.Objectives:This initiative developed a Taiwan-specific, consensus framework for managing mCRC in the third line and beyond.Design:This consensus integrated global evidence with local practice, real-world data, and reimbursement considerations.Methods:A multidisciplinary panel of 20 experts convened by the Taiwan Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (TSCRS) used a modified Delphi process. Initial recommendations were adapted from European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), Pan-Asian ESMO, and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, supplemented by Taiwan real-world data. Consensus was achieved through three structured rounds of voting and discussion, with final statements graded by level of evidence and strength of agreement.Results:Twelve recommendations spanning three domains were endorsed: disease management, third-line options, and extended strategies. Key local adaptations include aligning imaging schedule intervals with Taiwan’s National Health Insurance reimbursement policies and prioritizing trifluridine/tipiracil and regorafenib as preferred third-line agents. The panel acknowledged emerging options such as fruquintinib, epidermal growth factor receptor antibody rechallenge, and novel chemotherapy combinations. While the panel strongly supported biomarker-informed treatments, their broader implementation is constrained by limited reimbursement coverage for next-generation sequencing. Other challenges included limited drug access, suboptimal uptake of biomarker testing, and reimbursement constraints.Conclusion:This Taiwan-specific consensus provides a practical, evidence-based framework for late-line mCRC treatment, bridging global standards with local realities to improve policy alignment and optimize patient outcomes.

Introduction

Metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) presents a substantial clinical challenge and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality worldwide. In Taiwan, colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most commonly diagnosed malignancies and the third leading cause of cancer-related death.1,2 Despite advances in screening, early detection, and standard first- and second-line treatments,3,4 including chemotherapy regimens such as leucovorin (LV), 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) plus oxaliplatin, and LV, 5-FU plus irinotecan (FOLFIRI), as well as targeted therapies such as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) and anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (anti-EGFR) agents, frequently administered in RAS (rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog) wild type (WT) mCRC, 5 the disease continues to represent a major therapeutic challenge in Taiwan. 6 Beyond the second-line setting, treatment options are limited, and survival benefits remain modest in real-world practice.

While international treatment guidelines continue to evolve and offer a range of options beyond second-line therapy, the prognosis for patients with mCRC remains poor, reflecting persistent unmet needs. 6 The Pan-Asian adapted European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines emphasize the importance of regionally tailored approaches that account for differences in tumor biology, drug accessibility, and healthcare infrastructure across Asia. 7 However, most supporting data comes from Western populations, which limits its direct applicability to Taiwanese clinical practice. Although biomarker-guided strategies—including KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog), NRAS (neuroblastoma rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog), BRAF (v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1), and HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2) mutational status, as well as MSI/MMR (microsatellite instability/mismatch repair) status—are standard in treatment selection,7,8 evidence on the optimal sequencing within the local context remains limited. 6

Emerging real-world data from Taiwan have identified distinct patterns in the efficacy and tolerability of third-line agents such as trifluridine/tipiracil (FTD/TPI) and regorafenib, particularly regarding survival outcomes compared to Western cohorts.9,10 These findings underscore the need to contextualize treatment strategies based on regional evidence and practice patterns. 7

To address these gaps, this initiative developed a Taiwan-centric framework through a structured modified Delphi process. The resulting consensus builds upon prior guidance from the Taiwan Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons,6,11 which initially focused on patient selection for cytoreductive surgery and general principles of mCRC management. This updated consensus builds on that foundation by addressing the complex therapeutic decisions required in third-line and subsequent treatment settings, integrating global evidence and local clinical insights to provide practice-ready recommendations for Taiwanese patients with mCRC.

Methodology

Rationale for a modified Delphi approach

Given the evolving treatment landscape and diverse clinical considerations in mCRC beyond the second-line setting, a modified Delphi approach was adopted to develop a contextually relevant consensus for Taiwan. This method is particularly suited for synthesizing expert opinion where high-quality data are limited, and where regional healthcare systems, such as Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI), significantly impact treatment decisions.12–15

In contrast to a conventional anonymous Delphi design based exclusively on repeated anonymous questionnaires, this modified approach incorporated iterative expert panel discussions, conducted both virtually and in person to facilitate dynamic exchange, consensus refinement, and contextual calibration of recommendations. This approach was intentionally selected to balance methodological rigor with feasibility, and to allow contextual interpretation of evidence within Taiwan’s healthcare system and real-world practice environment. Emphasis was placed on integrating international evidence with local practice realities to ensure relevance and feasibility. The reporting of this study conforms to the Conducting and REporting DElphi Studies (CREDES) statement, 16 and the completed checklist is provided as Supplemental Table S1. In this context, “evidence-based” refers to the integration of available clinical evidence, international guideline recommendations, real-world data, and structured expert consensus in areas where high-level prospective evidence remains limited.

Evidence synthesis

Preliminary recommendations were derived from a systematic review of current international clinical guidelines, including the 2023 ESMO Guidelines, 3 the Pan-Asian Adapted ESMO Clinical Practice Guidelines, 7 and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines. 4 To enhance contextual relevance and real-world applicability, these international guidelines were supplemented with a review of Taiwanese real-world data and insights derived from local clinical practice.

A multidisciplinary expert panel was assembled to ensure both scientific rigor and practical insight. The panel comprised colorectal surgeons and medical oncologists from institutions across Northern, Central, and Southern Taiwan. Inclusion criteria were as follows:

Consensus development

Three expert meetings involving 20 participants were conducted using an iterative, modified Delphi process (Figure 1). Draft statements were iteratively reviewed and refined. For each statement, both the level of evidence and the grade of recommendation—adapted from the Pan-Asian ESMO Guidelines 7 —were proposed by the facilitation team and then independently voted on by the panel. Voting was conducted anonymously during each round to minimize bias and ensure independent judgment. Disagreements were addressed through structured group discussions moderated by facilitators, followed by iterative revision of statements and subsequent re-voting where needed. Consensus thresholds were predefined, and statements not reaching consensus were revised or consolidated based on panel feedback. The criteria for achieving consensus were predetermined, and the final agreement levels were reported descriptively, in accordance with established Delphi methodology.13,15 Participation across rounds reflected the pragmatic nature of this modified Delphi process.

Validation of scientific and practical relevance

Throughout the process, each recommendation was appraised against two key criteria: scientific validity and operational feasibility. Considerations included alignment with global evidence, relevance to Taiwan’s healthcare infrastructure, and barriers such as patient access and reimbursement constraints. This dual-filtered evaluation ensured that the final guidance is both evidence-based and implementable in routine practice. 15

Results

Twelve consensus recommendations were finalized (Table 2), encompassing three core domains: disease management, third-line treatment options, and extended treatment duration. Each recommendation is presented alongside its corresponding level of evidence and the degree of expert consensus achieved, as determined through the structured modified Delphi process. Relative to the 10 initial draft statements, 2 were subdivided to improve clinical specificity, and several others were consolidated following iterative panel feedback.

Disease management

Recommendation 1.1: Imaging assessment

It is recommended that imaging evaluations (computed tomography (CT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)) are conducted at least every 6–12 weeks, with adjustments based on patient condition [IV, A].

This recommendation underscores the pivotal function of periodic imaging in the timely identification of disease progression. The expert panel emphasized that maintaining a 6- to 12-week imaging interval is essential not only for clinical oversight but also for conforming to reimbursement provisions under Taiwan’s NHI system. Specifically, continual reimbursement for therapies such as FTD/TPI and regorafenib frequently requires imaging re-evaluation bi-monthly.

This approach is broadly aligned with global directives, encompassing the ESMO and Pan-Asian ESMO guidelines, which advocate for imaging every 8–12 weeks during active treatment to evaluate disease management and inform therapeutic decisions.3,7 However, in Taiwan, imaging is often performed at the shorter end of the recommended interval due to reimbursement requirements under the NHI system.

Recommendation 1.2: Tumor marker assessment

It is recommended to perform tumor marker evaluations (carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) ± carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9)) at least every 12 weeks, with frequency adjusted based on patient condition [I, A].

CEA remains a broadly endorsed biomarker for evaluating tumor burden and therapeutic efficacy in mCRC. The inclusion of CA 19-9 in this recommendation should be interpreted as adjunctive rather than routine, particularly in scenarios where tumor heterogeneity may result in variable biomarker expression, a consideration of practical significance in Taiwanese clinical practice.17,18 By integrating CA 19-9 alongside CEA, clinicians can attain a more exhaustive understanding of disease dynamics. While international guidelines such as NCCN and ESMO primarily emphasize CEA for tumor marker surveillance, they also advocate systematic tumor marker monitoring as part of overall disease assessment.3,4

Recommendation 2: Systemic treatment plus local therapy

It is recommended to consider local therapies during systemic treatment for patients with oligometastatic or stable disease to improve disease control [II, A].

The integration of local therapies into systemic treatment protocols plays an important role in the management of mCRC, particularly beyond the second-line setting. A robust consensus was reached among all panel experts (100%), affirming that appropriately timed local interventions can enhance disease management and should be considered an indispensable element of comprehensive mCRC care.

Specifically, a panel survey demonstrated high levels of concordance regarding the optimal timing of local therapy:

This consensus aligns with ESMO and Pan-Asian ESMO recommendations, which advocate for the integration of local therapies either as an initial strategy or subsequent to systemic disease control, particularly in patients with oligometastatic disease.3,7

Widely adopted modalities—defined as those utilized by ⩾70% of centers—include surgical resection, radiation therapies (e.g., stereotactic body radiotherapy, intensity-modulated radiation therapy, proton therapy, heavy-ion therapy), and thermal ablation methods such as radiofrequency ablation and microwave ablation. Thermal ablation is increasingly acknowledged as a minimally invasive and effective alternative, as underscored in the phase III COLLISION study. 20 Less frequently employed local therapies (<70%)—including hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), cryoablation, and selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT-Y90, selective internal radiation therapy transarterial radioembolization)—are limited to specialized centers and clinical trials. 21 Notably, a recent meta-analysis suggests that HAIC may improve overall survival (OS) and tumor response in patients with colorectal liver metastases, although considerable heterogeneity restricts the generalizability of these findings. 22

Ultimately, the selection and timing of local therapy should be individualized, encompassing tumor biology, systemic treatment response, patient performance status, and institutional capabilities. Multidisciplinary team involvement remains crucial to navigate the integration of local therapy within the overarching mCRC treatment strategy.3,4,7

Third-line treatment options

Recommendation 3: Treatment options—principles

It is recommended to prioritize agents not previously used in earlier lines of therapy, in alignment with established international guidelines (NCCN/ESMO), health insurance coverage, biomarker-directed strategies, and available clinical trials [IV, A].

This recommendation reflects the principle of individualized treatment sequencing, which aims to maximize efficacy and avoid resistance by selecting therapies based on prior exposure, tumor biology, and molecular profile. While global guidelines provide a foundation,3,4 their application in Taiwan requires adaptation due to differences in drug accessibility and reimbursement coverage.

Following progression on standard first- and second-line regimens, foundational third-line agents, such as FTD/TPI and regorafenib, provide modest survival benefits and are widely used in unselected patient populations. Their clinical utility is supported by real-world data demonstrating measurable improvements in OS. 23 More recently, combinations such as FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab and fruquintinib have demonstrated superior clinical outcomes and are now considered standard later-line therapies. 24

In molecularly defined subgroups, treatment personalization is critical. RAS WT patients without resistance mutations on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) may be candidates for anti-EGFR rechallenge. Targeted therapies, including HER2-directed regimens, BRAF-targeted combinations (e.g., encorafenib plus cetuximab), and KRASG12C inhibitors, have demonstrated meaningful clinical benefit when appropriately selected. 24 A recent publication in Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology lends further support to this paradigm, underscoring the importance of evidence-based sequencing strategies and providing a practical algorithm to guide therapeutic selection in later-line mCRC. 24

The panel unanimously agreed that selecting unused agents, while considering Taiwan’s healthcare constraints, reflects sound clinical judgment and may improve outcomes by avoiding resistance to prior therapies.

Recommendation 4.1: Drug—FTD/TPI

It is recommended that the following regimens are used for FTD/TPI-based treatment:

FTD/TPI monotherapy [I, A]

FTD/TPI monotherapy [I, A]

FTD/TPI + bevacizumab [I, A]

FTD/TPI + bevacizumab [I, A]

FTD/TPI + anti-EGFR (panitumumab/cetuximab) [II, B]

FTD/TPI + anti-EGFR (panitumumab/cetuximab) [II, B]

FTD/TPI + chemotherapy (irinotecan/oxaliplatin) [II, B]

FTD/TPI + chemotherapy (irinotecan/oxaliplatin) [II, B]

FTD/TPI monotherapy is a well-established third-line treatment or beyond for mCRC, substantiated by high-level evidence from the RECOURSE and TERRA phase III trials, both of which demonstrated significant improvements in both OS and progression-free survival (PFS).25,26 Consequently, it is endorsed in international guidelines, including ESMO, Pan-Asian ESMO, and NCCN.3,4,7

The panel reached unanimous consensus (100%) on the recommendation for both FTD/TPI monotherapy and its combination with bevacizumab. The latter is substantiated by phase III SUNLIGHT trial findings, which evidenced superior OS and PFS in comparison to FTD/TPI alone, 27 resulting in its inclusion as a preferred regimen in ESMO and NCCN guidelines.3,4 A nationwide, population-based study using Taiwan’s NHI database by Chang et al. reported a median OS of 7.5 months for FTD/TPI and 6.5 months for regorafenib. While these real-world findings are informative, interpretation is limited by the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of biomarker or performance status data. 10 The optimal treatment sequence between FTD/TPI and regorafenib remains unclear. While two recent multicenter retrospective studies in Italy reported their survival outcomes with different treatment sequences,28,29 our nationwide, population-based study using Taiwan’s NHI database by Hsiao et al. 9 demonstrated that FTD/TPI first, followed by regorafenib, showed a better survival benefit in chemotherapy-refractory patients with mCRC across all sidedness. 9

Regarding investigational combinations, 79% of the panel advocated for the utilization of FTD/TPI in conjunction with anti-EGFR agents (e.g., cetuximab or panitumumab) in RAS WT patients, particularly in the rechallenge setting. This reflects an increasing institutional experience and preliminary-phase studies suggesting clinical benefit, especially in patients previously responsive to anti-EGFR therapy.30,31 Local retrospective data from Taiwan further demonstrated a disease control rate (DCR) of 70.0% versus 30.0% with FTD/TPI plus anti-EGFR rechallenge compared to FTD/TPI plus anti-VEGF, in patients previously treated with first-line anti-EGFR regimens. 32

Additionally, 79% of panelists endorsed the combination of FTD/TPI with cytotoxic agents such as irinotecan or oxaliplatin. Although these regimens remain investigational, emerging data from exploratory studies including a recent phase II trial of FTD/TPI plus irinotecan and bevacizumab demonstrating an objective response rate (ORR) of 25.8% and median PFS of 9.2 months, suggest potential benefit in selected patients. 33

Recommendation 4.2: FTD/TPI—Dosing schedule

It is recommended that for patients receiving FTD/TPI-based therapy, the 5–9 dosing schedule (5-day-on/9-day-off, every 2 weeks) is considered alongside the standard 2-week-on/2-week-off regimen every 4 weeks to improve treatment tolerance and reduce adverse effects [II, A].

The adoption of the 5–9 dosing schedule has been demonstrated to mitigate hematological adverse effects—particularly neutropenia—without compromising therapeutic efficacy. This dosing paradigm was supported by the panel based on empirical evidence from the BiTS & TAS-CC4 studies.34,35 By facilitating a more gradual exposure to the pharmacological agent, the alternative regimen enhances treatment tolerability and patient quality of life. The recommendation underscores the necessity for personalized dosage adjustments predicated on patient tolerance and clinical discernment.

Recommendation 5.1: Drug—Regorafenib

It is recommended that the following regimens are used for regorafenib-based treatment:

Regorafenib monotherapy [I, A]

Regorafenib monotherapy [I, A]

Regorafenib + immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [III, B]

Regorafenib + immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [III, B]

Regorafenib + chemotherapy [IV, U]

Regorafenib + chemotherapy [IV, U]

Regorafenib monotherapy is a well-established option for patients with mCRC who have progressed following standard treatments. Its efficacy is supported by robust evidence from pivotal phase III trials—the international CORRECT trial and the Asia-specific CONCUR trial—which demonstrated noteworthy survival.36,37 This recommendation aligns with major international guidelines advocating sequential treatment to ensure patients receive all active agents throughout the disease management continuum.3,4,7

The panel reached unanimous consensus (100%) in endorsing regorafenib monotherapy. Taiwanese real-world evidence further supports its clinical applicability: the CORRELATE Taiwan study documented a median OS of 11.6 months with manageable toxicity. 38 A nationwide cohort analysis of 3643 patients showed that left-sided primary tumors and regorafenib–chemotherapy combinations were independently associated with improved OS, particularly in those with KRAS WT tumors. 39

Combination strategies integrating regorafenib and ICIs garnered support from 84% of the panel, reflecting encouraging early-phase findings. Notably, the REGONIVO study demonstrated promising response rates in microsatellite stable mCRC treated with regorafenib plus nivolumab. 40 A subsequent phase II trial in the United States reported modest overall activity; however, a superior ORR of 22% was observed in patients without liver metastases. 41 Although preliminary, these findings suggest potential clinical benefit in selected patients with favorable disease biology or constrained remaining treatment alternatives.

Regorafenib–chemotherapy combinations remain investigational. A prospective study combining regorafenib with FOLFIRI, including UGT1A1-guided irinotecan dose escalation, demonstrated a DCR of 69.2%, a median PFS of 9.5 months, and a median OS of 13.0 months in previously treated patients with mCRC. 42

Although merely 47% of panelists endorsed further exploration of this approach, consensus emphasized the necessity for validation through larger, controlled clinical trials. Until such evidence is available, these regimens should be confined to academic or clinical trial settings where institutional expertise and appropriate patient selection can be ensured.

Recommendation 5.2: Regorafenib—Dose escalation

It is recommended that for patients receiving regorafenib-based therapy, a modified dose-escalation strategy—starting with a lower initial dose and gradually increasing it based on patient tolerance to the full-dose 160 mg daily—is considered to improve adherence and maintain treatment efficacy without compromising safety [II, A].

This strategy is strongly supported by the phase II ReDOS trial, which demonstrated that initiating regorafenib at 80 mg/day with weekly increments to 160 mg significantly improved treatment continuity and was associated with fewer high-grade toxicities compared to starting at the standard full dose. 43 Patients in the dose-escalation arm were more likely to initiate subsequent treatment cycles and reported better early quality-of-life outcomes. Similar results were also found in a comprehensive sub-analysis of the Italian ReTrITA study, 44 involving 729 patients who received treatment with regorafenib. Patients utilizing a dose-escalation strategy demonstrated extended PFS and a reduction in severe adverse events relative to those on fixed dosing, with OS remaining similar across both groups. The findings demonstrate that flexible dose escalation is a viable method to achieve a balance between efficacy and tolerability in refractory mCRC within a routine practice context, thereby endorsing its application as a practical strategy to enhance treatment outcomes. Real-world prescribing patterns in Taiwan similarly favor lower initial doses, which have not been associated with compromised survival outcomes, further validating this approach in routine clinical settings. 39 The panel noted that this strategy aligns with NCCN guidelines, which advocate for individualized dosing to optimize tolerability while maintaining clinical benefit. 4

Recommendation 6: Drug—Fruquintinib

It is recommended that fruquintinib be considered as a treatment option for patients who have progressed after at least two prior lines of therapy, in accordance with its current regulatory approval in Taiwan [I, B].

Fruquintinib has shown significant clinical benefit in heavily pretreated mCRC, as demonstrated in the FRESCO-2 trial, which reported improvement in OS and PFS. 45 Reflecting these findings, recent international guidelines—including NCCN and Pan-Asian ESMO—now list fruquintinib as a recommended later-line therapy for mCRC.4,7

In Taiwan, fruquintinib has now received regulatory approval for third-line treatment of mCRC, aligning with its use in international practice. The expert panel emphasizes the need for structured implementation pathways and targeted clinician education to support its safe and effective integration into routine clinical care.

Recommendation 7: Genetic testing and biomarker-guided therapy

It is recommended that for mCRC patients undergoing third-line treatment, comprehensive genetic testing be conducted if not previously performed. This should include assessment of KRAS/NRAS and BRAF mutations, HER2 amplification, and MSI/MMR status, using either individual assays or next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels where feasible [I, A].

Despite these limitations, local data support the clinical value of precision treatment. A Taiwanese multicenter registry of patients with RAS WT mCRC treated with first-line anti-EGFR therapy (primarily cetuximab-based) reported a median OS of 35.1 months and PFS of 14.6 months. Left-sided mCRC had a significantly higher DCR (88.9% vs 73.1%, p < 0.001) and better OS (36.4 vs 19.6 months, p < 0.001). 5

In addition, third-line rechallenge with anti-EGFR based chemotherapy is an effective option for patients whose RAS status remains wild type. 59 In selected RAS/BRAF WT patients, anti-EGFR rechallenge has demonstrated clinically meaningful activity, with reported response rates of approximately 20%–30% in phase II studies. 60 Re-biopsy for molecular profiling or ctDNA analysis is strongly recommended to confirm RAS status prior to initiating anti-EGFR-based therapy. 57 However, repeat RAS testing and ctDNA analysis are not reimbursed by Taiwan’s NHI, which limits the widespread use of anti-EGFR-based chemotherapy in the third-line setting. 61

In parallel, a national review highlighted the potential of comprehensive genomic profiling to uncover actionable targets, although system-level challenges limit widespread adoption. 61

Routine biomarker testing prior to third-line therapy is thus essential to enabling precision oncology in Taiwan. Additional details on test availability and reimbursed treatments are summarized in SupplementalTables S2 and S3.

Extend treatment duration

Recommendation 8: Continuation of subsequent therapy beyond the third line

It is recommended that patients with mCRC who remain medically fit should be offered subsequent lines of therapy beyond the third-line setting [IV, A].

This recommendation reflects the expert panel’s consensus that continued treatment, even after standard therapies have been exhausted, may confer meaningful clinical benefits in appropriately selected patients. Treatment decisions should be individualized, taking into account the patient’s performance status, prior response to therapy, comorbidities, and overall treatment goals.3,4

Recommendation 9: Rechallenge and new combination regimens

It is recommended that patients with mCRC receiving third-line and beyond treatments be considered for rechallenge with previously effective therapies or the introduction of new combination regimens [II, A].

The panel unanimously affirmed anti-EGFR rechallenge as a valid option for patients who previously derived clinical benefit. However, the majority (84%) would reserve its use for the fourth-line or later settings, with only 16% recommending it in the third-line setting. Rechallenge decisions should be individualized based on molecular profiling, resistance assessment, and the duration since prior anti-EGFR exposure. Data from the CHRONOS, GERCOR, and CRICKET trials highlight the value of ctDNA in selecting RAS/BRAF WT mCRC patients most likely to benefit.57,58,60

Alternative regimens to consider include biweekly irinotecan–cetuximab, 62 the SL regimen (S-1 with LV), 63 reduced-intensity FOLFOXIRI, 64 and lenvatinib-based combinations. 65 Real-world Taiwanese data support the use of bevacizumab-based regimens in patients with brain metastases, 66 and continued anti-VEGF therapy beyond progression, such as bevacizumab, remains an accepted strategy in local practice. 67

Although clinical evidence is limited, recent findings—including a phase II trial—support the feasibility of these alternatives. 68 The panel agreed they warrant consideration in patients with no remaining standard options.

Discussion

This consensus underscores the need for mCRC treatment strategies in Taiwan that are not only evidence-based but also attuned to local clinical realities. International guidelines from ESMO, Pan-Asian ESMO, and NCCN serve as valuable references, but their direct application is often limited by local reimbursement policies, healthcare infrastructure, and population-specific practice patterns.

NHI-driven divergence in disease monitoring

A notable divergence from international guidelines lies in disease monitoring. Taiwan’s NHI mandates bi-monthly renewal applications for late-line agents such as FTD/TPI and regorafenib, contingent on radiologic evidence of disease control. As a result, routine CT or MRI scans are typically scheduled every 6–8 weeks—more frequent than the typical 8–12-week intervals recommended internationally3,7—placing additional demand on radiology resources and emphasizing the importance of early imaging response.

Pragmatic sequencing and reimbursement barriers

The panel broadly supported initiating third-line therapy with novel agents not previously used, most commonly FTD/TPI or regorafenib. In selected cases, such as rapid progression or visceral crisis, earlier anti-EGFR rechallenge may be justified, particularly in patients with prior durable responses and no resistance mutations on ctDNA.

While both FTD/TPI and regorafenib are reimbursed as monotherapies, their combination with targeted agents or ICIs requires out-of-pocket payment, limiting patient access and highlighting the need for reimbursement reform to support evolving evidence-based practices.

Fragmented genomic testing and access disparities

Taiwan’s precision oncology landscape is shaped by a two-tiered testing system. Single-gene assays (e.g., KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, HER2, MMR) are covered by NHI, but comprehensive NGS remains self-funded. This fragmented approach impedes the widespread implementation of biomarker-driven therapies and reinforces inequities in access to personalized care.

Evidence and implementation gaps

Despite the strengths of this consensus, several gaps warrant further investigation. There is a paucity of local real-world data on the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of combining FTD/TPI or regorafenib with biologics or immunotherapies. Prospective, Taiwan-specific data in the third-line and later-line mCRC setting remain limited, and this consensus necessarily relies on expert judgment in areas where high-level evidence is lacking. The consensus-based nature of this work may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, both beyond Taiwan and outside major academic centers. Furthermore, the use of structured discussion in the modified Delphi process may have introduced some degree of group influence compared with a purely anonymous design. Meanwhile, access to comprehensive genomic profiling is constrained, particularly due to reimbursement limitations under Taiwan’s NHI system. Although multidisciplinary management is advocated across guidelines, its availability and coordination remain inconsistent in community and regional hospital settings. There may be variability in the implementation of these recommendations across different healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This consensus bridges international evidence with local clinical realities, reflecting both the aspirations and limitations of mCRC care in Taiwan. By addressing systemic constraints imposed by NHI and advocating for biomarker-guided, rationally sequenced therapies, the recommendations provide a pragmatic roadmap for improving outcomes. Future policy adjustments, particularly in reimbursement for combination therapies and comprehensive genomic testing, will be essential to realize the full potential of precision oncology in Taiwan.
