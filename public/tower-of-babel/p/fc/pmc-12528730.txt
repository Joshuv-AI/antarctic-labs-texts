Celiac disease in children with focus on symptoms and quality of life
Authors: Gesala Perez-Junkera, Arrate Lasa, Lissette Delgado-Sanzonetti, Ainara Lekuona Serrano, Maialen Vázquez-Polo, Itziar Churruca, Virginia Navarro, Idoia Larretxi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The treatment for celiac disease (CD) consist of adhering to a gluten-free diet (GFD). Studies have reported that an appropriate and close nutritional assessment would be beneficial in improving symptoms and quality of life (QoL). This study investigated the effect of a nutritional intervention on the evolution of gastrointestinal (GI) and extraintestinal (EI) symptoms in children newly diagnosed with CD, and its association with QoL. 46 children who had recently been diagnosed with CD were followed up on three occasions during the year: at the time of diagnosis, and after 3 and 12 months (vt0, vt3 and vt12). Participants completed questionnaires on GI- and an EI-symptom, QoL and diet. Recovery was assessed based on clinical improvement and serological response. FODMAP consumption was analysed as a possible cause of symptoms. All patients received dietary advice and face-to-face follow-up. Although following a GFD is beneficial for symptom remission, recovery varies between patients. However, following a GFD is not sufficient to achieve a good QoL, with overall scores suggesting that participants have a poor QoL. Their parents/carers also have this perception. FODMAP consumption cannot be associated with a reduction in symptoms. Follow-up and nutritional education are important for improving GI and EI symptoms. However, more effort is needed to improve QoL in children with CD.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is a multisystemic autoimmune disorder triggered by the ingestion of gluten, a protein found in some cereals. It primarily affects the small intestine and occurs in genetically susceptible individuals1–3. Although the disease primarily affects the small intestine, people with CD can present gastrointestinal (GI) and/or extraintestinal (EI) symptoms or may be asymptomatic4–6. Moreover, clinical manifestations change markedly with age, with GI signs more common in children and other symptoms more common in adults. Actually, less than 25% of adults diagnosed have GI signs7. Very young children often present with symptoms such as diarrhoea or abdominal distension, whereas abdominal pain, vomiting and constipation are more common in older children8. EI manifestations include growth retardation, short stature, dental manifestations, low bone mineral density, anaemia, headaches, behavioural changes9,10 and mental disorders. Narrative reviews have suggested that CD may be associated with several psychiatric disorders, including anxiety, depression or fatigue, among others11–14. These mood disorders are common in CD, both before and after diagnosis, and are associated with an increased risk of developing psychosocial difficulties later in life15. One review concluded that there is sufficient evidence to support an association between CD and depression, and to a lesser extent, eating disorders16. Indeed, a research showed that people with CD are more likely to suffer from neuropsychiatric disorders than the general population12.

Lifelong adherence to a strict gluten-free diet (GFD) helps people with CD manage their symptoms and prevent of further complications17. This diet involves eliminating gluten from diet18–20 a storage protein found in certain common cereals (such as wheat, rye and barley) and their derivatives (such as triticale, malt, spelt and kamut)21. Although removing gluten improves the symptoms and signs of CD, some people report experiencing persistent symptoms despite following an apparently strict GFD22–24. In fact, up to 30% of patients continue to experience symptoms and/or have persistent bowel inflammation25. This has led to interest in investigating whether other dietary components may be responsible for the persistent clinical manifestations, and to what extent they may be so.

Among them, Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols (FODMAPs) have become a matter of concern in recent years. FODMAPs are poorly absorbed short-chain carbohydrates and polyols that, when introduced into the diet, can worsen bowel symptoms by increasing small bowel water volume, colonic gas production and bowel motility26. Dietary sources of FODMAPs are indigestible ‘oligosaccharides’, which include fructo-oligosaccharides (fructans), found in garlic, onions, wheat, barley and rye, and galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), found in pulses, nuts and soya products. “Lactose” (a disaccharide that is not absorbed unless undigested by lactase) is found in milk and dairy products; “fructose” (a monosaccharide), when in excess of glucose, is found in honey and certain fruits such as apples and pears; and “polyols” (or sugar alcohols), which include sorbitol and mannitol in stone fruits, mushrooms and artificial sweeteners27–29. These FODMAPs may be possible factors in the persistence of symptoms in people with chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBS)30 and irritable bowel syndrome30,31. Therefore, it has been suggested that eliminating foods containing these components from the diet may also be beneficial for people with CD who do not respond adequately to a GFD32–34 although there is some controversy surrounding this approach35 due to its restrictive profile36.

Whatever the cause of the symptoms, all strategies should be focused on improving the wellbeing of people with CD, as their treatment may be challenging, especially for young people37–39. Adherence to a strict GFD have a positive effect on quality of life (QoL) due to the reduction of symptoms38,40, but it can also have a negative effect due to social exclusion, a lack of information in restaurants, the high food prices, risk of gluten cross-contamination, and the fear of social exclusion41. Patients need to be vigilant about their diet, which makes the adherence to the GFD more difficult and stressful42,43. Long-term adherence to a GFD can lead to challenges in daily living, family, social and/or work life, resulting in a deterioration in the patient’s QoL44,45. In particular, people have reported that taking part in leisure activities, including eating out and spending time with friends, had a negative impact on their ability to maintain a GFD46–49 and could subsequently lead to social isolation, anxiety and, in general, to a reduced QoL50. Additionally, following restrictive diets in pediatric ages means involving the whole family in the process, with parents and/or carers playing a key role in influencing their children’s eating habits35. In this line, family members/carers have previously reported that the GFD negatively impacts their social activities, finances, and induces feelings of anxiety51,52.

Taking all this into consideration, it is clear that there is a clear need to investigate the potential contributor(s) to symptom severity and, consequently, QoL deterioration. The main objective of this study was to analyse the effect of a nutritional intervention and follow-up in the evolution of GI and EI symptoms during the first year on a GFD of children with CD, besides its association with their QoL. The secondary objective was to clarify whether the ingestion of FODMAPs could be associated with changes in these two parameters.

Material & methods

Recruitment

The recruitment process for children who had recently been diagnosed with CD was conducted between 2020 and 2023 in six clinics in the Basque Country (Hospital Universitario de Cruces, Donostia, Araba, Alto Deba, Mendaro, Zumarraga) and in the Celiac Association of Madrid. All participants (n = 46; 35 girls, 11 boys; aged between 3 and 14 years old; mean age 8.3 ± 3.3 years) were referred to clinics for confirmed CD diagnosis during this period (2020–2023). The diagnostic criteria were as follows: positive serology (anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA antibodies greater or equals to 10 times than the upper limit of normal values + positive anti-endomysial antibodies in a second sample) or anti-tTG IgA antibody levels < 10× the upper limit of normal plus histological confirmation of coeliac disease53.

The inclusion criteria were (i) being a pediatric patient who had volunteered to participate in the intervention study, (ii) having a recent diagnosis of CD, and (iii) being willing to follow a GFD. Exclusion criteria included (i) a history of chronic diseases or other GI diseases requiring a specific diet, and (ii) a lack of motivation to participate. Once the diagnosis of CD had been confirmed, the clinical staff provided the research staff with the relevant contact information. The research staff then contacted interested family members with information about the intervention study. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents and/or tutors of all participants after they had received the study information. Minor participants signed a consent form for participation. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Basque Country, code PI2020053.

Data collection

Following confirmation of a diagnosis of CD and consent to participate in the intervention, participants were consecutively recruited and followed for the first 12 months after confirmed diagnosis. All participants attended three medical/dietary visits: at the time of diagnosis (vt0), three months after a GFD (vt3) and 12 months after a GFD (vt12). Dietary, symptom-related, and QoL data were also collected at each visit and participants were asked to return these data within a few days after the visit.

Participants took part in an intervention consisting of a face-to-face follow-ups, which took place one month after each data collection,. These were referred to as IT1 (the first meeting one month after diagnosis), IT4 (the second meeting after the third month on a GFD) and IT13 (the third meeting after one year on a GFD). The intervention aimed to familiarise the participants and their families with the CD and the balanced and safe GFD. Two dietitians from the GLUTEN3S research team (UPV/EHU), specialised in the management of CD and GFD, conducted the intervention. To avoid interpersonal differences, common criteria for data analysis were defined in advance, and the dietitians collaborated throughout the intervention.

The first meeting (IT1) defined the basic concepts of CD, gluten and the GFD, as well as the guidelines for achieving a balanced diet. Furthermore, a didactic workshop on different flours and a comparison of organoleptic characteristics highlighted the differences between a gluten-free product and a gluten-containing product.

At IT4, the focus was on the safety of the GFD, with particular emphasis on cross-contamination, gluten-free labelling, current legislation and possible sources of gluten. Patients whose diet had been identified as ‘high risk’ for involuntary transgressions, they were informed and given the guidelines to minimise this risk.

At IT13, 12-month GFD data were presented to attendees. Between IT1 and IT4, gluten-free cooking workshops were organised for all participants and broadcasted remotely. These included preparing gluten-free recipes, a supermarket shopping session to understand food labels and identify gluten-free foods, and a cooking session itself with an emphasis on cross-contamination.

Analysis of the presence of GI and EI symptoms

To assess current GI symptoms, participants completed a self-administered Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) questionnaire22 which is a validated and widely used questionnaire in CD and other GI conditions54–60. It measures five sub-dimensions of GI symptoms: indigestion, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, reflux and constipation, consisting of 15 separate items. The scoring is based on a Likert scale from 1 to 7, with 1 being minimal GI symptoms and 7 being the most severe symptoms; however, we decided to use a range from 0 (no GI symptoms) to 6 (most severe GI symptoms). The intensity of GI symptoms was analysed. For this purpose, a minimum of 0 (presence of any symptoms) and a maximum of 90 (presence of all symptoms to a severe degree) were set. Similarly, the total number of symptoms was also quantified and all responses between 0 and 6 were included. All responses were divided into three groups: Group 1: 0 responses marked (i.e. no symptom presence); Group 2: 1–5 responses marked out of 15; and Group 3: 6 or more symptoms marked out of 15.

For EI symptoms, an adapted version of the non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) questionnaire from a prospective survey promoted by the Italian Association for CD and Celiac Foundation was used61. It includes 12 EI symptoms (asthma, rhinitis, joint/muscle pain, dermatitis, oral thrush, baldness, anaemia, weight loss, foggy mind, depression, anxiety, headache, tiredness, lack of wellbeing) and, the question “Do you suffer from any other symptoms not mentioned in this questionnaire?”, which allowed participants to report any other symptoms they were experiencing that were not mentioned in the questionnaire. Positive results were quantified.

Adherence

Adherence to the GFD was measured by both anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody values (considering positive serology when it was > 10 times the upper limit of normal values and positive anti-endomysial antibodies in a second sample)62 and a four-question questionnaire, which was used to measure adherence to the GFD. This was adapted from a questionnaire by Morinsky and Green63.

Analysis of quality of life

To assess the QoL of children with CD, the CD-specific questionnaire (CD-DUX) was used64. This instrument is based on 12 items divided into three subscales. “Having CD” subscale (3 items), provides information on how the child feels when offered food containing gluten or when thinking about food containing gluten. “Communication” subscale (3 items), provides information on how the child feels when talking to others about CD or when explaining what the disease is; and, “Diet” subscale (6 items), provides information on how the child feels about having to follow a strict diet for life or not being able to eat things that other people eat. The responses obtained were scored using the Likert scale, which assigns a score from 1 to 5 to each response. This scale is accompanied by facial expressions in pictures, which helps children to respond easily. A version of the CD-DUX has been developed for children’s parents/carers and was also used to determine their perceptions of children’s feelings about CD. Taking into account that higher scores correspond to poorer QoL, meaning 12 point very good and 75 very poor, the total and subscale scores were converted to a scale of 1 to 100, with 1 being very good and 100 being very bad. Using a 5-point Likert scale, a score of 1–20 is considered very good, 21–40 is good, 41–60 is neutral, 61–80 is poor, and 81 to 100 is very poor.

Determination of FODMAP daily intake

Participants completed 3-day 24-hour food recalls at each visit. Consumption of FODMAPs content was calculated using the Gluten3SDiet software -a GFD assessment software containing more than 700 gluten-free rendered foods and their composition-65. Daily intakes of total fructan, GOS (stachyose and raffinose), polyols (sorbitol and mannitol), fructose excess (calculated by subtracting glucose content from fructose content) and lactose were quantified. With the exception of fructans, FODMAP concentrations (g per 100 g of food) of common foods were obtained from the open-access food composition database Food Standards Australia New Zealand66. As this Australian database does not provide information on total fructan (only inulin), research articles were used as a reference for the total fructan content of vegetables and fruits67 and legumes68. In the case of cereal-based foods, both gluten-containing and gluten-free, fructan content data were obtained from our own determinations using a previously described methodology69. To gain further insight, Varney et al.70 defined a diet as high FODMAP if more than 0.5 g of total FODMAP (excluding lactose) was consumed in a meal, and this cut-off was used to classify participants’ diets. According to the researcher27, ≤ 0.5 g FODMAP (excluding lactose) is well tolerated in people with HNS and gastrointestinal symptoms. 24-hour questionnaires were used for 3 days and each day was reported as high or low FODMAP.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of results were performed by using the IBM SPSS statistical program, version 27 (IBM Inc., USA). Normality in the distribution was assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and homogeneity by Levene’s test. Statistical analyses were performed to calculate differences between measurements using Wilcoxon test in case of not normal distribution, and Student-t test in case of normal distribution (vt0 vs. vt3, vt0 vs. vt12 and vt3 vs. vt12). P values < 0.05 were accepted as statistically significant.

The number of EI symptoms among participants decreased after 3 and 12 months on a GFD from 3.5 ± 3 at vt0 to 2 ± 2 at vt3, and finally, to 1.5 ± 1.5 at vt12 (mean ± SD), the decrease being significant at each time (0,002, < 0,001 and < 0,05, respectively). Adherence

According to the adherence to the GFD, the percentage of participants with positive serology decreased during the intervention (70% at vt0, 7% at vt3 and 0% at vt12). Statistical differences were observed from Vt0 to Vt3 and from vt0 to vt12 (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). In relation to the adherence questionnaire all participants showed full compliance with the GFD during the intervention (data not shown).

FODMAP consumption

Total FODMAP consumption among participants increased throughout the intervention year, being significant from vt0 to vt12 and from vt3 to vt12 (Table 5). According to each FODMAP, significant increases were observed from vt3 to vt12 for excess of fructose sorbitol and lactose. For the latter, the increase was also significant from vt0 to vt12.

Discussion

The treatment of CD consist of following a GFD71 which aims to achieve partial or complete remission of symptoms and mucosal healing72–74. However, the time required to achieve symptom relief and remission varies between individuals75,76 and a small proportion of people continues suffering from symptoms despite a GFD76,77. The current intervention was effective in achieving significant improvement in GI and EI symptoms within the first three months, with an even greater improvement observed after one year. This is likely due to the initial motivation to start the intervention, the knowledge gained during the interventions/sessions and the subsequent high level of dietary adherence. In fact, the literature suggests that the higher the level of adherence, the more effective the GFD is in relieving symptoms78. In the current study, however, although a general improvement was observed, some participants still reported manifestations. This may be because symptom remission periods vary from person to person75,76. In our previous study, improvements in children’s GI symptoms were observed within the first 3 months, but not after 12 months, suggesting a relaxation of participants54. In another randomised controlled trial, adults who received group-based education had a significantly lower total GI scores than the control group 3 months after the intervention79.

Regarding the EI scores, those related to the psychological domain were almost eliminated after the intervention. This may be because receiving a diagnosis can reduce the stress and anxiety associated with uncertainty80,81. Moreover, the continuous follow-up and the information obtained during the whole intervention could help participants feel more relaxed about the disease and GFD. In the literature, while Smith et al. provided evidence to suggest that CD may indeed be the cause of these behavioural problems82 other authors have suggested that depressive symptoms are more likely to be related to the characteristics of CD than to a consequence of being affected by a chronic disease state83. In the present study, tiredness was reported by almost 40% of participants, which is consistent with the finding of Cook et al., who identified tiredness as the most prevalent symptom. They observed that 46% of participants experienced tiredness, with 50% of those reporting improvement following the introduction of a GFD84. Furthermore, other publications recommended long-term, periodic monitoring to improve symptom management85. These data therefore suggest that a continuous follow-up intervention is necessary to ensure total adherence to the diet and to maintain the beneficial results in symptom remission, not only in the short term, but also in the long term86. By vt12, patients were adhering well to the GFD, as evidenced by clinical improvement and serological response.

Although EI symptoms in the psychological domain had almost disappeared by vt12, this improvement was insufficient to observe an improvement in overall in QoL. The results showed that CD had a significant impact on this is parameter, especially as participants reported that they still did not feel fully integrated in the social media, similar to their parents´/carers´ perception. These findings are consistent with other studies demonstrating low public awareness and sensitivity to CD87. Similar findings were reported by Jericho et al., who found a moderate association between adolescents’ feelings of CD comparing to their parents´/caregivers ´ perception at a particular time88. This may be because parents/carers discern increased stress and concern about their child’s wellbeing and future implications. In the present study, Diet and Having CD were the main contributors to poorer QoL. In this context, it is important to acknowledge the challenges that children with CD have to face when dining out. They must be vigilant about avoiding gluten, which can be challenging given the lack of clear gluten-free options on menus and the limited selection of gluten-free alternatives in restaurants. Sharing food can also be complicated, as staff may be uninformed or dismissive about gluten-free diets12,42,45,89,90. It has therefore been suggested that progress must be made in nutritional education, not only for patients, but also for society, in order to improve the understanding and management of the disease as a whole and thus, to promote social inclusion45,91.

Other causes of symptoms, and, consequently, QoL, are being investigated among people with CD. Removing FODMAPs from the diet may be beneficial in several conditions, including IBS92,93. Similar to the data obtained in our previous study54 the current one did not show any changes in FODMAP intake during the first year after diagnosis in children with CD. Therefore, the observed reduction in symptoms cannot be related to a reduction in FODMAPs, nor to their consumption having a positive or negative effect on the QoL of the participants in the present study. However, data obtained by other researchers may differ partially from those in the present study. A review concluded that a short period of a gluten-free, low-FODMAP diet may be beneficial for people with CD who persist with IBS-like symptoms despite strict adherence to a GFD33. Another study conducted in children and adolescents with CD suggested that FODMAP intake may positively affect diet and life quality, but had no effect on GI symptoms94. Note that there is a lack of clinical trials aimed to investigate the efficacy of a low-FODMAP diet in children and adolescents35 and, as far as we know, this is the first study performed in newly diagnosed children with CD where FODMAP consumption has been followed during a year. More research aimed to analyse the role of FODMAPs in children with CD could serve to corroborate our findings.

Overall, it is clear that proper nutritional assessment, including nutrition education and continuous monitoring of the symptoms and QoL of the person with CD, carried out by experts in the field, is crucial to achieve positive results. This is more effective if it starts from diagnosis, at an early age, and continues throughout the person´s life. Nevertheless, there is a clear need to increase the knowledge and involvement of the wider community about CD and GFD in order to promote the inclusion and improve the QoL of people with CD.

This study has some limitations. It relied on self-reported questionnaires for GI and EI symptoms, QoL, and dietary intake. Self-reported data may be subject to recall bias and inaccuracy, affecting the reliability of the results, and although these tools have been validated, they may not capture all aspects of the participants’ experience and may have limitations in their sensitivity or specificity. Participation was voluntary, so the effectiveness of the dietary interventions and education sessions may have varied depending on the motivation of participants and families. There is also a lack of a control group of children with CD who did not receive any intervention. Having a control group would have been useful for observing the effectiveness of the intervention. However, comparing data from vt12 (after intervention) with data from vt0 (at diagnosis) can also demonstrate the effectiveness of the intervention.

Conclusion

Continuous follow-up involving nutritional education and monitoring is important for improving GI and EI symptoms in children with CD. However, more efforts are needed to improve their QoL. Implementing social strategies could increase awareness of CD and GFD in the general society, and thus improve the social inclusion of people with CD.
