The association between modified cardiometabolic index and cardiovascular disease risk in adults with early cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Hongcai Wang, Kaixuan Li, Chang Sheng, Xin Ye, Guoqiang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome highlights the interconnections between cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and type 2 diabetes (DM). The modified Cardiometabolic Index (MCMI), which integrates measures of insulin resistance, abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, and blood glucose, may better reflect metabolic and cardiovascular risk than the original index. However, its association with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease risk in individuals with early CKM syndrome (stages 0–3) remains unclear.MethodsThis longitudinal study utilized data from 6,953 participants in stages 0–3 of CKM syndrome from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The MCMI was calculated at baseline. Participants were followed for a median of 7.8 years to track the incidence of cardiovascular disease, heart disease, and stroke. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to analyze the association between MCMI levels and the risk of these outcomes, adjusting for various sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors.ResultsDuring follow-up, 1,534 participants developed CVD. Higher MCMI levels were significantly associated with increased risks of overall CVD and stroke. In fully adjusted models, each unit increase in MCMI was associated with a 21% and 47% higher risk of CVD and stroke, respectively. Participants in the highest MCMI quartile had a 55% higher risk of CVD and a 2.55-fold higher risk of stroke compared to the lowest quartile. The association with heart disease was weaker and became non-significant after full adjustment. Dose-response analyses revealed nonlinear relationships for CVD and stroke. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionMCMI is independently associated with an increased risk of overall CVD and stroke in individuals with early-stage CKM syndrome; its association with heart disease is weak, with statistical significance only observed in the highest MCMI quartile and partially attenuated by adjustment for traditional metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors. MCMI serves as a practical indicator for cardiovascular risk stratification, offering a valuable tool for early prevention and management of CVD and stroke in the preclinical phases of CKM syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05714-y.

Introduction

Abundant evidence indicates that type 2 diabetes (DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are three major health threats, these conditions are characterized by high incidence and mortality rates. Over the past two decades, the disease burden of metabolic disorders has surged by 49.4%. Metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance and inflammation, have emerged as prevalent risk factors for both CKD and CVD. Moreover, the comorbidity of CVD and CKD is on the rise, spurring the academic community to delve into their underlying associations [1–3]. Therefore, the American Heart Association (AHA) recently introduced the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) as a mixed disease, emphasizing the close association and interaction between these three health domains (CVD, CKD, and type 2 DM) [4].

First put forward a decade ago, the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) is used to identify diabetes mellitus, integrating parameters associated with abdominal obesity and dyslipidemia [5]. CMI is identified as an independent risk factor for the development of CVD in patients with CKM (HR: 1.07, p = 0.006) [6]. (MCMI) integrates indicators such as insulin resistance, abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, and blood glucose levels. Compared with the CMI, it incorporates fasting blood glucose parameters with natural logarithm transformation, enabling a more accurate reflection of the risks of metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other conditions [7]. However, there remains a research gap regarding the association between MCMI and the incidence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases in the context of CKM.

Notably, within the CKM staging framework, the AHA underscores the critical importance of screening individuals during the preclinical phase. The AHA further emphasizes that research endeavors targeting CKM populations at stages 1 to 3 should prioritize the prevention of CVD events [8]. To investigate MCMI and the risk of cardiovascular diseases in the CKM 0–3 population, this study explored the association between MCMI and the incidence risk of CVD, heart disease, and stroke in 6,953 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, using longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).

Materials and methods

Study population and study design

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The CHARLS employed multi-stage probability sampling to conduct face-to-face surveys among 17,708 individuals aged 45 and above across 150 counties in 28 Chinese provinces. This sample is nationally representative of the middle-aged and elderly population in China, demonstrating comprehensive representativeness across various demographic variables, including residential location, gender, and socioeconomic status. Aligned with over 25 health and retirement surveys worldwide, the CHARLS survey instrument enables the international comparison of its findings with those from other studies. Data from CHARLS is accessible at https://charls.pku.edu.cn. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011 (Wave 1), followed by four additional waves in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4) and 2020 (Wave 5) respectively [9]. The research complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It has been approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB000010052-11015). Written informed consent was properly acquired from every participant. All the research staff underwent systematic professional training and carried out face-to-face interviews with the use of standardized questionnaires [9].

In this study, the sample comprised participants from Wave 1, and Fig. 1 presents a flowchart illustrating the sample selection process. Exclusion criteria: (1) Participants with missing data on MCMI in Wave 1; (2) Participants with missing data for identification of CKM syndrome 0–3 at Wave 1; (3) Participants with stroke or heart disease in Wave 1; (4) Participants lost to follow-up.

Covariates

The research gathered the following information as covariates. The covariates included the following: Demographics: Age, sex, education level (illiterate, Primary school, Middle school, High school and above), marital status (currently married, other). Health behaviors: self-reported statuses regarding smoking and drinking (yes or no), BMI. Comorbidities and treatments: Systolic and diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and use of antidiabetic, antihypertensive or lipid-lowering medications. Laboratory indicators: Fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and uric acid (UA).

Participants were regarded as having hypertension if they stated a history of hypertension, or their baseline systolic blood pressure (SBP) was ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes was defined as a reported history of diabetes, or a baseline HbA1c level of ≥ 6.5%. Hyperlipidemia was categorized as a reported history of hyperlipidemia, or TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL. To evaluate renal function, eGFR was calculated based on the CKD-EPI Eq. [10].

Definition of CKM syndrome stage 0 to 3

The stages of CKM Syndrome from 0 to 3 are categorized in accordance with the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement on CKM Syndrome (Table S1) [4]. The stages are delineated as follows: Stage 0 involves the absence of CKM syndrome risk factors; Stage 1 is characterized by excess or dysfunctional adiposity; Stage 2 includes metabolic risk factors or CKD; and Stage 3 encompasses subclinical cardiovascular disease.

Definition of CVD

CVD events were determined based on self-reported physician diagnoses, covering heart diseases—such as myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, and heart failure—as well as stroke [11]. During follow-up, incident CVD cases were identified using the following questions: “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other heart problems?” and “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a stroke?” Participants were classified as having developed CVD if they reported a new physician-diagnosed event during follow-up. The date of diagnosis was recorded as the year of the interview at which the disease was first reported.

Missing data handling

In this study, several variables contained missing data, including smoking status (n = 1, 0.01%), drinking status (n = 5, 0.07%), systolic blood pressure (n = 23, 0.33%), diastolic blood pressure (n = 22, 0.31%), LDL-C (n = 10, 0.14%), BUN (n = 8, 0.12%), dyslipidemia medication (n = 94, 1.35%), hypertension medication (n = 9, 0.13%), and diabetes medication (n = 9, 0.13%) (Table S2). To mitigate the potential impact of missing data on study outcomes, variables with a missing rate of ≥ 20% were excluded from the analysis, while those with a missing rate of < 20% were subjected to multiple imputation. Specifically, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed using the mice package in R. Identifier variables (ID), CKM stage, the primary exposure variable MCMI, and the outcome variables (heart disease, stroke, and CVD) were excluded from the imputation process. All remaining covariates, including demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors, were imputed. Imputation models were specified according to variable type: logistic regression (logreg) for binary variables, polytomous regression (polyreg) for ordinal categorical variables with more than two levels, and random forest (rf) for continuous variables. Five imputed datasets (m = 5) were generated to balance computational efficiency and estimation precision, and the number of iterations was set to 50 (maxit = 50) to ensure convergence of the Markov chains.

To evaluate the potential influence of imputation on the study findings, the distributions of variables before and after imputation were compared. The results indicated that the imputation procedure did not materially affect the overall data structure or integrity (Table S3).

Statistical methods

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups for continuous variables were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages, with statistical evaluation performed via the chi-square test. To evaluate the associations of MCMI with the risks of CVD, heart disease, and stroke, participants were first divided into quartile groups (Q1-Q4) based on MCMI values. we used Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to estimate the cumulative event probability of CVD, heart disease, and stroke across different MCMI groups. Cumulative risk curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the Log-rank test was applied to assess group differences. Then, Cox proportional hazards regression models were then used to independently analyze the associations between MCMI and each of these outcomes. Covariates were selected based on prior studies and clinical expertise [4, 12, 13]. Prior to analysis, the proportional hazards assumption was visually checked by assessing Schoenfeld residuals (Table S4). We excluded the MCMI-related indices and their individual components. Multicollinearity testing showed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all covariates was below 5 (Table S5). Given that TCL and LDL-C exhibited relatively high variance inflation factors (VIF = 3.9 and 3.1, respectively), only TC was retained in the fully adjusted model, while LDL-C was excluded to avoid redundant lipid metabolism covariates. This further reduced model complexity while controlling for key lipid-related confounding factors. The risk of overfitting was rigorously assessed to ensure the reliability of the model estimates. The study included 6,953 participants with 1,534 incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) events, which far exceeds the widely accepted rule of thumb for Cox regression (≥ 10 outcome events per covariate). This sample size provided sufficient statistical power for estimating covariate effects and effectively alleviated concerns regarding overfitting.

In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analysis was employed to explore the potential linear relationship between MCMI and the incidence of CVD, heart disease, and stroke in participants with CKM syndrome. When non-linearity was detected, we further conducted a threshold effect analysis using a segmented Cox regression model. The optimal cut-off point was identified via a recursive algorithm, and a likelihood ratio test was subsequently performed to compare the linear model with the segmented model, enabling separate estimation of hazard ratios below and above the identified threshold. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, gender, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses were also performed to verify the robustness of the results. Statistical analyses were carried out using R 4.4.2. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants with different MCMI group in the longitudinal study

Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of participants stratified by MCMI quartiles. A total of 6,953 participants were included in the analysis. The mean age was 58.12 ± 9.52 years, and 46.4% of the participants were male. Using the lowest MCMI quartile (Q1) as the reference, participants in the higher quartiles had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Moreover, they had higher values of body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, and uric acid. However, their levels of HDL-c and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were lower. Additionally, the distribution of CKM stages showed a progressive increase in severity as the MCMI quartiles increased.

The association between MCMI and the incidence of CVD in a population with CKM syndrome stages 0–3

During a median follow-up of 7.8 years, 1534 participants developed incident CVD, comprising 1150 heart disease cases and 551 stroke events. The overall CVD incidence rate reached 22.1%. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated progressively increasing cumulative event probability curves across MCMI quartiles for all cardiovascular outcomes. These graded increases from Q1 to Q4 groups proved statistically significant (Fig. 2; log-rank test, P < 0.001).

The Kaplan–Meier curves analysis depict the cumulative event probability of heart disease, stroke and CVD across the MCMI quartiles. a heart disease; (b) stroke; (c) CVD

In multivariate Cox proportional hazards models, MCMI displayed significant associations with cardiovascular risk (Table 2). When treated as a continuous variable, each unit increase in MCMI corresponded to elevated CVD risk across all adjustment levels: Model 1 (HR 1.26, 95% CI: 1.19–1.32), Model 2 (HR 1.24, 95% CI: 1.17–1.30), and Model 3 (HR 1.21, 95% CI: 1.10–1.32). Similarly, stroke risk increased consistently with higher MCMI values, showing hazard ratios of 1.36 (95% CI: 1.26–1.46) in Model 1, 1.38 (95% CI: 1.29–1.49) in Model 2, and 1.47 (95% CI: 1.26–1.71) in Model 3. For heart disease, however, the association weakened after full adjustment, with Model 3 yielding a non-significant hazard ratio of 1.10 (95% CI: 0.99–1.22, p = 0.073).

Quartile-based analysis revealed a clear dose-response pattern. Participants in the highest MCMI quartile (Q4) experienced substantially increased CVD risk compared to the reference Q1 group, with hazard ratios of 1.96 (95% CI: 1.69–2.27) in Model 1, 1.90 (95% CI: 1.64–2.21) in Model 2, and 1.55 (95% CI: 1.31–1.83) in Model 3. Stroke risk exhibited even more pronounced increases, with Q4 participants showing 3.04-fold (95% CI: 2.31-4.00), 3.45-fold (95% CI: 2.61–4.56), and 2.55-fold (95% CI: 1.88–3.45) higher risks across the three models, respectively.

Heart disease associations followed a different pattern. While significant risk elevations appeared in Models 1 and 2, only the Q4 group maintained statistical significance in the fully adjusted Model 3 (HR 1.29, 95% CI: 1.07–1.56). The intermediate quartiles Q2 and Q3 showed non-significant trends toward increased risk.

These findings collectively indicate that elevated MCMI levels consistently predict higher CVD and stroke incidence, maintaining significance after comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, clinical, and biochemical factors. The association with heart disease, though present in the highest risk group, demonstrates more modest effects that largely attenuate after full covariate adjustment. For CVD incidence, a nonlinear association was observed with MCMI levels (P-nonlinear = 0.013). The overall relationship was statistically significant (P-overall < 0.001), with the hazard ratio curve showing a steeper increase at lower MCMI values that gradually plateaued at higher levels. Similarly, stroke risk exhibited a significant nonlinear relationship with MCMI (P-nonlinear = 0.022). The overall association was highly significant (P-overall < 0.001), displaying a J-shaped curve where risk accelerated more rapidly beyond certain MCMI thresholds. This suggests that stroke risk remains relatively stable at lower MCMI ranges but increases markedly once specific levels are exceeded.

Given the nonlinear relationship between MCMI and the risks of CVD and stroke, we further performed threshold effect analyses using two-piecewise Cox regression models. For CVD, an inflection point was identified at MCMI = 2.00. Below this threshold, MCMI was inversely associated with CVD risk (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.98, P = 0.038), whereas above the threshold, each 1-unit increment in MCMI was associated with a significantly higher risk of CVD (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.17–1.42, P < 0.001). The log-likelihood ratio test indicated that the two-piecewise model fitted significantly better than the linear model (P = 0.003). For stroke, the inflection point for MCMI was estimated at 3.45. Above this threshold, no significant association was observed (HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.67–1.36, P = 0.8). Below the threshold, however, a higher MCMI was significantly associated with an increased risk of stroke (HR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.44–2.17, P < 0.001). The log-likelihood ratio test also supported the presence of a threshold effect (P = 0.005) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

To enhance the reliability of our study results, we conducted three robust sensitivity analyses. First, we re-analyzed the dataset after excluding all missing values, and the results were consistent with the main findings (Table S6). Second, to rigorously assess the robustness of our results to potential confounding from medication use, we systematically excluded participants taking anti-diabetic drugs, anti-dyslipidemic drugs, and anti-hypertensive drugs before conducting further analyses; no substantial changes in the results were observed (Table S7–S9). Third, recognizing that participants in stage 0 may have few metabolic or cardiovascular risk factors or that these factors may not reach clinically significant thresholds, we performed an additional analysis excluding these participants, which also supported the main results (Table S10).

Discussion

In this study, based on Wave 1 data from the CHARLS cohort, we explored the association between MCMI and the incidence risks of CVD, heart disease, and stroke in the CKM stage 0–3 population through longitudinal analysis, and verified the value of MCMI as a risk assessment indicator. This provides a new basis for CVD risk stratification in the CKM stage 0–3 population, facilitates preclinical cardiovascular risk management, and responds to the needs of chronic disease prevention and control. Numerous studies have demonstrated the association between CMI and CVD, heart disease, and stroke. Patients with hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), elevated CMI is significantly associated with an increased risk of CVD and its subtypes (coronary heart disease and stroke) [14]. Notably, as a composite indicator of abdominal obesity and dyslipidemia, CMI may indirectly increase the risk of heart disease through metabolic disorders (such as insulin resistance and inflammation), and it is closely associated with MCMI [15]. Insulin resistance is the core driver of CKM syndrome, permeating the entire course of metabolic abnormalities, renal injury, and cardiovascular events. Insulin resistance interacts with obesity and DM, triggering chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which in turn lead to vascular endothelial dysfunction and organ damage [16]. Insulin resistance directly impairs glomerular function, accelerates the decline of renal function, and is closely associated with the progression of chronic kidney disease [17]. Animal models have demonstrated that insulin resistance induced by a high-fat diet can disrupt renal mitochondrial function and exacerbate kidney injury [18]. Meanwhile, insulin resistance can also increase the risk of cardiovascular events (such as stroke) by promoting atherosclerosis, dyslipidemia, and hypertension [19]. Lipid dysregulation and chronic low-grade inflammation are two major and interrelated processes that impair insulin action across multiple tissues [20]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that the prevalence of insulin resistance increases progressively with elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL-C levels [21]. Intervention trials have also shown that improving lipid profiles—such as lowering triglycerides and the total cholesterol/HDL-C ratio—can significantly reduce the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) [22]. Trials have shown that reductions in fasting glucose are often accompanied by significant decreases in insulin levels and HOMA-IR [22]. As a representative indicator of abdominal fat accumulation, waist circumference is closely associated with insulin resistance. Studies have shown that each 1-standard-deviation increase in waist circumference (15 cm) is associated with significant elevations in inflammatory markers, including a 22% increase in TNF‑α, a 35% increase in IL‑6, and a 46% increase in hsCRP. These inflammatory factors are known to exacerbate insulin resistance [23].

The results indicated the weaker association between MCMI and heart disease compared to stroke. MCMI is constructed from metabolic and visceral obesity indicators (waist circumference/height, TG, glucose, HDL‑C), which are strongly linked to small vessel disease, endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and prothrombotic state—all dominant mechanisms in stroke [24, 25]. Stroke is more strongly driven by diffuse metabolic dysfunction that MCMI precisely captures [26, 27]. Heart disease involves additional factors independent of metabolism (e.g., coronary anatomy, hemodynamic stress, aging, non‑metabolic inflammation), which weaken the independent association after full adjustment [28–30]. The attenuation in heart disease also reflects biological overlap between MCMI and covariates adjusted in Model 3 (e.g., BMI, lipids, glucose), leading to over-adjustment rather than a lack of clinical value.

The AHA defines CKM syndrome as a systemic vicious cycle formed by the pathological and physiological interactions of metabolic risk factors, CKD, and CVD, ultimately leading to multi organ damage and adverse outcomes, therefore, multiple factors are inevitably involved [31, 32]. AHA divides CKM syndrome into stages 0–4, reflecting the continuous process of disease from the accumulation of risk factors to the end organ damage. In the early stage (stage 0–2), metabolic risk factors (such as obesity, pre diabetes) are the main ones, and vascular endothelial damage is initiated through inflammation and oxidative stress. In the late stage (stage 3–4), subclinical or clinical cardiovascular/renal events (such as left ventricular hypertrophy, proteinuria) occur, and multi system interaction accelerates disease deterioration. This study aims to investigate MCMI as a risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in the CKM population. It provides a practical risk assessment tool for clinical practice, enabling earlier identification of potentially high-risk individuals and preventing delayed interventions.

We found that the significant inflection point for total CVD was observed at an MCMI of 2.00. Below this value, MCMI was inversely associated with CVD risk (HR = 0.68, P = 0.038), whereas each unit increase in MCMI beyond 2.00 conferred a 29% higher CVD risk (HR = 1.29, P < 0.001). This transition likely marks the shift from compensated metabolic dysregulation to a state of cumulative endothelial injury driven by insulin resistance and chronic inflammation. For stroke, the inflection point was notably higher at 3.45. A strong positive association with stroke risk was evident only below this threshold (HR = 1.77, P < 0.001), with no significant correlation observed at extreme MCMI values (P = 0.80). This plateau effect suggests that once severe metabolic dysfunction is established, stroke risk is dominated by advanced vasculopathy rather than incremental metabolic changes. The superior fit of the two-piecewise model (log-likelihood ratio P < 0.005) confirms that MCMI’s predictive power is context-dependent. These findings establish MCMI as a practical, independent tool for defining actionable risk thresholds, enabling stage-specific interventions to mitigate cardiovascular events in early CKM syndrome.

In our study, with the increase in MCMI quartiles (Q1 to Q4), the proportion of females, BMI, and the prevalence of chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes) increased significantly, while HDL-c and eGFR decreased significantly. Literature indicates that metabolic abnormalities (such as hyperglycemia and obesity-related mixed types) can significantly increase the 11.1-year CVD risk [33]. This is consistent with the trend of increasing BMI and diabetes prevalence across quartiles, suggesting that metabolic disorders accelerate atherosclerosis through pathways like insulin resistance and chronic inflammation [13]. The elevation of BMI across quartiles may reflect visceral fat accumulation, which secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), further exacerbating metabolic disorders and vascular damage [34]. Similarly, these inflammatory factors can also promote insulin resistance [23]. Low HDL-c, an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis, impairs anti-inflammatory and reverse cholesterol transport functions, accelerating the formation of vascular plaques [35]. Decreased eGFR indicates impaired renal function, which is closely associated with CKM syndrome. Diminished renal function can exacerbate hypertension, electrolyte disturbances, and accumulation of uremic toxins, further damaging blood vessels and increasing CVD risk [36]. The increase in the proportion of females may be associated with decreased estrogen levels after menopause, as estrogen deficiency can exacerbate metabolic abnormalities (such as increased visceral fat and reduced insulin sensitivity) [37]. In summary, this indicates a potential association between the aforementioned trend from Q1 to Q4 and the exacerbation of vascular damage and increased CVD risk due to metabolic abnormalities.

It is worth noting that the weakened MCMI-heart disease association in the fully adjusted Model 3 (HR = 1.10, p = 0.073) is a key methodological and biological finding, stemming from the profound shared biological basis between MCMI and the covariates adjusted in Model 3 rather than a lack of predictive value of MCMI. MCMI is a composite index derived from core metabolic indicators (waist circumference, TG, FBG, HDL-C), and its biological construction overlaps with Model 3 covariates (BMI, total cholesterol, CRP, etc.)—the physiological states MCMI aims to summarize. Full adjustment thus peels off the metabolic risk signals encapsulated by MCMI, leading to HR attenuation and lost statistical significance; this reverse validates MCMI’s construct validity (its predictive power stems from integrating obesity, dyslipidemia, inflammation, etc.). Stepwise model analysis reveals MCMI’s core clinical value: Model 2 (sociodemographic/lifestyle-adjusted, HR = 1.17, p < 0.001) confirms MCMI is a powerful independent predictor of heart disease via its aggregated pure metabolic risk information, validating the utility of this composite index. Notably, the highest MCMI quartile still showed borderline significant heart disease risk in Model 3 (HR = 1.29, p = 0.008), implying MCMI captures unique metabolic dysregulation patterns/synergistic effects not fully reflected by individual traditional indicators.

In this study, elevated MCMI was accompanied by increases in SBP and DBP as well as a decrease in HDL-c. Previous studies have also indicated that metabolic abnormalities accelerate arteriosclerosis, which in turn increases CVD risk [38–40]. This indirectly supports the notion that MCMI influences disease outcomes by reflecting the state of metabolic disorders.For example, ePWV quantifies arterial stiffness and incorporates risk factors like age and blood pressure, emerging as a multidimensional indicator for assessing CVD risk. An increase in ePWV not only directly reflects the decline of vascular function but also systematically heightens the risk of CVD occurrence and mortality by promoting atherosclerosis, organ ischemia, and interacting with traditional risk factors [41, 42].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, CVD events were adjudicated based on self-reported physician diagnoses without direct verification via medical records or objective clinical examinations (e.g., electrocardiogram, brain computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging), which may introduce non-differential misclassification bias. Underreporting of mild/subclinical CVD events or rare overreporting could occur due to participant recall bias or lack of disease awareness; however, such misclassification is unlikely to be associated with MCMI levels and typically attenuates the observed exposure-outcome association toward the null hypothesis. The robust, statistically significant association between MCMI and CVD/stroke risk observed in our study therefore suggests the true association may be stronger, and this bias is unlikely to invalidate our core findings. Additionally, stroke—a severe acute CVD subtype with high recall accuracy—showed an even stronger association with MCMI, further supporting the reliability of our results despite self-reporting limitations. Second, the ascertainment of incident endpoints during follow-up was based on five waves of data collection (Wave 1–5). This design did not allow for the estimation of precise time-to-event metrics on an annual or monthly scale. Furthermore, the unequal intervals between survey waves may have introduced potential bias in the determination of exact onset times for cardiovascular events.In addition, competing risks (e.g., non-cardiovascular death) were not explicitly accounted for in the Cox regression models. Although non-cardiovascular death may be a relevant competing event in this elderly study population, information on cause of death in the CHARLS database was only available in the 2013 exit interview and not consistently ascertained across all follow-up waves, which prevented reliable implementation of competing risk analysis (e.g., Fine–Gray model). The potential impact of unaddressed competing risks cannot be entirely ruled out.

This prospective cohort study confirms that MCMI is an independent predictor of increased overall CVD and stroke risk in adults with early CKM syndrome (stages 0–3). For heart disease, the association with MCMI is weak: continuous MCMI shows no statistically significant association with heart disease risk after full adjustment for traditional metabolic, inflammatory, and cardiovascular risk factors, and statistical significance is only observed in the highest MCMI quartile (Q4 vs. Q1). This weakened heart disease association is partially attributable to biological overlap and partial over-adjustment between MCMI and traditional risk factors in the fully adjusted model. MCMI serves as a practical and sensitive indicator for CVD and stroke risk stratification in the early CKM syndrome population, and it can guide targeted early prevention and intervention strategies for individuals with elevated MCMI—especially those in the highest quartile. For heart disease risk assessment, MCMI should be used in combination with traditional cardiovascular risk factors to improve stratification accuracy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, MCMI is robustly and independently associated with the increased risks of overall CVD and stroke in participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Its association with heart disease is weak: the association for continuous MCMI is not statistically significant after full adjustmet for traditional risk factors, with statistical significance only retained in the highest MCMI quartile (Q4), and this association is partially attenuated by adjustment for metabolic and inflammatory covariates that share biological overlap with MCMI. MCMI can serve as a practical indicator for cardiovascular risk stratification—especially for CVD and stroke—in this population, providing a new basis for early prevention and management of cardiovascular events in the preclinical stage of CKM syndrome.
