Introduction

Schwannomas also called neurilemmoma are encapsulated nerve sheath tumors that correspond to a proliferation of Schwann cells derived from the neural crest. They are preferentially localized on the head, neck, and flexor surfaces of the extremities [1]. Retroperitoneal schwannoma is extremely rare. It represents only 4 % of all retroperitoneal tumors and 3 % of all schwannomas [2]. It is most often asymptomatic.

This case report presents a 36-year-old female with a long-standing, asymptomatic retroperitoneal mass, eventually diagnosed as Retroperitoneal schwannoma following comprehensive imaging and histopathological analysis. It is written in accordance with SCARE guidelines [3].

Case presentation

A 36-year-old female presented with a long-standing lump in the right lower abdomen, first noticed approximately 10 years ago. The onset of the lump was insidious, with a gradual increase in size from an initial 2 × 2 cm to its current size of approximately 7 × 7 cm. The lump was mobile and non-tender, and the patient did not experience any associated symptoms such as fever, changes in bowel or bladder habits, weight loss, or loss of appetite. Her past medical history was unremarkable.

On primary physical examination, the patient was found to be in fair general condition, conscious, with stable vital signs. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen with no organomegaly. A 7 × 7 cm mobile, soft, non-tender mass was palpable over the right iliac fossa. Chest examination revealed bilateral normal vesicular breath sounds, and cardiovascular examination showed normal heart sounds.

Additional laboratory tests included urine microscopy, which was within normal limits except for 1–2 pus cells per high-power field. Hematological analysis showed a slightly elevated total leukocyte count (TLC) at 8100/mm3, though other differential counts and hemoglobin levels were normal. Biochemical tests, including urea, creatinine, electrolytes, and liver function tests, were all within normal ranges. The coagulation assay revealed a slightly prolonged prothrombin time (PT), but with a normal International Normalized Ratio (INR), indicating that this finding was not clinically significant. Serological exams were negative for Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), Hepatitis C antibody (HCV Ab), and HIV antibody, ruling out these viral infections.

Further investigations were conducted to evaluate the nature of the mass. An ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis, revealed a 7.6 × 5.7 cm heteroechoic solid cystic lesion in the right paraumbilical region, abutting the inferior surface of the liver and the inferior pole of the right kidney. There was no increased vascularity within the lesion, and a follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scan (CECT) was recommended. The subsequent CECT of the abdomen and pelvis, identified an 8.3 × 6.2 × 6.2 cm well-defined cystic mass in the right retroperitoneum. The mass contained multiple foci of calcification and thick septa, which enhanced on post-contrast images. It was noted to displace and mildly compress the infrahepatic inferior vena cava, and it abutted the right kidney, psoas muscle, L3-L4 vertebral body, hepatic flexure, and the D3 segment of the duodenum (Fig. 1A–D).

Cytopathology from a fine needle aspiration of the retroperitoneal mass showed macrophages, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and degenerated cells, with no evidence of atypical or malignant cells, suggesting a benign lesion. Histopathological examination of the biopsy specimen revealed spindle-shaped cells arranged in fascicles with oval to elongated nuclei and mild chromasia, consistent with a diagnosis of Schwannoma, a benign nerve sheath tumor. Immunohistochemical staining was diffusely positive for S100, and the Ki-67 index was less than 1 %, indicating low proliferative activity and confirming the benign nature of the tumor.

The patient was taken to the operating room for an exploratory laparotomy and excision of the retroperitoneal mass. After administering prophylactic antibiotics (1 g ceftriaxone), the patient was placed in the lithotomy position for the insertion of a right-sided double J (DJ) stent by the Urology department. The patient was then repositioned in the supine position, and the abdomen was prepared and draped in a sterile manner. A midline incision was made, and the abdomen was opened in layers. Upon entering the abdominal cavity, the greater omentum and small bowel were retracted to expose the retroperitoneal space (Fig. 2). A well-circumscribed, globoid tumor mass, approximately 7 × 7 cm in size, was identified in the right retroperitoneum. The tumor was carefully dissected and mobilized. The ureter was lateralized to prevent injury. The mass was found to be abutting the inferior vena cava (IVC) and the third part of the duodenum (D3), from which it was meticulously dissected free. The gonadal vessels were ligated and divided to facilitate tumor excision. The feeder vessels supplying the tumor were also ligated to control bleeding. After ensuring complete mobilization, the tumor was excised en bloc, taking care to preserve surrounding vital structures. The surgical field was inspected for hemostasis. The linea alba was closed with a PDS loop knot, and the abdominal incision was closed in layers. The excised specimen was sent for histopathological examination to confirm the diagnosis.

The mass is in close proximity to the inferior vena cava (IVC) and the third part of the duodenum (D3).

There were no intraoperative and immediate post-operative complications. The cut of specimen revealed a globoid shape with both solid and cystic components, including laminated areas and multiple hemorrhagic pseudocysts (Fig. 3). The intraoperative DJ stent was removed on the 4th post-operative day after consultation with the urology team. Post-operatively, the patient was managed with IV fluids and medications. At the time of discharge, on the 4th post-operative day, the patient was tolerating a normal diet, free of pain, and had stable vital signs. The patient was discharged with instructions for regular follow-up. There have been no signs of recurrence, and the patient is enjoying a good quality of life.

Discussion

Schwannoma is a nerve tumor arising from Schwann sheath cells, seen mostly in women (male/female ratio 2:3) between the 3rd and 5th decade. Wu et all suggested that early age of onset (mean age = 40.67 years) when the tumor was immediately adjacent to vital vessels [4]. But In our case, the patient was asymptomatic although, the mass was displacing and mildly compressing the infrahepatic inferior vena cava. Often benign in 97 %–98 %, presenting as a single or sporadic form. The rarer malignant form is often associated with neurofibromatoses (Von Recklinghausen disease) [5,6], as well as the multiple form [7].

The clinical presentation of retroperitoneal schwannomas tends to be non-specific. In a series of 82 patients with Retroperitoneal Schwannoma, the majority of patients had abdominal distension with no other associated signs (30.5 %) or the diagnosis was incidental (34.1 %), with the remaining patients having abdominal pain (20.7 %), low back pain (6.1 %) or digestive problems (6.1 %) [8]. Although it arises from the nerve sheath, neurological symptoms are rare [9]. This case reflects those findings, with the patient remaining asymptomatic other than the abdominal lump, resulting in a delayed diagnosis.

Imaging methods for retroperitoneal schwannomas include ultrasound, CT, and MRI. Abdominal USG shows cystic mass with semisolid areas in the retroperitoneum which may suggest this diagnosis because most other tumors in the retroperitoneum are not cystic [9]. In our case as well, a 7.6 × 5.7 cm heteroechoic solid cystic lesion was seen in the right paraumbilical region, abutting the inferior surface of the liver and the inferior pole of the right kidney. Abdominal CT usually shows an encapsulated, solid mass when small, although hemorrhagic and necrotic remodeling is frequently found when larger [10]. Calcifications are also possible, an argument in favor of degeneration [9]. Similar to the literature, CECT of the abdomen and pelvis, identified a well-defined cystic mass in the right retroperitoneum with multiple foci of calcification and thick septa. So, we recommend this diagnosis should be suspected in asymptomatic patients with a paravertebral mass showing evidence of internal calcification and cystic degeneration. However, while ultrasound and CT scans are useful, they do not always provide a definitive preoperative diagnosis [11]. Therefore, preoperative diagnosis of retroperitoneal schwannoma is very difficult regardless of the use of advanced imaging techniques, and biopsy is not recommended due to the increased risk of blood vessel damage especially when complex blood vessels are adjacent [11]. At the same time, due to the heterogeneity within the tumor lesions, the needle biopsy may be inaccurate [12]. The cell pleomorphism in the degenerated area may lead to the misdiagnosis of malignant tumors. If nuclear atypia, mitotic figures, and vascular invasion are observed histologically, the diagnosis of malignant retroperitoneal schwannoma can be suggested [13]. So, pre-operative biopsy is not useful in case of retroperitoneal schwannoma.

In cases where the tumor is asymptomatic and benign, conservative management with radiologic surveillance may be an option to avoid the complications associated with surgery [14,15]. Both laparoscopic and open surgery can be performed for treatment of this condition [16,17]. In our case, we performed exploratory laparotomy and excision of the retroperitoneal mass since it was a resource limited setting.

Complete surgical excision is considered the best treatment for retroperitoneal schwannomas, as it is curative and helps prevent recurrence [9,18,19]. The other reason is the difficult to correctly diagnose schwannoma and distinguish between benign and malignant through imaging analysis, and needle biopsy has certain limitations as mentioned above. However, controversy exists over the necessity of negative soft-tissue margins especially when adjacent tissue or viscera need to be sacrificed [20]. Although some investigators advocate complete excision including the sacrifice of adjacent tissue if necessary [20],others believe that because of the benign nature of this tumor, simple enucleation or partial excision is sufficient [21]. They cite examples of tumors partially excised showing no subsequent enlargement or malignant change [22]. Still, most authors believe that to confirm the diagnosis and rule out any possible malignancy, curative resection should be performed to obtain a negative soft tissue margin, even including the sacrifice of surrounding structures, as incomplete resection results in local recurrence in ∼10–20 % of patients [23]. In our case, our patient underwent complete resection and had no evidence of recurrence and any complications at follow-up. So, we recommend complete resection to be done since one of the major postoperative complication of incomplete resection is recurrent schwannoma (5–10 %) [16]. Re-surgical resection is recommended for recurrent schwannoma as it is insensitive to radiation and chemotherapy [23,24].

Conclusion

Retroperitoneal schwannomas are rare tumors that are difficult to diagnose preoperatively, as radiologic findings are often nondiagnostic. Our case highlights the importance of thorough imaging and histopathological evaluation in establishing the diagnosis. The treatment of choice is complete surgical excision, which was successfully performed in this patient, resulting in no signs of recurrence postoperatively.
