Testing a multi-faceted strategy to support the implementation of ACEs screenings in primary care: results of a stepped-wedge pilot trial
Authors: Mónica Pérez Jolles, Wendy J Mack, Samantha Rubio, Laura J Helmkamp, Lisa Saldana, Gregory A Aarons, Anna S Lau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) screenings are increasingly being used in primary care clinics to identify toxic stress and potential trauma in children. ACEs are negative life events (e.g., violence exposure) occurring before age 18, that can increase health risks when unaddressed. However, we lack evidence on the impact of ACEs screenings and how they can be feasibly implemented in community-based clinics. We partnered with federally qualified health clinics to test the impact of a multifaceted implementation strategy on ACEs screening reach and mental health referrals for children ages 0–5.MethodsWe conducted a Hybrid Type 2 pilot trial using a stepped-wedge design (2021–2024). Reach data was measured as the proportion of eligible children screened for ACEs, with data collected from Electronic Health Records. We also assessed the percentage of mental health service referrals among all eligible children. Study clinics (n = 3) switched from no ACEs screenings (control) to implementing ACEs screenings supported by the multi-faceted ACE implementation strategy (intervention). The tested strategy comprised personnel training (e.g., trauma-informed care), integrated technology, team-based screening workflows, and ongoing care team implementation support. Additional clinics (n = 2) implemented ACEs screenings as usual without the strategy and served as additional comparison sites for exploratory analyses. We found that a multi-faceted implementation strategy informed by partners and designed to support ACEs screenings in community-based clinics was feasible. Background

Adverse Childhood Experiences or ACEs during childhood can cause or worsen physical and mental health conditions and can interrupt a child’s developmental trajectories to adulthood [1]. ACEs have been defined as potentially traumatic events occurring before age 18, such as maltreatment and domestic violence [2, 3]. Originally centered on household dysfunction and interpersonal trauma, the ACEs literature has expanded to recognize the impact of societal-level adversities such as exposure to racism [4]. ACEs are prevalent in the pediatric population in the United States. In 2017–2018, 33% of children ages 0–17 had experienced at least one parent-reported ACE in their lifetime; 14.1% experienced two or more ACEs [5, 6].

Exposure to prolonged stressors such as ACEs during childhood can lead to a toxic stress response[7, 8], which in turn is associated with physical health conditions such as asthma and cardiovascular disease, as well as low school performance[9–11]. ACEs have also been associated with mental health needs such as post-traumatic stress disorder, depression and suicide[12]. Emerging literature strongly suggests that psychosocial screenings in primary care are effective in identifying needs and initiating service referrals [13].

In 2020 the state of California enacted a fee-for-service reimbursement healthcare policy called ACEs Aware[14], the first in the country, designed to promote universal ACEs screenings in primary care. The goal of this policy is to identify toxic stress and potential trauma early on through annual ACEs screenings [15]. As part of the ACEs Aware policy, clinics are reimbursed $29 for every screening of their Medicaid (Medi-Cal in California) pediatric patient population [16] during well-child visits [17, 18].

Since the enactment of this policy, ACEs screenings in clinical settings have been increasingly implemented in California. In 2024, the ACEs Aware initiative had reimbursed a total of 1,267,340 ACEs screenings conducted in primary care and among unique Medi-Cal recipients ages 0 to 17 between 2020 and 2024 [19]. Thus, primary care has become a critical setting in identifying ACEs and linking families to support services, especially among underserved patient populations that often endure higher rates of ACEs due to systemic and historical inequities [20]. This policy development offered a unique opportunity for a natural experiment to test an implementation strategy tailored to primary care settings to increase the impact of the ACEs Aware initiative and to assess the impact of ACEs screening on mental health referrals [21–24].

While there is strong evidence linking ACEs to long-term health outcomes, there is limited evidence demonstrating whether ACEs screenings, on their own, improve patient outcomes such as linkage to referral services [47]. This NIMH-funded pilot study seeks to contribute to this gap in the literature by presenting the results of a piloted multi-faceted implementation strategy (i.e., ACEs_IS) designed to support ACEs screenings in community-based clinics and in partnership with a large Federally Qualified Health Center (FQHC) system in inland Southern California.

Guided by the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment Framework or EPIS [25], this pilot trial: (a) designed the ACEs_IS to increase the readiness of clinical settings to adopt ACEs screenings during the preparation phase of the study; (b) examined the impact of ACEs_IS on the reach of ACEs screenings and rates of mental health service referrals, and (c) assessed the acceptability and feasibility of ACEs_IS among clinical personnel. The EPIS framework also informed relevant outer and inner contextual factors during each phase of the study that were relevant for this study (Fig. 1).

EPIS outer and inner context informing the pilot study

During the preparation phase, the ACEs_IS activities included tailoring patient screening procedures and team-based workflows to local clinics through implementation mapping. During the implementation phase, the activities comprised using electronic tablets to have patient caregivers complete screenings, ongoing care team coaching, and Electronic Medical Record (EMR) record extraction for tracking and reporting reach and referrals.

Details on the study protocol have been published elsewhere [26]. During the trial, changes to the original study protocol were needed to respond to unexpected outer-context events, including the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on clinic operations, co-occurring organizational financial crises, and resultant clinic closures and workforce instability within the partner FQHC system.

Methods

Study design

This pilot study was conducted using a stepped-wedge pragmatic trial without transition periods [27]. Eligible children were aged 0–5, on public insurance (i.e., Medi-Cal), and attending an annual wellness visit with the participating clinic; ACEs screenings were given to caregivers at the clinics during three/six/nine month visit as well as yearlyThe partner FQHC system planned to start ACE screenings on its own in 2021 and have selected clinics join this trial by adding the ACEs_IS to their ongoing screening efforts. However, due to the pandemic and organizational challenges, clinics were not able to start ACEs screenings as planned.

As a result, the research team revised the trial design and the study arms: a) Control status: clinics had not begun implementing ACEs screenings, and were using routine procedures (i.e. family conversations with pediatricians) to identify behavioral needs during annual wellness visits for children ages 0 to 5 years old, and activating a mental health referral as indicated, and b) Intervention status: the same clinics initiated ACEs screenings with the support of the ACEs_IS according to the stepped-wedge schedule. The results of these two arms are presented in this paper as the primary data analysis results. We also conducted sub-group analyses to examine intervention differences in reach and mental health service referrals by children’s age, gender, and race/ethnicity.

Additionally, we obtained data on the outcomes of reach and mental health service referral rates from two additional comparison clinics that were part of the FQHC system, but not enrolled in the trial. These two clinics had begun to implement ACEs screenings in early 2020 on their own with consulting support from the state of California’s ACEs Aware initiative but without ACEs_IS. We leveraged these data to perform exploratory data analyses on outcomes by comparing clinics implementing screenings plus the ACEs_IS (intervention status) against clinics implementing screenings without the ACEs_IS (comparison clinics).

Stepped-wedge pragmatic trial

The pragmatic pilot trial began in December of 2021 and lasted 19 months and was conducted in seven 10-week periods. A sixth and seventh periods were added to the planned schedule to extend data collection and meet expected data collection goals. Following an initial control 10-week period, clinics sequentially switched from control to intervention. EMR data for eligible children were collected from each partner clinic on an aggregated level. Data collected included the number of eligible children in the study, mental health referrals due to those screenings, child socio-demographics (i.e., age, sex, gender, race, and ethnicity), and the total number of eligible well-child visits. At the start of the study, the partner FQHC system comprised a network of 27 clinics in three California counties. The patient demographic predominantly consisted of Hispanic individuals (70%), serving diverse patient populations, including migrants, agricultural laborers, first—and second-generation immigrants, and refugees. Selection of study clinics

We initially randomly selected five clinics from a pool of 16 eligible clinics that provided family medicine, pediatric, or primary care for children ages 0 to 5 and across five regions to maximize variation (e.g., clinic characteristics such as resources and size). The study PI and first author, and FQHC champions invited the identified clinics via email to an informational video session to introduce the study.

Once the pilot study started, clinic managers from two of the selected clinics indicated they could not participate—one clinic had closed their doors, and one clinic lost the pediatrician leading ACEs screenings. Due to extremely high clinical staff turnover and closures, two additional selected clinics could not participate in this study. Thus, of the three clinics that ultimately enrolled in the trial, one was originally randomly selected, and two clinics were recruited to join based on their organizational capacity to participate at that time. Clinical care team members (i.e., pediatricians, medical assistants, promotoras, and paid family support specialists) implemented the ACEs screenings and were supported by the ACEs_IS after crossing into intervention status. It is important to note that promotoras played a key role in coordinating and implementing the pediatric screenings but not all clinics in the study had a promotora as a staff person. Among these clinics, ACEs screenings were implemented mainly by Medical Assistants and Pediatricians.

The ACEs Implementation Strategy (ACEs_IS)

The multifaceted ACEs_IS pilot tested in this study was designed by the research team by incorporating the required aspects of ACEs Aware policy. The ACEs_IS is comprised of the following activities at each phase:

Preparation Phase (i.e., five months prior to the trial initiation and eight weeks for clinical pre-launch period).

Activity 1

Increased readiness for change.Clinical and caregiver partners worked alongside the research team to co-create implementation activities using implementation and process mapping methodology. A previous publication fully describes the use of this methodology [31]. A group of 13 Latina mothers participated and provided recommendations such as tailoring ACEs-related scripts to be used by care teams to introduce the screenings to families. These co-creation activities aligned ACEs_IS with outer (i.e., ACEs Aware policy requirements) and inner (FQHC system and local community-clinics) contextual characteristics. This phase also focused on increasing clinics’ readiness to adopt and implement ACEs screenings.

Activity 2

Early engagement of multi-level champions: a) Implementation champion: An internal staff member of the partner healthcare system was actively involved during idea development, grant proposal, and preparation phases; b) Implementation teams representing clinics: Clinic managers, pediatricians and their care team (e.g., medical assistant, scriber, and promotora if present at the clinic) were part of the implementation mapping; and c) Trauma-Informed Care workgroup (TIC): Representatives from the partner FQHC (i.e., administration, healthcare providers) and researchers from various institutions (between 10–15 partners) met for one hour every other month to coordinate and support the launching of the ACEs initiative.

Activity 3

Tailored training: Four weeks before implementation, care teams and clinic personnel received tailored training designed to complement the state’s ACEs Aware online training. The training included a refresher on ACEs and impact on a child’s health, PEARLS scoring, trauma-informed care, and a review of a tailored clinical workflow co-created with clinical partners using implementation mapping methodology [31] (Fig. 3).

Team-based workflow in primary care to implement ACEs screenings

As requested by clinical partners during the preparation phase, clinical partners and implementation champions received training on TIC. Two nationally recognized coaches provided this training online through a 60-min session. Coaches shared information on toxic stress and led discussions to strategize how care teams could respond to potential scenarios that were shared by attendees (e.g., caregiver becoming upset when asked about ACEs during a wellness visit).

The implementation champion also attended an online implementation training from the VA Quality Enhancement Research Initiative (QUERI) [32]. This training is provided by the US Department of Veterans Affairs with a focus on implementation science and materials tailored to implementers as the audience. Attendees learn the basics of implementation science as well as learning hubs related to implementation facilitation, adaptations and a ‘QUERI Implementation Roadmap’ [33]. This training allowed the study champion at the partner AHRQ to increase knowledge and skills in preparation for her role as internal implementation champion and facilitator. This individual was tasked with providing technical support to each clinic during the implementation phase (i.e., ensuring completion of training activities for care teams, and managing communication between the research team, clinics’ care teams, and clinical leadership).

Activity 4

Preparation for the use of technology: Electronic tablets were linked to a HIPAA-compliant online REDCap electronic data system [34] hosted at the first author’s institution. The PEARLS questions were presented within the REDCap project with automatic scoring and the ACEs health-related checklist for the screener to complete based on the PEARLS scoring. In addition, the Pediatrics Symptoms Checklist (PSC) was integrated into the system with automatic scoring. The PSC is a validated measure to identify caregiver concerns about their child’s emotional and behavioral development [35, 36], and was used to complement the ACEs screenings. The REDCap system used an enhanced algorithm integrating scores from the PEARLS, ACEs-Associated Health Conditions Checklist, and PSC to suggest concrete action from care teams (e.g., education with handouts for low risk, and referral to mental health services for evaluation if not already referred for higher risk). Details on this ACEs-related screening algorithm are published elsewhere [26].

Implementation phase

Following trial initiation, the following activities were delivered as part of the ACEs_IS:

Activity 1

Ongoing implementation coaching with care teams: Two research team members and the implementation champion met with care teams online every 4–5 weeks for 35–40 min. Meetings were held during the team’s administrative time, with approval from clinic managers, to avoid interrupting clinic activities. Fidelity and adaptations to the ACEs_IS were assessed during these meetings.

Activity 2

Data tracking and reporting: The partner FQHC’s implementation champion and data coordinator for the study (i.e., the internal data team) prepared and transferred EMR data for each study clinic. The EMR data included the aggregated number of ACEs screenings aggregated by each clinic and by child’s characteristics (i.e., age, gender, race, ethnicity), and total number of eligible child visits as denominator). EMR data were transferred to the first author’s institution using a password-protected and HIPAA-compliant secure server and transfer system.

Activity 3

Check-ins with leadership: Every 4–5 weeks, the research team and the implementation champion met online with the FQHC middle and upper management individuals (e.g., CEO, Mental Health Coordinator, and Pediatrics department chair) to share study updates and discuss ways to support care team members who were implementing screenings and the ACEs_IS at each clinic.

Assessment of outcomes

Pre-implementation phase

Two to three weeks before clinics started, ACEs screenings and the use of the ACEs_IS, inner-context data were collected using validated measures through online surveys completed by clinic personnel. The measures were: a) organizational readiness for implementing change [37], defined as readiness to start ACEs screenings at the clinic; b) implementation climate [38], defined as the shared perceptions from individuals that an innovation is rewarded and supported within an organization [39], and c) implementation leadership [40] assessing the degree to which leadership (i.e., managers) is proactive, knowledgeable, and supportive, regarding a practice implementation. Staff reported about their supervisor/leader, and the supervisors/leaders reported about themselves. These three measures use a Likert agreement scale with higher scores reflecting higher organizational agreement on readiness, and a workplace’s climate and leadership that supports the adoption and implementation of ACEs screenings.

Implementation phase

The reach of the ACEs screenings supported by the ACEs_IS was estimated using EMR data. Reach was calculated in participating clinics by dividing the number of children screened for ACEs by the total number of eligible children seen in a given period. The main hypothesis for the study was that clinics crossing over to intervention status and using the ACEs_IS will have the capacity to adopt ACEs screenings and report higher reach over time compared to their control status. Before starting the trial, we aimed to reach at least 80% of eligible children toward universal screenings.

Referrals to mental health services after the ACEs screenings (i.e., clinical outcome) were assessed through EMR data and calculated in each 10-week period as the number of referrals divided by the total number of study-eligible children. Mental health referrals were reported from the EMR system after the date when children were screened for ACEs. Based on the FQHC system, referrals were defined as any referrals to behavioral analysis, mental health, care coordinator, care management, child development center, or social work. At the end of data collection (Step 7 in the stepped wedge schedule), care teams evaluated the ACEs_IS regarding its feasibility and acceptability using validated measures [40–42] and through an online survey.

Statistical analysis

Survey data on contextual measures and the ACEs_IS’s feasibility and acceptability were analyzed using aggregated intra-class correlations (ICC) to assess within-group agreement, whether clinic care teams shared perceptions of readiness for change during the preparation phase, and implementation climate and leadership. ICC is the variance proportion attributed to care teams [43]. An agreement of 0.70 or higher reflects an agreement among raters on the organizational measure [44].

EMR data assessing reach and referrals to mental health services were analyzed using descriptive statistics and adjusted models. Children's characteristics (i.e., age, gender, race, and ethnicity) were described using counts and percentages, and intervention versus control were compared on these characteristics using chi-squared tests. We calculated risk differences with confidence limits calculated overall and compared within subgroups (by age, gender, race, and ethnicity) using asymptotic Wald confidence limits. The outcome of screenings’ reach was analyzed using log-binomial models and the outcome of mental health referrals was analyzed using modified Poisson regression with a robust covariance matrix. Risk differences were estimated comparing the intervention and control arms. All models were adjusted for clinical site and children’s race and ethnicity. Missingness in demographic data was handled by including an ‘unknown or not reported’ category. All analyses were conducted in SAS software, version 9.4 [45], and p-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

During the pre-implementation phase, online surveys were implemented among 47 clinical care teams across five clinics planned for trial participation to assess inner-context characteristics. Survey data with average scores higher than three showed that clinic personnel felt ready to start the ACEs screenings, and that there was a positive climate for implementation of the screenings. Both scales, readiness for change and implementation climiate, showed that these perceptions were shared among clinic personnel for most clinics (reliability threshold of 0.70 +). There was higher disagreement about implementation leadership, as shown by lower within-clinic ICC thresholds for clinic staff and leadership and wide variation across clinics. Implementation coaching

A total of 12 coaching sessions were conducted across clinics. During these sessions, clinic personnel shared challenges during the early implementation phase of the ACEs screenings. Challenges from caregivers included a reluctance to answer some of the PEARLS questions as they were seen as intrusive and as sensitive information, even when caregivers answered the survey by providing a total number of questions endorsed, rather than responses to specific question items. Challenges within clinics included staffing shortages, increased patient flows, and heavy workloads, which impacted ACEs-related workflows. In some cases, lower capacity within clinics for service referral tracking led to delays in linking families to services.

The fidelity of the ACEs_IS was assessed during the coaching sessions, to troubleshoot, and track fidelity and adaptations using an Excel tracking tool. A key adaptation that emerged during the implementation process to maintain fidelity of the ACEs_IS, and shared by care teams during the coaching sessions, was peer support. An individual from the first clinic who served as a champion during the preparation and early implementation of the ACEs screenings stepped in to serve in this role. During the first two weeks of ACEs screenings implementation in a new clinic, this champion spent a few hours at the new clinical site supporting peer care team members by reviewing the clinical workflow steps, observing, and troubleshooting. Second, researchers used the scripts provided by the ACEs Aware website as a starting point to tailor messages by incorporating recommendations from the caregivers and clinical partners who participated in the planning process. This adaptation had a positive impact in supporting clinics’ launch of ACEs screenings and efforts to embed the ACEs workflow into their daily routine.

Survey data were analyzed to assess the feasibility and acceptability [40–42] of the ACEs_IS activities among clinical care teams. A total of 10 participants completed the survey, representing care teams that were part of the study across the three clinics that completed the study trial. From that group, 40% were Medical Assistants, 40% were promotoras, and 20% were providers (e.g., MD, DO, NP). A total of 80% self-identified as female, and 10% as male, with the remaining 10% as ‘other’. Of those who answered the question about race and ethnicity (n = 6), all self-identified as Hispanic.

Feasibility and implementation were rated on a 5-point response scale with higher scores reflecting higher feasibility, and acceptability. Based on individual survey data completed by clinic personnel and using validated scales, the feasibility of ACEs screenings was perceived to be slightly higher (average mean = 3.32), than the feasibility of the ACEs_IS (average mean of 2.97). The acceptability of the ACEs screenings (average mean = 3.4) was also higher than the acceptability of the ACEs_IS (average mean = 2.98). Lastly, the top-rated implementation strategy activities in terms of usefulness (i.e., best supported their screening efforts) as the use of technology and ongoing coaching for implementation support.

Discussion

The objective of this pilot study was to assess the feasibility of the ACEs_IS using a participatory approach with partners (i.e., caregivers, clinical personnel, FQHC administrators, and researchers) [31] to increase clinics’ capacity to implement ACEs screenings in primary care.

The preparation activities during the pre-implementation phase involved multi-level partners such as caregivers, clinic and system personnel (e.g., health service providers, support staff, data managers, and information technology), managers, and leadership. The goal was to promote a supportive work climate and perceived leadership support for this initiative while increasing clinics’ capacity to reach eligible children with the ACEs screenings. Results showed that personnel at each clinic shared the perception that they were ready to start ACEs screenings and use the ACEs_IS and that these efforts were recognized and rewarded in their workplace. However, there was less agreement among clinic staff and leaders on concrete leadership support for this innovation and for implementers’ efforts, likely reflecting extreme turnover in managerial and frontline levels at the clinics as a contributing factor.

The study clinics represented various geographic regions and organizational sizes and served primarily Hispanic child patients. Post-implementation, ACEs screenings reached 11.2% of eligible children. This percentage does not include caregivers who declined to participate in these screenings. These results could be partly explained by a lack of agreement among clinic personnel and leadership about the latter support of this initiative and of implementers’ efforts, as shown in our survey data results. Despite this result, we emphasize that the preparation and use of a multifaceted implementation strategy supported community-based clinics’ capacity to launch and implement ACEs screenings throughout the 19 months of the pilot trial. We also found a representative reach of the intervention by child’s gender, race, and ethnicity. The only significant difference in reach was a higher percentage of screenings among older children ages 18 to 60 months, compared to younger children. In addition, our intervention group’s reach of 11% was higher than the 4% reach of comparison clinics that started screening on their own, without an academic partnership and use of implementation protocols. Considering this study aligned with the start of COVID-19, which greatly impacted the operations and funding of community-based clinics [46], our study results are still within the lower end of what it has been reported in the literature (i.e. 4% to 12% overall) [47].

Study results also showed that the ACEs screening and the piloted ACEs_IS increased mental health service referrals from 0.4% to 7.2% across study clinics, compared to a 0.2% referral rate across comparison clinics. In addition, there were significant differences in referrals by age between the implementation and control clinic status, with older children (ages 18–60 months) having a greater increase in referrals, as well as the ‘other or unknown’ race group having a large increase in referrals. For the former, it is possible that older children were already connected to school-based and early development programs, which may have made it easier for care teams to initiate a service referral. Our ability to draw conclusions on the ‘other/unknown’ racial group is limited given lack of information on what this group entails. Overall, this study results aligned with the literature [48] showing that ACEs screenings increase identification of adverse experiences in children, and an association with increased service referrals. Although we acknowledge the importance of gathering data on the impact of ACEs screenings on referral completion for mental health referrals, and some evidence in the literature supporting this association [49], this was beyond the scope of this two-year pilot study.

Importantly, the denominator used for mental health referral rates reflects the total number of eligible children, not necessarily the number of children with a positive ACEs score or ACE-related health conditions. These data were not tracked in the electronic medical record (EMR) system at the FQHC. We acknowledge the importance of tracking this data, and literature showing that ACEs and referrals increase over time [50]. It is reasonable to assume that ACES and referrals increasing over time could explain the larger intervention effect in older ages found in this study. However, that effect of increasing with age should show in both intervention and control periods, and the intervention effect is expected to have had controlled for that.

Overall, these study results need to be interpreted with limitations within context. For outer-context factors, the external environment during the enactment of the 2020 ACEs Aware policy and clinical adoption of ACEs screenings coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic. Clinics participated in the study while facing unprecedented negative shocks to their system including the pandemic that strained clinic capacity due to financial strain and negative workforce impacts. Indeed, FQHCs experienced a significant economic hit in 2020–2023 that forced several clinics to close their doors and worsened workforce turnover and burnout [44].

From an ACEs Aware policy perspective, California reimbursed clinics for submitting ACEs screening scores. The policy defined a complete ACE screening as involving “clinical assessment, including for protective factors and ACE-associated health conditions. Together, these indicators inform assessment of toxic stress risk.” [51] Yet, this fee-for-service approach offering $29 reimbursement for each completed screening offered little capacity and incentive for clinics to update their EMR reporting systems to incorporate other ACEs-related data (e.g., risk levels by child characteristics), and to focus on the implementation of ACEs screenings with fidelity while capturing caregiver experiences and needed adaptations. In addition, the reimbursement of $29 per screening event appeared unlikely to provide enough resources for clinics. For example, none of the study clinics hired additional personnel or added work hours from current staff to lead these new screenings, as ACEs screenings were added to already strained workflows. Last, at the time of enactment and adoption in 2020 and 2021, the ACEs Aware policy did not offer clear guidance on how clinics could strengthen service referral processes or how this policy addressed a lack of mental health resources in some communities. These conditions posed barriers to adoption among safety net clinical settings.

Inner-context factors were related to implementation capacity (i.e., infrastructure and resources). Due to extreme personnel shortages, turnover, and strained workflows in these community-based settings, worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare teams and managers had a difficult time, despite their efforts, adding participatory planning and implementation activities to support the screenings. From this perspective, as care teams implemented family-centered strategies (i.e., the use of scripts to introduce the study and verbal checks with caregivers to ensure reading comprehension of the PEARLS questions), the efficiency of clinical workflows decreased as screenings took longer.

On the other hand, the ACEs Aware policy put ACEs on the map for clinics and fostered open discussions among clinical personnel and between care teams and caregivers about ACEs, and their impact on children’s toxic stress and potential trauma. Financial reimbursement from ACEs Aware policy provided strong motivation to adopt these screenings, especially in under-resourced community clinics. Yet, the policy did not offer enough funding for these settings to implement screenings from a family-centered approach (e.g., reading screening questions to some caregivers to assure comprehension), to build Electronic Medical Records to track and report data, and to set up systems to successfully implement ‘completed ACEs screenings’ [51] as fostered by the policy. Care teams and clinic personnel recognized the value of assessing for ACEs and made great efforts to adopt screenings and support families despite multi-level challenges.

The next steps include scaling up these efforts to continue testing the impact of ACEs screenings on children’s service referrals and ultimately on their health, and continue to test the ACEs_IS, including adaptations, in other community-based clinics. We also recommend refining the ACEs Aware policy to require documentation supporting a comprehensive approach to ACEs screening as part of reimbursement. However, that would likely require significantly more resources in under-resourced clinical settings. Increased funding is likely needed to support the role demands of screening within care teams at FQHCs.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first NIH-funded study examining an implementation strategy wrapped around the ACEs Aware policy. Despite multi-level challenges to implementing psychosocial screenings in primary care, careful planning that allows additional time to involve clinical and caregiver partners to inform implementation was beneficial. Partners were involved in the local tailoring of clinical workflows, coordinating the use of technology across clinics, providing ongoing support to healthcare teams during the implementation of screenings, and developing family-centered scripts for use by care teams to ease implementation.

Overall, aligned with the goal of pilot studies [52], we found that a multifaceted implementation strategy designed to support ACEs screenings, and the screenings themselves, in community-based clinical settings were feasible. However, the strategy's impact was attenuated by the ACEs Aware policy requirements and unprecedented outer-context events impacting the FQHC system under study.
