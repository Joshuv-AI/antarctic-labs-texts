Exposure definition sensitivity unmasks hidden confounding in crystalloid target trial emulation
Authors: Qingxia Dai, Yu Hao, Jie Shen, Xingxing Ren, Chaoyuan Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTarget trial emulation (TTE) is increasingly used to estimate causal effects from observational data. We tested whether satisfying standard diagnostics is sufficient by emulating a balanced crystalloid versus 0.9% saline trial in Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) (n = 42,883) with eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) external validation. Two pre-specified exposure definitions—initial assignment and 48-h dominant strategy—yielded directionally opposite major adverse kidney events within 30 days (MAKE-30) estimates (odds ratio [OR]: 0.49 vs. OR: 2.51) despite satisfactory propensity score diagnostics (C-statistic: 0.787; maximum standardized mean difference [SMD]: 0.048). Treatment switching reached 89.4%, reflecting local fluid workflows. Pre-specified negative control outcomes flagged residual confounding but were length-of-stay dependent. The pattern was absent in eICU-CRD, where recorded switching was 1.2%. Standard TTE diagnostics did not detect confounding from time-varying institutional practice. We propose that operationalization sensitivity, switching characterization, and directed acyclic graph (DAG)-guided assessment become routine TTE components.Keywords: target trial emulation, causal inference, exposure definition, negative control outcomes, crystalloid therapy, time-varying confounding

Introduction

Target trial emulation (TTE) provides a structured framework for estimating causal effects from observational data by explicitly specifying the hypothetical randomized trial that the analysis seeks to approximate.1,2 Once the target trial has been defined, standard practice involves verifying adequate overlap in treatment probability, achieving covariate balance through weighting or matching, and benchmarking estimates against existing evidence.3 When these diagnostic checks are satisfactory, the resulting estimates are generally treated as credible.

Whether these diagnostics are truly sufficient has received less systematic attention. In particular, when treatment patterns evolve over time within an institution—as is common in intensive care unit (ICU) settings—baseline propensity score methods may fail to capture confounding that operates through the dynamics of care delivery rather than through pre-treatment characteristics.

We exploited the well-characterized clinical question of crystalloid fluid choice—balanced crystalloids versus 0.9% normal saline (NS)—as a methodological test case. Large pragmatic trials (SMART,4 BaSICS,5 and PLUS6) have established that the true treatment effect is small or null (major adverse kidney events within 30 days [MAKE-30] odds ratio [OR] approximately 0.904). This makes the question useful as a diagnostic probe: estimates that diverge markedly from this benchmark likely reflect bias rather than genuine treatment heterogeneity.

We designed this study with three objectives: (1) to construct a TTE of crystalloid therapy that satisfies standard causal inference diagnostics, (2) to test whether two commonly used but mechanistically distinct exposure operationalizations produce concordant estimates, and (3) to evaluate whether pre-specified negative control outcomes and design diagnostics detect residual confounding that standard diagnostics miss. This work is a diagnostic methodology study, not an attempt to add to the randomized clinical evidence base for balanced crystalloids versus NS.

Results

Cohort characteristics

From 94,458 ICU stays in MIMIC-IV, 51,373 patients met eligibility for the initial assignment analysis, and 42,883 were eligible for the 48-h analysis (30,510 balanced-dominant and 12,373 NS-dominant); 8,490 patients were excluded as mixed users or for insufficient data. Before weighting, substantial baseline differences existed: balanced-dominant patients were less likely to be mechanically ventilated (26.5% vs. 60.3%; standardized mean difference [SMD]: 0.725) or receiving vasopressors (15.8% vs. 42.3%; SMD: 0.611). After overlap weighting, all SMDs were reduced to ≤0.048 (Table 1).

Causal inference diagnostics

The 48-h propensity score model yielded a C-statistic of 0.787 (initial assignment: 0.730). After average treatment effect in the overlap population (ATO) weighting, the maximum SMD was 0.048 across 23 baseline covariates (Figure 1). Effective sample sizes were 19,604 (64.3%) and 10,246 (82.8%) for the balanced and NS groups, respectively. By conventional criteria applied in published TTEs, these diagnostics would be considered satisfactory.

Contradictory estimates under different operationalizations

The two operationalizations produced directionally opposite estimates for every outcome (Table 2; Figure 2). The 48-h strategy yielded a MAKE-30 OR of 2.51 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.31–2.72), suggesting harm from balanced crystalloids, while initial assignment yielded an OR of 0.49 (95% CI: 0.46–0.55), suggesting benefit—both diverging markedly from the SMART trial benchmark of approximately 0.90.4 Recomputing initial assignment within the 48-h cohort (n = 42,883) confirmed that the reversal was driven by the classification rule itself, not by differential sample composition (MAKE-30 OR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.45–0.54).

Fluid trajectory analysis and conceptual DAG

Treatment switching explained the discrepancy. Among 42,883 patients, 89.4% had a different first fluid type than their 48-h dominant type (Figure 3). The dominant trajectory (70.5%) was NS as the first fluid followed by balanced-dominant status—consistent with, but not proving, a Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC)-specific institutional pattern of NS for acute resuscitation followed by lactated Ringer’s for maintenance. The NS-throughout group (10.0%) had the highest rates of mechanical ventilation (73.4%) and vasopressor use (65.5%), representing the sickest patients who never transitioned off resuscitation fluids (Table S4). Because this pattern was not reproduced in eICU-CRD, we interpret it as a local workflow signal rather than as a generalizable ICU protocol.

Fluid-switching trajectories

To make the structure of the bias explicit, we constructed a conceptual directed acyclic graph (DAG; Figure 5). The DAG shows that baseline overlap weighting can balance pre-treatment severity and comorbidity but cannot close post-baseline paths that flow through evolving clinical status, switching decisions, length of stay/time at risk, latent ICU subphenotypes, and institutional workflow. Negative control outcomes therefore sit at the end of paths that are partly shared with the primary outcomes, which explains why they parallel the primary estimates rather than nullify them.

Conceptual DAG for operationalization-dependent confounding

Baseline weighting can balance measured pre-treatment covariates but cannot close post-baseline paths involving clinical trajectory, switching, survival/continued ICU stay, length of stay, latent ICU subphenotypes, and institutional workflow. Negative control outcomes (pressure ulcer and UTI) share the length-of-stay and time-at-risk paths with the primary outcomes (MAKE-30, mortality, AKI, and RRT), which is why parallel directional shifts under different operationalizations indicate residual confounding rather than purity of the negative controls.

Negative control outcomes

Both operationalizations produced significant associations with negative control outcomes (Table 2; Figure 2), and the direction paralleled the primary outcomes: under the 48-h strategy, balanced crystalloids were associated with higher rates of pressure ulcers (OR: 1.60) and urinary tract infections (UTIs) (OR: 1.48); under initial assignment, the direction reversed (ORs: 0.66 and 0.68, respectively). All associations reached p < 0.001. Because pressure ulcers and UTIs are length-of-stay dependent, these results do not prove confounding by themselves. They are most informative as part of a broader diagnostic pattern that also includes the operationalization reversal, extensive switching, divergence from randomized trial evidence, the DAG (Figure 5), and cross-database comparison.

Sensitivity analyses, subgroups, and external validation

A 48-h landmark analysis (n = 19,778 patients alive and in ICU at 48 h) confirmed persistent associations (MAKE-30 OR: 2.09, 95% CI: 1.88–2.32; mortality from 48 h onward OR 3.40, 95% CI: 2.93–3.95) (Table S1). This restriction means that 23,105 of the 48-h cohort (53.9%) were not in the conditional landmark risk set; therefore, the landmark analysis changes the estimand and does not eliminate selection or time-dependent classification bias. Subgroup analyses showed consistent patterns across sepsis, mechanical ventilation, and non-cardiac care unit (CCU) strata; only the CCU subgroup showed no significant association (OR: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.72–1.69; p = 0.46), with the lowest reported propensity-score discrimination (C-statistic: 0.718) (Table S3). Subgroup-specific switching rates and full within-subgroup SMD diagnostics were not available in the aggregate submission package and should be assessed in future line-level analyses. In eICU-CRD (n = 2,346), where recorded switching was 1.2%, mortality was identical between groups (8.7% vs. 8.7%), and no operationalization discrepancy was observed (Table 3; Figure 4) (Table S5). Discussion

Different operationalizations target different estimands

The directional reversal does not indicate instability of a single causal effect. Each operationalization targets a distinct estimand: initial assignment estimates the effect of being the type of patient who receives a particular first fluid (confounded by resuscitation acuity), while the 48-h strategy estimates the effect of being classified by cumulative fluid portfolio (confounded by survival and clinical trajectory). In a setting where 89.4% of patients switch fluid types, both operationalizations capture phase-of-illness effects rather than crystalloid-choice effects. Published TTEs typically report one operationalization without testing alternatives; our findings demonstrate the risk of this practice.7

Why baseline diagnostics missed the bias

Propensity score diagnostics were satisfactory under both operationalizations. As shown in Figure 5, baseline weighting addresses pathways from measured pre-treatment factors but does not address post-baseline paths that link evolving hemodynamic and renal status, clinician switching, length of stay, and latent ICU subphenotypes to outcomes. This is a structural limitation rather than a tuning issue: even a perfectly balanced baseline propensity score cannot block these paths.

Only the negative control outcomes flagged residual confounding. The directional reversal of negative control associations—parallel to the primary outcomes—provides evidence that the confounding is systematic and operationalization dependent. However, pressure ulcers and UTIs correlate with length of stay and nursing intensity, limiting their purity as negative controls. We therefore interpret them cautiously: they support residual confounding when combined with the operationalization reversal and switching evidence, but they do not by themselves exclude a time-at-risk pathway. These results add to the growing evidence that negative control outcomes should be routine but carefully interpreted TTE components.8,9

Clinical heterogeneity and subphenotypes

ICU populations are highly heterogeneous, and recent work has emphasized the existence of latent subphenotypes—for example in sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)—that respond differently to fluid management.10,11,12,13 In our study, sepsis, mechanical ventilation, and CCU subgroups all yielded broadly consistent patterns, but our crude subgroups cannot resolve latent subphenotype structure. Some of what is captured by the operationalization reversal may therefore reflect heterogeneous treatment effects across unmeasured subphenotypes rather than only time-varying confounding. Phenotype-aware reanalysis is an important next step.

Implications for TTE practice

Our findings argue for five additions to standard TTE protocols: (1) exposure operationalization sensitivity testing, reporting whether multiple definitions converge or diverge; (2) pre-specified negative control outcomes that should show null associations under adequate confounding control, interpreted in light of time-at-risk mechanisms; (3) treatment-switching characterization, quantifying the rate and patterns of switching to assess whether baseline exposure classification can meaningfully approximate a sustained treatment strategy; (4) DAG-guided reporting (Figure 5) to distinguish baseline paths from post-baseline clinical trajectory, survival, length-of-stay, and institutional workflow paths; and (5) subgroup or subphenotype checks in heterogeneous ICU populations. When extensive switching is present, baseline propensity score methods may be insufficient, and time-varying approaches (marginal structural models and g-estimation) should be considered—though we note that our 89.4% switching rate may exceed the capacity of even these methods and that they would target a different estimand and require high-quality time-stamped covariate, treatment, and outcome data.14,15,16,17

A TTE of crystalloid fluid therapy passed positivity and covariate balance diagnostics but produced directionally opposite estimates under two common exposure operationalizations and failed negative control testing. Treatment switching (89.4%), driven by local institutional practice, created time-varying confounding that baseline propensity score methods could not address. The study is therefore best interpreted as a diagnostic demonstration of hidden confounding in TTE design, not as new clinical evidence about crystalloid choice. These findings argue for routine incorporation of exposure operationalization sensitivity testing, negative control outcome validation, treatment-switching characterization, DAG-guided assessment (Figure 5), and subgroup or subphenotype diagnostics in TTE studies, particularly for interventions with time-varying treatment patterns.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a diagnostic methodology case study and should not be regarded as adding to the clinical evidence base for balanced crystalloids versus saline; randomized trials remain the appropriate evidence source for that question. Second, MIMIC-IV is a single-center database, and the fluid-switching pattern is institution specific; this limits generalizability but illustrates the vulnerability of single-center TTEs. Third, we did not impose a new-user requirement because most ICU patients receive crystalloid before ICU admission. Fourth, median imputation was used for missing laboratory values, and lactate missingness (35.0%) may introduce differential misclassification. Fifth, we did not implement time-varying causal inference methods (marginal structural models and g-estimation), as our objective was diagnostic rather than corrective. Sixth, our negative control outcomes correlate with length of stay and nursing intensity, limiting their purity. Seventh, the 48-h landmark analysis is conditional on being alive and still in ICU at 48 h and therefore does not eliminate selection bias. Eighth, subgroup-specific switching rates and full within-subgroup balance diagnostics were not available in the aggregate package. Ninth, ICU populations are heterogeneous, and unmeasured subphenotypes may contribute to operationalization-dependent estimates.10,11,12,13 Tenth, eICU-CRD infusion recording completeness is variable, and the low observed switching rate may partly reflect documentation gaps.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study analyzed de-identified adult ICU patient records from MIMIC-IV v3.1 (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, BIDMC; admissions 2008–2022)18 and eICU-CRD v2.0 (approximately 200 United States hospitals; 2014–2015).19 Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with a first ICU admission who received at least 1 L of crystalloid within 24 h of ICU entry. Patients with pre-existing end-stage kidney disease or chronic dialysis were excluded. Sex, age, and other demographic distributions follow the source populations of these databases; demographics for the analytic cohorts are reported in Tables 1 and S7. Both databases were collected under approvals already granted to the source institutions, and use of de-identified data is exempt from additional institutional review board approval. The analytic cohorts had a mean age of approximately 63 years, and 53.9–61.9% of patients were male (Table 1). Race and ethnicity are recorded in the source databases but were not used as model covariates and were not analyzed as effect modifiers; the influence of race or ethnicity on these results was therefore not assessed. Sex was included as a baseline covariate and was balanced after overlap weighting (Table 1); no pre-specified sex-stratified analysis was performed, so a formal assessment of the influence of sex on the findings was beyond the scope of this diagnostic study.

Method details

Target trial specification and emulation

We emulated a two-arm trial comparing balanced crystalloids versus 0.9% normal saline. Time zero was the first crystalloid administration in the ICU. Treatment strategies were defined as receiving all crystalloid as balanced (Strategy A) versus as NS (Strategy B). Reporting follows TARGET20,21 and STROBE22 guidelines. Table 4 summarizes the idealized target trial, the observational emulation, and the main vulnerabilities of each design element.

Exposure operationalizations

Two pre-specified operationalizations were compared. (i) Initial fluid assignment: the first crystalloid type administered in the ICU, regardless of subsequent fluid use. (ii) 48-h dominant strategy: the crystalloid type comprising ≥80% of total volume over the first 48 ICU hours; patients with neither type reaching 80% were excluded as mixed users. The primary classification did not exclude patients solely because they died or were discharged before 48 h if a dominance threshold could be assigned from recorded 0–48-h fluids. To isolate the effect of operationalization from sample composition, initial assignment estimates were also recomputed within the 48-h cohort.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was major adverse kidney events within 30 days (MAKE-30), defined as in the SMART trial4: death, new renal replacement therapy (RRT), or sustained creatinine elevation (≥200% of baseline). Secondary outcomes included hospital mortality, acute kidney injury (AKI; KDIGO stage ≥1 by creatinine criteria), and new RRT within 30 days. Pressure ulcers and urinary tract infections were pre-specified as negative control outcomes.23 Outcomes were ascertained from structured EHR fields and discharge diagnosis codes.

Covariates and missing data

Twenty-three baseline covariates ascertained before time zero were used: age, sex, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, ICU type, emergency admission, comorbidities (sepsis, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, congestive heart failure, cirrhosis, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, atrial fibrillation), and baseline laboratory values (chloride, potassium, creatinine, sodium, lactate, hemoglobin, bicarbonate, blood urea nitrogen). Missing laboratory values were imputed with median values; missingness was 1.8–2.1% for most analytes and 35.0% for lactate (Table S6).

Conceptual DAG

A conceptual DAG (Figure 5) was constructed to clarify which baseline, post-baseline, institutional, length-of-stay, and heterogeneity pathways are not addressed by baseline propensity score weighting. The DAG was used qualitatively to guide interpretation, not as the basis of a formal identification analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Propensity score model

Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression with the 23 baseline covariates above. Model discrimination was reported as the C-statistic (48-h: 0.787; initial assignment: 0.730).

Weighting and effect estimation

Overlap weights targeting the average treatment effect in the overlap population (ATO)24 were the primary weighting method. Inverse probability weights targeting the average treatment effect (ATE) were computed for sensitivity. Treatment effects were estimated using weighted logistic regression with heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC) standard errors.25 Diagnostic checks included the propensity score C-statistic, maximum absolute standardized mean difference (SMD) after weighting, and effective sample size (ESS).

Sensitivity, subgroup, and external validation analyses

Pre-specified sensitivity analyses included (1) ATE weighting; (2) inclusion of SOFA in the propensity model; (3) a 48-h landmark analysis restricted to patients alive and still in the ICU at 48 h (n = 19,778); (4) subgroup analyses for sepsis, mechanical ventilation, and CCU admission; (5) fluid trajectory characterization; and (6) DAG-based qualitative assessment. External validation in eICU-CRD used identical operationalizations and analytic pipeline. p values are two-sided; statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Software

Analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11) with scikit-learn for propensity score estimation and statsmodels for weighted regression with HC standard errors. Reproducibility scripts and aggregate results are available at https://github.com/Jimmmmgh/Target-Trial-Emulation.
