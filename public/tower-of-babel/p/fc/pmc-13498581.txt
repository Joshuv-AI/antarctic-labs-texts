An in vitro comparison of color stability between alkasite and different restorative materials in various staining solutions
Authors: Merve Ağaccıoğlu, Merve Nur Yılmaz, Elif Soslu Bulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the color stability of an alkasite material exposed to different solutions compared with a high-viscosity glass hybrid and a nanohybrid composite resin. A total of ninety-six disc-shaped samples (n=8) were prepared from an alkasite material (Cention N;CN), a high-viscosity glass hybrid (Equia Forte HT;EF), and a nanohybrid composite resin (Estelite Posterior;EP). After baseline color measurements, the samples were stored in three different solutions (distilled water, coffee, and Coca-Cola Zero). Color measurements were repeated on the 7th and 28th day. The discolorations were recorded as ΔE001 (baseline-7 days) and ΔE002 (baseline-28 days) (CIEDE2000). Generalized linear models were used to compare the data across material, solution, and time interval main effects and their interactions. Multiple comparisons were performed using the Tukey test. Statistical significance was determined as p < 0.05. According to the ∆E00 values, material, solution, and time interval were significant main effects (p < 0.001). The lowest and highest ∆E002 values were found in EP-distilled water and CN-coffee groups, respectively. Overall, CN demonstrated higher ∆E001 and ∆E002 results. Alkasite material demonstrated higher discoloration than the nanohybrid composite resin and the high-viscosity glass hybrid material. All tested materials exceeded the acceptability and perceivability thresholds for ΔE001 and ΔE002.Keywords: Alkasite, Glass hybrid, Composite resin, Color stabilitySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Aesthetic characteristics of dental restorations are among patients’ main expectations. Therefore, color stability is of great importance in the restorative material selection process1. Color stability is associated with factors such as material composition, water sorption, staining beverages, and staining duration2. The aesthetic and physical integrity of restorative materials can be significantly compromised by the consumption of beverages like coffee, tea, and acidic carbonated drinks, which are particularly popular among younger demographics. Furthermore, the severity of material degradation is directly proportional to the volume and frequency of exposure3.

Various types of glass ionomer cements (GICs) have maintained a significant role in restorative dentistry for decades. Conventional GICs have several disadvantages, including limited mechanical and aesthetic properties, despite their widespread use due to their fluoride content and adhesion to dental hard tissues. However, composite resins provide superior aesthetic results to conventional GICs. To minimize the disadvantages of GICs, other materials have been introduced to the market, such as high-viscosity glass ionomers and glass hybrids1,4. Improved physico-mechanical properties of glass hybrids, especially, provide wider applications, i.e., class I and II restorations of posterior teeth5. But anterior restorations with glass hybrids have yet to achieve successful aesthetic results due to poor color-match. High-viscosity GICs have been developed over the past two decades through modifications to traditional GICs. After that, Equia Forte HT (EF) was introduced to the market in 20196 as a glass hybrid material containing glass particles, thereby improving its translucency and aesthetic qualities5. EF’s predecessors were as follows: Equia, Equia Fil, and Equia Forte, from the earliest to the latest.

Beyond aesthetic results, restorative materials should also possess enhanced mechanical properties such as wear resistance and fracture strength. Therefore, the development of current materials is ongoing, leading to the introduction of alternative restorative materials. A recent subcategory of composite resins is named ‘alkasite materials’, which demonstrates alkalizing qualities via ion-releasing (Ca2+, F⁻, and PO43⁻ ions)7,8. Alkasite material, a tooth-colored bioactive composite resin, claims to have improved mechanical and aesthetic properties9. Cention N (CN), introduced in 2016, is a commercial product categorized as an alkasite restorative material and is available as hand-mixed powder and liquid or packaged as capsules10. Previous studies focused on various physical properties of alkasite material11–13, however, there are few studies regarding its color stability in different staining conditions14–16.

Regarding alternative restorative materials for posterior restorations, it is essential for clinicians to consider their properties in relation to long-term success and durability. Although conventional nanohybrid composite resins offer superior mechanical and aesthetic properties, due to their hybrid filler composition, their clinical success is highly technique-sensitive, requiring strict moisture isolation, adhesive protocols, and incremental layering14. These requirements pose significant operative challenges in pediatric, geriatric, and special care populations, particularly those with high caries risk or limited cooperation. In contrast, glass hybrid and alkasite systems provide the advantages of bioactive ion release and reduced technique sensitivity16. In contrast, glass hybrid and alkasite systems provide the advantages of bioactive ion release and reduced technique sensitivity, serving as simplified, bioactive alternatives to nanohybrid composite resins. However, while their handling and bioactive properties are advantageous, the long-term esthetic performance of these materials under dietary exposure remains a critical factor for overall clinical acceptability. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the color stability of an alkasite material exposed to different beverages with that of glass hybrid and nanohybrid composite resin across two time intervals. The null hypothesis of the study was that there would be no significant differences in color stability between alkasite material and the other tested materials when exposed to the same solution.

Material and method

Sample preparation

In this study, three different solutions (distilled water, coffee, and cola) and three different restorative materials (alkasite material (Cention N, Ivoclar Vivadent, Switzerland), glass hybrid (Equia Forte HT, GC Corp., Japan), and a nanohybrid composite resin (Estelite Posterior (EP), Tokuyama, Japan)) were used. The sample size was determined as n = 8 using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software, based on a 95% confidence level, a theoretical power of 0.80, and an effect size of 1.5517,18. A total of 96 disc-shaped samples were prepared from each material using a mold (5 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness). A mylar strip was placed under the mold on a glass plate, and then the restorative materials were placed in the mold. A mylar strip and on top of that a glass plate were placed on the mold. The composite resin was polymerized using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing device (1200 mW/ cm2, Blue Lex GT-1200, Monitex, Taiwan, China) for 10 s. The light intensity of the light-curing device was controlled using a digital radiometer (Hilux Ultra Plus Curing Units, Benlioglu Dental, Türkiye) for four samples. The chemically activated glass hybrid and alkasite material were allowed to cure for the time specified by the manufacturer’s instructions. The top surfaces were then polished with 600-grit silicon followed by 1200-grit carbide paper to achieve standardized surfaces. For the glass hybrid material, surface coating (Equia Forte Coat) was applied as recommended by the manufacturers. All samples were incubated at 37° for 24 h.

Staining process and color measurements

After preparing the samples, baseline color measurements were made using a spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade Advance, VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany) against a white background according to the CIE (Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage) L* a* b* system. The average L*, a*, and b* values from three measurements of each sample were then recorded. Distilled water, coffee, and cola were used in this study. The coffee (Nescafé Classic) solution was prepared by adding 2 g of coffee to 200 ml of boiling water. The test materials were stored in cola (Coca-Cola Zero) and distilled water, then incubated at room temperature. Solutions were changed every 24 hours over a 28 day test period.

After the staining procedure, the samples were rinsed in distilled water for 1 minute, dried, and the color measurements were repeated on the 7th and 28th days. The color difference between baseline and the 7th day was calculated as ΔE001, and the color difference between baseline and the 28th day was calculated as ΔE002. KL, KC, and KH are parametric factors calculated for lightness, chroma, and hue, respectively. In this study, these parametric factors were determined as 1. The standard thresholds (50%:50% perceptibility threshold (PT) as 0.8 and 50%: 50% acceptability threshold (AT) as 1.8) of the CIEDE2000 formula were used in this study19.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

The powder component of the glass hybrid and alkasite material, and the composite resin were subjected to SEM analysis (Jeol JSM-7001F, Tokyo, Japan; 15 kV accelerating voltage) to visualize filler particles. The organic matrix of the composite resin was dissolved by storing in acetone before imaging. No additional treatment was carried out for the powder components of the glass hybrid and alkasite materials. The images were obtained at ×3000 magnification.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using Minitab v14 software. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the LN transformation was applied to the quantitative variables Δa00 and Δb00. Generalized linear models were used to compare ΔE00, ΔL00, Δa00, and Δb00 values based on material, solution, and time interval main effects and their interactions. Multiple comparisons were carried out using the Tukey test. Data were described as mean±standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was determined using p < 0.05.

Results

The ANOVA table, mean ± SD values and the bar graph of ∆E00 are shown in Tables 2, 3, and Fig. 2, respectively. According to the ANOVA analysis of ∆E00 values, material, solution, and time interval were significant main effects (p < 0.001), whereas the material-solution-time interval interaction was not significant (p = 0.104, η2 =0.059) (Table 2). The lowest and highest ∆E002 values were found in EP-distilled water (2.13±0.81) and CN-coffee groups (23.28±1.8), respectively (Table 3). In distilled water and cola groups, from the highest to the lowest for both time intervals, ∆E00 values were as follows: CN > EF > EP (Table 3). Regardless of different solutions, there were no significant differences between EF and EP’s total ∆E001 and ∆E002 values (p > 0.05), whereas CN’s total ∆E001 and ∆E002 values demonstrated significant differences (p < 0.05) (Table 3). Regarding material and solution interaction, there were significant differences between all the total ∆E00 values (p < 0.05), except for CN-cola (12.69±2.25) -EF-coffee (13.94±1.97) comparison and EP-distilled water (2.26±1.03)-EP-cola (3.12±0.41) comparison (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the color stability of an alkasite material was evaluated against a glass hybrid and a nanohybrid composite resin under exposure to various solutions over different time intervals. According to the ∆E00 results of the study, although the material-solution-time interval interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.104, η2 =0.059), the material-solution interaction was significant (p < 0.001, η2 =0.689). Regarding material-solution interaction within the same solution groups, all tested materials demonstrated significant differences (p < 0.05). The alkasite material overall exhibited higher discoloration than the glass hybrid and the nanohybrid composite resin. Thus, the null hypothesis, which stated that there would be no significant differences in color stability between alkasite material and the other tested materials when exposed to the same solution, was rejected.

Given their continuous exposure to dietary pigments in the oral cavity, the color stability of restorative materials is a critical consideration for clinicians. A previous study investigated the color stability of CN, including both self-cure and light-cure groups, EF, a nanohybrid composite resin (Z550), and a bulk-fill composite resin, at four time intervals (336 min, 24 h, 144 h, and 12 days)14. Similarly, as in the present study, regardless of the solutions, both CN groups showed significantly higher discoloration than all tested materials at all time intervals. Further, the hand mixing and its consequent air bubbles may contribute to the alkasite material’s solubility and water sorption8,20,21. The degradation of the matrix of the alkasite material due to water sorption may have contributed to the poor color stability of this material. Additionally, the release of ions from the incorporated alkaline glass fillers is considered to induce degradation within the structure, resulting in a surface susceptible to staining22. There was no significant difference between EF and the nanohybrid composite resin as opposed to the total ΔE001 and ΔE002 results of the current study. These differences may be due to methodological discrepancies, such as differences in time intervals and in the composition of the nanohybrid composite resins. The combination of a substantial inorganic load (70% by volume) and a relatively hydrophobic monomer, such as Bis-MPEPP, may be associated with the nanohybrid composite resin’s (EP) (Fig. 6) superior color stability23. Hybrid fillers (1-10 µm) of the composite resin and larger clusters of small particles were observed in alkasite and glass hybrid materials in Fig. 6. Another study compared the color stability of CN to ormocer, giomer, glass carbomer, glass hybrid (Equia Forte), and nanohybrid composite resin. Unlike the present study, Equia Forte was reported to show significantly greater discoloration than CN (p < 0.05)22, which may be due to improvements in EF compared with Equia Forte. The inclusion of higher-molecular-weight polyacrylic acid reinforces the structural integrity of this system. It also utilizes a novel coating agent containing an advanced monomer to protect the material during the vulnerable early stages of maturation. Additionally, compared to the previous formulation, EF benefits from a highly optimized distribution of filler particle sizes24.

An in vitro study investigated the color stability of Equia Forte and CN in various staining solutions, including coffee and cola, at different time intervals (7th, 14th, 21st, and 28th day). Similarly to our study, the 7- and 28 day coffee groups and the 28 day cola groups, CN showed higher discoloration than Equia Forte25. Although previous studies reported that the color stability of alkasite materials is inferior to that of composite resins and glass hybrids, findings indicate that they exhibit superior color stability compared to conventional and resin-modified GICs26,27. Glass hybrids may be considered superior, as the application of Equia Forte Coat surface varnish provides enhanced resistance to discoloration. These findings suggest that alkasite materials show potential for further clinical improvement. However, future studies focusing on optimizing material composition and surface characteristics are needed to improve long-term color stability.

In the present study, the three-way interaction among material, solution, and time interval did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.104). A plausible explanation for this finding is that the capacity of these materials to absorb pigments from the staining agents may be limited, potentially reaching a saturation point within the observed timeframe. While a recent review suggests that a 7-day immersion period is typically sufficient to exceed clinical perceptibility and acceptability thresholds, the marginal trend observed in our data (p = 0.104, η2 = 0.059) implies a subtle interaction that warrants further investigation28. Future research with larger sample size may be better suited to determine whether this result represents a consistent, statistically significant effect.

The CIEDE2000 formula is preferred as a primary parameter for assessing the clinical significance of color changes. Within the CIEDE2000 system, a color shift becomes perceptible at a threshold of 0.8, whereas the limit for clinical acceptability is reported as 1.819. In this study, all materials exhibited ∆E00 values above both thresholds, and the nanohybrid composite resin had the closest ∆E00 values to these thresholds. Thus, alkasite material and glass hybrid materials may not be ready to replace composite resins for aesthetically demanding restorations.

The color stability of composite resins depends on the complex interaction between their intrinsic structure, water sorption characteristics, and dynamic changes in the oral environment. The organic matrix of composite resins absorbs most of the water within four to seven days of liquid exposure. Since pigments from staining solutions infiltrate the resin by traveling with the absorbed water, most of the material’s discoloration occurs within the first week29. In both time intervals, coffee caused greater discoloration of the tested materials, in accordance with previous studies30. Coffee is one of the most commonly consumed beverages across many societies. Compared to tea or cola, coffee contains high molecular-weight yellow pigments, tannin, and chlorogenic acids31, leading to considerable staining. The discoloration capacity of different beverages depends heavily on their chemical composition and distinct physical characteristics3,32. The color of Coca-Cola Zero is primarily attributed to its caramel content, and its low pH (⁓2.62) promotes the adsorption of staining agents onto dental material surfaces33. Furthermore, the nanohybrid composite resin was light-cured, whereas EF and CN were self-cured. The differing polymerization processes of these materials introduced an additional variable alongside their inherently distinct properties. Moreover, the superior color stability of Estelite Posterior can also be attributed to its advanced photopolymerization mechanism (radical amplified photopolymerization (RAP) technology). This technology promotes a high degree of conversion, which reduces the amount of unreacted residual monomers. A higher polymer network density effectively limits water sorption and subsequent staining, further contributing to the material’s esthetic longevity34.

A primary limitation is the in vitro design, which only partially simulates the dynamic oral environment. For instance, the study did not incorporate mechanical degradation or various polishing protocols, both of which can significantly influence surface topography. Clinically, the concurrent effects of wear and water sorption can sometimes result in smoother material surfaces over time; however, this reduction in roughness does not necessarily mean decreased discoloration. Environmental factors such as thermal stress may additionally induce dimensional expansion, surface degradation, and microcracking. These structural defects facilitate the penetration of extrinsic colorants, regardless of the initial surface polish, demonstrating that the relationship between topography and color stability is highly multifactorial. This study did not incorporate a comparison of different polishing routines which may affect the surface of the materials. Related to this complexity, our analysis of the three-way interaction yielded a marginal trend (p = 0.104, η2 = 0.059), suggesting that this result should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, the experimental design did not account for patient-specific oral hygiene routines or clinical prophylaxis, which significantly mitigate extrinsic staining, nor did it evaluate the recently introduced capsule form of the alkasite material, which may offer improved structural integrity by eliminating hand-mixing variables.
