Association of weight change with all-cause and cause-specific mortality: an age-stratified analysis
Authors: Qing-Mei Huang, Dong Shen, Jian Gao, Huan Chen, Jia-Hao Xie, Hao-Yu Yan, Bin Wu, Zhi-Hao Li, Gang Liu, Chen Mao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe associations of weight change with all-cause and cause-specific mortality stratified by age remains unclear. We evaluated the age-stratified (< 65 vs ≥ 65 years) associations of weight change with all-cause and cause-specific mortality in a large sample of Chinese adults.MethodsOur cohort study included 746,991 adults aged at least 45 years from the Shenzhen Healthcare Big Data Cohort in China. BMI change were categorized as change within 5% (stable), decrease by 5% to 10%, decrease by > 10%, increase by 5% to 10%, and increase by > 10%. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause, non-communicable disease, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and cancer mortality according to BMI change, with adjustment for potential confounders.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 2.2 years (2,330,180 person-years), there were 10,197 deaths. A notable interaction emerged between weight change and age. For participants ≥ 65 years, compared with stable BMI, more than a 10% decrease in BMI was associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.54–1.86), non-communicable disease mortality (HR: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.52–1.84), CVD mortality (HR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.34–1.80), and cancer mortality (HR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.33–1.92). Similar patterns of results for 5% to 10% decrease in BMI were observed. More than a 10% increase in BMI was associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04–1.24), non-communicable disease mortality (HR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.04–1.25), and CVD mortality (HR: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.12–1.44). For participants < 65 years, only more than a 10% decrease in BMI was associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.12–1.77), non-communicable disease mortality (HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.13–1.81), and cancer mortality (HR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.29–2.47).ConclusionsWeight loss and excessive weight gain were associated with increased risks of mortality among older adults, while only excessive weight loss was associated with increased risks of mortality among middle-aged adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03665-9.

Background

There is ample evidence for the U-shaped or J-shaped relationships between body mass index (BMI) and mortality risk [1, 2]. However, most previous studies ignored the dynamic feature of body weight over time. Many adults may experience weight gain due to a sedentary lifestyle and excessive dietary intake [3]. In contrast, a progressive decrease in weight is often observed later in life [4]. Although the association between weight change and mortality has recently attracted much attention, the findings remain inconclusive. A meta-analysis of 26 prospective studies has shown that both weight gain and loss were associated with increased mortality risk among middle-aged and older adults [5]. In contrast, a longitudinal study in Australia indicated that weight loss, but not weight gain, was associated with increased all-cause and cause-specific mortality among older adults [6]. Previous studies predominantly focused on Western populations and were mostly limited to relatively small sample sizes. Meanwhile, these studies differed substantially, involving inconsistent weight indicators (mostly absolute weight) [7, 8] and variability in the categorization of weight change [6, 7]. Moreover, the threshold of weight loss or weight gain at which the risk of mortality begins to increase remains inadequately delineated. Therefore, a study that encompasses a large-scale community-dwelling population and utilizes an appropriate nonlinear methodology is imperative to understand the association between weight change and mortality risk.

Age differences have also been observed in the association between weight change and mortality [7, 8]. A longitudinal study conducted in Korea confirmed the interactive associations between absolute weight loss and mortality among middle-aged and older adults [8]. Using prospective data from two longitudinal cohort studies, Yuan et al. found that absolute weight gain was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality among participants aged < 60 years, while this association was not observed among those aged ≥ 60 years [7]. Thus, our analyses were stratified by age.

In this large-scale prospective cohort study of nearly 0.75 million Chinese adults, we evaluated the associations of percentage change in BMI and absolute weight change with mortality from all causes, non-communicable disease, CVD, and cancer.

Methods

Study population

This prospective cohort study used data from the Shenzhen Healthcare Big Data Cohort (SHBDC) in China. Details of the study design and population have been reported in Additional file 1. In brief, the SHBDC has enrolled participants at any time during 2009–2022 in Shenzhen, China. Follow-up measurements were performed periodically during the annual health examinations once a year. At each visit, data regarding demographics, medical history, diet, and lifestyle were collected using structured questionnaires. The SHBDC includes de-identified data (identifiable data of participants were removed and only unidentifiable data were used) sourced from community health check-ups, including face-to-face interviews and physical examination, and is linked to national death registration records, including both the dates and causes of death. In the present study, participants were followed up until November 7, 2022. The study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (202,378). The participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

In this analysis, we exclusively included 1,341,998 participants aged 45 or over in the baseline survey given that a significant portion of mortality was concentrated among middle-aged and older adults. We excluded participants who lost to follow-up after baseline (n = 573,632), those without complete BMI data from baseline (visit 0) to visit 2 (those who died before the visit 2 [n = 3646] and those with missing data in any round of baseline [n = 14,041] and visit 2 [n = 2204]), and those with extreme BMI values (< 15 or > 50 kg/m2) (n = 1484). As a result, 746,991 participants remained in our cohort for analysis (Additional file 1: Figure S1).

Assessment of weight change

We calculated the percentage change in BMI from the difference in BMI over the first 2 years after enrollment as (BMI at visit 2 − baseline BMI) / baseline BMI, expressed as a percentage. The values for BMI change were divided into five categories: change within 5% (stable BMI), decrease by 5% to 10% (lesser decrease in BMI), decrease by > 10% (excessive decrease in BMI), increase by 5% to 10% (lesser increase in BMI), and increase by > 10% (excessive increase in BMI). We also classified absolute weight change into five groups: change within 2.0 kg (stable weight), decrease by 2.0 kg to 5.0 kg (lesser weight loss), decrease by > 5.0 kg (excessive weight loss), increase by 2.0 kg to 5.0 kg (lesser weight gain), and increase by > 5.0 kg (excessive weight gain).

Ascertainment of deaths

Information about the date and cause of death was obtained from the national death registration information system. The outcomes were all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) code recorded as the underlying cause of death. For this analysis, we measured all-cause mortality and mortality due to non-communicable disease (codes C00-N98), CVD (codes I00-I99), cancer (codes C00-C97), and non-CVD noncancer non-communicable disease (codes D50-G98, J00-N98). We counted the length of time for the survival analysis from visit 2 to the date of death, loss to follow-up, or November 7, 2022, whichever came first.

Covariates

Model adjustment variables included demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and clinical characteristics, which were measured at visit 2. Demographic characteristics assessed at baseline included age, sex (male or female), and education level (college or above, high school, or less than high school). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (no smoking, former smoking, or current smoking), alcohol drinking (yes or no), and regular exercise (yes or no). Clinical characteristics included hypertension (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), digestive diseases (yes or no), CVD (yes or no), cancer (yes or no), kidney diseases (yes or no), and respiratory diseases (yes or no). Personal history of hypertension, diabetes, digestive diseases, CVD, cancer, kidney diseases, and respiratory diseases were measured with the following question: ‘Have you been diagnosed by a doctor with the conditions listed below?’. CVD was defined as heart disease (heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems) or stroke.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of the participants were compared for continuous variables using analysis of variance and for categorical variables using χ2 tests, according to the BMI change categories. To minimize the potential for inferential bias and to maximize the decreased statistical power due to exclusion of participants with missing covariate data, we used the multiple imputation with predictive mean matching to impute missing covariate values [9]. All variables including outcomes were included in the multiple imputation model. The number and proportion of missing variables are shown in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the associations of percentage change in BMI and absolute weight change with all-cause and cause-specific mortality. The hazard ratios (HRs) along with the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. We used the Schoenfeld residual test to evaluate the Cox proportional hazards assumption, and no evidence of a violation of the assumption was observed. Four sets of models were used. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and baseline BMI. Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, education level, smoking, alcohol drinking, regular exercise, and baseline BMI. Model 4 was for the same factors as model 3 and included hypertension, diabetes, digestive diseases, CVD, cancer, kidney diseases, and respiratory diseases. We also used a restricted cubic spline to explore the potential dose–response pattern, with predefined knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentile of BMI change and weight change [10].

A notable interaction emerged between weight change and age, with age groups defined as < 65 years and ≥ 65 years, and this interaction remained significant in the fully adjusted models. In contrast, there was no interaction between weight change and gender. Consequently, the analyses were stratified by age. Several additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our findings. Firstly, sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding participants with a history of CVD, cancer, respiratory disease, kidney disease, and digestive disorders, which could cause potential reverse-causation bias. Secondly, sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding participants with extreme percentage change in BMI (< -15% or > 25%) to exclude any potential effects of the few BMI change outliers. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed to remove participants with missing values for covariates, exclude those who died during the first year of follow-up, restrict the analyses to non-smokers, additionally adjust for year of recruitment, and restrict the analyses to participants aged ≥ 75 years. Additional stratified analyses were performed based on obesity status (non-obesity [BMI < 24 kg/m2] vs obesity [BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2]) in baseline.

All statistical analyses were performed in R software, version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Reported P values were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristic of participants

Of the 746,991 participants (mean [SD] age, 62.1 [9.4] years; 370,397 female [49.6%]), 10,197 deaths were recorded during a median follow-up of 2.2 years (2,330,180 person-years), of which 9858 deaths due to non-communicable diseases, 4823 deaths due to CVD, and 3277 deaths due to cancer (Table 1). The BMI of 561,377 (75.1%) participants remained within 5% (stable) during the first 2 years (baseline to visit 2), 53,807 (7.2%) decrease by 5% to 10%, 21,988 (2.9%) decrease by more than a 10%, 66,240 (8.9%) increase by 5% to 10%, and 43,579 (5.8%) increase by more than a 10%. Compared with participants with stable BMI, those with other BMI change patterns were more likely to be older, female, less educated, and were less likely to be drinkers. All-cause mortality

For participants ≥ 65 years, a 5% to 10% decrease in BMI was associated with a 30% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.19–1.43), whereas the HRs were 1.69 (95% CI: 1.54–1.86) for more than a 10% decrease in BMI and 1.13 (95% CI: 1.04–1.24) for more than a 10% increase in BMI. For participants < 65 years, only more than a 10% decrease in BMI was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.12–1.77).

Non-communicable disease mortality

A 5% to 10% decrease in BMI (HR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.20–1.44), more than a 10% decrease in BMI (HR: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.52–1.84), and more than a 10% increase in BMI (HR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.04–1.25) were associated with higher risk of non-communicable disease mortality among participants ≥ 65 years. More than a 10% decrease in BMI was associated with higher risk of non-communicable disease mortality among participants < 65 years (HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.13–1.81).

CVD mortality

A 5% to 10% decrease in BMI (HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.25–1.63), more than a 10% decrease in BMI (HR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.34–1.80), and more than a 10% increase in BMI (HR: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.12–1.44) were associated with higher risk of CVD mortality among participants ≥ 65 years. BMI change was not associated with CVD mortality among participants < 65 years.

Cancer mortality

A decrease in BMI was associated with higher cancer mortality among participants ≥ 65 years (5% to 10% decrease HR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.02–1.45; more than a 10% decrease HR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.33–1.92). For participants < 65 years, only more than a 10% decrease in BMI was associated with higher cancer mortality (HR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.29–2.47).

Non-CVD noncancer mortality

A more than a 10% decrease in BMI was associated with higher risk of non-CVD noncancer mortality among participants ≥ 65 years (HR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.54–2.29). However, this association was not seen for participants < 65 years (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Sensitivity analyses

Similar associations of BMI change with all-cause and cause-specific mortality were observed when we excluded participants with prevalent CVD, cancer, respiratory disease, kidney disease, and digestive disorders (Additional file 1: Table S5), exclude those with extreme percentage change in BMI (< -15% or > 25%) (Additional file 1: Table S6), excluded those missing covariate data (Additional file 1: Table S7), excluded those who died during the first year of follow-up (Additional file 1: Table S8), restricted the analyses to non-smokers (Additional file 1: Table S9), additionally adjusted for year of recruitment (Additional file 1: Table S10), and restrict the analyses to participants aged ≥ 75 years (Additional file 1: Table S11). Similar associations of BMI change with all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality were observed among participants with or without obesity (Additional file 1: Table S12).

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort study involving 746,991 individuals, we found that weight loss and excessive increase were associated with a higher risk of mortality among older adults. By comparison, excessive decrease in BMI, but not lesser decrease in BMI and increase in BMI, was associated with higher risk of mortality among middle-aged adults.

Our study revealed a statistically significant association of a decrease in BMI and an excessive increase in BMI with increased all-cause mortality in older adults (≥ 65 years). Consistent with our findings, a systematic review indicated that changes in body weight, either through loss or gain, were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality among adults aged ≥ 65 years [11]. Conversely, a cohort study conducted in Australia and the United States has shown that weight loss, but not weight gain, was associated with increased all-cause mortality among individuals aged ≥ 65 years [6]. The inconsistent results in this study may be attributed to the limited sample size, with only 107 and 207 participants in the > 10% weight gain category for men and women, respectively [6]. Consequently, the results were not statistically significant for weight gain groups.

Our investigation revealed that an excessive decrease in BMI, but not a lesser decrease in BMI and an increase in BMI, was associated with increased all-cause mortality among middle-aged adults. Corresponding with our results, two other studies conducted in Western nations similarly observed that weight loss, but not weight gain, was associated with increased all-cause mortality among middle-aged adults [12, 13]. In contrast, several studies with relatively small sample sizes found no significant association between weight loss and mortality, while weight gain was associated with increased mortality [14–16].

Two previous longitudinal studies examined the associations of weight change with CVD and cancer mortality, yielding conflicting results [17, 18]. A study conducted among 1664 adults aged ≥ 65 years revealed that weight loss was associated with higher CVD mortality but not cancer mortality [17]. In contrast, the Guangzhou Biobank study indicated that self-reported weight loss was associated with higher CVD mortality and cancer mortality among older adults, which is consistent with our findings [18]. Nevertheless, our study showed that excessive increase in BMI was also significantly associated with increased CVD mortality among older adults, while BMI change was not associated with CVD mortality among middle-aged adults.

The possible explanation for the inconsistent findings in previous studies, apart from variations in the ages of the study participants [6, 7] and the relatively small sample sizes [14, 15, 17], may be also attributed to variability in the weight indicators [6, 19], different categorizations of weight change [6, 18], use of self-reported weight [18, 20], long duration of changes [20, 21], and the absence of accounting for potential confounding factors, such as baseline BMI [20, 22]. An alternative explanation may be that the results in many cohort studies could be influenced by potential reverse causation bias. For instance, the majority of these studies did not comprehensively exclude individuals with a history of serious illnesses [21, 23, 24]. In our study, we reduce the reverse causation bias by further excluding participants with CVD, cancer, respiratory disease, kidney disease, or digestive disorders. Additionally, we adjusted for baseline BMI to control the impact of confounding, as obese individuals often exhibit weight loss tendencies in contrast to lean individuals, while having a much higher mortality rate.

One of the mechanisms by which a decrease in BMI would lead to a significantly higher risk of mortality is through reducing physical capability, impairing cardiopulmonary performance an increased risk of falls, and disability [25]. However, a lesser decrease in BMI was associated with increased mortality risk among older adults, but not middle-aged adults. We suggest the reason may be the distinct body composition traits specific to these age groups [26]. BMI as an indirect anthropometric measure primarily reflects muscle mass rather than body fat content. Among older adults, a greater proportion of their body mass comprises muscle and bone mass, unlike middle-aged adults, who possess a larger proportion of body mass as fat. Older adults are less effective in regulating weight than younger adults [27]. If weight loss occurring prior to the onset of chronic illness primarily involves muscle and bone mass reduction, it could explain the differences observed between older adults and middle-aged adults. In addition, weight loss in older age is closely linked to the increased risk of unintentional weight loss compared to middle age, potentially serving as an indicator of underlying ill health [28, 29]. We also found that an excessive increase in BMI was associated with increased mortality risk among older adults, but not middle-aged adults. Excessive weight gain may herald an impending epidemic of obesity-related chronic diseases, notably CVD, and associated health outcomes, especially mortality [2, 30]. As noted previously, compared with middle-aged adults, older adults are more susceptible to obesity-related chronic diseases [31].

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, this study is an observational study and cannot evaluate or establish causality. However, our results were robust after excluding participants with prevalent CVD, cancer, respiratory disease, kidney disease, and digestive disorders, and excluding those who died during the first year of follow-up. Secondly, it was not feasible to distinguish intentional from unintentional weight change. Obtaining additional information about the reasons behind weight change would be beneficial for interpreting future study results and devising appropriate intervention strategies. Thirdly, we were unable to assess the associations between changes in other weight-related markers, such as fat mass, and mortality risk. Further studies with repeated data on these markers may lead to a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between weight change and mortality risk. Fourthly, to accurately assess BMI change, relying solely on follow-up intervals may not be sufficient as it might fail to account for short-term weight fluctuations. In addition, it might not be enough to reveal the longitudinal associations of long-term trajectories of BMI with mortality using three-wave data. Further studies with more waves and longer times are warranted to examine the aforementioned issue by using more appropriate analysis methods like a multi-level model. Fifthly, we do not have access to information about mortality due to communicable diseases and coronavirus disease 2019 which could be an essential contributing factor to our results. Finally, because our study sample only came from the elderly, hypertension patients, diabetes patients, pregnant women, and other key groups in China from the SHBDC with their unique characteristics such as low BMI compared to populations of high-income countries or weakness, its generalizability may be limited.

Conclusions

The findings indicated that weight loss and excessive weight gain were associated with increased risks of mortality among older adults, while only excessive weight loss was associated with increased risks of mortality among middle-aged adults. These results offer valuable insights into understanding the complex associations between weight change and mortality, underscoring the significance of weight maintenance, particularly in older adults.
