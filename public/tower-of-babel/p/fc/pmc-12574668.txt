G-quadruplex ligand RHPS4 compromises cellular radioresistance by inhibiting the mitochondrial adaptive response induced by ionizing irradiation
Authors: Sabine Tricot, Capucine Siberchicot, Isaline Bontemps, Chantal Desmaze, Gueorgui Kratassiouk, Marie Vandamme, Guillaume Pinna, Ivan Nemazanyy, J Pablo Radicella, Guy Lenaers, Xavier Renaudin, Jérome Lebeau, Anna Campalans
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA major challenge in radiotherapy is to enhance tumour cell sensitivity to radiation while minimizing damage to healthy tissues. Enhancing the effectiveness of radiotherapy can be achieved by combining irradiation with small radiosensitizing molecules, which promote cancer cell death and allow for reduced radiation doses, thereby limiting harm to surrounding healthy tissues. Since mitochondria play a key role in tumour cell proliferation, they represent a promising therapeutic target for cancer treatment. In this study, we characterized the impact of irradiation on mitochondrial function in radioresistant cancer cells. Our findings revealed several adaptive responses that may contribute to radioresistance, including increased mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content, mitochondrial mass, enhanced activity, and hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network. Notably, the use of mitochondrial-targeted G-quadruplex (G4) ligands, which block mtDNA replication and transcription, disrupted these responses, reducing cancer cell survival in a mtDNA-dependent manner. These results demonstrate that mitochondrial adaptations contribute to radioresistance and highlight mitochondria as a novel target for the radiosensitizing effects of G4 ligands.

Introduction

Radiotherapy, together with surgery and chemotherapy, is one of the major forms of cancer therapy. Its primary goal is to target malignant cells by inducing non-reparable DNA damage, while sparing the healthy tissue surrounding the tumour as much as possible. Despite the efficiency of radiotherapy in cancer treatment, the adaptive response of cancer cells to radiation-induced damage is a major problem at the origin of therapeutic failure and relapse. Another major drawback of radiotherapy is the secondary effects induced by irradiation in healthy tissues. Therefore, intense research has been dedicated to improve the therapeutic index of radiotherapy either by developing new irradiation strategies allowing a more precise targeting of the tumour (proton beam therapy or FLASH-RT) or by developing targeted drugs enhancing cancer cell radiosensitivity and therefore allowing reduction of the dose delivered [1].

Although the effects of ionizing radiation on nuclear DNA have been extensively studied, much less is known concerning its impact on mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and function. Human cells contain multiple copies of mtDNA, a small circular molecule of 16 kb that encodes 13 subunits of the mitochondrial respiratory chain together with rRNAs and tRNAs involved in their translation. mtDNA is therefore required to sustain oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial functions, essential for the proliferation of cancer cells [2–4]. Irradiation has been previously reported to increase the induction of the common deletion in mtDNA [5–7], representing a loss of roughly a third of the mtDNA molecule (4977 bp), containing five tRNA genes and seven genes coding for subunits of the respiratory complexes [8]. Fragmentation of the mitochondrial network has also been observed after irradiation, suggesting the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction a few hours after treatment [9]. Furthermore, mitochondrial adaptation plays a crucial role in the development of chemo- and radioresistance, hindering cancer treatment by potentially leading to tumour relapse and metastasis [10].

Therefore, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction is emerging as a promising strategy in cancer treatment, and many molecules have been developed targeting mitochondrial metabolism, mitochondrial redox signalling pathways, mitochondrial dynamics, and mtDNA [11]. Several compounds have been identified to affect the stability and activity of mtDNA, and it has been shown that elimination of mtDNA results in an inhibition of cancer cell proliferation, therefore limiting tumorigenesis [12]. An example is the recently developed small-molecule inhibitors of the human mitochondrial RNA polymerase POLRMT, that induce a defect in the biogenesis of the oxidative phosphorylation system resulting in the specific inhibition of cancer cell proliferation and tumour growth in a pre-clinical mouse model [13].

One of the features of mtDNA is its high GC content and its GC strand asymmetry, with a 2-fold enrichment of guanines on one strand contributing to the very high density of sequences with the potential to form G-quadruplexes (G4s). G4s are secondary structures formed in guanine-rich regions by stacked sets of four guanines that have been involved in regulation of replication, transcription, and telomere maintenance of nuclear DNA. Evidence indicates that G4 may also play important roles in the regulation of mtDNA replication and transcription [13–15], and—being particularly enriched at breakpoints of pathogenic mtDNA deletions—they are at the origin of mtDNA instability [16–19].

G4 structures have been identified as promising anti-cancer targets, and several hundred G4 ligands with the capacity to recognize and stabilize these structures have been developed. G4 ligands can target multiple genomic regions and affect cancer cell proliferation by inducing telomere instability or stabilizing G4s at promoters, and therefore altering oncogene expression [20]. Radiosensitizing effects of G4 ligands leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis induction have been reported and explained by an increase in telomere dysfunction [21, 22]. A very promising molecule is the G4 ligand RHPS4 that, combined with irradiation, reduces tumour mass and avoids tumour relapse by inducing telomere dysfunction, replicative stress, and accumulation of nuclear DNA damage depending on the cellular context [23]. Interestingly, recent findings have shown that RHPS4 can also localize to mitochondria and affect mitochondrial transcription and replication, resulting in a loss of mtDNA and a depletion of respiratory complexes [16, 17].

In this study we have characterized the effects of ionizing radiation on the mitochondrial network and activity, and shown an adaptive response resulting in an increase in mtDNA copy number, mitochondrial mass, and activity, together with the hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network, potentially contributing to cellular radioresistance. The mitochondrially localized G4 ligand RHPS4 compromises the mitochondrial adaptation induced by irradiation and increases cellular radiosensitivity in a mtDNA-dependent manner, highlighting a novel therapeutic application of this promising small molecule in cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

All cell lines were cultivated in a Heraeus Thermo Scientific BBD6220 incubator at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Absence of mycoplasma was regularly tested with the MycoAlert™ Mycoplasma detection kit from Lonsa Group, Ltd. U2OS cells and tumour breast cancer cell lines T47D were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Rockville, MD, USA), and the Human Mammary Epithelial HMLE cell line was kindly provided by Professor Robert A. Weinberg (Whitehead Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA). All cell lines were grown at 37°C in adherent conditions in cell culture medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma, F7524) and 5% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco 15140122). U2OS cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), low glucose, GlutaMAX™ Supplement, and pyruvate (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). mtDNA depletion was induced by incubating cells in DMEM high glucose containing 100 ng/ml ethidium bromide (EtBr) or 10 μM 2,3 dideoxycitidine (ddC) complemented with 50 μg/ml uridine. For T47D and MCF7, DMEM, high glucose, GlutaMAX™ Supplement, and pyruvate (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) was used. HMLE cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 Nutrient Mixture, GlutaMAX Supplement (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) with 10 ng/ml human epidermal growth factor, 0.5 μg/ml hydrocortisone, and 10 μg/ml insulin (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Merck).

Cell treatments and irradiations

Gamma-irradiations were performed on a GSR D1 irradiator (Gamma Medical Service, Leipzig, Germany) and cells harvested at indicated times. This self-shielded device irradiates with four sources of 137Cs, with a total activity of ∼180.28 TBq (measured in March 2014). The samples were irradiated at different single doses of 2, 4, or 6 Gy, with a dose rate of 1.5 Gy/min, taking the radioactive decrease into account. The samples were irradiated in 6-well plates or Ibidi μ-Slide 4-well plates and recovered at the indicated times after irradiation. Prior to irradiation, dosimetry was performed. A PTW 31010 cylindrical ionization chamber was used as per the recommendation of the AAPM TG-61. This ionizing chamber has a cavity of 0.125 cm3 calibrated in 137Cs air kerma in free air at the PTB reference facility numbers 1904442 and 2108382401. The polarity and the ion recombination were measured for this 137Cs source. Each measurement was corrected by the temperature–pressure correction factor (kTP) to take the variation of temperature and atmospheric pressure into account.

For X-ray irradiation, an ELEKTA VERSA HD irradiator with a 6 MV photon beam was used with a dose rate of 4.5 Gy/min and an irradiation field size of 30 × 30 cm². The irradiated plates were positioned at the treatment isocentre, which corresponds to a source-to-surface distance (SSD) of 1 m. At this point, the dose and dose rate were determined. Before reaching the plates, the beam passed through 8 cm of PMMA (polymethyl methacrylate), followed by an additional 2 cm. The delivered dose was measured prior to each irradiation series using a Farmer ionization chamber (PTW 30013), calibrated in water-equivalent conditions. The chamber was placed in the same position and under the same conditions as the plates to ensure accurate dose and dose rate measurements.

RHPS4 ligand (RHPS 4-methosulfate; Tocris; Ref 5311) was added to the culture medium at the indicated concentrations for 1 h before irradiation and kept in the medium for the whole recovery period before cell harvest or fixation.

Quantitative PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from 1 × 106 cells using the Qiamp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN, #51304) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA concentration was measured with a Nanodrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A 75 ng aliquot of DNA was used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) with the iTaq Universal Probes Supermix (Biorad #1725130) utilizing the Applied Biosystems™ QuantStudio™ three Real-Time PCR System. The TaqMan multiplex real-time PCR approach was used combining mitochondrial probes labelled with 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) with a probe against the nuclear gene RNase P labelled with VIC™ fluorescent dye (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific), allowing normalization for nuclear copy number. Taqman probes against ND1 (Hs02596873_s1), ND4 (Hs02596876_g1), COX2 (Hs02596865_g1), COX3 (Hs02596866_g1), CYTB (Hs02596867_s1), tRNA1 (Hs02596871_s1), tRNA2 (Hs02596871_s1), and RNaseP (4401631) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Each sample was run in triplicate. Mean Ct values were used to calculate the mtDNA copy number relative to untreated samples using the ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Cell pellets (from 3 × 106 cells) were resuspended in 50 μl of Lysis buffer [20 mM Tris, 20 mM NaCl, 0.1% sodium dodecylsulfate (SDS), 1 mM MgCl2, 0.25 U/μl benzonase, and 1× Protease inhibitor cocktail] and sonicated for 10 min (with pulses 30 s on/30 s off). After sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 12 000 g at 4°C for 5 min and the supernatants were recovered. Protein concentration was measured with the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad #500-0006), Laemmli buffer was added at 1× concentration (0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol, 0.0005% Bromophenol blue, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 63 mM Tris pH 6.8) before heating for 5 min at 95°C, and 20 μg of protein extracts were loaded in a mini PROTEAN TGX stain-free gel (Bio-Rad #4568083). Precision plus protein dual colour standards (Bio-Rad #1610374) were used as the molecular weight marker. The transfer onto the nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad #1704157) was performed with the Trans-Blot® Turbo™ Transfer System (Bio-Rad #1704150). The membrane was blocked for 1 h in blocking solution [phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)–0.1% Tween-20 containing 5% milk], and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1). Membranes were washed three times for 5 min with PBS–0.1% Tween-20, and further incubated for 45 min in secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) in PBS–0.1% Tween-20 containing 5% milk. Western blots were imaged with the Li-Cor Odyssey DLx system or with the iBright FL1500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #A44241).

Flow cytometry, acquisition, and analysis

For cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed in 70% cold ethanol and stored at –20°C. Before analysis, cells were washed in PBS and stained in PBS containing 25 μg/ml propidium iodide (Sigma, France) and 50 μg/ml RNase A (Roche Diagnostics, France) for 30 min at 37°C, then analysed by flow cytometry. Mitochondrial mass and mitochondrial membrane potential were analysed using MitoTracker Green (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific M7513) and TMRE (tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester perchlorate; Sigma-Aldrich 87917), respectively. Following soft trypsinization, cells were loaded with 200 nM MitoTracker Green and/or 10 nM TMRE, and incubated for 20 min at 37°C, then immediately analysed by flow cytometry. Cells were analysed on a SORP LSR-II analyser, and data were further processed with FlowJo v10.7.1 (Tree Star).

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) were analysed using CellROX Deep Red (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific C10422). Following trypsinization, cells were loaded with 5 μM CellROX DR in DMEM (without phenol red) and incubated for 1 h at 37°C, then immediately analysed by flow cytometry on a NovoCyte (ACEA Bioscience; configuration: APC). Data were analysed with FlowJo v10.7.1 (Tree Star).

Immunofluorescence, live cell microscopy, and image analysis

For microscopy experiments, cells were grown on Ibidi μ-Slide 4-well plates for 2 days before the experiment. For live imaging, cells were washed twice with warm DMEM and incubated with 100 nM MitoTracker (Green, Red, or Deep Red as indicated), or 10 nM TMRE (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) for 20 min at 37°C followed by washing with complete medium. Image acquisition was performed using a NIKON Ti2/GATACA, W1 spinning disc microscope with a ×60 oil immersion objective (Plan Apo NA 1.4) in a chamber maintained at 37°C with 95% relative humidity, 5% CO2, and 18% oxygen.

For immunofluorescence experiments, cells were washed with DMEM and immediately fixed for 20 min at 37°C with 2% formaldehyde in DMEM solution, rinsed with PBS, and permeabilized at room temperature in PBS–0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. Cells were incubated in blocking solution [PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 1% normal goat serum] at 37°C for 1 h. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) diluted in blocking solution for 1 h at 37°C, washed three times for 5 min in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, and further incubated with secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) diluted 1/1000 in blocking solution for 45 min at 37°C. Cells were washed three times for 5 min in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. Nuclear DNA was counterstained with 1 μg/ml 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI).

Image acquisition was performed using a NIKON Ti2/GATACA, W1 spinning disc microscope with a ×60 oil immersion objective (Plan Apo NA 1.4) or a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with a ×63 oil immersion objective (HC PL Apo NA 1.4). Image analysis was performed with ImageJ/Fiji [24].

Mitochondrial morphology alterations were analysed by applying the Kernel algorithm [25] to binary images. This approach allows measurement of morphological parameters for individual mitochondria, such as a measure of mitochondrial count and area. Quantification of the number of mitochondrial nucleoids was performed with an anti-DNA antibody after subtraction of the nucleus stained with DAPI. Pearson and Mander’s correlation coefficients were calculated with ImageJ using the plugin JACoP [26].

For each parameter, the number of cells analysed from at least two or three independent experiments is indicated in the figure legends.

ATP measurements

U2OS cells were seeded in 6-well plates with or without 0.5 μM RHPS4 and exposed to 6 Gy of irradiation or left untreated. Ninety-six hours after irradiation, cells were rapidly washed twice with cold PBS, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and immediately stored at −80°C. Cell numbers were estimated by counting after trypsinization of parallel plates treated under the same conditions.

For metabolomic analysis, metabolites were extracted using solution composed of 50% methanol, 30% acetonitrile (ACN), and 20% water. The volume of the extraction solution was adjusted to cell number (1 × Exp6 cells for 1 ml). After addition of extraction solution, samples were vortexed for 5 min at 4°C and centrifuged at 16 000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected and stored at −80°C until analysis. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC/MS) analyses were conducted on a QExactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with an Ion Max source and a HESI II probe coupled to a Dionex UltiMate 3000 UPLC system (Thermo). The 5 μl samples were injected onto a ZIC-pHILIC column (150 mm × 2.1 mm; i.d. 5 μm) with a guard column (20 mm × 2.1 mm; i.d. 5 μm) (Millipore) for LC separation. Buffer A was 20 mM ammonium carbonate, 0.1% ammonium hydroxide (pH 9.2), and buffer B was ACN. The chromatographic gradient was run at a flow rate of 0.2 μl/min as follows: 0–20 min, linear gradient from 80% to 20% of buffer B; 20–20.5 min, linear gradient from 20% to 80% of buffer B; 20.5–28 min, 80% buffer B. The mass spectrometer was operated in full scan, polarity switching mode with the spray voltage set to 2.5 kV and the heated capillary held at 320°C. The sheath gas flow was set to 20 units, the auxiliary gas flow to 5 units, and the sweep gas flow to 0 units. The metabolites were detected across a mass range of 75–1000 m/z at a resolution of 35 000 (at 200 m/z) with the automatic gain control target at 106 and the maximum injection time at 250 ms. Lock masses were used to ensure mass accuracy below 5 ppm. Data were acquired with Thermo Xcalibur software (Thermo). The peak area of ATP was determined using Thermo TraceFinder software (Thermo), identified by the exact mass of each singly charged ion and by the known retention time on the HPLC column.

Proliferation/survival assay

For the measurement of the IC50 of RHSP4, U2OS cells were seeded in black-walled clear bottom 96-well culture plates (Greiner 655090) at a density of 800 cells. After 24 h, cells were treated with a range of different concentrations of RHPS4 (0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 3.5, 5, 10, and 20 μM) or with dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) as a vehicle control. Plates were incubated for 96 h at 37°C and fixed with 2% (w/v) paraformaldehyde containing 4 μg/ml Hoechst 33342 (Sigma). Images were captured using an automatized epifluorescence microscope Endevor (ARACELI Bioscience) at ×20 magnification (excitation: 380 ± 20 nm, emission: 445 ± 35 nm). Segmentation and nuclear counting were performed with Clairvoyance software. Nuclear count in wells treated with RHPS4 were normalized to wells treated with equivalent doses of DMSO, and the IC50 was estimated with GraphPad using a dose–response non-linear fit.

When required, U2OS cells were pre-treated with 10 μM ddC for 3 days before the plate seeding, and then throughout the experiment, to induce and maintain mtDNA depletion. Cells—either mtDNA depleted or not—were seeded in black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well culture plates (Costar #3904, Corning). For endpoint measurement 6 days post-seeding in non-irradiated and irradiated conditions, 300 cells were plated per well. For daily measurement of cell growth in non-irradiated and irradiated conditions, 750 and 2000 cells, respectively, were plated per well. Cells were treated with RHPS4 on the same day (for the 6 day experiments) or 1 day post-seeding (for daily growth measurements) at concentrations ranging from 100 nM to 2 μM, or with DMSO as a vehicle control, and irradiated 3 h later. Plates were incubated at 37°C, then fixed at the indicated time points post-RHPS4 treatments with 2% (w/v) paraformaldehyde. Nuclear DNA was stained with 2 μg/ml Hoechst 33342 (Sigma). Images were captured using an automated epifluorescence microscope (Operetta, Perkin Elmer) at ×10 magnification, with nine fields per well in the blue fluorescence channel (excitation: 380 ± 20 nm, emission: 445 ± 35 nm). An automated algorithm, developed in Harmony 3.0 (Perkin Elmer), was used to quantify cell numbers. In brief, nuclear regions of interest (ROIs) were segmented based on Hoechst staining, and the relative cell proliferation/survival in each condition was calculated as the number of cells in experimental conditions relative to the average cell count in the reference control wells.

Clonogenicity assay

U2OS cells were seeded in black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well culture plates (Costar #3904, Corning) at densities of 15 cells/well. Cells were then treated with RHPS4 on the same day at concentrations ranging from 100 nM to 2 μM, or with DMSO as a vehicle control, and irradiated 3 h later. Plates were incubated at 37°C, then fixed at the indicated time points post-RHPS4 treatments with 2% (w/v) paraformaldehyde, and nuclear DNA was stained with 4 μg/ml Hoechst 33342 (Sigma). Images of clones were captured using an automated epifluorescence microscope (Operetta, Perkin Elmer) at ×2 magnification, with one field per well in the blue fluorescence channel (excitation: 380 ± 20 nm, emission: 445 ± 35 nm). An automated algorithm, developed in Harmony 3.0 (Perkin Elmer), was used to quantify clone numbers. In brief, nuclear ROIs were segmented as fluorescent spots based on Hoechst staining. Clones were then defined as clusters of nuclei in close proximity (≤4 pixels) and made up of ≥50 cells. For each condition, the number of clones represents the sum of clones in 10 wells. The number of colonies was divided by the number of seeded cells and normalized on the plating efficiency of the non-irradiated controls. Data from three independent experiments were used for the statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad. The statistical test used and the number of cells and independent experiments analysed are indicated in the figure legends.

Results

Increase in mtDNA, mitochondrial mass, and membrane potential induced by ionizing radiation

In order to evaluate the impact of ionizing radiation on mitochondrial content, mtDNA copy number was monitored by qPCR at different times after a single dose of γ-irradiation at 6 Gy. In order to evaluate the eventual accumulation of molecules with the common deletion in irradiated cells, we selected probes against the mitochondrial genes ND1, ND4, COX2, COX3, and CYTB, and two intergenic regions located throughout the mtDNA molecule, inside or outside the common deletion. The levels of mtDNA were normalized using the amplification of the nuclear gene coding for RNase P. An increase in the levels of mtDNA was clearly observed 96 h after a single irradiation at 6 Gy in surviving cells for all the probes used, indicating an amplification of the entire mtDNA molecule (Fig. 1A).

Mitochondrial nucleoids were visualized by immunofluorescence using an antibody against DNA, revealing a signal specifically located within the mitochondrial network labelled with MitoTracker Red, and co-localizing with TFAM, a core protein of the mitochondrial nucleoid, in both irradiated and non-irradiated cells (Fig. 1B). In agreement with the results from qPCR, an increase in the number and the surface of nucleoids per cell was observed in irradiated cells (Fig. 1C, D).

The increase in mtDNA correlates with an increase in mitochondrial mass and membrane potential measured using MitoTracker Green and TMRE probes, respectively (Fig. 1E–G). Microscopy images confirm the specificity of the mitochondrial staining (Fig. 1E). Even if both signals, MitoTracker Green and TMRE, increased in irradiated cells, the increase was more pronounced for TMRE, resulting in higher TMRE/MitoTracker Green ratios, suggesting a higher mitochondrial activity in irradiated cells (Fig. 1F, G).

Both microscopy and cytometry analyses revealed a gradual increase in cell size induced by ionizing radiation over time (Supplementary Fig. S1A), with a proportion of large cells reaching up to 60% of the population 4 days after irradiation. The irradiation induced an increase in mitochondrial mass and mitochondrial membrane potential (Supplementary Fig. S1C), and a gradual increase in mtDNA copy number (Supplementary Figs S1B and S6E) was specifically observed in these larger cells. Altogether, these long-term effects observed several days after a single dose of irradiation indicated that surviving cells experienced an adaptive mitochondrial response.

Ionizing radiation induces hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network

The effect of ionizing radiation on the mitochondrial network was evaluated 96 h after a single dose of 6 Gy of γ-rays. Quantitative analysis of mitochondrial morphology in both living and fixed cells revealed an increase in mitochondrial footprint and in the number and the surface of branches (contacts between different parts of the network) induced by irradiation, clearly indicating a hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network (Fig. 2A–C; Supplementary Fig. S2). Mitochondrial hyperfusion has been previously described as an adaptation phenomenon allowing cells to survive different kinds of stresses such as nutrient deprivation or oxidative stress [27]. This adaptive response helps in maintaining cellular energy balance and protects cells against apoptosis, and therefore could contribute to cellular radioresistance.

Mitochondrial dynamics, encompassing the processes of fusion and fission, are critical for maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis. Fusion facilitates the mixing of mitochondrial contents to mitigate damage, whereas fission enables mitochondrial division and the removal of impaired mitochondria via mitophagy. The equilibrium between these opposing processes preserves mitochondrial morphology and function, which are essential for cellular energy production and viability [28, 29]. Given that mitochondrial hyperfusion is typically driven by an imbalance between fusion and fission [30], we assessed the expression levels and mitochondrial localization of key proteins regulating mitochondrial morphology to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying irradiation-induced mitochondrial hyperfusion. While no difference was observed after irradiation in the global amounts of DRP1 or OPA1, proteins involved in mitochondrial fission or fusion, respectively (Fig. 2D), a clear decrease in the mitochondrial localization of DRP1 was observed in irradiated cells, indicating that hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network was a consequence of a reduction in the levels of fission (Fig. 2E–H). The specificity of the signal obtained with antibodies against DRP1 and OPA1 was evaluated by western blot and immunofluorescence in cells transfected with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) against DRP1 or OPA1 (Supplementary Fig. S3A–D). No difference was observed in the localization of proteins involved in mitochondrial fusion such as OPA1, MFN1, or MNF2 (Supplementary Fig. S3E).

To evaluate if the hyperfused mitochondrial network was electrically continuous, we performed microirradiation using a focalized laser in order to induce depolarization of a small part of the network and evaluated the spread of depolarization through the mitochondrial network [31]. To do so, mitochondria were stained with both MitoTracker Green and TMRE, and loss of TMRE was monitored both in the microirradiated region and in the entire mitochondrial network. We observed a fast depolarization of mitochondria within the microirradiated region as well as in areas of the network located at a distance of several micrometres from it, indicating connected mitochondrial branches (Fig. 2I; Supplementary Fig S4; Supplementary Movie S1). Interestingly, the mitochondrial membrane potential was rapidly recovered in the affected regions, indicating the capacity of the mitochondrial network to compensate for localized damage.

Altogether, our results show a clear alteration of the mitochondrial network induced by ionizing radiation, with an increase in mtDNA levels, mitochondrial mass, membrane potential, and hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network. All these adaptation responses, known to enhance cellular fitness and reduce apoptosis, could contribute to cellular radioresistance. Therefore, blocking this adaptive response by using small molecules targeting mitochondrial stability could have a major therapeutic potential for cancer treatment.

G4 ligand RHPS4 localizes to mitochondria and blocks the irradiation-induced mitochondrial adaptation

To assess if G4 ligands that stabilize mitochondrial G4s can counteract mitochondrial adaptation to ionizing radiation, we first evaluated in our model the effects of RHPS4, a G4 ligand that enters mitochondria and disrupts mtDNA transcription and replication, causing mitochondrial dysfunction [16, 17]. RHPS4 rapidly accumulated in the mitochondria of U2OS cells, as observed by live time imaging in cells in which the mitochondrial network was stained with MitoTracker Deep Red (Fig. 3A). Exposure of cells to 10 μM RHPS4 for 24 h results in an alteration of mitochondrial morphology, with clustering of the mitochondria and nucleoids around the nucleus (Fig. 3B–D). In order to evaluate if the effects of RHPS4 on mitochondrial morphology were dependent on the presence of mtDNA, we generated cells depleted of mtDNA by adding EtBr to the culture medium, inhibiting the replication of mtDNA. Loss of mtDNA was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. 3C) and by immunofluorescence using an antibody against DNA (Fig. 3B). No effect of RHPS4 on mitochondrial clustering was observed in cells depleted for mtDNA (Fig. 3B, D), clearly indicating that RHPS4 induces mitochondrial dysfunction by directly targeting mtDNA.

The targeting of RHPS4 to mitochondria and its clear impact on mtDNA replication/loss and mitochondrial morphology prompted us to evaluate if the addition of RHPS4 to the culture medium could limit the mitochondrial adaptive response observed 96 h after exposure to ionizing radiation. To evaluate the long-term effects of RHPS4, the molecule was added to the medium at lower concentrations of 2 and 0.5 μM immediately before irradiation and kept during the whole recovery period. The use of lower concentrations of RHPS4 allowed toxicity to be reduced and the nuclear DNA damage previously observed after exposure to 10 μM of RHPS4 to be limited [16]. In agreement with previous observations [16], RHPS4 was exclusively localized in the mitochondria of living cells and the irradiation did not modify the subcellular localization of the ligand that remains at undetectable levels in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S5A). A nuclear localization of RHPS4 was only observed in fixed cells, suggesting that nuclear localization is mostly due to the procedure of cell fixation resulting in a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Supplementary Fig. S5B). The increase in mtDNA copy number induced by irradiation was clearly hampered in the presence of 0.5 or 2 μM RHPS4, as measured by both qPCR and immunofluorescence analyses (Fig. 4A–C). The same response was observed after irradiation with X-rays and in other tumoral cell lines such as T47D and HMLE (Supplementary Fig. S6A–C). Cytometry analysis showed that the addition of RHPS4 does not block the increase in cell size induced by irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S6D). However, the increase in mtDNA specifically observed in the cells that became bigger after irradiation is completely blocked in the presence of RHPS4 (Supplementary Fig. S6E), being not significantly different from the levels measured in non-irradiated cells.

The increase of mitochondrial membrane potential measured by the TMRE probe observed in irradiated cells was also significantly reduced in cells irradiated in the presence of RHPS4 (Fig. 4D, E). While it was possible to discriminate TMRE signal from RHPS4 fluorescence by cytometry, the overlap of the fluorescence profiles between the two molecules did not allow microscopy analysis. To circumvent this limitation, MitoTracker Deep Red, a dye accumulating in active mitochondria in a potential-dependent manner, was used. The loss of mitochondrial membrane potential induced by RHPS4 was also observed by microscopy in cells stained simultaneously with MitoTracker Deep Red and an antibody against MPPB, a subunit of the mitochondrial processing peptidase, allowing visualization of the mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 4F, G). While a perfect correlation was measured between both mitochondrial markers in the absence of RHPS4, a loss of MitoTracker Deep Red staining was observed in cells exposed to RHPS4, indicating a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential induced by the ligand in both irradiated and non-irradiated cells (Fig. 4F, G).

In the same manner, hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network induced by irradiation was also affected in the presence of the ligand, with a clear reduction in the area and number of branches observed in cells exposed to irradiation in the presence of RHPS4 (Fig. 4H, I). The measurement of the mitochondrial morphology parameters form factor and aspect ratio, allowing quantitative measurement of the mitochondrial length and degree of branching, confirmed the impact of RHPS4 on the mitochondrial network of irradiated cells (Supplementary Fig. S7). In agreement with previous reports indicating a good correlation between hyperfusion of the mitochondrial network and ATP production, we could measure an increase in ATP levels in irradiated cells that was impaired in the presence of RHPS4 (Fig. 4J).

In order to evaluate if the profound alteration of mitochondrial function induced by RHPS4 impacted the levels of mitochondrial ROS induced by ionizing radiation, we performed cell cytometry analysis using the probe CellROX DeepRed that is specifically localized in the mitochondria. This approach allowed us to measure a higher level of ROS in irradiated cells, but no significant difference was observed in the presence of RHPS4 (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Altogether, our results demonstrate that in the presence of RHPS4, the mitochondrial adaptive response triggered in cells surviving irradiation is impaired. We observed a strong correlation between increased mtDNA content, mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondrial hyperfusion, and ATP levels, indicating enhanced mitochondrial activity 4 days post-irradiation. RHPS4, by targeting mtDNA transcription and replication, prevents these radiation-induced changes.

RHPS4 increases radiosensitivity in a mtDNA-dependent manner

Treatment of U2OS cells at increasing concentrations of RHPS4 allowed us to calculate a IC50 of 1.74 μM (Supplementary Fig. S9A). In agreement with previous findings indicating a radiosensitizing effect of RHPS4, we could observe a reduction in cellular proliferation (Fig. 5A) and clonogenicity (Fig. 5C, D) of cells irradiated in the presence of 0.5–2 μM RHPS4. While no impact of RHPS4 on the cell cycle was observed in non-irradiated cells, the presence of 0.5 μM RHPS4 induced a prolonged cell cycle arrest in G2/M induced by irradiation (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Fig. S9B). The effect of the RHPS4 ligand on telomere maintenance has been proposed to be responsible for the radiosensitizing effect of the molecule. However, we observed a reduction in cellular proliferation and viability after irradiation in the presence of very low concentrations of the ligand that have previously been shown not to induce a detectable amount of nuclear DNA damage, as measured by γ-H2AX levels, or telomeric shortening [16]. In agreement with these previous observations, we did not observe induction of double-strand breaks (DSBs) in non-irradiated cells exposed to 0.5 μM RHPS4 or any significant difference in the levels of γ-H2AX induced by irradiation in the presence or absence of RHPS4. As expected, a maximum level of DSBs was detected 1 h after irradiation and these were gradually repaired with the same kinetics in the presence or absence of the G4 ligand, being undetectable 24 h after irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S10). Therefore, in order to evaluate if the direct targeting of mtDNA by RHPS4 could be responsible for the increased sensitivity to irradiation, we performed a proliferation assay comparing cells depleted of mtDNA with normal cells. The depletion of mtDNA was induced by addition of ddC to the culture medium [32]. In our experimental conditions, a strong depletion of mtDNA levels was achieved after 5 days of exposure to ddC as measured both by immunofluorescence and by qPCR (Fig. 5E–G). The presence of RHPS4 in the mitochondria in cells depleted or not of mtDNA was confirmed by live imaging, showing that the import of the molecule was not impaired in the absence of mtDNA. However, after a washing step, the ligand was rapidly released from the mitochondria of ddC-treated cells, confirming that mtDNA is a direct target of RHPS4 (Supplementary Fig. S5C). While a reduction in cell survival was observed in wild-type cells irradiated in the presence of increasing amounts of RHPS4, the effect of the ligand was significantly reduced in cells depleted in mtDNA (Fig. 5G), indicating that targeting the stability of mtDNA by RHPS4 contributes to cellular radiosensitivity.

Discussion

Our results show that cells surviving ionizing radiation develop a mitochondrial adaptation resulting in an increase in mtDNA, mitochondrial mass, and membrane potential (Fig. 1). An increase in mtDNA copy number and mitochondrial biogenesis is considered to be a compensatory mechanism aimed at sustaining oxidative phosphorylation activity in order to overcome the eventual defects in bioenergetics induced by mtDNA damage to some molecules [33]. Indeed, several studies have demonstrated that increasing mtDNA copy number by different genetic tools boosts mitochondrial biogenesis, rescues mitochondrial dysfunction, and may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of mitochondrial diseases [34].

Previous reports have shown increased mtDNA content along with enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential, respiration, and ATP production in cells exposed to a single 10 Gy dose of X-rays [35, 36]. The authors hypothesized that these mitochondrial changes were a consequence of irradiation-induced accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase. However, our results demonstrate that while irradiation combined with RHPS4 treatment causes an even stronger G2/M arrest, it simultaneously impairs the mitochondrial adaptive response (Figs 4 and 5), indicating that these processes are uncoupled and that additional factors must be involved.

Our study shows a strong correlation between the irradiation-induced increase in mtDNA, mitochondrial mass and activity, and mitochondrial hyperfusion observed 96 h after a single dose of irradiation of 6 Gy. Mitochondrial hyperfusion is a cellular response to various stress conditions, characterized by the extensive merging of mitochondria into elongated and interconnected networks. So-called stress-induced mitochondrial hyperfusion (SIMH) was described for the first time in 2009 as an adaptive pro-survival response of cells against stress [27] and has been observed after UV exposure, cycloheximide and actinomycin D treatments, or serum deprivation. The results presented in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4 show that the hyperfused mitochondrial network is electrically continuous. Therefore, mitochondrial damage induced in a small part of the network by laser microirradiation rapidly spreads, affecting other regions that are a several micrometres away. This electrical continuity has been shown to enhance the overall bioenergetic efficiency of the mitochondria, allowing the cell to optimize ATP production under stress conditions [31, 37, 38]. The interconnected network can therefore protect against localized damage, as healthy mitochondria can support and compensate for those which are impaired within the network, as can be observed by the fast recovery of TMRE in regions affected by microirradiation-induced damage (Fig. 3; Supplementray Fig. S4). Although, to our knowledge, no previous reports have shown ionizing radiation-induced SIMH, a slight increase in mitochondrial elongation, together with an increase in respiration, following ionizing irradition has been recently reported in irradiated mesenchymal stem cells [39]. The impact of irradiation on mitochondrial morphology remains controversial, with some studies reporting irradiation-induced mitochondrial fragmentation, while others describe elongation driven by increased fusion [36, 39, 40]. These divergent effects probably depend on radiation dose, time post-irradiation, and cell type, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Depending on the nature of the stressor, different molecular mechanisms that promote fusion or inhibit fission have been described to trigger mitochondrial hyperfusion [30]. This hyperfusion results from an imbalance between fusion and fission events, and is strongly diminished in cells lacking MFN1/2 and OPA1, which are essential for outer and inner mitochondrial membrane fusion, respectively [27, 37]. In our study, we did not observe changes in the subcellular localization of MFN1/2 or OPA1 (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S3). However, a decrease in mitochondrially localized DRP1, a key mediator of mitochondrial fission, was evident in cells 96 h after irradiation (Fig. 2), suggesting that reduced fission contributes to the hyperfusion observed in surviving cells. Interestingly, previous reports have shown that increased DRP1 recruitment to mitochondria occurs at earlier time points following irradiation, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation. DRP1 deficiency or inhibition has been shown to reduce fragmentation and has been associated with enhanced radiosensitivity, probably due to decreased mitotic catastrophe, reduced cytochrome c release, and cell death [9, 41]. This suggests that the temporal regulation of DRP1 activity and localization may play a critical role in the adaptive response of cells that survive irradiation, potentially contributing to radioresistance. DRP1 recruitment to mitochondria is tightly regulated by post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation of Ser637 that has been shown to be required for mitochondrial elongation after starvation [42]. In addition, interactions with adaptor proteins, such as MID49, MID51, MFF, and FIS1, facilitate docking of DRP1 to the outer mitochondrial membrane and promote fission [43, 44]. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying impaired mitochondrial DRP1 recruitment in irradiated, surviving cells.

The adaptive mitochondrial response could help cells to better cope with and recover from stressful conditions, supporting overall cellular survival and radioresistance. Recent findings highlight the central role of mitochondrial adaptation in chemoresistance of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). An increase in mitochondrial mass and respiratory activity has been observed in cells developing drug resistance, and several molecules targeting mitochondrial metabolism or dynamics have been shown to enhance cellular sensitivity to different chemotherapeutical agents and open up new therapeutical avenues for AML [45, 46].

Mitochondria are essential for the proliferation of cancer cells, not only because of their main role in ATP production but also because of their essential roles in cellular signalling, apoptosis, and pyrimidine synthesis needed for DNA replication. Therefore, many molecules inducing a mitochondrial dysfunction are gaining interest for their potential use in cancer treatment. The term MITOCANS, for MITOchondria and CANcer, has even been proposed for anti-cancer drugs that induce mitochondrial dysfunction by targeting different mitochondrial pathways [47]. As mtDNA is essential for oxidative phosphorylation biogenesis, several molecules targeting mtDNA stability, both indirectly by inducing mtDNA damage and dysfunction and directly by binding or cleaving mtDNA, are emerging as promising tools for cancer treatment [48, 49]. Among the proteins regulating mtDNA transcription and replication, TFAM and POLRMT play an essential role and have been identified as potential targets for anti-cancer therapy. The anti-tumour efficacy of POLRMT inhibitors has been confirmed in ovarian, lung, and cervical cancer [50]. Considering the recent discovery of a direct link between high mtDNA levels and accelerated lung cancer progression, along with the fact that an impaired increase in mtDNA copy number hinders tumour growth, targeting mtDNA offers an interesting perspective for cancer treatment [51].

In this context, targeting mitochondrial G4 by using mitochondrial localized G4 ligands with the capacity to block transcription and replication of mtDNA [16] represents a promising therapeutical strategy. Up to now, radiosensitizing effects of G4 ligands, including RHPS4, have been explained by their effect on telomere stability, replicative stress, and cell cycle arrest [22, 23]. Interestingly, a systematic comparison of the effects of RHPS4 at different concentrations has shown that while telomeric dysfunction and accumulation of nuclear DSBs are observed at higher concentrations of the ligand (10 μM), the incubation of cells with a low dose (2 μM) interferes with mtDNA copy number but does not induce direct detectable nuclear DNA damage [16]. In agreement with previous findings, we have observed that RHPS4 preferentially localizes to mitochondria in living cells and affects mtDNA content and mitochondrial morphology by directly targeting mtDNA without inducing nuclear DNA damage (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Figs S5 and S10 [16]). Therefore, an alternative, though not exclusive, explanation for the telomere instability induced by RHPS4 could involve an indirect effect resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction. Interestingly, oxidative stress has been shown to accelerate telomere loss [52], and a direct and tight link between mitochondrial dysfunction and telomere instability has been established. The use of a chemoptogenetic tool allowing the induction of singlet oxygen specifically in the mitochondrial matrix has recently been shown to induce a secondary wave of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, and rapid telomere dysfunction as characterized by 53BP1-positive telomere-induced DNA damage foci (TIFs), telomere fragility, and telomere loss in the apparent absence of general nuclear DNA strand breaks [53]. These findings could explain the telomere instability observed in cells exposed to low concentrations of RHPS4 (0.2, 0.5, or 1 μM) during 5 days [23], that could indeed be indirectly induced by a mitochondrial dysfunction. Although we cannot exclude a direct effect of RHPS4 on nuclear DNA, these results together with our findings suggest that mtDNA is a major target of RHPS4.

Similar results have been obtained with G4 ligands derived from BMVC that accumulate in mitochondria of cancer cells and can induce cancer cell death without damaging normal cells. Interestingly, and in agreement with our observations showing that the radiosensitizing effects of RHPS4 are dependent on mtDNA, no effect of the ligand on cell death was observed in cells depleted of mtDNA, indicating that mtDNA is the major target of the G4 ligand BMVC [54]. The far-red carbazole–benzoindolium fluorescent ligand BKN-1 has been recently shown to target mitochondrial G4s. This G4 ligand is imported in mitochondria where it interacts with mitochondrial DNA and induces mitochondrial dysfunction as monitored by the release of cytochrome c and loss of mtDNA and mitochondrial membrane potential. The mitochondrial dysfunction was considered to be a pivotal event inducing the apoptosis of cancer cells both in vitro and in a tumour mouse model [55]. Interestingly, the G4-binding platinum complex Pt-ttpy has also been shown to induce loss of mtDNA and mitochondrial dysfunction, but its effects seem to be partially indirect, as a consequence of its impact on nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial proteins [56]. Considering that mitochondrial function is ensured by a tight coordination between nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, a careful characterization of the cellular effects of G4 ligands is essential to better understand their molecular mechanism of action and characterize their direct and indirect targets.

Our results show that the G4 ligand RHPS4, with a primary mitochondrial localization, specifically targets mtDNA and induces mtDNA loss and mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in a blockage of the adaptive mitochondrial response induced by ionizing irradiation and therefore increasing radiosensitivity (Fig. 6). These results are not only a major fundamental advance concerning the understanding of radiation effects on the mitochondrial network but also offer new therapeutical possibilities for the use of this molecule and other mitochondrial imported G4 ligands in cancer treatment.

Radiosensitizing effects of RHPS4 mediated by mitochondrial targeting. RHPS4 interferes with the mitochondrial adaptation induced by ionizing radiation by blocking the increases in mtDNA, activity, and the hyperfusion of the network, therefore reducing cellular radioresistance.
