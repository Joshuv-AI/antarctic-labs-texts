Dose-response relationship of physical activity and sedentary time with mortality in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: an analysis of UK biobank accelerometer cohort
Authors: Weijiao Zhou, Philip T. Veliz, Junlan Pu, Wei Luo, Shaomei Shang, Janet L. Larson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COPD guidelines recommend engaging in regular physical activity and reducing sedentary time (ST), but little is known about the optimal or minimal dose of physical activity and ST. This study aimed to quantify the prospective dose-response relationships between daily time spent in moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA), light physical activity (LPA), ST and mortality, and examine the theoretical consequences of replacing ST with equal time of MVPA or LPA.MethodsA population-based cohort study of 1,551 individuals with COPD enrolled in the UK Biobank. MVPA, LPA, ST were measured with the wrist-worn Axivity AX3 accelerometer. All-cause mortality was obtained through the linkage to death registries. Restricted cubic splines were used to assess the dose response associations of MVPA, LPA, ST and all-cause mortality. Isotemporal substitution models were used to estimate the theoretical effect of replacing ST with MVPA or LPA.Results54% were male, and the mean (SD) age was 66.31 (6.52) years. Over a mean (SD) follow-up of 7.44 (1.67) years, 244 (15.7%) died. We observed a significant L-shaped association between MVPA and all-cause mortality, with an optimal amount at 60 min/day (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.18–0.41). For LPA, we observed a significant U-shaped association, with an optimal amount at 5.2 h/day (HR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.10–0.25). The threshold for ST was 12.43 h/day, above which a significant increase in mortality was observed. Replacing 30 min/day of ST was associated with 34% decreased risk in mortality for MVPA (HR = 0.66, 95%CI: 0.55–0.81, P < .001) and 10% lower mortality for LPA (HR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94, P < .001).ConclusionsThe findings of this study suggest non-linear associations of MVPA, LPA, ST and all-cause mortality. Replacing ST with either MVPA or LPA is associated with decreased risk of mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03969-3.

Physical activity is a key element in promoting health outcomes among people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [1, 2]. Existing evidence demonstrates the benefits of physical activity on the risk of mortality and acute COPD exacerbation [3–5]. The COPD guidelines recommend engaging in regular/daily physical activity and reducing ST, but the optimal or minimal dose has not been identified [6, 7]. Retrospective studies of patients with COPD established thresholds of steps to predict all-cause mortality as approximately 4,000 steps per day [8, 9]. Step-based recommedations are easily measurable and integrated into daily life. However, step counts may not capture the intensities of activities or account for differences in fitness levels. A robust body of evidence is needed to inform the intensity-based recommendations.

For general adults, the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines provide specific intensity-based recommendations of at least 150–300 min of moderate physical activity per week [10]. However, this recommendation is not applicable for adults with COPD; adults with chronic conditions are advised to consult with a physical activity specialist or healthcare professional for the type and amounts of activity [10]. The current study focused on the intensity-based metric to inform future physical activity guidelines for people with COPD, by quantifying the dose-response relationships of time spent in different intensities of physical activity and mortality.

Examining the shape of dose-response curves is crucial to identifying the minimal or optimal amount of physical activity, and maximum amount of ST for mortality reduction. However, only a few studies have explored the dose-response associations in this population [11], and the evidence is limited by self-reported physical activity, which is subject to reporting bias and does not capture LPA well. An understanding of the effects of LPA is particularly important for people with COPD. Due to symptom burden and functional limitations (i.e., dyspnea, fatigue, skeletal muscle dysfunction) [5, 12], LPA may be more feasible and achievable than MVPA in this population. Although it is recommended that ST be replaced with PA [10], there is limited evidence regarding the impact of substituting ST with MVPA or LPA.

We examined the prospective dose-response relationships between daily time spent in MVPA, LPA, ST and all-cause mortality among people with COPD, using accelerometer data from the UK Biobank. In addition, we examined the theoretical consequences of replacing ST with MVPA or LPA on all-cause mortality using isotemporal substitution models (ISM).

Methods

Study design and participants

We used data from the UK Biobank, a prospective cohort of over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years in England, Scotland, and Wales [13]. Baseline assessments of 500,000 participants were undertaken in 22 centers between 2006 and 2010, followed by a first repeat assessment of 20,000 participants between 2012 and 2013. Between 2013 and 2015, 236,519 participants were invited to wear an accelerometer under free-living conditions, of whom 106,053 agreed to wear the monitor and 103,579 returned data (Supplemental Fig. 1) [14]. Hospital admission records and death registry data were linked to the UK Biobank to provide information on disease diagnosis and death. This study was approved by the UK’s National Health Service National Research Ethics Service (Ethics Committee reference number: 11/NW/0382).

We included participants who had a COPD diagnosis before wearing the accelerometer and who had valid accelerometer data [14] (n = 1,628), and excluded those with missing values on covariates (n = 77). A total of 1,551 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Measures

Raw accelerometer-measured physical activity was collected using a wrist-worn tri-axial accelerometer (Axivity AX3, designed by Open Lab, Newcastle, UK). Participants were instructed to wear it for seven continuous days (during awake and sleep time) on their dominant wrist and carry on with their normal activities. Time spent in MVPA, LPA and sedentary behaviors were derived from raw accelerometer data using a previously published machine learning algorithm developed and validated for use with the UK Biobank [15]. Briefly, this algorithm was developed from an accelerometer validation study of 152 free-living adults (aged 18–91 years) who wore the AX3 accelerometer and a wearable camera and kept a time use diary [15]. Using camera images and time-use diaries, researcher annotated accelerometer data based on the Compendium of Physical Activities, labeling specific activities with their metabolic equivalent of task (METs) [16]. Accelerometer data were then defined as sleep, sedentary behavior, LPA and MVPA. Using the labelled data from this accelerometer validation study, machine-learning models were trained to classify behaviors in the UK Biobank accelerometer data. Performance of this algorithm has been supported with a mean accuracy of 88% (95% CI, 87% to 89%) and Cohen’s kappa of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.79 to 0.82) [15]. This behavior classification method has been used in other UK Biobank studies [17–19].

The COPD diagnosis and date of diagnosis were identified through the UK Biobank algorithms, via hospital admission records and self-reported medical conditions (see code list in Supplemental Table 1). Date of death was obtained through the linkage to death registries. Participants were followed up until the date of death, or the date of censoring (30 November 2022), whichever came first.

Potential covariates were collected at baseline and first repeat assessment. Socio-demographic characteristics included age at accelerometer measurement (continuous in years), sex (male, female), race and ethnicity (white, non-white), educational attainment (college/university, A level/national vocational qualification [NVQ] or equivalent, and O level/certificate of secondary education [CSE]/none), and Townsend Deprivation Index (a greater TDI indicating a lower overall socioeconomic status). Additional confounders included non-physical activity lifestyle factors: smoking status (never, former, current); alcohol frequency (never/occasionally, 1–3 times/week, ≥ 3 times/week), and healthy diet adherence (yes, no; Supplemental Table 2) and body mass index (BMI, underweight, normal, overweight, or obese). Self-reported chronic conditions included coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, asthma, and cancer. Years between initial COPD diagnosis and accelerometer measurement were also collected as a covariate.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as percentages for categorical variables, means (standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed variables, and medians (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. We compared the characteristics of participants between those deceased and not deceased using χ2 test for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U test or t test for continuous variables. We conducted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the hazard ratio (HRs) and 95% CI for all-cause mortality according to physical activity exposures (MVPA, LPA, ST). Models were adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, Townsend Deprivation Index, smoking status, alcohol frequency, healthy diet adherence, body mass index, pre-existing chronic diseases (coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, asthma, and cancer) and years between initial COPD diagnosis and accelerometer measurement. We further adjusted for either two of MPVA, LPA, and ST (two-factor model) and all three activity factors (partition model) (See Supplemental Methods). The proportional hazards assumption was checked using Schoenfeld residuals and no violation was found (P >.05 for all models).

We used restricted cubic splines to assess the dose response associations of MVPA, LPA, ST and all-cause mortality, allowing for potential non-linearity. Knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentile. The reference value was set at the lowest exposure level. Non-linearity was assessed with Wald tests. Based on the shape of dose-response associations, we estimated the threshold amount of ST (i.e., the exposure level above which a mortality risk increase was observed) and the optimal amount of MVPA or LPA (i.e., the exposure value at which the maximum significant risk reduction was observed). Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex and age at physical activity measurement (45–64 years, 65 years or older).

The sleep time, ST, LPA and MVPA are collectively known as the 24-hour movement behaviors. The isotemporal substitution models (ISM) is an analytic approach to evaluate the hypothetical effects of reallocating time spent on one activity to another across different movement behaviors [20]. We used isotemporal substitution models (ISM) to estimate the theoretical effect of replacing 15 min/day or 30 min/day of ST with an equal time spent in MVPA or LPA, adjusting for potential confounders.

We conducted sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of our findings. To minimize the possibility of reverse causality, we did a landmark analysis excluding deaths occurring within the two years after accelerometer measurement (n = 1,503). Due to the large volume of missing spirometry data, we did not adjust for baseline lung function in the main analysis. To further investigate the confounding by baseline lung function, in another sensitivity analysis, we repeated the main analyses with further adjustment of FEV1/FVC ratio (n = 1,047). We also conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding COPD patients with concomitant asthma (n = 929).

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata/SE 17.0 (StataCorp) and R Software 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing); a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. The present analysis was performed from May 31 to October 15, 2024.

Results

A total of 1,551 individuals with COPD were included in the final analytic sample (mean age, 66.31 [SD 6.52] years; male, 54.03%). The characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. 46% of participants met the guideline of 150 min of MPVA per week. The median MVPA was 18.06 (IQR 35.38) min/day, mean LPA was 4.62 (SD 1.73) hours/day, and mean sedentary time was 9.99 (SD 2.05) hours/day. There were 244 (15.7%) cases of death during the mean follow-up time of 7.44 (SD 1.67) years.

Deceased participants were more likely to be older, male, lower educated, at lower socio-economic status, previous/current smokers, never/occasionally drinking, not adherent to a healthy diet, and were more likely to report coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and cancer (P <.05).

In adjusted single-factor models, we found that 15–30 min/day of MVPA was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality compared to < 15 min/day (HR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.31–0.67); this association remained significant when further adjusted for other activity factors, indicating this association was statistically independent of LPA and ST (Table 2). Compared to LPA < 3 h/day, 3–5 h/day of LPA was associated with lower risk of mortality (HR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.36–0.65); this association was independent of MVPA and ST. We found ST ≥ 12 h/day was associated with higher all-cause mortality, compared to ST < 8 h/day (HR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.34–3.71); this association was independent of MVPA, but it was not significant when adjusted for LPA.

The dose-response associations of PA and mortality are shown in Fig. 1. We observed a significant L-shaped association between MVPA and all-cause mortality after adjusting for potential confounders. The optimal amount of MVPA was at 60 min/day (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.18–0.41) for reducing mortality, and no additional benefits were found when exceeding this amount; For LPA, we observed a significant U-shaped non-linear association after adjusting for potential confounders, with an optimal amount (i.e., the nadir of the curve) at 5.2 h/day (HR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.10–0.25); A threshold effect was found in the relationship between ST and all-cause mortality after controlling for potential confounders. The threshold of ST was 8.96 h/day, above which a mortality risk increase was observed, but the increased mortality risk was not statistically significant until ST reached 12.43 h/day.

In subgroup analysis (Supplemental Figs. 2 and 3), we found that for males, the optimal amount of MVPA and LPA was at 62.1 min/day (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.17–0.45) and 4.92 h/day (HR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.24), respectively; the threshold of ST was 10.76 h/day. For females, the non-linearity associations of MPVA and ST with mortality was not supported (nonlinearity for MVPA, P =.625; nonlinearity for ST, P =.758); the associations of MVPA and ST with mortality may be linear; the optimal amount of LPA was at 5.82 h/day (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.12–0.60). For people with COPD aged 65 years or older, the optimal amount of MVPA and LPA was at 58.59 min/day (HR = 0.26, 95%CI: 0.16–0.40) and at 5.35 h/day (HR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.24) respectively. For those aged 45–64 years, the optimal amount of MVPA was below 110 min/day (HR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.03–1.00.03.00) and the optimal amount of LPA was at 5.24 h/day (HR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.06–0.37).

Isotemporal substitution analyses with fully adjusted models showed a significant theoretical effect of replacing ST with equivalent MVPA or LPA on all-cause mortality. Substituting 15 min/day of ST with MVPA or LPA was associated with a risk reduction of 19% (HR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.74–0.90, P <.001) and 5% (HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97, P <.001), respectively. Replacing 30 min/day of ST was associated with 34% decreased risk of mortality for MVPA (HR = 0.66, 95%CI: 0.55–0.81, P <.001) and 10% lower all-cause mortality for LPA (HR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94, P <.001).

In the landmark analysis, we repeated the main analysis among participants with at least 2 years of follow up, and the HRs remained essentially unchanged (Supplemental Table 3). In another sensitivity analysis that further adjusted for lung function, the association between LPA, ST and all-cause mortality were similar, except for MVPA (Supplemental Table 4). Comparing to MVPA < 15 min/day, 30–45 min/day or 45–60 min/day of MVPA was not significantly associated with lower risk of all-cause mortality. In the sensitivity analysis excluding participants with asthma, the associations between MVPA, LPA, ST and all-cause mortality remained similar (Supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this large cohort study provides the first evidence of the dose-response associations of accelerometer-measured physical activity and ST with all-cause mortality among people with COPD. We found non-threshold-based, non-linear associations of MVPA and LPA with all-cause mortality; the optimal amount of MVPA and LPA was at 60 min/day and 5.2 h/day, respectively, with no additional benefits when exceeding these volumes. For females and people with younger age (45–64 years), additional amount of MVPA exceeding 60 min/day may achieve extra benefits. The threshold of ST was identified at 12.43 h/day, above which a significant increase in mortality risk was observed. Using ISM, we also observed that replacing ST with either MVPA or LPA was associated with a reduction in mortality risk. These findings inform physical activity recommendations specific for people with COPD. Of noted, the findings from this observational study should be interpreted with caution. Clinical practitioners may advise physical activity promotion and ST reduction with specific targeted goals based on COPD severity and functional capacity.

This study found non-threshold-based, L-shaped, or U-shaped non-linear associations of MVPA and LPA with mortality in people with COPD. The finding of a non-linear curve is consistent with studies of self-reported physical activity in people with COPD [21, 22]. Two prospective cohort studies also found non-linear curves, which dropped markedly between 0 and 10 MET h/week and flattened out afterward [21, 22]. These findings from observational studies should be interpreted with caution and more robust evidence of randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm the dose-response association. We found no threshold for the beneficial effect of MVPA or LPA on mortality; even low levels of physical activity were beneficial compared to being physically inactive. This supports the WHO guidelines of “some physical activity is better than none” [10] and it is encouraging for people with COPD who cannot achieve a high volume of physical activity.

The identified optimal dose of MVPA and LPA established an upper limit for mortality benefits; the benefits tend to remain unchanged or even diminish at higher volumes. However, achieving 60 min/day of MVPA or 5.2 h/day of LPA exceeds most physical activity guidelines and could be challenging and overwhelming for people with COPD, given the symptoms of dyspnea, fatigue and functional limitations [5, 12]. From our study, only 14.25% of participants engaged in > 60 min/day of MVPA. The observed optimal dose for maximal mortality benefit could be incorporated into COPD guidelines while emphasizing that “some physical activity is better than none.”

People with COPD are less active than the general population [23, 24] and our physical activity results are consistent with two national studies of objectively measured PA in people with COPD. A study from the Canadian Health Measures Survey (CHMS) dataset reported a mean of 16.4 (SE = 1.5) min/day of MVPA and 215.2 (SE = 5.6) min/day of LPA [25]. A study using the National Health and Nutrition Evaluation Survey (NHANES) dataset reported slightly less time in PA, with a mean of 6.36 (SD = 11.37) min/day of MVPA and 249.52 (SD = 169.74) min/day of LPA [24]. Our results from the UK Biobank suggest that people with COPD spend slightly more time in MVPA and LPA, but the differences are minor and likely related to differences in accelerometers. The CMHS and NHANES data were collected using uniaxial accelerometers worn at the hip, and UK Biobank data were collected using triaxial accelerometers worn on the wrist. The consistencies among these studies provide strong support for our findings, regardless of the differences in measurement methods.

People with COPD spend more ST compared with healthy controls [26], due to dyspnea, low exercise capacity, comorbidities, et al. [27] WHO guidelines recommend reducing ST, without providing an exact time limit [10]. This current study addressed this gap and identified the threshold of ST at 12.43 h/day, above which a significant mortality risk increase was observed. Reducing ST below 12.43 can be achievable, as our study found 85.62% of participants had ST < 12 h/day. Moreover, we found that replacing ST with either MVPA or LPA would reduce the risk of mortality. For some people with COPD, MVPA may be difficult to achieve, and replacing ST with LPA would be a more feasible goal [28]. Our study found that replacing 15 min and 30 min of ST with LPA was associated with a mortality reduction of 5% and 10%, respectively, suggesting the benefits of modest levels of physical activity.

This study is the first population-based prospective cohort study to explore the dose-response associations between accelerometer-measured physical activity and all-cause mortality in people with COPD. Strengths of this research include the large sample of people with objectively measured physical activity and a prolonged follow-up for mortality. Nonetheless, the findings should be interpreted with caution and this study is subject to several limitations. First, the diagnosis of COPD was based on self-report and hospital admission records; this was not validated by the FEV1/FVC. The spirometry data were only collected at baseline (2006–2010), but 49% of participants were diagnosed with COPD after the year 2006 (30% diagnosed after the year 2010). The severity of COPD was not available in the dataset, which may confound the association between physical activity and mortality. In addition, the findings are not applicable to inform disease severity-specific physical activity guidelines. Second, baseline covariates were obtained approximately six years before the accelerometer data collection. Thus, residual confounding is possible owing to changes in baseline covariates (i.e., lifestyle factors). Third, the UK Biobank had a low response rate, and participants of this study are not representative of the general COPD population in the UK. Participants with missing data are not included in the analysis, which may lead to selection bias. Most of the participants in our study were overweight or obese and 1% were underweight, indicating that most patients with the “pink puffer” phenotypes were not included in the dataset. Finally, physical activity was measured at a single time point and does not reflect changing patterns of PA [29, 30]. Additional studies are needed to investigate the associations of long term patterns of physical activity and mortality.

Conclusions

Using a population-based prospective cohort study with accelerometer-measured physical activity, we found non-threshold-based, non-linear associations of MVPA, LPA with all-cause mortality and confirmed the benefits of replacing ST with either MVPA or LPA. Our findings reinforced the importance of LPA for mortality risk reduction, particularly for those with difficulty in achieving higher intensity of physical activity. The dose-response nature of the association may help inform future COPD guidelines promoting physical activity and reducing ST.
