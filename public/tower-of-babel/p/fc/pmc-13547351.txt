A global dataset and map of soil phosphorus retention / sorption for assessing phosphorus use efficiency and loss to water
Authors: Richard W. McDowell, Crile Doscher, Alasdair Noble, Lucy Burkitt, Paulo S. Pavinato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil phosphorus (P) retention can inform both P use efficiency and the potential for dissolved P loss from soil to water. We compiled and harmonised global measurements of topsoil (0–20 cm) P retention to produce a consistent database and predictive global map. Data from international soil surveys, national repositories, and published studies were screened for coordinate accuracy, methodological consistency, sampling depth, and duplication. After filtering, 6,478 observations from 68 countries were paired with climatic, land-use, soil, and ecological variables at 10-km2 resolution. Several modelling approaches were assessed, with quantile random forests selected to predict the median, 20th, and 80th percentiles of global P retention. Predictions were generated on a 10-km2 grid consistent and resampled to finer resolution for visualisation purposes without increasing the effective spatial detail. The resulting maps show broad spatial patterns and associated uncertainties that reflect major controls such as soil weathering intensity and hydrology, as well as soil physical properties indirectly related to texture. The dataset provides observation-based, quantitative estimates of topsoil P retention that improve upon earlier qualitative global assessments derived from soil classification. These data are intended for application at catchment to regional scales to support assessments of P use efficiency and dissolved P loss risk, rather than fine-scale management decisions.

Background & Summary

Soil phosphorus (P) retention is a measure of the soil’s capacity to remove and retain P from the soil solution. The original method was developed in 1965 in New Zealand to distinguish different soil types1, and subsequently adopted in the United States to identify andic soil properties in USDA Soil Taxonomy2. Phosphorus retention is now also used either in its original or modified form to explain crop response to P fertiliser additions and to predict the potential for dissolved P loss from soil to water either via surface runoff or leachate3,4. The original method involved shaking soil with a buffered solution at pH 4.6 containing 1000 mg L−1 P. This high concentration of P effectively saturated the soil, overcoming any prior P additions in most agricultural soils, and resulted in repeatable data across soil types whose P sorption was characterised by a broad concentration range of aluminium (Al) and iron (Fe) oxides. Subsequent methods have used lower P concentrations or removed the buffer to better differentiate soil types owing to much stronger Al and Fe oxide concentrations (e.g., adapted to Oxisols in Brazil5) or a greater influence of calcium (e.g., phosphate buffering index used in Australia6–8).

The International Soil Reference and Information Centre developed a global map of P retention using data from the World Inventory of Soil Emission database (WISE) in 2011. The map classified P retention (0–100%) for a soil profile into 16 classes (very low to very high) based off an understanding of the retention of P in response to soil pH, cation exchange capacity, and soil mineralogy as mapped by FAO soil name (at 1:5,000,000 scale)9. However, the authors noted that the data have large (but undefined) uncertainties associated and recommend that the map only be used in an exploratory manner until more detailed information is available at a regional level. Furthermore, the data were derived for the entire soil profile, whereas using P retention to estimate P loss from soil to water or crop response is best correlated with topsoil (0–20 cm) characteristics10,11.

Despite the important of P retention for both agronomic and environmental applications, globally consistent, observation-based datasets of topsoil P retention remain limited. Over time, P retention data have been collected and stored by a range of jurisdictions and made publicly available12,13, but the datasets are are heterogeneous in terms of analytical methods, sampling depth, and reporting standards. This heterogeneity limits their direct use for global assessments without careful harmonisation. To address this gap, we collated and standardised global measurements of topsoil P retention, aligning them with consistent soil depth definitions and environmental covariates. This harmonised dataset provides a foundation for developing spatially explicit estimates of P retention that are directly linked to measured observations rather than inferred soil classes.

In contrast to earlier approaches based primarily on soil classification, the present study combines this harmonised dataset (n = 6,478) with climatic, soil, land-use and environmental predictors to generate quantitative, spatially continuous estimates of topsoil P retention. This approach enables the representation of global patterns in P retention while also providing associated uncertainty estimates derived from the quantile random forest model.

We envisage that the resulting dataset and maps will be appropriate to inform assessments at the catchment or regional scale of phosphorus use efficiency and the risk of dissolved P from soil to water. The data are not intended for field- or farm-level decision-making, where local variability in soil properties may not be greater than that captured by our model.

Geographically, the continent with the most data was North America, followed by Oceania and Africa (Table 2; Fig. 1). After inspecting the data, we found that four biomes suffered from a paucity of data (n < 15): boreal forests/Taiga, Mangroves, tropical and subtropical coniferous forests, and tundra. These biomes represented 12.6%, <1%, ~2% and 8%, respectively, of the global land area, but apart from the small area of tropical and subtropical coniferous forests, are all largely unproductive.

Modelling

The filtered data were paired with predictor variables obtained from a variety of sources (Table 3). These predictor variables were chosen due to their high likelihood of influencing soil P retention and included climate parameters, land use, soil chemical and physical parameters and ecological classifications15,16. Additional geographic and socio-economic descriptors (e.g., country and income class) were included to capture regional differences in land management intensity and environmental conditions that may indirectly influence soil development and P dynamics. We extracted data for each predictor variable from the sources outlined in Table 3 at a resolution of 10 km2, filtering out polar regions in permafrost, deserts and mountainous regions that are unlikely to be used for primary production17 leaving 12,453,854 filtered points. Soil P retention data were confirmed as approximately normally distributed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. A range of models (classification and regression trees18, multivariate adaptive regression splines19, gradient boosted regression trees20, random forests regression21, and quantile random forests regression22,23) were trialled to predict P retention. Although not strictly necessary to ensure the good performance of these models, predictor variables were either natural log-transformed, or arcsine transformed (for values between 0–100%) to generate predictors with equal variance and maximise the chance that predictors had an unbiased chance of being used by the model. During modelling we used 70% of the data to train the models, while the remaining 30% was reserved to evaluate model performance. To minimise geographic bias and reduce the potential influence of spatial autocorrelation on model evaluation, training and validation datasets were constructed to include comparable proportions of monitoring sites from each continent. This approach ensured that model performance reflected global rather than regionally dominated patterns.

After selecting quantile random forest regression as the best model (Table 4), we assessed the risk of overfitting due to spatial autocorrelation using cross-validation24. The final model was retrained using the full dataset to leverage all available information for spatial prediction25,26. This approach improves predictive accuracy once the model’s structure, parameters and performance have been validated27,28. Performance metrics for the final predictions were not calculated, as no independent data remained for testing. Model performance was instead evaluated using held-out validation data and cross-validation prior to final model fitting. The best performing model (quantile Random Forest) used 26 predictors each contributing to predictive performance to varying degrees (Fig. 2). In contrast to parametric regression models, predictors in random forest models are not retained based on statistical significance but on their contribution to predictive accuracy and interactions with other variables. Consequently, predictors with relatively low individual importance may still improve model performance when included with other variables. The relative importance values therefore reflect the contribution of predictors to model performance rather than direct causal relationships.

The most important variables were annual runoff, soil water holding capacity, soil bulk density and annual precipitation, whereas soil group and the presence (or absence) of cropland were among the least influential. The importance of runoff and rainfall may reflect strongly leached soils in high rainfall environments1,5 (Fig. 3). Indeed, predicted P retention was noted to increase above an annual rainfall of 1100 mm and runoff of 150 mm (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Relative importance of predictor for the estimation of soil P retention. The partial dependence plots (Fig. 4) provide further insight into these relationships. The increase in P retention with runoff and rainfall is consistent with enhanced soil weathering and the accumulation of aluminium and iron oxides, which provide reactive surfaces for P sorption. Similarly, higher soil water holding capacity, often associated with finer-textured soils, reflects greater surface area and sorption capacity. In contrast, bulk density may influence P retention indirectly through its effects on soil structure and porosity, which regulate water movement and contact time between soil particles and dissolved P. These predicted spatial patterns are broadly consistent with earlier qualitative assessments of global P retention potential derived from soil classification9, but the present dataset provides quantitative estimates of topsoil P retention derived from measured observations and environmental predictors, enabling improved spatial representation and uncertainty estimation. Land use characteristics and soil classifications were of low importance, reflecting their likely capture in more important climatic variables and ability to farm almost any soil type with appropriate management. Factors affecting soil P sorption such as soil textural composition29, soil organic carbon30 and native or non-anthropogenic soil P31 were of modest importance.

To assess whether model performance was driven primarily by a small number of predictors, we re-ran the model using only the five most important variables. This reduced the coefficient of determination from R2 = 0.68 using all 26 predictors to R2 = 0.62, indicating that while the most influential predictors explain much of the variation in soil P retention, the remaining variables collectively provide additional predictive information.

Predictions of soil P retention in topsoil were projected globally in Q-GIS to a grid at a spatial resolution of 10-km2, which corresponds to the coarsest grid cells associated with the input data as listed in Table 3. For visualisation purposes, these predictions were resampled to 30 arc-seconds (~1 km2 near the equator) using nearest-neighbour interpolation, which does not introduce additional spatial information but allows the results to be displayed at a resolution commonly used for global environmental datasets. Both the predicted 1-km2 grids (EPSG8857) and geotiffs in a WGS84 (EPSG:4326) projection are included in the Figshare repository14. Once projected globally, the mean predicted median value for P retention was 20%. The corresponding mean of the 20th and 80th percentiles were 10 and 34%, respectively indicating a relatively tight range of P retention values with only a few areas exhibiting high P retention percentages. As per previous attempts to measure and map P retention9,32, high P retention values tended to be greatest in the tropics and the least towards the temperate regions. Usage Notes

The dataset is not intended for use at fine spatial scales such as individual farms. The effective resolution of the predictions is constrained by the coarsest input data (~10 km2), and local variability in soil properties may not be captured. However, the data may be used for catchment (>100 ha) or regional assessments about improving the utilisation of added P to different soil types and minimising the loss of dissolved P from the soil.

Curves between crop yield and soil test P are used for P fertilizer recommendations39. However, these response curves vary by soil P retention. For example, New Zealand data for the response of pasture to soil Olsen P indicates an optimal yield at 20 and 35 mg kg−1 for two soils with a P retention of about 30–50% and 60–80%, respectively (Fig. 8)40. Over time, the extra fertilizer P required to maintain soil Olsen P at higher concentrations may be very expensive. Hence, a good estimate of P retention, even at a regional scale, will save many farmers money.Fig. 8Relationship between soil Olsen P and the percentage relative pasture yield and P lost in surface runoff. Phosphorus retention is an analogue for P sorption capacity. When combined as the denominator with soil test P as the numerator, the quotient can act as an indication of how much sorption capacity has been occupied by P additions. Moreover, as a single indicator, P retention will help identify break points in soil test P that can be modelled by piecewise regression to show where P loses in surface runoff accelerate3. Conceptually, the balance between production potential and environmental loss can be seen in Fig. 8 as the different in soil Olsen P concentrations between the point at which the P loss in surface runoff and 95% relative pasture yields are obtained for two soils of different P retention. This information allows land managers to adjust P additions cognizant of their production potential and environmental loss. In this example, there is little justification for enriching Olsen P beyond the 95% relative yield, but doing so would pose a greater risk to surface water quality in the soil with lower P retention.
