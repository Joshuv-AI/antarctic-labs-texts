Invasive meningococcal disease in adolescents in Europe and select geographies: Disease burden, unmet medical need, and optimizing prevention
Authors: Anar Andani, Victoria Abbing-Karahagopian, Justina Kavaliauskaite, Thierry Oliver Schaffner, Woo-Yun Sohn, Maria Gabriela Graña, Helen Marshall, Federico Martinon-Torres, Paolo Bonanni, Rino Rappuoli, Muhamed-Kheir Taha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInvasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is uncommon but serious; the case fatality rate is 8–15% and up to 20–40% of survivors experience disabling sequelae, with a substantial socioeconomic impact. Although incidence is highest in infants and young children (aged ≤4 y), a second epidemiologic peak occurs in adolescents and young adults (aged 15–24 y), who are the main carriers of Neisseria meningitidis. We reviewed the burden of IMD in adolescents and young adults in Europe and select geographies including Australia, New Zealand, and Brazil. Globally, there are differing, often suboptimal, vaccination policies with variable vaccination coverage for IMD prevention caused by the major serogroups (A, B, C, W, and Y). The increased risk in adolescents and young adults may relate to their social behaviors. They are difficult to reach due to a reduced incidence of chronic diseases, age-related perceptions of not being at risk, in addition to reliance on technology-based rapid health information searches rather than healthcare professional – based consultations. Absence of standard and comprehensive vaccination programs for the prevention of IMD in adolescents and young adults presents a clear unmet medical need. Existing vaccines against IMD in adolescents are underutilized compared with vaccines against diseases with better awareness and reimbursement schemes. Increasing access to vaccines against IMD, together with innovative strategies such as awareness campaigns and the use of artificial intelligence, as well as practical strategies, such as in-pharmacy vaccination, will be needed to increase the prevention of IMD in adolescents and young adults.KEYWORDS: Invasive meningococcal disease, Neisseria meningitidis, epidemiology, immunization programs, adolescents, Europe, prevention, guidelines, vaccines, disease burden

Introduction

Raising awareness of invasive meningococcal disease burden and the long-term impact

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is a sudden, devastating, and unpredictable life-threatening condition caused by Neisseria meningitidis, primarily caused by the serogroups A, B, C, W, and Y.1,2 The case fatality rate is 8–15% and up to 20–40% of survivors experience disabling sequelae, with a substantial socioeconomic impact.3 Survivors and their families are impacted both emotionally and mentally, with a devastating effect on their quality of life (QoL).4–8

The incidences of IMD and disease burden are highest in infants and young children, with a second epidemiological peak of disease in adolescents and young adults, and a third in older adults.9,10 The risk of IMD in adolescents and young adults may relate to high transmission/acquisition rates, high-contact social behaviors, lifestyle changes, and overall adolescent mind-set.11–15

Many patients who survive IMD experience serious long-term consequences of the disease including mobility issues, limb amputations, skin scarring, hearing loss, seizures, cognitive deficits, and limb weakness. Adolescent survivors reported significantly poorer physical and mental health, as well as QoL; some are unable to finish schooling or enter the workforce.5–8 This can have a profound economic impact. For example, in France it is estimated that the long-term economic cost of IMD can range from €768,875 to €2,267,251 and the mean cost of index hospitalization was estimated to be between €11,256 and €20,000 for subsequent annual costs in cases with two or more sequelae.8 Societal relationships are often significantly impacted as survivors may develop separation anxiety, communication disorders, social anxiety disorder, or depression.2,6–8 Furthermore, the implications of the long-term consequences may not be adequately captured due to under reporting of symptoms.

Challenges in vaccine uptake and health equity

IMD is largely a vaccine-preventable disease for which existing multivalent vaccines have been available for the last 10 y and yet there is still an unmet need. The absence of a single vaccine covering all disease-associated serogroups can make adequate vaccine coverage highly challenging. There are recommendations for vaccination with meningococcal C (MenC) and meningococcal ACWY (MenACWY) vaccines; however, there is low awareness and few national immunization programs (NIPs) across Europe for vaccination/prevention in adolescents and young adults for meningococcal B (MenB). Where recommendations are in place, they are heterogeneous, lack clarity, and are often suboptimal. Disparities in health equity also impact access to IMD vaccines and overall disease awareness.

In France, vaccine recommendation was required for a given vaccine to be reimbursed, and the main obstacle to not offering four-component serogroup B meningococcal (4CMenB) vaccination is non-reimbursement (33.6%).16 A case-control study suggested that the risk of IMD hospitalization was associated with medical and socioeconomic factors. The latter included low-income status and residing in a socially deprived area as risk factors. The IMD hospitalization risk odds ratio (95% confidence interval) for low-income populations was 1.7 (1.6–1.9) and was even higher for children aged <1 y (4.8 [3.6–6.5]).17 These data help argue that social equity “is broken” when the risk of a vaccine-preventable disease is higher in a group where a vaccine is available, but not accessible due to a lack of reimbursement. Currently, the 4CMenB vaccine is recommended by the Haut Autorité de Santé for children under the age of 2 y and recommends a booster dose of the MenACWY vaccine for adolescents between 11 and 14 y of age. Further, Haut Autorité de Santé recommends serogroup B vaccination to be reimbursed for all adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 y wishing to be vaccinated.18 With the publication of the new vaccination calendar in France, the vaccination against MenB may be offered to adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 y.19 It will be important for policymakers to target other patient groups, such as adolescents, who are at risk for IMD and ensure social equity in the face of this serious, but preventable disease.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has initiated a global road map to pursue the defeat of meningitis, regardless of cause, by 2030 with three goals: elimination of bacterial meningitis epidemics; reduction of cases of vaccine-preventable bacterial meningitis by 50% and deaths by 70%; reduction of disability and improvement of QoL after meningitis due to any cause.20 To achieve this vision, it is necessary to take action to address IMD in adolescents and young adults building on the demonstrated impact and success of infant programs. Herein, we investigate the burden (reported cases) of IMD in adolescents and young adults and review how comprehensively meningococcal vaccination programs target this age group.

Methods

As Europe was the focus of this research, a targeted literature search was conducted of Europe-wide and national websites and supplemented with searches for studies or review articles using national data on IMD using PubMed and the Embase library. The aims of this targeted search were to review the burden (reported cases) of IMD in adolescents and young adults, and how comprehensively meningococcal vaccination programs target this age group. Search terms included were targeted to each country: “invasive meningococcal disease OR IMD” AND adolescents; “invasive meningococcal disease OR IMD” AND adolescents AND “vaccine coverage”; “invasive meningococcal disease OR IMD” AND adolescents AND “MenACWY vaccine coverage”; “invasive meningococcal disease OR IMD” AND adolescents AND “MenB vaccine coverage”; “invasive meningococcal disease OR IMD” AND adolescents AND “vaccination rates”; “invasive meningococcal disease OR IMD” AND adolescents AND “4CMenB vaccine coverage.”

Relevant data of reported IMD cases and vaccine schedules were obtained mainly from online resources, including the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) Surveillance Atlas for country-specific case numbers, the ECDC Vaccine Scheduler for country-specific national immunization programs, and national institute of health websites for the countries in scope, e.g., the Department of Health and Aged Care (Australia), National Institute for Health and Disability Insurance (Belgium), Ministry of Health (Brazil), Czech Vaccinology Society ČLS JEP (Czech Republic), Santé Publique France (France), Federal Ministry of Health (Germany), the Ministry of Health (Italy), the Dutch National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (Netherlands), Health New Zealand, the Federal Office of Public Health BAG (Switzerland), the Ministry of Health (Spain), and the UK Health Security Agency. Vaccine recommendations through the end of April 2025 were included in this report. Ethical approval was not required for this study because it did not involve clinical procedures or human/animal subjects.

Results

Burden of disease and epidemiology

The notification rate of IMD in individuals aged 15–24 y was 0.6 per 100,000 population across the European Union and European Economic Area in 2022, with the predominant Neisseria meningitidis disease-causing serogroups being B (68%), Y (15%), W (7%), and C (4%) (Figure 1(A,B)).10

Australia

Similar heterogeneity is seen with Australian infants recommended to receive the MenACWY vaccine at 12 months, followed by a booster aged 14–16 y. MenB vaccination is funded for Aboriginal infants on the NIP in Australia due to their higher risk of IMD. In South Australia, Queensland, the Northern Territory, and a program recently announced in Tasmania, the 4CMenB vaccine is funded for infants and adolescents by the state/territory governments.38,39 In other states, it is recommended for those at high risk of MenB disease, including specific immune deficiency disorders.39

New Zealand

MenACWY and MenB vaccination are recommended in New Zealand for adolescents/young adults aged 13–25 y in their first year of boarding school, hostels, tertiary education halls of residence, military barracks, or correctional facilities.40

Brazil

In Brazil, MenACWY vaccination is recommended for adolescents as early as age 11 through age 14 y.41

Overview of current adolescent immunization coverage

Europe

Individuals with a low socioeconomic status have the highest risk of IMD and the lowest uptake of meningococcal vaccines. In an English study of MenACWY vaccine uptake in children aged 13–14 y, from 1,432 schools, vaccine coverage of >80% was reported in 68% of state-funded schools, 55% of non-state-funded (private) schools, 27% of schools for children with special educational needs, and 21% of schools for children excluded from mainstream education.42 Another study evaluated the influence of school characteristics on uptake of the MenACWY vaccine in England among children aged 14–15 y. Out of ~20,000 eligible individuals, 81% were vaccinated. Schools judged by authorities to be least effective in the delivery of education had a lower vaccine uptake of 63% versus the most effective schools, which had a vaccine uptake of 87%.43

In Europe, vaccination coverage against the five most common serogroups causing IMD, particularly serogroup B, was lacking compared with vaccination coverage with vaccines of a higher degree of awareness, such as human papillomavirus (HPV) (Table 1).

Australia and New Zealand

Vaccines given during school and funded nationally in Australia include MenACWY, with state-funded programs for 4CMenB in South Australia, Queensland, the Northern Territory, and soon to commence in Tasmania, while in New Zealand only HPV, diphtheria, tetanus, and acellular pertussis combined with inactivated poliovirus vaccines are given in schools (Table 1).

Best practices in vaccination policies

Vaccination policies in Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and the Czech Republic are best practice examples of NIPs providing coverage for all five predominant IMD serogroups in adolescents and young adults. Switzerland and Liechtenstein have a general recommendation of co-administration of MenACWY and MenB between the ages of 11 and 15, with a catch-up program in place for those aged 16–19 y who missed previous doses.57,65

Similarly, the Czech Republic provides a publicly funded program for MenACWY and MenB for those aged 14–16 y. MenB and MenACWY can be administered simultaneously. Data from a surveillance program in the Czech Republic between 2006 and 2022 reported 958 cases of IMD, of which 2.19% had a history of vaccination with one of the meningococcal vaccines. It was found that these vaccinated patients with IMD were not vaccinated against the causative serogroup and/or did not receive a booster dose. The results of this analysis show the benefit of providing both vaccines to young people, i.e., the MenB vaccine as well as the tetravalent conjugate vaccine MenACWY, and the importance of providing booster doses.66

Discussion

The current challenges surrounding adolescent IMD vaccination

IMD is an unpredictable sudden, rapidly evolving, devastating and, in some cases, life-threatening disease. IMD notification rates indicated a decrease until the COVID-19 pandemic. Afterward, rates rebounded among adolescents/young adults, exceeding pre-pandemic levels in some countries, such as the UK, Germany, and France, and continue to evolve. Adolescents/young adults are at increased risk of IMD due to high acquisition/transmission rates, high-contact social behaviors, and suboptimal vaccine recommendations. Additionally, adolescent mind-sets may contribute to their risk as they might not be aware of their susceptibility and rely on quick and easy searches for health information, such as with Google or from social media.67 While social media can be a powerful tool for sharing information, it comes with caveats as well. Misinformation is a considerable barrier, particularly in regard to vaccines.68 There is a need for strategies to combat misinformation that might challenge a social media campaign. Adolescents/young adults are a key age group affected by IMD, and there is a need to collaborate with them for prevention strategies to be effective.

Absence of uniform and comprehensive vaccination programs for the prevention of IMD in adolescents and young adults covering the five most prevalent serogroups present a clear unmet medical need in Europe. There is a disparity in recommendations and NIPs between countries, with some recommending either one or both vaccinations, while others have no program in place for adolescents/young adults. In some countries, governments may not provide funding at all, despite the recommendation to vaccinate, and in others, vaccination may be fully funded even without an NIP. While many countries recommend MenACWY vaccination, there are many that do not have a recommendation for MenB vaccination in this age group. This gap in adolescent meningococcal vaccination strategy must be addressed since serogroup B remains a major cause of IMD in all age groups.69 Coverage in Australia is high due to delivery through school programs.70 However, there is a disparity between recommendations and the health environment where vaccinations are not made available in schools, which can make access challenging.

Further, there are a number of vaccines available such as the 4CMenB vaccine (Bexsero, GSK), meningococcal (serogroups A, C, Y, and W-135) oligosaccharide diphtheria CRM197 conjugate vaccine (Menveo, GSK), meningococcal group A, C, W-135, and Y conjugate vaccine (Nimenrix, Pfizer), meningococcal (serogroups A, C, Y, W) tetanus toxoid conjugate vaccine (MenQuadfi, Sanofi), and meningococcal group B vaccine (Trumenba, Pfizer). However, they currently remain underutilized in the prevention of IMD for adolescents. Despite the number of options and time available on the market, some have been available for >10 y, there remain gaps in vaccine coverage for adolescents. Advocating for their use may contribute to establishing a new standard of care.

Factors impacting disease burden in adolescents

Increased incidence of IMD may relate to social behaviors that increase the risk of acquisition of invasive isolates, such as attending crowded parties, night clubs, pubs, or discos, smoking, vaping, and sharing drinks.12–14,71,72 Adolescents and young adults may also live in crowded accommodation, such as dormitories or university accommodation, where young people from diverse geographic areas become quickly colonized with strains of Neisseria meningitidis that they may not have previously encountered.12–14

Barriers to vaccination access and lack of comprehensive recommendations

Adolescents are the most difficult age to reach by healthcare. This is the first time they are potentially away from a parent or caregiver’s care, and they may no longer be under the care of a pediatrician. If they are away from home, they are unlikely to have changed care to a general practitioner (GP) or university GP. The responsibility to vaccinate is often diffused when there is no clear accountable healthcare professional (HCP). Further, when an adolescent is under care, there is also a lack of clarity on vaccine recommendations for HCPs and for patients – up to a third of parents of high-school adolescents reported the reason for their child not being vaccinated against MenACWY was that it was not recommended by an HCP.73 Lack of funding or reimbursement negatively impacts the likelihood of an HCP recommending a vaccine. For example, in the case of varicella vaccines, only 25% of 600 surveyed HCPs discussed non-funded vaccines during routine immunization visits.74 Additionally, HCPs often lack the knowledge of how to relate to adolescents and how to offer them the required preventions through vaccination. Behavioral science interventions may be helpful to upskill HCPs in this area. Finally, the laws governing an adolescent’s right to self-consent to a vaccine are not always clear, leading to confusion among both patients and HCPs, preventing some from receiving a vaccine and leaving them vulnerable to serious disease.15

Health equity challenges

Several aspects of equity need to be considered. Social equity mainly refers to the income levels of the population and to the place of residence, such as deprived areas. Health equity evolves with age, and in general, there is a trend toward a higher burden beyond childhood. There is a potential need for an age-specific vaccine calendar to address equity among adolescents. Travel and mass gatherings are associated with IMD, requiring continuous adaptation of recommendations according to the risk evaluation, which highlights the need to address national and supranational equity. For example, the recent reemergence of IMD due to serogroup W of the genotype CC11, associated with travel to Saudi Arabia for Umrah pilgrimage, urges the need for meningococcal vaccination not only for the Hajj, but also for other types of travel/travelers to Saudi Arabia.75

Some countries have an NIP in place that funds meningococcal vaccination, while other countries have recommendations, but the vaccines are not funded by the national health system, further driving inequity in vaccine access. Strategies for addressing inequities should include comprehensive and clear recommendations as part of NIPs with strong implementation, initiatives to improve meningococcal vaccine knowledge among disadvantaged communities, and consideration of equity of access to vaccines at national and regional levels in plans to promote vaccination.24

Potential solutions to improve vaccine coverage

To achieve the WHO vision of defeating meningitis by 2030, action needs to be taken to increase accessibility to vaccination. A key first step would be to effectively utilize existing vaccines. Another crucial step would be to reduce the financial burden by addressing costs and the number of healthcare visits required by including vaccination checks with other visits (such as sport certificates). Improving convenience by including vaccinations in school/university programs or increasing availability at pharmacies would address compliance and timeliness of vaccination. Finally, coadministration with other vaccinations and reducing/managing discomfort and adverse events, such as use of prophylactic paracetamol where indicated, may also improve vaccine coverage.

Vaccine programs in countries with effective practices could be used as guidelines for establishing and building on guidelines in countries with suboptimal programs. Vaccine coverage may be increased by extending MenB adolescent vaccination into countries that have not yet included it in their NIPs and by establishing school-based programs to facilitate delivery. Such programs have been very successful for other vaccines such as HPV, which has achieved high coverage rates.76,77 Further protection against the five prevalent serogroups causing IMD can be achieved by co-administration of MenB and MenACWY in adolescents, or administration of a vaccine such as 4CMenB which is effective and has an acceptable safety profile in children, adolescents, and adults78 or the recently approved MenABCWY pentavalent vaccine (Penmenvy).79

Potential solutions to improve risk perception and tackle vaccine hesitancy

Ultimately, education is the first step to increase risk perception, overcome vaccine hesitancy, and improve vaccine coverage. Effective awareness campaigns for adolescents, patients, and HCPs should be implemented to assuage vaccine hesitancy and inform on the impact of vaccination. Adding immunization to the school curriculum can increase awareness among adolescents. Additionally, reaching adolescents and young adults directly via social media-based awareness campaigns, expanded use of registries, and pharmacy vaccinations may help to close the vaccination inequity gap.67 One successful example of a campaign, called Flutext-4 U, to promote influenza vaccination took place in South Australia. Short message service (SMS) nudges were sent to a child’s primary caregiver. The campaign resulted in significantly increased influenza vaccination rates (from a rate of 26.2% in the control group vs. 38.6% in the SMS nudge group; p = .001) and shorter time to vaccine receipt. SMS messages represent a low-cost method of reaching both parents and young adults alike.80 Inevitably, the most successful programs would be co-developed with adolescents.

As artificial intelligence (AI) advances, there is potential for these technologies to strengthen vaccine programs and support awareness campaigns. An AI model based on data from Pakistan has been developed to predict vaccine acceptability with 98% accuracy.81 In the United States, three AI models were evaluated for accuracy in identifying factors associated with COVID-19 vaccine uptake. Age, income, marital status, employment status, ethnicity, educational level, and sex differed by vaccine uptake.82 While this specific model is not generalizable to other regions, models like it can provide key insights into demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors that are most strongly associated with vaccine hesitancy or acceptance and can be used to tailor immunization campaigns.81

Limitations for this work include variability among the countries reporting methods of IMD case rates and vaccine coverage. These were not accounted for in comparisons made herein. School-based delivery is a key contributor to vaccine coverage; however, we do not explore differences in vaccine access in public versus private schools. Future work would benefit from a more granular analysis of country-specific coverage rates, barriers to adolescent vaccination, and policy implementation gaps across Europe and the rest of the world.

Conclusions

Adolescents, parents, HCPs, and decision-makers may be unaware adolescents are at risk for IMD or of the devastating and potentially lifelong impact it may have on survivors. Existing vaccines such as 4CMenB and MenACWY against IMD are highly underutilized and more comprehensive vaccination programs are needed, ensuring equitable access, to prevent IMD in adolescents/young adults in Europe and Latin America. The path to defeating meningitis by 2030, aligned to WHO goals, will be paved by increasing awareness and expanding accessibility of vaccination in adolescents and young adults.

Implications and contribution

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is a sudden, devastating, and unpredictable life-threatening condition. Absence of standard and comprehensive vaccination programs in adolescents and young adults presents a clear unmet medical need. Multiple strategies and best practices could be used to address vaccination disparities in this key population.
