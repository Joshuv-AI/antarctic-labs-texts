Opposing effects of efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab on serum albumin levels in patients with generalized myasthenia gravis
Authors: Manato Yasuda, Akiyuki Uzawa, Hideo Handa, Etsuko Ogaya, Kentaro Kurumada, Kyosuke Takasaka, Masahiro Mori, Satoshi Kuwabara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab are both neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) inhibitors for generalized myasthenia gravis (MG), they possess distinct molecular structures: efgartigimod is an engineered immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) Fc fragment, whereas rozanolixizumab is an IgG4 monoclonal antibody. This study aimed to investigate whether these distinct molecular structures induce differential effects on serum albumin in a real-world setting. We conducted a single-center observational study in patients with generalized MG who newly initiated efgartigimod or rozanolixizumab, measuring serum IgG and albumin levels at baseline and the end of the first treatment cycle. We analyzed 21 treatment cycles (efgartigimod, n = 16; rozanolixizumab, n = 5) from 20 patients, including one who received both therapies. Both drugs induced comparable reductions in serum IgG (median: efgartigimod 671.5 mg/dL vs. rozanolixizumab 734.0 mg/dL, p = 0.59). In contrast, their effects on serum albumin levels were diametrically opposed. In the efgartigimod group, albumin levels increased in 15 (94%) cases (median: + 0.45 g/dL), whereas in the rozanolixizumab group, levels decreased in 5 (100%) cases (median: − 0.40 g/dL). The between-group difference in median albumin change was substantial (p = 0.001). Efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab exert opposing effects on serum albumin levels despite similar IgG-lowering efficacy. This modality-specific pattern suggests distinct pharmacological interactions with the FcRn–albumin recycling pathway, potentially influencing treatment selection in specific patient populations.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder caused by pathogenic immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies (Punga et al. 2022). Targeting the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which is a key regulator of IgG and albumin homeostasis (Ward et al. 2022), has transformed MG treatment (Pyzik et al. 2023). FcRn inhibitors accelerate autoantibody clearance by blocking IgG recycling. In Japan, the FcRn inhibitors efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab have been approved for the treatment of generalized MG. Although both target FcRn, the agents have fundamentally different structures: efgartigimod is an engineered IgG1 Fc fragment (Howard et al. 2021), whereas rozanolixizumab is a full IgG4 monoclonal antibody (mAb) (Bril et al. 2023).

The interaction of these agents with serum albumin remains poorly understood. FcRn preserves the long half-life of both IgG and albumin via distinct, non-overlapping binding sites (Andersen et al. 2012; Ma et al. 2024; Ward et al. 2022). Consistent with this mechanism, Phase 3 clinical trials for efgartigimod (ADAPT) (Howard et al. 2021) and rozanolixizumab (MycarinG) (Bril et al. 2023) have reported no clinically significant reductions in serum albumin levels. However, no direct comparison between efgartigimod (an IgG1 Fc fragment) and rozanolixizumab (a full-length IgG4 mAb) has been conducted to determine whether these structural differences result in divergent metabolic profiles in clinical practice. In this study, we investigated the effects of efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab on serum albumin levels in a clinical cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This single-center, retrospective observational study was conducted at Chiba University Hospital, Chiba, Japan. We identified patients with generalized MG who newly initiated efgartigimod (Vyvgart or Vyvdura; Argenx, Tokyo, Japan) or rozanolixizumab (Rystiggo; UCB, Tokyo, Japan) treatment at our institution from June 1, 2022, to September 30, 2025. Inclusion required the completion of a standard first treatment cycle with approved doses (efgartigimod: four doses, intravenous [IV] or subcutaneous [SC]; rozanolixizumab: six doses, SC, administered weekly). Patients were excluded if they had participated in a prior clinical trial of any investigational drug or lacked the necessary serum data at baseline or after treatment. For patients who transitioned from efgartigimod IV to SC formulation, only data from the initial IV cycle were analyzed. For the patient who received both therapies, each treatment cycle was considered an independent event. We compared serological and clinical data between the efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab groups.

Data collection

Serum IgG and albumin levels were measured at baseline (within 1 week before treatment initiation) and post-treatment (1 week after the final dose of the first cycle ± 1 week). These analyses were performed in the clinical laboratory of Chiba University Hospital using an automated analyzer (BioMajesty JCA-BM8040G; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Serum IgG was measured using a turbidimetric immunoassay, and serum albumin was measured using the modified bromocresol purple method. The normal reference ranges at our institution were 861–1747 mg/dL for serum IgG and 4.1–5.1 g/dL for serum albumin. In addition to these serological data, patient demographic data (age, sex, and antibody status) were extracted from the medical records. Clinical status was assessed using the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) score (Wolfe et al. 1999), which was recorded at baseline and post-treatment timepoints.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Student Edition 19.0.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Between-group comparisons of continuous and ordinal variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and proportions were compared using Fisher’s exact test. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess within-group changes from baseline to post-treatment. Correlations between the changes in serum IgG (ΔIgG) and albumin (ΔAlbumin) levels were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous and ordinal variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]).

Results

Patient characteristics

Our analysis included 21 treatment cycles from 20 patients with generalized MG: 16 in the efgartigimod group (10 IV and 6 SC) and 5 in the rozanolixizumab group (all SC). One patient received both therapies sequentially and was included in both groups. We excluded 15 patients, as 5 had participated in previous clinical trials, 7 (6 efgartigimod and 1 rozanolixizumab) had missing baseline or post-treatment serum data, and 3 did not complete the standard first treatment cycle. At baseline, patients’ age, sex, antibody (anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody, anti-muscle-specific kinase antibody, and double seronegative) status, and clinical severity were comparable between the two groups (Table 1), and serum IgG levels were similar (efgartigimod 910.5 [809.0–1181.8] mg/dL vs. rozanolixizumab 963.0 [744.0–1179.5] mg/dL, p = 0.934). The median baseline albumin level was higher in the rozanolixizumab group (4.4 [4.4–4.5] g/dL) than in the efgartigimod group (4.2 [4.1–4.4] g/dL) (p = 0.048) (Table 1). Effects on serum IgG and clinical scores

Both treatments were highly effective. The efgartigimod group showed a median IgG reduction of 671.5 (511.8–812.8) mg/dL, and the rozanolixizumab group showed a median reduction of 734.0 (520.0–948.0) mg/dL. The magnitude of reduction was similar between the groups (p = 0.59; Table 1; Fig. 1A–B), indicating comparable primary pharmacodynamic efficacy. Effects on serum albumin levels

Despite comparable IgG reductions, the two drugs exerted diametrically opposed effects on serum albumin levels (Table 1; Fig. 1C). In the efgartigimod group, serum albumin levels increased from 4.2 (4.1–4.4) g/dL at baseline to 4.7 (4.5–4.9) g/dL after treatment (p < 0.001), indicating a consistent change observed in 15 of 16 cases (94%). In contrast, in the rozanolixizumab group, serum albumin levels decreased in all five cases (100%), from 4.4 (4.4–4.5) g/dL at baseline to 4.0 (3.9–4.2) g/dL after treatment (p = 0.063). This divergence was evident in the median change: + 0.45 (0.20–0.60) g/dL in the efgartigimod group versus − 0.40 (− 0.45 to − 0.30) g/dL in the rozanolixizumab group (p = 0.001; Fig. 1D). Consequently, post-treatment albumin levels were higher in the efgartigimod group than in the rozanolixizumab group (median: 4.7 g/dL vs. 4.0 g/dL, p = 0.014). Notably, in the single patient who received both therapies sequentially, serum albumin levels increased during efgartigimod treatment (4.5 to 4.7 g/dL), whereas they decreased during rozanolixizumab treatment (4.4 to 4.0 g/dL), reflecting the divergent effects observed in the overall cohort.

In the efgartigimod group, the number of patients with serum albumin below the normal reference range (4.1–5.1 g/dL) decreased from three at baseline to two after treatment. Conversely, in the rozanolixizumab group, three of five patients fell below this lower limit after treatment, despite all having normal baseline levels.

Correlation between serum IgG and albumin changes

No significant correlation was observed between ΔIgG and ΔAlbumin in either the efgartigimod group (ρ = − 0.27, p = 0.31) or the rozanolixizumab group (ρ = 0.46, p = 0.43).

Discussion

This real-world study demonstrated that efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab exert opposing effects on serum albumin levels. Efgartigimod consistently increased serum albumin, whereas rozanolixizumab consistently decreased it. This finding indicates that the effect on serum albumin is modality-specific rather than a uniform class effect, as this divergence occurred despite comparable IgG-lowering efficacy. Furthermore, the comparable MG-ADL improvement suggests that nutritional recovery was not the primary driver of the observed changes in albumin. As an additional observation, within each treatment group, the change in serum albumin did not significantly correlate with the magnitude of IgG reduction in this study.

Preclinical studies of other full-length anti-FcRn mAbs, including batoclimab and nipocalimab analogues, have shown that their bivalent structure induces FcRn cross-linking and lysosomal degradation, thereby reducing albumin recycling (Ma et al. 2024). Despite earlier suggestions that rozanolixizumab-mediated FcRn cross-linking was unlikely (Smith et al. 2018), this mechanism may explain the consistent albumin reduction observed in our study, in addition to potential steric hindrance.

In contrast, the increase in serum albumin levels with efgartigimod, an Fc fragment, may reflect its distinct molecular engineering. A mechanistic study indicated that efgartigimod competitively reduces IgG levels without inducing FcRn degradation or blocking the albumin binding site and may slightly increase the intracellular FcRn pool (Ma et al. 2024). This enhanced receptor availability likely promotes albumin salvage, potentially explaining the serum albumin elevation observed in our study. A similar “albumin-sparing” effect has been reported in mouse models (Ma et al. 2024) and confirmed in clinical trials of pemphigus (Goebeler et al. 2022).

The primary limitations of this study are its retrospective design and small sample size, particularly in the rozanolixizumab group. The within-group change in the rozanolixizumab group did not reach statistical significance, which is a clear consequence of the small sample size. Nevertheless, the uniform direction of change (all five cases decreased) and the pronounced between-group difference in the magnitude of change (p = 0.001) provide strong evidence of a consistent effect. While the decrease in serum albumin was observed in all patients in the rozanolixizumab group, none reached clinically dangerous levels of hypoalbuminemia requiring medical intervention. Larger, prospective registries are necessary to confirm these findings and to further evaluate the clinical safety and long-term impact of these agents on albumin homeostasis.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that efgartigimod consistently increases serum albumin levels, whereas rozanolixizumab consistently decreases them, highlighting their divergent effects on albumin homeostasis. Although the long-term clinical implications of these changes remain unknown, this distinct profile represents a notable pharmacological characteristic. It may warrant consideration in clinical practice, particularly for patients with or at risk of hypoalbuminemia, such as older or malnourished individuals.
