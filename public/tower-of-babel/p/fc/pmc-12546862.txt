In Reply to Devlin et al
Authors: Erin McClure, Mairead Moloney, Yuxuan Jin, Lio Yu
Published: 2025

To Drs Devlin and Likhacheva and Mr White,

Thank you for reading our article and sharing your concerns.1 Overall, while we appreciate you taking the time to discuss our work, we were surprised to find that the majority of the comments were anecdotal, written in somewhat inflammatory language, or related to issues regarding remuneration and intersociety politics. In contrast, we are presenting a scientific retrospective review of the efficacy of a new technology.

The only citation provided in your letter to the editor that is unfavorable toward image guided superficial radiation therapy (IGSRT) is an American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) task force consensus paper written before any reporting of this new technology was in practice.2 It is curious and telling that this article recommended the following on page 16: “Daily imaging is neither necessary nor useful when treating with electron beam, ELS (electronically generated low-energy sources), or skin surface brachytherapy” in the absence of supporting reference(s). We believe this suggests that the consensus panel is out of date and should meet again to consider the now sizable body of literature on the efficacy of IGSRT. Furthermore, it is important for readers to note that Dr Devlin is on the ASTRO Radiation Oncology Institute Board and the Chair of the Radiation Oncology Institute Finance Committee, which we perceive as significant conflicts of interest.

With respect to the methodological concerns you raised, ethical approval was waived for this study by the Western-Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board because the data set were deidentified prior to analysis, and all data personnel adhered to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and ethical standards to protect patient information. Furthermore, because superficial radiation therapy (SRT) without imaging has fallen out of favor, there are no modern reports available; hence, the use of older studies for comparison.

We stand by the decision to write this paper with retrospective data. This was a limitation noted in the Discussion section, and we conceded that randomized controlled trials (RCTs) would offer higher-quality data. Conducting RCTs remains a goal for future studies. However, this article represents an important starting point in establishing the merit behind the continued investigation of IGSRT in nonmelanoma skin cancer (NMSC). The initial steps in scientific discovery are not typically conducted at the level of RCTs. Indeed, much of evidence-based medicine is based on data from retrospective studies.

We were able to include the protocol for simulating and treating patients with IGSRT because Dr Yu, one of our coauthors, is a radiation oncologist familiar with the standardized IGSRT treatment protocol used to generate the patient data in the IGSRT data set. To maximize the transparency of our methodology and the reproducibility of our study, we reported the IGSRT treatment protocol in detail. A similar description can be found in the appropriate use criteria for IGSRT, as released by the Dermatology Association for Radiation Therapy.3,4 This is important, as standardization of the technique for this new technology is a key first step in understanding its efficacy by removing sources of uncertainty related to the technique.

The results of our paper support the use of ultrasound technology in IGSRT. Specifically, the improved freedom from recurrence observed with IGSRT compared with SRT in our study demonstrates that visualizing tumor margins more precisely with high-resolution dermal ultrasound (HRDUS) is associated with improved patient outcomes. This is analogous to differences in outcomes observed when NMSCs are excised with standard excision versus Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS), which provides precise and complete margin evaluation. By your rationale, micrographic assessment should also have no benefit, which is patently wrong.

Tumors with low-risk features for recurrence (located in area M or L, less than 10 or 20 mm in size, respectively) are evaluated clinically and then excised with standard margins. However, skin cancers that are larger, on cosmetically sensitive areas, or in high-risk regions like the face are treated with the tissue-sparing surgical technique of MMS. Standard surgical excision has been shown to have a 5-year recurrence rate of 10.1% for basal cell carcinomas versus 1.0% for MMS.5 Similarly, standard surgical excision of squamous cell carcinomas resulted in a local control rate of 92% versus 97% for MMS.5 Clearly, the 100% margin evaluation possible with MMS improves the outcomes of standard excision, whereas margin evaluation at the time of the procedure is not generally performed. Therefore, it stands to reason that the improved margin evaluation achieved with IGSRT is likely responsible for the improved outcomes relative to SRT.

Additionally, the use of HRDUS guidance at each IGSRT treatment session allows for adaptive planning (allowing up to daily adjustments, if necessary, in radiation energy and dosing to compensate for changes in tumor size or other changes in target configuration such as edema, bleeding, etc), which facilitates optimal tumor control while minimizing toxicity to surrounding healthy tissues.6,7 This is similar in spirit to other image guided radiation therapy trials found in radiation oncology literature.8 Furthermore, this approach is consistent with the guiding principle of low as reasonably achievable radiation dosing, where care is taken to avoid additional radiation dosing that will not provide a direct benefit.9

We were surprised to see that Current Procedural Terminology codes are a topic of discussion in this letter to the editor. The billing structure of IGSRT is not relevant to our publication, which focused on the outcomes/efficacy of IGSRT in an academic journal.

With respect to the comments about the capability of the ultrasound system used in the studies reported here for IGSRT, perhaps your team is unfamiliar with this technology. It was designed specifically to resolve changes in depth and margin assessment for dermatologic purposes. To this end, it employs a mathematical linkage between the imaging system and the delivery system. We encourage you to read about this new technology. A retrospective study on 1507 NMSCs treated with IGSRT recorded changes in tumor dimensions and compensatory changes in radiation treatment parameters throughout treatment.7 This study used image guidance via HRDUS for NMSC lesions prior to the delivery of each fraction. This revealed that 92% of NMSC lesions (1386 out of 1507) had daily depth fluctuations. Forty percent of lesions required at least 1 adaptive replanning during therapy. This clearly demonstrates that IGSRT can detect changes in tumor size, and these changes can impact treatment plans. Additionally, a 2019 review article on the use of HRDUS for skin cancer noted that this valuable technology is currently underused by dermatologists, and advocates for expansion of its use.10

The practice guidelines you noted were authored in 2019 and published in 2020, before any formal assessments of IGSRT were published.2 Change can take time, and it is unrealistic to expect all medical societies to recommend a relatively novel technology. However, an assessment of the modern literature may lead ASTRO to arrive at a different conclusion.

We encourage continued professional discussions on the merit behind IGSRT. Our paper is a statistical assessment of the efficacy of a new technology. In line with this, bringing up the cost of IGSRT is inappropriate in your letter to the editor, as this is not a topic covered in our paper. However, it may be useful to review a paper published in 2017, which reported that MMS had accrued $537 million in Medicare expenditures, and all skin cancer procedures combined had accrued $806 million.11 This puts your claims regarding the $20 million Medicare spent on IGSRT into context. Furthermore, we do not understand how the physician’s letter on Medicare pertains to the science presented in our article. We are eager to study and promulgate our scientific findings on this new technology so that physicians, physicists, patients, and scientists can work together to advance medicine. We stand by our findings here, which have, since publication, been validated in much larger cohorts.12, 13, 14, 15 We invite you to present scientific evidence in response to ours, at which time, a more formal discussion would be welcomed.
