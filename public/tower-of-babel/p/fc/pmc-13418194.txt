Absence of insulin receptor substrate 2 (IRS2) in myeloid cells results in enhanced hypoxia-induced remodeling of the pulmonary vasculature and heart with modest effects on allergic lung inflammation
Authors: Luz M Villanueva, Homare Ito, Xiulan Qi, Svetlana P Chapoval, Rose M Viscardi, Stefanie N Vogel, Achsah D Keegan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe previously reported that global insulin receptor substrate-2 (IRS2)-deficient mice demonstrated enhanced pulmonary vascular remodeling in response to allergen exposure or to hypoxia. To evaluate the specific contribution of IRS2 in myeloid cells to this process, we analyzed responses in mice with conditional IRS2-deficiencies in myeloid cells by crossing Irs2fl/fl mice to LysM-Cretg/wt mice and examined responses to allergens and hypoxia. There were no significant differences in the allergic inflammatory response induced by either ovalbumin adsorbed to aluminum hydroxide or by house dust mite (HDM) between Irs2fl/flLysM-Crewt/wt (Cre−/−) and Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt (Cre+/−) littermates. However, there was a significant difference between Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice in the HDM-induced remodeling of small pulmonary blood vessels. Such remodeling can lead to changes in cardiopulmonary function including increases in right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) and pulmonary hypertension (PH). While we did observe an increase in RVSP in response to hypoxia or allergen, there was no significant difference in RSVP between Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. However, there was a significant difference in right heart remodeling as indicated by changes in right ventricular internal diameter and Fulton Index. These results indicate that IRS2 expression in myeloid cells limits pulmonary and cardiac remodeling responses that are induced by allergic inflammation or hypoxia.

Introduction

Macrophages are versatile cells that rapidly polarize their function in response to cues found in the tissues in which they reside.1 Macrophages exposed to pro-inflammatory cues, termed M1 macrophages, become highly microbicidal, producing high amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines and pro-oxidant enzymes including TNF-α, IL-6, and cyclooxygenase-2.1–3 These M1 macrophages are efficient at eliminating intracellular bacteria but also can cause bystander tissue damage. Conversely, macrophages exposed to the T helper 2 (Th2) cytokines IL-4 or IL-13 develop into M2 macrophages that counter the strong pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and participate in tissue repair. This M1, M2 dichotomy is an oversimplification as it is known that macrophages develop into a spectrum of phenotypes including M2a (IL-4- or IL-13-activated), M2b, M2c, and regulatory subtypes that are distinguished by the method of activation, expression of surface markers, and cytokines produced.1–6

We previously reported that M2a macrophages actively participate in enhancement of allergic lung inflammation and increased muscularization of small pulmonary vessels in response to hypoxic conditions.7,8 These responses are dependent upon IL-4 and the IL-4 receptor.9–11 Furthermore, we showed that insulin receptor substrate 2 (IRS2), a signaling molecule downstream of the IL-4 and insulin receptors,12,13 has an anti-inflammatory role in Th2-skewed lung inflammation and pulmonary vascular remodeling in vivo using global Irs2−/− mice.7,8 In the complete absence of IRS2, we observed an increase in IL-4-induced M2a gene expression including resistin like alpha (Retnla), also known as Found in Inflammatory Zone -1 (FIZZ1), and hypoxia-induced mitogenic factor-1 (HIMF-1), Chitinase-like protein 3 (Chil3), and arginase 1 (Arg1).7 Similarly, there was an increase in M2a gene expression stimulated by hypoxia in Irs2−/− macrophages.8

We also observed that IRS2 suppressed eosinophilic inflammation induced by immunization with ovalbumin (OVA)/aluminum hydroxide (Alum) and challenge with inhaled soluble OVA.7 Global IRS2 deficiency led to a dramatic increase in the total number of cells in the BAL of OVA/Alum-treated mice. There was an increase in tissue eosinophils and M2a macrophages in the lungs of Irs2−/− mice compared to Irs2+/+. However, the loss of IRS2 did not affect lung epithelial goblet cell differentiation or mucus production.

M2a protein products including HIMF-1 (product of the Retnla gene) and arginase-1 have been shown to promote airway and vascular remodeling.14–16 We demonstrated that the majority of small (≤ 80 µm) pulmonary vessels in OVA/Alum primed Irs2+/+ mice were non-muscularized (NM).7 However, global IRS2-deficient mice exhibited a significant decrease in the fraction of NM vessels, with a concomitant increase in the percentage of small vessels with partially or fully muscularized phenotypes. In addition, Irs2+/− mice showed a gene dosage effect; they developed intermediate levels of pulmonary vascular remodeling. A similar impact of IRS2-deficiency was observed in response to hypoxia.8 Irs2−/− mice demonstrated enhanced pulmonary remodeling in response to hypoxia that was associated with changes in cardiopulmonary function including remodeling of the heart.

IRS2 is expressed in many cell types in addition to macrophages.13 The adoptive transfer of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMM) isolated from Irs2−/− mice to either WT or Irs2+/− recipients resulted in an increase in the number and percent of eosinophils in the BAL and lung of OVA/alum-primed mice.7 Adoptive transfer of Irs2−/− BMM to Irs2+/+ or Irs2+/− recipients also resulted in increased pulmonary vascular remodeling with the greatest impact observed in the Irs2 heterozygous recipients. However, it did not result in an increase in FIZZ1 staining around small pulmonary vessels above that characteristic of each recipient genotype. These results indicate that enhanced inflammation and remodeling observed in allergen-challenged Irs2−/− mice appears to be, in part, intrinsic to macrophages. However, it is not known whether IRS2 expression in macrophages specifically is sufficient to enhance allergic inflammation or vascular remodeling induced by allergen or hypoxia. Therefore, to evaluate the specific contribution of IRS2 in myeloid cells, we analyzed responses to allergens and hypoxia in mice with conditional IRS2-deficiencies in myeloid cells17 driven by the expression of the Cre recombinase transgene (tg) under control of the LysM promoter.

Materials and methods

Mice

C57BL/6 Irs2fl/fl mice were obtained from Dr. Morris White (Harvard Medical School) and backcrossed on the C57BL/6J genetic background for >7 generations.17,18 These mice were then crossed with C57BL/6J LysM-Cretg/tg (B6.129P2-Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo/J; 004781 JAX19 to generate the C57BL/6 Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt line that is heterozygous for the endogenous wild-type (wt) LysM gene and the Cre recombinase transgene (tg) inserted in the endogenous LysM site and controlled by the LysM-promoter as described.17,19 The mice were bred at the University of Maryland School of Medicine by brother-sister mating of Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt to Irs2fl/flLysM-Crewt/wt (Fig. S1). Mouse genotypes from tail biopsies were determined using real time PCR with specific probes designed for each gene by Transnetyx, (Cordova, Tennessee, USA).18 Progeny are designated as Cre−/− (essentially wild-type [wt] for endogenous LysM) or Cre+/− throughout to indicate the absence or presence of the Cre recombinase transgene in the LysM locus, respectively. In some cases, Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt were interbred to generate Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/tg mice with 2 copies of the Cre recombinase transgene (termed Cre+/+). Both male and female littermates (6–8 wk old) were randomized to treatment groups in all studies. All animal husbandry and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Maryland School of Medicine.

Cell isolation

Cells from untreated mice (4/group) were isolated after euthanization by cervical dislocation. Total splenocytes were prepared by disruption of spleens using a nylon mesh filter. Lung macrophages were prepared by first generating single cell suspensions of lungs by digestion of perfused lungs with collagenase and DNase without a prior bronchoalveolar lavage as described.20 These cells were plated at 2 × 106/mL in the presence of 20 ng/mL MCSF (R&D Systems) for 7 d to establish adherent macrophages. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMM) were obtained as previously described.10

Detection of Cre-mediated deletion of genomic DNA

For detection of Cre-mediated excision of the Irs2 locus, genomic DNA was isolated from the cell populations by heating in 50 mM NaOH at 100 °C for 10 min. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using primers located 1.1 kb upstream of the 5′ loxP site (5′-GGGAACCTGACAAGTGAATG-3′) and 0.2 kb downstream of the 3′ loxP site (loxP 5′-AGTCCACTTTCCTGACAAGC-3′). As reported, these primers amplified a 1.3-kb product only if recombination had occurred.18

Allergic lung inflammation

House dust mite (HDM)-induced allergic response. Mice were treated with HDM allergenic extract (lot no. 360924, Greer Laboratories, endotoxin concentration: 13.14 EU/50 µg HDM dose) as previously described.20 Briefly, 50 µg of extract in 50 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was applied to isofluorane anesthetized mice intranasally 4 times 4 d apart. The resulting inflammatory response was assessed 48 h after the last HDM application.

House dust mite (HDM)-induced allergic response. Mice were treated with HDM allergenic extract (lot no. 360924, Greer Laboratories, endotoxin concentration: 13.14 EU/50 µg HDM dose) as previously described.20 Briefly, 50 µg of extract in 50 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was applied to isofluorane anesthetized mice intranasally 4 times 4 d apart. The resulting inflammatory response was assessed 48 h after the last HDM application.

Ovalbumin (OVA)-induced allergic response. Mice were immunized and challenged with OVA (Endofit OVA, low-endotoxin concentration; InVivoGen Labs) adsorbed to aluminum hydroxide (Alum) as described previously.7,8 Briefly, mice were immunized with either 100 μg of OVA/Alum or Alum alone on day 1 and again on day 6. Mice were challenged with aerosolized PBS or 1% OVA in PBS for 40 minutes each day on days 12 and 14. The assessment of allergic lung response was performed 48h later.

Ovalbumin (OVA)-induced allergic response. Mice were immunized and challenged with OVA (Endofit OVA, low-endotoxin concentration; InVivoGen Labs) adsorbed to aluminum hydroxide (Alum) as described previously.7,8 Briefly, mice were immunized with either 100 μg of OVA/Alum or Alum alone on day 1 and again on day 6. Mice were challenged with aerosolized PBS or 1% OVA in PBS for 40 minutes each day on days 12 and 14. The assessment of allergic lung response was performed 48h later.

Analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)

Mice were anesthetized with Avertin (0.3 mg/kg by i.p. injection). The tracheas of mice were cannulated and lavaged one time with PBS to obtain the BAL samples. The BAL was centrifuged, and the BAL fluid (BALF) was stored at −80 °C. BAL cells were resuspended in PBS and counted using the Countess cell counter (Countess II, ThermoFisher, Bothell, Washington, USA). BAL cells were centrifuged onto slides using the Cytospin (Cytospin 2; Shandon Inc., Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA) and stained with Diff-Quick (Dade Behring, Deerfield, IL). The differential cell counts were determined from 4 high-power fields by an investigator blinded to group identity.20

The levels of BALF cytokines and chemokines were determined using Searchlight Proteome Array (Aushon) at the Cytokine Core Lab, Center for Innovative Biomedical Resources (CIBR), University of Maryland School of Medicine. The array data were analyzed using the ArrayVision software.

Histochemistry and score-based quantification of lung histology

After the BAL procedure, mouse heart and lungs were perfused with 10–15 mL of PBS and immediately fixed with 10% formalin. Heart and lung sections were embedded in paraffin and processed at the University of Maryland School of Medicine’s Pathology Histology core within CIBR; lung sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) or Periodic acid Schiff (PAS) of deparaffinized lung tissues. Lung sections were evaluated by an investigator blinded to the experimental conditions. H&E and PAS stains were evaluated as previously described:9 for H&E, (0) no signs of inflammation; (1) light and dispersed infiltrate in only a few areas of the section; (2) modest infiltrate surrounding major vessels and airways, with little in distal airway unit; (3) moderate infiltrate surrounding <50% of distal airways and vessels; and (4) heavy and focused eosinophilic infiltrate surrounding numerous distal vessels and airways, signs of increased alveolar macrophage numbers with macrophage fusions and extracellular matrix deposition. For PAS: (0) no signs of mucus in airways or elevated numbers of PAS+ cells; (1) no mucus in airways, slight increase in numbers of PAS+ cells in a few major airways; (2) some mucus detectable in major airways; (3) mucus detected in major airways with <50% of distal airway epithelial cells PAS+ in distal airway units; and (4) mucus plugging of airways observed in several airways and majority of distal airway epithelial cells are PAS+ in multiple distal airways per section.

For immunohistochemistry, lung sections were stained with rabbit anti-mouse smooth muscle cell actin (SMA) (1:100; Abcam, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA) followed by biotinylated anti-rabbit antibodies (1:200; Vector Laboratories Inc.). Slides were then incubated with ABC Elite reagent (Vector Laboratories) and developed in 3,3-diaminobenzidine chromogen and counterstained with Mayer’s Hematoxylin. The slides were evaluated by an investigator blinded to the experimental conditions and scored for SMA staining in small (≤ 80 µm) pulmonary vessel walls. For SMA: (0) no signs of staining; (1) light staining of small pulmonary vessels with very thin walls; (2) slight increase in thickness of vessel wall staining in <50% of small vessels; (3) increased staining and vessel wall thickness in >50% of small vessels; and (4) dramatic thickening of vessel walls in numerous instances.

Hypoxia induction

In vivo hypoxia induction was performed as described previously.8,21 Briefly, mice were placed in a Plexiglas chamber maintained at 10% O2 or in a chamber open to room air for 3-weeks with a 12:12-h, light-dark cycle. Oxygen concentration was regulated by solenoid-controlled nitrogen flow linked to a Clark electrode (Pro-Ox controller; BioSpherix, Redfield, New York, USA). The chambers were continuously scavenged for CO2 and ammonia. Mice were euthanized by exsanguination, and the lungs were removed en bloc. For histology, the left lung lobe was inflated under constant pressure with 1% low-melting-point agarose in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and placed on ice. The inflated lung was then placed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently processed for histology as described previously.21,22

Hemodynamic analysis and Fulton index measurement

Right ventricular function of mice was assessed in vivo by a pressure-volume catheter as described previously.8 Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 75 mg/kg intraperitoneal (i.p.) urethane, 5 mg/kg i.p. etomidate, and 1 mg/kg i.p. morphine. Then they underwent tracheostomy and were ventilated with 6–7 μL/g tidal volume at 130 breaths/min. Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) measurements were recorded and data were analyzed with the AD Instruments Powerlab 8/35 (AD Instruments, Colorado Springs, Colorado, USA) and Millar MPVS Ultra (Millar Instruments, Houston, Texas, USA). Hypoxia-induced right heart hypertrophy was also assessed by echocardiogram by the Animal Physiology Core facility in CIBR and by analysis of the Fulton Index.22 Echocardiograms were performed on live mice using the VevoF2 imaging system (Visual Sonics). The right ventricular internal diameter was measured as described by the manufacturer. For the Fulton Index, the heart was dissected free of all major vessels, separated into right ventricle (RV) and left ventricle (LV) plus septum (S), and weighed. The Fulton index (RV/LV+S) was determined as described previously.8

qRT-PCR

The right lung lobe from treated mice was stored at −80 °C prior to extraction of total lung RNA using TRIzol Plus RNA Purification Kit (cat. no. 12183555, Invitrogen).23 Briefly, the lung tissues samples were placed in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent and homogenized using an immersion homogenizer (Omni International, Southern Labware, Cumming, Georgia, USA). Chloroform and 4-bromoanisole additions were used for lysis, and aqueous solution with RNA was treated with ethanol for precipitation. The PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen) was used for RNA membrane-binding and elution. In brief, iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad) was used for the first strand DNA syntheses using a Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) was used for qRT-PCR workflow on QuantStudio 3 (Applied Biosystems). Specific primer pairs were used to amplify target genes including Hypoxanthine Phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (Hprt1), Chitinase-like protein 3 (Chil3), mannose receptor (Mrc1), Relmα (Retnla), and arginase 1 (Arg1) shown in Fig. S2 (Integrated DNA Technologies). The expression of the target gene was calculated using the − ΔCt method,24 with the expression level relative to Hprt1 as the reference gene. RNA and DNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific).

Statistics

Data were summarized as mean ± SEM. Differences between experimental groups were compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test (GraphPad Prizm8) as indicated in the figure legends.

Results

While we previously demonstrated that global loss of IRS2 resulted in enhanced lung inflammation and vascular remodeling induced by allergen or hypoxia and macrophage adoptive transfer experiments suggested that IRS2-expressed in macrophages was in part responsible,7,8 IRS2 is expressed in other cell types that may contribute to the phenotype observed in global Irs2−/− mice.13,25 In addition, the global Irs2−/− mice develop elevated blood glucose as they age due to the loss of pancreatic β cells which may confound experimental results.26 To evaluate the specific contribution of IRS2 in a cell type-specific manner, we analyzed responses in mice with conditional IRS2-deficiencies in myeloid cells by crossing Irs2fl/fl mice to LysM-Cretg/tg (Fig. S1). The mice were intercrossed by brother-sister mating of Irs2fl/flLysM-Crewt/wt × Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt to generate experimental Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt (Cre+/−; 50%), and control Irs2fl/flLysMwt/wt (Cre−/−; 50%) littermates as shown (Fig. S1). The designation Cre−/− indicates a lack of the Cre recombinase, indicating that Irs2 could not be excised. Male and female littermates of similar ages (6–8 wk old) were randomized to treatment groups and evaluated and compared in all experiments.

We confirmed the loss of the Irs2 gene in adherent lung macrophages and MCSF-dependent, bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMM) by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of genomic DNA (Fig. 1A). Lung macrophages and BMM from Cre+/− mice, but not from Cre−/− mice, demonstrated a product using primers located 1.1 kb upstream of the 5′ loxP site and 0.2 kb downstream of the 3′ loxP site in the targeted Irs2 gene.18 We also generated Cre+/+ mice by brother-sister mating of Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt × Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt to compare Irs2 deletion in lung macrophages prepared from Cre+/− and Cre+/+ mice. The Cre heterozygotes demonstrated efficient deletion of Irs2 comparable to Cre homozygotes (Fig. 1A).

We next evaluated IL-4-induced IRS2 signaling comparing Cre−/− to Cre+/− in BMM or total spleen cells (Fig. 1B). IL-4 failed to induce the tyrosine phosphorylation of IRS2 in BMM derived from Cre+/− mice while it was able to do so in BMM from Cre−/− mice, yet phosphorylation of STAT6 was readily observable in both macrophage genotypes. In contrast, in total splenocytes, a mixed population of cells known to express IRS2 including B cells,27 the IL-4-induced phosphorylation of IRS2 and STAT6 was observable in both Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. Analysis of tyrosine phosphorylation is a very sensitive readout for IRS2 as it is heavily phosphorylated and direct anti-IRS2 protein reagents did not blot well.27

To examine the contribution of myeloid-cell IRS2 to allergic lung inflammation, we first utililized the classic OVA/Alum prime, boost, challenge model (Fig. 2).9 OVA/Alum stimulated hallmark features of allergic inflammation compared to the Alum control groups in both Cre−/− and Cre+/− littermates with no significant differences between the genotypes. OVA/Alum induced an increase in eosinophil representation in the BAL with a concomitant decrease in macrophage representation as expected (Fig. 2A) with modest impact on neutrophils and lymphocytes (Fig. S3) in Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. It also stimulated increases in the levels of IL-4 and IL-5 in the BAL fluid similarly in both genotypes. IL-13 was below the limit of detection in all samples. Furthermore, analysis of lung inflammation and mucus production by histochemistry (Fig. 2B, C) demonstrated OVA/Alum-induced increases in lung inflammation and mucus staining with PAS in both Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. In addition, we observed similar OVA/Alum-induced increases in the abundance of the IL-4-inducible genes Retnla (FIZZ1), Chil3 (YM1), Arg1(arginase 1), and Mrc1 (CD206) in lungs of Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice (Fig. 3).

We next compared responses to the complex human allergen house dust mite (HDM) extract. Similar to the OVA/Alum responses, we observed that HDM treatment induced several features of allergic inflammation in both Cre−/− and Cre+/− littermates with no significant differences between the genotypes, with one notable exception (Figs. 4–6). HDM induced an increase in eosinophil representation in the BAL with a concomitant decrease in macrophage representation (Fig. 4A), with modest impact on neutrophils and lymphocytes (Fig. S3) in Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. It also stimulated increases in IL-4 and IL-5 levels in the BAL fluid similarly in both genotypes. Furthermore, analysis of lung inflammation and mucus production by histochemistry (Fig. 4B, C) demonstrated HDM-induced increases in lung inflammation and mucus staining with PAS in both Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. The HDM-induced increases in the abundance of the IL-4-inducible genes Retnla (FIZZ1), Chil3 (YM1), and Arg1(arginase 1), were similar in the lungs of Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice, while Mrc1 (CD206) was significantly reduced in lungs of Cre+/− mice (Fig. 5).

However, we observed evidence of enhanced airway smooth muscle cell (SMC) layer thickening in HDM-treated Cre+/− mice when compared to the Cre−/− mice in the PAS-stained sections (Fig. S4A). This enhanced thickening was confirmed by immunohistochemistry with anti-SMC-actin (SMA) (Fig. S4B). We previously reported that a major impact of global IRS2 deficiency was enhanced remodeling of small pulmonary vessels (<80 µM) with enhanced muscularization that was mediated, in part, by macrophages.7 Therefore, we evaluated the impact of myeloid-cell deletion of IRS2 on remodeling of small, pulmonary vessels (Fig. 6A, B). Control, PBS-treated Cre+/− and Cre−/− mice showed light staining of SMA in small, pulmonary blood vessels with an average staining score of 1 for both genotypes. HDM treatment induced a significant increase in SMA staining of small vessels in Cre−/− mice to an average staining score of 2.3. However, in the Cre+/− mice, HDM-induced the staining score to 3.5, significantly higher than observed in the Cre−/− mice. These results suggest that IRS2 expressed in myeloid cells modulates the remodeling, muscularization of small pulmonary vessels in response to HDM.

We previously reported that global Irs2−/− mice demonstrated enhanced pulmonary vascular remodeling and right heart hypertrophy in response to hypoxia.8 We observed similar changes in the lungs of mice exposed to OVA/Alum (Fig. 7A). Allergic asthma does not typically lead to changes in right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) that leads to the development of pulmonary hypertension (PH) in humans; however, pulmonary arterial remodeling was induced by a Th2 response in a mouse model of chronic allergen exposure.28 We detected a comparable, small increase in mean RVSP in both Irs2+/+ and global Irs2−/− mice primed and challenged with OVA/Alum (Fig. 7A). Interestingly, the Irs2−/− mice demonstrated greater OVA-induced changes in the heart; right ventricular hypertrophy indicated by the Fulton Index was significantly increased in Irs2−/− mice compared to Irs2+/+ mice suggesting that IRS2 limits cardiovascular changes in the setting of allergen exposure. We further evaluated hypoxia-induced changes in the Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt mice (Fig. 7B). We observed that hypoxia induced a similar increase in RVSP in Cre+/− and Cre−/− mice. However, right ventricular hypertrophy indicated by the Fulton Index and by measure of right ventricular internal diameter (RVID) by echocardiogram was significantly greater in Cre+/− mice compared to Cre−/− mice. These results suggest the IRS2 signaling adaptor in myeloid cells specifically dampens cardiovascular remodeling induced by hypoxia.

Discussion

We initially predicted that deletion of Irs2 in myeloid cells would result in enhanced proinflammatory responses to allergen including increases in eosinophilia. However, we did not observe consistent, significant differences in allergen-induced inflammation between Irs2fl/flLysM-Crewt/wt (Cre−/−) and Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt (Cre+/−) littermates. Our previous report demonstrated that adoptive transfer of IRS2-expressing macrophages was sufficient to limit the lung inflammatory responses induced by allergen.7 Our current studies indicate that IRS2-expressing myeloid cells may not be necessary to limit the inflammatory responses if they are in the presence of other IRS2-expressing cell types. IRS2 is expressed by many other cell types in the lung and colocalizes with pulmonary vasculature in human lungs.8,13 Multiple studies suggest the importance of cross-talk between macrophages and other lung cell types for maximal inflammation and remodeling in models of Th2-driven inflammation.7,9,29 Consistent with this idea, we previously observed that transfer of Irs2−/− macrophages supported significantly more vascular remodeling in Irs2+/− recipient mice than in Irs2+/+ mice7 indicating that other IRS2-expressing cell types contribute to the degree of vascular remodeling.

In both the allergen and hypoxia models, the inflammatory and vascular remodeling responses were dependent upon IL-4 and the IL-4 Receptor.9–11, 22 Since IRS2 is a critical signaling adaptor for IL-4 Receptor signaling,12 we believe the effects of LysM-Cre-mediated deletion of Irs2 are mediated downstream of the IL-4–IL-4 receptor axis. However, IRS2 plays a role in signaling by the insulin and insulin-like growth factor receptors that control many aspects of metabolism.13 Indeed, Rached et al. reported that deletion of Irs2 in macrophages resulted in enhanced systemic metabolism in mice due to increased activity in brown adipose tissue activated by adipose tissue catecholamine release.17 This was correlated with changes in macrophage phenotypes including a markedly reduced pro-inflammatory profile, and the expression of genes involved in catecholamine scavenging leading to cross-talk with adipose tissue and sympathetic innervation.17 Changes in macrophage metabolism were not specifically measured. It is possible alterations in systemic metabolism could influence vascular remodeling. Since macrophage metabolic states have been linked to phenotype (glycolysis to M1 and oxidative phosphorylation to M2),30 it is also possible the loss of IRS2 in macrophages would shift metabolism of myeloid cells and alter their phenotype and production of soluble mediators.

While our previous in vitro analyses demonstrated enhanced M2a gene expression in macrophages in the complete absence of IRS27,,8 for the most part, there was no significant difference in the abundance of allergen-induced M2a genes in total lung mRNA isolated from Cre+/− or Cre−/− mice, with the exception of Mrc1 in HDM-treated mice (Figs. 3 and 5). Such an analysis of bulk lung mRNA would include many cell types in addition to lung macrophages, including epithelial cells known to produce many of the same genes characteristic of M2a macrophages, potentially masking changes in gene expression that may be occurring in individual myeloid cell subsets that deleted Irs2. It is also possible that there may be other gene products and cell-cell communication between myeloid, epithelial, endothelial, and smooth muscle cells leading to the enhanced vascular remodeling observed in mice with myeloid-targeted deletion of Irs2.

We did observe significant differences between Cre+/− and Cre−/− mice in vascular remodeling in the lungs after HDM exposure and in right ventricular remodeling of the heart under hypoxic conditions. These results indicate that IRS2 expression in myeloid cells limits remodeling responses that are induced under allergic inflammation or hypoxia. Pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular hypertrophy are hallmarks of PH, and macrophages are known to contribute to the progression of PH by driving perivascular inflammation and vascular remodeling through the release of growth factors and cytokines.31,32 Current models suggest that pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages responding to an inflammatory stimulus drive disease onset, while the later accumulation of M2 macrophages stimulate tissue repair with pathologic remodeling through the production of PDGF, VEGF, and Arginase 131. We previously showed that hypoxic conditions significantly increased Arg1 and Vegfa expression in global Irs2−/− macrophages compared to Irs2+/+ macrophages.8 The fact that IRS2 expression is significantly reduced in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with PH compared to control subjects further supports the idea that myeloid expression of IRS2 limits vascular and cardiac remodeling responses.33

The abnormal remodeling of pulmonary vessels under conditions of hypoxia are thought to begin with vascular endothelial cell injury and elevated proliferation of vascular SMCs31 A decrease in the availability of nitric oxide (NO) is one of the major drivers of pulmonary SMC constriction and thus vascular contraction.31,32 NO is generated through the metabolism of L-arginine to citrulline by nitric oxide synthases (NOS).34 On the other hand, the enzyme arginase 1 converts L-arginine to ornithine and polyamines at the expense of NO. Thus, we speculate that the enhanced production of arginase 1 by perivascular Irs2−/− macrophages could contribute to the vascular smooth muscle cell thickening observed in the lungs of the myeloid-specific Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt (Cre+/−) mice.

Several reports suggest that targeting macrophage polarization may offer therapeutic benefits for PH by reducing inflammation and improving right ventricular function.32 It has been shown that inhibiting or depleting macrophages reduces right ventricular burden and ameliorates PH. Furthermore, adoptive transfer of regulatory macrophages producing high levels of IL-10 was shown to reduce vascular remodeling and severity of PH35. Interestingly, these protective, regulatory macrophages were prepared in the presence of IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β, and share expression of some genes also characteristic of bona fide M2a (or IL-4-activated) macrophages including the macrophage mannose receptor (CD206) and FIZZ1. Our current study, as well as previous publications, suggest that IRS2 dampens expression of M2a genes to minimize pathologic remodeling responses.7,8 The differential results may be due to timing as the adoptive transfer of regulatory macrophages was only protective for PH if they were provided during the early phase of hypoxia exposure35 when induction of disease promoting M1 macrophages is thought to occur.31 It will be interesting and important to compare full gene expression profiles of macrophages early and late in the hypoxia-induced model in the presence and absence of IRS2 to identify sets of disease-promoting and disease-mitigating genes.

The development of right heart hypertrophy in PH is typically accompanied by a measurable increase in RVSP31,32 While we did observe an increase in RVSP in response to hypoxia or allergen, there was no significant difference in RSVP between Cre−/− and Cre+/− mice. However, there was a significant difference in right heart remodeling as indicated by changes in RVID and Fulton Index. Interestingly, macrophage infiltration of the heart has been linked to maladaptive remodeling of the RV, including hypertrophy and fibrosis.32 In addition, loss of IRS2, or IRS1, in cardiac cells leads to pathological cardiac remodeling and heart failure.36 Thus, the IRS2 adaptor may act in multiple cell types including infiltrating myeloid cells or cardiac cells themselves to control cardiac remodeling in response to stressors such as hypoxia.

Our studies have focused on the contribution of macrophages,7–10 and as discussed above, the literature supports a role for macrophages in lung, vascular, and cardiac remodeling. However, LysM-driven Cre expression can support deletion of genes in neutrophils and dendritic cells as well.19 In this study, we did not observe an impact of Irs2 deletion on neutrophilic infiltration (Fig. S3) and we did not directly evaluate dendritic cell numbers or phenotype. While there is little known about the role of IRS2 in neutrophil or dendritic cell function specifically, these cell types have been reported to modulate vascular and cardiac remodeling in other settings.37,38 Dendritic cells, particularly the DC2 subset, have been shown to participate in vascular remodeling that occurs in hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension by orchestrating inflammatory and remodeling responses.37 In addition, neutrophils are reported to promote lung vascular and right ventricular remodeling in response to hypoxia by the production of S100A9.38 Thus, it is possible the changes in remodeling we observed were also influenced by neutrophils or dendritic cells.

One limitation of these studies is that analysis for efficient deletion of IRS2 in individual lung cell types could not be performed. Importantly, we were able to demonstrate deletion of the Irs2 locus in total lung macrophages and in MCSF-dependent BMM in the Irs2fl/flLysM-Cretg/wt line. LysM-Cre has been used extensively to delete genes in myeloid cells and has been described as a highly specific deleter strain for macrophages.19,39 However, not all myeloid populations in the lungs demonstrated Cre-mediated deletion after exposure to parasite egg antigen.40 Vanella et al showed that the Il4rfl/fl allele was not deleted in monocyte-derived infiltrating lung macrophages during parasite-egg allergen-induced inflammation.40 Thus, the addition of a deletion-specific marker will be important to report Cre-mediated deletion in diverse lung cell types under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions.
