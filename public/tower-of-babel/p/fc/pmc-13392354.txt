Modulated high-frequency stimulation enables precisely timed, selective nerve block
Authors: Philipp Schnepel, Alejandro Crespo, Gabriela Yordanova, Yamiral Bezwork, Vicent Roig, Nicolò Rossetti, Vojkan Mihailović
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) therapies are often constrained by the anatomical and functional complexity of nerves, resulting in limited efficacy and unwanted side effects. This study introduces a novel approach that enhances both spatial and functional selectivity in PNS by precisely controlling parameters of high-frequency sinusoidal stimulation delivered to one set of contacts and trapezoidal blocking pulses delivered to another set of contacts. Using an earthworm model, we demonstrate directional blocking of action potentials and precise temporal control over their propagation. By systematically varying stimulation waveform type, blocking pulse amplitude, and relative timing between the stimulation and blocking, we identified parameters that permit selective inhibition or activation of specific nerve fibers. Our findings show that slow and fast fibers can be independently targeted, and that action potential (AP) propagation can be modulated or blocked without interfering with initiation at the stimulation site. The study further proposes integrating independent anodal blocking with intermittent interferential current stimulation (i2CS), which could enable spatio-temporal control of nerve activity. This framework holds promise for advancing selective neuromodulation strategies in complex mammalian nerves, suggesting that precise control of stimulation and blocking via electrode configuration and waveform modulation can significantly improve the specificity and effectiveness of neuromodulation interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49335-z.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Engineering, Neuroscience

Introduction

Peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) is rapidly emerging as a therapy for many conditions such as epilepsy, depression and motor rehabilitation1–3, and demonstrates considerable potential for additional indications including e.g. overactive bladder4 or inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis5–7. The challenge of achieving therapeutic efficacy without excessive side effects arises from the intricate composition of peripheral nerves such as the vagus nerve (VN), which contain a heterogeneous mix of fibers targeting distinct organs and muscles. This complexity is compounded by the fact that different fiber types exhibit varying activation thresholds that are correlated with the fiber thickness and level of myelination; large fibers often associated with undesired side effects possess lower thresholds, while those targeted for therapeutic modulation typically are smaller and require higher stimulation intensities (order of recruitment). Secondly, fibers close to the edge of the nerve (i.e. close to the electrode contacts) will be activated first compared to fibers that are located more at the center of the nerve (order of activation). Due to these limitations, it is practically impossible to activate target fibers that are located deeper within the nerve without also activating non-target fibers. To enhance the selectivity of electrical PNS, different strategies have been deployed, including multi-contact electrode cuffs8, waveform optimization methods such as interferential current stimulation9,10, and nerve blocking techniques using high-frequency stimulation or anodal block11.

Multi-contact electrodes can enhance selectivity when target fibers are close to one of the contacts; however, they do not address the ‘order of activation’ challenge for deeper fibers. Continuous temporal interference stimulation (TIS) operates on a distinct principle, applying high-frequency sinusoidal electrical fields with slightly different carrier frequencies at two or more contacts to produce a spatial pattern of interference in the tissue with a low amplitude modulated (‘beat’) frequency. Neurons close to the contacts respond to the pure sinusoidal component, while those at the focal point of interference are activated by the beat frequency component. In conventional TIS, prolonged exposure to high-frequency sinusoidal stimulation may result in a depolarization block of neurons near the contacts, although tonic and phasic firing patterns persist for neurons at varying depths12–14. This characteristic can limit the method’s ability to resolve order of activation and order of recruitment problems.

The i2CS approach, a variant of TIS, relies on brief (< 1 ms), high-frequency intermittent stimulation shown to achieve greater selectivity via an alternative effect: the sinusoidal component activates fibers close to the contact, but fibers at the interference focus require higher current stimulation amplitudes and are activated with delays due to reduced charge in the amplitude modulated waveform compared to the pure sinusoidal waveform9. Although facing similar limitations as TIS for order of recruitment and activation, its advantage is the high temporal precision and control of single APs. Conversely, classic anodal blocking techniques adjust the anodal segment of a biphasic stimulation pulse to selectively block fiber activation in one direction while maintaining it in the other11. High-frequency methods depend on depolarization block, which occurs when neurons or fibers are driven to fire at elevated frequencies until available voltage-activated sodium channels for action potential generation become depleted15,16.

Although these techniques have shown promising results for achieving directional blocking of activity or general suppression of responses across a broad spectrum of fibers, the ability to block the activity of certain fibers (e.g. carrying a side-effect) while stimulating other fibers (e.g. carrying the target effect) has not been achieved yet in modern neuromodulation approaches17. These limitations stem from the fundamental mechanisms of how axons of nerve fibers respond to electrical stimulation, which can be effectively studied in simple animal models such as earthworms. The basic physiology underlying action potential (AP) generation in these models closely resembles that of mammalian nerves18,19, offering valuable insights into the mechanisms of action potential activation and blocking within nerve fibers.

In this study, we sought to test two primary hypotheses: (1) evoked compound action potentials (eCAPs) initiated by a high-frequency (5 kHz) stimulation waveform delivered to a set of stimulation electrodes can be abolished through appropriately timing a monopolar anodal blocking waveform delivered to a separate electrode at a different location; and (2) the effective temporal window for blocking depends on the precise timing of eCAP initiation, which can be controlled via the appropriate choice of modulation of the sinusoidal stimulation waveform. Finally, we propose a framework that integrates independent anodal blocking with temporally precise spatial steering using intermittent interferential current stimulation (i2CS) to achieve improved fiber selectivity for peripheral nerve stimulation approaches.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation and experimental setup

N = 27 earthworms were immobilized in a 12.5% ethanol solution for 10–12 min to suppress movement while preserving neural excitability20,21. Anesthetized worms were then placed as straight as possible in a custom-made, V-shaped holder that allowed stimulation and recording in different configurations (Figs. 1A, S 1A). A linear array of stainless-steel push pins was used as electrodes for stimulation, blocking, referencing and recording. A grounded piece of tinfoil was positioned between stimulation and recording sites to shield the recording from artefact noise. The return anode for the blocking cathode was also realized as a large piece of tinfoil to minimize unwanted activation. All experiments were conducted at room temperature (~ 22–23 °C) in a climate-controlled room.

(A) Recording setup for earthworm experiments. The earthworm is anesthetized in 15% alcohol solution and placed in a holder (see Fig. S 1). Household pins with 1 cm distance are used as electrodes. Bipolar stimulation is controlled by a NI-DAQ system and implemented via independent Howland current sources connected to neighboring anode and cathode pins (red). The blocking anode is connected to a pin next to the stimulation cathode while the return electrode is realized as a tinfoil sheet at the head of the worm. Data is recorded with an Intan RHS system. To reduce artifact noise, a grounded tinfoil sheet is placed between the stimulation and recording electrodes. (B) Waveforms used for stimulation (sinusoidal or modulated, SIN/MOD) and blocking (pulse + ramp for charge-balancing) and positioning of the electrodes relative to the nerve fibers (medial and lateral giant fibers, MGF/LGF). SIN and MOD waveforms are characterized by their frequency and amplitude, while the blocking pulse is characterized mainly by amplitude (AMP) and pulse width (PW), with a fixed ramp return to baseline (RAMP1 = 0.25 ms) and a fixed ramp for repolarization and charge balancing (RAMP2 = 10 ms) as well as an interphase gap (IG, 15 ms) to disconnect the blocking part from the charge-balancing part in the time domain. (C) Initial test stimulations with both the sinusoidal and blocking pulse to determine the threshold for subsequent experiments. Red traces denote no response. Arrows mark the MGF and LGF response, respectively. Threshold stimulation values are determined as at least two amplitude steps above the minimum needed to elicit a response (e.g. 4 mA, bold). Threshold values for the blocking pulse are defined as at least one amplitude step above the minimum needed to elicit a response (e.g. 0.65 mA, bold). Scale bars are 2 ms / 75 µV.

Stimulation and recording instrumentation

Stimulation waveforms were generated with a Digital-Analog Converter card (NI-PCIe-6363 DAC, National Instruments, TX, USA) and converted to currents by independent Howland current sources9. Recordings were obtained with an Intan-based data acquisition system (RHS Stim/Recording system RHS2116, Intan Technologies, CA, USA); signals were acquired with a bandwidth of 0.19 Hz to 9.99 kHz prior to digitization at 30 kHz. Control and data acquisition were implemented with Python for hardware control and the Intan acquisition software for real-time streaming and data storage.

Stimulation paradigms and control experiments

The stimulation electrodes elicit a CAP that travels bidirectionally towards the head and the tail of the animal. In the blocking experiment (depicted in Fig. 1A), the blocking cathode is placed between the stimulation and recording electrodes to block the CAP at the tail of the worm. In the control experiment, recording electrodes near the head are used to record the CAP that is conducted when using the same stimulation/blocking parameters (with the return electrode for the blocking cathode being placed at the tail end of the worm). In both blocking and control experiments, the two stimulus conditions being compared are ‘sinusoidal’ (SIN, 5 kHz carrier frequency, 1 ms pulse width) or ‘amplitude modulated sinusoidal’ (MOD, based on a carrier frequency of 5 kHz and a beat frequency of 500 Hz, 1 ms pulse width) waveforms to control the timing of the initiation of the CAP (Fig. 1B). The type of stimulation waveform and the amplitude and timing of the blocking pulse (amplitude range: 0.1-1 mA; fixed pulse width of 0.5 ms + 0.25 ms ramp return to baseline) were changed systematically (Fig. S 1B). To determine the appropriate stimulus intensities for both stimulation and blocking, thresholding experiments were performed before and either during or after the main experiment to ensure the stability of the experimental conditions (Fig. 1B). For both stimulation waveforms, an amplitude value two steps above threshold was chosen to ensure stable generation of CAPs (e.g. 4 mA in the example in Fig. 1C). For the blocking pulse, the threshold was defined as one amplitude step above the minimum needed to elicit a response (i.e. 0.65 mA in the example in Fig. 1C). This value was further used as the reference for the different steps of amplitude values used in the main experiment (i.e. 80%, 120% and 200% of threshold). The delay between stimulation and blocking pulses varied systematically from − 0.3 ms (blocking preceding stimulus) to 1.5 ms (blocking following stimulus) in increments of 0.075 ms, yielding 25 discrete delay values. Stimulus presentations were randomly interleaved, with a 10 s pause between stimulations.

Pre-processing and data analysis

Offline signal pre-processing included an 8th order high-pass Butterworth filter at 250 Hz cutoff. Each stimulation train was 2 s long and comprised 20 repetitions at 10 Hz; repetitions were averaged to yield a single trace for template matching. To determine if blocking occurred at a given combination of amplitude and delay, eCAPs were detected using template matching with a flipped second-order Hermite–Rodriguez wavelet, chosen for its similarity to biphasic eCAP waveforms22,23. The signal was clipped at 2 ms to remove the stimulation artefact, and both signal and template were normalized before cross-correlation. Peaks were accepted if they surpassed the mean+3xSD (standard deviation), exceeded 10 µV in amplitude, and met a 2 ms refractory period criterion to prevent duplicate detections. Each amplitude/delay bin in the parameter space (3 × 25) was thus labeled with a 0 or 1 to denote the presence of a CAP. The effective blocking window (EBW) was determined by applying a median filter of length 3 to each ‘row’ of this binary representation to create a continuous window and filter out spurious failures in blocking. The minimum length of the EBW was set to 2 consecutive delay bins. Population analysis was performed on measures of the start, center and duration of the EBW for both conditions (sinusoidal and modulated) across all subjects. Normality of the resulting distributions was assessed using the Shapiro Wilk test. Depending on the outcome, either parametric (Student’s t tests for paired or independent samples) or nonparametric (Wilcoxon signed rank test) comparisons were performed at a significance level of α = 0.05. Data analysis was performed in MATLAB (2025a, Mathworks, CA, USA).

Computational modeling

The 3D computational model was developed using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 (COMSOL Inc., Burlington, MA). The model was constructed within a cylindrical medium (extracellular fluid) with radius of 20 mm, whose outer surface was set to ground. The muscle and endoneurium of the worm were defined as cylindrical domains with radius 5 mm and 1 mm respectively, and a total length of 100 mm. A single line, at the center, was used for the computation of the electric potential from the two electrodes (platinum), positioned perpendicularly with respect to the muscle, approximately 1 mm away from its upper surface. The mesh size was pre-defined as very fine with maximum and minimum element sizes of 2 mm and 0.02 mm, respectively. The extracted potentials from COMSOL were post-processed in MATLAB, where they were conditioned and combined by a positive (1 mA) and a negative (-1 mA) sign for the cathodic and the anodic stimulation. The activating function (AF) was derived from the first and second derivatives of the obtained potential profiles using MATLAB’s finite-difference gradient function. The main hyperpolarization point was taken as the minimum negative peak of the activating function, while the virtual cathode points were taken as the maximum after the hyperpolarization peak.

Results

To evaluate our hypotheses, we stimulated earthworms (n = 27) and recorded the resulting eCAP responses in a custom-made setup (Fig. 1A). The stimulation pulse consisted of either sinusoidal (SIN) or amplitude modulated sinusoidal (MOD) waveforms delivered to a stimulation electrode pair, maintaining consistent amplitude and pulse width across trials (Fig. 1B). An anodal blocking pulse was delivered to a third electrode site. Before performing the main experiment that included stimulation and blocking, the threshold for activating the median and lateral giant fibers (MGF and LGF) was determined for all waveforms used (Fig. 1C). LGF fibers exhibited a generally higher threshold for activation than MGF fibers and were only present in a subset of worms (n = 5).

While keeping the stimulation amplitude and pulse width fixed to ensure CAP generation, we systematically varied the amplitude (3 levels) and relative delay (25 intervals) of the blocking pulse in n = 27 earthworms (see Figs. 2, S 1B). Our data revealed that specific combinations of waveform type, blocking amplitude, and timing produced robust inhibition CAPs, typically manifesting as a distinct temporal ‘effective blocking window’ (EBW). Notably, the EBW was shifted depending on the stimulation waveform: modulated waveforms generally resulted in a delayed onset of effective blocking compared to sinusoidal stimulation (Fig. 2B, cf. Fig. S 3). Control recordings from electrodes placed near the worm’s head, using identical stimulation and blocking parameters, verified that CAPs were successfully initiated in both directions and that blocking occurred selectively on one side. This finding ruled out the possibility that the blocking pulse interfered with CAP initiation at the stimulation site. Additionally, the effectiveness and duration of CAP blocking scaled positively with increasing blocking pulse amplitude (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, an amplitude of 200% above threshold was the most effective in achieving blocking without generating a CAP by itself. One hypothesis for this effect is that the electric field of the blocking and stimulating electrodes together creates an activating function24 along the fibers that effectively reduces the likelihood of initiation of a CAP by the blocking cathode (Fig. S 2). The activating function indicates the likelihood of a neural fiber generating an action potential due to extracellular stimulation. Positive values mark depolarization areas (likely to fire), while negative values mark hyperpolarization regions (likely to inhibit firing). In a simple anodal monopolar setup, a main hyperpolarization lobe (anodic stimulation) is flanked by two depolarization regions called virtual cathodes (Fig. S 2 C). Blocking action potentials requires higher currents than activation, so even low virtual cathode amplitudes can trigger activation. The AF ratio (ratio between the absolute values of the main hyperpolarization lobe and the virtual cathode) serves as a measure of anodal blocking efficiency: higher ratios favor blocking, while lower ratios may cause unwanted activation at the virtual cathode location. Adding a second electrode of opposite polarity increases the AF ratio, allowing higher blocking currents before activation at the virtual cathode occurs (Fig. S 2D–F).

(A) Example MGF responses for the concomitant presentation of stimulation and blocking pulse at different amplitudes (y-axis, in % of blocking pulse threshold value) and delays between the stimulation and blocking pulse (x-axis, range: -0.3-1.5 ms). The top row shows the responses to modulated stimulation + blocking while the bottom row shows the responses to sinusoidal stimulation + blocking. The right column of responses depicts the control recordings performed at the head of the worm to ensure that an eCAP was generated with the parameter settings of the blocking experiment (see Methods). Scale bar is 2 ms / 60 µV. (B) Same as in A but combining the data across n = 27 worms as a measure of eCAP probability (normalized eCAP occurrence for each bin in the parameters space).

This higher AF ratio could be the reason why during in vivo experiments blocking is possible at amplitudes higher than the activation threshold for the blocking pulse alone. Focusing thus on the highest blocking amplitude tested (200% of threshold), we quantified the EBW’s size and position for both sinusoidal and modulated stimulation conditions (Fig. 3). Statistical analysis demonstrated significant shifts in both the EBW’s onset and center between waveforms. For sinusoidal stimulation, the EBW started at 0.06 ± 0.21 ms and was centered at 0.30 ± 0.14 ms, while for modulated stimulation, the EBW started at 0.50 ± 0.21 ms and was centered at 0.70 ± 0.15 ms (Fig. 3B,C). The overall size of the EBW, however, did not differ significantly between the two waveform types (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, we also observed that the CAP responses would start to be delayed first before being completely blocked, which indicates the possibility to modulate the timing of not only the blocking, but also the CAP themselves (Fig. S 3).

(A) Blocking windows for sinusoidal (red) and modulated (green) stimulation for all worms. (B) Distribution of the difference between both stimulation conditions for the start (left) and center (right) of the blocking window. (C) Mean window starts and centers between sinusoidal and modulated stimulation are significantly different from each other (p = 5.22e-6 and p = 5.38e-6, respectively, signrank test). (D) The size of the effective blocking window is similar between conditions (p = 0.61, signrank test).

In a subset of experiments, where stable LGF responses could be observed (n = 5 worms), we assessed the impact of blocking on both MGF and LGF responses (Figs. 4, S 4). In one representative case (Fig. 4A), negative delay settings (-0.075 ms) and sinusoidal stimulation ‘gated’ LGF CAPs to propagate while selectively blocking MGF CAPs. Conversely, at more positive delay settings (1.5 ms) and with modulated stimulation, the blocking effect could be shifted to ‘gate’ MGF propagation while inhibiting LGF responses. At intermediate delay settings (0.9 ms), the effects would be turned around for each condition: LGF are now blocked with sinusoidal stimulation while MGF are blocked by modulated stimulation. Another example (Fig. 4B) demonstrated that certain parameter combinations could be used to ’gate’ only LGF responses initiated by the modulated waveform.

(A) Example responses at the 200% threshold level showing MGF and LGF responses to SIN (top) and MOD (bottom) stimulation. Blocking can be effective at different delay values for MGF and LGF, enabling fiber-selective blocking by choosing the right combination of stimulation waveform and delay (shaded bins), e.g. at 0.9 ms delay, fast MGF fibers activated by SIN and slow LGF fibers elicited by MOD stimulation will be ‘gated’. (B) similar to A, but for a different animal where only slow LGF fibers are ‘gated’ at a delay of 0.6 ms.

These results indicate that it is possible to control selectivity by exposing the fibers to the right combination of waveform and blocking pulse delay.

Discussion

This study presents a proof-of-concept for integrating a precisely timed anodal blocking waveform with the controlled temporal activation of nerve fibers, utilizing high-frequency sinusoidal and amplitude-modulated sinusoidal stimulation waveforms in a simple animal model. However, the full potential of this approach can only be realized by applying true interferential stimulation paradigms that utilize multiple electrode contacts facilitating spatio-temporal control of the wave shape as it is rendered within the tissue9. In spatial current steering techniques such as i2CS, the ‘beat’ waveform will be rendered strongest at the focus of interference. This spatial focus can be controlled by changing the relative amplitude of the current sources. Through optimizing the parameters of the stimulation waveforms (e.g. pulse width, amplitude, beat frequency, relative amplitude, number and arrangement of electrode contacts) and precise timing of the blocking pulse, different fibers with different conduction velocities can be targeted for either stimulation or blocking, overcoming the ‘order of recruitment’ problem. Furthermore, the combination of these approaches could allow the spatial ‘steering’ of the EBW to certain parts of the nerve (Fig. 5). This would be of essential importance for ‘gating’ fibers located deeper within peripheral nerves, thus overcoming the ‘order of activation’ problem (Fig. 5).

Schematic of combining i2CS with anodal block in a more complex peripheral nerve. i2CS stimulation at t = t0 leads to initiation of APs in the fibers that are close to the contacts (activated early, yellow) compared to fibers around the focus of interference, which are activated with a delay (t = t1, purple). Applying precisely timed anodal blocking pulses at t = t2 to a separate set of electrodes that are also close to the fibers that were activated early (yellow) will abolish their propagation without affecting the delayed APs in the more central fibers (purple). This effectively allows to ‘gate’ the action potentials (t = t3) at the focus of interference, which can be moved spatially by changing the relative amplitude of the sinusoidal stimulation currents and electrode configuration, allowing to overcome the ‘order of activation’ problem. Concomitantly, fibers with different conduction velocities could also be targeted by this approach, gating either ‘faster’ or ‘slower’ APs depending on the timing of the anodal blocking pulse, effectively overcoming the ‘order of recruitment’ problem.

The results from earthworm experiments in this study are not directly translatable to mammals. For example, CAPs in earthworms are closer to a single action potential since the axons in the MGF and LGF behave like a single fiber when stimulated18,19. Additionally, mammalian nerve anatomy is more complex and defining an EBW would require the optimization of several aspects such as mixture and distribution of fiber types, fascicular organization and generally faster conduction velocities, especially at physiological temperatures25. One approach to overcome some of these constraints could be the design of a multi-contact cuff with optimized electrode distance and layout to facilitate blocking of non-target fibers only, considering the difference in conduction velocities compared to target fibers. Furthermore, our modelling results imply that controlling the activating function will be key to achieving blocking in this approach. Although our computational model assumes a straight nerve and ideal conditions with respect to electrode placement and electric field distribution and thus does not consider the geometric variability arising from e.g. nerve curvature, a recent, comprehensive modelling study has shown that extrusion-based models provide reasonably good estimations of activation thresholds compared to ‘true 3D’ models of human nerves in many circumstances26. Finally, the implications for the mechanism derived from this study can inform novel strategies for selective nerve stimulation techniques: various conditions such as Parkinson’s or epilepsy could benefit from therapies that can control/disrupt network oscillations on a precise temporal scale27,28. Motor restoration therapies targeting efferent fibers (e.g. in the sciatic nerve) and, even more importantly, any approach that can benefit from the ability to target fibers deep within a nerve or selectively stimulate different types of fibers could be improved by applying this combined stimulation and blocking technique.

As the anatomical heterogeneity of peripheral nerves will impose strong constraints on this and other techniques, the predictions from this study should be tested in more complex animal models and nerves, such as the vagus nerve, which has a distinct functional and anatomical structure, especially in larger animals and humans29,30.

Conclusions

The ability to precisely control stimulation and blocking of action potentials in peripheral nerves could fundamentally change the effectiveness of current and future neuromodulation approaches. Here, we provide first evidence that blocking fiber responses using specific current waveforms delivered to blocking electrodes is possible and that a combination with spatial current steering techniques, including amplitude modulated sinusoidal and intermittent interferential current stimulation (i2CS), could enable more selective activation of specific fibers in a nerve.
