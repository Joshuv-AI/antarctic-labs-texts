Assessment of the trueness of the tissue surface for 3D-printed and injection-molded mandibular complete denture base fabrication
Authors: Kai Li, Yanfang Zhao, Lin Ai, Huan Liu, Haitao Xin, Yan Zhang, Yulu Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs digital methods are widely used in clinical dentistry, the accuracy of dental prostheses remains critical. However, how each step in both conventional and digital methods affects the accuracy of the overall area and each area of the tissue surface remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the effects of three-dimensional (3D) printing and injection molding processes on the trueness of mandibular complete denture bases.MethodsTen physical impressions were made using a standard edentulous lower jaw plaster model. In accordance with the scan data from these impressions, the denture bases were designed and fabricated using 3D printing. Additionally, conventional polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) denture bases were made from the same impressions via the injection molding method. The tissue surfaces were scanned and divided into four regions. Deviation analyses were performed using the root mean square (RMS) method. Two-way ANOVA was employed for statistical analyses.ResultsA significant difference in trueness was found between the two methods (p < 0.05). In the injection molding process, the largest deviation originated primarily from the plaster filling process (222.35 ± 11.77 μm), followed by wax-up fabrication (211.10 ± 55.25 μm) and resin injection (161.87 ± 59.95 μm). The deviation of the border seal area was the greatest among the regions. In the 3D printing process, the CAD design showed the greatest deviation (179.22 ± 55.13 μm). The maximum deviation was 219.83 ± 45.37 μm in the border seal area. The printing process was associated with a deviation of 138.10 ± 24.42 μm, with the maximum deviation in the retromolar pad area reaching 193.92 ± 28.13 μm.ConclusionsBoth methods demonstrated clinically acceptable tissue surface adaptation. The 3D-printed base better adapted to the integrated tissue surface, especially in the border seal area. However, owing to the significant differences observed in this study, further clinical validation is needed.Keywords: Mandibular complete denture, Tissue surface, Trueness, 3D printing, Deviation analysis

Background

Conventional complete dentures (CDs) are the most commonly used prosthetic modality to rehabilitate edentulous patients because of their cost-effectiveness and minimal requirements for oral and systemic conditions [1]. The tissue surface of the denture base, which is well fit to the mucosa, is a crucial factor in determining the retention and stability of CDs [2, 3]. However, the conventional CD restoration process is complex, with each step potentially leading to deviations in the fit of the fabricated denture base, making it challenging to guarantee the accuracy of CDs.

With the introduction of dental computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) technology, prosthesis fabrication techniques have significantly evolved [4–6]. Currently, intraoral scans are widely used for the restoration of fixed prostheses; however, they are seldom utilized for the capture of impressions in the fabrication of CDs. Digital workflows now include all prosthetic fabrication procedures except impression making and intermaxillary relationship registration, which remain analogue-dependent because of biological variability [1]. Compared with the conventional CD production method, the use of a digital production process can simplify the treatment process, reduce the number of patient visits, and increase patient comfort [7, 8]. Additionally, compared with digital milling, 3D printing has significant advantages, including greater flexibility in constructing geometric shapes, finer detail reproduction, reduced material waste, and higher energy efficiency [9, 10]. Although some studies have reported that 3D printing technology has been used to fabricate CDs for edentulous patients [11–13], further investigations are needed to analyse the accuracy of 3D-printed dentures before 3D printing is recommended as the standard manufacturing process for CDs.

Previous studies have shown that the denture base of 3D-printed CDs has good adaptability and is suitable for clinical utilization [12]. Several studies have examined the adaptability of 3D printing and conventional thermoplastic resin manufacturing technology to denture bases; however, zoning analyses of tissue surfaces have not yet been conducted [14–16]. Furthermore, few studies have focused on the sources of deviations in the various steps of digital and conventional CD production processes [8, 17], The purpose of this study was to evaluate the trueness of different areas of denture bases manufactured using 3D printing and conventional injection molding methods. Additionally, this study aimed to identify the sources of deviation in each step of these two processes for CD base production. The first null hypothesis is that there is no difference in the trueness of the integrated tissue surfaces of denture bases in various steps of digital and conventional CD production processes. The second null hypothesis is that there is no difference in the trueness of the tissue surfaces of denture bases between different areas in various steps of 3D-printed and injection-molded CD production processes.

Methods

The objective of this in vitro study is to assess the deviation in the trueness of the tissue surface during each step of the 3D printing (digital light processing, DLP) and conventional (injection molding) procedures used for fabricating mandibular denture bases in integrated surfaces and four regions. The study scheme is illustrated in Fig. 1. A Standard edentulous lower jaw plaster model (402-L; Nissin Dental Products, Kyoto, Japan) served as the basis for the preparation of mandibular prostheses. The residual ridge morphology of the master model was categorized as Type A according to the classification proposed by the American College of Prosthodontists and did not include tissue undercut. Soft tissue replicas that mimic soft tissues were not included in in vitro experiments. Afterwards, a silicone material (Express STD, 3 M ESPE, Maplewood, MN, USA) was used to take impressions. On the basis of the results of previous reports, approximately 10 samples are needed to detect significant differences between groups at an 80% confidence level [8, 18]. Therefore, a sample size of 10 was considered appropriate. The ten physical impressions were first used for optical scanning to fabricate 3D-printed denture bases. Because optical scanning is a noninvasive procedure, these impressions were subsequently used to produce injection-molded denture bases.

Flowchart of the research protocol

For the 3D printing method, virtual impressions were obtained by scanning physical impressions with a desk scanner (D750, 3Shape, Denmark). The data were saved in standard tessellation language (STL) files to design 2-mm-thick virtual denture bases using dental software (dental designer 2021 and model builder 2021, 3Shape, Denmark) [19]. On the basis of the designed files, physical denture bases were printed with light-cured resin (E-Denstone Grey, Envision TEC, Germany) using a DLP printer (Perfactory Vida; Envision TEC, Germany). Each model was horizontally positioned on the platform and printed at a 100 μm thickness [15, 20]. Finally, ten 3D-printed denture bases were fabricated. After printing, the support structures were removed. The denture bases were ultrasonically cleaned in 90% isopropyl alcohol for 10 min and further treated with a light postpolymerization machine (Otoflash, Envision TEC, Germany) for 2 min, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All CD bases were obtained by the same experienced operator for testing denture bases with a desk scanner (D750, 3Shape, Denmark). The desktop scanner was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s protocol before the scan data were captured.

The ten physical impressions were subsequently poured in type IV stone (die-stone, Heraeus Kulzer, Hanau, Germany) and used to fabricate standard edentulous models (Fig. 2A). The 2-mm-thick denture base wax patterns (type one, Shanghai New Century Dental Materials Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) were produced according to the intaglio surfaces of the stone casts. Wax patterns and models were embedded with silicone material (zetalabor, Zhermack, Kouigo, Italy). The holes were drilled in silicone material at the dorsolingual and medioanterior regions of the wax patterns. Afterwards, the wax patterns were removed using hot water and a high-pressure spray gun. Finally, the models were filled with denture polymer (self-curing, Vertex-Dental B.V., Zeist, The Netherlands). Ten conventional denture bases were produced. One very experienced dental technician manufactured these dentures in a university-based dental laboratory.

A Standard edentulous model. B The scan file of the tissue surface. C Visual representation of areas of interest (yellow: primary stress-bearing area, green: secondary stress-bearing area, orange: border seal area, red retromolar pad area)

For the injection molding method, digital scan of the intaglio surfaces of the impressions were obtained, and the data were imported into a software program (Geomagic Studio 2014, 3D Systems Inc., USA). In the polygon phase, the data were saved and named the impression. Next, the stone casts were scanned, and the data were imported into the software. The data were then saved and named the stone cast (Fig. 2B). The denture base wax-up patterns were scanned, and the data were imported into the software. The data were saved and named the wax denture base. Finally, the conventional denture bases were scanned, and the data were imported into software, saved, and named the conventional denture base.

For the 3D printing method, the denture bases were designed on the basis of the impression data. The intaglio surfaces of the designed denture bases were imported into the software, saved, and designated as the designed denture base. Then, 3D printing was subsequently applied to fabricate the denture bases. Digital scanning was then applied to these 3D-printed denture bases. The data were imported into software, saved and named the 3D-printed denture base.

For each document saved in Geomagic, four regions were identified and named the primary stress-bearing area, the secondary stress-bearing area, the border seal area, and the retromolar pad area (Fig. 2C). Trueness was defined as the average absolute variation between the reference files and the experimental files [21]. The reference files and the experimental scan files were aligned using the best fit algorithm, after which 3D deviation analysis was performed to evaluate the trueness of the four regions of interest and the integrated tissue surface.

The root mean square (RMS) values were imported into statistical software (SPSS Statistics v22.0; IBM, USA). Three-dimensional deviation analysis data are presented as the means and standard deviations. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Student’s t test with Bonferroni correction only when the ANOVA yielded statistically significant results. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance for all tests.

Deviation analysis revealed that conventional denture fabrication resulted in progressive dimensional inaccuracies, with stone cast production introducing the greatest deviation (222.35 ± 11.77 μm), followed by wax pattern fabrication (211.10 ± 55.25 μm) and final polymerization (161.87 ± 59.95 μm); the lowest deviation of 73.83 ± 18.44 μm occurred in the first step. Among the four regions, border seal areas were the most significantly affected among the production steps. Notably, the deviations were all between 200 μm and 300 μm despite the deviation in the first step (Table 1). The corresponding colour maps are listed in Fig. 3.

Deviation analysis revealed that digital design was the predominant source of dimensional inaccuracies in 3D-printed denture bases, with a deviation of 179.22 ± 55.13 μm. This stage critically comprised the trueness in the border seal areas (219.83 ± 45.37 μm). Consistent with the conventional method, the greatest deviations were observed in border seal areas in approximately all the processing steps; however, all the values remained below 200 μm. Notably, there was a marked increase in dimensional inaccuracies in printing within the retromolar areas, with the deviation reaching 193.92 ± 28.13 μm (Table 2). The corresponding colour maps are shown in Fig. 4.

The cumulative deviations between the injection molding and 3D-printed manufacturing methods are listed in Table 3; the deviations are all less than 200 μm for the integrated surfaces. For the conventional method, the greatest deviation occurred in border seal areas, which exceeded 200 μm and reached 284.23 ± 29.35 μm. For the 3D printing method, the greatest deviations occurred in the retromolar areas, which were less than 200 μm and reached 195.22 ± 27.21 μm. The corresponding colour maps are shown in Fig. 5.

The deviation cloud maps of the first model among the 10 groups of models are shown in Figs. 3, 4 and 5. Each group of figures includes legends for the overall model and 4 subregions. Specifically, each figure includes a deviation cloud map, a colour spectrum, and the corresponding values of the colour spectrum. Positive deviations gradually transition from green to red, whereas negative deviations gradually transition from green to blue. The magnitude of the deviation is evaluated using the values corresponding to the colours.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess the trueness of injection molding and 3D printing methods for the fabrication of CD bases and to analyse the sources of errors in the fabrication of CD bases using each method. The results of this study indicated a significant difference in the trueness of the two methods for the integrated tissue surface. Therefore, the first null hypothesis was rejected. A statistically significant difference in the trueness between the retromolar pad area and the border seal area of the mandibular CD base was noted between the two methods. Thus, the second null hypothesis was also rejected. In this study, the deviation of the injection-molded denture bases for the integrated tissue surface was 187.40 ± 60.04 μm, whereas the deviation of the 3D-printed denture bases for the integrated tissue surface was 137.70 ± 14.86 μm (Fig. 6). In previous studies [3, 17, 22], denture base adaptation ranged between 58 μm and 290 μm for digital methods and between 105 μm and 300 μm for conventional technologies. In this study, all the trueness values were within the clinically acceptable range [23–25].

To our knowledge, few comparative studies have investigated the trueness of different areas during each step in the production of CD bases with 3D printing and conventional injection molding methods. Furthermore, the sources of bias in the process of producing CD bases using both methods have rarely been evaluated. In this study, the tissue surface was divided into four areas of interest on the basis of the functional areas of CDs in clinical practice, and the evaluation of the trueness of the denture base was refined during each step.

In the injection molding process, there are varying degrees of deviation in the procedures used to make the alginate impression, obtain the gypsum model, and polymerize resin denture base. These deviations may affect the trueness of the fabricated denture base. In this study, silicone rubber was employed to produce physical impressions of the edentulous jaws. This material was more stable over an extended period than alginate. According to the American Dental Association standards, the size of nonaqueous impression materials (such as silicone rubber) should change by less than 0.5% [26]. The deviation generated during the impression production process was the smallest at 73.83 ± 18.44 μm (Fig.7). Furthermore, the deformation in all four regions was smallest in this step.

Throughout the production process, the tissue surface deviation between the gypsum model and the impressions was the greatest at 222.35 ± 11.77 μm (Fig. 7). This deviation is attributed to the change in size during the gypsum solidification process, and the expansion rate within 24 h ranged from 0.01%±0.008% to 0.13%±0.017% [27]. According to the American Dental Association specification, an expansion of up to 0.3% is allowed [28].

The deviation of the tissue surface between the stone cast and the injection-molded denture base was 161.87 ± 59.95 μm (Fig. 7). In this study, polymethyl methacrylate was used to fabricate a denture base, and this material is also commonly used in clinical practice. Polymerization shrinkage (linear and volume) may lead to significant dimensional changes and inaccuracies during the denture manufacturing process [29], which is the main factor leading to deviation in the tissue surface of the resin denture base. Compared with conventional compression molding, the injection molding technique used in this study compensates for polymerization shrinkage through the continuous injection of PMMA, which to some extent increases the accuracy of border seal areas [30]. However, this approach still cannot completely eliminate the deviation generated in this area, which was the largest value of 291.93 ± 80.55 μm (Fig.7).

Despite the shrinkage or expansion of the dental materials, only minor deformations were observed in all the subsequent steps, as shown in Fig. 7. Factors influencing these deviations include the complexity of the manipulation steps and the waxing, investing, and wax elimination procedures. However, the deviation between the resin denture base and the standard plaster model indicates that the final deviation is not a simple sum of the deviations that occur during each step (Fig. 6). The three-dimensional direction in which deviations occur must also be considered. For instance, the expansion of gypsum material and the shrinkage of impression material on the surface of the gypsum model in the first two steps can be considered the offsetting effect of the errors. Notably, the greatest deviation of 284.23 ± 29.35 μm was found in the border seal areas (Fig. 7). This may be due to gypsum expansion, which pressed on the edge of the impression, leading to greater deformation of the border seal area. These experimental results can be better understood by employing the finite element analysis method.

Compared with more complex steps in conventional methods, the number of treatment steps and the deviation between the resin denture base and the standard plaster model are both reduced with 3D printing approaches (Figs. 7, 8 and 6). The results of this experiment indicated that the tissue surface deviation between the impressions and the CAD design denture bases during the digital production process was the greatest at 179.22 ± 55.13 μm, with the most significant deviation occurring in the border seal areas (219.83 ± 45.37 μm). This step involved scanning impressions, designing the denture base, filling, chamfering, and smoothing the tissue surface in 3Shape design software and its algorithm. The use of different CAD software processes involving the STL file may lead to different deviations, especially for models with complex geometries. In this study, the greatest deviation was observed in the marginal areas, such as the border seal area. Second, the deviation of the 3D printing process was 138.10 ± 24.42 μm (Fig. 8). In this step, a VIDA printer, which is a DLP printing technology for photopolymerization printers, was used for denture base printing. Among the various 3D printing technologies, DLP is among the most commonly used resin model manufacturing technologies in dentistry [31–34]. Many factors affect the accuracy of prostheses. The two main factors are printing thickness and orientation. For the DLP printer, the printing thickness (Z direction) can range from 25 μm to 150 μm. Some reports have indicated that a layer thickness less than 100 μm is adequate for dental prostheses printing [23]. Decreasing the layer thickness can notably increase the number of layers and printing time; however, the effect on precision is negligible [22]. For the printing orientation, a vertical setting for the model is recommended in most reports focused on printing accuracy. Different printing orientations are needed to set support structures in various areas. The horizontal setting indicates that the support structures are rarely located in marginal areas, such as the retromolar pad area and border seal area, with higher deviations of 193.92 ± 28.13 μm and 186.52 ± 58.48 μm, respectively (Table3; Fig. 5). With respect to the vertical orientation, the support structure is located in the posterior area, which leads to a large deviation in the concentrated distribution in the posterior area and may exceed 300 μm [22]. In addition, the printing resin material used in this study was light-cured polymethyl methacrylate, which has the advantages of easy manufacturing and complete control of curing. The residual monomer quantity of light-cured PMMA is lower than that of the other materials, and the degree of polymerization shrinkage is lower [35, 36]. This may explain why the border seal area of the 3D-printed CD base was better than that of the conventional CD base in this study.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. It is an in vitro study in which intraoral try-in and evaluation were lacking, and the amount of soft tissue compression or deformation cannot be standardized or reproduced under the test conditions. Although 10 models were fabricated via each manufacturing method, rigorous reproducibility testing among these models was not performed. This critical validation should be incorporated into future experimental designs to strengthen the methodological robustness. Clinical assessments of CDs are associated with retention, stability, and support, which are influenced by distortion, and attempts to minimize the distortion in each step should be considered. Although the statistical analyses of deviations indicated that 3D printing is superior to injection modelling for integrated surface and border seal areas, clinical evidence should be investigated in future studies. Only one typical process is selected for each conventional and 3D printing technology. Previous research has reported that injection molding may increase the accuracy and stability of the denture base [31]. Among the four technologies used for 3D printing, namely, stereolithography (SLA), digital light processing (DLP), material jetting (MJ), and material extrusion (ME), SLA and DLP are widely used in dental applications. Compared with SLA, which requires more production and postprocessing time, DLP is considered a superior application for printing denture bases. Notably, for a comprehensive study, more applications of each technology should be considered. Although 2-mm uniformly thick denture resin bases are widely used clinically, experimental studies should incorporate nonuniform thickness configurations and comparative trials across varying thickness gradients. Further studies are needed to investigate the influence of different manufacturing technologies and alveolar ridge shapes on the fitting accuracy of CD bases and the dimensional stability of 3D-printed denture bases. In addition, reducing the deviation of each step in the process of CD manufacturing and improving the accuracy of CD manufacturing remain challenges. However, the digital manufacturing of CDs has immense potential in the modernization and simplified manufacturing of CDs, which remains the future development trend of digital dentistry.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, the trueness of 3D-printed (DLP) mandibular CD bases is better than that of injection-molded CD bases; however, both are clinically acceptable. The deviation in the injection molding method for producing CD bases is due mainly to the casting of the plaster model and the subsequent injection molding of the denture base. The deviation in the digital manufacturing process primarily arises from the CAD design of the denture base followed by the printing process. Although 3D printing results in statistically significant differences in trueness deviations in the border seal areas of mandibular CD bases compared with those in injection-molded CD bases, whether this translates to superior clinical outcomes in terms of retention and stability requires further validation.
