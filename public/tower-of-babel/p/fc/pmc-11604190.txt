Emergency admission preceding malignancy diagnosis: Insights from a study at a tertiary care hospital
Authors: Hussain A. Redha, Kawther S. Al Hatmi, Safa K. Al-Ghaithi, Khalfan B. Al Zeedy, Abdullah M. Al Alawi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:A considerable number of cancer patients are diagnosed following presentation at emergency department with symptoms related to their condition, yet research in this area remains scarce. This study aimed to identify the clinical characteristics and evaluate the health outcomes of patients who presented to the emergency department with symptoms of cancer.MATERIALS AND METHODS:We analyzed data for patients diagnosed with cancer postemergency department presentation at a tertiary care hospital between 2015 to 2021. Data on patient characteristics, clinical features, and health outcomes was abstracted through meticulous review of the patients’ medical record. SPPS was used for data analysis. Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, were used to determine statistical significance for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Regression analysis determined factors associated with treatment and mortality. Kaplein-Meier analysis was performed to determine survival time.RESULTS:During the study period, 108 patients were diagnosed with new malignancies following acute medical admission. The median age of these patients was 67 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 56–75), and 58 (53.7%) of these patients were men. The most common presenting complaints were pain (55.6%), weight loss (44.4%), and poor appetite (36.1%). The median time from the onset of symptoms to hospital presentation was 30 days (IQR 8–62). The most common types of malignancies diagnosed in the study were hepatobiliary (18.5%), gastroesophageal (15.7%), and lung cancer (14.8%). Two-thirds of patients had Stage IV malignancy, and 64.81% presented with distant metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis. Older age (69 vs. 61.5 years; P = 0.04), lower level of general education and higher attainment (11.4% vs. 44.4%; P < 0.01), advanced malignancy stages (P < 0.01), and metastatic disease at diagnosis (75.4% vs. 44.4%; P = 0.01) were associated with higher mortality.CONCLUSION:Emergency presentations for newly diagnosed cancer, often seen in older patients with lower education and multiple comorbidities, were generally associated with poor survival rates owing to advanced disease with distant metastasis. Enhancing awareness of critical symptoms could improve early detection rates.

Introduction

Despite all medical advances, including diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, cancer is still considered a major global burden in health, being one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide. In 2020, it was estimated globally that around 19.3 million people were diagnosed with cancer, 10 million of whom died.[1]

A significant number of cancer patients are diagnosed following emergency presentation with symptoms related to cancer.[2] In England, a significant number of cancer cases are identified following emergency visits to general practitioners or hospital emergency departments.[3] Research shows that factors such as older age, gender, socioeconomic factors, and specific types of cancer, including brain and lung cancers, are indicators of an emergency presentation.[4] Patients diagnosed with cancer in an emergency setting generally experience worse short-term survival rates compared to those diagnosed through other methods, even when adjusting for variables like age, stage of cancer, and comorbidities.[5] Similar observations have been made in Scotland, particularly for patients with breast and colorectal cancers.[6] It is also common for patients who present with cancer in an emergency to have had previous encounters with a general practitioner in the period leading up to their emergency presentation. Receiving a diagnosis in an emergency can significantly elevate stress and anxiety for both patients and their families.[7] Furthermore, emergency diagnoses place additional strain on healthcare systems, especially outside regular hours. Previous studies have shown that cancer diagnoses made in emergency settings are linked to poorer prognoses owing to factors such as advanced disease stage at diagnosis, socioeconomic and demographic disparities, inefficiencies in the healthcare system, higher recurrence rates, inadequate prognostic awareness, and increased mortality.[8] There are challenges in the efforts to reduce cancer diagnoses through emergency presentations. While some cases may be unavoidable because of such factors as tumor type, aggression, or location, leading to sudden severe incidents without warning signs, other cases involve symptoms that precede the malignancy but are not recognized by patients or doctors in a timely fashion.[7] Therefore, the reasons for emergency diagnoses extend beyond biological explanation to include psychological barriers to prompt search for help and difficulties doctors encounter when symptoms presented are atypical.[6]

There are many factors associated with the delay in the diagnosis of cancer. A study from England showed that sociodemographic elements greatly impact the delay in diagnosing six types of cancer, with delays being longer for females, younger individuals, those who are single, from lower social strata, and belonging to black and South Asian communities compared to white individuals.[9] In another study on upper gastrointestinal cancer, it was observed that patients often delay more than practitioners due to their failure to recognize the seriousness of symptoms. On the other hand, practitioner delay is linked to misdiagnosis, issues with test interpretation, and complications from existing diseases.[10]

As the population of elderly people increases in the Middle East, the incidence of cancer also rises, given that age is the primary risk factor for the disease. This trend is observed across all populations in the region.[11] A study published in Saudi Arabia in 2020, estimated that in 2018, there were 24,485 newly diagnosed cancer cases, and 10,518 deaths related to cancer.[12] In Oman, the crude incidence rate of cancer stood at 45.7 per 100,000 for females and 49.4 per 100,000 for males. The equivalent age-adjusted rates were 80.4 for females and 91.4 per 100,000 for males, respectively.[13] In India, a study showed that 78% of newly diagnosed cancer patients were presented at advanced stages.[14] Moreover, a local study conducted in Oman found that all Stage IV colorectal cancer patients diagnosed within the region passed away within a period of 5 years.[15]

Data regarding the presentation of cancer patients, their treatment, and factors leading to diagnostic delays in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region are limited. This study seeks to identify the clinical characteristics and evaluate the health outcomes of patients who presented to the emergency department with cancer symptoms and had an acute medical admission prior to their diagnosis.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective study was carried out at a leading tertiary hospital and academic institution in Oman from January 2015 until December 2021. The study included all patients who presented to the emergency department with symptoms of cancer, leading to acute medical admission, and were subsequently diagnosed with cancer. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board vide Letter No. 2594 dated 06/10/2021, with a waiver of informed consent since there was no direct involvement of human subjects in this study.

The medical records were meticulously reviewed for the following data: Patient age, sex, level of general education, and comorbidity profile including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), a history of alcohol consumption, a history of smoking, and previous cancer. Additionally, the records analyzed the number of days from the onset of symptoms to hospital presentation, the time taken from hospital presentation to a diagnosis, and the diagnostic methodology, whether based on tissue histology or radiological features. Information on the site and stage of the malignancy, available treatment options (curative, palliative, or no treatment), the duration of follow-up in days, and the current survival status of the patients was also included. The survival status was determined through a combination of follow-up phone calls and review of medical records until December 2022. The survival time was calculated from the date of cancer diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up. For patients who did not die and were still alive at the last follow-up, the time to censoring was defined as the time from diagnosis to the last known contact date. Patients with missing follow-up data were excluded from the survival analysis to ensure data accuracy and reliability.

The patients’ functional status was evaluated using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Scale.[16]

Stage 4 cancer is characterized by the presence of distant metastases identified at the initial point of diagnosis.[17]

Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Since the continuous variables were abnormally distributed, they were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare these continuous variables between deceased and surviving patients and between those who were offered cancer-specific treatment and those not offered treatment due to poor prognosis. The association between categorical variables was examined using the Fisher’s exact test or Chi-square test, as appropriate. All relevant characteristics with a P < 0.2 were included in a backward regression analysis to ascertain independent factors associated with the likelihood of undergoing cancer-specific treatment and associated mortality. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with survival time calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or censoring at the last follow-up. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to assess the effect of multiple variables on survival, and hazard ratios (HR) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp, 1985–2021, Stata Statistical Software, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

During the study period, 108 patients were diagnosed with new malignancies following presentation to the emergency department with cancer symptoms. The median age of these patients was 67 (IQR: 56–75) years, and 58 (53.7%) of these patients were men. The most common associated risk factors were hypertension (n = 60, 55.6%), diabetes mellitus (n = 38, 35.2%), and a history of smoking (n = 27, 25.0%). The most common presenting complaints were pain symptoms, whether generalized or localized to the site of the tumor (n = 60, 55.6%), weight loss (n = 48, 44.4%), poor appetite (n = 39, 36.1%), and nausea and/or vomiting (n = 22, 20.4%) [Table 1].

The median time from symptom onset to hospital presentation was 30 days (IQR 8–62), and 15 days (IQR 10–25) from hospital presentation to the confirmation of the diagnosis. The majority of the patients were diagnosed using tissue samples (n = 89, 82.4%). However, only 19 patients (17.6%) were diagnosed on the basis of radiological findings, including results of computed tomography, positron emission tomography scans, and magnetic resonance imaging, administered as appropriate. The most common types of malignancies diagnosed in the study were hepatocellular (n = 20; 18.5%), gastroesophageal (n = 17; 15.7%), and lung cancer (n = 16; 14.8%). Seventy-three patients (67.6%) were found to have Stage IV malignancy, and 70 patients (64.8%) presented with distant metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis. Out of all the patients, only 18 (16.7%) underwent a curative treatment plan, including 12 (11.1%) who received curative surgery, 15 (13.9%) who underwent curative chemotherapy, and 3 (2.8%) who had curative radiotherapy. Conversely, 46 (42.6%) patients were managed palliatively; 35 (32.4%) had palliative chemotherapy and 10 (9.3%) underwent palliative surgery [Table 2].

After excluding patients who passed away during the index admission and those who were lost to follow-up, the study followed a total of 88 patients. Over a median follow-up period of 127 days (IQR 21–850), only 20.0% (18 out of 88) patients survived [Table 2].

Univariate analysis revealed that factors such as older age (69 vs. 61.5 years; P = 0.04), lower attainment of a general education certificate or above (11.4% vs. 44.4%; P < 0.01), a longer median period from symptom onset to hospital presentation (19 vs. 63.5 days; P = 0.021), advanced malignant stages (P < 0.01), the presence of metastatic disease at diagnosis (75.4% vs. 44.4%; P = 0.01), and the lack of specific cancer treatment options (50.7% vs. 16.7%; P = 0.02) were associated with increased mortality. Conversely, the intention of providing curative management (55.6% vs. 4.4%, P < 0.001) was associated with improved survival [Table 5].

Discussion

This is one of the few studies that investigated patients diagnosed with cancer following emergency presentation as a result of symptoms caused by the underlying cancer.[1819] The majority of these patients were diagnosed with advanced cancer and had very poor survival rates. Abdominal cancers were the most common among these patients. The relatively short duration between the onset of symptoms and hospital presentation may indicate either the subtle nature of cancer or poor recognition of symptoms by patients.

Cancers identified after visits to emergency departments or through emergency admissions are linked to lower survival rates and may be attributed to avoidable delays in diagnosis.[20]

The median age was 67 years, and besides acknowledging that old age is a risk factor for many cancers, several studies have also identified older age as a risk factor for delayed cancer diagnosis.[29] Hypertension (55.6%) and diabetes mellitus (35.2%) were prevalent in this cohort but this did not impact the outcome in the cohort probably because of the small sample size. Diabetes mellitus is a risk factor for several cancers, including colon, rectal, pancreatic, liver, and endometrial cancers, and is associated with a higher risk of increased mortality.[21] Also, some studies have suggested that hypertension is associated with an increased risk of renal cell carcinoma, breast cancer, and prostate cancer. Also, hypertension may increase mortality in patients with cancer.[22] Surprisingly, only 17 (15.7%) patients had a general education certificate or above.[23] Socioeconomic factors, such as education and income significantly affected overall mortality and life expectancy in the United States and other Western countries.[24] However, there is a dearth of studies that evaluated the impact of socioeconomic factors in the MENA region. Several studies have concluded that low education levels are associated with delayed diagnoses of cancers. The most likely explanation for this difference is a lack of awareness of the symptoms of malignancy.

The median time from the onset of symptoms to hospital presentation was 30 days (range 8–62 days). Previous studies have reported different time periods between the onset of symptoms and hospital presentation.[252627] A study from Scotland found that the median time between symptom onset and presentation to a healthcare facility by patients diagnosed with lung cancer was 99 days, and 75% of patients had unrecognized cancer symptoms.[27] Another study involving 10,297 patients from England found that the overall median time from recognizing symptoms to seeking medical advice by cancer patients was 10 days, with 56% of the patients presenting promptly owing to concerning cancer symptoms. The likelihood of promptly presenting with cancer symptoms varied significantly, with higher rates of delayed presentations observed in cases of oropharyngeal and esophageal cancers.[25] However, given that 64.8% of patients in our cohort were diagnosed with distant metastatic cancer, it is conceivable that the initial symptoms of cancer were either unrecognized or subtle. This is further supported by the fact that the majority of cancers in our patients were abdominal malignancies, which tend to be silent and present with nonspecific symptoms.[26]

The most common initial complaints in our cohort included pain, weight loss, poor appetite, and nausea and/or vomiting. This observation is in line with prior findings that patients with advanced cancer showed a range of symptoms, among which pain, anorexia, and fatigue were the most prevalent and significant across most primary sites.[28]

The average time from acute medical admission to the diagnosis of cancer was approximately 15 days (IQR 10–25 days) following hospital presentation. The previously reported time from symptom onset to diagnosis varied greatly, with the reported duration ranging from a few weeks to several months, depending on factors such as the healthcare setting, the type of cancer, and availability of healthcare resources.[293031] The prompt diagnosis following emergency presentation and obtaining a diagnosis in our context could be attributed to the fact that our care setting are specialized and access to diagnostic services is obtainable.

In terms of cancer types, the four most common cancers identified in the cohort in the study were hepatobiliary, gastroesophageal, lung, and colorectal cancers. Locally, stomach cancer was the most common in males and breast cancer was the most common in females.[13] However, the most recent study published in 2024 found that colorectal cancer was the most prevalent among males and the second most common among females, indicating a downward trend in the incidence of stomach cancer.[32] These discrepancies are due to the fact that some cancers, including gastrointestinal malignancies, have undifferentiated symptoms, leading to diagnoses following emergency presentations.[33] Additionally, national screening programs for certain cancers, including breast cancer, facilitate early recognition and diagnosis. Hence, they are underrepresented in our study and mostly included patients with advanced malignancies diagnosed after emergency presentations and acute medical admissions because of cancer symptoms.

The study revealed that most of the population presented with advanced stages of cancer (Stage III at 17.6% and Stage IV at 67.7%), 64.8% of whom were diagnosed with metastatic disease upon presentation. Locally, it was observed that a majority of newly diagnosed cases of colorectal cancer were found to be in the advanced stages at the time of diagnosis.[15] Additionally, a 2023 study in Algeria showed that 63% of newly diagnosed cases of pancreatic cancer were identified at a metastatic stage.[34] Research conducted in Ethiopia in 2020 of 281 patients with newly diagnosed cancer found that 71% presented with advanced stages of the disease.[35] A study published in the British Journal of Cancer in 2015 examined 152,821 newly diagnosed cancer cases internationally, revealing that upon presentation, 49% of lung cancer cases were in the metastatic stage.[36] These findings underscore the typically silent course of such cancers, which often progress swiftly and without noticeable clinical symptoms until they reach advanced stages.

Regarding treatment, the study revealed that the majority of patients received either palliative care or were not offered any cancer-specific treatment (42.6% and 38.0%, respectively), while only 16.7% underwent curative treatment. Logistic regression analysis identified the presence of CKD, diagnosis of hepatobiliary cancers, pancreatic cancer, advanced cancer stage, and poor performance status as independent factors associated with no offer of treatment.

The majority of the patients died during the follow-up period, and the analysis of time to event demonstrated that the median survival duration for all patients was 4.2 months. Notably, patients presenting with distant metastasis had a median survival of only 1.8 months, whereas those without distant metastasis experienced a significantly longer median survival time of 17.4 months. This survival rate is well below the locally reported survival rate for many cancers.[153738] On the other hand, having a general education certificate or higher, being diagnosed with gastroesophageal cancer, and undergoing curative treatment were all substantial independent factors associated with survival, as demonstrated in the regression analysis.

In this current study, several factors were associated with increased mortality. The mortality rates were higher in older patients, those with lower educational attainment, and patients with advanced stages of malignancy or metastatic disease at diagnosis. Additionally, the lack of treatment was linked to increased mortality. Data analysis of 22,654 patients determined that factors such as older age, lower body mass index, worse performance status, and a greater number of metastatic sites were significant findings.[39] Another study found that factors like metastatic status, being over 80 years old, having a higher number of severe comorbidities, and malnutrition contribute to higher mortality rates of elderly cancer patients.[40]

This study is one of the few that concentrate on reporting the characteristics and health outcomes of patients diagnosed with cancer following emergency presentation for cancer-related symptoms. It collected pertinent clinical and demographic information and monitored patients over a long enough period.

This study’s limitations are notably its small sample size, which may impact the generalizability of its findings. Additionally, the retrospective nature of data collection introduces challenges, including the possibility of incomplete data or such biases as selection bias and recall bias.

Conclusion

Emergency presentations that result in acute medical admissions often serve as the initial event for a new cancer diagnosis and are frequently associated with a particularly poor survival rate. These patients are often older, have lower levels of education, and suffer from multiple comorbidities. The majority present with distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis, which regularly precludes curative treatment as a feasible option. Improving awareness of the warning signs, such as unexplained loss of appetite, fatigue, and weight loss, could be a crucial strategy for the achievement of earlier cancer diagnosis.
