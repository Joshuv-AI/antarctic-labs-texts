Inhibition of the Metalloproteinase ADAMTS5 Suppresses Colorectal Cancer Metastasis via the PEDF/Wnt/β‐Catenin Pathway
Authors: Xuan Sun, Hanqing Zhang, Yan Hu, Shengyu Jiao, Zesheng Yao, Ke Yi, Guojian Gu, Xiaohui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAt present, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent cancer globally and is the second most common cause of mortality associated with cancer. The expression of A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs 5 (ADAMTS5) is upregulated in CRC, and high ADAMTS5 expression strongly correlates with an unfavorable prognosis. However, the function of ADAMTS5 in CRC remains unknown. This study aimed to investigate the role of ADAMTS5 in CRC and its potential mechanisms of action. The results showed that ADAMTS5 expression was higher in CRC tissues than in paracancerous tissues. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that its expression gradually increased with tumor progression and that high expression was correlated with poor prognosis. ADAMTS5 knockdown suppressed the proliferation and migration of HCT116 and HT29 cells, and ADAMTS5 inhibition suppressed epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in HCT116, HT29, and patient‐derived organoids. Mechanistic analysis using the STRING database revealed that ADAMTS5 expression was strongly correlated with the Wnt signaling pathway. Western blotting analysis confirmed that ADAMTS5 inhibition suppressed tumor cell invasion and migration and blocked EMT in vitro through the PEDF/Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. Furthermore, ADAMTS5 inhibition significantly inhibited tumor proliferation and metastasis within a nude mouse CRC liver metastasis model. These findings indicated that inhibition of ADAMTS5 could suppress CRC progression and metastasis via the PEDF/Wnt/β‐catenin pathway.

Introduction

CRC is a major malignant tumor of the digestive system. CRC has the third highest incidence rate and the second highest mortality rate among malignant tumors [1]. The incidence rate of CRC is increasing annually, and most CRC patients are diagnosed in the middle or late stages [2, 3]. The significantly higher mortality rate of advanced CRC patients is closely related to the occurrence of distant metastasis; the liver is the most common site of CRC metastasis, and fewer than 20% of patients diagnosed with metastatic CRC survive for more than 5 years [4, 5]. Therefore, investigating the potential pathogenic mechanisms of colorectal cancer [6] and identifying effective target genes are crucial for the treatment of colorectal cancer [7, 8].

In the process of tumor progression and metastasis, the tumor microenvironment (TME) is considered a key factor; thus, the TME is currently a major focus of tumor research. Specifically, remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) of the TME has been identified as a key factor in cancer progression [9], and ECM metalloproteinases play important roles in this process. ECM metalloproteinases include matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), “a disintegrin‐like and metalloproteinase” (ADAM) family members, and “a disintegrin‐like and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs” (ADAMTS) family members. These metalloproteinases (MMPs, ADAMs, and ADAMTSs) cocleave many ECM substrates and play critical roles in cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation [10, 11, 12]. Studies have shown that the expression levels of ADAMTS proteases are correlated with the risk of various diseases, including various cancers, developmental defects, osteoarthritis, cardiovascular disease, and reproductive disorders [12, 13, 14, 15].

ADAMTS5 is a multidomain protein with several distinct structural domains, and the known proteoglycan substrates of this metalloprotease include aggrecan, versican, brevican, and neurocan [16]. ADAMTS5 plays an essential role in CRC and is highly expressed in CRC tissues; notably, the proportion of infiltrating lymphoid cells and the incidence of lymph node metastasis are significantly increased in patients with high ADAMTS5 expression [17]. In addition, the specific structural domains of ADAMTS5 may promote the proliferation, adhesion, migration, and metastasis of CRC cells by modifying the structure and function of the ECM, further accelerating the progression of CRC and shortening the overall survival of patients in advanced stages [18, 19]. Moreover, in advanced CRC, its expression level is even greater; high ADAMTS5 expression may contribute to the advanced tumor phenotype by promoting the degradation of ECM components that constitute the tumor invasion barrier [19, 20]. Consequently, reducing the expression of ADAMTS5 may be a novel therapeutic strategy for patients with CRC. However, the specific regulatory processes and underlying molecular mechanisms of ADAMTS5 in CRC remain poorly understood.

EMT is a process by which epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics. In cancer, EMT is associated with tumorigenesis, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to therapy [21]. During EMT, tumor cells undergo dissolution of cell–cell junctions and loss of cellular polarity, leading to cytoskeletal rearrangement and subsequent alteration of cell shape, transforming the cells into a mesenchymal phenotype, which renders them more invasive [22]. Therefore, many studies indicate that EMT, as an important factor in the biological properties of tumor cells, can be used as a prognostic predictor and a potential therapeutic target for CRC [23, 24]. The correlations between ADAMTS5 expression and CRC cell invasion and EMT remain unclear. Our team previously reported that ADAMTS5 inhibitors have significant therapeutic effects on glioblastoma. Given the functional roles of ADAMTS5 in CRC, we hypothesized that ADAMTS5 plays a pivotal role in the liver metastasis of CRC and thus may be a therapeutic target in this context. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the effects of ADAMTS5 inhibition on EMT and the Wnt signaling pathway in CRC cells.

We observed that ADAMTS5 expression is upregulated in CRC tissues compared with normal tissues and that high ADAMTS5 expression is correlated with a poor prognosis. Furthermore, we found that ADAMTS5 inhibition suppressed the proliferation, EMT, and migration of CRC cells, which are crucial processes involved in CRC cell invasion and metastasis. Mechanistically, we discovered that ADAMTS5 inhibition suppresses the EMT and metastasis‐promoting behaviors of CRC cells via the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. In addition, in vivo experiments revealed that downregulation of ADAMTS5 has an inhibitory effect on CRC metastasis and tumor growth in the liver. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate an important role of ADAMTS5 in CRC progression. These findings provide new and valuable insights into the development of new strategies for CRC targeted therapy.

Methods

Clinical Sample Collection

The cancer tissues (T) and paired paracancerous tissues (N) (intestinal tissue obtained during surgical resection, located at least 5 cm from the edge of the primary colorectal tumor, and confirmed by postoperative pathological examination to show no obvious cancer cell infiltration) used in this study were obtained from CRC patients who underwent surgical treatment between June 2022 and June 2023 at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery of the First People's Hospital of Taicang. All enrolled patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) confirmed as CRC by histopathological examination; (2) no prior exposure to any antitumor therapy, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy, before surgery. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of other malignancies within the past 5 years; (2) concurrent severe infectious or autoimmune diseases; (3) incomplete clinical or pathological data. All samples were collected with the written consent of the patients and their families. The clinicopathologic characteristics of all CRC patients are shown in Table S3. In addition, the study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First People's Hospital of Taicang (#2023‐ky‐049).

Bioinformatics Analysis

Expression of ADAMTS5 in CRC and its association with survival were validated using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA; http://gepia.cancer‐pku.cn/) database. This database contains cancer and normal gene expression data. In addition, we performed gene information retrieval and analyses via the GeneCards database website (https://www.genecards.org/) and the STRING database website (https://cn.string‐db.org/) to predict proteins potentially interacted with ADAMTS5.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

CRC cell lines (HCT116, HT29, HCT8, SW480, LoVo) and normal human intestinal epithelial cells (HIEC) were procured from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). The used cell lines were authenticated by an expert before being used for experimentation and routine mycoplasma testing was performed periodically, and none of the cell lines were contaminated with mycoplasma throughout the experiment. The CRC cell lines HCT116 and HT29 were cultured in McCoy's 5A medium (#PM150710; Procell, Wuhan, China), whereas LoVo cells, SW480 cells, and HIECs were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, #C11995500BT; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and HCT8 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (#C11875500BT; Gibco). The media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #10099141C; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (#15140122; Gibco). The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Cell Transfection

ADAMTS5‐targeting shRNA constructs (sh‐ADAMTS5) along with a control shNC were obtained from RiboBio Company (Guangzhou, China). 100 ng plasmids were transfected into HCT116 and HT29 cells via Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (#L3000001; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for transient silencing of ADAMTS5 expression. To transiently silence PEDF expression, 100 nM siRNAs (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) were transfected into HCT116 and HT29 cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (#13778150; Invitrogen). Cells were cultured in complete medium supplemented with transfection reagent for 24 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium, followed by an additional 24‐h incubation period before experimental use. All transfection steps were carried out in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The sequences of shRNA and siRNA are provided in Table S1.

Total RNA Extraction and Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR)

Total cellular or tissue RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (#15596026; Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (for real time) (#RR036A; TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) strictly adhering to the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. RT‐qPCR was carried out with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (#RR820A; TaKaRa) on a CFX96 Real‐time PCR System manufactured by Bio‐Rad. The thermal cycling conditions comprised an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 60 s. GAPDH was used as an internal reference, and RNA expression was analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCT method, where ΔΔCT = [CT (target gene) − CT (GAPDH)] sample − [CT (target gene) − CT (GAPDH)] control [25]. All RT‐qPCR primers were designed and synthesized by RiboBio Company, and their sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western Blotting

Cell or tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (#P1045; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a cocktail of phosphatase and protease inhibitors, following the manufacturer's instructions. The protein concentration was measured with a BCA protein assay kit (#P0010; Beyotime). The extracted proteins were transferred onto a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (#IPVH00010; Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) via SDS‐PAGE (Tris‐HCl). After blocking with 5% non‐fat dry milk for 1 h at ambient temperature, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies (all diluted 1:1000) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:10,000) for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were visualized using an ultrasensitive ECL reagent (#P10300; NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) and a Chemiscope S6 chemiluminescent imager (Clinx, Shanghai, China). Details on the protein‐targeting antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Viability Assays

CRC cells in the logarithmic growth phase were plated into 96‐well plates at a density of 4 × 103 cells per well, with four technical replicates for each experimental condition. After 24 h, the cells were subjected to different treatments. Cell viability was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h using a CCK‐8 kit (#C6005; NCM Biotech). The optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was detected using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated by the following formula: cell viability rate = (OD in experimental group/OD in control group) × 100%. The growth curves were plotted and analyzed via GraphPad 9.0 software.

EdU Incorporation Assay

The treated cells were cultured in medium containing the EdU working solution (#C10310; RiboBio Company) as per the manufacturer's protocol and further incubated in the incubator for 2 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution (#BL539A; BioSharp, Hefei, China). Finally, the cells were incubated with Hoechst dye (RiboBio Company). Positive cells were then counted using a fluorescence microscope.

Transwell Migration Assay

Approximately 2 × 105 treated CRC cells were resuspended in 200 μL of serum‐free medium, and the suspension was added to the top chamber of the Transwell insert. The top chamber was equipped with an 8 μm pore size membrane (#3422; Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA). The lower compartment was filled with 700 μL of complete culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The cells were then further incubated at 37°C for 36 h. The cells that passed through the membrane into the lower chamber were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet solution (#C0121; Beyotime) for 20 min and rinsed with PBS to remove excess stain. Finally, the cells were observed and photographed using a microscope.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assay

Incubate the whole‐cell lysates of harvested CRC cells with the primary antibody against the target protein (e.g., anti‐ADAMTS5) or an equal amount of normal rabbit/mouse IgG (as a negative control) overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. After washing three times with lysis buffer, add beads (#sc‐2003; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) to the cell lysate at room temperature for 1 h, followed by three additional washes. Boil the protein complex and proceed with Western blot analysis.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

HCT116 and HT29 cells were transfected with TOP flash plasmid (TCF reporter plasmid; #D2501; Beyotime) and FOP flash (containing a mutated TCF binding site; #D2503; Beyotime). After 24 h, add the relevant drugs for stimulation. At 36 h after treatment initiation, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (#E1910; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Relative luciferase activity is defined as the ratio of firefly/Renilla luciferase activity.

CRC Patient‐Derived Organoid (PDO) Culture

Surgical samples from patients with CRC liver metastases selected with the same criteria described above were used for the construction of CRC organoids. Approval for the study was granted by the hospital's Ethics Committee. All samples were collected with the consent of the patients and their families, who signed the relevant informed consent forms. The colorectal tumor organoids were constructed and cultured as described previously [26].

The removed tumor tissue was washed three times with precooled tissue wash solution; sterile forceps were used to remove the surrounding fat and necrotic areas, and the remaining tissue was subsequently cut into small pieces via surgical scissors. The tissue was further washed with precooled DMEM and digested with collagenase for 30 min at 37°C. The single‐cell suspension was collected by filtration through a 100 μm filter and precipitated by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. After removal of the supernatant, the cell pellet was re‐suspended in culture medium. The cell suspension was combined with matrix gel at a ratio of 1:1, then dispensed dropwise into a multi‐well culture plate and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to promote solidification of the matrix gel. Then, 500 μL of complete CRC organoid medium was added to the wells, and the plates were subsequently stored in an incubator. All the reagents were obtained from Jiyan Biomedical Technology Co. Ltd. (Suzhou, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining

CRC organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, subsequently embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 10‐μm‐thick sections. The organoid samples were then blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 2 h. The following primary antibodies were added: anti‐ADAMTS5 (1:250, #HA722011; HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), anti‐E‐cadherin (1:500, #3195; Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), anti‐vimentin (1:100, #5741; Cell Signaling Technology), and anti‐Snail (1:250, #26183‐1‐AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) antibodies; the samples were incubated at 4°C overnight and then incubated with Goat anti‐Rabbit Alexa Fluor 546 (#A‐11035; Invitrogen), Goat anti‐Rabbit Alexa Fluor Plus 680 (#A32734; Invitrogen), Goat anti‐Rabbit FITC (#F‐2765; Invitrogen), and Goat anti‐Rabbit TRITC (#T‐2769; Invitrogen) at a dilution of 1:200 for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved by incubating the samples with DAPI for 10 min. Afterward, the samples were transferred to slides and observed via confocal microscopy (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Mouse CRC Liver Metastasis Model

Six to 8 weeks female BALB/c athymic nude mice with body weights ranging from 18 to 22 g were obtained from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co. Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). All animals were housed in individually ventilated cages under conditions of controlled temperature and humidity. They were maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle within a standard laboratory setting and had ad libitum access to autoclaved chow and sterilized water in a specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) facility. The mice were anesthetized with an anesthetic agent (compound anesthetic: 20 mg/mL ketamine, 3 mg/mL xylazine, 5 μL/g dosage, intramuscular injection). After anesthesia, a transverse incision of approximately 1 cm was made at the skin site approximately 1–2 cm below the left chest rib. The spleen was gently pulled out of the body, and approximately 50 μL of an HT29 cell suspension containing approximately 5 × 106 cells was slowly injected at one end of the spleen. The spleen was gently pushed back into the abdominal cavity. Finally, the wound was sutured and sterilized, and an analgesic was administered. All the instruments were sterilized before use, and all the operations were performed in a sterile workbench.

At 14 days post tumor cell implantation, the animals were randomly assigned to either the ADAMTS5 inhibitor treatment arm or the vehicle control (DMSO) arm, with each cohort comprising eight mice. On the day of random grouping, the first dose was administered according to the experimental design. The compound was delivered intravenously through the tail vein at a dosage of 4.8 mg/kg body weight, repeated three times per week over a 3‐week period. The body weights of the mice were measured twice a week after the start of treatment. After 3 weeks of treatment, they were euthanized and dissected, and the animals, spleen, and liver were photographed separately. The animals were euthanized as follows: Nude mice were transferred into an induction chamber, and carbon dioxide was introduced at a displacement rate of 50%–70% of the chamber volume per minute. The nude mice were confirmed to be immobile and not breathing with dilated pupils. Following termination of CO2 flow, the animals were monitored for a 2‐min period to ascertain mortality. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting guidelines. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Lanly Bioscience (Suzhou) Co. Ltd. (#AM‐2024‐0024).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

The liver tissues of the mice were immersed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for fixation, followed by paraffin embedding. Serial sections of approximately 5 μm in thickness were prepared, subsequently deparaffinized, and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (#C0105; Beyotime). Then, the sections were observed under a microscope and photographed, and representative images were randomly selected.

Statistical Analysis

All in vitro experiments were independently replicated at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3 per group). In vivo experiments employed biologically independent replicates using distinct, independent individual animals, and the results are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). The data collected were analyzed via GraphPad Prism 9.0. Use an unpaired Student's t‐test to compare differences between two treatment groups, and use one‐way ANOVA combined with post hoc tests to compare differences among three or more treatment groups. Significant differences are indicated as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

High Expression of ADAMTS5 in CRC Is Associated With a Poor Prognosis

A search of the GEPIA database revealed that the expression of ADAMTS5 increased with increasing tumor stage (Figure 1A). We subsequently assessed the prognostic significance of ADAMTS5 expression in patients with CRC. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with high ADAMTS5 expression had shorter overall survival and disease‐free survival than did those with low ADAMTS5 expression (Figure 1B,C). We selected 15 samples of CRC tissues (T) and paired adjacent normal tissues (N) from CRC patients who underwent surgical treatment and were definitively diagnosed postoperatively through pathological examination (five patients each for stages I, II, and III) and analyzed the expression of ADAMTS5 via RT‐qPCR. ADAMTS5 was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues compared with normal tissues (p < 0.01) (Figure 1D). Furthermore, we selected 30 CRC patients and divided them into groups (10 patients each for stages I, II, and III) using the same criteria, and RT‐qPCR and Western blotting were performed. The results revealed that the expression level of ADAMTS5 increased slightly with increasing CRC stage (Figure 1E,F), which is consistent with the bioinformatics results. In addition, Western blotting results revealed that the expression levels of ADAMTS5 were relatively greater in CRC cell lines (HCT116, HT29, LoVo, SW480, and HCT8) than in the normal human intestinal epithelial cell line (HIEC) (Figure 1G). In summary, the expression of ADAMTS5 is significantly greater in CRC tissues than in normal tissues and is associated with a poor prognosis.

ADAMTS5 Inhibitor Treatment Suppresses the Proliferation, Invasion, and Migration of CRC Cells

To explore the biological effects of ADAMTS5 inhibition on CRC cells, we selected HCT116 and HT29 cells for subsequent experiments, as these cells had the highest ADAMTS5 levels among the panel of cell lines assessed. First, CRC cells were treated with different concentrations of the ADAMTS5 inhibitor (#HY‐114996; MCE, NJ, USA), and cell viability was assessed via the CCK‐8 assay. The results revealed that the proliferative activity of HCT116 and HT29 cells was reduced after treatment with various concentrations of the ADAMTS5 inhibitor, indicating that this ADAMTS5 inhibitor suppresses the proliferation of CRC cells in a dose‐dependent manner. The half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the ADAMTS5 inhibitor for HCT116 cells after 48 h was approximately 27 μM, and for HT29 cells, it was approximately 43 μM, as calculated via GraphPad 9.0 software (Figure 2A). Therefore, we selected the above concentrations for subsequent experiments with HCT116 and HT29 cells. Additionally, we conducted a CCK‐8 assay, which demonstrated that the ADAMTS5 inhibitor suppressed the proliferation of colon cancer cells in a time‐dependent manner (Figure 2B). The results of the EdU assay provided additional support for these conclusions (Figure 2C). Consistently, the Transwell assay results confirmed that the ADAMTS5 inhibitor suppressed the migration and invasion of HCT116 and HT29 cells (Figure 2D). Compared with control treatment, treatment with the ADAMTS5 inhibitor increased the expression of E‐cadherin and decreased the expression of vimentin and Snail (Figure 2E). These results demonstrated that treatment with an ADAMTS5 inhibitor significantly suppressed the proliferation, migration, and EMT of CRC cells.

ADAMTS5 Knockdown Suppressed CRC Cell Proliferation, Migration and Invasion

For a more in‐depth validation of the biological role of ADAMTS5, we conducted experiments aimed at suppressing ADAMTS5 expression in HCT116 and HT29 cells. Plasmids were used to knock down ADAMTS5, and Western blotting was subsequently used to assess protein levels. We selected sh‐ADAMTS5‐1 and sh‐ADAMTS5‐2 for subsequent experiments because they effectively induced ADAMTS5 knockdown (Figure 3A). Similar to previous experiments, CCK‐8 assays were used to detect the proliferation of HCT116 and HT29 cells after ADAMTS5 knockdown. The proliferation of these CRC cells was significantly inhibited (Figure 3B). The results of the EdU assay revealed that ADAMTS5 knockdown inhibited the proliferation of HCT116 and HT29 cells, which was consistent with the results of the CCK‐8 assay (Figure 3C). Additionally, the Transwell assay confirmed that the downregulation of ADAMTS5 inhibited the migration of HCT116 and HT29 cells (Figure 3D). Similarly, the Western blotting results revealed that, compared with the sh‐NC group, the ADAMTS5‐knockdown group presented increased expression of E‐cadherin and decreased expression of vimentin and Snail (Figure 3E). These findings indicate that ADAMTS5 knockdown effectively inhibits the proliferation, migration, and EMT of CRC cells. These findings also support the prospective application of an ADAMTS5 inhibitor in the treatment of CRC.

ADAMTS5 Inhibition Suppressed EMT in CRC Organoids

PDO models of cancer largely maintain the heterogeneity of the primary tumor, which makes them more relevant than traditional cell‐based models for identifying therapeutic targets and validating drug responses; thus, they are valuable tools for simulating tumorigenesis, metastatic progression, and therapeutic responses [27, 28]. In this study, we successfully constructed PDOs. The process of organoid collection and culture of CRC liver metastases is shown in Figure 4A. To assess the growth status of the organoids, images of the organoids were taken daily for 1 week after resuscitation from frozen samples (Figure 4B). To further evaluate the organoids, we performed IF staining and found that ADAMTS5 inhibitor treatment increased the expression of E‐cadherin and decreased the expression of ADAMTS5, vimentin, and Snail (Figure 4C), indicating that both ADAMTS5 expression and EMT were inhibited in the organoids; these results were consistent with the results of our cell line‐based experiments. In conclusion, ADAMTS5 inhibition effectively suppressed EMT in organoids, which further confirmed the reliability of our in vitro experimental results.

ADAMTS5 Inhibition Suppressed CRC Cell Invasion and Metastasis via Inhibition of the Wnt/β‐Catenin Signaling Pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of ADAMTS5 inhibition on CRC cells, we utilized the STRING database to predict the protein interacted with ADAMTS5. Functional enrichment revealed a strong correlation between the ADAMTS5 and Wnt signaling pathway (Figure 5A). A large body of evidence has shown that activation of the canonical Wnt/β‐catenin pathway affects cell proliferation, differentiation, organ development, and tumorigenesis [30, 31]. Therefore, we hypothesized that ADAMTS5 may regulate CRC cell proliferation, EMT, and migration by cleaving and degrading various ECM components, causing ECM remodeling and subsequent modulation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. The results of the Western blotting analysis of critical Wnt‐β‐catenin pathway components confirmed our hypothesis. Upon the addition of the ADAMTS5 inhibitor, the expression of β‐catenin, p‐GSK3β, and the downstream target genes TCF4, LEF1, Cyclin D1, and c‐Myc was downregulated (Figure 5B and Figure S1A). We subsequently treated the cells with an ADAMTS5 inhibitor followed by LiCl, a GSK3β inhibitor that activates β‐catenin‐mediated Wnt/β‐catenin signaling [32]. We found that LiCl could reactivate the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway and restore the expression of β‐catenin, p‐GSK3β, and the downstream target genes TCF4, LEF1, Cyclin D1, and c‐Myc (Figure 5C and Figure S1B). Furthermore, dual luciferase assays revealed that the addition of ADAMTS5 inhibitors reduced the level of luciferase activity, which led to the suppression of Wnt signaling pathway activation. Conversely, the addition of LiCl restored the level of luciferase activity, indicating that the Wnt signaling pathway was reactivated (Figure S1C,D). Additionally, LiCl reduced E‐cadherin expression and increased Vimentin and Snail expression at the protein level, suggesting that LiCl rescues the EMT phenotype of CRC cells (Figure 5D). Moreover, Transwell assays revealed that the activation of β‐catenin by LiCl attenuated the inhibitory effect of ADAMTS5 inhibition on cell migration (Figure 5E). These results indicate that ADAMTS5 may maintain cancer stemness and the mesenchymal phenotype through the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway and that ADAMTS5 inhibition can suppress the invasion and metastasis of CRC cells by inhibiting this pathway.

ADAMTS5 Regulates the Wnt/β‐Catenin Signaling Pathway via PEDF in CRC

Pigment epithelium‐derived factor (PEDF), an anti‐oncogene, possesses antiangiogenic, anti‐inflammatory, antitumor growth, and antimetastatic properties [33, 34]. Kumar et al. [16] reported that the overexpression of ADAMTS5 in mouse B16 melanoma downregulates the expression of PEDF. On the basis of our previous experimental results, we hypothesized that ADAMTS5 regulates the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway through PEDF in CRC. The RT‐qPCR and Western blotting analyses demonstrated that PEDF expression was markedly increased in HCT116 and HT29 cells treated with the ADAMTS5 inhibitor (Figure 6A,B). Co‐IP assay results further validated the functional interaction between ADAMTS5 and PEDF (Figure 6C). We then transiently transfected HCT116 and HT29 cells with si‐PEDF or si‐NC to inhibit PEDF expression. After verifying the knockdown efficiency of si‐PEDF by Western blotting (Figure 6D), we used si‐PEDF‐3 and si‐PEDF‐4 to transfect HCT116 and HT29 cells that were simultaneously treated with the ADAMTS5 inhibitor. The Western blotting results revealed that the introduction of si‐PEDF led to a significant reduction in PEDF expression. Additionally, a reduction in PEDF expression effectively counteracted the inhibitory effect of ADAMTS5 inhibition on β‐catenin in HCT116 and HT29 cells (Figure 6E). These findings indicate that ADAMTS5 regulates the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway through PEDF in CRC.

ADAMTS5 Inhibition Suppressed the Growth and Metastasis of CRC In Vivo

To investigate the effect of an ADAMTS5 inhibitor on CRC tumor proliferation in vivo, we established an orthotopic liver metastasis mouse model by injecting HT29 cells into the spleens of mice. We subsequently intravenously administered the ADAMTS5 inhibitor (4.8 mg/kg) to examine its effect on tumor growth and metastasis, with DMSO used as the vehicle control. Measurements of mouse body weights were performed twice per week subsequent to treatment commencement. After 3 weeks of treatment, the animals were euthanized and dissected, and the animals and their livers were photographed (Figure 7A,B). Compared with the control, the ADAMTS5 inhibitor markedly suppressed tumor growth and metastasis in vivo (Figure 7C). We subsequently performed H&E staining of liver samples from both groups, which revealed that the proportion of tumor lesions in the liver samples from the ADAMTS5 inhibitor group was significantly lower than that in the control group (Figure 7D,E). We then selected three mouse liver samples from each group and detected the expression of ADAMTS5 in the metastatic tumors via Western blotting and RT‐qPCR. The results revealed that the xenografts from the ADAMTS5 inhibitor group presented significantly reduced expression of ADAMTS5 (Figure 7F,G). These results indicated that ADAMTS5 inhibition markedly decreased the metastatic potential of CRC cells in the livers of the mice.

Discussion

In this study, we focused on the role of ADAMTS5 in CRC progression and metastasis and its underlying molecular mechanisms. Our results revealed that the expression level of ADAMTS5 was significantly greater in CRC tissues than in adjacent noncancer tissues and that it gradually increased with increasing tumor stage. Moreover, we observed that high expression of ADAMTS5 can indicate poor CRC prognosis and poor patient survival.

As a crucial metalloproteinase in the extracellular matrix, ADAMTS5 plays a pivotal role in malignant tumors [35]. However, the role of ADAMTS5 in various cancer types is inconsistent. It was previously reported that ADAMTS5 acts as an oncogene in gliomas [36], whereas Huang et al. [37] reported that ADAMTS5 functions as a tumor suppressor to inhibit migration, invasion, and neovascularization in human gastric cancer. This apparent contradiction highlights the importance of cellular context, substrate specificity, and microenvironmental factors in determining the net effect of ADAMTS5. In colorectal cancer, the role of ADAMTS5 remains less explored. Here, our study revealed that the inhibition of ADAMTS5 suppressed the proliferation, migration, and survival of CRC cells. Moreover, Western blotting of EMT‐related molecules revealed that ADAMTS5 inhibition significantly suppressed the invasive phenotype of CRC cells by inhibiting the EMT of CRC cells. In addition, we further verified that ADAMTS5 knockdown inhibited the migration, EMT, and migration of CRC cells via plasmid transfection with sh‐ADAMTS5‐1 or sh‐ADAMTS5‐2; these findings aligned with those of the ADAMTS5 inhibitor treatment. To further assess the role of ADAMTS5, we constructed patient‐derived organoids and demonstrated that ADAMTS5 inhibition suppressed EMT in these organoids. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that EMT is a prerequisite for initial tumor cell migration and invasion, which lead to the recurrence and metastasis of most tumors [21, 38]. Therefore, targeted modulation of the biochemical composition of EMT has emerged as a modality for cancer therapy. Collectively, these results indicate that ADAMTS5 can be used as a potential therapeutic target for CRC; thus, ADAMTS5 inhibitors are promising treatment options for CRC.

Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway is a classical signaling mechanism associated with the occurrence, progression, and metastasis of various tumors, especially CRC [39, 40]. When the pathway is activated, it can increase the amount of β‐catenin protein in the nucleus to regulate the expression of target genes (cyclin D1, c‐Myc, etc.), which is key to tumor progression and metastasis. Targeting the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway is considered a new opportunity and challenge for CRC treatment [31, 41, 42]. In this study, we predicted ADAMTS5‐interacting genes through an online site. The Wnt pathway caught our attention. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that downregulation of ADAMTS5 inhibited the EMT and metastasis of CRC cells by suppressing the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway. Furthermore, activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway in vitro reversed the inhibitory effect of ADAMTS5 inhibition on the EMT and metastasis of CRC cells.

PEDF is an anti‐oncogene that has been demonstrated to have antiangiogenic, antitumor, and antimetastatic properties in many studies [33, 43, 44, 45]. Early studies revealed a regulatory relationship between PEDF and the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. For example, in a study by Protiva et al. [34], PEDF was found to inhibit Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in the liver by suppressing LRP6, and Kim et al. [46] identified PEDF as a hepatic‐derived soluble Wnt inhibitor that suppresses intestinal stem cell (ISC) hyperproliferation to inhibit Wnt/β‐catenin signaling. In this study, we observed elevated expression of PEDF in HCT116 and HT29 cells treated with an ADAMTS5 inhibitor. Importantly, PEDF knockdown eliminated the effect of ADAMTS5 silencing on β‐catenin expression in CRC cells, as evidenced by the Western blotting results. However, the mechanism by which ADAMTS5 regulates PEDF is currently unknown, and the precise mechanism requires additional investigation.

In addition, to further verify the effect of the ADAMTS5 inhibitor on CRC cells in vivo, we successfully constructed a xenograft mouse model with tumor liver metastasis, and the study's findings additionally revealed that the ADAMTS5 inhibitor significantly reduced the metastatic potential of CRC cells in the mouse liver, which is in line with the results of our in vitro experiments in this study. However, we acknowledge that previous CRC studies on related ADAMTS5 have yielded variable results [47]. A direct comparison with those studies is limited by differences in experimental models, readouts, and clinical cohorts. Future studies using standardized approaches and larger patient cohorts are needed to clarify the precise role of ADAMTS5 in CRC and to resolve apparent discrepancies.

This study has several limitations. First, although extensive literature indicates that the primary functions of the ADAMTS5 family proteins depend on their proteolytic activity [48, 49, 50], certain members have also been reported to possess non‐catalytic functions [51, 52]. In this investigation, we did not thoroughly explore whether ADAMTS5 regulates the PEDF/Wnt/β‐catenin pathway through its enzymatic activity or alternative mechanisms; our results only tentatively suggest a correlation between them, and this will be the focus of our next phase of work. Second, the relatively limited sample size may constrain the generalizability of our findings and potentially increase the risk of detecting false positives or false negatives. Meanwhile, the strength of clinical conclusions is limited by the lack of a comprehensive investigation into the associations with clinically relevant outcomes, such as survival rates and specific clinical characteristics. Consequently, further studies involving larger patient populations are warranted to corroborate our findings on the clinical significance of ADAMTS5 in CRC. Third, we did not systematically collect standardized and consistent data on treatment regimens and treatment response assessments from eligible patients. This precluded an association analysis between ADAMTS5 expression levels and patient therapeutic outcomes. Fourth, in the PDO experiment, we selected only one representative PDO, which limits the generalizability of the results. Future studies should involve multiple patient‐derived organoids to validate the reliability of these findings. Fifth, in the in vivo experiments, we had only six samples per group, which to some extent compromised the rigor of the study. Finally, our research was conducted exclusively in nude mouse models, and its direct relevance to human pathophysiology remains unclear. Given the potential specific differences in the Wnt signaling pathway and ADAMTS5, as well as the significant differences in the tumor microenvironment and immune status of nude mice compared to human patients, future validation using human samples or patient‐derived xenograft models is required. Meanwhile, the widespread expression and multifunctionality of ADAMTS5 across multiple tissues pose significant difficulties for its development as a therapeutic target. Therefore, translating these fundamental research findings into clinical practice still faces numerous challenges.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study investigated the role and molecular mechanisms of ADAMTS5 in CRC progression, and the effects of an ADAMTS5 inhibitor on the proliferation, invasion, and migration of CRC cells. The results of this study demonstrated that inhibition of ADAMTS5 suppresses the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of CRC cells through the PEDF/Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway (Figure 8). Therefore, targeting the ADAMTS5/PEDF/Wnt/β‐catenin axis may be a feasible therapeutic strategy for primary and metastatic CRC.
