Radiotherapy for Recurrent Intracranial Epidermoid Cysts: A Case Report With 3-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Bomi Choi, Shin-Woong Ko, Myung-Hoon Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracranial epidermoid cysts have high recurrence rates after surgical resection, and alternative strategies are needed when additional surgery is not feasible. We report a 46-year-old woman with a right cerebellopontine angle epidermoid cyst that recurred three times after surgical resections in 2010, 2016, and 2019, complicated by hydrocephalus requiring ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. At the third recurrence in 2023, the patient refused further surgery, and radiotherapy (RT) to 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions was delivered using a Novalis Tx linear accelerator. During RT, partial neurological improvement was observed. However, approximately one month after RT completion, neurological deterioration occurred, likely attributable to radiation-induced peritumoral edema and tumor swelling, which persisted for approximately two years. Beginning at 2 years post-RT, progressive neurological improvement was observed with radiographic tumor regression. At 3-year post-RT follow-up, the patient continues to improve, with no evidence of malignant transformation. This case uniquely demonstrates the therapeutic effect of standalone RT without concurrent surgical debulking, characterized by a delayed response pattern of transient post-RT worsening followed by gradual improvement.Keywords: Cerebellopontine angle; Epidermoid cyst; Neoplasm recurrence, local; Radiotherapy; Treatment outcome

INTRODUCTION

Intracranial epidermoid cysts are rare, slow-growing, benign lesions that account for approximately 0.5%–1.8% of all intracranial tumors [1]. These lesions are thought to arise from ectodermal cell remnants trapped during neural tube closure in early embryogenesis and most commonly occur in the cerebellopontine angle (CPA), where these lesions represent the third most common CPA mass after vestibular schwannomas and meningiomas [2]. Epidermoid cysts are characterized by a thin capsule of stratified squamous epithelium that progressively desquamates keratin, cholesterol crystals, and cellular debris into the cyst cavity, resulting in gradual volumetric expansion along the cisterns and around neurovascular structures [1].

The standard treatment for symptomatic intracranial epidermoid cysts is surgical resection [2]. While gross total resection is the ideal goal, complete capsule removal is frequently not achievable due to the intimate adherence of the cyst wall to critical neurovascular structures, particularly the cranial nerves and brainstem [2,3,4]. Subtotal resection inevitably predisposes to tumor recurrence, with reported rates ranging from 10% to 47% over long-term follow-up [3,4]. Each successive surgical exploration carries incrementally higher morbidity owing to progressive adhesion formation and distorted anatomy [5].

In cases where surgical options are exhausted or declined by the patient, alternative therapeutic strategies are limited. Radiotherapy (RT) has been proposed as a potential treatment for recurrent epidermoid cysts, based on the radioresponsiveness of the proliferating squamous epithelium that constitutes the cyst wall [5]. Notably, epidermoid cysts are not considered highly radiosensitive in the classical sense; rather, the squamous epithelial lining of these cysts exhibits a degree of radioresponsiveness that is distinct from that of truly radiosensitive tumors such as germinomas or lymphomas. However, the evidence supporting this approach remains limited to a few case reports and retrospective case series [5,6,7,8]. The largest published series, by Morshed et al. [5] from UCSF (University of California San Francisco), reported eight patients treated with adjuvant RT (50.4 Gy in 28 fractions) following subtotal resection, demonstrating no tumor progression or malignant transformation during the reported follow-up period. All previously reported cases involved RT administered as adjuvant treatment after subtotal resection or for residual disease, thereby making isolating the therapeutic effect of RT alone challenging.

Thus, this study aimed to report a unique case of a recurrent epidermoid CPA cyst treated with standalone RT after three prior surgical resections, with a 3-year post-RT follow-up demonstrating a characteristic delayed response pattern of transient neurological worsening followed by progressive improvement and tumor regression. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the isolated therapeutic effect of RT without concurrent surgical debulking over a prolonged observation period.

CASE REPORT

A 33-year-old woman presented with a persistent headache. MRI revealed a large cystic lesion in the right CPA, demonstrating characteristic features of an epidermoid cyst: hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging, heterogeneous signal on T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences, and no contrast enhancement (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, the patient underwent surgical resection in September 2010 via a right combined subtemporal transpetrosal and retrosigmoid approach, incorporating posterior petrosectomy for improved access to the CPA. Histopathological examination confirmed an epidermoid cyst. Postoperative CT confirmed subtotal resection anticipated owing to the large initial tumor volume and intimate adherence to surrounding neurovascular structures (Fig. 1B). The postoperative course was uneventful, and her headache resolved.

Follow-up MRI in March 2013 showed no evidence of recurrence. However, by August 2016, MRI demonstrated significant recurrent tumor growth in the right CPA (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, a second surgical resection was performed in September 2016 via a right combined subtemporal transpetrosal approach with partial posterior petrosectomy. Similarly, complete capsule removal was not achievable due to firm adhesion to the brainstem and cranial nerves.

In December 2018, the patient developed symptoms of obstructive hydrocephalus, which were confirmed through CT. A ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt was placed. In June 2019, follow-up MRI revealed a second recurrence with expansion of the residual tumor (Fig. 1D). The patient also developed ventriculitis, necessitating shunt removal and placement of external ventricular drainage. A third surgical resection was performed in July 2019 via a right retrosigmoid suboccipital craniotomy. The VP shunt was subsequently re-implanted (Fig. 1E).

By February 2023, MRI demonstrated a third recurrence with significant tumor regrowth in the right CPA and posterior fossa (Fig. 1F). Now 46 years old, the patient presented with progressive dysarthria and right-sided motor weakness. Owing to the escalating morbidity of repeated craniotomies, the patient refused further surgery, and RT was recommended as an alternative therapeutic strategy.

RT was delivered using a Novalis Tx linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems/Brainlab AG) over six weeks in spring 2023. The prescribed dose was 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions (1.8 Gy per fraction), consistent with the protocol established by Morshed et al. [5]. Treatment planning utilized volumetric modulated arc therapy to optimize dose conformity while minimizing radiation exposure to the brainstem and adjacent critical structures (Fig. 2).

During RT, CT imaging showed a stable tumor size, and the patient experienced partial improvement in both dysarthria and motor weakness (Fig. 3A). However, approximately one month after completion of RT, the patient developed progressive neurological worsening with deterioration of motor function and speech. This worsening was presumed to be attributable to a combination of radiation-induced peritumoral edema and transient swelling of the irradiated tumor mass, which, in the absence of prior surgical debulking, likely exerted increased compressive effects on the adjacent brainstem and cranial nerves. Dexamethasone was initiated at 8 mg/day and tapered gradually over approximately three months. Given the persistence of neurological deficits, the patient was subsequently transitioned to oral prednisolone, which was maintained at a low dose for approximately six months. Despite partial symptomatic relief with corticosteroid therapy, neurological deficits persisted throughout this worsening phase. This phase lasted for approximately two years. The follow-up MRI, performed at 1-year post-RT, demonstrated a persistent large tumor with surrounding edema (Fig. 3B).

Beginning at approximately two years post-RT, the patient began to show gradual neurological improvement. MRI at 2.5 years post-RT demonstrated an appreciable reduction in tumor size compared with the 1-year study (Fig. 3C). The most recent MRI at three years post-RT showed stable tumor size without significant interval change compared with the 2.5-year post-RT study (Fig. 3D); however, the patient continued to demonstrate gradual neurological improvement in motor function and dysarthria. At the 3-year post-RT follow-up, no evidence of malignant transformation was observed.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights several important aspects of RT for recurrent intracranial epidermoid cysts: 1) the feasibility of standalone RT as a therapeutic option when further surgery is not feasible, 2) the characteristic delayed response pattern, and 3) the favorable safety profile at medium-term follow-up.

Epidermoid cysts grow through a unique mechanism: the squamous epithelial lining of the cyst wall continuously desquamates keratin and other cellular debris into the cyst cavity, leading to progressive volumetric expansion [1,9]. This proliferating epithelium is analogous to other squamous epithelial-lined lesions, such as craniopharyngiomas, which share a degree of radioresponsiveness [10]. RT targets the mitotically active basal layer of the cyst epithelium, inducing DNA damage that halts cell division and, consequently, desquamation. Once new debris production ceases, the contents of the existing cyst are gradually resorbed by the surrounding brain parenchyma, resulting in delayed tumor shrinkage [5].

The literature on RT for intracranial epidermoid cysts is sparse but growing (Table 1). Parikh et al. [7] reported a single case of symptom resolution and tumor shrinkage following conventional RT to 45 Gy in 1995. Meanwhile, in 2013, Davies et al. [6] reported three patients treated with 50–54 Gy in 1.8–2.0 Gy fractions, with no tumor progression at a median follow-up of 3.5 years. The largest and most influential series is by Morshed et al. [5] from UCSF, who reported eight patients treated with adjuvant RT (50.4 Gy in 28 fractions) following subtotal resection, with no tumor progression or malignant transformation at a median follow-up of 2.9 years. More recently, Verma et al. [8] published a systematic review of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) for intracranial epidermoid tumors, reporting no malignant transformation among the cases reviewed. Our case employed the same dose regimen as the UCSF series (50.4 Gy in 28 fractions) and achieved a comparable outcome. Importantly, our case is unique in that RT was administered as a standalone treatment without any concurrent or preceding surgical debulking at the time of the third recurrence, providing a rare opportunity to observe the pure therapeutic effect of RT on an epidermoid cyst.

A key finding of this case is the characteristic delayed response pattern following RT. We observed three distinct phases: 1) transient improvement during RT (March–May 2023), 2) neurological and radiological worsening for approximately two years post-RT (June 2023–June 2025), and 3) gradual neurological improvement with tumor regression beginning at approximately two years post-RT (June 2025–present) (Fig. 4). The initial transient improvement during the RT course may be explained by an early reduction in desquamation rate as the proliferating epithelium is damaged, leading to a mild decompressive effect. The subsequent worsening is most likely attributable to a combination of radiation-induced peritumoral edema and transient swelling of the irradiated tumor mass, which, in the absence of surgical debulking, may have exerted increased compressive effects on the brainstem and adjacent cranial nerves. Radiation-induced edema is a well-recognized complication of irradiation to the posterior fossa, particularly when the treatment field involves structures adjacent to the brainstem [5]. The delayed improvement beginning at approximately two years is consistent with the time required for resorption of accumulated cyst contents after cessation of new debris production. This timeline aligns with the biological mechanism of RT in epidermoid cysts: while the epithelial component demonstrates a degree of radioresponsiveness and responds gradually, the keratin and cholesterol debris within the cyst cavity are acellular and must be slowly cleared by the surrounding tissue through phagocytosis and natural resorption processes [1,5]. This delayed response pattern may have important clinical implications. Based on our observations, neurological worsening following RT does not necessarily indicate treatment failure; however, neurological worsening may instead represent a phase of the therapeutic response. Although further evidence is needed, premature intervention based on early post-RT worsening should be considered with caution, as delayed improvement may follow.

A major concern regarding RT for epidermoid cysts has been the potential for radiation-induced malignant transformation to squamous cell carcinoma [11,12]. While malignant transformation of epidermoid cysts has been reported in the literature, this process is exceedingly rare and has been documented even in untreated cases, suggesting an intrinsic malignant potential independent of radiation exposure [11]. In the UCSF series, no cases of malignant transformation were observed among eight patients with a median follow-up of 2.9 years [5]. The systematic review by Verma et al. [8] reported no malignant transformation across the reviewed cases of SRS for epidermoid tumors. In our case, no evidence of malignant transformation was observed at 3-year post-RT follow-up. Nevertheless, the theoretical risk mandates long-term MRI surveillance with vigilance for any changes suggestive of malignant degeneration, including new contrast enhancement, rapid growth, or development of peritumoral edema on follow-up imaging.

Several studies have also reported the use of SRS for intracranial epidermoid cysts (Table 2). Kida et al. [13] first reported the use of Gamma Knife radiosurgery for epidermoid tumors in 2006, treating seven patients with a mean marginal dose of 14.6 Gy and achieving 100% tumor control with symptom relief at a mean follow-up of 52.7 months. Subsequent series by Vasquez et al. (three cases) [14] and El-Shehaby et al. (12 cases, the largest SRS series to date) [15] confirmed favorable tumor control with Gamma Knife. Lieberson et al. [16] demonstrated that CyberKnife radiosurgery could also control recurrent epidermoid cysts. The systematic review by Verma et al. [8], pooling data from 38 patients across six studies, reported 77.7% complete symptom relief following SRS.

The distinctiveness of this case lies in the administration of RT as a standalone treatment without concurrent surgical debulking. In all previously reported series, RT was delivered either as an adjuvant to subtotal resection or for residual tumor following surgery [5,6,7,8]. The absence of surgical intervention at the time of RT in our case provides an unconfounded observation of the therapeutic effect of RT on an epidermoid cyst. Therefore, the documented tumor regression and neurological improvement can be attributed solely to RT, suggesting the potential efficacy of RT as an independent treatment modality. However, additional cases and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm this observation.

This report has several limitations inherent to the case–report design. First, the findings from a single patient cannot be generalized, and the optimal dose, fractionation, and patient selection criteria for RT in epidermoid cysts remain to be established through larger studies. Second, the 3-year post-RT follow-up, while longer than in most published reports, may not be sufficient to fully assess the long-term risks, particularly malignant transformation, which has been reported decades after initial diagnosis [11]. Third, the degree of tumor regression and neurological improvement observed may not be representative of all patients, as outcomes likely vary with tumor location, size, and extent of prior surgical manipulation. Fourth, the mechanisms underlying the delayed-response pattern, while biologically plausible, have not been confirmed by histopathological analysis.

In conclusion, RT (50.4 Gy in 28 fractions) represents a viable salvage therapeutic option for recurrent intracranial epidermoid cysts when further surgical intervention is not feasible. In this case, RT alone produced meaningful tumor regression and progressive neurological improvement over a 3-year follow-up, without evidence of malignant transformation. Clinicians should anticipate a characteristic delayed-response pattern, including transient post-RT neurological deterioration followed by gradual improvement over approximately two years, before determining that treatment has failed. Long-term MRI surveillance remains essential, and larger prospective studies are warranted to define optimal RT treatment parameters in this setting.
