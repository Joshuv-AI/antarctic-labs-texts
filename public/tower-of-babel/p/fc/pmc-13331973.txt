Retreatability of root canals filled with gutta percha and a novel bioceramic sealer- a scanning electron microscopy study
Authors: DSV Sindhuja, Prashant Bhasin, Hemanshi Kumar, Alfredo Iandolo, Dina Abdellatif, Lubna Ahmad, Alfonso Acerra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare effectiveness of 5% acetic acid, 10% formic acid, and chloroform used adjunctively with a rotary retreatment system in removing a premixed calcium-silicate sealer (Ceraseal) and gutta-percha from root canals, using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to quantify residuals across canal thirds.Materials and methodsThirty-six extracted single-rooted anterior teeth were instrumented, obturated (single-cone GP + Ceraseal), and stored for two weeks. Specimens were retreated with HyFlex Remover files and randomly assigned (n = 9/group) to: G1—5% acetic acid; G2— 10% formic acid; G3—chloroform; G4—distilled water (negative control). Two milliliters of test solution were used with repeated instrumentation until patency; canals were then irrigated with saline and dried. Roots were split; standardized SEM images (×1000) of coronal, middle, and apical thirds were scored.ResultsIntragroup, residuals increased apically in all solvent groups: in G1, apical > middle > coronal (all p = 0.021); in G2, apical > coronal/middle (p = 0.021); in G3, apical > coronal (p = 0.021); G4 showed no significant intragroup differences. Intergroup, coronal third: G1 had significantly lower residuals than G3 and G4; G1 ≈ G2; G2 ≈ G3 ≈ G4. Middle third: G1 and G2 < G4 (p = 0.021); no other pairwise differences. Apical third: no significant between-group differences.ConclusionsWhen combined with HyFlex Remover files, 5% acetic and 10% formic acids enhanced removal of Ceraseal/gutta-percha, particularly in coronal third; 5% acetic outperformed chloroform coronally. In middle and apical thirds, acidic solvents, chloroform, and water yielded broadly comparable outcomes.

Introduction

Non-surgical endodontic treatment exhibits a success rate between 86% and 93%.1 Failures nevertheless continue to occur due to numerous factors such as poor aseptic control, missed canals, inadequate root canal instrumentation and disinfection, improper obturation and microleakage via post endodontic restorations.2

A key determinant of retreatment success is the complete removal of existing root canal filling materials, particularly from complex anatomical areas where remnants may harbor microorganisms and compromise re-obturation.

The recent era has shown an increasing popularity of bioceramic (BC) sealers with premixed formulations like Ceraseal due to their biocompatibility, insolubility in tissue fluids, antibacterial, and antifungal properties.3 They possess hydrophilicity and set by up taking moisture in the canal.4 Sealer particles penetrate the dentinal tubules, while minerals diffuse into the intertubular dentin, forming a zone of mineral infiltration with hydroxyapatite deposition that establishes bonding with the dentin. While bioceramic sealers exhibit most of the characteristics expected of an ideal root canal sealer, concerns remain regarding their ease of retreatment and susceptibility to dissolution in endodontic solvents.2

The selection of 5% acetic acid and 10% formic acid was based on their previously reported effectiveness in facilitating the dissolution and retreatability of calcium silicate-based sealers. Weak organic acids have calcium-chelating and demineralising properties that may interact with the calcium-rich phases of bioceramic sealers, disrupting the calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) structure formed during setting.5 In particular, formic acid may react with calcium-containing compounds to form soluble calcium formate, thereby enhancing sealer disintegration.6 Acetic acid has also demonstrated the ability to improve regaining apical patency and reduce retreatment time in canals obturated with bioceramic sealers.7 The selected concentrations were chosen because they represent clinically applicable weak-acid formulations previously investigated in endodontic retreatment studies, while minimising excessive dentinal alteration associated with stronger acidic solutions.6 Unlike chloroform, which primarily softens gutta-percha, these acidic solvents are expected to chemically interact with the inorganic calcium-silicate matrix of bioceramic sealers, potentially improving their removal from canal walls and from anatomical irregularities.

Traditionally, gutta-percha was the main core filling material with minimal sealer. However, new hydraulic techniques use more BC sealer because of its biocompatibility, flow, and ability to bond with dentin. Consequently, the quantity of remaining sealer tends to be higher in intricate anatomical areas, including isthmuses, ramifications, and dentinal tubules.2

Many approaches have been recommended for easy removal of root filling materials while retreatment, namely heated pluggers, manual files, ultrasonic instruments, and different retreatment file systems in combination with chemical solvents.8 Offlately, HyFlex Remover retreatment file system (HR; Coltene/Whaledent, Allstatten, Switzerland) has been introduced which is heat treated with C wire technology. It has a non-cutting tip with a 1 mm diameter that is less-invasive.9 Nonetheless, multiple studies have demonstrated that residues of filling materials—particularly sealers located on the canal walls and within fine anatomical extensions—are difficult to reach with endodontic instruments. In these situations, organic solvents are essential for complete removal of endodontic sealers.10

In order to improve the effectiveness of its removal, a solvent which disintegrates set bioceramic sealer would be advantageous.2 Among various solvents used for retreatment, chloroform is the most widely employed.11 Although chloroform is commonly used to aid removal of obturation materials, concerns regarding its toxicity have prompted the search for safer alternatives.12

Given how sensitive the bioceramic sealer is to pH, organic acids represent another possible category of solvents for retreatment.2 Since bioceramic sealer is an emerging material, there is currently lack of evidence validating effectiveness of solvents in its dissolution. Organic acids such as acetic acid and formic acid may interact with calcium-based sealers and facilitate their removal. Therefore, evaluating the effectiveness of these solvents in combination with a rotary retreatment system for the removal of Ceraseal and gutta-percha from different root canal thirds is clinically important, as incomplete removal may compromise canal disinfection and the success of subsequent re-obturation.

Numerous methods, like digital radiography, confocal and optical microscopy, microcomputed tomography, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), have been used in the scientific literature to assess bioceramic material remnants.13 As SEM assesses the cleanliness of dentinal tubules, therefore, high-resolution SEM photomicrographs were utilised in this investigation to examine the bioceramic sealer residues in retreated canals that were earlier obturated with Ceraseal and gutta percha.

Due to the scarcity of studies investigating the impact of acidic solutions on the solubility of bioceramics, this study aimed to compare the efficacy of two different acidic agents i.e., 5% Acetic acid and 10% Formic acid with a traditional retreatment solvent i.e., chloroform in the dissolution of a premixed bioceramic sealer when used along with retreatment files.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board with approval number IEC/2023/02/01.

Sample selection

Thirty-six extracted human permanent anterior teeth were selected.

Inclusion criteria

Human permanent anterior teeth with a single root, extracted for purposes not associated with the study (e.g., periodontal/orthodontic). Single root and single canal configuration confirmed on mesiodistal and buccolingual periapical radiographs; absence of additional canals within the root. Fully formed apices, absence of previous endodontic treatment, cracks, resorption. Straight canals with curvature ≤10° by Schneider's technique.

Exclusion criteria

Multi-rooted teeth, roots with multiple canals, or evidence of accessory canals on screening radiographs. Immature apices, internal or external root resorption, or apical pathology compromising apical anatomy. Canal curvature >10°, S-shaped curvature, severe dilaceration, or anatomical anomalies. Calcific metamorphosis or canal obliteration preventing #10 K-file patency to the estimated working length.

Sample preparation

Following extraction, periodontal tissues were mechanically removed from external surfaces, and specimens were stored in physiological saline at 4 °C until experimentation. To standardize the sample length, each tooth was decoronated at cementoenamel junction, ensuring root length of 17 mm.

Operator standardization

All instrumentation, obturation, retreatment procedures, and specimen preparation were performed by a single experienced operator under standardised experimental conditions in order to minimise inter-operator variability. Prior to commencement of the study, the operator performed calibration procedures on pilot specimens to standardize canal preparation, obturation, and retreatment protocols.

Endodontic treatment

Canal instrumentation

The canal patency was checked and working length was determined using #15-K file until its tip was just visible at the major apical foramen; working length was then set 0.5 mm short of this length.

Canals were instrumented using JIZAI rotary files (Mani, Tochigi, Japan) with continuous motion in a sequence: 25/0.14, 13/0.04 (300 revolutions per minute and torque 1.0 N), 20/0.04, 25/0.04, 20/0.06 till size 25/0.06 in maxillary anterior teeth and in the same sequence till size 25/0.04 in mandibular anterior teeth at a rotation speed of 500 revolutions per minute and torque of 3.0 N.

Irrigation protocol

During canal instrumentation, irrigation was performed with a total of 5 mL of 3% NaOCl (Avarice, India), via a 30 G side-vented needle (SuperEndo, India) followed by rinsing with sterile saline (Swaroop Pharmaceuticals Pvt. Ltd, India), further irrigation with 3 mL of 17% EDTA (NeoEDTA, Orikam, India) for 1 min was performed for eliminating smear layer and lastly rinsed with 3 mL sterile saline. Patency was maintained throughout shaping by recapitulation with a #15 K-file to working length between instruments and reconfirmed after final irrigation.

Canal obturation

The canals were dried with absorbent paper points (Meta Biomed, Korea). All canals were obturated using a single cone matched taper gutta percha technique with Ceraseal (Meta Biomed, Korea) and were sealed coronally. The samples were then stored in an incubator (Navyug Udyog, India) at 37 °C in 100% humidity for 2 weeks to allow sealer to fully set.

Post-obturation storage conditions

These storage conditions were maintained to ensure complete setting and maturation of the calcium silicate-based sealer prior to retreatment procedures.

Following this, retreatment procedures were initiated immediately thereafter.

Specimen grouping

Specimens were randomly allocated to the four experimental groups (n = 9 per group) using a computer-generated randomisation sequence (Random Allocation Software version 2.0, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran). Allocation concealment was maintained until initiation of the retreatment procedures in order to minimise selection bias (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Decoronated anterior teeth divided as four experimental groups.

Retreatment protocol using hyflex remover

In each retreatment group, the following protocols were used: With the help of orifice modifier (25/0.09, HyFlex Remover, Coltene) that was inserted into the gutta-percha for 2–3 mm at 400–800 revolutions per minute and 2.5 N cm of torque, the cervical-third of the gutta-percha was removed (Fig. 2a). Following that, the remover file (30/0.07, HyFlex Remover, Coltene) was inserted into the orifice modifier-created opening and moved apically for a distance of 2–3 mm at 400–800 rpm and torque of 2.5 N cm till mild resistance to inward movement was felt (Fig. 2b). To eliminate all the gutta-percha, it was subsequently pulled out while leaning on specific canal walls.Fig. 2a) Coronal Gutta Percha removal using Hyflex Orifice Modifier, b) Remaining Gutta Percha removal using Hyflex remover file, c) Test solution being introduced into the canal, d) Remover file packed with dissolved Bioceramic Sealer And Debris.

a) Coronal Gutta Percha removal using Hyflex Orifice Modifier, b) Remaining Gutta Percha removal using Hyflex remover file, c) Test solution being introduced into the canal, d) Remover file packed with dissolved Bioceramic Sealer And Debris.

Retreatment protocol using respective solvents

Following instrumentation, 2 ml of group-specific test solutions were added to the entire canal using a 30 G side-vented needle (Fig. 2c and d). No agitation of the solvent was performed.

Objective retreatment endpoint criteria

The retreatment procedure was considered complete when apical patency was re-established with a #15 K-file reaching the full working length, and no visible remnants of obturation material were observed on the flutes of the retreatment instrument during the final instrumentation cycle.

After retreatment, the canals were re-shaped using JIZAI rotary files (Mani, Tochigi, Japan) in the same sequence mentioned earlier during initial preparation of the canals. After that, 3 ml of saline solution was utilised to irrigate the entire specimen, and absorbent paper points were used to dry the canal.

Specimen preparation for SEM examination

Grooves were made along the surface of the specimens vertically using a diamond disk (Atozshop11, India) and then samples were split longitudinally along the length of the root, into two halves using a chisel (GDC Fine Crafted Dental Pvt. Ltd., India). Three grooves were prepared in the root surface, indicating the coronal (9 mm from apex), middle (6 mm from apex), and apical (3 mm from apex) thirds (Fig. 3a). The sections were dried at room temperature for 24 h and then sputter-coated with gold using a sputter coater (BT150, HHV Limited, United Kingdom) and prepared for SEM analysis (EVO 18, Carl Zeiss, Germany) (Fig. 3b).Fig. 3a) Longitudinally split samples, b) Gold coated samples for SEM evalaution

a) Longitudinally split samples, b) Gold coated samples for SEM evalaution

SEM image evaluatuon

SEM images were obtained at a standard magnification of ×1000 at the coronal, middle and apical third of root canal using SmartSEM software. The residual material on dentin was evaluated using a predefined scale quoted by Kakoura F et al13:

Score 1: No or slight presence of remnants (0%–25%)

Score 2: Presence of some remnants (25%-50%)

Score 3: Frequent presence of remnants (50%–75%)

Score 4: Remnants present almost everywhere (75%–100%)

SEM image evaluation was performed under blinded conditions. All images were coded by an independent operator not involved in the scoring procedure, and the examiner remained unaware of group allocation during image assessment. After completion of the analysis, the images were decoded. Intra-examiner reproducibility was assessed by re-evaluating a randomly selected subset of images after a one-week interval, yielding a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.81, indicating substantial agreement.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, version XX; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.0) based on a Chi-square test for contingency tables, assuming an effect size of 0.6, α = 0.05, and statistical power of 80%, resulting in a minimum sample size of 36 specimens.

Since the SEM evaluation was based on a four-point categorical scoring system, the obtained data were considered ordinal in nature. Therefore, non-parametric statistical methods were applied throughout the analysis. Descriptive statistics were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), together with frequency distributions of the SEM scores for each experimental group and canal third. Because SEM scores represented ordinal categorical data, medians and interquartile ranges were considered more appropriate descriptive statistics than arithmetic means and confidence intervals.

Intragroup comparisons among the coronal, middle, and apical thirds were performed using the Friedman test, followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pairwise comparisons. Intergroup comparisons at each canal level were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When statistically significant differences were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing and reduce the risk of type I error.

Exact p-values are reported throughout the manuscript. Effect sizes were additionally calculated to evaluate the magnitude of differences observed between groups (η2 values for Kruskal–Wallis analyses). Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Successful endodontic outcomes largely depend on the quality of root canal obturation and the properties of the materials used, especially the endodontic sealer14.15 Despite meticulous initial endodontic treatment, success is not absolute, and persistent or recurrent infection may necessitate secondary intervention.16 When traditional endodontic therapy fails, nonsurgical endodontic retreatment is regarded as the preliminary intervention.17

In our study, a premixed calcium silicate sealer i.e., Ceraseal (Metabiomed, Korea) was used.18 Its exceptional flowability enables it to penetrate all aspects of the canal anatomy, including intricate and complex areas, thereby ensuring a high-quality obturation and thus posing a challenge in the retreatability.19

Various retreatment methods have shown inconsistent effectiveness in removing BC sealers,20 thus novel organic solvents have emerged. It has been shown that using rotary NiTi file systems to remove root filling materials during retreatment is more reliable and faster.21 It is anticipated that solvents could lower the adhesion of obturating materials within the canal, facilitating their elimination with the aid of instruments.22 Zhekov KI and Stefanova VP, recommended the use of rotary instruments along with solvents for retreatment of canals obturated with GP/BCS.23 Accordingly, this study employed a strategic approach rooted in a comprehensive understanding of sealer chemistry and the pivotal role of rotary instrumentation, integrating organic solvents with a rotary system to enhance effective removal of the sealer.

Core outcome of the study

In conjunction with HyFlex Remover files, 5% acetic and 10% formic acids enhanced removal in coronal third, with 5% acetic acid demonstrating superior efficacy over chloroform.

In the middle and apical thirds, all solvents exhibited comparable effectiveness.

Intragroup comparison

In Group 1,2 and 3, the reason for less residual material in coronal third could be attributed to the ability of instruments to touch root canal walls more efficiently and better access of solvents as compared to middle and apical thirds. Moreover, the apical third exhibited a notably higher quantity of remaining material which could be due to higher occurrence of ramifications/accessory canals in the apical third.

In our study, distilled water was used as a negative control in Group 4. Since it has neutral pH and lack of solvent properties, therefore no significant dissolution of Ceraseal was observed yet some degree of solubility could be seen.

Intergroup comparison

Coronal Third

Comparable results between Group 1 and Group 2 could be attributed to the ease of accessibility of instruments and organic solvents that led to less residual material. Furthermore, Group 1 had less residual material than Group 3 since chloroform has a neutral pH and is therefore a weak solvent. Additionally, Group 1 had less residual material than Group 4, which utilised distilled water as the negative control. Moreover, Groups 2, 3, and 4 exhibited similar results, possibly influenced by differences in experimental variables, including the sample size.

Our results align with the observations made by Sappal I S in which 5% Acetic acid could establish the working length and patency faster than chloroform in the course of retreating canals previously filled with BC Sealer.2 Also, according to Garrib and Camilleri, the use of 10% formic acid together with mechanical instrumentation provided the highest efficiency in eliminating gutta-percha and bioceramic sealer residues.6 Carillo CA et al found that 5% acetic acid was effective in restoring apical patency while requiring less chair side time for the removal of the bioceramic sealer.7

Middle Third

Group 1 and Group 2 exhibited less residual material when compared to Group 4 due to the use of distilled water as a negative control and efficient solvent action of 5% Acetic acid and 10% Formic acid. Group 1 showed comparable results with Group 2 as both are organic solvents and have equal calcium depleting tendency. Group 3 showed no discernible difference when compared to Group 1 and Group 2, which could be due to limited penetration/leaching of the solvent in the middle third. Group 3 and Group 4 also showed comparable outcomes, which could be due to chloroform's intrinsically inferior solvent action in dissolving Ceraseal.

Apical Third

Non-significant difference among all groups could be attributed to limited instrument access and poor solvent penetration owing to greater anatomical variations such as ramifications and accessory canals. Our results are consistent with the observations of Sherif DA et al, indicating that, independent of the retreatment method used, a greater quantity of residual material persisted in the apical third compared to the middle and coronal thirds.24,25

Mechanistic explanation

Both 10% formic acid and 5% acetic acid are organic solvents with an acidic pH that have the ability to deplete calcium and disrupt the crystallisation of C-S-H, thereby weakening the strength of bonds in Ceraseal. Additionally, a readily soluble compound i.e., calcium formate is formed due to reaction between calcium silicate and formic acid.6

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the present study include the incorporation of a negative control group, rigorous standardisation of specimen selection and preparation, computer-generated randomisation, blinded SEM image assessment, and substantial intra-examiner reliability. Collectively, these methodological measures enhanced the internal validity, reproducibility, and reliability of the experimental findings. In addition, the use of standardised SEM imaging parameters and controlled retreatment protocols improved the consistency of comparisons among the experimental groups.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was an in vitro investigation and therefore could not fully reproduce the biological, thermal, mechanical, and microbiological conditions encountered clinically. Second, only single-rooted teeth with relatively straight canals were included to minimise anatomical variability and improve standardisation. Consequently, the present findings may not be directly generalisable to multi-rooted teeth or more complex canal anatomies. In teeth with multiple roots, curved canals, isthmuses, fins, accessory canals, and anatomical irregularities may influence instrument accessibility, solvent penetration, and the persistence of residual filling materials, potentially altering retreatment outcomes. A two-week interval was selected prior to retreatment to ensure standardization of samples rather than to simulate long-term clinical conditions and furthermore, longer aging could introduce additional variables such as increased sealer hardness and dentin interaction, potentially confounding the results. However, this may be considered a limitation of the study, as in clinical practice, older obturations could present greater difficulty during removal.

Another limitation relates to the ordinal nature of the SEM scoring system, which may inherently reduce measurement sensitivity compared with fully quantitative volumetric techniques such as micro-computed tomography. Furthermore, the absence of irrigant activation or enhanced agitation may have contributed to persistence of apical remnants.

Nevertheless, the use of non-parametric statistical methods and blinded SEM assessment strengthened the reliability and validity of the present findings.

Comparison with other studies

In comparison to previous studies that have primarily assessed retreatability of bioceramic sealers using either mechanical methods alone or conventional solvents such as chloroform, the present study provides more systematic and standardized comparison of multiple solvent systems, including underexplored weak organic acids, in conjunction with rotary retreatment. Furthermore, while prior studies have frequently relied on qualitative or two-dimensional assessment methods, the use of SEM-based evaluation in our study allows for more precise, region-specific assessment, which has been limited in earlier literature.

Clinical implications

When retreating calcium-silicate sealer obturations, weak organic acids can be prioritized to facilitate coronal and, to a lesser extent, middle-third debridement. Given the lack of significant intergroup differences apically, clinicians should anticipate persistent apical remnants and consider adjunctive strategies to optimize cleanliness.

Future research

Priorities include: (i) controlled trials incorporating irrigant activation (ultrasonic, sonic, laser) with weak acids; (ii) evaluation in curved and multi-rooted teeth and in the presence of complex anatomies (isthmuses, fins); (iii) standardized, multi-modal outcome assessment combining SEM with volumetric micro-CT; and (iv) investigation of biocompatibility and dentin effects of repeated weak-acid exposures during retreatment. Future studies should investigate the effectiveness of these solvents in multi-rooted teeth and in canals with greater anatomical complexity in order to improve the clinical applicability and external validity of the findings.

Conclusion

The methodological rigor of the present investigation was strengthened by the inclusion of a negative control group, standardised experimental procedures, blinded outcome assessment, and reliability testing, thereby supporting the validity and reproducibility of the findings.

When used adjunctively with a rotary retreatment system, weak organic acids like 5% Acetic acid and 10% Formic acid improved removal of a premixed calcium-silicate sealer and gutta-percha primarily in the coronal portion of the canal and thus shows promising results for retreatability of bioceramic sealers within the limitations of an in vitro experimental design.
