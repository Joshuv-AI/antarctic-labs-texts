Importance of antibody validation in detecting cell cycle regulatory protein p16 INK4A by immunohistochemistry on pancreas tissue
Authors: Jasmine Pipella, Peter J. Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/Objectivep16INK4A is a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor that plays important roles in pancreatic islet beta-cell maturation, function, and senescence. As staining pattern consistency and reproducibility have been challenging in the field, our objective was to validate a monoclonal antibody for the detection of p16INK4A in mouse and human fixed pancreas sections.MethodsFour commercial monoclonal antibodies reported to react against mouse p16INK4A were tested by fluorescent immunohistochemistry on positive and negative control formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded pancreas sections from two different mouse strains. Lot-to-lot consistency was also evaluated. Fixed human donor pancreas sections were evaluated for reactivity with the monoclonal p16INK4A antibody that performed best on mouse tissues.ResultsOne commercial monoclonal antibody robustly detected a nuclear antigen in both mouse and human pancreas sections, consistent with the previously reported p16INK4A staining pattern, and its role as a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor in beta cells. This antibody also showed lot-to-lot consistency in staining pattern.ConclusionOur findings highlight the importance of antibody validation for p16INK4A detection in mouse and human pancreas sections and suggest caution in using untested commercially available antibodies to this protein for immunohistochemistry of the pancreas.

p16INK4A belongs to the family of proteins called cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, which arrest the cell cycle by inhibiting cyclin/cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) complexes. p16INK4A is best known for its role in enforcing a G1/S arrest via inhibition of Cyclin D-CDK4/6 complexes in the context of cellular senescence. 1 , 2 In pancreatic islets, p16INK4A was among the first cell cycle inhibitors shown to play a role in age-dependent regenerative decline in beta cells. 3 Knockout of the Cdkn2a locus to specifically ablate p16INK4A expression without affecting the alternative reading frame transcript encoding p19ARF led to increased beta cell replication during aging and conversely transgenic overexpression of p16INK4A suppressed beta cell replication in mice 3 and increased functional maturity and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. 4 Other studies suggest that p16INK4A expression can have negative impacts on islet function, particularly during the development of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T1D and T2D) in mouse models, where it is associated with a senescence response to stress. 5 Cellular senescence is a stress response leading to cell cycle arrest, in conjunction with a variety of other phenotypes, including persistent DNA damage response, a senescence-associated secretory phenotype, prosurvival signaling, and changes in the lysosomal compartment. 1 Beta cells expressing high levels of p16INK4A accumulate early prior to the onset of hyperglycemia in both the nonobese diabetic (NOD) mouse model of T1D 6 , 7 as well as in aged (7-month-old) high-fat diet (HFD) fed mice as a model for T2D and mice treated with the insulin receptor antagonist S961 to model acute insulin resistance. 8 Senolytic compounds, which tip the balance towards apoptosis in senescent cells, deplete p16INK4A-expressing beta cells in NOD mice and a similar approach using either senolytics or genetic ablation of senescent cells in HFD mice, each lead to favourable outcomes on disease progression and metabolic phenotypes. 5 Studies on human islets and EndoC-βH3 cells reveal that p16INK4A expression enforces a senescence cell cycle arrest that improves islet glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and is associated with a more mature, differentiated phenotype. 9 These studies highlight the important and context-dependent roles played by p16INK4A in islet biology and diabetes, beta cell function, and senescence, and hence the need for reliable reagents for studying this protein.

A large body of previous studies have used antibodies to detect p16INK4A in immunohistochemistry on mouse and human pancreas samples. While most studies have shown exclusively nuclear staining in mouse 6 , 10-12 and humans, 6 other studies have shown nuclear and cytoplasmic staining patterns in both mice and humans. 4 , 9 Though recent evidence suggests that p16INK4A may show different localization patterns depending on its turnover, 13 little consideration has been given to defining the localization patterns of this protein in pancreas tissue sections. In addition, concerns around lot-to-lot consistency are a common and ongoing problem that hinders reproducibility in immunohistochemistry studies, 14 including islet biology research. Currently, there are no reports systematically validating p16INK4A antibodies, which creates challenges for consistent and reproducible investigations of this protein in islet biology research, given the extensive use of immunohistochemical staining assays on pancreas tissue from mice and human donors in this field.

Here, we address this concern by systematically validating a commercially available monoclonal p16INK4A antibody in both mouse and human fixed pancreas tissue sections. We confirmed this antibody in positive and negative controls and demonstrated the consistent nuclear localization of its target antigen in both mice and humans. Together, these results provide guidance for the islet biology research community in selecting a validated p16INK4A antibody for immunohistochemistry studies on mouse and human pancreas tissue.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics and tissue processing

All the use of experimental animals for research was approved by the Central Animal Care Committee at the University of Manitoba. C57BL6/J mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory and euthanized at different ages for pancreas dissection and fixation for immunohistochemistry. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded pancreas blocks from postnatal day 5 (P5) (unsexed) and 20-month-old female C57BL6/J mice were a gift from A. Bhushan (University of California, San Francisco). Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded pancreas blocks from 2-month-old and 9-month-old male C57BL6/J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory.

Human donor pancreas tissue

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded human pancreas sections were provided by the Alberta Diabetes Institute IsletCore at the University of Alberta in Edmonton (www.bcell.org/adi-isletcore) with the assistance of the Human Organ Procurement and Exchange (HOPE) program, Trillium Gift of Life Network (TGLN), and other Canadian organ procurement organizations. Islet isolation was approved by the Human Research Ethics Board at the University of Alberta (Pro00013094). All donors' families provided informed consent for the use of pancreatic tissue in research. Human pancreas sections were also obtained through the Network for Pancreas Organ Donors with Diabetes (nPOD; RRID:SCR_014641), a collaborative type 1 diabetes research project supported by Breakthrough T1D and The Leona M. & Harry B. Helmsley Charitable Trust (Grant#3-SRA-2023-1417-S-B). Human pancreas donor information is shown in Table 1. Immunofluorescence and microscopy

Immunofluorescence staining, epifluorescence and confocal microscopy were performed as described. 16 Slides were rehydrated through graded xylene and ethanol washes, incubated for 10 min in 1% hydrogen peroxide to reduce tissue section autofluorescence, followed by washing in distilled water. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in 1X citrate buffer, pH 6.0 (Vector Labs) using a laboratory-grade pressure cooker (BioSB Tinto Retrieval system) for 15 min on ‘high’ setting. The sections were permeabilized with TBS + 0.1% Triton-X-100 for 10 min and then incubated in a humid chamber overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table 2): guinea pig polyclonal anti-Insulin (1:2, IR0002, Agilent Canada), mouse monoclonal anti-p16INK4A (1:200 or 1:1000, sc-1661, Lot# C3021 and Lot# L1825, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), rabbit monoclonal anti-p16INK4a (1:200, or 1:1000, Cat#23200, Lot#1 Cell Signaling Technology), and mouse monoclonal anti-p16INK4A (1:200 or 1:1000, Cat# ab54210, Lot#1115442-11 Abcam) or mouse monoclonal anti-p16INK4A (1:200 or 1:1000, Cat# MA5-17142, Lot# SJ2464303, Thermo Fisher). The slides were washed in TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and incubated with Alexa488-conjugated secondary antibodies to detect insulin antibody and with Alexa594-conjugated secondary antibodies to detect p16 antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) at a dilutionof 1:500 for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then counterstained with ProLong Diamond Anti-Fade with DAPI mounting media (Thermo Fisher). Where possible, serial sections were stained with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies only as negative controls. Mouse pancreas sections were imaged using a Zeiss Axioscope2 epifluorescence microscope and a Zeiss LSM-700 confocal microscope, while human pancreas sections were imaged only using the LSM-700 confocal microscope. Merged images were compiled in ImageJ for figure preparation.

p16INK4A expression at the mRNA level in islets and protein level in beta cells is detectable at very low levels starting from after postnatal maturation in mice (0–1 month of age) and gradually increases during aging to reach relatively high levels in mice >6 months–1 y of age. 3 , 4 , 6 , 10 , 11 , 17 Most studies detecting the protein demonstrate exclusively nuclear staining in both mouse and human, consistent with its known roles as a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor. 6 , 10 , 11 To evaluate p16INK4A antibodies in immunohistochemistry staining on pancreas, we tested a panel of commercial monoclonal antibodies from major suppliers (Abcam, Invitrogen, Cell Signaling Technology, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) on formalin-fixed paraffin embedded specimens from 9 month-old C57BL6/J mice (Figure 1A and B), which should express abundant p16INK4A due to aging of mice over 6 months. 3 , 10 , 11 We tested two different dilutions (1:200 and 1:1000) representing a range of concentrations that we have found to work optimally for most antibodies in other studies 6 , 16 using heat-mediated antigen retrieval with citrate buffer pH 6.0. Strikingly, only the Santa Cruz Biotechnology antibody showed a strong nuclear signal in Ins+ and Ins− cells in a dilution-dependent manner, whereas the staining was much stronger in Ins+ cells (Figure 1A and 1B , zoomed insets). A weaker nuclear signal was detected in some Ins+ cells with the Abcam antibody, but only at relatively high antibody concentrations (1:200, Figure 1A and B). To further verify the p16 staining pattern found with epifluorescence, we performed confocal imaging of these sections and observed the same result (Supplementary Figure 1).

To confirm the specificity of the Santa Cruz p16INK4A antibody, we used three additional validations, including: (1) biological negative control samples – pancreas sections from young mice; (2) secondary antibody-only (2° only) controls—on serial sections of positive control samples from older mice; and (3) staining of pancreas sections from nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice, which show heterogeneous and precocious accumulation of p16+ beta cells during disease progression from 8 to 15 weeks of age 6 as an independent model for senescent beta cells (Figure 1C and D). As expected, p16INK4A expression was undetectable in pancreas sections from postnatal day 5 (P5) C57BL6 mice, but clearly increased in Ins+ and some Ins- cells in pancreas sections from 2-month-old, 9-month-old, and 20-month-old C57BL6 mice (Figure 1C, zoomed insets). In all the C57BL6 samples, secondary only the negative controls did not show staining (Figure 1C). p16INK4A expression was also low in 8-week-old female NOD mouse pancreas, consistent with recent work using a different antibody, 7 but showed positively stained beta cells in 14-week-old NOD pancreas tissue (Figure 1D). Notably, p16INK4A staining with the Santa Cruz antibody was heterogeneous with no beta or non-beta cell staining positive in these samples, which is consistent with the heterogeneity observed in mice with previous p16INK4A antibodies. 4 , 6 Again, secondary only negative controls on NOD pancreas sections showed no signal (Figure 1D), confirming that the signals were dependent on the p16INK4A antibody.

To determine whether this antibody showed lot-to-lot consistency, we also purchased a different lot of the same p16INK4A antibody in 2026, approximately 5 y after the first batch we tested in Figure 1 was manufactured (purchased in 2021). Consistent with the findings of the 2021 study, the newer lot of the same p16INK4A antibody reproduced the nuclear staining patterns observed in 2 month-old versus aged 9-month-old C57BL6/J mouse pancreas tissue, confirming its lot-to-lot consistency (Figure 2, zoomed insets).

The antibody datasheet indicated reactivity with human p16INK4A in addition to the mouse protein. Therefore, we further tested this antibody on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded human donor pancreas tissue sections obtained from the Alberta Diabetes Institute (ADI) and the Network for Pancreas Organ Donors with Diabetes (nPOD) (Table 1), where images were captured in a manner blinded to donor age (Figure 3). We found that this antibody produced a strong pan-nuclear signal in a few INS+ cells in a pancreas section from a young pediatric donor (R465, 4-year-old) with qualitatively higher expression in INS+ and INS- cells in an elderly adult donor (R529, 71-year-old), and this specificity was confirmed by secondary-only negative controls (Figure 3A, zoomed inset). We repeated staining with this antibody on pancreas sections obtained from nPOD, with two ~3-year-old donors (6350, 6292) and two 20–21 y old donors (6366, 6234) and observed more stained cells in the latter (Figure 3B, zoomed insets) as expected based on donor age. 4 , 6 Again, p16INK4A staining was heterogeneous with not every beta cell, or non-beta cell staining positively in each of these cases (Figure 3A and B), consistent with previously reported heterogeneity in human pancreas samples. 6 , 9 These results support that this monoclonal p16INK4A antibody reliably detects the human protein in immunohistochemistry on fixed pancreas sections.

p16INK4A is a critical regulator of cell cycle arrest in beta cells, and plays roles in beta cell maturation, insulin secretion, and senescence. 4 , 9 While p16INK4A can be measured at the mRNA level in islets, 3 , 4 its levels are usually very low, and primers must be carefully designed to avoid the alternative reading frame transcript for p19ARF in mouse (p14ARF in humans) also encoded by the Cdkn2a/CDKN2A gene. The use of antibodies for detecting mouse p16INK4A by immunohistochemistry on pancreas has been unreliable and inconsistent in the islet biology field over the past several years, with some batches of antibodies working well and showing the expected staining pattern, whereas others produce patterns that are difficult to reconcile with its canonical functions as a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, as supported by earlier genetic studies. 3 Issues of lot-to-lot consistency have also led to concerns with reproducibility in detecting p16INK4A. Indeed, while p16INK4A was successfully detected with an earlier lot of Abcam antibody (from 2016 to 2017) ab54210 in NOD and C57BL6/J mouse pancreas, 6 our staining here, which used a newer lot from 2026 (Figure 1A, Abcam), as well as previous staining, which used another lot number we have tested (data not shown), suggested that this monoclonal antibody may not perform consistently between lots. Although lot-to-lot variability is generally less common for monoclonal antibodies produced with modern manufacturing techniques, inconsistencies in antibody supply can still occur. Together, these concerns have formed barriers to reliable and reproducible immunohistochemistry for p16INK4A in pancreas tissue.

Here, we address a technical challenge in the field by identifying and validating a suitable monoclonal antibody for the detection of both mouse and human p16INK4A in pancreatic tissue by immunohistochemistry. We found an antibody that showed consistent pan-nuclear staining in both C57BL6 mouse and human donor pancreas INS+ and some INS- cells in an age-dependent manner, which is consistent with the well-described increase in the abundance of p16INK4A during aging in islets of mice and humans. 3 , 4 , 9 , 18 Using this antibody, we further confirmed the previous staining pattern of p16INK4A in the context of autoimmune diabetes in NOD mice, where it marks a subset of stressed senescent beta cells accumulating from 8 to 15 weeks of age in euglycemic animals. 6 Importantly, we show the lot-to-lot consistency of this antibody, as an older lot number that was at least 5 y old had the same staining pattern on mouse pancreas as a newer lot number of the same antibody purchased this year. Interestingly, and as expected, p16INK4A staining was heterogeneous in all cases with not all beta cells showing staining. With respect to the human samples, we noted that p16INK4A was already clearly detectable in a few cells of 3–4-year-old pediatric donor pancreas samples and showed qualitative increases in young adult donors from the nPOD, as well as in elderly adult donor samples from the ADI. Previous work with a different p16INK4A antibody that showed nuclear and cytoplasmic staining showed a strong age-related relationship with positively-stained beta cells, 9 and we recently reported an association between donor age and total islet p16INK4A levels 18 from public mass spectrometry data from the Humanislets.com database. 15 Importantly, we confirmed primary antibody specificity in all positively stained samples with secondary-only controls. Together, these findings support and validate a monoclonal antibody for detecting mouse and human p16INK4A in immunohistochemistry on fixed pancreas tissue for the islet biology community.

As a limitation of this study, we recognize that we tested only a small number of possible antibody conditions in our immunofluorescence experiments, and thus it is possible that the antibodies that did not produce a signal might perform better under other conditions, such as a higher concentration than we used here (e.g., 1:50 or 1:100 dilution). In addition, we did not have access to Cdkn2a knockout mouse pancreas tissue to unequivocally demonstrate that the nuclear antigen detected in our staining was, in fact, p16INK4A. This concern is not trivial, as it was recently shown that GLP-1R antibodies previously presumed to be specific showed positive signals in immunoassays on bona fide Glp1r knockout mouse tissues. 19 On the other hand, the performance of this antibody with respect to bona fide positive and negative controls was unequivocal, and thus we emphasize the importance of not taking supplier validation data at face value, but incorporating appropriate positive and negative controls in each experiment to confirm antibody validity. Additional validation of this antibody using Cdkn2a knockout mouse pancreas is clearly warranted.

In conclusion, we identified and validated a suitable monoclonal p16INK4A antibody for the detection of this protein in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded mouse and human pancreas sections. Our observations suggest that caution should be exercisedwhen using unvalidated commercial antibodies to detect p16INK4A for immunohistochemistry studies on the pancreas. Validation of other p16INK4A antibodies with positive and negative control samples, as well as Cdkn2a knockout tissue where available, should confirm the expected nuclear localization and specificity before experiments are conducted to ensure reliable and reproducible data for the islet biology research community.
