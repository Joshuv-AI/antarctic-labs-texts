LncRNA EGFR-AS1 inhibits ferroptosis to reduce radiosensitivity of cervical cancer through the m6A/IGF2BP3/APAF1 axis
Authors: Xianglong Chen, Jingyu Li, Yujie Sun, Zhaoyu Ye, Man Long, Junguo Bu, Zirong Fan, Zhaojing Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer is a significant concern among women, and radioresistance is a key obstacle in its treatment. Ferroptosis is a new way of death, which offers a fresh idea for tumor radiotherapy. We aimed to elucidate the mechanisms of lncRNA-mediated ferroptosis in radiosensitivity of cervical cancer.MethodsIn this study, TCGA database analysis was used to determine key lncRNAs associated with ferroptosis and radiosensitivity in cervical cancer. The function and mechanism of lncRNAs on ferroptosis and radiosensitivity was investigated by in vitro and in vivo.ResultsWe found that radiotherapy triggered cervical cancer cell ferroptosis and ferroptosis inducer inhibited the viability of radiotherapy-treated cervical cancer cells. Bioinformatics analysis identified EGFR-AS1 as a lncRNA associated with radiosensitivity and ferroptosis in cervical cancer. EGFR-AS1 was significantly increased in radiotherapy-treated cervical cancer cells and radioresistant tissues. Interfering with EGFR-AS1 expression induced ferroptosis of cervical cancer cells. Animal experiments revealed that EGFR-AS1 inhibits radiosensitivity in vivo. Mechanically, EGFR-AS1 bound to the m6A recognition protein IGF2BP3. Meanwhile, EGFR-AS1 was involved in the p53 signaling pathway and regulated APAF1 through IGF2BP3 and m6A modifications. Furthermore, EGFR-AS1 restrained ferroptosis in cervical cancer cells via the m6A/APAF1 axis.ConclusionsIn conclusion, this study revealed that EGFR-AS1 suppressed ferroptosis of cancer cells via the m6A/IGF2BP3/APAF1 axis, thereby decreasing radiosensitivity of cervical cancer. Our study offers fresh ideas for the radiosensitization mechanism of cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15596-0.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is considered to be a frequently occurring malignancy affecting the female reproductive system globally. Recently, the occurrence of cervical cancer has been increasing among young people, seriously endangering women’s health and life. In 2020, 604,127 cases and 341,831 deaths from cervical cancer were reported globally [1]. The development of cervical cancer may be caused by a variety of risk factors, including human papillomavirus, age of menarche and age of first sex [2]. The current options for treating cervical cancer encompass surgical procedures, radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Radical radiotherapy is the preferred treatment for intermediate and advanced stages of cervical cancer. However, it is worth noting that about 50% of patients with advanced cervical cancer are insensitive to radiotherapy, leading to tumor recurrence and metastasis, and poor prognosis [3]. Therefore, it is crucial to expedite new targets and develop novel drugs that can improve the radiotherapy sensitivity of cervical cancer.

Ferroptosis is identified as a distinct mode of programmed cell death that presents genetic, biochemical, and morphological disparities from necrosis, autophagy, and apoptosis [4]. Morphologically, mitochondrial shrinkage, increased density of mitochondrial membranes, and disappearance of mitochondrial crest are prominent features of ferroptosis [5, 6]. Studies have demonstrated a strong association between ferroptosis and the regulation of growth in various cancer types, including ovarian, pancreatic, liver, and lung cancers [7, 8]. Recently, the function of ferroptosis in the treatment of cervical cancer has received increasing attention. Oleanolic acid induced ferroptosis of cervical cancer cells via enhancing ACSL4 levels, thus reducing the survival rate in cervical cancer cells. The discovery provides a promising approach for treating cervical cancer [9]. Cdc25A up-regulated ErbB2 by dephosphorylating PKM2, which in turn inhibited ferroptosis of cervical cancer cells [10]. In addition, ferroptosis has been found to be closely linked to the radiotherapy effect in cancer. For example, research by Zhang et al. demonstrated that ferroptosis can enhance the responsiveness of triple-negative breast cancer to radiotherapy [11]. Lang et al. discovered that radiotherapy induced ferroptosis in ovarian cancer cells, and the ferroptosis antagonist liproxstatin-1 limited the efficacy of radiotherapy in tumor models [12]. However, the extent to which ferroptosis is involved in cervical cancer radiotherapy remains uncertain.

In recent years, studies have demonstrated the involvement of lncRNAs in the resistance and sensitivity to radiotherapy in various types of cancers [13, 14]. LncRNAs have been found to play a role in regulating the sensitivity of cervical cancer cells to radiotherapy. The suppression of lncRNA PCAT1 increased sensitivity to radiotherapy in cervical cancer cells through the miR-128/GOLM1 axis [15]. Overexpression of lncRNA GAS5 can up-regulate IER3 through miR-106b, leading to increased sensitivity in cervical cancer cells to radiotherapy [16]. Furthermore, lncRNAs have been implicated in the mechanism of ferroptosis. In prostate cancer, the miR-128-3P/SLC7A11 signaling axis is involved in the mechanism by which the lncRNA OIP5-AS suppresses ferroptosis [17]. LncRNA GABPB1-AS participates in ferroptosis induced by erastin in liver cancer cells by regulating oxidative stress [18]. However, it remains unknown whether lncRNAs affects radiotherapy sensitivity of cervical cancer by regulating ferroptosis.

m6A, a prevalent RNA modification in eukaryotic cells, is primarily regulated by m6A methyltransferase (writer), m6A demethylase (erasers) and m6A recognition protein (reader) [19]. m6A modification plays a significant role in cervical cancer. PDK4 plays a positive role in regulating the translation elongation and stability of its own mRNA via interacting with IGF2BP3, thus regulating glycolysis of cervical cancer cells [20]. METTL3 and IGF2BP3 establish an interaction to stabilize ACIN1 mRNA and induces invasiveness of cervical cancer cells [21].

In this study, we aimed to explore how ferroptosis affects radiotherapy in cervical cancer and unravel the mechanism of lncRNA-mediated ferroptosis in the sensitivity of cervical cancer to radiotherapy. Our study might provide novel insight into epigenetic regulation in radiosensitivity of cervical cancer.

Materials and methods

Patients and sample collection

A total of 22 patients with cervical cancer who received surgery at Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University were included in the study, among whom 9 were radiotherapy-resistant and 13 were radiotherapy-sensitive. Tumor tissue sections were collected from 9 cervical cancer patients belonged to radiation-resistant group and 13 cervical cancer patients belonged to the radiation-sensitive group. Six tumor tissue sections were used for immunohistochemistry, including three cases of radioresistance and three cases of radiosensitivity. Twenty-two tumor tissue sections were used for in situ hybridization, including 9 cases of radioresistance and 13 cases of radiosensitivity. All samples were reserved at − 80 °C for the subsequent research. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University. All cervical cancer patients offered their informed consent.

Cell culture and treatment

Human cervical cancer cell lines CaSki (iCell-h040) and SiHa (iCell-h188) were obtained from iCell Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). CaSki cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. SiHa cells were cultured in MEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

For radiotherapy treatments, we exposed cervical cancer cells to different doses of X-ray irradiation (2, 4, 6, and 8 Gy) at a rate of 2 Gy/min by utilizing a 6-MV photon beam using a linear accelerator (Clinac 21EX, Varian Medical Systems, Inc., USA). An optimal dose was selected for subsequent experiments. For radiotherapy and ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) treatments, we treated the cultured cells with 2 µM of Fer-1 for 2 h, following this treatment, the cells were exposed to a 4 Gy dose of X-ray irradiation.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime). Briefly, cervical cancer cells were divided into two groups: (i) control group, which received graded doses of irradiation (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 Gy); and (ii) erastin group, which was received the same doses of irradiation followed by erastin treatment. For irradiation, cells were exposed to X-ray at a dose rate of 2 Gy/min, corresponding to 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 min of exposure, to achieve final doses of 0–8 Gy. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^3 cells per well (100 µL/well). According to group assignment, 20 µM Erastin was added to the treatment wells for 24 h, while an equal volume of DMSO was used in the control wells. Prior to measurement, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was then determined using a microplate reader (Infinite M1000, TECAN).

Immunohistochemistry

The radiosensitive and radioresistant cancer tissue samples from cervical cancer patients were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The tissue sections were treated to remove the wax and hydrated. Afterwards, the sections were soaked in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min for inactivation and rinsed with running water for 10 min. To retrieve the antigens, the sections were immersed in a sodium citrate buffer solution (0.01 M) and heated to 100 °C for 15 min. To block nonspecific binding, the tissue sections were treated with 5% BSA for 35 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against ACSL4 (ab155282, Abcam), and GPX4 (ab125066, Abcam) IGF2BP3 (14642-1-AP, Proteintech), and APAF1 (sc-65891, Santa Cruz) over night at 4 °C. Tissue sections were then washed with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (B001, Ebiogo) at 37 °C for 35 min. The sections were stained with diaminobenzidine, washed with running water, followed by redyeing with hematoxylin. The immunohistochemistry-staining intensity of ACSL4 and GPX4 was determined using ImageJ software.

Data download from the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) database

Cervical cancer data were downloaded from GDC website, and 120 patients were included for subsequent analysis after excluding samples that had received radiotherapy and chemotherapy before surgery or treatment. Among them, 103 individuals were classified as radiosensitive (Complete Response, CR) and 17 individuals were in the radioresistant group (Stable Disease/ Progressive Disease, SD/PD). To analyze the differentially expressed genes and lncRNAs, the R package pheatmap was employed to generate clustering heatmaps. TCGA CESC data were used for survival analysis of the above radiation-related lncRNAs, and lncRNAs associated with prognosis were screened. The R package survminer was employed to determine the optimal cutoff of lncRNAs on the basis of the expression value/risk score, the survival time, and the survival status. LncRNA expression above the optimal cutoff was classified as high expression, while expression below was classified as low expression. Correlation between EGFR-AS1 and expression of ferroptosis-related genes was performed.

To further identify the target genes of EGFR-AS1 regulating ferroptosis in cervical cancer cells, we divided TCGA cervical cancer samples into two groups according to EGFR-AS1 expression and performed GSEA analysis. Correlation between IGF2BP3 and expression of target genes was performed. eCLIP-seq data based on ENCODE database was analyzed to determine the interaction between IGF2BP3 and target genes.

Analysis of IGF2BP3 binding with EGFR-AS1

The Starbase website was used to determine the protein binding with EGFR-AS1. The eCLIP-seq data was analyzed to determine the interaction between EGFR-AS1 and IGF2BP3.

Molecular docking

The x3dna database (http://web.x3dna.org/fibermodel/rnaseq) was applied to construct RNA 3D model based on APAF1 sequences. Afterward, IGF2BP3-APAF1 molecule docking was established by using US-align database (https://zhanggroup.org/US-align).

Cell transfection

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting EGFR-AS1 and IGF2BP3, and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting APAF1 were acquired from GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Non-targeting shRNAs and siRNAs were served as negative control (sh-NC and si-NC). shRNAs and siRNAs were transfected into cervical cancer cells using Lipofectamine 2000 Reagents (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s manual.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Cervical cancer cells were subjected to total RNA extraction using Trizol (Invitrogen). Afterwards, RNA was reversely transcribed to cDNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher). For qRT-PCR, we used the SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Roache). The qRT-PCR was performed on ABI QuantStudio 6 RT-PCR system (Applied Biosystems Inc., USA). The amplification protocol involved an initial step at 95 °C for 10 min and 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Each reaction was conducted in triplicate. GAPDH was regarded as a reference gene. The gene relative expression was quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Detection of malondialdehyde (MDA)

We measured the levels of MDA in cervical cancer cells using MDA Assay Kit (S0131S, Beyotime, China). The experimental procedures followed the instructions provided by the respective manufacturers.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) level measurement

We measured the level of ROS by the ROS Assay Kit (CA1410, Solarbio). Briefly, cervical cancer cells were suspended in 2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, we washed cells three times with serum-free cell culture medium. Finally, we observed and captured images of the samples by a fluorescence microscope.

Colony formation assay

Cells were evenly distributed into 6-well plates with approximately 2000 cells in each well and allowed to grow for 3 weeks. After this incubation period, the cells were washed three times with PBS and subsequently fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime). Once fixed, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet to visualize and assess their morphology or other characteristics. Colonies (more than 10 cells) were counted and colony number was determined by employing ImageJ software.

In situ hybridization (ISH)

The ISH assay was conducted using the RNAscope®2.5 HD Detection Reagent-Brown kit (Cat#322310, Advanced cell diagnostics, USA). In brief, the radiosensitive and radioresistant cervical cancer paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to target retrieval by boiling. Proteinase K treatment was then carried out. Subsequently, sections were incubated with probes EGFR-AS1 for 4 h at 40°C. After rinsing, sections were treated with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and counterstaining was carried out with hematoxylin.

Western blot

Cervical cancer cells were lysed by RIPA buffer (Thermo), allowing for the extraction of total protein. The concentration of protein was determined utilizing a BCA protein assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich). The protein samples were equally loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfatepolyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then resolved through electrophoresis. The proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking the membranes with 5% skim milk for 3 h, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GPX4 (1:1000, ab125066, Abcam), FTH1 (1:1000, Sc-376594, Santa Cruz), IGF2BP3 (1:5000, 14642-1-AP, Proteintech), APAF1 (1:3000, 29022-1-AP, Proteintech), and GAPDH (1:10000, 60004-1-Lg, Proteintech) and then were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (1:5000, SA00001-2, Proteintech) and Goat Anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (1:5000, SA00001-1, Proteintech) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the bands were observed using an ECL reagent (Thermo) and quantified by employing ImageJ software.

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were performed after obtaining approval from the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (LAEC-2024-078). A total of 10 BALB/c nude mice were acquired from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China) and divided into two groups: the shRNA-NC group (injected with SiHa cells), and the shEGFR-AS1 group (injected with SiHa cells with EGFR-AS1 knockdown). Each group of mice received a subcutaneous injection of 2 × 106 corresponding cells into the right thigh. Once the tumor diameter reached 8 mm, all mice were irradiated with X-ray radiation at a single dose of 4 Gy (dosage rate: 2Gy/min; source skin distance: 100 cm) and radiation therapy was performed once a week. A total of four sessions of radiotherapy were conducted throughout the entire experiment. After 4 weeks, the mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxia and tumor tissues were weighted. Tumor size was tested every 3 days by utilizing a vernier caliper. Tumor volume was determined as follows: length × width2 × 0.5.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

RIP assay was performed utilizing Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, USA). In brief, cells were lysed using RIP lysis buffer. The magnetic beads were mixed with either anti-IGF2BP3 or anti-IgG. After rinsing with RIP wash buffer for three times, the immunocomplexes bound to the beads were then subjected to RT-qPCR.

m6A RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assays

The MeRIP assay was performed to identify the m6A modification of APAF1 by the Magna MeRIP™ m6A kit (Millipore, MA, USA). Total RNA was isolated and then fragmented into nucleotide debris. The samples were immunoprecipitated with an anti-m6A antibody or IgG. After elution from magnetic beads, RNA was quantified via RT-qPCR.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed with SPSS 17.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The differences were determined using Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ferroptosis enhances radiosensitivity of cervical cancer cells

To understand how radiotherapy affects ferroptosis in cervical cancer cells, we treated two cervical cancer cells (CaSki cells and SiHa cells) with different doses of radiotherapy and detected GPX4, FTH1, MDA, and ROS. GPX4 is a central negative regulator of ferroptosis and depletion of GPX4 leads to ferroptosis [4]. FTH1 participates in iron storage and downregulation of FTH1 increases free iron availability, thereby promoting ferroptosis [22]. MDA is a byproduct of lipid peroxidation and is widely recognized as a biochemical marker of ferroptosis-associated oxidative damage [23]. ROS, particularly lipid ROS, are critical executors of ferroptosis, and their accumulation serves as a hallmark of ferroptosis [24]. The findings revealed that radiotherapy for 2 min significantly suppressed the expression of GPX4 and FTH1, while elevated the level of MDA in CaSki cells and SiHa cells, and had the greatest effect on ferroptosis at 4 Gy irradiation (CaSki: GPX4 0.431 ± 0.045 vs. 1.124 ± 0.114, FTH1 0.468 ± 0.106 vs. 1.100 ± 0.094, MDA 34.253 ± 2.001 vs. 22.447 ± 4.093 µmol/µg; SiHa: GPX4 0.628 ± 0.023 vs. 1.593 ± 0451, FTH1 0.291 ± 0.048 vs. 0.523 ± 0.093, MDA 34.927 ± 3.430 vs. 17.507 ± 0.770 µmol/µg; Fig. 1A–C). Therefore, the following study was performed at 4 Gy irradiation for 2 min. Treatment with Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, was found to lessen increased MDA and ROS levels in CaSki cells and SiHa cells induced by irradiation (CaSki: MDA 31.267 ± 1.663 vs. 38.973 ± 3.918µmol/µg, ROS 0.947 ± 0.247 vs. 3.532 ± 0.001; SiHa: 28.920 ± 1.143 vs. 35.430 ± 1.629µmol/µg, ROS 1.269 ± 0.234 vs. 5.188 ± 0.063; Fig. 1D and E). The inhibitory impact of 4 Gy irradiation on FTH1 and GPX4 protein expression was reversed by Fer-1 (CaSki FTH1 0.716 ± 0.145 vs. 0.335 ± 0.098 vs. 1.000 ± 0.077, GPX4 0.679 ± 0.071 vs. 0.428 ± 0.052 vs. 1.000 ± 0.049; SiHa FTH1 0.905 ± 0.150 vs. 0.400 ± 0.086 vs. 1.000 ± 0.138; GPX4 0.803 ± 0.103 vs. 0.556 ± 0.105 vs. 1.000 ± 0.035; Fig. 1F and G). The results from clone formation assay indicated that irradiation significantly inhibited viability of SiHa cells, and Fer-1 reversed the inhibited viability of irradiation-treated SiHa cells (CaSki: colonies 265.333 ± 16.563 vs. 159.667 ± 13.868 vs. 331.667 ± 26.502; SiHa: colonies 215.333 ± 9.292 vs. 133.000 ± 13.077 vs. 268.667 ± 14.572; Fig. 1H). The immunohistochemistry results demonstrated that radiosensitive cervical cancer tissues had a notable decrease in GPX4 expression and a significant increase in ACSL4 expression compared to radioresistant cervical cancer tissues (Fig. S1). To further explore the role of ferroptosis in radiosensitivity, we treated cervical cancer cells with the ferroptosis inducer erastin and then administered radiotherapy. The results showed that in both CaSki and SiHa cells, erastin combined with radiotherapy significantly reduced the IC50 value of the cells. Compared with radiotherapy alone, erastin combined with radiotherapy more strongly inhibited cell survival (Fig. 1I, J). Overall, ferroptosis promotes radiosensitivity of cervical cancer cells.

EGFR-AS1 is related to ferroptosis and radiosensitivity in cervical cancer

To screen lncRNAs associated with radiotherapy sensitivity and ferroptosis, we performed bioinformatics analysis using the TCGA cervical cancer (CESC) database. Differential expression analysis was performed through edgeR R package in tissues from cervical cancer patients with radiosensitivity and radioresistance. A total of 562 different genes (|log2FC|≥1, FDR < 0.05) were screened, including 143 differentially expressed lncRNAs. The differentially expressed genes were clustered with the R package pheatmap (Fig. 2A, B). Next, we used TCGA cervical cancer tumor data to perform survival analysis on the above radiotherapy-related lncRNAs. Six prognostic lncRNAs (EGFR-AS1, LINC00200, AL158166.2, AL158166.1, AC012531.1, LINC01206) were consistent with their expression trend in radiotherapy resistance group (Fig. 2C, Fig. S2A-S2E). The expression of EGFR-AS1 (2.868 ± 1.850 vs. 2.132 ± 1.305), LINC00200 (0.720 ± 1.987 vs. 0.255 ± 0.731), AL158166.2 (1.843 ± 1.928 vs. 1.230 ± 1.224), AL158166.1 (2.788 ± 2.085 vs. 2.288 ± 1.506), and AC012531.1 (3.941 ± 2.361 vs. 2.891 ± 1.915) was dramatically enhanced, while LINC01206 (2.042 ± 1.235 vs. 2.065 ± 2.064) was significantly reduced in radioresistant tumor tissues (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2F-S2J). To evaluate whether these six lncRNAs involved in ferroptosis, we analyze their expression correlation with ferroptosis-related genes in cervical cancer. We observed a strong correlation between these six lncRNAs and ferroptosis-related genes (Fig. 2E). We next sought to determine which lncRNA was most altered in cervical cancer cells and tissues following radiotherapy treatment. Using qRT-PCR, we measured the expression of LINC00200, AL158166.2, AL158166.1, EGFR-AS1, AC012531.1 and LINC01206 in cervical cancer cells after radiotherapy for a series of times. We observed a notable increase in EGFR-AS1 expression in cervical cancer cells after radiotherapy for 2 min (CaSki: 1.554 ± 0.127 vs. 1.095 ± 0.082; SiHa: 0.698 ± 0.181 vs. 0.231 ± 0.024; Fig. 3A). Therefore, EGFR-AS1 was selected for subsequent study. To further verify the connection between EGFR-AS1 and radiotherapy sensitivity in cervical cancer, we conducted in situ hybridization to assess the expression of EGFR-AS1 in cervical cancer patients classified as radiotherapy-resistant or radiotherapy-sensitive. The results revealed a high expression of EGFR-AS1 in cervical cancer patients with radiotherapy resistance (Fig. 3B). Overall, EGFR-AS1 is associated with ferroptosis and radiosensitivity in cervical cancer.

EGFR-AS1 inhibits ferroptosis in irradiation-treated cervical cancer cells

To elucidate the role of EGFR-AS1 in irradiation-treated cervical cancer cell ferroptosis, we performed shRNA-mediated knockdown of EGFR-AS1 in CaSki cells and SiHa cells. Following EGFR-AS1 knockdown, we observed a significant reduction in its expression (CaSki: sh-2657 0.352 ± 0.048 vs. 1.000 ± 0.137; SiHa; sh-EGFR-AS1-3 0.340 ± 0.048 vs. 1.000 ± 0.059; Fig. 4A and B). ROS detection results showed that radiation induced a significant increase in ROS levels compared with the control group, and this effect was further enhanced by EGFR-AS1 silencing in SiHa and CaSki cells (SiHa: ROS 2.847 ± 0.051 vs. 2.059 ± 0.177 vs. 1.000 ± 0.019; CaSki: ROS 2.168 ± 0.049 vs. 1.401 ± 0.082 vs. 1.000 ± 0.041; Fig. 4C). Consistently, MDA assays demonstrated that radiation obviously elevated MDA content relative to the control group, and EGFR-AS1 knockdown resulted in an additional increase in MDA content in SiHa and CaSki cells (SiHa: MDA 0.096 ± 0.005 vs. 0.062 ± 0.011 vs. 0.021 ± 0.009 µmol/mg; CaSki: MDA 0.098 ± 0.017 vs. 0.055 ± 0.003 vs. 0.020 ± 0.003 µmol/mg; Fig. 4D). We then evaluated the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins. Western blot analysis indicated that radiation remarkably downregulated GPX4 and FTH1 compared with to the control group, and EGFR-AS1 knockdown further amplified these changes in SiHa and CaSki cells (SiHa: FTH1 0.209 ± 0.033 vs. 0.435 ± 0.066 vs. 1.000 ± 0.111; GPX4 0.299 ± 0.023 vs. 0.604 ± 0.137 vs. 1.000 ± 0.156; CaSki: FTH1 0.359 ± 0.029 vs. 0.463 ± 0.019 vs. 1.000 ± 0.063; GPX4 0.435 ± 0.017 vs. 0.699 ± 0.010 vs. 1.000 ± 0.160; Fig. 4E and F). Clone formation assays showed that radiation significantly reduced colony formation compared with the control group, and EGFR-AS1 knockdown further decreased colony formation in SiHa and CaSki cells (SiHa: colonies 68.000 ± 3.606 vs. 137.000 ± 22.000 vs. 369.333 ± 38.553; CaSki colonies: 109.667 ± 13.577 vs. 198.333 ± 5.132 vs. 597.667 ± 16.503; Fig. 4G). These findings indicate that EGFR-AS1 represses ferroptosis in irradiation-treated cervical cancer cells.

EGFR-AS1 inhibits radiosensitivity in vivo

To unravel the function of EGFR-AS1 on cervical cancer radiosensitivity in vivo, nude mice were subcutaneously injected with SiHa cells silencing EGFR-AS1 expression or NC and treated with 4 Gy irradiation (Fig. 5A). We found that EGFR-AS1 knockdown remarkably reduce tumor size, volume (65.225 ± 18.511 vs. 91.348 ± 11.290 mm3), and weight (0.212 ± 0.033 vs. 0.340 ± 0.052 g; Fig. 5B, 5C and D). Moreover, qRT-PCR results showed that EGFR-AS1 knockdown markedly downregulated EGFR-AS1 expression in tumor tissues under irradiation (0.440 ± 0.095 vs. 1.000 ± 0.090; Fig. 5E). Taken together, EGFR-AS1 suppresses radiosensitivity in vivo.

EGFR-AS1 binds to m6A recognition protein IGF2BP3 and IGF2BP3 knockdown inhibits cervical cancer cell ferroptosis

To determine how EGFR-AS1 influences the sensitivity of cervical cancer cells to radiotherapy, we analyzed binding proteins of EGFR-AS1 through the Starbase database. Based on eCLIP-seq data, we discovered that EGFR-AS1 could interact with 21 RNA-binding proteins, including m6A recognition protein IGF2BP3 (Fig. 6A). Previous studies have indicated a close association between m6A modification and ferroptosis [25], but whether it mediates ferroptosis to regulate tumor radiotherapy sensitivity remains unclear. So we focus on m6A recognition protein IGF2BP3. To confirm the association between EGFR-AS1 and IGF2BP3, we conducted a partial analysis using ENCODE database and found that IGF2BP3 was enriched on EGFR-AS1 (Fig. 6B). To investigate the potential interaction between IGF2BP3 and EGFR-AS1, we employed RIP-PCR. The results revealed that IGF2BP3 could enrich EGFR-AS1 (1.073 ± 0.129 vs. 0.290 ± 0.118%; Fig. 6C). Moreover, the immunohistochemistry results revealed that IGF2BP3 expression was significantly decreased in radioresistant cervical cancer tissues compared to radiosensitive cervical cancer tissues (0.930 ± 0.018 vs. 1.000 ± 0.019; Fig. S3A). The mRNA and protein expression of IGF2BP3 was remarkably decreased in CaSki cells and SiHa cells induced by irradiation (SiHa: IGF2BP3 mRNA 0.500 ± 0.058 vs. 1.000 ± 0.130; CaSki: IGF2BP3 mRNA 0.404 ± 0.043 vs. 1.000 ± 0.107; SiHa: IGF2BP3 protein 0.346 ± 0.054 vs. 1.000 ± 0.073; Caski: IGF2BP3 protein 0.348 ± 0.048 vs. 1.000 ± 0.137; Fig. S3D, S3E, S3H, S3I). Subsequently, to probe into the function of IGF2BP3 in cervical cancer cell ferroptosis, IGF2BP3 shRNAs were transfected into SiHa and CaSki cells. qRT-PCR analysis verified that sh-IGF2BP3 1–3 obviously decreased the expression of IGF2BP3, with sh-IGF2BP3-1 achieving the best effect (SiHa: sh-IGF2BP3-1 0.374 ± 0.018 vs. 1.000 ± 0.127; CaSki: 0.331 ± 0.069 vs. 1.000 ± 0.132; Fig. 7A, S4A), which was selected for the next study. The knockdown efficiency of IGF2BP3 was further confirmed by western blot (SiHa: 0.569 ± 0.086 vs. 1.000 ± 0.106; CaSki 0.444 ± 0.057 vs. 1.000 ± 0.103; Fig. 7B, S4B). IGF2BP3 knockdown reversed the promotive impact of EGFR-AS1 knockdown on ROS and MDA levels (SiHa: ROS 1.456 ± 0.001 vs. 0.286 ± 0.018 vs. 1.655 ± 0.001 vs. 1.000 ± 0.072; MDA 20.167 ± 3.294 vs. 8.040 ± 2.043 vs. 38.753 ± 2.852 vs. 19.400 ± 1.107 µmol/µg; CaSki: ROS 1.155 ± 0.094 vs. 0.598 ± 0.028 vs. 1.811 ± 0.013 vs. 1.000 ± 0.130; MDA 28.003 ± 2.245 vs. 11.853 ± 0.461 vs. 41.607 ± 2.886 vs. 24.790 ± 2.176 µmol/µg; Fig. 7C and D, S4C, S4D). Silencing of IGF2BP3 resulted in a notable increase in the expression of GPX4 and FTH1 proteins, and IGF2BP3 knockdown reversed the inhibitory impact of EGFR-AS1 knockdown on FTH1 and GPX4 protein expression (SiHa: FTH1 0.885 ± 0.076 vs. 1.532 ± 0.233 vs. 0.415 ± 0.064 vs. 1.000 ± 0.239; GPX4 1.090 ± 0.044 vs. 1.418 ± 0.150 vs. 0.474 ± 0.023 vs. 1.000 ± 0.118; CaSki: FTH1 1.049 ± 0.143 vs. 1.892 ± 0.203 vs. 0.382 ± 0.061 vs. 1.000 ± 0.061; GPX4 1.000 ± 0.143 vs. 1.576 ± 0.104 vs. 0.394 ± 0.119 vs. 1.000 ± 0.141; Fig. 7E, S4E). The clone formation assay indicated that EGFR-AS1 knockdown significantly inhibited viability of SiHa cells, whereas IGF2BP3 knockdown showed the opposite results (SiHa: colonies 256.333 ± 25.423 vs. 467.333 ± 10.017 vs. 139.333 ± 13.204 vs. 271.333 ± 11.240; Fig. 7F). Notably, IGF2BP3 knockdown counteracted the effect of EGFR-AS1 knockdown. These results revealed that EGFR-AS1 inhibits cervical cancer cell ferroptosis via IGF2BP3.

EGFR-AS1 interacts with IGF2BP3 to regulate APAF1 expression via m6A modification

To further determine the signaling pathway that EGFR-AS1 was involved in, we divided TCGA cervical cancer radiotherapy samples into two groups according to the EGFR-AS1 expression and performed GSEA analysis. GSEA analysis showed that the EGFR-AS1 high expression group was mainly involved in the p53, and NOD_LIKE_RECEPTOR signaling pathways (Fig. 8A). Previous studies have established a link between the p53 signaling pathway and ferroptosis [26, 27]. Therefore, we next revolve around the p53 signaling pathway. Further research of the correlation between the 12 genes with the highest enrichment degree in the p53 signaling pathway and IGF2BP3 showed that most genes were significantly positively correlated with IGF2BP3 (Fig. 8B). eCLIP-seq data in ENCODE database showed that APAF1 could bind with IGF2BP3 (Fig. 8C). Molecular docking further verified the interaction between IGF2BP3 and APAF1 (Fig. 8D). The SRAMP database analysis showed that APAF1 had a few m6A modification sites with high confidence (Fig. 8E). The m6A modification site was shown in Fig. 8F. The MeRIP-Seq results of the CVm6A database also show that EGFR-AS1 had m6A modification (Fig. 8G). MeRIP-PCR results revealed that the level of m6A modification of APAF1 was notably enriched (0.363 ± 0.046% vs. 0.026 ± 0.011%; Fig. 8H). Moreover, the immunohistochemistry results revealed that APAF1 expression was prominently reduced in radioresistant cervical cancer tissues compared to radiosensitive cervical cancer tissues (0.887 ± 0.012 vs. 1.000 ± 0.031; Fig. S3B). EGFR-AS1 knockdown significantly increased APAF1 mRNA expression in mice tumor tissues under irradiation (1.647 ± 0.379 vs. 1.000 ± 0.114; Fig. S3C). The mRNA and protein expression of APAF1 was remarkably decreased in CaSki cells and SiHa cells induced by irradiation (SiHa: APAF1 mRNA 0.432 ± 0.042 vs. 1.000 ± 0.060; CaSki: APAF1 mRNA 0.383 ± 0.032 vs. 1.000 ± 0.069; SiHa: APAF1 protein 0.355 ± 0.106 vs. 1.000 ± 0.112; CaSki: APAF1 protein 0.263 ± 0.099 vs. 1.000 ± 0.065; Fig. S3F, S3G, S3H, S3I). These results suggest that EGFR-AS1 bound IGF2BP3 to regulate APAF1 expression via m6A modification.

EGFR-AS1 inhibits ferroptosis via the m6A/APAF1 axis in cervical cancer cells

To investigate whether the role of EGFR-AS1 in ferroptosis depends on the m6A/APAF1 axis, the rescue experiments were carried out by knockdown APAF1 expression in EGFR-AS1 knockdown SiHa and CaSki cells. qRT-PCR and western blot analysis verified that the expression of APAF1 was remarkably reduced after APAF1 was knockdown (SiHa: qRT-PCR 0.368 ± 0.032 vs. 1.000 ± 0.025, western blot 0.391 ± 0.113 vs. 1.000 ± 0.156; CaSki: qRT-PCR 0.309 ± 0.054 vs. 1.000 ± 0.053; western blot 0.325 ± 0.042 vs. 1.000 ± 0.078; Fig. 9A and B, S5A, S5B). qRT-PCR assay indicated that EGFR-AS1 knockdown prominently enhanced the mRNA expression of APAF1, while this trend was reversed by IGF2BP3 knockdown or DAA, an m6A inhibitor (2.075 ± 0.091 vs. 2.108 ± 0.304 vs. 4.023 ± 0.385 vs. 1.000 ± 0.082; Fig. 9C). In addition, EGFR-AS1 deficiency dramatically facilitated ROS generation and increased MDA levels in cervical cancer cells, whereas APAF1 knockdown or DAA could inhibit this effect (SiHa: ROS 1.150 ± 0.099 vs. 1.122 ± 0.158 vs. 3.690 ± 0.059 vs. 1.000 ± 0.027; MDA 27.727 ± 3.439 vs. 27.807 ± 2.036 vs. 38.110 ± 2.576 vs. 19.510 ± 2.453 µmol/µg; CaSki: ROS 1.134 ± 0.164 vs. 1.133 ± 0.099 vs. 4.141 ± 0.001 vs. 1.000 ± 0.103; MDA 33.610 ± 1.948 vs. 35.083 ± 2.213 vs. 52.287 ± 3.630 vs. 24.757 ± 0.620 µmol/µg; Fig. 9D and E, S5C, S5D). APAF1 knockdown or DAA reversed the inhibitory impact of EGFR-AS1 knockdown on FTH1 and GPX4 protein expression (SiHa: FTH1 0.737 ± 0.147 vs. 0.652 ± 0.115 vs. 0.275 ± 0.063 vs. 1.000 ± 0.086; GPX4 0.694 ± 0.035 vs. 0.722 ± 0.119 vs. 0.336 ± 0.024 vs. 1.000 ± 0.067; CaSki: FTH1 0.912 ± 0.020 vs. 1.105 ± 0.142 vs. 0.457 ± 0.016 vs. 1.000 ± 0.106; GPX4 1.024 ± 0.329 vs. 1.412 ± 0.195 vs. 0.376 ± 0.031 vs. 1.000 ± 0.223; Fig. 9F, S5E). Overall, EGFR-AS1 depended the m6A/IGF2BP3/APAF1 axis to restrain cervical cancer cell ferroptosis (Fig. S6).

Discussion

Radiotherapy is a commonly used treatment for cervical cancer in clinical practice, which makes some patients get better treatment, but the emergence of radiation resistance causes the recurrence and metastasis of cervical cancer patients after radiotherapy [28]. In recent researches, the significance of ferroptosis in the effectiveness of radiotherapy for specific types of tumors has been emphasized. For example, Lei et al. found that in lung cancer cells, radiotherapy induced ferroptosis and ferroptosis inducers could make radiation tolerant cancer cells and xenograft tumors sensitive to radiotherapy [29]. Chen et al. found that induction of ferroptosis promoted radiosensitization of hepatocellular carcinoma [30]. Consistent with these findings, we revealed that compared to radiotherapy-resistant tissues, the expression of ferroptosis-related molecules was higher in radiotherapy-sensitive cervical cancer tissues, and ferroptosis inducer enhanced the suppression action of radiotherapy on the viability of cervical cancer cells. Therefore, targeting ferroptosis is a novel method to intensify sensitization to radiotherapy in cervical cancer.

Recent researches have demonstrated the involvement of lncRNAs in the radiotherapy sensitivity of various cancers. LncRNA PCAT1 silencing intensify sensitization to radiotherapy in cervical cancer via targeting miR-128/GOLM1 [15]. Reduction in lncRNA HOTAIR expression enhanced radiosensitivity in cervical cancer cells by up-regulating p21 [31]. EGFR-AS1 is highly expressed in various tumors and is closely related to the proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis of tumor cells. For example, EGFR-AS1 upregulated miR-449a by sponging HDAC1 to enhance lung cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [32]. EGFR-AS1 enhanced the expression of EGFR to drive bladder cancer progression [33]. EGFR-AS1 facilitated invasion and migration in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma by acting as a sponge for miR-145 and increasing ROCK1 expression [34]. In this study, EGFR-AS1 was elevated in radiotherapy-resistant tissues and EGFR-AS1 knockdown promotes ferroptosis in irradiation-treated cervical cancer cells. Therefore, EGFR-AS1 is a potential target for regulating the radiosensitivity of cervical cancer.

Radiation simultaneously induces ferroptosis and the upregulation of EGFR-AS1 (which has an anti-ferroptosis function), reflecting a classic “induction-confrontation” dynamic balance within cancer cells. Radiation first triggers an acute ferroptosis signal in the overall cell population, including a surge in ROS, accumulation of lipid peroxides, and a decline in GPX4 activity, which are dose-time-dependent early events. However, after sublethal doses or repeated irradiation, the surviving “adaptive” subpopulations activate multiple negative feedback pathways, one of which is the upregulation of lncEGFR-AS1. This lncRNA enhances GPX4, inhibits ROS and MDA levels through the m6A/IGF2BP3/APAF1 axis, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and further promoting radioresistance. In other words, ferroptosis is dominant in the acute phase, then suppressed by the anti-ferroptosis program mediated by lncEGFR-AS1, and eventually manifests as radiotherapy resistance. In short, cells “allow” ferroptosis to occur first, and then use EGFR-AS1 as a “brake” to select for radiotherapy-resistant clones capable of continued proliferation.

m6A recognition proteins, such as the YTH domain family (YTHDF1/2/3 and YTHDC1/2), heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (HNRNPs, hnRNPC, hnRNPG and hnRNPA2B1) and insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA binding protein (IGF2BP1-3) [35]. These proteins can bind to m6A methylation sites, thereby influencing RNA modifications. Recently, evidence has unveiled that lncRNAs bind with IGF2BP3 and exert key role in cancer. For example, lncRNA CERS6-AS1 facilitated the progression of breast cancer through binding to IGF2BP3 [36]. LINC00467 enhanced hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation via interacting with IGF2BP3, resulting in increased stability of TRAF5 mRNA [37]. In our study, we discovered that EGFR-AS1 interacted with IGF2BP3, and silencing of IGF2BP3 significantly enhanced the protein expression of FTH1 and GPX4. Hence, EGFR-AS1 may inhibit ferroptosis of cervical cancer cells via interacting with the IGF2BP3.

To further identify the target genes through which EGFR-AS1 inhibits ferroptosis of cervical cancer cells, we categorized TCGA cervical cancer radiotherapy samples as two groups according to the expression level of EGFR-AS1 and conducted GSEA analysis. The results unveiled a notable enrichment of the p53 and NOD_LIKE_RECEPTOR signaling pathways in the EGFR-AS1 high expression group. We also found that APAF1 mRNA in the p53 signaling pathway had binding ability to IGF2BP3. Previous research has demonstrated that lncRNAs can mediate the stability of target genes through m6A modification and participate in cancer progression. For example, LINC00942 directly recruited METTL14, leading to stabilization of downstream targets like CXCR4 and CYP1B1 through post-transcriptional m6A modification, thus advancing the progression of breast cancer cells [38]. LINC00460/DHX9/IGF2BP2 complex mediates the stability of HMGA1 mRNA via m6A modification, advancing the proliferation in colorectal cancer [39]. Therefore, IGF2BP3 might bind with APAF1 mRNA and depend on m6A modification to enhance the stability of APAF1. However, further in-depth investigations are critical for completely comprehend the mechanistic details of this process in cervical cancer.

There are several limitations in this study. First, although we identified EGFR-AS1 as a key lncRNA modulating ferroptosis and radiosensitivity through in vitro and in vivo experiments, the clinical relevance was based primarily on public database analyses and limited tissue samples. Further validation in large-scale, independent clinical cohorts is required. Second, while we demonstrated that EGFR-AS1 regulates APAF1 expression via the m6A reader IGF2BP3, other potential RNA-binding proteins or upstream regulators may also participate in this pathway and warrant further investigation. Lastly, although our in vivo model supports the role of EGFR-AS1 in reducing radiosensitivity, it may not fully mimic the tumor microenvironment of human cervical cancer. Future studies using patient-derived xenografts or organoid models could provide more translational insight.

To summarize, this study unveiled that EGFR-AS1 inhibited ferroptosis and reduced radiosensitivity of cervical cancer cells. Mechanically, EGFR-AS1 competitively bound to the m6A recognition protein IGF2BP3 and mediates APAF1 expression to regulate ferroptosis in an m6A dependent manner. This study offers novel insights into the modulation of radiotherapy sensitivity of cervical cancer mediated by epigenetic modification.
