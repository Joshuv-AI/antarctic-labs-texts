Solar stills: A review for water scarcity solutions
Authors: Sujit Kumar, Asim Ahmad, Kashif Irshad, Om Prakash, Rukaiya Kausher, S M Mozammil Hasnain, Shatrudhan Pandey, Anipa Tapalova, Nurgali Akylbekov, Rustem Zairov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar stills represent a crucial technology in the quest to provide clean and accessible water, particularly in regions facing water scarcity and limited energy resources. This study investigates various solar still designs, exploring their advantages and limitations in water purification. Through an analysis of the current global landscape of solar still usage and ongoing research endeavors, this study aims to highlight the potential of solar stills and propose modifications to enhance their efficiency. The study also investigates various extensive analysis of diverse solar still designs and their performance metrics, including factors like reflectors, wick materials, absorber plates, and air velocity, this study elucidates key strategies to augment water output and energy utilization. Reflectors and pebbles, variations in wick materials, and modifications in absorber plate design have shown promising results in increasing productivity. Additionally, insights into the impact of environmental variables such as solar radiation, wind velocity, and ambient temperature on solar still performance are provided, informing the design optimization process. Through an analysis of global usage patterns and ongoing research efforts, this study highlights the untapped potential of solar stills and proposes strategies to enhance their performance. Furthermore, it suggests avenues for future research, including the integration of heat storage materials and the development of advanced modeling and control algorithms, to further enhance the performance of solar stills. Through these efforts, solar stills can emerge as a sustainable and reliable solution for water purification, contributing significantly to global efforts to combat water scarcity.Keywords: Solar energy, Single slope solar still, Sensible heat storage, Latent heat storage, Hybrid solar still

Introduction

Water is an indispensable element for all living beings on Earth. While Earth's surface is predominantly covered with water, only a small fraction of this water is readily accessible for human consumption. As the global population continues to grow, concerns over water scarcity are escalating. Many experts predict that water scarcity could spark conflicts between nations, particularly in regions where freshwater resources are already scarce. This is particularly concerning for developing countries, many of which already face challenges in providing adequate freshwater supplies for their citizens.

The scarcity of useable-grade water is exacerbated in war-affected countries and regions struck by natural disasters. These areas often experience disruptions in water infrastructure and sanitation systems, leading to a lack of access to clean and safe drinking water. Water is essential for sustaining life, and every living organism on Earth deserves access to this fundamental resource. Conventional water sources can only partially meet the growing demand for water. As existing freshwater sources are unable to accommodate the current water needs, innovative solutions are needed. Saline water, which constitutes over 96 % of Earth's water, is readily available but cannot be directly consumed. Therefore, we must find ways to convert saline water into useable-grade freshwater.

Desalination, the process of removing salt from seawater or brackish water, offers a promising solution to address water scarcity. Desalination technologies have evolved significantly in recent years, making them more efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly. Reverse osmosis and multi-stage flash distillation are two prominent desalination methods that are widely employed to produce potable water from saline sources. By implementing effective desalination techniques, we can expand our access to freshwater and alleviate the growing water scarcity crisis. This will not only ensure a sustainable supply of water for human consumption but also safeguard the health and well-being of communities worldwide.

The present review expands on previous research in solar still technologies by offering an in-depth evaluation of their efficiency, design variations, and overall impact on sustainable water production. While earlier studies predominantly concentrated on specific elements, such as material selection or performance enhancement methods, this review presents a broader view, integrating various aspects like economic viability, environmental impact, and advancements in hybrid systems, as detailed in Table 1. This provides a holistic understanding of the current state and future potential of solar still technology, highlighting areas for further improvement and innovation.

Water crisis -world scenario

A water crisis occurs when the available water resources are not sufficient to meet the needs of the population. A water emergency occurs when there is a shortage of clean water that is sufficient to meet the basic needs of people living in a particular area. Developing countries are currently facing a lack of access to clean water. Natural water sources cannot meet the demands of the population, resulting in a severe shortage of clean water for human needs.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 2.6 billion people currently lack access to clean drinking water. This lack of access to clean water contributes to 25 % of the world's disease burden. More than 1.6 million people die each year from diarrhea caused by unsafe water and sanitation, and 90 % of these deaths are children under the age of five [4].

Topographical examination shows that more than two-thirds of the Earth's surface is covered in water, of which 97 % is saline, 2.6 % is ice, and just under 1 % is freshwater available for human use. Due to population growth, the demand for water is increasing rapidly. The availability of water for both quantitative and qualitative use is a major challenge today. By 2025, it's predicted that every person would be under water stress [5].

Industrialization, agriculture, and human waste contamination is dumped directly into rivers, lakes, and ponds, creating approximately 12,000 cubic kilometers of wastewater each year. If this trend continues, the world will produce 18,000 cubic kilometers of wastewater by 2050 [6].

Addressing this requires sustainable resource management, infrastructure development, and international collaboration. Environmental sustainability hinges on water conservation, ecosystem preservation, and reducing pollution. The water crisis & A story of hope- A case study article

Around a billion people worldwide still lack access to drinking water, which means they must fetch and drink surface water from contaminated streams and rivers, suffering the consequences.

Around 1.6 million people in the developing world die each year from preventable diseases caused by lack of access to safe drinking water and basic sanitation. Sadly, 90 % of these deaths are children under 5. Millions more are infected with hepatitis, worms, and diseases that cause blindness.

Every hour, 115 people across Africa die from diseases linked to contaminated water. Sub-Saharan Africa has some of the world's worst water statistics, with 330 million people lacking access to clean water. The impact of this tragedy is far-reaching. In a region already overwhelmed by health risks, treating severe diarrhea caused by contaminated water consumes a staggering 12 % of the health budget. On a typical day, more than half of the available hospital beds in Sub-Saharan Africa are occupied by patients suffering from fecal-related, water-borne diseases [8].

The impact of the water crisis does not end there. It is estimated that approximately 40 billion working hours are exhausted each year for the collection of drinking water in Sub-Saharan Africa, equivalent to a year's worth of labor in France. Women in developing nations walk an average of 4 miles every day to fetch water. This time spent collecting water could be invested in education or income generation. Studies have revealed the shocking fact that the water crisis is the sole reason why many girls in Sub-Saharan Africa drop out of school. It can be overwhelming to try to conceptualize the far-reaching impact of the water crisis. However, it can also be exciting to be a part of the solution and to witness the hope that is generated by simple access to this basic resource: clean water [9].

Healthline Clinic, located in Barnesville, Liberia, an impoverished suburb of Monrovia, provides essential healthcare services to a community that lacks access to piped water. Despite the difficult environment and devastating lack of basic resources, the clinic has been a beacon of hope. The clinic is managed by Tage Swallie, a local pastor and his wife, a nurse midwife. Together, they serve thousands of patients each year, providing pediatric and maternal care to the needy members of their community. Compassion Corps has been able to provide some construction materials and supplies to help support the clinic's work [10].

The clinic has operated without on-site water for years, but thanks to the fundraising efforts of the cyclists in last summer's Wheels for Wells event and the donors who supported them, the clinic will now have ready access to water for cleaning, washing, and drinking! Compassion Corps is excited to announce the installation of the well and pump funded by last year's bike ride. Tage Swallie is thrilled at the new hope and possibilities that clean water will offer his clinic, its patients, and the members of the surrounding community, who will have free access to the well during certain open public hours [11].

The new well installation was completed on June 1st. In early July 2022, a Compassion Corps short-term team will travel to Liberia to observe the use and impact of the well and to help Healthline put on a community outreach clinic. Clean water will undoubtedly prove to be very helpful!

Solar energy

Solar energy is the driving force for life on Earth. It is responsible for wind flow, climate change, rain, and photosynthesis. Solar energy is interconverted into heat, the main thrust and power of life on Earth [11].

Solar energy is free, economically viable, and inexhaustible. This incredibly clean energy can substitute for petroleum and its derivatives, which are harmful to the environment. Failing to exploit such an easily accessible and low-impact resource would be an injustice to future generations. In a broad sense, solar energy is the foundation for all energy available on Earth. Fossil fuels are formed from biomass, which is formed from the prolonged decay of plants trapped in swamps and buried underground for many years. Wind energy is created by temperature differences caused by the sun's rays. This wind drives windmills [12].

Solar energy is a promising renewable energy source due to its clean nature, abundance, and low cost. India has an enormous scope for generating solar energy because of its geographical advantage and excellent solar radiation across the country. As a tropical country, India receives solar radiation throughout the year, amounting to nearly 3000 h of sunshine annually. Almost all parts of India receive 4–7 kWh of solar radiation per square meter, with a mean solar hour of 8 h per day and around 300 sunny days each year [13].

India's potential for solar energy is estimated to be 178 billion megawatts (MW), which is approximately 20,000 times the world's current energy demand. However, large-scale production has not yet been realized. The aggregate capacity of solar energy in India, based on solar potential alone, is estimated to be comparable to 6000 MW. The solar power where the sun hits the atmosphere is 1017 W, while the solar power on the Earth's surface is estimated to be 1016 W. The aggregate overall power required for progress is estimated to be about 1013 W. The sun provides us with 1000 times more power than we require. Therefore, if we could utilize 5 % of this energy, it would be 50 times what the world would need [13].

Solar desalination

Solar desalination utilizes the sun's energy to extract fresh water from saline sources such as brine or brackish water. This process replicates the natural water cycle through evaporation and condensation. In this system, sunlight strikes the top surface of a cover plate, which is designed to transmit maximum light flux to the underlying basin containing the saline water. Both radiative and convective heat transfer play crucial roles in this process. The basin's darkened surface efficiently absorbs the incident radiation, minimizing reflection back into the environment [16].

Evaporation, influenced by water temperature across all ranges, is accelerated by heating. Heating enhances the rate of heat transfer from the surrounding environment to the water. The resulting water vapor rises and reaches the significantly cooler interior surface of the cover plate, where it undergoes condensation. During this process, the vapor releases its latent heat of condensation and transforms back into a liquid state. This condensed liquid then flows down the trough channels and is ultimately collected within a bottle [17]. The classification of desalination process is shown in Fig. 3.

Desalination is essential for providing potable water in arid and semi-arid regions around the world where fresh water is scarce. Sustainable energy sources are being used for desalination in these regions [19]. A satisfactory and reliable supply of drinking water is essential for the public. Water is the key to human success; it is inextricably linked to the advancement of human civilization. Distilled water obtained from rivers, lakes, and reservoirs in abundance is becoming scarce due to industrialization and population growth. The lack of fresh water has always existed, but it was previously limited to areas where only brackish water was available [20]. Solar distillation is a subset of solar desalination. It involves using solar energy to evaporate water, which then condenses to produce purified water. This process is akin to the natural water cycle, where solar energy heats the water, causing it to evaporate, and then it condenses to form fresh water.

Principle of solar distillation

Distillation is a simple process in which impure water is evaporated, leaving behind the impurities. The vapor is then condensed to produce pure water. This is basic physics. The hydrological cycle is also based on the same concept. However, converting water to vapor and then back to water requires energy. The steps of the hydrological cycle can be summarized in chronological order as follows: evaporation and vapor production, condensation of the vapor as it rises in the atmosphere, and precipitation, which results in rainfall. Therefore, distillation is the underlying concept of rainfall [21].

This natural process is closely replicated in a solar still. Solar stills are easy to construct and have almost no moving mechanical parts. They are also much cheaper and can be built from simple and readily available materials. Today, there are many facilities available for obtaining drinking water, but they may be uneconomical or inaccessible. Solar distillation stands out among all water purification methods because it is economical and only requires solar energy to convert brackish water to pure water. Design parameters analysis of solar still

The output of a solar still is largely determined by key design parameters such as the depth of brine water, the thickness of the condensing cover, and the angle of its inclination. These factors are crucial as they directly affect the efficiency of the solar still by influencing the rates of evaporation and condensation.

Brine depth

Brine depth is a crucial parameter for designing solar still as it contributes to the distillate output [25]. Research was done which authenticate the fact that brine depth and distillate output are related. Panchal et al. [26] in his work developed a solar still with four still units and performed experiments on them using different water levels which ranged from 2 cm to 7 cm in Suez Gulf area. In his work he found that as the brine depth increased output showed a decreasing nature and it almost decreased by 14 %. More experiments conducted by Khalifa and Hamood [27] to establish the dependency of brine water depth to distillate outcome, in their experimental work five various depths 1 cm, 4 cm, 6 cm 8 cm and 10 cm were considered. It was also concluded by them at higher depths, nocturnal output increased due heat stored in daylight. Also there were many attempts to understand the how the heat and mass transfer coefficients behaved with different brine depths, for both passive and active modes. As the volumetric heat capacity inside basin is higher at low depths thus, the distillate output is higher. When active and passive solar stills were compared it was found active solar still was more productive than passive, since the temperature difference water and inner glass is more in case of active solar still when compared with passive one [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]].

Material, thickness and inclination of condensing cover material

The thickness, material, and angle of the cover all affect the solar still's output. For locations in the northern hemisphere, the inclination angle should be south, whereas for locations in the southern hemisphere, it should be north. The latitude and inclination angle of the location should be the same [33]. This is done so as that the radiation remains contant throughout the year. The characteristics of the glass cover should be transparent and with low reflectivity [34]. Thickness of the cover also has effect on output, lower the thickness higher is the transmittivity and absorptivity. Thus, increasing the solar radiation inside the basin which results in higher output [35]. It was found copper when used as cover material has higher yields owing to its higher thermal conductivity when compared with glass and plastic by Tiwari and Tiwari [36] in their work.

The effect of thickness of glass cover also contributes to the output, this was investigated by Badran [37] in his work. Bardan took cover of thickness ranging from 2 mm to 6 mm rest parameters remaining same. The output has a decreasing trend for both active and passive modes as reported by Bardan. Relation of inclination of glass cover with output was explained by Abdallah et al. [38] in his work done in New Delhi, India in time span of one year with three different inclinations of 15°, 30° and 45°. Output was maximum for 15°.

Types of solar still

Double basin still produces higher yield than single basin still [39]. The latent heat of condensation of lower basin is utilized by water in upper basin thus, enhancing the yield whereas, there is loss of latent heat of condensation to the atmosphere from the glass in single basin stills.

Passive solar still - single basin

Asphalt was one of the basin enhancers employed by Dev and Tiwari [40] to boost the output of a single basin solar still. They employed a constant head tank in conjunction with a feed tank and a solar still to keep a constant depth in the basin. It was found in this study that enhancers, such as sprinklers and asphalt, increase distillate output by 16 % at night and 51 % during the day. Dwivedi and Tiwari [41] employed four identical-sized solar stills with various absorbing materials to investigate the impact of various absorbing materials on output. Black rock was discovered to be the most productive in their work.

Active solar still - single basin

Active solar stills are those that utilize a solar collector in conjunction with the basin to achieve higher thermal energy levels. This additional heat input allows for increased distillate production compared to passive solar stills, as less energy is required for evaporation [42]. Rajaseenivasan and Kalidasa [43] used a double slope active solar still setup in natural circulation mode for a period of six months. The distillate increased by 51 % in comparison to traditional stills, and the results matched both theoretical and experimental findings [44]. Boukar and Harmin [45] compared the output of a traditional solar still with a modified still fitted with a flat plate collector and discovered that the two variables have a significant impact on the radiation and ambient temperature.

Passive solar still - multi basin

Multi basin solar still uses the latent heat of condensation of the lower basin in upper basin, thus increasing the output when compared with conventional solar still. Rajaseenivasan et al. [46] developed a solar still with two basins and double slope under the climatic conditions of Madurai, India. In their experimental work the yield increased by 85 % when compared to conventional still.

Active solar still - multi basin

Output of the still majorly depends on temperature difference between the basin water and the inner wall of condensing plate. This temperature difference increases as the water inside basin increases. The only drawback associated with multi basin still is low radiation collection of lower basin than upper basin [47,48]. Thus, this becomes mandatory to introduce external sources for enhancement of thermal energy inside the basin. Panchal [49] in his experimental analysis introduced evacuated tubes to the lower basin of a double basin still without any heat absorbing material. The distillate for the mentioned set-up increased by.

56 % compared to conventional still and the output spiked to 65 % when the identical set-up was equipped with black granite gravel.

Climatic influence on solar still

Climatic conditions cannot be controlled by humans beings as they live at the mercy of nature. Distillate output increases with the small duration of clear sky. The same can be replicated by a thin layer of water allowed to flow over the glass cover. Also, the ambient temperature, velocity of wind and solar radiations are also categorized under climatic factors [[50], [51], [52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58]]. There is a clear relationship between the outside temperature and the output of a solar still as shown in Table 2 [59]. Data suggests that higher ambient temperatures generally lead to increased efficiency and output of solar stills. Higher temperatures enhance the evaporation process, which is essential for the operation of a solar still. Findings indicate that productivity increases with rising ambient temperatures, particularly under optimal conditions such as specific orientations (south–north) and tilt angles (10°). Data shows that lower water depths and higher solar radiation reception contribute to higher productivity. For practical and efficient use, solar stills should ideally operate within the 35 °C–45 °C ambient temperature range. This range ensures maximum productivity by taking advantage of optimal solar radiation and evaporation conditions. While solar stills are theoretically functional across a broad temperature spectrum, their efficiency and practical usability are highest within this specific range, as it balances the necessary thermal conditions for optimal water distillation [59].

Wind velocity

The variation in velocity of wind is also one of the governing factor for still output. It has been observed that convective heat transfer co-efficient from glass to ambient increases as wind velocity increases accompanied by gradual increase in the difference of temperature between inner side of glass cover and water thus, enhancing still's performance. Khalifa et al. [53] carried out an indoor experiment with varying wind speed of 1.14 m/s, 2.06 m/s, 2.92 m/s and 4.01 m/s and with no wind using a fan. The performance of still increased with increase in wind speed. It was also observed reasonable speed of wind gave more yield than wind speeds at very high level. This phenomenon was also proved legit when El-Sebaii [50] performed his experiments on active and passive solar stills in Jordan.

The effect of ambient temperature on solar still output was studied by Malik et al. [55]. Malik developed a theoretical model to study the effect of ambient temperature on still distillate. Very small increase of the order of 2–3% was found at an ambient temperature of 5 °C.

Solar radiation is one of the main ingredients for distillate, without it the concept of conventional still couldn't be conceived [59]. Many researchers tried to understand the relationship of solar radiation with distillate output, and a general understanding was reached that with increase in radiation the output increases. Energy and exergy analysis of solar stills

Energy analysis is a thermodynamic tool based on the first law of thermodynamics. It assesses how energy is utilized within a process and its components. The energy efficiency of a Solar Still is generally a function of the distillate output, latent heat of evaporation, incident solar radiation, and the area of the still. In hybrid systems, the input and output parameters vary based on the hybridization configuration [28].

While energy balance does not provide information about energy or resource depletion, exergy analysis does. Exergy analysis uses the principles of mass and energy conservation alongside the second law of thermodynamics to analyze, design, optimize, and improve energy systems. It evaluates the energy quality within a thermodynamic system by measuring exergy in forms such as kinetic, potential, work, chemical, and thermal [29].

One primary objective of exergy analysis is to determine the location and causes of exergy destruction or loss. Exergy destruction, or irreversibility rate, is a crucial concept in analyzing thermal systems like boilers, turbines, and thermal power stations. Exergy applications in HSS analysis are limited but essential for understanding exergy destruction in hybrid systems. The exergy efficiency is the ratio of net exergy output to input exergy [30].

Fig. 5, Fig. 6 present the energy and exergy efficiencies of various SS configurations. It is noted that solar stills integrated with photovoltaic thermal (PVT) systems show higher exergy efficiencies due to the combined electrical and thermal exergy efficiencies. This high efficiency is particularly observed during months with lower ambient temperatures, reducing thermal losses in PV systems. Further studies are required to comprehensively understand exergy destruction in hybrid solar still systems. Improving solar stills with micro/nanoparticles

Micro/nanoparticles in passive solar stills

Nanoparticles, known for their ability to absorb solar radiation and transfer heat to water, significantly enhance the evaporation process in solar stills (SS). In a well-insulated container with a tightly sealed glass cover, brackish or saline water mixed with nanoparticles heats up, leading to rapid evaporation. The resulting vapor condenses on the cooler inner glass surface, and the condensate is collected as freshwater.

Oxide nanoparticles are commonly used in SS due to their durability and cost-effectiveness. Among these, Al2O3 has been found particularly effective. In April, at Kovilpatti, India, Al2O3 nanofluid achieved a 29.95 % productivity enhancement, outperforming SnO2 (18.63 %) and ZnO (12.67 %) [74]. This is attributed to their thermal conductivities: Al2O3 (0.6355 W/m·K), SnO2 (0.6215 W/m·K), and ZnO (0.6105 W/m·K).

Theoretical analysis in New Delhi showed similar results for CuO, TiO2, and Al2O3 at concentrations of 0.2–0.3 % wt, with Al2O3 yielding the highest energy efficiency (50.34 %) compared to pure water (37.78 %) [75]. Despite low concentrations, significant enhancements were observed, suggesting that higher concentrations could further improve productivity. However, white oxide nanoparticles may reflect too much light at higher concentrations, making black-colored nanoparticles like CuO and carbon-based particles preferable.

Sharshir et al. demonstrated that using graphite micro-flakes (GMF) and CuO nanoparticles in SS, along with glass cover cooling, can enhance productivity by 44.91 % and 53.95 %, respectively. With GMF, productivity can be improved by 73.80 % when combined with phase change materials [76].

Micro/nanoparticles in active solar stills

Active SSs benefit more from nanofluids due to enhanced heat and mass transfer rates. External condensers are particularly effective, achieving high evaporation and condensation rates. Kabeel et al. reported that Cu2O nanoparticles, at an optimal concentration of 0.2 % with a vacuum fan condenser, increased freshwater output by 93.87 % without a fan and 133.64 % with a fan [77]. Omara et al. found that using Cu2O and Al2O3 in a hybrid SS increased productivity by 285.10 % and 254.88 %, respectively [78].

Nanofluid-based solar collectors also improve active SS performance. However, productivity enhancements are modest. Mahian et al. noted only a 1 % improvement using SiO2 nanofluid [79], while Sahota et al. reported enhancements of 31.49 %, 7.26 %, and 26.4 % for CuO, TiO2, and Al2O3, respectively [80].

Cost analysis of using micro/nanoparticles

The cost of water production varies based on system type and modifications. Sharshir et al. reported that pyramid SS with CuO nanofluid costs around $0.015/L, lower than conventional SS at $0.021/L, due to significant productivity enhancements [81]. Panchal et al. found that passive stepped SS with 0.2 % MgO has half the cost and payback period compared to those without nanofluids [82].

For active SS, Kabeel et al. found that using external condensers and fans, Al2O3, and Cu2O nanoparticles reduced costs to $0.038/L and $0.035/L, respectively, lower than passive SS but higher than active SS without nanoparticles [83].

Challenges and perspectives

Erosion and Corrosion: Nanofluids can enhance erosion and corrosion on metallic surfaces in SS. Proper testing, system design, and maintenance can mitigate these effects [80].

Stability of Nanofluids: Stability remains a major challenge, with issues like aggregation and settlement of particles. This affects both passive and active systems, potentially increasing pressure drop and operational costs [78].

Complexity-Productivity Trade-off: Balancing cost, productivity, and complexity is crucial. In regions with ample land but limited resources, simple conventional SS might be more feasible, while nano-enhanced SS could be advantageous in developed regions with higher initial investment capacity [78].

By considering local conditions and system design, solar stills can be optimized for both productivity and cost-effectiveness.

For a glass cover thickness of 3 mm, an increased efficiency of 26.22 % was observed compared to glass cover thicknesses of 5 mm and 6 mm. This suggests that increasing the glass thickness leads to a decrease in efficiency due to the reduced transmittance of the glass surface.

Zerrouki et al. [93] performed a numerical simulation of a capillary film solar still coupled with a conventional solar still in series. They developed mathematical models for both solar stills and determined the various thermophysical properties of system. A computer program was written in FORTRAN-90 language to solve the nonlinear equations, using the Runge-Kutta method. The investigation results showed that the distillate yield of the coupled system was higher than that of the conventional solar still system.

Maalem et al. [94] solved the heat and mass transfer equation system for a trapezoidal solar still system with three non-adiabatic walls using COMSOL Multiphysics software. The finite element approach was used to solve the energy balance equations. The temperature curves and condensed water production were found to be in good agreement with the experimental data, with a total production variance of less than 7 %.

Adhikari et al. [95] conducted research on a multi-stage stacked tray solar still. To improve heat transfer, they coupled the solar still with a solar collector in a computer simulation they created for steady-state circumstances. For the active solar still, thermophysical properties were determined in order to evaluate heat transfer coefficients, and mathematical modeling was carried out. To measure temperatures and track data, HP-BASIC was employed. To forecast the water's steady-state temperature and the associated yield output, a FORTRAN-77 computer program was created. The findings demonstrated that the still's estimated performance was quite satisfactory and that the parameters could be predicted.

A double-slope solar still was numerically analyzed by Ali et al. [96], with a particular enhancement carried out to increase efficiency by combining the brackish water with granular activated carbon. The different heat fluxes were calculated using MATLAB software, and a thorough study was carried out with graphical answers shown in MATLAB. The model was simulated using the Simulink toolbox, while MATLAB coding was done using ASCII text M-files.

Developmental CFD codes in solar still studies

Rheinlander [97] applied the Finite Difference Method (FDM) to solve the governing equations for a two-dimensional model of a single basin, double slope (SBDS) solar still (SS) under unsteady state conditions. Additionally, the k-ε model was used to solve the transport equation for kinematic eddy (turbulent) viscosity of fluid flow. The mass-transfer rates computed by Rheinlander were compared with experimental measurements by Cooper [98], showing a maximum error of 25 %.

Djebedjian and Abou Rayan [99] conducted a numerical investigation using FDM to predict performance and enhancements in a single basin, single slope (SBSS) SS design. Their mathematical model was based on the time-averaged Navier-Stokes equations under steady-state, two-dimensional conditions, and considered the impact of variable fluid properties using an air and vapor mixture in the SS. Their results revealed different circulation zones with reverse velocity inside the SS cavity, emphasizing the importance of numerical investigations prior to SS sizing.

Rahbar and Esfahani [34,35] examined the impact of free convection in a two-dimensional SBSS SS under steady-state, laminar, and incompressible ideal gas conditions using a developed CFD code. They derived a relationship for determining the heat transfer coefficient (HTC) by convection based on their numerical results, showing that the Rayleigh number (Ra) directly affects the Nusselt number (Nu). At constant Ra, Nu increases with an increasing aspect ratio. The highest HTC was observed in the zone where the flow moves downward from the glass to the water. Their numerical results were compared with experimental results and the Chilton–Colburn analogy reported by Shawaqfeh and Farid [100].

Juárez et al. [101] numerically investigated double-diffusive free convection and surface thermal radiation in an inclined space simulating a SS using the Finite Volume Method (FVM) to solve governing equations under two-dimensional steady-state conditions. They presented streamlines, isotherms, iso-lines of vapor and water mass flow rate, and average Nusselt (Nu) and Sherwood (Sh) numbers for various angles of the glass cover. Their findings indicated that surface thermal radiation changes the fluid flow from a single-cell to a multi-cellular pattern, increasing velocity near the walls and consequently boosting the average convective Nusselt number, total Nu, and Sh by around 25 %, 175 %, and 15 %, respectively. The mass flow rate of distillate increased with the aspect ratio and cover angle. Their average Nu results were compared with those by Béghein et al. [102] and Sezai and Mohamad [103], showing a maximum error of 2.1 %.

Rashidi et al. [104] developed a CFD code to optimize the position and size of segments inside a SBSS SS. Segments were introduced independently at the base surface and glass cover to enhance performance and improve heat exchange pathways, thereby increasing still efficiency. The optimization procedure aimed to maximize the normalized Nu of base-introduced segments, finding optimal parameters of X’ = 0.23 and Y’ = 0.18. The Nu results were compared with data from Rahbar et al. [105], showing a maximum error of 1.87 %.

In another study, Rashidi et al. [106] performed experimental and numerical investigations on the impact of segments on SS performance. Using numerical simulations based on the SIMPLE algorithm and various contours in two dimensions utilizing FVM, they found that stills with segments operated at significantly higher temperature contrasts between water and the condensing zone, particularly in the afternoon. Introducing segments into the still cavity increased the water production rate.

Rashidi and Esfahani [107] utilized CFD simulations to analyze local entropy generation for design improvements in a SBSS SS. Their numerical approach, based on the SIMPLE algorithm, simulated double diffusive natural convection in the SS and solved mass, momentum, energy, and concentration equations in two dimensions using FVM. They studied the effects of aspect ratio and temperatures of the glass cover and water on different types of entropy generation, including frictional, diffusive, and thermal. Results showed that an increase in glass and water temperatures led to higher entropy generation, and stills with higher aspect ratios produced more entropy. The Nu results were compared with those from Rahbar et al. [108], with a maximum error of 1.87 %. Table 4 shows the various computational studies with developed CFD codes.

Conclusion

This work is an attempt to understand the factors which effects the performance of solar still. The most crucial parameters of solar still are radiation, climate and deign of still. After experiments and analysis, a direct relation between output and conditions like solar radiation, wind velocity and ambient temperature can be established. Also, the latitude of the place decides the face of glass cover, that is towards south when still is in northern hemisphere and vice versa. So, it can be concluded that orientation of the glass cover is decided by the latitude of that place. Now, the inclination of the cover should be such that the rate of condensation should match the exit of the condensate form the still without getting dropped back in the basin. Thus, the output depends on solar radiation, ambient temperature, cover material and it's wetting property. During experiments it was also found that productivity increase when the cover temperature is lowered. For this an intermittent stream of water is flowed on the glass cover which, replicates short duration of rainfall. We also did conclude the initial temperature of basin water governs the depth of the basin water. During daytime increasing basin depth during daytime increases productivity while the situation is vice versa during night. Productivity also depends on thermal conductivity and thickness of glass cover. Lower the thickness higher the productivity. About inclination it was found, for winters large tilt angle and small inclination in summers. Heat absorbers with black painted basin increases the productivity by increasing solar absorptivity. As discussed, earlier distillate output for multi-basin still is more than conventional due temperature difference between glass cover and water. It was also found during experiment that, introducing heat storage material increases productivity.
