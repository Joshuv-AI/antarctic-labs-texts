The impact of obesity and overweight on maternal and fetal health with conservative preconception interventions: a structured narrative review
Authors: Szczerba Mateusz, Hejnosz Aleksandra, Falecka Katarzyna, Urban Aleksandra, Kacperczyk-Bartnik Joanna, Romejko-Wolniewicz Ewa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeOverweight and obesity before pregnancy are associated with adverse maternal, pregnancy-related, and longer-term health outcomes. This review aimed to summarize recent evidence on preconception overweight and obesity and to synthesize findings on conservative preconception or interpregnancy interventions, including lifestyle, dietary, behavioral, weight-management, and supplementation-based strategies.MethodsA structured narrative review with a predefined database search was conducted. PubMed and Embase were searched for studies published between January 2019 and December 2023 using the search string ((“obesity” OR “overweight”) AND “preconception”). Due to heterogeneity in study design, populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and follow-up durations, findings were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively. Thirteen studies were included: 11 intervention studies and 2 observational cohort studies retained as contextual evidence.ResultsObservational evidence associated higher preconception BMI with adverse pregnancy-related outcomes, including labor induction, cesarean delivery, preterm birth, neonatal care admission, stillbirth, and post-delivery dysglycemia. Conservative interventions showed heterogeneous effects. Preconception weight loss was associated with selected fertility-related and glucose-related benefits in some studies, including shorter time to pregnancy after a very-low-energy diet intervention and lower odds of recurrent gestational diabetes mellitus when meaningful weight loss was achieved. Supplementation-based interventions showed inconsistent findings across BMI subgroups. Safety-related outcomes varied between studies, and long-term follow-up suggested that sustained benefits may depend on successful weight loss and maintenance of behavioral change.ConclusionsConservative preconception interventions may provide selected benefits for women with overweight or obesity, particularly when clinically meaningful weight loss is achieved. However, current evidence remains heterogeneous and does not define a single optimal intervention model, timing, or universal weight-loss target.

Introduction

Obesity and overweight pose significant global health challenges. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of overweight and obese individuals nearly tripled between 1975 and 2016 [1]. Pregnant individuals are typically categorized as obese or non-obese based on their pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), with a threshold of BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [2].

Pregnancies among women with obesity are associated with a higher risk of potentially life-threatening complications and increased mortality compared with pregnancies in women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI [3]. This is particularly concerning as the population of obese women giving birth is increasing [4, 5].

The pathophysiological impact of increased adiposity on pregnancy, childbirth, and subsequent maternal and offspring outcomes is multifactorial. Evidence suggests obesity may influence reproductive and pregnancy-related outcomes through metabolic, endocrine, inflammatory, vascular, and placental pathways, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, low-grade chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, altered placental metabolism, and impaired nutrient transport [4, 6–9].

Previous reviews have addressed maternal obesity in pregnancy and preconception weight-loss interventions, often focusing on broad pregnancy-related risks, fertility outcomes, assisted reproduction, or weight reduction as the main endpoint [10–13]. Limited attention was given to integrating evidence on conservative preconception interventions with determinants of intervention effectiveness, safety-related pregnancy and neonatal outcomes.

Bariatric surgery is an established treatment for obesity with recognized implications for subsequent pregnancy. However, this review focuses on conservative preconception strategies, including lifestyle, dietary, behavioral, weight-management, and selected supplementation-based interventions.

This structured review aims to summarize recent evidence on the associations of preconception overweight and obesity with maternal and pregnancy-related outcomes, and to synthesize available evidence on conservative preconception interventions.

Materials and methods

This article was designed as a structured narrative review with a predefined database search. We focused on recent evidence concerning preconception overweight and obesity, pregnancy-related maternal outcomes, and conservative preconception or interpregnancy interventions. Because of substantial heterogeneity in study design, populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and follow-up duration, findings were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively. The study-selection process was documented using a PRISMA 2020-based flow diagram to improve transparency [14].

Search strategy

A search strategy was constructed using the string (("obesity" OR "overweight") AND "preconception"). The search was conducted in January 2024 in the PubMed and Embase databases, limited to studies published between January 2019 and December 2023. The search primarily targeted English-language clinical trials and randomized controlled trials. During full-text assessment, two observational cohort studies were additionally retained. The search yielded 100 records, including 39 from PubMed and 61 from Embase. Titles and abstracts were screened, and eligible articles proceeded to full-text review, data extraction, and quality assessment.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were structured according to population, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcomes, and study design, and adapted to the structured narrative design of this review. The review focused on women with overweight or obesity in the preconception setting. Postnatal or interpregnancy studies were eligible only when directly relevant to preparation for a subsequent pregnancy or to longer-term maternal outcomes after a preconception intervention.

Eligible intervention studies assessed conservative strategies implemented before conception, including lifestyle, dietary, behavioral, weight-management, or supplementation-based interventions. Observational studies were considered only as contextual evidence on associations between preconception overweight or obesity and maternal or pregnancy-related outcomes. Eligible comparators included usual care, no intervention, placebo, alternative conservative interventions, or normal-weight reference groups, depending on study design.

Outcomes of interest included reproductive, maternal, pregnancy-related, postpartum, neonatal, and longer-term maternal health outcomes. Controlled intervention studies and randomized controlled trials were prioritized. Observational cohort studies were retained only as contextual evidence and were not synthesized together with intervention trials as a single pool of evidence.

Studies were excluded if they focused solely on offspring outcomes without relevant maternal or pregnancy-related data, addressed preconception nutritional status without an intervention or relevant maternal outcome, evaluated interventions implemented exclusively during pregnancy, or did not report original results. Bariatric surgery and anti-obesity pharmacotherapy as standalone interventions were outside the primary eligibility scope because they represent surgical or pharmacological obesity treatments rather than conservative preconception interventions. These approaches were considered only as contextual issues in the Discussion.

Study selection and data extraction

After duplicate removal, records were screened sequentially by title and abstract, followed by full-text eligibility assessment. Two authors independently assessed records and full-text reports according to the eligibility criteria and predefined scope. Disagreements were resolved through discussion among the authors; if consensus could not be reached, the final decision was made by the study supervisor.

Although the initial search prioritized clinical trials and randomized controlled trials, two observational cohort studies [15, 16] were retained as clinically relevant contextual evidence. These studies were not combined with intervention trials and were discussed separately within the narrative synthesis. This deviation from the initial trial-focused scope is acknowledged in the limitations section.

Extracted data included author, publication year, study design, population characteristics, BMI category, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcomes, follow-up duration, effect estimates where reported, and main findings. Data extraction was performed by two authors and reviewed by a third author.

Critical appraisal tool and risk of bias assessment

Study quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by two review authors using National Institutes of Health (NIH) tools. Eleven intervention studies were assessed with the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Controlled Intervention Studies, and two observational cohort studies were assessed with the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-sectional Studies [17]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion among all authors.

Narrative synthesis approach

Because of heterogeneity in study design, populations, interventions, comparators, outcome definitions, effect measures, and follow-up duration, quantitative pooling was not performed. Findings were synthesized narratively and grouped by outcome domain: maternal and pregnancy-related risks associated with preconception overweight or obesity, determinants of successful lifestyle change, supplementation-based interventions, fertility-related outcomes, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and antenatal outcomes, neonatal outcomes, and long-term maternal cardiometabolic outcomes.

Findings were reported separately for overweight and obesity categories whenever the original studies provided stratified results. When studies reported combined BMI categories or did not provide stratified estimates, results were described according to the original grouping. Controlled intervention trials and observational cohort studies were interpreted separately. Reported effect estimates, including relative risks, odds ratios, hazard ratios, confidence intervals, and p-values, were extracted where available. The synthesis focused on the direction, consistency, and clinical relevance of findings rather than pooled effect sizes. Quality appraisal informed interpretation, and findings from studies rated as fair were interpreted with particular caution.

Results

Study selection

The database search identified 100 records, including 39 from PubMed and 61 from Embase. After removal of 24 duplicate records, 76 records underwent title screening. Of these, 11 records were excluded after title screening, and 65 records proceeded to abstract screening. After abstract screening, 27 records were excluded, leaving 38 reports for full-text eligibility assessment. During full-text assessment, 25 reports were excluded for predefined reasons: study protocol only (n = 10), related only to offspring outcomes (n = 7), no relevant connection with conception or preconception care (n = 5), preconception nutritional status without intervention (n = 2), and intervention implemented exclusively during pregnancy (n = 1). Finally, 13 studies were included in the narrative synthesis. Maternal overweight and obesity, pregnancy-related outcomes, postpartum dysglycemia, and mental well-being

Akselsson et al. analyzed pregnancies among 64,632 women, categorized into five groups according to BMI [15]. Women classified as overweight, obese, or severely obese had a higher frequency of concurrent health conditions compared with women with normal weight, with a dose–response pattern across increasing BMI categories. Summary of main findings and implications across included studies

The synthesis of findings across the 13 included studies highlights both significant intervention-related effects and neutral or descriptive outcomes. Key significant results included improved fertility measures following VLED, lower postprandial glucose levels and fewer adverse pregnancy outcomes, reduced recurrence of GDM associated with achieved weight loss, and lower long-term energy intake after lifestyle intervention. Overall, the findings were heterogeneous and should be interpreted at the level of individual studies rather than as evidence supporting a single universal intervention approach.

Women categorized as obese or severely obese had a higher likelihood of labor induction and cesarean delivery, including both pre-labor and in-labor cesarean delivery (p < 0.001). Instrumental delivery was also more frequent in women with higher BMI categories (RR 1.26; 95% CI: 1.21–1.31; p < 0.001). Higher BMI was associated with a higher risk of preterm delivery before 37 completed gestational weeks (p < 0.001), transfer to neonatal care (RR 1.38; 95% CI: 1.26–1.50), and Apgar score < 7 at 5 min (RR 1.54; 95% CI: 1.24–1.90) compared with normal weight. A significant association was also reported between higher BMI and reduced spontaneous onset of labor (OR 1.27; 95% CI: 1.13–1.43; p < 0.001), and stillbirth risk was higher among women with overweight or obesity from 40 weeks of gestation [15].

Chen et al. used data from the GUSTO mother–offspring cohort, in which 1,450 participants were recruited; analyses of post-delivery dysglycemia were performed in 692 women with available antenatal and post-delivery OGTT data [16]. The study evaluated the combined contribution of GDM, pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, excessive gestational weight gain, and substantial post-delivery weight retention to future dysglycemia. GDM, pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, and substantial post-delivery weight retention each independently increased the risk of post-delivery dysglycemia. GDM alone was associated with a similar risk as the combined presence of pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity and post-delivery weight retention without GDM [16].

Women with GDM who were overweight or obese before pregnancy and retained ≥ 5 kg postpartum had a 2.38-fold higher risk of dysglycemia than lean women with GDM and no significant weight retention (95% CI: 1.29–4.41). Overall, GDM was associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes (RR 12.07; 95% CI: 4.55–32.02) and dysglycemia (RR 3.02; 95% CI: 2.19–4.16) compared with women without GDM. Within 4–6 years after delivery, 43.4% of women with GDM developed dysglycemia. Compared with women without GDM, pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, or substantial post-delivery weight retention, women with all three risk factors had a 10.64-fold higher risk of dysglycemia at 4–6 years post-delivery (95% CI: 5.02–22.58). Excessive gestational weight gain did not show consistent associations with post-delivery dysglycemia in this cohort [16].

A secondary analysis of the INTER-ACT randomized controlled trial evaluated mental health during the interpregnancy period among 276 women with a subsequent pregnancy and available mental health measurements at 6 weeks postpartum and at the start of the next pregnancy [18]. Women with pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 more frequently reported symptoms of anxiety and depression at the onset of the subsequent pregnancy. Anxiety rates increased by 13% (p ≤ 0.001), and depressive symptoms increased from 8 to 17% (p = 0.01). Among women without anxiety at 6 weeks postpartum, 39% reported anxiety by the next pregnancy, whereas 67% of women who were already anxious postpartum remained anxious at the beginning of the subsequent pregnancy. Pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 was associated with a higher frequency of anxiety and depressive symptoms at the start of the subsequent pregnancy [18].

Factors associated with lifestyle intervention effectiveness

Karsten et al. evaluated determinants of successful lifestyle change during a 6-month preconception lifestyle intervention in women with obesity and infertility [19]. Factors associated with successful program completion or weight-related behavioral change included higher external eating behavior (OR 1.10; 95% CI: 1.05–1.16), higher levels of self-efficacy (p < 0.01), and a higher stage of change toward physical activity or weight loss [19]. A high stage of change toward physical activity was associated with more daily steps (p = 0.03), while a high stage of change toward weight loss was associated with completion of the intervention (p = 0.04) [19]. Previous dietary support was associated with smaller weight loss during the intervention period, with women who had received prior dietetic support losing 0.94 kg less body weight (95% CI: 0.01–1.87 kg) [19]. Partner’s higher age was associated with higher energy intake, estimated as an increase of 6 kcal per year of partner age (95% CI: 3–13) [19].

A secondary analysis of socio-demographic data from the original GLOWING randomized trial evaluated postnatal diet, physical activity, and weight-related patterns among women with obesity from a highly deprived population in the UK [20]. The analysis focused on women of low socioeconomic status with obesity, defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 in the first trimester of pregnancy [20]. Postnatal diet and physical activity behaviors in this population did not meet guideline recommendations [20]. The study reported that strategies aimed at limiting excessive gestational weight gain and promoting early postnatal weight loss were linked to the possibility of achieving a postnatal weight lower than first-trimester gestational weight [20]. Another included study reported socioeconomic status among baseline participant characteristics but found no association between socioeconomic status and the effectiveness of the lifestyle program; detailed results for this factor were not presented [19].

Supplementation-based preconception interventions

In the Calcium and Preeclampsia trial, women were randomized before pregnancy to receive either 500 mg of elemental calcium or placebo until 20 weeks of gestation, after which all participants received 1.5 g of calcium until delivery [21]. Among women with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), weight gain was numerically lower in the calcium group at 8 weeks (1.0 kg; 95% CI: − 3.2–1.2; p = 0.330) and 32 weeks (2.1 kg; 95% CI: − 5.6–1.3; p = 0.225) compared with placebo [21]. Among women with overweight (BMI ≥ 25 to < 30 kg/m2), weight gain was also numerically lower in the calcium group at 8 weeks (0.9 kg; 95% CI: − 2.5–0.7; p = 0.261) and 32 weeks (0.1 kg; 95% CI: − 2.6–2.4; p = 0.936) compared with placebo. None of these differences reached statistical significance [21].

Another study evaluated preconception supplementation with myo-inositol, probiotics, and micronutrients, including vitamins B2, B6, B12, D, and zinc, compared with control supplementation containing folic acid, iron, calcium, iodine, and β-carotene [22]. Among women with overweight, the intervention group had a shorter time to conception than the control group, regardless of ethnicity (12-month HR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.07–2.02; p = 0.016; 20% conceived by 84.5 vs. 117.0 days). In contrast, among women with obesity, time to conception was longer in the intervention group than in controls (12-month HR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.47–1.00; p = 0.053; 20% conceived by 132.7 vs. 108.5 days). CRP showed a lower trend in the overweight intervention group compared with the overweight control group (mean difference − 0.12 SD; 95% CI: − 0.26–0.01; p = 0.078), whereas no difference was observed in the obese group (mean difference − 0.032 SD; 95% CI: − 0.14–0.07; p = 0.55). Overall, time to natural conception and clinical pregnancy rates within 12 months were similar between the intervention and control groups, with 20% achieving natural conception by 90.5 days (95% CI: 80.7–103.5) versus 92.0 days (95% CI: 76.0–105.1), respectively [22].

Fertility-related outcomes of lifestyle and weight-loss interventions

The FIT-PLESE randomized controlled trial evaluated a 16 week lifestyle intervention in women with obesity and infertility [23]. The intervention included meal replacements, physical activity, and orlistat and resulted in an average weight loss of approximately 7% of body mass. The control group received physical activity intervention only. No significant differences were observed between groups in live birth rates (p = 0.672), multiple pregnancies (p = 0.319), or time to pregnancy (p = 0.564), although selected cardiometabolic indicators improved in the intervention group [23].

Price et al. evaluated a very-low-energy diet (VLED) intervention and compared it with a standard diet intervention (SDI) in women with obesity [24]. The VLED group achieved greater weight loss than the SDI group (11.9% vs. 3.1%). The VLED intervention was associated with a higher pregnancy rate (p = 0.03) and a higher rate of spontaneous pregnancies (p = 0.03), whereas the rate of assisted pregnancies did not differ between groups (p = 0.99). Median time to pregnancy was shorter in the VLED group than in the SDI group (51.0 days [Q1–Q3: 32.0–169.0] vs. 140.5 days [Q1–Q3: 75.3–211.0]), particularly within the first 90 days after the intervention [24].

Pregnancy, neonatal, and safety-related outcomes

In the FIT-PLESE study, pregnancy and neonatal complications were assessed as safety-related outcomes [23]. Pregnancy loss was more frequent in the intervention group than in the control group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.087) [23]. Maternal adverse events, including ectopic pregnancy, preterm labor, preeclampsia, GDM, premature rupture of membranes, postpartum infection, and postpartum hemorrhage, were also reported more frequently after the intervention, but these differences were not statistically significant [23]. Among neonatal outcomes, newborn hospitalization and intrauterine growth restriction were more frequent in the intervention group, whereas myelomeningocele occurred less frequently than in the control group [23]. Other included studies did not report significant differences in pregnancy loss [25, 26]. In contrast to FIT-PLESE, Price et al. reported a lower overall composite of adverse pregnancy outcomes [26].

GDM and glucose-related outcomes

Price et al. evaluated the effect of a VLED compared with a standard diet intervention (SDI) on glucose-related pregnancy outcomes in women with obesity [26]. The VLED intervention did not significantly affect fasting glucose levels at 26–28 weeks of pregnancy [26]. However, 2-h postprandial glucose levels were significantly lower in the VLED group than in the SDI group (p = 0.04) [26].

Phelan et al. assessed a prepregnancy lifestyle intervention aimed at reducing recurrence of GDM [25]. The study reported that the effect on GDM recurrence differed according to achieved weight loss. Independent of intervention group, greater prepregnancy weight loss was associated with a 21% reduction in the odds of GDM recurrence (OR 0.79; p = 0.008). Weight loss of ≥ 5% before conception reduced the odds of GDM recurrence by up to 82% (p = 0.03). Fasting glucose levels were similar between groups in the same study [25]. In the long-term follow-up study by Wekker et al., reduced fasting glucose levels at 6 years were observed only among women who lost weight during the intervention period [27].

Long-term maternal outcomes after preconception lifestyle interventions.

In the 5.5 year follow-up study by Van Elten et al., preconception lifestyle intervention was associated with a statistically significant reduction in mean daily energy intake compared with the control group (− 216 kcal/day; p = 0.04) [28]. No significant differences were observed between groups in dietary composition or physical activity outcomes. The reduction in energy intake was independent of whether weight loss had been achieved during the intervention period [28].

In the long-term follow-up reported by Wekker et al., women who lost weight during the intervention period had a lower BMI than women who did not lose weight despite the intervention and women in the control group [27]. Weight reduction, defined as loss of ≥ 5% of body weight or achievement of BMI < 29 kg/m2, was associated with more favorable cardiometabolic outcomes after 6 years, including lower body weight, smaller waist circumference, lower HbA1c, lower fasting glucose, and higher HDL-C [27].

The synthesis of findings across the 13 included studies highlights both significant intervention-related effects and neutral or descriptive outcomes. Key significant results included improved fertility measures following VLED, lower postprandial glucose levels and fewer adverse pregnancy outcomes, reduced recurrence of GDM associated with achieved weight loss, and lower long-term energy intake after lifestyle intervention. Overall, the findings were heterogeneous and should be interpreted at the level of individual studies rather than as evidence supporting a single universal intervention approach.

Discussion

Principal findings

This narrative review summarized recent evidence on conservative preconception interventions in women with overweight or obesity, together with contextual evidence on maternal and pregnancy-related outcomes associated with preconception weight status. The included studies addressed three main areas: maternal and pregnancy-related consequences of preconception overweight or obesity, determinants of successful preconception weight reduction, and the effects of conservative preconception interventions on fertility, pregnancy-related outcomes, and longer-term maternal health.

One cohort study reported associations between higher preconception BMI and adverse delivery or perinatal outcomes, including delivery complications and stillbirth [15]. Another cohort study reported an association between preconception risk factors, including overweight or obesity, and post-delivery dysglycemia [16]. One included study also reported associations between higher pre-pregnancy BMI and mental health-related outcomes, including symptoms of anxiety and depression during the interpregnancy period or at the beginning of a subsequent pregnancy [18]. These findings support the clinical relevance of addressing overweight and obesity before conception, but they should not be interpreted as evidence that conservative interventions directly reduce all these risks.

The associations observed in the included cohort studies are consistent with broader evidence linking higher maternal BMI before or in early pregnancy with adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Aune et al. reported that increasing maternal BMI was associated with higher risks of fetal death, stillbirth, neonatal death, perinatal death, and infant death [22]. Marchi et al. also reported that maternal obesity was associated with higher risks of GDM, pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, depression, instrumental and caesarean birth, surgical site infection, preterm birth, large-for-gestational-age infants, congenital anomalies, and perinatal death [10]. These broader findings support the clinical relevance of the outcomes described in the included observational studies, while reinforcing the need to distinguish observational associations from intervention effects.

The findings from Akselsson et al. are also consistent with previous evidence linking higher pre-pregnancy BMI with longer gestation and a higher likelihood of delivery at or beyond 40, 41, and 42 weeks of gestation [29]. However, these data should be considered observational and should not be interpreted as evidence that any specific obstetric intervention directly reduces the excess risk associated with higher BMI.

The findings from the GUSTO cohort are consistent with broader literature showing that GDM is associated with later impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance, and type 2 diabetes after delivery [30]. Schummers et al. reported that lower preconception BMI was associated with lower risks of GDM and other adverse pregnancy outcomes, supporting the clinical relevance of weight management before pregnancy [31].

The biological plausibility of these associations is supported by evidence that obesity may affect reproductive and pregnancy-related outcomes through endocrine, metabolic, inflammatory, and placental pathways. Obesity has been linked to impaired female fertility through mechanisms including insulin resistance, endocrine dysregulation, menstrual dysfunction, anovulation, altered oocyte quality, and impaired endometrial receptivity [7]. In pregnancy, maternal obesity and GDM have also been associated with changes in maternal, fetal, and placental inflammatory profiles [8]. Placental dysfunction has also been proposed as one pathway linking maternal obesity with adverse fetal and neonatal outcomes, including through altered placental metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, and nutrient transport [9]. These mechanisms may partly explain why obesity before conception is associated with a broad range of maternal, fertility-related, and perinatal outcomes.

The mental health findings from INTER-ACT should also be interpreted within the broader literature on pre-pregnancy BMI and perinatal psychological outcomes. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses reported that women with overweight or obesity had higher odds of depressive symptoms during pregnancy and the postpartum period than women with normal weight, whereas evidence for anxiety symptoms was less consistent [32, 33]. This supports the interpretation that the relationship between obesity and maternal health is multidimensional and may include both physical and psychological outcomes.

Determinants and timing of intervention effectiveness

The effectiveness of lifestyle interventions appeared to depend on several behavioral and contextual factors. External eating behavior, self-efficacy, readiness to change, previous dietary support, and partner-related factors were associated with intervention completion or weight-related behavioral outcomes [19]. Socio-demographic and postnatal factors were also examined in the GLOWING analysis, among women with obesity from highly deprived populations [20]. These findings suggest that preconception lifestyle interventions may need to be tailored according to behavioral profile, socioeconomic context, and baseline motivation.

The behavioral determinants may be interpreted through established behavior-change theory. The Behaviour Change Wheel and COM-B model propose that behavior change depends on capability, opportunity, and motivation [34]. This may explain why the effectiveness of preconception lifestyle interventions differs according to self-efficacy, readiness to change, social support, previous unsuccessful dietary support, and socioeconomic context. In this sense, interventions based only on general lifestyle advice may be insufficient for some women, particularly when structural or behavioral barriers are present.

The preconception period was the primary focus of this review, some included studies addressed the postnatal or interpregnancy period when it was relevant to preparation for a subsequent pregnancy or to longer-term maternal outcomes after a preconception intervention [20, 25, 27, 28]. This suggests that weight management may need to extend beyond the immediate preconception window. Pregnancy and the postnatal period have previously been described as potential “teachable moments” for weight control and obesity prevention, because women may have contact with healthcare professionals and increased motivation related to maternal and child health [35]. Broader literature also supports the need to consider preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum periods as connected phases of maternal weight management [36]. However, in the present review, the postnatal and interpregnancy period should be considered complementary to, rather than a replacement for, preconception care. The available evidence does not define the optimal timing, duration, or intensity of such interventions.

Supplementation and lifestyle intervention effects

Supplementation-based interventions showed inconsistent findings. Calcium supplementation before and during early pregnancy was associated with numerically lower weight gain among women with overweight or obesity, but these differences were not statistically significant [21]. Supplementation with myo-inositol, probiotics, and micronutrients showed different effects across BMI subgroups, with a shorter time to conception among women with overweight, whereas this pattern was not observed among women with obesity [22]. These findings do not allow firm conclusions regarding the effectiveness of supplementation-based preconception strategies.

Lifestyle and weight-loss interventions also produced heterogeneous results. FIT-PLESE did not show significant differences in live birth, multiple pregnancies, or time to pregnancy despite weight loss and improved selected cardiometabolic indicators [23]. In contrast, the VLED intervention reported greater weight loss, higher pregnancy and spontaneous pregnancy rates, and a shorter median time to pregnancy [24]. In studies addressing GDM recurrence and long-term metabolic outcomes, achieved weight loss appeared to be more closely associated with favorable outcomes than intervention allocation alone [25, 27, 28]. Overall, the findings suggest that achieved weight loss may be an important marker of intervention effectiveness, although the available evidence remains insufficient to define a universal target or intervention model.

The heterogeneity observed across the included intervention studies is consistent with earlier systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Lan et al. reported that preconception lifestyle interventions were associated with weight loss and increased natural pregnancy rate, but not with improved live birth or birth weight [11]. Best et al. similarly concluded that evidence for weight-loss interventions improving fertility outcomes in women and men with overweight or obesity was mixed and limited by heterogeneity [12]. A later update of randomized trial evidence suggested that combined diet and exercise interventions may improve pregnancy and live birth rates compared with no or minimal intervention, although this effect was not observed when controls had immediate access to assisted reproductive technology, and the evidence was limited [13]. Together, these findings suggest that intervention intensity, achieved weight loss, adherence, infertility phenotype, and population characteristics may be more informative than intervention allocation alone.

Safety considerations and clinical implications

Safety-related outcomes were reported inconsistently across the included studies. The FIT-PLESE study reported non-significant increases in pregnancy loss and selected maternal or neonatal adverse events in the intervention group [23]. Other included studies did not report significant differences in pregnancy loss or reported lower overall adverse event rates in the intervention group [25, 26]. Therefore, the available evidence does not show a consistent statistically significant increase in pregnancy or neonatal complications after conservative preconception interventions, but it also does not establish a definitive safety profile for all intervention types.

From a clinical perspective, the findings support the relevance of preconception counseling and individualized weight-management strategies for women with overweight or obesity. These findings are consistent with current clinical guidance emphasizing preconception counseling, individualized weight-management strategies, optimization of modifiable risk factors before pregnancy, and careful assessment of maternal and fetal safety in women with overweight or obesity. ACOG [37] and FIGO [38] guidance supports the clinical relevance of addressing obesity before and during pregnancy, whereas ESHRE [39] guidance in reproductive medicine highlights the importance of BMI optimization in selected fertility-related contexts, including recurrent pregnancy loss. However, the heterogeneity of available intervention studies means that current evidence does not define a single optimal conservative preconception intervention model or universally applicable weight-loss target.

Conservative interventions may offer selected benefits, particularly when meaningful weight loss is achieved. However, the heterogeneity of study designs, interventions, populations, outcomes, and follow-up periods prevents firm conclusions regarding the most effective intervention type, intensity, timing, or target population. The available evidence also indicates that future interventions should not focus only on weight loss as a numerical target, but should consider behavioral, socioeconomic, metabolic, and reproductive factors that may influence both adherence and clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. The search was limited to PubMed and Embase and used a focused search strategy, which may have missed relevant studies. Grey literature and trial registries were not searched. The included studies were heterogeneous in design, populations, interventions, comparators, outcome definitions, effect measures, and follow-up duration; therefore, quantitative pooling was not performed. No formal GRADE assessment of certainty of evidence was performed, which limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding the overall certainty of the findings. Two observational cohort studies were retained as contextual evidence despite the trial-focused eligibility scope. These studies were interpreted separately and were not synthesized together with intervention trials as a single pool of evidence. Several intervention studies were assessed as fair quality, which reduces confidence in the conclusions. Finally, this review focused on conservative preconception interventions; bariatric surgery and anti-obesity pharmacotherapy as standalone interventions were outside the primary eligibility scope.

Future directions

Future studies should use standardized outcome definitions, longer follow-up, and detailed reporting of achieved weight loss, adherence, adverse events, fertility outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, and long-term maternal cardiometabolic health. Future research should also distinguish more clearly between overweight and obesity categories, because the available evidence suggests that intervention effects may differ across BMI subgroups [21, 22]. Studies are needed to clarify which women benefit most from conservative preconception interventions and what degree of weight loss is clinically meaningful before pregnancy.

Future trials should also evaluate whether more intensive, individualized, and theory-informed interventions produce clinically meaningful improvements beyond weight loss alone. Although this review focused on conservative lifestyle-based interventions, the increasing use of anti-obesity pharmacotherapy, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, in women of reproductive age indicates that future research should clarify how pharmacological approaches may relate to preconception weight management. Available literature emphasized that evidence on anti-obesity medications in relation to human fertility, pregnancy, and breastfeeding remained limited, and that most such medications were not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding because of known or uncertain fetal and neonatal risks [40]. Therefore, pharmacological approaches should be evaluated separately from conservative preconception interventions, with careful assessment of reproductive safety, timing of discontinuation before conception, and pregnancy outcomes.

Conclusions

Conservative preconception interventions in women with overweight or obesity may be associated with selected fertility-related, glucose-related, and long-term cardiometabolic benefits, particularly when meaningful weight loss is achieved. However, the available evidence remains heterogeneous, and findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further studies are needed to define optimal intervention timing, intensity, target populations, clinically meaningful weight-loss thresholds, and safety outcomes before pregnancy.
