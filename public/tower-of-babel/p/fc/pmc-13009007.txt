Long-term supplementation of exogenous bile acids enhanced growth efficacy, hematology, non-specific immunity, and intestinal histomorphology, with significant liver dysfunction in Nile tilapia fingerlings
Authors: Elsayed AA Eldessouki, Gehad E Elshopakey, Shaymaa Rezk, Mona Elsayed, Ahmed EA Mostafa, Samia Elbahnaswy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 16-week feeding trial was conducted to examine the effects of dietary exogenous bile acids (BAs) on growth performance, digestive enzyme activities, biochemical indices, antioxidant capacity, non-specific immune response, gene expression of proinflammatory cytokines, and liver and intestinal morphology in Nile tilapia (initial weight, 5.10 ± 0.22 g) reared in earthen ponds over an extended period. Two isonitrogenous (31.65% protein) and isolipidic (4.71%) experimental diets were formulated by adding a control diet (BA0) and 0.25 g bile acid per kg diet (BA0.25), respectively. The diets were randomly assigned to 6 hapas, each stocked with 20 fish and fed for 16 weeks. Supplementing with BA0.25 significantly enhanced growth metrics and intestinal digestive enzyme activities compared to BA0. Hematological analysis showed increased levels of white blood cells (WBC), lymphocytes, and neutrophils in the BA0.25 group compared to the control. Additionally, respiratory burst activity, phagocytic activity, and serum lysozyme activity were notably higher in fish fed BAs at 0.25 g kg− 1. Antioxidant parameters (SOD, CAT, GSH) were elevated, and the expression of the proinflammatory cytokine IL1β was downregulated in intestinal tissue from the BAs-supplemented group. Significant improvements in intestinal histomorphometry were observed in the BAs-supplemented group. BAs supplementation altered intestinal microbial composition, increasing total and Pseudomonas counts while reducing coliform levels. Interestingly, glucose, triglycerides, cholesterol, serum hepatic markers (ALT, AST, and ALP), hepatic oxidative MDA levels, and proinflammatory IL1β mRNA expression increased markedly in the supplemented-BAs group, while activity of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GSH) and HSP70 mRNA expression were notably diminished. Moreover, significant alterations were shown in the hepatic tissue of the BAs-supplemented group. Consequently, dietary supplementation with bile acids for a prolonged period improves growth, intestinal health, and immunity; however, it harms the liver health of Nile tilapia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11259-026-11129-z.Keywords: Nile tilapia, Bile acids, Growth, Physiological indices, Inflammatory gene expression, Intestinal count, Tissue histomorphology

Introduction

Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) is one of the most historically cultured species, ranking third among freshwater fish worldwide. Its production reached 4407.2 thousand tons, about 9% of the total volume (Kwikiriza et al. 2025; Nunes et al. 2024). The economic benefits of tilapia are mainly due to its efficient feed conversion and growth, good meat quality, higher yields, and competitive market price, along with its ability to tolerate a wide range of environmental stressors (Marzouk et al. 2025). These advantages make Nile tilapia a valuable aquaculture species, particularly in Egypt (Khanjani et al. 2022; Prabu et al. 2019).

Feeding expenses represent a vital part of the production sector, making up at least 50% of variable aquafeed costs linked to protein ingredients. As a result, most producers opt for more affordable plant-based proteins to lessen the financial challenges caused by rising feedstock prices (El-Ouny et al. 2023). However, long-term reliance on plant protein sources has led to several negative impacts on growth and fish health (Ren et al. 2020; Romano et al. 2020). To address these burdens, adding functional feed additives to fish diets can improve growth performance, immunity, and overall health of farmed species.

Bile acids (BAs), amphipathic sterol compounds, are the main functional components of bile secretions in the gallbladder, which empties its content into the intestine after contraction upon receiving hormonal cholecystokinin when chyme enters (Molinaro et al. 2018). They are synthesized from the oxidation of cholesterol in the liver, playing crucial roles in the digestion and absorption of dietary lipids in the intestine by emulsifying fats and increasing the activity of bile salt-activated lipase in several fish species (Kortner et al. 2013; Zhou et al. 2018). Moreover, BAs have been reported to enhance glucose absorption, regulate cholesterol levels, and modulate immunity in aquatic species (Fu et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022; Xie et al. 2021). Consequently, BAs have been approved as feed additives for aquaculture species in China since 2014 by the Ministry of Agriculture (Wang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2022).

Recently, the beneficial effects of dietary BAs on the productivity and intestinal health of aquaculture species have been studied, including grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) (Zhou et al. 2018), Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) (Jiang et al. 2018), large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea) (Ding et al. 2020), largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) (Guo et al. 2020), and black seabream (Acanthopagrus schlegelii) (Jin et al. 2019). New studies have shown the remarkable role of dietary BAs supplementation in enhancing immune function, as indicated by improvements in lysozyme activity, respiratory burst, alternative complement activities, and immunoglobulin levels (Abdel-Tawwab et al. 2023; Adam et al. 2023; Marzouk et al. 2025). However, fish species were adversely affected by high dietary bile acids (60–500 mg/kg diet), as indicated by increased inflammatory responses in the liver and elevated serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Chen et al. 2023; Su et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2021). Meanwhile, the long-term effects of dietary bile acids on fish in natural earthen ponds remain unreported, and it is entirely unknown whether bile acids can help maintain hepatic and intestinal functions, antioxidant defenses, and immune response. The balance between BAs benefits and toxicity may depend on tissue-specific receptor responses and exposure duration, creating potential for gut improvement alongside hepatotoxicity. Thus, our experiment was designed to explore how dietary BAs influence growth efficiency, activity of specific digestive enzymes, antioxidant capacity, immune response, the expression of proinflammatory gene markers, and the histological structure of the intestines and liver in O. niloticus kept in earthen ponds over a 16-week feeding trial.

Materials and methods

Experiment design and diet processing

The dietary components for the experiment were formulated as indicated by the requirements outlined in NRC (1993) (Table 1). Commercial BAs were purchased from a private company (Lachance, RUNEON, Shandong, China). Two isonitrogenous (31.65% protein) and isolipidic (4.71%) diets were designed: the control group was fed a basal formulated diet (BA0), and the BA0.25 group was supplemented with 0.25 g kg− 1 of diet. For diet preparation, all ingredients were ground together and mixed thoroughly for several minutes before adding sunflower oil. The final dough was stiffened with warm water and passed through a 3.0 mm grinder. The pellets were processed using a mill machine (Table 2).

Fish and rearing facilities

From a fish farm in Bahr El-Baqar, south of Port Said, Egypt, the experiment was conducted in earthen ponds containing hapas, where Nile tilapia was cultured. Ninety (90) apparently healthy Nile tilapia fingerlings, with an average initial body weight of 5.10 ± 0.22 g, were stocked in batches of 15 fish in 6 nylon net hapas (70 × 50 × 70 cm), with 3 replicate hapas per group. The physical distances between hapas are about 1 m to ensure adequate water flow and prevent waste buildup. The hapas were covered with mesh barriers. For adequate aeration, hapas were supplied with air stones connected to blower motors. Approximately 15% of the pond water was drained and replaced daily with fresh water.

The average fish weight and water parameters in each hapa were monitored once weekly. The fish in the hapas were hand-fed twice daily at 3% of their body weight for 16 weeks. A HANNA (HI 9829) multiparametric device was applied to check pH, temperature (°C), and dissolved oxygen (mg L− 1). Meanwhile, unionized ammonia (mg L− 1) and nitrates (mg L− 1) were examined and quantified with a DREL spectrophotometer 2000 (HACH Company, Loveland, CO, USA).

Fish growth assessment

Fish were given a 24 h fast after the end of the feeding experiment. The growth parameters were computed from six fish of each group after being weighed using the previously reported equations, according to Adam et al. (2023).

Sample collection

Five fish from each hapa were collected and then anesthetized with clove oil (7 mL L− 1) at the end of the experiment. To count and analyze the respiratory burst activity and phagocytic index, blood was drawn from the caudal veins of fish and placed in Eppendorf tubes with tripotassium EDTA. Subsequently, additional blood samples (from five fish per aquarium) were collected and allowed to coagulate for two hours. The recovered plasma and serum were stored in a refrigerator after centrifugation at 3500×g for 15 min at 4 °C for serum biochemical parameters analysis. A cold solution of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.5) was used to homogenize the fish after parts of its liver and intestine were removed and washed three times with a cold 0.9% NaCl solution. For assessing antioxidants, oxidative stress molecules, and digestive enzymes activities, the homogenates were carefully collected in a sterile tube and centrifuged for approximately fifteen minutes at 3500 ×g (Fernandez-Botran et al. 2002). The protein level in the homogenate was determined using the methods of Bradford (1976). Another intestine and liver samples were dissolved in RNA later (Sigma Aldrich, USA) and kept at − 20 ◦C for examining the mRNA expression levels. The same tissues were dissected for putting in 10% buffered formalin for monitoring histological traits.

Total and differential blood cell count

After dilution in Natt-Herrick’s solution, erythrocytes (RBCs) and leukocytes (WBCs) were manually counted (Hrubec et al. 1996). Additionally, the methods of Drabkin (1946) and Grant (1987) were used to analyze hemoglobin (Hb) and packed cell volume (PCV). Wright’s Giemsa stain was also applied to blood films to determine the differential leukocytic count (Hrubec et al. 1996).

Serum biochemical parameters

Using a spectrophotometer (Lambda EZ201; Perkin Elmer) and fish specific kits, the aspartate aminotransferase (AST, REF; 20764949322; Roche Cobas Co., Indianapolis, USA), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, REF; 20764957322; Roche Cobas Co., Indianapolis, USA), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP, catalog No.; MBS033204; MyBioSource Co., California, USA), besides total protein (catalog No.; SB-0250–500; Stanbio Laboratory Co.,), albumin (catalog No.; SB- 028–500; Stanbio Laboratory Co.,) (California, USA), urea (catalog No.; URE118200) (BioMed Co., Cairo, Egypt), creatinine (catalog No.; 10051) (Human Co., Germany) and uric acid (catalog No.; MD41001) (Spinreact Co., Santa Coloma, Spain) were evaluated by the standard procedure of their pamphlets. Moreover, test kits from Spinreact Co. (Santa Coloma, Spain), Biotrend Co. (Maryland, USA), and Hendox LTD. (Brentford, England) were used to analyze glucose (REF; 1001192), cholesterol (Catalog No.: AE63340FI), and triglycerides (Catalog No.: AE63342FI-4800).

Immune markers

Respiratory burst activity using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) reduction assay was used to determine how many oxygen radicals were produced by phagocytes (Abdelhamid et al. 2020). After equally diluting NBT (1 mg/ml) with whole blood, it was incubated for 30 min at 25 °C. 1.0 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide solution was added, and the NBT-blood cell suspension was centrifuged for five minutes at 3000 ×g. Using a spectrophotometer (5010, Photometer, BM Co., Germany), the absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm was measured.

With a few adjustments, Elbahnaswy and Elshopakey (2020) published procedures that took advantage of phagocytic activity. In summary, the blood was mixed with latex beads (107 beads mL− 1; 1.094 L; Poly Sciences, USA) and allowed to sit at room temperature for 30 min. The prepared smears were fixed with 95% ethanol, allowed to air dry, and then stained with Giemsa. Two hundred bead-consuming cells were observed under a microscope. (Phagocytic leucocytes/200) times 100 is the phagocytic activity percentage.

With minor adjustments, the lysis of Micrococcus lysodeikticus (Sigma Co., USA) served as the basis for measuring serum lysozyme activity in accordance with Ghareghanipoora et al. (2014). Five minutes were spent at 25 °C reacting with the serum and M. lysodeikticus solution (0.2 mg/mL in 0.05 M PBS, pH 6.2). After that, the optical density was measured at 540 nm for five minutes at one-minute intervals using a 5010 photometer (BM Co., Germany). A calibration curve produced with various dilutions of lyophilized chicken egg-white lysozyme (Sigma Co., USA) was used to determine the serum lysozyme concentration.

Activity of intestinal digestive enzymes

After being homogenized in a phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.5) (1:9), the middle portion of the intestinal tissues was centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C and 5000 revolutions per minute. Until they were prepared for additional examinations, the supernatants were sterilely assembled and kept at −20 °C. Triglyceride hydrolysis was used to assess lipase activity after Cherry and Crandall Jr (1932). By digesting 0.1 mL of crude enzyme extract with 2.0 mL of casein buffer substrate for 15 min at 28 °C and measuring the absorbance of the supernatant at 280 nm, protease activity was ascertained. Following Bernfeld (1955) approach, 1% starch solution in 0.1 M Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0) was used as a substrate to assess α-amylase activity. The enzyme activity was reported as U/mg protein.

Oxidant/antioxidant markers

Lipid peroxidation and enzyme activity were measured in hepatic and intestinal homogenates of Nile tilapia using commercial test kits from Bio-diagnostic Co., Cairo, Egypt. Ohkawa et al. (1979) stated that the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA) was tested to assess the degree of lipid peroxidation. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured by its capacity to stop the reduction of the nitroblue tetrazolium dye to formazan, which is mediated by phenazine methyl sulfate (PMS) (Sun et al. 1988). Additionally, the reduction in H2O2 concentration following the addition of 10 µL of serum to the kit reagent was used to measure catalase (CAT) activity (Aebi 1984). The glutathione (GSH) level was demonstrated using Ellman’s reagent, and the yellow chromogen that was created was detected at 412 nm (Gl 1959).

Estimation of intestinal count

Fish were randomly selected from each pond, collected in sterile plastic bags, and carried to the laboratory for further biological analysis. The fish were starved for 24 h before sampling, and the surface was disinfected with alcohol (70%). The fish were dissected under aseptic conditions, and the intestines were taken out, cut into small pieces, and homogenized in 10 ml tryptone soya broth by Bergey’s manual method (Bergey 1994). Counting was performed according to the plate count method, Tryptone soya agar for total bacterial count, Muller and Hinton for Pseudomonas count, MacConkey agar for coliform count, followed by 24h incubation at 37 °C to count the total colony of bacteria (Paludan-Müller et al. 1999).

Assessment of gene expression

Total RNA was manually extracted from 100 mg of liver and intestinal tissues of Nile tilapia fish immersed in one mL of GenzolTM (Geneaid Biotech Ltd., Taiwan) and crushed using a handheld homogenizer without DNase treatment. The resulting pellet was dissolved in TE buffer (pH 8.0) as previously described (Al-Wakeel et al. 2024). Then, agarose gel electrophoresis and an A260/A280 ratio check were used to assess the integrity and quantity of the extracted RNA. Complementary DNA (cDNA) containing 1 µg of total RNA was synthesized using the TOPscript™ RT DryMIX (dT18) cDNA Synthesis Kit (Enzynomics Co Ltd., Daejeon, Republic of Korea) following the protocol. Specific primers were used to amplify selected immune-related genes (IL1β, TNF, HSP70) of Nile tilapia (Elbahnaswy et al. 2021; Habotta et al. 2022), namely the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin 1-beta (IL1β), tumor necrosis factor (TNFα), and heat shock protein (HSP70), with beta actin (β-actin) used as the housekeeping gene. The QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to quantify gene expression using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Applied Biosystems, California, USA). The qPCR mixture (20 µl) was prepared in special tubes containing 6 µl of deionized water, 1 µl of each primer, 10 µl of SYBR Green master mix (2×), and 2 µl of diluted cDNA. Thermocycling conditions were set initially at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. Relative gene expression levels were assessed in triplicate, including template controls, using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Schmittgen and Livak 2008).

Histomorphometric analyses

For the histomorphometric analyses, the intestine (anterior, middle, and posterior) and hepatopancreatic samples were immediately dissected out from each group as previously described with Pirarat et al. (2011). After that, the samples were fixed in neutral buffer formalin 10% for 24 h and were processed through the conventional paraffin embedding technique. Briefly, samples were dehydrated in ascending concentrations of ethyl alcohol and cleared in xylene, then embedded and blocked in liquid paraffin wax. Sections of 5 μm thickness were obtained using a rotatory microtome, then stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) (Bancroft and Layton 2013) for general histological observations and measurements of intestinal and testicular tissues. Additionally, PAS stain was used to estimate the goblet cell density with a positive PAS reaction (Gargiulo et al. 1998). After that, many representative microphotographs were taken from the stained sections using a digital camera attached to a light microscope (Olympus CX 41, Shinjuku-ku, Tokyo, Japan). For the histomorphometry (Moustafa et al. 2020), For the histomorphometry (Moustafa et al. 2020), three fish were utilized in each group. Five histological Sect. (5 μm thick) were taken from each fish and three non-overlapping microscopic fields in each section were examined under a light microscope. For intestinal tissues, five well-oriented villi per each field were selected and measured for villus height and width (x10) and epithelial length (x 40). For hepatic tissues, the mean number of hepatocytic cells per each field with viable vesicular nuclei (x 40) was analyzed, along with the mean area of each hepatic nucleus (x 40). The Image J program version 1.36 (NIH, USA) was used to analyze these morphometric analyses. To ensure accuracy and reproducibility, all measurements were taken by an observer who was blind of the treatment groups.

Statistical clarification

The GraphPad® statistics package software (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA; version 8.4.2) was used to perform a statistical analysis. Data normality and homogeneity were assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Anderson-Darling tests. Analyses were conducted with a parametric and two-tailed unpaired t-test at a significant level of *; P < 0.05, **; P < 0.01, ***; P < 0.001, ****; P < 0.0001, and all data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the final body weight, WG, ADG, and SGR % values. Overall, the growth parameters significantly improved when supplemented with BAs at 0.25 g/kg. A statistically substantial increase in final weight, WG, ADG, and SGR % values (P < 0.001) was exhibited in 0.25 g kg− 1 BAs supplementation compared to control diets (Figure. 1).

Hematological variables

With respect to the control group, WBCs and lymphocytes exhibited a significant (P < 0.001) increase in count with a significant decrease (P < 0.001) in neutrophil count after feeding Nile tilapia on a diet containing bile acid (0.25 g BAs/kg feed) (Table 3). On the contrary, dietary bile acid did not significantly impact the values of RBCs, Hb, and PCV (P > 0.05) (Table 3). No statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed, though monocytes showed a numerical increase in the BA0.25 group (Table 3). This indicated that dietary bile acid at this dose appears to modulate the immune system by enhancing lymphocyte-mediated immunity while reducing neutrophil numbers, potentially fine-tuning immune responses to maintain health without triggering excessive inflammation.

Serum biochemical indices

As shown in Table 4, dietary supplementation with BAs (0.25 g/kg feed) resulted in a significant elevation of liver function enzymes, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT; P < 0.01), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; P < 0.001), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP; P < 0.001), compared to the control group. However, levels of total protein, albumin, globulin, urea, creatinine, and uric acid remained statistically unchanged (P > 0.05). Additionally, BA supplementation significantly increased serum levels of glucose, cholesterol, and triglycerides (P < 0.001), indicating altered metabolic responses relative to the control. These results reflected the potential hepatic damage and metabolic disturbances induced by dietary BA (0.25 g/kg feed).

Immune response and digestive enzyme activities

Serum immune parameters, including RB (P < 0.001), phagocytic (P < 0.01) and lysozyme (P < 0.01) activities were significantly enhanced with dietary BAs, 0.25 g/kg feed, compared to the control fish (Figure. 2). Notably, feeding fish 0.25 g BAs/kg quadratically (P < 0.001) enhanced the activities of intestinal proteases, lipase, and amylase, With respect to the control group (Figure. 3). These findings suggest that low-dose bile acid supplementation (0.25 g/kg) enhances both immune defense mechanisms and digestive capacity in fish, supporting its use as a functional feed additive to promote health, resilience, and nutrient utilization, especially under intensive aquaculture conditions.

Lipid peroxidation and antioxidant activity

The hepatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD; P < 0.001), catalase (CAT; P < 0.01), and reduced glutathione (GSH; P < 0.01) were significantly decreased, while malondialdehyde (MDA; P < 0.001) levels were significantly increased in fish fed the bile acid-supplemented diet compared to the control group (Fig. 4A, B, C & D). In contrast to the liver, dietary bile acid supplementation profoundly boosted intestinal antioxidant defenses, as evidenced by a significant increase in SOD (P < 0.01), catalase (P < 0.001), and GSH (P < 0.0001) levels, with respect to the control fish (Figure. 4 A, B & C). Simultaneously, levels of MDA (P < 0.001), a marker of oxidative damage, were markedly reduced, unlike the control group (Fig. 4D). The contrasting effects highlighted a tissue-specific response to bile acid supplementation; while it may impose oxidative stress on the liver, it simultaneously boosted antioxidant defenses in the intestine. This dual action suggests a complex role of bile acid, potentially beneficial for gut health but requiring caution regarding hepatic impact.

Intestinal count

From the current results, fish fed with bile acid in the diet showed a higher bacterial count (2.50 × 106) compared to the control group (1.42 × 106), and Pseudomonas count was relatively higher in the bile acid group (1 × 106) than in the control group (9 × 105). Meanwhile, coliform count was lower in the bile acid-supplemented group (5 × 103) than in the control group (2 × 104).

Discussion

More attention has been given to bile acids as a feed supplement for enhancing growth function and health in farmed aquatic species, which are fed diets containing low fishmeal, high plant ingredients, or high lipids (Hang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2022; Yao et al. 2021). Data variation across studies has been observed in fish enriched with bile acids that could be related to several factors, including species, inclusion level, and basal diet formulation (Li et al. 2024). According to the experiment’s findings, Nile tilapia fed soybean meal-based diets containing 0.25 g BAs per kg had the highest growth performance and feed utilization, along with increased digestive enzyme activities. Supplementing bile acids at appropriate dosages increased the final body weight, WG, and SGR of largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) (Yang et al. 2023b). These results align with nutritional studies conducted in striped catfish, Pangasianodon hypophthalmus (Adam et al. 2023), tilapia (Jiang et al. 2018), grass carp (Peng et al. 2019), and a large yellow croaker (Ding et al. 2020). Various studies have documented the developmental efficacy and growth benefits of dietary bile acids in fish species (Li and Chiang 2014; Li et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2018), as BAs can improve the intestinal fat absorption by increasing lipase enzyme activity for lipid emulsification, leading to better lipid digestion and absorption (Adam et al. 2023; Maldonado-Valderrama et al. 2011). The growth was also markedly augmented in thinlip mullet, Liza ramada, fed soybean meal-based diets enriched with exogenous BAs for eight weeks (Abdel-Tawwab et al. 2023). Many reports found that BAs inoculation to plant protein-based diets has a beneficial impact on fish growth via maintaining the integrity of the intestinal epithelium by bile salts (Abdel-Tawwab et al. 2023; Gu et al. 2017; Yamamoto et al. 2007).

Hematological indices are widely regarded as reliable biomarkers for evaluating the health and nutritional status of fish in aquaculture (Fazio 2019). In the present study, dietary bile acids (BAs) supplementation at 0.25 g/kg significantly increased WBCs and lymphocyte counts while reducing neutrophils in Nile tilapia, without altering RBC, Hb, PCV, or monocyte values. Furthermore, WBC levels continued to rise with the inclusion of higher dietary BAs, reaching their maximum in thinlip mullet (Liza ramada) fed 130 mg BAs/kg feed (Abdel-Tawwab et al. 2023). These findings are consistent with previous research in Micropterus salmoides juveniles, where supplementation with 350 mg/kg BA significantly elevated leukocyte counts under high starch feeding conditions (Guo et al. 2020). Similarly, the beneficial effects of BAs supplementation at 0.4 g/kg on hematological parameters were more evident when provided in combination with a 7% fat diet, suggesting possible interactions between dietary lipid levels and BAs activity (Yuan et al. 2025). Although the precise mechanisms responsible for the hematological improvements observed in Nile tilapia remain to be elucidated, it is reasonable to propose that enhanced nutrient utilization, improved metabolic efficiency, and better growth performance in BAs-fed groups may contribute to these effects.

Serum biochemical indices are valuable tools for assessing the relationship between fat deposition and fish health status (Esmaeili-Mahani 2024; Esmaeili 2021). In the present study, dietary bile acids (BAs) supplementation elevated serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, glucose, cholesterol, and triglycerides, while other biochemical parameters remained unaffected. These results are consistent with Jiang et al. (2018), who reported increases in T-CHO, LDL-c, and HDL-c following BA supplementation in genetically improved farmed tilapia (GIFT) tilapia, and with findings in Micropterus salmoides juveniles, where BA supplementation under high-starch diets increased plasma TG and T-CHO (Guo et al. 2020). Such discrepancies across studies may be attributed to differences in BAs dosage, the specific bile acids used, and the composition of the basal diet (lipid-, carbohydrate-, or protein-based). In contrast to our findings, Yang et al. (2023a) and Jiang et al. (2024) demonstrated that dietary BAs supplementation reduced cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose, ALT, and AST in common carp and Nile tilapia. The elevation of lipid parameters observed in some studies may be linked to the stimulatory role of BAs in hepatic lipid metabolism. Beyond their role as emulsifiers, BAs act as signaling molecules regulating lipid absorption, transport, and synthesis through activation of nuclear receptors such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and membrane receptors such as TGR5 (Chávez-Talavera et al. 2017; Li and Chiang 2014). Excessive or imbalanced BAs supplementation may enhance hepatic lipid uptake and lipogenesis, thereby raising circulating cholesterol and triglyceride levels. This metabolic stimulation is frequently accompanied by increased activity of liver enzymes, including ALT and AST, reflecting both intensified hepatic metabolism and mild hepatocellular stress (Li et al. 2010).

The pronounced liver dysfunction observed in this study is unlikely to be explained solely by the duration of bile acid administration. This discrepancy suggests that additional mechanistic factors may be contributing to the severity of hepatic injury. Three interconnected processes appear particularly relevant, including enterohepatic recirculation overload, imbalanced farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling between the liver and intestine, and mitochondrial stress within hepatocytes (Sharma et al. 2023). Under normal physiological conditions, approximately 95% of secreted bile acids are absorbed in the ileum and returned to the liver through enterohepatic circulation to maintain metabolic balance (Ridlon and Gaskins 2024). When this system becomes overwhelmed, excessive bile acid accumulation can occur, leading to hepatocellular toxicity and inflammation (Wang et al. 2025). Such dysregulation has been reported in metabolic disorders, diets enriched with fermentable carbohydrates, cholestasis, and following certain xenobiotic exposures that disrupt intestinal FXR signaling (Zeng et al. 2024). FXR is a key regulator of bile acid synthesis, transport, and inflammatory responses and is expressed in both the intestine and the liver (Kydd-Sinclair et al. 2025). In hepatocytes, FXR activation induces SHP, which suppresses CYP7A1 and thereby reduces bile acid synthesis and cellular accumulation. In contrast, intestinal FXR activation stimulates the production of FGF15/19, which travels to the liver and further suppresses bile acid synthesis by binding FGFR4 (Wang et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024). Disruption of this coordinated liver–intestine signaling axis, such as reduced intestinal FXR activity without adequate hepatic compensation, has been shown to intensify bile acid toxicity and exacerbate liver damage (Xiao et al. 2023). Additionally, excessive concentrations of hydrophobic bile acids may directly impair mitochondrial structure and function, resulting in elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), altered mitochondrial dynamics, and activation of programmed cell death pathways. Mitochondrial dysfunction is strongly associated with oxidative stress, lipid accumulation, hepatocyte senescence, and the progression of cholestatic liver injury (Mignini et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025). Together, these mechanisms may form a self-reinforcing cycle in which bile acid overload disrupts FXR signaling, induces oxidative stress, and promotes progressive cellular injury (Sharma et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). Therefore, the hepatic pathology observed in the present study is likely multifactorial rather than solely dependent on exposure duration, emphasizing the need to consider bile acid signaling dynamics and cellular stress responses when interpreting the biological outcomes.

Phagocytic activity is a fundamental element of the innate immune system in fish. Phagocytes, such as neutrophils and macrophages, play a pivotal role in host defense by recognizing, engulfing, and eliminating invading pathogens (Secombes and Fletcher 1992). A critical component of this process is the respiratory burst, during which activated phagocytes produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) that contribute to the oxidative killing of microbes. Together, these mechanisms form a primary line of cellular defense and are widely employed as biomarkers for evaluating immune competence and health status in aquaculture species (Magnadóttir 2006; Whyte 2007). Lysozyme, a key humoral defense factor, plays a central role in the innate immune system of cultured fish by hydrolyzing bacterial peptidoglycans, particularly in Gram-positive bacteria, thus facilitating recognition and phagocytosis by immune cells. Our results revealed that RB, phagocytic, and lysozyme activities were significantly enhanced with dietary BAs, 0.25 g/kg feed, compared to the control fish. Previous study also demonstrated that fish fed diets supplemented with 1.0 and 1.25 g BA/kg feed exhibited the highest values of phagocytic and lysosomal activity, suggesting an enhancement of both innate and adaptive immune functions in juvenile Pangasius hypophthalmus (Adam et al. 2023). The improvement in immune parameters may be attributed to the antimicrobial properties of BAs and their ability to modulate phagocytic responses, thereby increasing phagocytic activity and index (Di Ciaula et al. 2018; Salinas et al. 2008). Moreover, the lysozyme activity and immunoglobulin M levels were significantly increased in tongue sole (Li et al. 2021) and Micropterus salmoides juveniles (Guo et al. 2020). Similarly, thin lip mullet fed BA-enriched diets exhibited marked improvements in serum immune biomarkers, including lysozyme, respiratory burst activity, and alternative complement hemolytic activity (ACH50), with the highest values recorded in fish receiving 130 mg BA/kg feed (Abdel-Tawwab et al. 2023). Despite these promising results, the precise immunomodulatory mechanisms through which exogenous BAs act in fish remain largely unclear. It is therefore necessary to conduct further studies to elucidate the underlying pathways and to determine how BAs supplementation contributes to strengthening the innate immune system of aquaculture species.

Interestingly, our study showed that dietary supplementation with 0.25 g BAs/kg feed significantly enhanced the activities of key intestinal digestive enzymes, including protease, lipase, and amylase, in Nile tilapia. This increase suggests that exogenous BA promotes more efficient digestion and utilization of proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates, thereby supporting improved nutrient assimilation and growth performance. Similar to our results, dietary exogenous BAs up to 130 mg/kg feed significantly enhanced the activities of intestinal protease, α-amylase, and lipase in thinlip mullet (Abdel-Tawwab et al. 2023). However, enzyme activities declined in fish fed diets supplemented with 350 mg BAs/kg, suggesting that excessive supplementation may impair digestive efficiency. These findings contrast with those of Zeng et al. (2016), who reported that Schizothorax prenanti juveniles exhibited the highest intestinal lipase activity when fed diets containing 300 mg BAs/kg. Similarly, in tongue sole, dietary BAs supplementation enhanced digestive capacity, with lipase activity elevated at 900 mg/kg and amylase activity increased at both 300 and 900 mg/kg diets (Li et al. 2021). More recently, it was demonstrated that hepatic protease, lipase, and amylase activities in tongue sole increased progressively with dietary BA inclusion at 300 and 900 mg/kg (Wang et al. 2022). These results highlight dose-dependent effects of BAs on digestive enzyme activity, underscoring the importance of optimizing supplementation levels for different aquaculture species. Besides, our results suggest that the bile acids did not reduce overall bacterial load but supported bile-tolerant species such as Pseudomonas, and it has inhibitory effects on coliforms, which are more sensitive to bile salts. This means that the bile acids have a potential antimicrobial effect, suppressing bile-sensitive bacteria (coliforms) while favoring bile-resistant organisms (Pseudomonas).

BAs were increasingly recognized for their role in modulating oxidative stress in fish (Li et al. 2024). Malondialdehyde (MDA) is widely used as a biomarker of lipid peroxidation, where elevated levels reflect oxidative stress and cellular injury (Souza et al. 2019). Under oxidative challenge, fish typically activate enzymatic antioxidant defenses, including SOD, catalase, and GSH, which act synergistically to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintain redox homeostasis (Das et al. 2024; Siddik et al. 2022). In the present study, BA supplementation induced oxidative stress in the liver, as indicated by suppressed SOD, CAT, and GSH activities alongside elevated MDA levels. By contrast, intestinal antioxidant defenses were markedly improved, showing enhanced SOD, CAT, and GSH activities together with reduced MDA concentrations. Interestingly, these contrasting responses may be linked to BAs-driven stimulation of hepatic lipid metabolism. Excessive BAs intake may promote lipid uptake and lipogenesis, increasing mitochondrial β-oxidation and ROS production, thereby aggravating oxidative stress in the liver. Supporting this notion, Dawood et al. (2025) reported that BA supplementation reduced MDA levels and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, GPx) in Nile tilapia fed high-fat diets, emphasizing its protective role against lipid-induced oxidative damage. Similar protective effects of BAs have also been demonstrated in Nile tilapia (Bhusare et al. 2023), yellow croaker (Ding et al. 2020), juvenile leopard coral grouper (Gao et al. 2023), and largemouth bass (Yin et al. 2021). Li et al. (2021) also reported that dietary BAs significantly improved the intestinal antioxidant status of tongue sole, as evidenced by enhanced SOD and CAT activities and reduced MDA levels. It has been documented that overdosing BAs on fish diets containing plant ingredients impaired the hepatic circulation with gallstones formation (Zhang et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2018). Our findings suggest that BAs supplementation exerts tissue-specific effects, alleviating oxidative stress in the intestine while simultaneously exacerbating it in the liver.

TNF-α and IL-1β are the main cytokines extensively encompassed in pro-inflammatory reactions and innate immune responses of fish species (Cho et al. 2001; Wang et al. 2022).

Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) is a conserved cytoprotective protein, facilitating the proper folding of nascent proteins under stress conditions (Mengal et al. 2023). Thus, HSP70 plays a critical role in the health and immunomodulation of aquatic animals against various stressors, particularly involved in regulating innate and adaptive immune responses (Sánchez-López et al. 2019). In our study, the expression level of IL-1β in the intestine was sharply decreased, while the levels of HSP70 were notably increased with dietary BAs. The decreased intestinal expression of IL-1β suggests reduced inflammatory responses in the intestinal tissue. Additionally, higher intestinal expression of HSP70, as a molecular chaperone, helps protect the fish by maintaining protein homeostasis and boosting the host’s innate immune response. However, the lower hepatic expression levels of HSP70, along with higher hepatic expression levels of IL-1β, accelerated the tissue damage and weakened the protective mechanism. Interestingly, these results are aligned with the antioxidant enzyme capacity in the intestine and liver tissues.

Fish depend on their intestines to absorb and process nutrients. The integrity of the gut structure influences fish growth and development. Intestinal morphometric parameters are often used to assess villus shape and function, determining the efficiency of nutrient absorption (El Asely et al. 2020; Khalil et al. 2022). Optimal intestinal health, improved nutrient absorption, and overall fish well-being are directly related to the length and width of intestinal villi. In the current study, the histoarchitecture of the intestine (anterior, middle, and posterior parts) from fish supplemented with 0.25 g of bile acid showed no histoarchitecture alteration compared to the control group without bile acid (BA0 group). Additionally, the length and width of the intestinal villi and the epithelial thickness were significantly increased in most areas of the intestinal wall in the BA0.25 group compared to the BA0 group. Goblet cells play a crucial role in protecting the intestinal mucosal layer by secreting mucus and antibacterial compounds (Gustafsson and Johansson 2022). In this study, BA0.25 supplementation was associated with marked increases in goblet cell density in the intestinal wall, confirming the positive impact of BAs on enhancing nutrient utilization and gut health. According to previous research on fish, a high-fat diet can damage gut health by altering intestinal architecture and normal microbiota, but adding exogenous bile acid may reduce these detrimental effects (Ma et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2019). The beneficial effects of BAs may stem from their organic acid and salt content, particularly sodium butyrate, which has been identified as an important energy source for intestinal epithelial cells (Piva et al. 2002), and influences various cellular processes essential for maintaining intestinal integrity (Hamer et al. 2008). In this study, the positive effect of bile acid supplementation on the histomorphometry of the intestinal wall was consistent with previous studies, including those previously published (Marzouk et al. 2025; Yin et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2025).

Hepatocytes are known to be cytotoxically affected by bile acids (Guicciardi and Gores 2002; Malhi et al. 2010). According to earlier research, dietary lithocholic acid supplementation caused bile infarcts and intrahepatic cholestasis in mice (Woolbright et al. 2014), and the excessive accumulation of bile acids was a key driver of cholestatic liver injury in humans (Woolbright 2015). In the current study, in the BA0.25 group, most hepatocytes appeared degenerated, with dark pyknotic nuclei or nuclei showing complete chromatolysis, and the pyramidal acini of the exocrine hepatic pancreas appeared degenerated. Additionally, the portal afferent vein appeared dilated and severely congested. These findings confirm previous studies that demonstrated the cytotoxic effect of bile acid supplementation on the hepatocytes of Nile tilapia (Adam et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2018; Jin et al. 2019; Yuan et al. 2025). Marzouk et al. (2025) stated that moderate inclusion of BA in fish diets can enhance intestinal health and nutrient absorption without causing negative effects on the liver. Consequently, our results verify that the hepatotoxic effect observed in the current study may be attributed to the high level of dietary bile acid supplementation.

Conclusion

This study concludes that dietary BAs at 0.25 g/kg supplementation for a prolonged period markedly improved the intestinal health status of Nile tilapia. The biochemical indices closely related to growth, immune, and intestinal antioxidants were notably improved. Consistent results were also observed in gene expression and in intestinal morphometry. However, long-term inoculation of bile acids resulted in reduced liver function and increased the inflammatory responses of Nile tilapia, as evidenced by liver morphology and hepatic oxidative stress. This study explores that using bile acids as a feed additive for more nutritious periods would adversely affect fish’s liver health. So, many advised applications should be given for fish farmers, such as intermittent dosing schedules, potential combination with hepatoprotectants, or lower dosage strategies for long-term use
