Dexamethasone enhances cold atmospheric-pressure plasma-induced antitumor effects in lung adenocarcinoma partly through ROS-associated apoptosis and suppression of PI3K/AKT signaling
Authors: Ling Kong, Tao Sun, Changqing Liu, Siyuan Sui, Wencheng Song, Guohua Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, and the efficacy of conventional therapies is often limited by drug resistance and treatment-related toxicity. Cold atmospheric-pressure plasma (CAP) generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species and has been shown to exert antitumor effects, but its potential synergy with clinically used drugs remains underexplored. In this study, CAP combined with dexamethasone (DEX) reduced the viability and impaired the migratory capacity of lung adenocarcinoma cells, with combination index analysis indicating synergism under most tested conditions. CAP plus DEX increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and induced apoptosis, accompanied by changes in p53 and mitochondrial apoptosis-related proteins, as well as reduced PI3K/AKT-related protein expression. Rescue experiments with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) indicated that these antitumor effects were at least partly ROS-dependent. The combination reduced cell viability in multiple lung adenocarcinoma cell lines (A549, SPC-A1, and Calu-1) and inhibited tumor growth in a Calu-1 xenograft mouse model. Together, these results suggest that DEX enhances the antitumor activity of CAP under the experimental conditions used in this study, supporting CAP–DEX combination therapy as a potential strategy for lung adenocarcinoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53040-2.Keywords: Cold atmospheric-pressure plasma, Dexamethasone, Reactive oxygen species, Lung adenocarcinoma, Apoptosis, Synergistic inhibitionSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a major global health burden and is among the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide1,2. Although advances have been made in surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, the overall prognosis of patients with lung cancer remains poor, largely due to tumor heterogeneity, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and treatment-related toxicity3,4. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies with improved efficacy and acceptable safety.

Cold atmospheric-pressure plasma (CAP) has emerged as a promising biomedical technology with potential applications in cancer therapy, wound disinfection, and tissue regeneration5–9. Increasing evidence indicates that CAP exerts antitumor activity against a variety of cancers, including lung cancer10–22. Importantly, CAP may preferentially induce cytotoxicity in cancer cells relative to normal tissues, highlighting its potential as both a standalone treatment and an adjunct to conventional therapies23–30.

The anticancer activity of CAP is thought to be largely mediated by reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) generated during plasma discharge31,32. In the gas phase, CAP produces short-lived species such as hydroxyl radicals (OH), superoxide anions (O2−), and singlet oxygen (1O2), which subsequently interact with aqueous environments to generate longer-lived species including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), ozone (O3), and nitrate ions (NO3−)33–36.

Under physiological conditions, RONS play essential roles in cellular homeostasis; however, excessive accumulation of these species disrupts redox balance, induces oxidative stress, and can trigger cell death, thereby contributing to the anticancer effects of CAP37,38. Consistent with this view, numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that controlled CAP exposure suppresses cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis, at least in part through regulation of cell-cycle progression39–51. CAP may also be combined with pharmacological agents to further improve therapeutic efficacy52–54. Such combination strategies may help overcome tumor resistance and amplify oxidative stress–mediated cytotoxicity.

Dexamethasone (DEX) is a synthetic glucocorticoid widely used as an adjunct to chemotherapy or radiotherapy to alleviate treatment-related adverse effects, such as nausea, pain, and cerebral edema. Although DEX has been reported to exert context-dependent effects on tumor biology in certain experimental settings, its role in cancer therapy remains complex and may vary across tumor types and treatment conditions55–57. Based on prior evidence that DEX can modulate PI3K/AKT signaling under specific circumstances58, we hypothesized that DEX may enhance CAP-induced antitumor effects, at least in part, by modulating survival signaling pathways. Owing to its clinical availability and established tolerability, DEX was selected to evaluate whether it could act as a CAP sensitizer in lung adenocarcinoma models.

In this study, we investigated whether DEX enhances the antitumor activity of CAP in lung adenocarcinoma. Using Calu‑1 cells as the primary model, we performed mechanistic validation in A549 cells, while SPC-A1 cells were used only for cell viability assays. Cell viability, migration, ROS generation, apoptosis, and PI3K/AKT signaling were assessed, and the therapeutic efficacy of the combination was further examined in a xenograft mouse model.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

Human lung adenocarcinoma cell lines Calu-1, A549, and SPC-A1 (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For combination experiments, cells were pretreated with dexamethasone (DEX; Blue Skies Biotechnology, China) at 1 μM for 24 h before CAP exposure, based on previous reports. N-acetylcysteine (NAC; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used as an antioxidant and ROS scavenger at 5 mM and added 1 h prior to CAP exposure.

Primary antibodies against p53 (Abcam, #ab26), PI3K (Abcam, #ab139307), AKT (Abcam, #ab38449), Bax (Abcam, #ab216494), Bcl-2 (Abcam, #ab117115), pro-caspase-3 (Abcam, #ab32499), cleaved caspase-3 (Abcam, #ab214430), and GAPDH (Abcam, #ab8245) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). The primary antibody against Ki67 (Abcam, #ab16667) was used for immunohistochemistry.

CAP treatment system

CAP was generated using a custom-built dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) device, as previously described58. The system consisted of four high-voltage copper electrodes (60 mm diameter) with a 1‑mm‑thick quartz glass layer serving as dielectric insulation. The discharge gap between the quartz surface and the treatment sample was fixed at 5 mm. Helium gas (99.999% purity) was supplied at a flow rate of 1.0 L/min. The applied voltage and frequency were maintained at 14.4 kV (peak‑to‑peak) and 14.2 kHz, respectively. The schematic structure of the DBD chamber and representative discharge photographs are shown in Fig. 1A and B, respectively. All experiments followed the workflow outlined in Fig. 1C.

Experimental setup and study workflow. (A) Schematic illustration of the dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma device. (B) Representative photograph of the CAP device during operation. (C) Schematic workflow of the in vitro and in vivo experimental design, including CAP/DEX treatment, cell function assessment, and tumor verification in vivo.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 60-mm dishes at 1 × 106 cells per dish in triplicate and allowed to adhere overnight. After 24 h of DEX pretreatment (1 μM), the medium was replaced and cells were exposed to CAP for different durations (0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 s). Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit‑8 (CCK‑8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Varioskan Flash microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Results are expressed as a percentage of the untreated control group.

Propidium iodide staining

To assess membrane integrity, cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI). After the indicated treatments, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with PI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL for 15 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. For all groups, images were captured under identical magnification, field size, exposure time, and gain settings. PI-positive cells were counted from five randomly selected fields per well. Because no nuclear counterstaining was performed, the PI staining results were not used to calculate the absolute percentage of dead cells; instead, the number of PI-positive cells per field was used for comparison among treatment groups.

Combination index analysis

The interaction between CAP and DEX was evaluated using the Chou–Talalay method and analyzed with CompuSyn software. The combination index (CI) was calculated based on the median-effect equation. CI < 1 indicates synergism, CI = 1 indicates an additive effect, and CI > 1 indicates antagonism.

Measurement of extracellular H2O2 and NO

Extracellular H2O2 levels in the culture medium were measured using a fluorescence-based assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After treatment, culture supernatants were collected and incubated with the assay reagents. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader, and H2O2 concentrations were calculated from a standard curve. Nitrite levels, as an indirect indicator of NO production, were measured using a Griess reagent kit. After treatment, culture supernatants were incubated with Griess reagents, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm. Nitrite concentrations were calculated based on a sodium nitrite standard curve.

Intracellular ROS assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). At 24 h after CAP treatment, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA, diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium, at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by three washes with serum-free medium to remove excess probe. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica DMI 4000B fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.52).

Measurement of intracellular H2O2 and NO

Intracellular H2O2 levels were measured using a fluorescence-based assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After treatment, cells were lysed, and the lysates were centrifuged to remove debris. The supernatants were incubated with assay reagents, and fluorescence was measured. H2O2 concentrations were calculated using a standard curve and normalized to total protein. Intracellular NO levels were detected in live cells using DAF‑FM DA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Fluorescence was measured at excitation/emission of 500/515 nm, and relative NO levels were expressed as fluorescence intensity normalized to the control group.

NAC rescue experiment

To assess the contribution of ROS to the combined treatment effect, cells were pretreated with NAC (5 mM) for 1 h before exposure to CAP and/or DEX. Cell viability and protein expression were then analyzed as described above.

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Calu-1 cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells per dish and treated as indicated. After treatment, cells were washed twice with cold PBS and resuspended in 500 μL of 1× binding buffer. Apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were analyzed using a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (Becton–Dickinson) with FACS suite software.

Wound healing assay

Cells were grown to confluence in 6-well plates, and a uniform wound was created using a sterile 200-μL pipette tip. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, the remaining cells were cultured in serum-reduced medium. Wound closure was monitored at 0 and 24 h using an Olympus light microscope (100× magnification).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with IRDye-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR).

In vivo xenograft model

Six‑ to eight‑week‑old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from a commercial vendor (Jiangsu, China) and housed under specific pathogen‑free (SPF) conditions. After 1 week of acclimation, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of Avertin (1.25%; #KP13883; KKL Med Inc., China) at a dose of 200–300 μL per 10 g body weight. Anesthesia depth was confirmed by the absence of a pedal withdrawal reflex in response to toe pinch. Under anesthesia, 5 × 106 Calu‑1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. After the procedure, mice were returned to their home cages and kept warm on a heating pad.

When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): (1) control (saline only), (2) CAP alone, (3) DEX alone (1 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), and (4) CAP plus DEX (DEX injection 24 h prior to each CAP treatment). For each treatment session, mice in the treated groups were anesthetized using the same Avertin protocol. CAP was applied directly to the tumor surface for 3 min per session under standardized conditions. Treatments were administered every other day for a total of six sessions.

Tumor dimensions (length L and width W) and body weight were measured every two days. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W2)/2. The predefined humane endpoints were: (1) tumor burden exceeding 1500 mm3, (2) body weight loss of > 20%, or (3) signs of severe distress or morbidity. No animals reached these endpoints prior to the scheduled conclusion of the study.

At the end of the experiment, euthanasia was performed using carbon dioxide (CO₂) from a compressed gas cylinder. Mice were placed in a transparent plexiglass chamber for continuous observation. CO2 was introduced at a fill rate not exceeding 30% of the chamber volume per min. The mice remained in the CO2‑saturated environment for 5–6 min. After cessation of breathing was observed, mice were left in the chamber for an additional 2 min. The animals were then immediately removed for tissue collection. A secondary physical method, bilateral thoracotomy, was performed to confirm death.

All animal procedures were approved by the Anhui Medical University Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No: LLSC20210791). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and this study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

CAP and DEX synergistically reduce lung cancer cell viability

Cell viability in all tested lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, including A549, SPC-A1, and Calu-1, decreased in an exposure duration‑dependent manner following CAP treatment, with Calu-1 cells exhibiting the highest sensitivity (Fig. 2A). Calu-1 cells were therefore selected for subsequent mechanistic studies. DEX alone at concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 10 μM did not significantly affect cell viability in any of the tested cell lines (Fig. 2B). Based on these findings and previous reports, 1.0 μM DEX was selected for combination experiments. Notably, CAP combined with 1.0 μM DEX significantly enhanced the cytotoxic effect across all three cell lines, with the strongest response observed in Calu-1 cells (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that DEX enhances CAP-induced cytotoxicity in a CAP exposure time-dependent manner. PI staining was further performed to assess membrane integrity in Calu-1 cells. Under standardized imaging conditions, CAP increased the number of PI-positive cells per field, and this effect was further enhanced by DEX, suggesting that DEX potentiates CAP-induced membrane permeability changes (Fig. 2D). CI analysis demonstrated synergism for most CAP exposure durations across all three cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S1).

CAP and DEX reduce lung cancer cell viability. (A) Cell viability of A549, SPC-A1, and Calu-1 cells following CAP exposure for the indicated durations. (B) Cell viability of A549, SPC-A1, and Calu-1 cells treated with DEX alone at concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 10 μM. (C) Cell viability of cells treated with CAP for 0 s, 10 s, 20 s, 30 s, or 40 s in the absence or presence of DEX (1 μM). (D) Representative PI staining images of Calu-1 cells after the indicated treatments. PI-positive cells were counted per microscopic field from five randomly selected fields per well under identical imaging conditions. The bar graph shows the mean number of PI-positive cells per field. Data are presented as the mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

CAP–DEX combination induces marked morphological changes in Calu-1 cells

Pronounced morphological alterations were observed in Calu-1 cells treated with CAP in combination with DEX, whereas DEX alone had no obvious effect (Fig. 3). With increasing CAP exposure time, cells exhibited reduced adhesion, pronounced shrinkage, and apparent membrane integrity changes, accompanied by widespread loss of cellular integrity and an increase in detached cells. The addition of DEX further intensified these morphological changes, consistent with the corresponding decline in cell viability.

CAP and DEX induce morphological changes in Calu-1 cells. Representative phase-contrast images of Calu-1 cells treated with CAP for 10 s, 20 s, 30 s, or 40 s, with or without DEX (1 μM). CAP treatment induced cell shrinkage, loss of adhesion, and increased cell detachment, and these effects were further enhanced by DEX co-treatment.

Combined treatment increases reactive species accumulation

To determine whether oxidative stress contributes to CAP-mediated cytotoxicity, we first performed rescue experiments using the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC). CAP treatment alone significantly reduced the viability of A549, SPC-A1, and Calu-1 cells, whereas NAC pretreatment substantially restored cell viability in all three cell lines, indicating that reactive species are important contributors to CAP-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 4A).

CAP and DEX increase intracellular and extracellular reactive species levels in Calu-1 cells. (A) Effect of NAC pretreatment on CAP-induced cytotoxicity in A549, SPC-A1, and Calu-1 cells. (B) Extracellular and intracellular H2O2 and NO levels after CAP exposure for 0 s, 10 s, 20 s, 30 s, or 40 s. (C) Representative fluorescence images and quantitative analysis of intracellular ROS levels in Calu-1 cells treated with CAP for 40 s, DEX (1 μM), or CAP plus DEX. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

We then measured extracellular and intracellular H2O2 and NO levels to assess RONS accumulation. Extracellular and intracellular H2O2 levels, as well as extracellular nitrite and intracellular NO levels, were significantly higher in the CAP plus DEX group than in the CAP-alone group at the same exposure time (Fig. 4B). Intracellular ROS production in Calu-1 cells was further evaluated using DCFH-DA fluorescence staining. Fluorescence intensity increased with CAP exposure time and was further enhanced by DEX co-treatment (Fig. 4C). Quantitative analysis using ImageJ confirmed that intracellular ROS levels were significantly higher in cells receiving the combined treatment than in those treated with CAP alone. These findings support the involvement of reactive species in CAP-induced cytotoxicity and are consistent with the enhanced oxidative stress observed after CAP plus DEX treatment.

CAP and DEX synergistically promote apoptosis

We next examined whether the combination treatment promotes apoptosis. Flow cytometric analysis showed that CAP plus DEX induced a significantly higher apoptotic rate in Calu-1 cells than CAP alone (Fig. 5A,B). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that the combined treatment increased p53 and Bax expression, increased cleaved caspase-3 levels, and decreased pro-caspase-3 and Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 5C–E). Quantitative analyses of these apoptosis-related proteins in Calu-1 cells and A549 cells are shown in Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3, respectively. These findings suggest that CAP plus DEX promotes apoptosis, potentially in association with ROS-related p53 activation and downstream regulation of apoptosis-related proteins.

CAP and DEX synergistically induce apoptosis in Calu-1 cells. (A) Representative apoptosis profiles detected by flow cytometry in Calu-1 cells. (B) Quantification of apoptotic rates. (C) Western blot analysis of apoptosis-related proteins in Calu-1 cells treated with CAP for 20 s or 40 s, with or without DEX. (D) Western blot analysis of apoptosis-related proteins in Calu-1 cells treated with DEX at the indicated concentrations. (E) Western blot analysis of apoptosis-related proteins in Calu-1 cells treated with CAP for 10 s, 20 s, 30 s, or 40 s. Data are mean ± SD. ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Combined treatment suppresses cell migration

A wound healing assay was used to assess the effect of treatment on cell migration. CAP alone significantly impaired wound closure, and this effect was further enhanced by DEX co-treatment, suggesting reduced migratory capacity under the experimental conditions used (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the combination treatment produced a significantly greater inhibition of wound closure than CAP alone, particularly at the 20- and 40-s exposure time points (Fig. 6B). Western blot analysis showed that both CAP alone and the CAP plus DEX combination decreased PI3K and AKT protein levels in an exposure time-dependent manner, with more pronounced suppression observed in the combination group (Fig. 6C). Given that PI3K/AKT signaling is frequently activated in lung cancer and supports proliferation, survival, and migration, these results suggest that the enhanced inhibition of wound closure may be associated with reduced PI3K/AKT-related protein expression and ROS accumulation.

CAP and DEX suppress Calu-1 cell migration and downregulate PI3K/AKT signaling. (A) Representative wound healing images of Calu-1 cells treated with DEX alone, CAP alone at 20 s or 40 s, or CAP plus DEX. (B) Quantitative analysis of scratch distance. (C) Western blot analysis and densitometric quantification of PI3K and AKT expression in Calu-1 cells treated with CAP for 20 s or 40 s, with or without DEX. Data are presented as the mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

CAP and DEX cooperatively inhibit tumor growth in vivo

To validate the in vitro findings, a Calu-1 xenograft model was established in nude mice. Animals were randomly assigned to four groups: control, CAP alone, DEX alone, and CAP plus DEX. CAP was applied directly to the tumor surface for 3 min every other day, and DEX was administered intraperitoneally 24 h before each CAP treatment. As shown in Fig. 7A–D, the combination treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with either monotherapy or control. Immunohistochemical analysis of Ki67 revealed a marked reduction in proliferating cells in the combination group (Fig. 7E,F). These in vivo findings confirm that CAP and DEX act cooperatively to inhibit lung cancer growth.

CAP and DEX combination therapy inhibits tumor growth in vivo. (A) Representative images of tumor-bearing mice from each treatment group. (B) Excised tumors at the end of the experiment. (C) Tumor weights at the end of the experiment. (D) Tumor volumes at the end of the experiment. (E) Representative immunohistochemical staining of Ki67 in tumor sections from the Ctrl and CAP + DEX groups. (F) Quantification of Ki67-positive cells. Scale bar, 100 μm. Data are presented as the mean ± SD.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that the combination of CAP and DEX exerts stronger antitumor effects against lung adenocarcinoma cells than either treatment alone. This conclusion was supported by reduced cell viability, increased apoptosis, enhanced reactive species accumulation, changes consistent with activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, reduced wound closure, and improved tumor control in vivo. CI analysis further indicated that the interaction between CAP and DEX was synergistic under most tested conditions.

Our findings support the view that CAP is a potential anticancer modality, particularly when combined with agents that modulate cellular stress responses. CAP generates a broad spectrum of RONS capable of disrupting redox homeostasis and triggering apoptosis or other forms of cell death59. In the present study, CAP increased both extracellular and intracellular RONS, and this effect was further amplified by DEX. DCFH-DA staining further indicated increased intracellular ROS accumulation. The NAC rescue experiments provided additional evidence that oxidative stress is a major contributor to the observed cytotoxicity. Specifically, NAC attenuated the loss of cell viability and partially reversed the oxidative stress-associated effects, supporting a partial causal role for ROS in the combined treatment effect.

DEX has been reported to exert context-dependent effects on tumor biology through mechanisms involving glucocorticoid receptor signaling and survival pathway modulation60–65. Under the experimental conditions used in our lung adenocarcinoma models, DEX alone did not produce a detectable antiproliferative effect; however, it clearly enhanced the response to CAP. One possible explanation is that DEX may alter cellular redox buffering capacity and attenuate compensatory stress signaling, thereby rendering tumor cells more vulnerable to CAP-induced oxidative injury; however, this possibility requires further verification. These results suggest that, under the conditions used in this study, DEX may function primarily as a CAP sensitizer rather than as a direct cytotoxic agent.

Apoptosis appears to be an important downstream mechanism underlying the enhanced antitumor activity of CAP plus DEX. We observed increased expression of p53, cleaved caspase-3, and Bax, accompanied by decreased pro-caspase-3 and Bcl-2 expression, indicating activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic machinery. As p53 is a key regulator of cellular stress responses, its induction may link ROS accumulation to apoptotic execution. The increase in cleaved caspase-3 further suggests that the apoptotic cascade was functionally activated.

In addition to apoptosis, the combined treatment was associated with reduced PI3K/AKT-related protein expression. Because PI3K/AKT is frequently activated in lung cancer and promotes proliferation, survival, and migration, its inhibition likely contributed to the stronger antitumor effect of CAP plus DEX. Moreover, reduced PI3K/AKT-related signaling may contribute to the enhanced antitumor effect of CAP plus DEX by weakening survival signaling and limiting the capacity of cancer cells to resist oxidative stress. Thus, the combined treatment may act through a dual mechanism involving activation of pro-death signaling and attenuation of survival pathways. PI3K/AKT-related changes may contribute to, rather than fully explain, the enhanced oxidative stress response.

An additional strength of this study is that the viability-related effects were reproduced in multiple lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, supporting the robustness of the observed cytotoxic response. In vivo validation in a xenograft model further strengthened the evidence for the superior antitumor activity of the combination regimen. These results suggest that the synergistic effect is not restricted to a single in vitro model but is also relevant in a preclinical animal system.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although CI analysis supports synergism, additional quantitative approaches could further validate the interaction between CAP and DEX. Second, although NAC rescue experiments implicate oxidative stress in the observed effects, more detailed mechanistic studies, including pathway rescue experiments, are required to define the signaling hierarchy linking reactive species accumulation, p53 activation, apoptosis, and PI3K/AKT-related changes. Third, ultrastructural confirmation by electron microscopy was not performed and should be considered in future work. Finally, further validation in additional lung cancer models and more clinically relevant preclinical systems will be necessary before translational conclusions can be drawn.

In summary, our data indicate that DEX potentiates CAP-induced antitumor effects through reactive species accumulation, p53-associated apoptosis, and reduced PI3K/AKT-related protein expression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that DEX enhances the antitumor activity of CAP in lung adenocarcinoma under our experimental conditions through ROS-associated apoptosis and attenuation of PI3K/AKT-related signaling.
