Long-term return to work and income trajectories after major trauma: a 10-year nationwide retrospective cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: Ling-wei Kuo, Po-Chuan Ko, Chih-Yuan Fu, Chien-Hung Liao, Yu-Tung Huang, Yu-Hsin Wang, Shih-Ching Kang, Chi-Tung Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe financial impact on trauma patients can be devastating. Although Taiwan has universal health insurance coverage, medical bill reimbursement cannot compensate for the damage to a person’s ability to work. The aim of this study was to investigate personal financial outcomes after major trauma.DesignA retrospective cohort study.SettingNationwide data from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database.ParticipantsThis study included all patients with major trauma (injury severity score ≥16) in Taiwan from 2003 to 2007, and a 10-year follow-up was conducted. Patients aged 18–70 were enrolled. Patients who returned to work after trauma (RTW) and those who did not return to work (non-RTW) were compared. Basic demographics and short-term outcomes were analysed, and the 10-year trend of income variation was calculated.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes are the ratio of non-RTW after major trauma, and the independent risk factors for non-RTW. The secondary outcome is the 10-year trend of income variation of major trauma patients.Results5965 patients were included, with 4741 (79.5%) in the RTW group and 1224 (20.5%) in the non-RTW group. Hospital-acquired pneumonia, urinary tract infection, prolonged intensive care unit length of stay (LOS) and prolonged hospital LOS were identified as independent risk factors for future non-RTW. The mean monthly income of all patients declined in the three consecutive years postinjury and slowly returned to the preinjury level 9 years after the injury.ConclusionsLoss of working ability was noted in 20.5% of major trauma patients, and the income level for all patients did not recover to the preinjury level until 9 years after the injury. Further welfare planning beyond the extent of the National Health Insurance programme should be made to protect the financial shortcomings experienced by these patients.Trial registration numberISRCTN18269986.Keywords: Health policy, Health economics, TRAUMA MANAGEMENT

Background

Trauma has long been a daunting issue in Taiwan. In this country of 23 million people, approximately 6000 people suffer from major trauma, with an injury severity score (ISS) of more than 16, yearly,1 and it accounted for approximately 30 deaths per 100 000 population annually.2 The high mortality is alarming, but trauma survivors may also need our attention. Traumatic injuries are often unforeseeable, and often trauma victims are those with lower socioeconomic levels,3,6 so the consequences can be overwhelming. In fact, injury is one of the main reasons for bankruptcy in the USA.7 8 A common scenario is the co-occurrence of medical and employment challenges. For instance, a patient could be covered by employer-paid insurance initially, but an injury may result in an inability to return to the prior job; therefore, the patient would lose his or her insurance and incur medical debt.9

Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI) offers universal health insurance coverage, regardless of occupational status. Currently, >99% of Taiwan’s population receives medical care through the NHI.10 Theoretically, the NHI should relieve the financial impact of job interference after trauma. The NHI also provides flexible measures, including relief fund loans, premium instalments and referrals to charitable organisations for help with premiums, for insured individuals experiencing financial hardship.11 However, despite all the bailout packages, universal insurance can cover medical expenses, but the damage to a patient’s ability to work is something that cannot be reimbursed.

The financial loss due to compromised occupational ability is an issue more emphasised by public health or social science domains, but often neglected by the medical communities in Taiwan. A study published by Chin et al in 2013 reported that the rate of not returning to work after an occupational injury was 21.4% for injured workers in Taiwan.12 Another study by Lin et al in 2015 revealed that Taiwan’s annual economic loss due to injuries could exceed US$400 million.13 These studies provided valuable insights regarding the interaction between injuries, working abilities and financial deficits. However, a large-scale, comprehensive analysis with individual details is yet to be found. Therefore, the scope of this study was to investigate individual working ability and financial outcomes after major trauma from a nationwide perspective.

Methods

Data

Data regarding the medical services provided by the NHI are collected by the National Health Insurance Administration and entered into the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD). In this study, complete medical and surgical records from 2003 to 2017 were analysed, including all claims from the emergency department, in-hospital admission and outpatient clinics.

Study cohort

This retrospective observational study included all patients with major trauma in Taiwan from 2003 to 2007, and a 10-year follow-up was conducted. To identify the target population, the catastrophic illness certificate (CIC) was used as a guide since patients with an ISS ≥16 are eligible for the CIC, which exempts them from copayments for medical expenses related to the trauma. Patients who had previously received a CIC for other serious conditions were excluded from the analysis to avoid interactions between these conditions and trauma.

To narrow down the study population, we excluded patients who died within a year of the index trauma incident. We also set age criteria to exclude paediatric patients under 18 years old and patients over 70 years old, given the life expectancy in Taiwan during the study period was around 80 years.14 This was to avoid confounding effects from natural death on the 10-year follow-up. The final analysis included patients aged 18–70 years with no severe disease, who experienced major trauma between 2003 and 2007 and survived for more than a year after the index trauma incident.

Variables and outcomes

This study was intended to identify the risk factors for loss of work ability after major trauma and how much it would change the income of these patients. To define the income level of the target population, we first examined the NHI payroll bracket. The payroll bracket can be divided into six categories.15 Categories 1, 2 and 3 consist of the people who have registered sources of income, including all the employees, employers and self-employed workers who belong to occupational unions. Category 4 belongs to military servicemen/women and those who are serving sentences in a penitentiary. Categories 5 and 6 are welfare associated, mainly comprising members of low household income and veterans. For categories 1–3, the monthly income is equivalent to the insurance amount, but the premiums of the beneficiaries in categories 4–6 were calculated differently and did not reflect the actual income. Therefore, categories 4–6 were ruled out from our analysis. We then considered the identification (ID) number of the insured. If the personal ID number matched the insured ID number, it indicated that this person was an actual working person with registered income. If the ID numbers did not match, it implied that this person was fostered by another actual working person, and he or she had no registered income. In conclusion, we identified our target population by locating the actual working people in categories 1–3 from the payroll bracket; thus, their insurance amount reflected their income level. The median preinjury monthly income of the enrolled patients was US$640; hence, preinjury monthly income ≤US$640 was classified as low income, whereas >US$640 was considered high income.

Pre-existing factors, including age, sex and underlying comorbidities, were analysed, and comorbidities were quantified with the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The analysis also included specific injuries, sorted by the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, and the corresponding codes are noted in the online supplemental table. We also considered short-term outcomes and in-hospital complications during the index admission. Complications, including acute coronary syndrome, stroke, hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), urinary tract infection (UTI), wound complications, tracheostomy, dialysis and cardiopulmonary cerebral resuscitation (CPCR), were identified. Short-term outcomes, including prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV), prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LOS) and prolonged total LOS (PLOS), were also evaluated. PMV was defined as mechanical ventilation for 21 or more consecutive days;16,18 prolonged ICU LOS (PICULOS) was defined as ≥14 days,19,21 and the definition of PLOS was admission for >30 days, a quality index commonly used by the NHI.22,24 Post-discharge long-term survival was also calculated since it was a critical reference for financial analysis.

Data analysis

Patients were divided into two groups: returned to work (RTW) and did not return to work (non-RTW). We recorded the preinjury insurance amount of actual working people identified from categories 1–3. One month after being discharged from the hospital, patients were examined again. Those who moved to categories 4–6 were placed in the non-RTW group, indicating loss of work ability due to reliance on welfare or imprisonment. If their category remained in 1–3, but the insurance amount dropped to zero, it suggested that they no longer had registered income but were insured under another actual working person. These patients were also placed in the non-RTW group. Patients were placed in the RTW group only if they remained in categories 1–3 and continued to have registered income.

The two subgroups were compared to disclose any difference in the pre-existing factors, specific injury types, preinjury income level, and short-term outcomes and complications. The mean postinjury monthly income for all enrolled patients was calculated over a 10-year follow-up period to observe the income alteration of major trauma patients. The continuous variables were processed with the Mann-Whitney U test, and the categorical variables were processed with the χ2 test. Long-term survival was analysed by the Kaplan-Meier method. Logistic regression was performed to determine the factors that predicted non-RTW after index admission and variables with p value <0.05 in the univariate analysis were selected for multivariate analysis. These variables included the following: head injuries, thoracic injuries, abdominal and retroperitoneal injuries, pelvic injuries and spinal cord injuries within the injury type category, and CPCR, HAP, UTI, PMV, PICULOS and PLOS within the outcomes and complications category. All statistical analyses were performed with SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA), and a two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

99% of Taiwan’s residents were involved in the making of NHIRD. However, due to the retrospective and database-based nature of this study, patients and the public were not involved.

In terms of complications and short-term outcomes, the non-RTW group had more patients with a CPCR event during the index admission (1.14% vs 0.53%, p=0.0170), HAP (20.34% vs 11.71%, p<0.0001) and UTI (11.19% vs 6.48%, p<0.0001). There were no significant differences in the distribution of monthly income between the two groups.

In the univariate logistic regression model (table 3), many factors were shown to be associated with non-RTW. Head injuries (OR=1.298, 95% CI 1.113 to 1.514), spinal cord injuries (OR=1.351, 95% CI 1.105 to 1.651), CPCR (OR=2.183, 95% CI 1.131 to 4.212) during the index admission, HAP (OR=1.926, 95% CI 1.633 to 2.272), UTI (OR=1.820, 95% CI 1.473 to 2.250), PMV (OR=1.017, 95% CI 1.013 to 1.021), PICULOS (OR=1.027, 95% CI 1.023 to 1.032) and PLOS (OR=1.013, 95% CI 1.011 to 1.015) were associated with increased risk for non-RTW, whereas chest injuries (OR=0.747, 95% CI 0.640 to 0.871), abdominal and retroperitoneal injuries (OR=0.560, 95% CI 0.453 to 0.691) and pelvic injuries (OR=0.641, 95% CI 0.468 to 0.879) were negatively associated with the risk of non-RTW. Discussion

The long-term socioeconomic outcome of trauma is an issue that is not frequently discussed, and patients’ worry may extend beyond the physical outcomes. An article by Ioannou et al revealed that over 50% of trauma survivors expressed worry about financial matters.25 However, the topics regarding work status and financial issues are vague and not readily discussed. Most quantitative studies would use questionnaires to conduct the analysis, so the accuracy of the data would rely on patients’ willingness to give out information.26,28 The vastness of the NHIRD can compensate for this problem, but the shortcoming of this database is that certain details are not revealed,29 so it might be insufficient to review the outcomes from an individual level. However, the insurance amount and the categorisation of the payroll bracket provided us valuable information on the patient’s financial and occupational status.

A significant issue of RTW is the time frame. A clear-cut duration after the initial injury may not be sufficient to determine a patient’s recovery, as it may oversimplify the analysis into a dichotomised discussion. Kruithof and colleagues reported that trauma patients started to improve prominently on different aspects of general health status, including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and discomfort, and anxiety at approximately 1 to 3 months after injury.30 Judging from the fact that over 40% of our study cohort were admitted to the hospital for more than 30 days, 1 month after discharge would fall into the period of 1 to 3 months after injury for most of our patients. Therefore, 30 days after discharge was chosen as our cut point of RTW. However, RTW is a status of fluidity. According to a report published by Taiwan’s Ministry of Labor, as much as 20% of patients that suffered from occupational injuries could have a late recovery of working ability after 2 years postinjury, and for those who did not have a job during the survey, over 10% of them had returned to work at some point after injury.31 Therefore, instead of relying on a single time frame to determine RTW, we also added a longitudinal income analysis on our study cohort to follow-up in the long term.

In our study, we identified that only the short-term outcomes of the index admission were associated with the possibility of non-RTW, rather than the specific types of injuries. Spinal cord injuries and head injuries demonstrated increased risks for non-RTW in univariate logistic regression but failed to show statistical significance in multivariate analysis. This may be a result of the fact that data from the NHIRD did not contain the abbreviated injury scale (AIS); therefore, the severity of each injury was unknown. The fact that short-term outcomes could predict the chances in non-RTW further signified that severity is the key prognostic factor for not returning to work, not just the diagnosis. Previous studies had revealed that ICU admission and longer LOS were associated with non-RTW, which were compatible with our results.26 27 HAP and UTI might belong to another entity. HAP and UTI are both common hospital-acquired infections and are largely associated with frailty.32 33 Frailty is often related to poor long-term outcomes for injured patients, including discharging to a skilled nursing facility, not returning to home and mortality.34 35 It is reasonable to assume that HAP and UTI were the presentation of frailty, which led to poor long-term outcome after injury, and the inevitable non-RTW.

On the other hand, torso injuries (abdomen, retroperitoneum and pelvis) are related to better chances of RTW. In our previous study, we demonstrated that these injuries are related to lower in-hospital mortality rates.1 Therefore, we postulated that injuries to these regions are more likely to be treatable, and eventually lead to a better chance for RTW.

The scale of the personal financial burden is another critical issue of our study. We showed that major trauma patients experienced a decrease from a preinjury mean monthly income of US$646.43 to US$592.86 at PIY 3; thereafter, the monthly income gradually returned to the preinjury level at PIY 9, reaching US$680.69. To understand the income level, we examined the wage level in Taiwan. In 2003, the beginning of the study period, the minimum wage was US$528 per month. After several increases in the minimum wage, the minimum wage increased to US$700 per month in 2017, the end of our study period.36 37 Compared with the income change in our study population, it is clear that our patients made a living around the minimum wage. This finding indicated that the majority of these major trauma patients had lower socioeconomic status. To alleviate medical expenses, copayment exemptions are granted for patients with most CICs in a term of 5 years. However, a CIC for major trauma is reserved for 1 year only. Judging from the findings in the current study, we suggest that the term for major trauma CIC should be extended to at least 3 years.

Moreover, although these major trauma patients had a steady increase in income, can this trend keep up with the inflation rate? To further assess this issue, we considered the consumer price index (CPI). The CPI can be used as a measure of the extent of inflation,38,40 and the average yearly CPI increment in Taiwan during the study period was approximately 1.21% per year.41 If we set preinjury income as a benchmark and adjust it with the inflation rate, the ideal income at PIY 10 should be at least US$729 per month. However, the mean monthly income for major trauma patients at PIY 10 is sitting at US$684.01. Therefore, despite the postinjury income starting to rise at PIY 3, the actual living circumstance might still get worse. It is obvious that the challenges faced by major trauma patients extend beyond their medical condition. An effective social safety net should play a role in such circumstances, and the medical system alone cannot provide enough support for the financial shortcomings of patients recovering from major trauma.

In addition to the NHI, labour insurance in Taiwan can provide some financial aid for injured labourers. Starting in 1950, labour insurance was the first social insurance in Taiwan.42 Regardless of injury or illness, labourers who suffered from permanent disabilities could acquire an amount of subsidy according to the level of disability. However, the pension was given in a one-time manner, and it had long been criticised for its resemblance to ‘limb exchange for money’. It might not be a significant concern for older labourers, and young disabled labourers with decades of life expectancy were not protected by this one-time pension.43,45 Thus, the Permanent Disability Benefits of Labor Insurance was established and provided annually in 2009.46 However, labourers working in small businesses or atypical employment might not be insured with labour insurance, and they were denied the Permanent Disability Benefits when injured. The Labor Occupational Accident Insurance and Protection Act was enacted in 2021 to fix this loophole.47 This act requires all labourers to be insured with the Occupational Accident Insurance.

Despite having seemingly various subsidies for injured labourers, the social welfare system in Taiwan still has room for improvement. Social expenditure in Taiwan in 2019 accounted for 11% of the gross domestic product (GDP).48 Moreover, the average social expenditure across the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development countries was 20% of their GDP.49 The development of a welfare nation needs time, but the growth of social expenditure in Taiwan might be somewhat retarded when compared with other countries. Social expenditure accounted for 9.2% of GDP in 2001 in Taiwan, but it remained at 9.5% in 2011, and it started a slow growth since 2012, reaching 11% of GDP in 2019. On the other hand, a nearby country, Korea, had only 4.4% of GDP for its social expenditure in 2000,50 but it grew rapidly and reached 7.9% of GDP in 2010, and it surpassed Taiwan in 2019, with social expenditure accounting for 12.2% of its GDP.51 Stakeholders need to find a solution to further expand the extent of social welfare to provide further support beyond the NHI.

There are several limitations to this study. The most significant limitation is that the NHIRD does not contain details regarding ISS and AIS. We could only use CIC as a proxy to limit our cohort to major trauma, but we could not conduct a more in-depth analysis on the potential influence on the severity and different injury regions. Additionally, it was not mandatory for hospitals to include the external diagnostic codes for injuries in the NHIRD. Therefore, we were unable to evaluate the effect of different injury causes. These drawbacks had impeded the potential efficacy of the NHIRD. However, from a public health perspective, our analysis still provides a general understanding of what happens after discharge from the index admission for major trauma patients. Regarding patient selection, major trauma patients were extracted based on the CIC, which is applied by clinical physicians for the patient and verified by the NHI. However, physicians might omit the application for CIC, so the exact patient volume could be more than we presented in this article. On the evaluation of income, the salary from atypical employment might not be included in the insurance amount, which could lead to an underestimation of income. Furthermore, the NHIRD does not include job type in its database, so we could not evaluate the interaction of occupation difference and postinjury RTW. Last, the patients who relied solely on atypical employment might be listed with no registered income; hence, the analysis of these patients could not be achieved, and they were not represented in the outcomes of this study.

Conclusions

For patients who experienced a major trauma, HAP, UTI, PICULOS and PLOS during the index admission were identified as independent risk factors for non-RTW, whereas age advancement, abdominal and retroperitoneal injuries, and pelvic injuries were negatively associated with the risk of non-RTW. The overall income trend for all major trauma patients presented a gradual decline in PIY 1–3, followed by a gradual increase afterward. Most importantly, the incomes of these patients were approximately equal to the minimum wage. Further welfare planning should be made to protect the financial shortcomings experienced by these patients to achieve more thorough holistic care.
