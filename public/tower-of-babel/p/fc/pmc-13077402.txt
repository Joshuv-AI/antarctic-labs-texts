A clinician-oriented machine learning model for adult asthma exacerbation prediction: comparative analysis of nine algorithms
Authors: Ning Zhang, Haiyan Chen, Congyi Xie, Jinzhan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma exacerbations significantly contribute to morbidity and healthcare burden, yet accurate risk prediction remains challenging. This study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning (ML)-based model to predict the exacerbation risk in adult asthma patients.MethodsThis study analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected between 2007 and 2012, comprising a cohort of 1,480 adult participants diagnosed with asthma. A total of 37 candidate features were assessed, and feature selection via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) identified seven key predictors. Nine ML models were developed and evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) to determine the optimal model. The best-performing model underwent further validation using calibration curves, precision-recall (PR) curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) was applied to interpret the model’s predictions.ResultsA light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) model showed the best predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.902 in the training set and comparable discrimination in the validation set. Model performance in the validation cohort showed consistent results across calibration analysis, PR curves, and DCA. Model interpretability was examined using SHAP and a web-based calculator was developed to support individualized risk estimation.ConclusionsThis ML-based model demonstrated strong predictive accuracy and could serve as a valuable tool for risk assessment of adult asthma in clinical practice.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease that affects approximately 300 million people worldwide and imposes a significant burden on healthcare systems (1). Acute exacerbations, marked by sudden deterioration in respiratory function, account for a large proportion of asthma-related hospitalizations and deaths (2). Identifying patients at risk of exacerbations at an early stage is critical to improving clinical outcomes and reducing the overall burden of the disease. Despite advances in understanding the pathophysiology of asthma, predicting when exacerbations will occur remains difficult (3,4). A predictive model capable of supporting clinical decision-making and enabling timely, targeted interventions could significantly improve patient management and long-term outcomes (5).

The risk of asthma exacerbations is determined by a complex set of interrelated factors encompassing demographic characteristics, ventilatory function, airway inflammation, medication patterns, comorbid conditions, systemic immune activity, and nutritional status. Variations in age, sex, race, and obesity have been consistently linked to differences in disease expression, treatment responsiveness, and long-term outcomes (6-9), reflecting their importance in shaping clinical trajectories. Functional indices such as peak expiratory flow (PEF), forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC), and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) offer important insights into airway inflammation and ventilatory impairment (10-12). The therapeutic use of glucocorticoids, whether administered via inhalation or systemically, plays a pivotal role in maintaining long-term symptom control and mitigating the risk of acute exacerbations (13,14). The presence of comorbidities, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), diabetes mellitus, and mental health disorders, has been shown to negatively affect asthma control and increase the likelihood of adverse events (15-19). Inflammatory markers derived from complete blood count parameters, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation response index (SIRI), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), have gained recognition as accessible indicators of immune dysregulation and clinical risk (20-22). Nutritional exposures also contribute meaningfully to asthma outcomes, with higher intakes of antioxidants such as vitamin C, vitamin E, β-carotene, magnesium, and zinc being associated with reduced oxidative stress and improved respiratory function, while diets rich in sodium, refined carbohydrates, and alcohol appear to worsen airway inflammation and compromise pulmonary capacity (23-25).

Traditional multivariable regression models, such as logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression, have shown limited predictive performance in asthma risk assessment. Existing studies indicate that models built on conventional regression methods often achieve an area under the curve (AUC) between 0.65 and 0.75, reflecting suboptimal sensitivity and specificity for accurately identifying exacerbation events (26-28). With advances in artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML) has increasingly been applied to improve disease prediction and risk stratification (29,30). Several studies have demonstrated that ML-based models outperform traditional regression approaches, with improvements in predictive accuracy ranging from 5% to 15% and reported AUCs reaching 0.88 to 0.94 (31-33). Specifically, a ML-based framework has been developed to identify and characterise asthma subgroups at high risk of severe exacerbations using longitudinal real-world data, incorporating multidimensional clinical features and temporal disease trajectories (31). A ML-based approach has been used to predict severe exacerbations in adult-onset asthma by integrating demographic characteristics, prior exacerbation history, type 2 inflammatory biomarkers, and pulmonary function measures (32). Turcatel et al. (33) further expanded the scope of features by combining diagnostic codes, laboratory abnormalities, vital signs, medication history, vaccination records, and allergy profiles.

Although ML algorithms have shown considerable promise in predicting asthma exacerbations, existing studies remain constrained by several methodological limitations. Many have relied on a single modeling approach, incorporated a narrow spectrum of candidate features, or depended on complex and resource-intensive monitoring parameters that limit clinical applicability. This study developed and validated an ML-based model for adult asthma exacerbations by integrating systemic inflammatory markers, dietary factors, and clinical variables, aiming to support a practical, accessible, and effective tool for early risk identification and personalized management of asthma. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2420/rc).

Methods

Data source and study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is an ongoing, nationally representative survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Employing a complex, multistage probability sampling design, NHANES selects approximately 10,000 participants every 2 years from 15 counties across the United States to ensure representation of the non-institutionalized civilian population (34). Data collection involves structured interviews, standardized physical examinations, and laboratory-based assessments performed at Mobile Examination Centers, all conducted under rigorous quality control protocols. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The survey protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board (ERB) (Nos. 2005-06 and 2011-17), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection.

This cohort study was based on the asthma population from the 2007–2012 cycles of NHANES. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (I) age below 18 years; (II) presence of an ongoing asthma exacerbation; or (III) incomplete information on asthma exacerbation status.

Definition of asthma and outcome

Asthma status was determined using self-reported physician diagnosis from the NHANES Medical Conditions Questionnaire, in which participants answered the standardized question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have asthma?” Previous studies have validated the reliability and epidemiological applicability of self-reported asthma diagnoses in NHANES, demonstrating high concordance with clinical records and national health surveys (35-37).

The outcome was the incidence of asthma exacerbation within a 12-month period, identified through participants’ self-reported answers to the standardized survey item: “During the past 12 months, have you had an episode of asthma or an asthma attack?” Information was obtained via structured, in-person interviews carried out by trained interviewers, utilizing a uniform questionnaire administered either during household visits or at designated mobile examination units.

Candidate features

Building upon clinical expertise and current research advances, this study systematically identified candidate features across several domains, including demographic variables, pulmonary function and airway inflammation parameters, comorbid conditions, systemic inflammatory biomarkers, and indicators of dietary nutritional status, to ensure comprehensive coverage of potential factors influencing asthma exacerbations and mortality (6–25). A total of 37 candidate features were ultimately selected, comprising age (years), gender, race, poverty income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), and smoking status; FVC/FEV1, PEF (mL/s), and FeNO (ppb); serum sodium (mmol/L) and serum uric acid (µmol/L); glucocorticoid use, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, chronic heart failure (CHF), renal impairment, depression, stroke, and malignancy; peripheral eosinophil counts (103/µL), SIRI, SII, NLR, and PLR; as well as daily intake of saturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids (g), cholesterol (mg), sodium (mg), magnesium (mg), zinc (mg), vitamin A (µg), vitamin C (mg), vitamin E (mg), coffee (mg), and alcohol (g) consumption (Table S1).

Model development and evaluation

This study applied nine widely used ML algorithms, including decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), categorical boosting (CatBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), support vector machine (SVM), Naïve Bayes (NB), and logistic regression (LR), to develop prediction models for asthma exacerbation within 1 year. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7:3, with the training set used for model development and the validation set reserved for independent performance evaluation. Missing data of less than 20% (see Table S2) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. Predictive mean matching was applied to continuous variables, and logistic regression was used for binary variables. The fitted imputation models were subsequently applied to the validation set to prevent information leakage. Given the relatively balanced outcome distribution, no explicit resampling strategy was applied. Feature selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation embedded within the training set to prevent information leakage. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using randomized search combined with repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation to determine optimal parameter settings for each algorithm (Table S3).

Model performance was evaluated separately in the training and validation sets using multiple metrics, including AUC, accuracy, recall, precision, specificity, F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) (38). Sensitivity analysis was performed using repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 1,000 repetitions. The final model was selected based on the highest mean AUC obtained from cross-validation within the training set and subsequently retrained on the entire training cohort. Predictive discrimination in the validation set was quantified by AUC with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Model calibration was assessed using calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope, with the Brier score and mean absolute error (MAE) reported as complementary measures of overall probabilistic prediction error, based on non-resampled validation predictions (38). Precision-recall (PR) curves were constructed to further characterize performance, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the model across a range of risk thresholds (39,40). Robustness of performance estimates was assessed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations.

Model interpretation

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) was applied to explain the predictions of the optimal model. Global SHAP values were visualized using bar charts and Beeswarm plots to represent the average importance of each feature, while waterfall plots was used to analyze local predictions within the optimal model.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses accounted for the complex sampling design of NHANES to ensure national representativeness. Weighted analyses were conducted using the NHANES examination sample weights (WTMEC2YR) for the 2007–2012 survey cycles, which were combined according to NHANES analytic guidelines by dividing the 2-year weights by the number of cycles included. Application of NHANES-provided sample weights, together with stratification and primary sampling unit variables, ensured that the estimates were representative of the population.

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (see Table S4). Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a non-normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range; IQR). For statistical analysis, continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization prior to LASSO-based feature selection. Student’s t-test was employed to compare normally distributed continuous variables, whereas the Mann-Whitney U-test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages), and group comparisons were conducted using the Chi-squared test. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 and Python version 3.10.6 (Table S5). Analyses were conducted through DecisionLinnc version 1.1.5.9, a visual platform that integrates R and Python environments to facilitate data processing, statistical modeling, and ML applications (41).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study population

Figure 1 provides a visual representation of the participant selection process and overall study framework. A total of 4,110 participants with asthma were initially identified. After applying predefined exclusion criteria, 1,480 participants were included in the final analysis. The baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Compared with the control group, participants in the exacerbation group were younger [median age 47 (IQR, 32–60) vs. 51 (IQR, 31–65) years, P=0.004], more likely to be female (68% vs. 57%, P<0.001), had a lower PIR [1.20 (IQR, 0.70–2.56) vs. 1.38 (IQR, 0.76–3.16), P=0.004], and exhibited a higher BMI [30.55 (IQR, 25.10–35.97) vs. 29.39 (IQR, 25.20–34.63) kg/m2, P=0.02]. Key feature selection

LASSO regression was applied to the training set for automated feature selection (Figure 2). By tuning the regularization coefficient, lambda (λ), LASSO regression minimized the binomial deviance loss function, effectively shrinking the coefficients of certain variables to zero. To examine relationships among clinical characteristics, a correlation matrix was constructed based on calculated correlation coefficients. Among the 37 candidate features, seven were identified as the most predictive for inclusion in the ML model at lambda.1se =−3.473. These features included age, gender, BMI, eosinophil count, FeNO, SIRI, and vitamin C intake.

A web-based calculator

An online calculator was developed to estimate the one-year risk of asthma exacerbation based on the final predictive model (https://fast.statsape.com/tool/detail?id=13). The interface includes a module for entering patient-specific variables, a results panel displaying individualized risk estimates, and an informational section describing the model framework and its clinical context. To demonstrate model application, a worked example was generated using the calculator (Figure 6). A 46-year-old male patient (sex coded as 1= male) with a SIRI of 3.3, an eosinophil count of 0.5×103/µL, a daily vitamin C intake of 526 mg, a BMI of 27 kg/m2, and a FeNO level of 19 ppb was entered. Based on these inputs, the model predicted a 1-year asthma exacerbation risk of 62.2%. The calculator provides a standardized framework for individualized risk estimation, supporting the potential integration of model-based risk stratification into routine asthma management.

Discussion

Acute exacerbations are the leading cause of asthma-related hospitalizations and mortality, characterized by sudden symptom worsening (1). Accurately identifying patients at risk of acute exacerbations remains a major challenge in adult asthma management. Clinical features readily available in routine practice provide a practical foundation for early risk stratification without imposing additional clinical burden. In a large real-world cohort, Kallis et al. developed a predictive model based on conventional clinical variables and achieved moderate discrimination for exacerbation risk (AUC =0.72) (42). Xiong et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrating that models utilizing standard clinical parameters perform well across diverse populations, with pooled AUCs nearing 0.80 (4).

This study innovatively integrates a broader spectrum of risk factors for asthma exacerbations by incorporating novel inflammatory biomarkers and dietary components alongside conventional clinical variables, thereby providing a more comprehensive framework for disease prediction. The LightGBM model demonstrated strong predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.905 (95% CI: 0.835–0.975) alongside high accuracy, recall, F1-score, specificity, and precision. Compared to previous ML models, our model exhibited superior discriminative ability. The inclusion of emerging inflammatory and dietary predictors, beyond conventional clinical variables, likely contributed to this improvement.

We identified age, gender, BMI, blood eosinophil count, FeNO, SIRI, and dietary vitamin C intake as independent predictors of asthma exacerbations. Previous studies had highlighted the critical role of these factors in disease outcomes. Elderly patients exhibit distinct clinical characteristics and risk profiles compared to younger individuals, underscoring the need for age-specific management strategies (6). Gender-related differences have also been recognized, with women demonstrating a higher susceptibility to severe asthma and exacerbations, independent of baseline lung function, potentially influenced by hormonal fluctuations such as menstruation and menopause (7). Obesity has emerged as a major modifiable risk factor, strongly associated with poor asthma control and increased exacerbation frequency (8). Biomarkers such as eosinophil counts and FeNO are closely linked to disease severity and exacerbation risk, and their combined assessment improves disease monitoring and guides targeted therapy (11,12). Additionally, emerging inflammatory markers, including NLR, PLR, SII, and SIRI, have been identified as predictors of increased exacerbation risk and higher mortality in asthma populations (20,43). Finally, dietary and metabolic factors also influence asthma outcomes, with antioxidants such as vitamin C, vitamin E, and β-carotene contributing to the attenuation of oxidative stress and airway inflammation, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbations (23).

Additionally, SHAP importance analysis, Beeswarm plots, and waterfall plots enhanced the interpretability of our model by providing both individualized and global explanations of prediction outcomes, offering clinicians clearer insights into the model’s decision-making processes. Elevated BMI, FeNO, SIRI, and eosinophil counts emerged as key risk factors for exacerbations, whereas younger age, higher vitamin C intake, and male were associated with a lower risk. Originally developed to unify disparate interpretability methods, SHAP has demonstrated strong performance across complex ML models, including XGBoost and deep learning architectures (44). Its adoption in clinical prediction tasks has further confirmed its value in improving model transparency and highlighting critical predictive features (45). These capabilities are particularly important in healthcare settings, where explainability, individualized risk assessment, and clinician trust are essential for integrating ML models into routine practice.

This study has several limitations. While the large, nationally representative NHANES sample supports internal validity for the U.S. population, the model’s generalizability to other asthma populations requires further validation. Although a web-based calculator was developed, both the specific risk thresholds and their clinical effectiveness, need confirmation in prospective cohort studies. Therefore, future research should include independent validation cohorts and prospective multicenter studies to evaluate their impact on clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

This ML-based model demonstrated strong predictive performance and offers potential as a practical tool for early risk stratification and personalized management of adult asthma.
