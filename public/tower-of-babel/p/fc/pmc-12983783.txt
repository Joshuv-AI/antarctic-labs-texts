From fragmentation to integration: can social prescribing bridge the gap between multimorbidity and mental health in an ageing population?
Authors: Daniel E Radford-Smith, Daniel C Anthony
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults frequently experience depression alongside physical multimorbidity, with mutual reinforcement through biological, behavioural and social mechanisms. Traditional models of care often address these conditions in isolation, resulting in fragmented services poorly suited to the complex realities of ageing. Social prescribing has emerged in the UK as a potential solution, offering structured referrals from healthcare to community-based resources. While its theoretical promise aligns well with the needs of older adults, the evidence base remains limited, and current implementation strategies often fail to match individuals to interventions effectively. This narrative review explores how social prescribing might evolve to bridge the gap between mental health and multimorbidity in later life. Using depression and metabolic syndrome as a case example, we examine shared inflammatory mechanisms, barriers to behavioural change and the risks of individualising structural disadvantage. We propose a future framework in which social prescribing is supported by complexity-sensitive evaluation tools, including patient-reported outcome measures, biomarker-informed stratification and artificial intelligence-driven narrative assessment. We also highlight the critical role of integrated care systems in developing the community infrastructure necessary to sustain these interventions equitably. By reframing social prescribing as part of a broader public health strategy rooted in integration and prevention, we argue it can move beyond symbolic appeal to become a meaningful component of multimorbidity care in older adults.Keywords: Depression, Preventive Psychiatry, Preventive Medicine, Social Medicine, Community Health Planning

Introduction

Depression in later life commonly co-occurs with cognitive impairment, physical disability and medical multimorbidity.1 At least 5%–10% of adults over 60 in community settings experience clinically significant depressive symptoms, with prevalence increasing markedly alongside overall disease burden.2 3 The relationship is bidirectional: chronic illness increases risk of depression,4 while depression predicts poorer medical outcomes and mortality.4 These patterns challenge single-disease paradigms in which concurrent conditions are relegated to secondary ‘comorbidities’, because additive guidelines-based management often produces complex and poorly tolerated regimens in older adults.5 Depression itself is clinically heterogeneous, spanning persistent early-onset forms associated with stress reactivity,6 personality7 and genetic predisposition,8 and later-onset presentations more closely linked to systemic physiological processes, including inflammation and age-related vascular change.9 A multimorbidity framework, which places psychiatric and physical conditions on equal footing, provides a more realistic basis for delivering integrated, person-centred care in ageing populations.10

The challenge of delivering care aligned with a multimorbidity framework has catalysed the emergence of social prescribing as a key national health strategy.11 Social prescribing is defined as a structured mechanism through which healthcare providers connect individuals with non-clinical community resources via specialised link workers12. In practice, this might involve connecting an older adult with a local walking group to improve mobility, a bereavement support service, or a community kitchen to reduce isolation and nutritional risk – interventions that sit outside traditional clinical care but directly impact health and well-being.

Despite social prescribing’s theoretical capacity to interrupt the cycle of mental and physical deterioration among older adults, its potential is significantly undermined by an underdeveloped evidence base and evaluation frameworks that fail to capture clinical complexity. At the same time, fragmented services often leave older individuals responsible for navigating their own care, effectively shifting the burden of managing multimorbidity onto those least equipped to do so. For social prescribing to transition from an aspiration to a meaningful component of integrated geriatric care, its implementation must be guided by a deeper understanding of the clinical realities it aims to address. Using the example of depression and metabolic syndrome as a prevalent and consequential form of interacting multimorbidity, this narrative review examines the structural and evaluative limitations currently constraining social prescribing and outlines conceptual and practical shifts required to improve its effectiveness. These include the development of complexity-sensitive outcome measures, such as artificial intelligence (AI)-driven narrative assessment and integrated biomarker approaches, and the role of integrated care systems (ICSs) in building community infrastructures that support joined-up, age-friendly care.

Approach and scope of the review

This article is a narrative, theory-informed review of social prescribing in the context of mental and physical multimorbidity in later life, using depression and metabolic syndrome as an illustrative example. A narrative approach was chosen to integrate heterogeneous evidence spanning biological mechanisms, behavioural science, health services research and public health policy, where formal synthesis is constrained by variability in design, outcomes and context. Literature was identified through iterative searches of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar (English-language, primarily from 2000 onwards), alongside targeted review of UK policy and commissioning documents. Sources were selected purposively to capture influential empirical, conceptual and policy work relevant to social prescribing, ageing, multimorbidity and evaluation.

The review is anchored in the UK because social prescribing is most institutionalised within NHS policy, workforce roles and commissioning structures, enabling system-level analysis. Comparable models exist internationally under different labels (eg, social needs navigation and primary care–community linkage), and the principles advanced here (multimorbidity-oriented care, equity-sensitive evaluation, community capacity building and integration of biological, behavioural and social data) are intended to be transferable, while elements tied to NHS-specific infrastructures (eg, ICSs and national digital health platforms) are discussed as context-dependent.

Interplay between depression and metabolic syndrome in older adults

Inflammation, overlapping pathophysiology and the cycle of decline

Far from an accumulation of distinct “health problems”, multimorbidity in older adults is deeply intertwined with mental health, creating a complex and self-perpetuating cycle of decline. Shared risk factors, overlapping pathophysiology and polypharmacy all underscore the necessity of integrated specialist services over fragmented care organised solely by diagnosis.13 Among the clearest examples illustrating this complexity is the association between depression and metabolic syndrome, a cluster of conditions, including central obesity, insulin resistance and hypertension. A large-scale UK primary care study of over 1.7 million patients found that individuals with depression were significantly more likely to have a wide range of physical health conditions, with markedly higher odds of having multiple concurrent illnesses.14 A meta-analysis also found that obese individuals had a 55% increased risk of developing depression over time, while depressed persons were 58% more likely to become obese.15 This reinforces the presence of a dynamic, bidirectional relationship between diagnoses.

Despite such strong clinical associations between older age, multimorbidity and depression, accurate, contemporary statistics on depression prevalence in older adults remain limited, with even recent UK reports13 relying on historical data, potentially due to inconsistent age cut-offs, variable diagnostic approaches or depression being masked by or conflated with cognitive impairment and dementia. Furthermore, stigma, ageism and systemic bias frequently contribute to underdiagnosis and inadequate treatment of depression in older populations.16

Late-onset depression is especially relevant, as it is more often accompanied by greater medical morbidity and systemic inflammation.1 The biological basis of this mind-body connection is now widely understood to involve chronic, low-grade inflammation.17 Ageing promotes “inflammageing”,18 marked by elevated circulating cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)−6, IL-1β and tumour necrosis factor,19 which contribute to insulin resistance20 and dysregulated lipid metabolism, raising the risk of metabolic syndrome.21 These same inflammatory processes are implicated in the pathogenesis of late-life depression.22 Even after adjusting for behavioural and clinical covariates, including body mass index (BMI), smoking, socioeconomic status, early-life trauma and overall health, data from the UK Biobank show that C-reactive protein (CRP) levels remain significantly elevated in individuals with depression.23 While this suggests systemic inflammation may be biologically embedded in later-life depression,24 accompanying genetic data also show that genetic risk associated with elevated CRP levels is mediated primarily by BMI, rather than by immune gene pleiotropy.23 This aligns with Mendelian randomisation studies25 26 showing that elevated CRP is more likely a consequence than a cause of depression and is consistent with inflammation acting as a final common pathway, shaped by cumulative psychosocial and metabolic stressors, that contributes to the pathophysiology of depression.

When mental and physical conditions intersect, standard models of care often struggle to support effective self-management. The anhedonia, fatigue and amotivation characteristic of depression directly impair an individual’s ability to adhere to a medical regimen, particularly those involving the lifestyle adjustments crucial for managing metabolic syndrome.27 The resulting poor diet, glycaemic control and weight gain can, in turn, exacerbate the underlying inflammatory processes that contribute to depression. Poor mental health is a significant barrier to engagement with behavioural interventions; baseline depressive symptoms in older, frail adults have been shown to predict lower adherence to, and success in, weight loss efforts.28 The cumulative result is a cycle of deteriorating physical health, worsening mental health, accelerated cognitive decline and reduced capacity for self-management – one that siloed clinical services, focused on treating isolated conditions, are poorly equipped to address.29 Yet emerging evidence suggests that improving social connection can help reverse aspects of this decline: whereas depression increases the risk of non-adherence to treatment,27 greater satisfaction with social relationships has been associated with improved adherence to medications for affective disorders.30 Social prescribing may therefore address these inseparable biological, behavioural and social drivers of multimorbidity in later life.

Social prescribing as a holistic response to complex needs in older adults

It is in response to this growing burden of multimorbidity, mental illness and social inequality31 in the UK that social prescribing has been positioned as a potential solution. Longitudinal evidence underscores the scale and social patterning of this challenge. In a large cohort of adults aged 51 years and older, the proportion living with five or more long-term conditions in 2021 was almost twice as high in the most deprived decile compared with the least deprived, and individuals in the least deprived areas were around 40% less likely to progress from a single condition to complex multimorbidity over a 5 year period.32 Parallel trends are evident in mental health, with nationwide spatial analyses showing a sustained rise in recorded depression between 2011 and 2022, widening inequalities between areas, and a strong influence of neighbourhood deprivation on where this burden concentrates.33 Together, these data point to an increasingly intertwined accumulation of physical illness, mental distress and social disadvantage, particularly in later life, for which purely biomedical responses are poorly matched. The need for alternative, non-pharmacological approaches is particularly acute in late-life depression – treatment decisions for pharmacotherapy are often guided by data extrapolated from younger adults, and both older age and co-existing cognitive impairment independently predict poorer responses to standard antidepressants.34 By targeting modifiable social and behavioural determinants of health that underpin both mental and physical illness, social prescribing offers a practical means of addressing root causes that are often overlooked in clinical consultations.

This allows social prescribing to also act as a vital tool for primary prevention.35 Given the shared mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, targeting lifestyle and social factors may not just treat disease but prevent its progression.16 36 When coupled with the proactive identification of at-risk older individuals, this preventative potential is further enhanced. For example, in an older person with type 2 diabetes and sub-clinical depression, a successful social prescription that improves diet, physical activity and mood could prevent further morbidity, such as the progression of metabolic syndrome to advanced liver disease.37

Growing evidence supports individual components of social prescribing, particularly activities that promote connection, creativity and physical activity. Large-scale longitudinal research from Japan, for instance, has demonstrated that active engagement in art and cultural activities is inversely associated with the onset of depressive symptoms in older adults.38 Similarly, a major meta-analysis confirmed that having a hobby is linked to fewer depressive symptoms and higher life satisfaction across populations.39 For example, a referral to a green-prescribing walking group after a cardiac event may provide a supportive, low-stress environment to rebuild physical confidence and reduce anxiety.40 UK-based studies have shown the tangible benefits of this approach, with one museum-based intervention improving psychological well-being in adults aged 65–94.41

However, the evidence base for social prescribing as a comprehensive intervention remains limited, particularly for older adults with co-occurring mental and physical health conditions. A major systematic review by Kiely et al found that, across eight trials, social prescribing link workers had no consistent effect on quality of life or mental health outcomes.42 Even when benefits are observed, sustainability is threatened by underinvestment in the voluntary sector. Many schemes rely on short-term or grant-based funding, with smaller providers struggling to maintain services at scale. A recurring barrier identified across multiple reviews is the challenge of appropriately matching individuals to interventions that are relevant, acceptable and feasible for the individual.43,45 Without this, uptake and impact remain limited. Future models of social prescribing must therefore be informed by behavioural theory, adapted to local need, and accompanied by robust process evaluation.

Rebuilding the evidence base: from pragmatic tools to precision approaches

Redesigning metrics for multimorbidity

Despite the theoretical promise of social prescribing, its translation into a robust, evidence-based intervention remains constrained by poor measurement and limited evaluative frameworks.46 Evaluation in older adults is hindered by small samples, inconsistent methods and unstandardised outcome measures, which limit the strength of conclusions drawn.47 Historical frameworks tend to focus on system-level outputs, such as reductions in general practitioner (GP) appointments and visits to the emergency department (A&E). These indicators poorly capture outcomes that matter to older adults, such as care coordination, self-management or reduced isolation,46 and may even penalise appropriate healthcare use.

Evaluation of social prescribing models must be person-centred, prioritising patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs),48 yet also standardised to allow for consistent outcome reporting over time.49 PROMs designed specifically for older adults, including the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental well-being Scale, can help capture important dimensions such as maintenance of functional ability alongside psychosocial well-being.50 Embedding a comprehensive yet standardised set of metrics into NHS commissioning frameworks could incentivise more relevant data collection.

Beyond measurement, early identification also remains a critical challenge. Most social prescribing referrals occur reactively – only after someone presents to primary care, often in crisis. This delays intervention and limits impact.16 A more proactive model could enable earlier risk identification. Additionally, underdiagnosis of mental ill-health in this population is common, and late intervention limits the potential for upstream prevention.51 The Fire and Rescue Service, whose staff are trusted visitors to the homes of older adults, has been explored as a potential early detection and referral partner.52 Similarly, predictive tools using clinical data could identify individuals with patterns of instability who are most likely to benefit from targeted preventative support.53 These approaches would help shift the system from reactive triage to anticipatory care.

Digital foundations: making the NHS app work for older adults

The NHS’s strategic goal of creating a single, integrated patient record, accessible via an enhanced NHS app, provides the ideal platform for a more dynamic evaluation model.54 55 This presents a real opportunity to track meaningful outcomes over time if designed with the needs of older adults in mind. The app could be expanded to collect regular PROMs related to mood, loneliness and perceived quality of life, offering a more dynamic view of mental and social well-being.56 57

The True Colours platform – developed at the University of Oxford and already integrated across mental health58 and inflammatory bowel disease59 (IBD) services – demonstrates the feasibility and value of longitudinal PROM monitoring. It enables real-time symptom tracking with high patient compliance60 and provides a patient-facing portal that supports shared decision-making. In IBD, qualitative work suggests that remote monitoring can improve perceived agency and support proactive self-management,59 and in mental health, it can enhance continuity between appointments while providing clinicians with more granular symptom trajectories.58 For older patients, analogous functionality within the NHS app could help tailor social prescriptions to current need, provided that usability and accessibility barriers are addressed.

Accessibility for older patients and those with disabilities may include intuitive user interfaces, voice-command functionality and opt-in support from carers or link workers. Privacy protections must also be robust, especially if passive monitoring is involved. Yet with the right design, the NHS app could offer a scalable tool for real-world evaluation and patient empowerment.

Smarter stratification: using AI to tailor and evaluate social prescribing

Once such rich data streams are available, there is a need for tools to interpret them. Natural Language Processing (NLP), a branch of AI, can analyse unstructured text, such as patient feedback, link worker notes or free-text entries in PROMs. These narratives may help reveal a deeper context of a patient’s mental health struggles, for example, chronic pain, bereavement, isolation or loss of purpose. NLP is already being explored for detecting depression,61 including in older adults,62 and may support triage and depression subtype classification to guide more precise social prescriptions. A person expressing fatigue or pain might be directed to a pain-management group; another conveying grief might benefit more from peer support or creative therapies. AI could also estimate an individual’s readiness to engage, a key predictor of social prescribing success.63

Integrating AI into primary care workflows could help overcome the perceived time burden of social prescribing.64 Generative AI tools like ChatGPT are already used to generate clinical summaries from unstructured patient notes, though further research is required to determine whether this enhances or hampers clinical decision making while reducing workload.65 AI-driven triage could pre-process patient data and suggest suitable referrals, helping to personalise care while reducing pressure on appointments. In this way, AI could enhance targeting, equity and efficiency, all prerequisites for wider GP adoption.66

Looking to the future: integrating biomarkers for personalised, preventative care

In the future, integrating biological markers with psychosocial data may strengthen early detection and evaluation of multimorbidity risk, supporting more targeted social prescribing. This is particularly crucial in older adults, where depression and related neuropsychiatric symptoms are underdiagnosed in primary care, especially in the context of chronic illness, where symptoms may overlap,67 68 and where reliance on self-report measures with limited sensitivity and specificity can delay timely intervention.69 Metabolomic biomarkers, which reflect systemic health, immune tone and energy metabolism, may help stratify phenotypes, predict response to lifestyle change and track biological effects of non-pharmacological interventions, including shifts in inflammatory or metabolic stress pathways.70 Such stratification could help distinguish, for example, presentations dominated by metabolic dysregulation from those more strongly shaped by bereavement or isolation, enabling more clinically meaningful tailoring of support.71 72

The NHS is already investing in predictive analytics, illustrated by the Foresight project, which models disease risk from large-scale health records.73 Future iterations aim to integrate multiple data sources – from blood tests to imaging and clinical notes74 – accelerating the shift from reactive to anticipatory care. In research settings, combining blood biomarkers with psychosocial variables has predicted future depression and highlighted metabolic correlates of resilience.4 While these findings require replication in clinical and community contexts, early trials suggest that biomarker feedback is acceptable to older adults and may even increase engagement in lifestyle interventions.75 76

Still, this integrative model is not without risks. A biomarker for stress does not negate the structural or social cause of that stress, and there is a danger of over-medicalising distress or shifting responsibility onto the individual. If used responsibly, however, biomarkers can offer individuals a sense of agency by enabling them to understand and track their own health trajectories,77 and to validate the personal impact of lifestyle and social interventions. In doing so, biomarkers may not only help guide clinical decision-making but also support individual self-management. This integrated approach would not only validate social prescribing as a clinical intervention but also help build the case for its long-term sustainable funding, provided it is implemented in ways that enhance equity and access.

Beyond the prescription: embedding social care into the system

The risk of individualising the challenges of ageing

The growing enthusiasm for social prescribing risks obscuring a critical truth: without structural reform, it offers only a limited and temporary response to complex social realities. The very notion of ‘prescribing’ social activity or connection risks implying that these deficits are individual failings, rather than the result of long-standing disinvestment in social infrastructure.78 It is a seductive idea, especially in a system under pressure, to believe that loneliness, inequality and poor mental health can be resolved through time-limited referrals to community-based activities. Yet this framing distracts from the deeper determinants of ill health, including poverty, housing insecurity and cuts to social care.79 In doing so, it shifts the burden of action onto those least equipped to respond.

Social prescribing, in its current form, risks individualising structural disadvantage, placing responsibility on older adults to navigate fragmented services and overcome systemic barriers with little more than signposting of services.79 Rather than mitigating inequalities, it may inadvertently reinforce them. Epidemiological evidence demonstrates that engagement in cultural and community activities, often used as a proxy for community-based well-being interventions, is socially patterned, with participation concentrated among wealthier older adults, while depression disproportionately affects those with fewer resources.80 Longitudinal data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing show that this mismatch has persisted and worsened over time: despite similar or greater levels of depressive symptoms, older adults in lower wealth groups consistently accessed substantially fewer community and cultural activities than their more affluent peers. When participation was adjusted for mental health need, inequitable access increased over a decade, indicating that community-based well-being opportunities have become progressively less aligned with where depressive burden is greatest. Moreover, lower engagement with social prescribing has been observed in socioeconomically deprived areas (42% of patients referred for social prescribing were from the most socioeconomically deprived half of areas),81 82 raising concerns about its equity, reach and relevance. This is of particular importance given the clear evidence that both multimorbidity accumulation and depression burden are increasingly concentrated in socioeconomically deprived areas.32 33 Without adequate infrastructure, social prescribing risks reinforcing this divide by offering solutions that are only accessible to those with the time, money and social capital to participate.

These disparities are compounded by intersecting forms of exclusion. Cultural and linguistic barriers may prevent an older person from a minority ethnic background from participating in predominantly white, English-speaking groups, as shown in studies involving underserved populations such as Pakistani carers in the UK.83 In recognition of this, efforts are now underway to co-design culturally responsive services.84 Without meaningful attention to these systemic and structural issues, social prescribing risks becoming a symbolic gesture – well-intentioned but ultimately ineffective.

The ICS as the architect of an age-friendly ecosystem

If the problem is systemic, the response must be systemic. ICSs were established to strengthen “joined-up care” by aligning NHS bodies, local authorities and voluntary, community and social enterprise (VCSE) partners around prevention and population health,85 a priority given the costs of multimorbidity.86 An ICS is a collective responsibility for the health and resources of a geographical area. Nationwide spatial analyses of recorded depression prevalence illustrate why population-level coordination through ICSs is essential.33 Longitudinal mapping demonstrates marked geographic concentration and acceleration of depression burden over time, most prominently in the North West of England, where areas with persistently high prevalence occupy approximately 17% of the regional land area and more than 40% of localities exhibit rapidly increasing rates. Across England, neighbourhood-level deprivation accounts for a substantial share of observed variation in depression prevalence, explaining up to 39% of recorded burden in some areas. These findings underline the limitations of uniformly applied, individual-level interventions and support the case for geographically targeted commissioning of community, preventive and social care resources.

For social prescribing to succeed, ICSs must function as the architect of an age-friendly ecosystem, moving beyond simply commissioning link workers to actively cultivating the community infrastructure that makes referrals meaningful. A key responsibility of the ICS is to provide sustainable, long-term funding for the community delivery infrastructure, namely the network of VCSE organisations that underpin social prescribing but remain inadequately supported. This requires a fundamental shift in commissioning, away from short-term, competitive contracts and towards investment that builds community capacity.48 As evidence from the US context argues, enacting a policy is not enough; success requires dedicated funding for the staffing, infrastructure and resources needed for effective integration.87 While this might seem financially ambitious, pilot data from the Ways to Wellness social prescribing programme in Newcastle demonstrate the long-term savings that can result from such investment. Targeting people with long-term conditions in a socioeconomically deprived area, the scheme reduced NHS secondary care spending by up to 51% in those who actively engaged. When scaled, this equated to a £2.4 million annual saving.88 This shows that robust social prescribing is not a sunk cost, but a strategic investment in high-need populations. In practice, this implies ensuring a diverse range of accessible, age-appropriate and culturally competent services exist. For the older person with frailty and multimorbidity, successful personalised care planning depends on well-trained staff, strong primary care engagement and overcoming financial barriers, all of which are within the core remit of the ICS.89 By investing in community transport, digital inclusion initiatives and specialised groups that cater to people with physical and cognitive limitations, the ICS can dismantle the structural barriers to equitable social prescriptions. This systemic investment is not an act of charity, but an exercise in public health common sense. It is the most effective long-term strategy to empower older adults, break the cycle of multimorbidity and mental distress, and ultimately reduce the immense pressure on acute and emergency services.

Limitations and implementation challenges

This review argues that social prescribing could become a more credible component of multimorbidity care in later life if it is supported by stronger evaluation and systems infrastructure, but several key challenges to implementation remain. The prospect of AI-supported patient stratification and narrative analysis is heavily contingent on the quality of training data and appropriate governance. Algorithms trained on routine clinical records, free text or feedback data will inherit the biases of those sources, including under-recognition of depression in older adults, differential help-seeking and socially patterned diagnostic and referral practices.16 51 61 90 In this context, “efficiency” gains risk becoming a mechanised form of inequity unless AI is implemented with transparent governance, clear accountability and explicit auditing for representativeness and disparate impact, particularly for marginalised groups.59 64 65 The practical implication is that AI should be framed as decision support rather than decision making, with human oversight retained and evaluation extending beyond predictive performance to include fairness, accountability and evidence that model outputs remain interpretable and equitable at the level of individual patients across intersecting ethnic and sociodemographic contexts, rather than only at the cohort level.63,65

Similarly, although digital PROM collection via the NHS app offers a plausible route to longitudinal outcome monitoring, digital-first evaluation risks excluding precisely those older adults most affected by multimorbidity. Cognitive impairment, depressive amotivation, sensory disability and limited digital literacy may reduce engagement and generate systematic missingness, producing datasets biased towards healthier or more advantaged participants.53 55 57 Without explicit mitigation, such approaches risk preferentially capturing outcomes from those already better resourced, thereby limiting both equity and external validity. The feasibility signals from platforms such as True Colours are encouraging, but they also highlight the need for pragmatic, supported alternatives in older populations, including assisted completion with link workers, carer-supported reporting where appropriate and parallel non-digital routes so that evaluation infrastructure does not widen inequalities by design.58

While the review emphasises the role of biomarkers and data integration to strengthen causal inference and evaluation of complex illnesses, the risk of over-medicalising structurally produced distress remains real. Biomarker feedback may support engagement and provide objective indices of physiological change,73 75 but biomarkers cannot adjudicate the social origins of distress, and there is a danger that the rhetoric of “precision” shifts responsibility onto individuals to optimise behaviours in environments shaped by poverty, housing insecurity and eroded social care. If not carefully framed, increasingly granular stratification may therefore individualise risk without expanding the range of meaningful responses available. A more defensible framing is therefore that biological and digital tools should support, not substitute for, upstream action. In practice, this requires explicit linkage to an agenda in which local government, transport, housing, community safety and social care are recognised as co-producers of the conditions that make social connection and behavioural change feasible.78

Additionally, the feasibility of the ICS-led model must be acknowledged within a system experiencing workforce shortages, financial constraints and organisational change. While ICSs are designed to coordinate NHS bodies, local authorities and VCSE partners towards joined-up care,85 the supply side of social prescribing remains fragile. Evidence consistently points to the difficulty of matching individuals to relevant, acceptable and feasible interventions, and to the vulnerability of community provision under short-term or grant-based funding.43 There is therefore a real risk that increasingly precise identification of need may outpace the capacity, diversity and stability of available community-based resources. Community pharmacies have been proposed as a potential access point for social prescribing, reflecting their high street presence, frequent contact with older adults and the clinical and health literacy of pharmacists. While this model is intuitively attractive, the empirical evidence for its effectiveness, reach and sustained engagement remains limited, and significant barriers persist in the form of workload pressures, variable commissioning and the need for dedicated training and protected time.91 Without sustained investment in VCSE capacity, increasingly granular assessment may outpace what can actually be provided, particularly in rural or sparsely populated areas where service availability, transport infrastructure and population density constrain the range of feasible social prescriptions. These constraints may also disproportionately affect minority groups, including migrants, ethnic minorities and LGBTQ+ older adults, for whom culturally safe or identity-affirming spaces may be scarce outside urban centres.83 84 The implication is that stratification should be pursued in tandem with explicit commissioning commitments; otherwise, it risks becoming a sophisticated triage system for services that do not exist at scale.

Finally, several of the ambitions articulated in this review, particularly earlier risk identification and anticipatory support, require careful consideration of research design. Much of the existing evidence base is limited by small samples, inconsistent methods and outcomes that fail to capture what matters to older adults.44 45 This raises the possibility that a shift in research paradigm is required, moving beyond narrow effectiveness questions towards designs that can test whether improved identification and matching translate into sustained engagement, equitable access and meaningful downstream benefit. Testing whether earlier identification improves outcomes will therefore require evaluation approaches that combine pragmatic trials with implementation and process evaluation, and that explicitly measure whether improved targeting leads to better matching, sustained engagement and downstream health gains rather than merely earlier referral.44 45 49 In parallel, where predictive analytics are proposed, their clinical utility should be judged by impact on equitable access and meaningful outcomes, not only by model performance, with safeguards to ensure that anticipatory models do not systematically overlook groups already underserved by current diagnostic and referral pathways.

Conclusion

Depression and metabolic syndrome in older adults exemplify how mental and physical multimorbidity can co-develop through shared biological pathways and reinforcing social factors. In response to the increasing prevalence of multimorbidity, social prescribing offers a compelling model to manage symptoms while also mitigating further metabolic and mental decline. However, this potential will remain unrealised unless social prescribing is supported by adequate infrastructure and evaluated with metrics capable of capturing the complexity of multimorbidity.

Unlocking the value of social prescribing requires a framework that embraces complexity. Incorporating tools such as AI-driven narrative analysis, biological markers and patient-facing portals will help capture and track outcomes more meaningfully. At the same time, ICSs must move beyond commissioning link workers alone and invest in community infrastructure that sustains these interventions.92 With robust evaluation and systemic support, the NHS can generate the high-quality evidence needed to embed social prescribing within mainstream care. In doing so, it can help establish a model in which connection, purpose and mental well-being are recognised as central components of health rather than peripheral concerns.
