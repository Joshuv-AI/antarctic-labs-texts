Features predicting data exclusion in imaging studies of Alzheimer's disease

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPositron emission tomography (PET) without usable or accompanying magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is typically excluded in quantitative analyses of Alzheimer's disease, potentially limiting study generalizability. We investigated participant features predicting data exclusion in magnetic resonance (MR)‐dependent analyses and evaluated an existing MR‐free PET pipeline to quantify these missing data.METHODSImaging, clinical, cognitive, and sociodemographic data were analyzed for 2119 individuals in a multi‐site cohort. Agreement between MR‐dependent and MR‐free Centiloids (CL) assessed using intra‐class correlations and features predicting data exclusion were examined using logistic regressions.RESULTSMR‐free and MR‐dependent CLs generally agreed, but MR‐free CLs underestimated MR‐dependent cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. Approximately 19.5% (n = 405) of our cohort would have been excluded in MR‐dependent analyses. Age and cerebrovascular comorbidities were consistent exclusion features across multiple sites.DISCUSSIONData exclusion in imaging studies is not entirely random. Flexible quantification methods like MR‐free PET could supplement traditional methods to improve generalizability in large, multi‐site studies.Keywords: amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), Centiloid, data exclusion, generalizability, magnetic resonance (MR)‐free

BACKGROUND

Abnormal aggregation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques is the defining pathobiological feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and the primary target of recent anti‐amyloid immunotherapies (AATs). 1 Quantifying Aβ in vivo can be performed with positron emission tomography (PET) but often requires contemporaneous magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to delineate regions of interest (ROIs). Safety contraindicators such as pacemakers render MRI acquisition infeasible. Among participants who complete MRI, extensive neurodegeneration, comorbid vascular pathologies (e.g., white matter lesions), or imaging artifacts (e.g., head motion) may impede automated segmentation programs like FreeSurfer from defining reliable ROIs. Longitudinal studies and clinical trials may then primarily include healthier and less clinically impaired individuals in their analyses, unintentionally limiting study generalizability. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

Previous work identified potential features predictive of data exclusion. Imaging data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) revealed participants with depression were more likely to drop out of the study. 7 Clinical impairment may also prevent individuals from continuing in a study as symptoms worsen. 8 Cardiovascular risk factors may also contribute to participant dropout or exclusion 7 , 8 and are associated with the presence of white matter hyperintensity (WMH) lesions, 9 , 10 , 11 increasing the likelihood automated segmentation programs fail. 12 Socioeconomic status may play a modifiable role in these trends as it is inversely correlated with cardiovascular risk, 4 impacts depression treatment, 13 and is negatively associated with dementia incidence due to missed or delayed diagnosis. 4 , 14 , 15 Understanding the relationship between these characteristics and data exclusion would aid the field in developing statistical, recruitment, and study design methods that address unintended biases resulting from study procedures.

This study investigated sociodemographic, imaging, clinical, and cognitive variables to identify potential patterns of data exclusion in a multi‐site cohort. We further evaluated an existing PET quantification pipeline not reliant on MRI 16 to determine whether such non‐dependent quantification methods could recover excluded Aβ PET data.

METHODS

Participants

Baseline and longitudinal Aβ PET, structural head MRI, clinical, cognitive, and sociodemographic data were aggregated for 2119 individuals (1062 females; median age: 70.5 years, age range: 41.9 to 93.9 years) from several AD Research Centers (ADRCs) and studies, including the Charles F. and Joanne Knight ADRC at Washington University (Knight ADRC; n = 1118), Emory University ADRC (n = 18), University of Alabama at Birmingham ADRC (UAB; n = 80), University of Pennsylvania ADRC (UPenn; n = 271), the Harvard Aging Brain Study (n = 225), the Study to Evaluate Amyloid in Blood and Imaging Related Dementia (n = 246) 17 at Washington University, and the University of Wisconsin‐Madison's Alzheimer disease programs including the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (WRAP; n = 101) and the University of Wisconsin ADRC Clinical Core (UW; n = 60). All imaging data were reprocessed at Washington University, and the closest MRI, clinical, and cognitive data within 1 year of each Aβ PET were identified. Forty participants were removed from analysis as their Aβ PET could not be processed through the magnetic resonance (MR)‐free pipeline (30 for PET acquisition issues, four for incorrect image reconstruction, three for excessive motion, two for cerebellum exclusion, and one for a stroke affecting PET signal in the temporal region). The final cohort consisted of 2079 individuals.

We classified participants as having a missing MRI if no such scan occurred, as failed segmentation if segmented ROIs from acquired MRI were deemed unreliable based on visual examination from trained neuroimaging staff, or as usable MRI if segmented ROIs were deemed reliable. Structural MRIs from a subset of the Knight ADRC (n = 606) were reviewed by a licensed neuroradiologist for microbleeds, as well as small and large infarcts. To assess neighborhood deprivation (a proxy for health disparities), the 2020 US Census‐based area deprivation index (ADI) 18 was extracted as it provided the greatest statistical power across our cohort (Knight ADRC: n = 817, WRAP: n = 61, UAB: n = 30, UW: n = 19, UPenn: n = 11).

The Institutional Review Board at each participating institution approved all study procedures. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their designated representative.

Clinical and Cognitive Measures

Clinical data included the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global scores, the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), and APOE ε4 status. Body mass index (BMI) was based on weight and height at Aβ PET visit. A 10‐year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk score was calculated using the Framingham Heart Study's office‐based profile. 19

For measuring AD‐related cognitive impairment, scores from Trail Making Test Part B, Selective Reminding Free Recall, Animal Naming, and Digit Symbol were z‐transformed relative to the baseline cognitive visit across the aggregated cohort. These z‐scores were then averaged together into a composite cognitive measure, the Knight Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (Knight PACC). 20 Scores were analyzed for participants with at most one component task missing.

Imaging Acquisition and Processing

MRI

T1‐weighted structural images were segmented into cortical ROIs using FreeSurfer (version 5.3‐HCP or version 7; Martinos Center of Biomedical Imaging, Charlestown, MA, USA). ROI segmentations were visually inspected for reliability by trained neuroimaging technicians following the Knight ADRC quality control guidelines. 21

The TrUE‐Net neural network measured total WMH volume. 22 Each participant's T1 was preprocessed using the FMRIB Software Library's (FSL) Brain Extraction Tool (BET), aligned with a brain‐extracted FLAIR using FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT)’s rigid registration and binarized to create a mask in fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) space. Bias field correction was performed on both the co‐registered T1‐weighted warp parameters and the brain‐extracted FLAIR using FMRIB's Automated Segmentation Tool (FAST) before being fed into TrUE‐Net. As total WMH volume followed a log‐normal distribution, we log10‐transformed the data for analyses.

Amyloid PET

Aβ PET was performed with either 11C‐Pittsburgh compound B (PiB), 18F‐florbetapir (FBP), or 18F‐florbetaben (FBB) for the post‐injection windows of 30 to 60, 50 to 70, or 90 to 110 min, respectively. For MR‐dependent processing, PET data were analyzed using the FreeSurfer‐based PET Unified Pipeline (PUP), 23 which includes scanner resolution harmonization, inter‐frame motion correction, PET to MR registration, and non‐partial volume–corrected standard uptake value ratio (SUVr) analyses. Cerebellum‐cortex served as the reference region for all tracers. MR‐dependent amyloid burden was the arithmetic average of SUVrs for the bilateral precuneus, rostral middle and superior frontal, the superior and middle temporal, and the lateral and medial orbitofrontal. This average was converted to Centiloids (CL) 24 for FreeSurfer‐5.3 ROIs using published equations. 25 , 26

For MR‐free processing, PET data were corrected for inter‐frame motion and summed over frames covering the quantitative window using PUP before being fed into a local implementation of the MR‐free PET pipeline. 16 The preprocessed data were resampled and aligned to the MNI‐152 atlas using the SPM12 (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, London, UK) Coregister module. The MNI‐aligned PET data were spatially normalized to a tracer‐specific template using the Normalise module. We calculated a global SUVr on the spatially normalized, native resolution image using the standard CL cortical ROI with the standard CL whole cerebellum as the reference and then converted to CLs using equations from a Level 2‐calibration (Supplemental Materials, Supplemental Figure 1, and Supplemental Tables 1‐2).

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in the R statistical environment (version 4.4.1, R Core Team). Single‐measure, random‐effect intra‐class correlations (ICC) from the psych package 27 assessed agreement between CLs derived from the MR‐free and MR‐dependent PET pipelines using the first visit of each tracer. Agreement between the annualized rate of change in CLs derived from the MR‐dependent and MR‐free methods was assessed using similar ICCs for participants with at least two visits of the same tracer and at least a 1‐year interval between visits. Paired t‐tests examined the magnitude difference in the mean MR‐dependent and MR‐free CLs.

To investigate sociodemographic, clinical, cognitive, and imaging features between those excluded and included in MR‐dependent analyses, we first performed Welch t‐tests for continuous and χ2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables on the aggregated cohort, depending on sample sizes. Similar tests were conducted between participants with failed segmentations and with missing MRIs to determine differences within excluded participants. The same tests were then performed for each acquisition site to test replicability. For these tests, participants from UAB and Emory University ADRC were pooled together given their small samples and similar sociodemographic characteristics between the two sites. 28

Logistic regressions controlling for age, sex, and binarized CDR (CDR = 0 vs CDR > 0) were run for each baseline feature and then tested for significance in predicting whether an individual would be excluded (1) or included (0) for the aggregated cohort. We then characterized predictive features for those with failed segmentations using similar models with an added term for the FreeSurfer version to control for established differences in segmentations. 29 , 30 To control for different failure rates among sites, we ran mixed‐effects logistic models on the aggregated cohort with age, sex, binarized CDR, and FreeSurfer version as fixed effects and acquisition site as a random effect using the lme4 package 31 with default parameters.

Similar logistic regression models with age, CDR, and sex as main effects were run for each acquisition site. A FreeSurfer version term was only added to the data from Washington University as it was the only site with a mixture of 5.3‐HCP (25.4%, n = 332) and 7 (74.6%, n = 974) MRI data.

RESULTS

Evaluation of MR‐free PET

Our aggregated cohort contained 580 and 37 participants with usable MRI and a baseline FBP and FBB scan, respectively. CLs from the MR‐free and MR‐dependent pipelines showed significantly strong agreement for FBP (ICC [95% confidence interval] = 0.937 [0.800, 0.971], p < 0.001; Figure 1) and FBB (ICC = 0.965 [0.712, 0.989], p < 0.001). MR‐free CLs significantly underestimated MR‐dependent CLs by 7.842 CLs for FBP (t[579] = −17.696, p < 0.001) and 12.026 CLs for FBB (t[36] = −6.382, p < 0.001). Additionally, 887 participants had usable MRI and a baseline PiB scan. CLs from the MR‐free and MR‐dependent procedures showed significantly strong agreement for PiB (ICC = 0.971 [0.929, 0.984], p < 0.001). PiB MR‐free CLs significantly underestimated MR‐dependent CLs by 4.415 CLs (t[886] = −18.065, p < 0.001).

Characteristics of individuals excluded from imaging‐based analyses

In our aggregated cohort, 405 participants (∼19.5%) would have been excluded from a MR‐dependent PET analysis (Table 1). Demographically, excluded participants were male (χ2[1] = 8.141, p = 0.004), older (t[462.60] = 5.041, p < 0.001), non‐Hispanic White (χ2[1] = 8.331, p = 0.004) and differed in educational attainment (χ2[3] = 18.619, p < 0.001). Clinically, they exhibited depressive symptoms (t[143.74] = 2.978, p = 0.003), higher cardiovascular risk (t[97.81] = 4.174, p < 0.001), larger WMHs (t[180.86] = 5.828, p < 0.001), and greater amyloid measured by MR‐free CLs (t[554.69] = 3.745, p < 0.001). They were also more clinically impaired by MMSE (t[125.32] = −4.571, p < 0.001) and CDR (χ2[2] = 89.867, p < 0.001) and exhibited greater cognitive deficits by the Knight PACC (t[127.19] = −5.011, p < 0.001). While excluded participants came from areas with slightly higher ADI, the difference was not significant (t[176.62] = 1.309, p = 0.192). Across acquisition sites, only older age and larger WMHs were consistent features of excluded participants; however, statistical power among the sites varied substantially (Table S3).

Within the 405 excluded participants, 207 (∼50.9%) had a missing MRI and 198 (∼49.1%) had failed automated segmentation (Table S4). Participants with failed segmentations were significantly older (t[327.08] = 3.049, p = 0.002), clinically impaired by MMSE (t[48.18] = −4.074, p < 0.001), and exhibited cognitive deficits (t[30.42] = −6.844, p < 0.001), depression symptoms (t[70.95] = 2.772, p = 0.007), and greater amyloid burden (t[370.92] = 2.703, p = 0.007). Rates of missing MRI scans or failed segmentations significantly varied among acquisition sites in our cohort (χ2[5] = 406.041, p < 0.001; see Figure S2); however, sites with the highest rates of exclusion also contributed the lowest numbers of participants.

Predictors of missing MRI

Lower MMSE and Knight PACC scores, depression, WMHs, large infarcts, and microbleeds significantly predicted an acquired MRI failing automated segmentation after controlling for age, sex, and CDR status in the aggregated cohort (Table 3). DISCUSSION

Previous studies indicated that trends may exist in data excluded from conventional analyses that might hinder the translation and generalizability of research findings. 3 , 7 , 32 The current study makes several contributions to exploring these trends. First, we provide further evidence that conventional MR‐dependent analyses exclude subsets of participants. Second, while we did not find features that were predictive of data exclusion across sites, we did find several features that were consistent characteristics of excluded participants. Lastly, Aβ PET data could be recovered using MR‐free quantification to help reduce selection bias, but MR‐free quantification can also introduce quantitative bias by underestimating CLs relative to MR‐dependent methods both cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. These results underscore the need for robust study designs to enhance generalizability and minimize data loss.

In our aggregated cohort, 19.5% of participants were excluded due to non‐acquisition of MRI or poor automated segmentations. Moreover, these exclusions were not random and came primarily from failed MRI segmentations. Excluded participants tended to be older, male, non‐Hispanic White, more clinically and cognitively impaired and had comorbid depression, cerebrovascular pathology, and amyloid burden. Notably, age and cerebrovascular pathology were consistent features of exclusion across sites. The consistency of these features is not entirely surprising. Age has been strongly associated with incidence of cerebrovascular disease, including WMHs and infarcts. 33 Interesting connections emerge when age and cerebrovascular health are viewed in relation to the other identified features.

Cerebrovascular disease and amyloid are frequently comorbid in older adults with possible synergistic or additive effects on AD‐related cognitive impairment. 34 , 35 , 36 Further, baseline WMHs have been shown to be predictive of late‐life depression onset in longitudinal studies, even after adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors. 37 , 38 Older males are particularly susceptible to cerebrovascular issues, especially when comorbid cardiovascular risks are present. 33 The identification of non‐Hispanic Whites is interesting. Previous work suggested that infarcts were associated with steeper cognitive deficits compared to WMHs in non‐Hispanic Whites 39 ; however, as our cohort skewed toward non‐Hispanic Whites, caution with interpretation is warranted.

More concerning, cerebrovascular pathologies have also been linked to the development of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) in response to AATs. 40 Features we have identified as potential characteristics of exclusions may potentially hinder investigations of the relationship between pre‐treatment cerebrovascular health and ARIA. Future work should further investigate the interconnections between features identified in this study and how they potentially relate to data exclusion.

The aforementioned effects may also be mediated by socioeconomic factors. 5 Prior work demonstrated that structural disadvantage impacted cognition and brain aging through complex, multi‐level mechanisms. 13 , 14 , 41 Additionally, vascular pathologies are more prevalent in socioeconomically disadvantaged groups due to cumulative vascular risk and structural disparities in healthcare access. 42 , 43 This underscores the importance of evaluating socioeconomics not as a stand‐alone predictor but as a context that shapes health trajectories over time. Taken together, our results suggest multi‐faceted nuisances in AD pathological development may be occluded in traditional MR‐dependent analyses. 44

While multi‐site studies may implement procedures where failed segmentations are reviewed by experienced neuroradiologists or trained research technicians to salvage ROIs suitable for analysis, these procedures could produce subjective bias that is further compounded by the neuroanatomical expertise required to make such determinations. Proposed MR‐free methods 16 , 45 , 46 attempt to address this limitation by not requiring contemporaneous PET and MRI acquisition for quantification – an asset in multi‐site studies where PET and MRI locations may differ for participants. Additionally, MR‐free tools are directly translatable to the clinic, where contemporaneous acquisition is often less feasible. To date, several MR‐free tools have been approved for clinical use, such as Siemens's Syngo.PET Cortical Analysis and MIM Software's MIMneuro. However, these tools are not without downsides. MR‐free methods utilize non‐linear registration, which is not immune to misalignment due to severe atrophy and subsequently underestimate PET signal. 47 Partial volume correction is also not available to discriminate between gray and white matter uptake. Additionally, MR‐free relies on usable PET image quality. The impact of lower PET image quality from issues such as motion remain relatively unexplored or uncharacterized.

While our study provides several insights, it has limitations. First, we could not evaluate MR‐free CLs for longitudinal FBB due to insufficient data. Second, the current MR‐free pipeline does not perform scanner resolution harmonization as FreeSurfer‐based PUP, which may contribute to the observed quantitative difference between the two pipelines. Recent work has shown resolution differences can significantly impact CL measurements for MR‐free pipelines like CapAIBL, particularly at higher amyloid levels. 48 Atrophy may further exacerbate differences in CL measurement. In addition, our MR‐dependent and MR‐free pipelines used different reference regions (cerebellar gray vs whole cerebellum), and prior work suggested that whole cerebellum may be more robust to variations in scanner resolution and atrophy than cerebellar gray. 49 Some of the quantitative biases observed may then arise from the MR‐dependent CL estimates as well as MR‐free. Inclusion of resolution harmonization in the current MR‐free pipeline may help reduce CL error but may also add additional error due to reduced spatial resolution across scans. Further work is required to understand the relative contributions of image resolution, atrophy, and reference region on CL quantification and whether utilizing post‐processing harmonization tools such as ComBat may reduce CL measurement error. Third, failed segmentations were analyzed independent of MRI quality. It is unclear whether failed segmentations resulted mainly from features at the subject level (e.g., motion, WMHs), site level (e.g., older scanners with lower resolution), or both. Novel MRI protocols that correct for acquisition issues like motion may remediate some of these problems, but their impact on our analysis remains undetermined. Additionally, these subject‐ and site‐level issues may translate from MRI to PET, particularly in cases of cerebral pathology. Neuroradiologist and quality review of each MRI before segmentation and associated PET is needed to address these issues. Future work should incorporate these reports in analyzing features predicting data exclusion. Fourth, we were unable to parse several confounds in our analysis due to data available at the time of analysis and differences in sample size across sites. These confounds include recruitment and outreach methods, locations of physical imaging sites, PET imaging reconstruction procedures, vendor software versions, and study protocols. More detailed and comprehensive datasets are required to adequately answer these questions. Fifth, logistic regressions found a significant main effects of FreeSurfer version for some of our features, namely, depression and clinical and cognitive impairment. This may reflect algorithm changes or more representative cohorts for calibration across versions. Future work should investigate the impacts of FreeSurfer version on these features and PET quantification. Lastly, Aβ cortical summaries are based on large ROIs. While the present study showed MR‐free values generally agreed with MR‐dependent, smaller ROIs may show greater variability. Our results should be interpreted in the context of summary Aβ PET and cannot be assumed to translate to other non‐Aβ PET quantification.

Despite these limitations, the implications of these findings are broad. For clinical trials, exclusion based on MRI availability or segmentation quality may bias data toward younger, healthier, and less diverse populations – limiting generalizability and potentially exaggerating treatment efficacy. 2 , 3 , 8 Excluding any subset of participants is concerning as it may mischaracterize disease trajectories, overestimate intervention efficacy, and draw conclusions less applicable to those who would benefit from treatment. 5 This is increasingly problematic for tau‐targeting clinical trials where Aβ PET is required for enrollment or trial monitoring. 50 The integration of MR‐free and MR‐dependent tools for Aβ PET quantification may help studies routinely examine who is being excluded – and, importantly, why.
