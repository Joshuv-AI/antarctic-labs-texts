Magnetic resonance imaging quantifies causal body compartment effects on metabolic traits via immune phenotypes—a Mendelian randomisation study
Authors: Di Zhang, Liang-ping Li, Ying-hui Zhang, Pu Wang, Lei Lei, Yun Hu, Chao Zhou, Shu-shu Yang, Wei-hui Liu, Xin-yu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension, hyperlipidemia and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), are globally prevalent. However, traditional measures, such as body mass index (BMI), fail to capture body composition heterogeneity to accurately predict metabolic diseases. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides a precise quantification of body composition; however, the causal links among these compartments, immune phenotypes, and metabolic traits remain unclear. This Mendelian randomisation (MR) study aimed to investigate the compartment-specific effects of MRI-derived body composition measures on metabolic diseases and assess the mediating role of immune phenotypes.MethodsUsing a two-sample MR framework, genetic instruments for 10 MRI-assessed body composition traits were derived from GWAS data. The outcomes included metabolic diseases (T2D, MAFLD, and hypertension) and glycolipid metabolism traits. Mediation analysis integrated 731 immune cell types. Robustness was assessed using the MR-Egger, Weighted Median, and MR-PRESSO methods, addressing pleiotropy and heterogeneity.ResultsVisceral adipose tissue (VAT) and abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (ASAT) exerted strong causal effects on BMI (odds ratio [OR] = 1.136). The strongest causal effect on MAFLD risk was observed for liver proton density fat fraction (LPDFF) (OR = 1.833). VAT, weight-muscle ratio and ASAT positively influenced levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and total cholesterol, whereas they negatively influenced triglyceride levels. Immune phenotypes mediated 28 causal pathways: ectopic fat promoted pro-inflammatory immune profiles, transitional B cells and Memory-CD8+ T cells expanded positive correlation with BMI. CD14+ monocytes and CD16+ monocytes positively regulate Cholesterol and LDL-C levels, respectively.ConclusionOur study emphasised the correlation between MRI-derived body composition measures and metabolic diseases, particularly revealing VAT, subcutaneous adipose tissue, and liver proton density fat fraction as key causal drivers of metabolic diseases while uncovering the pivotal role of immune phenotypes in mediating these metabolic pathways. These findings support the integration of MRI-based body composition profiling into clinical screening for metabolic diseases of high-risk cohorts, and provide a novel theoretical foundation for immune-targeted therapies to combat metabolic dysregulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07241-4.

Background

Metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension, metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), hyperlipidaemia, and obesity, represent a global health crisis [1]. Although obesity is a well-established risk factor, traditional measures such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference fail to capture the heterogeneity of body composition, particularly in distinguishing metabolically harmful visceral adipose tissue (VAT) from benign subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) [2]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a powerful tool for precise body composition quantification, measuring VAT, SAT, ectopic fat, and lean muscle mass with greater accuracy than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and without the ionizing radiation risks of computed tomography (CT), enabling safer longitudinal metabolic risk assessment [3]. This shift highlights the critical role of MRI in linking body composition to metabolic dysregulations.

The relationship between body composition and metabolic diseases is bidirectional and compartment-specific. VAT drives systemic inflammation by releasing free fatty acids and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin (IL)-6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), directly causing hepatic insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction [4, 5]. In contrast, SAT may offer protection through adiponectin secretion; however, its excessive expansion in obesity is linked to ectopic fat deposition [6]. Ectopic fat in skeletal muscle worsens hyperglycaemia in T2D via lipotoxicity, whereas reduced lean muscle mass exacerbates dyslipidaemia by lowering the basal metabolic rate [7, 8]. In T2D, hyperinsulinaemia promotes muscle breakdown and VAT accumulation [9], and MAFLD triggers endocrine dysfunction through altered secretion of hepatokines, such as fetuin-A, mediating adipose tissue inflammation [10]. These findings underscore the need for compartment-specific analysis of simple adiposity measures [11].

Immune cell populations differ markedly across the body compartments and drive distinct metabolic disease pathways. VAT is enriched with pro-inflammatory CD11c+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, secreting TNF-α and IL-1β and promoting insulin resistance and hepatic lipid accumulation—hallmarks of T2D and MAFLD [4, 12]. These inflammatory immune profiles in VAT are strongly implicated in insulin resistance and atherosclerosis, exacerbating CVD risk [6]. In contrast, SAT contains protective CD206+ M2 macrophages and FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), enhancing insulin sensitivity [7, 13]. In the skeletal muscle, ectopic fat recruits CCR2+ monocytes, which differentiate into M1 macrophages, worsening insulin resistance and T2D progression [10]. These tissue-specific immune signatures demonstrate that metabolic diseases are influenced by fat mass and compartment-specific immune regulation.

Although associations among body composition, immune responses, and metabolic diseases have been reported, the precise causal relationships remain unclear. Mendelian Randomisation (MR) offers a robust framework for inferring causality using genetic variants as proxies for exposure and addressing confounding and reverse causations [14]. We aim to determine the causal relationship between body composition and different metabolic diseases using MRI-profiled body composition as exposures, metabolic traits as outcomes, and integrated immune cells as mediators. This approach clarifies causal hierarchies and reveals therapeutic targets for precise interventions against immunometabolic dysfunction.

Methods

Study design

The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The study aimed to investigate the causal relationships between MRI-derived body composition exposures and metabolic trait outcomes. We obtained the summary data of published Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS), including MRI-derived body composition, immune cells, and metabolic traits (Additional file 1 [Supplementary Table S1]). We employed MR using instrumental variables (IVs), specifically single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [15]. This approach leverages genetic associations identified through GWAS to mitigate confounding biases and reverse the common causality in observational studies [16]. The robustness of MR hinges on the following critical assumptions [17]: (1) Relevance: SNPs should exhibit strong associations with exposure to function as effective IVs; (2) Independence: SNPs should be unaffected by confounding factors in the exposure-outcome relationship; and (3) Exclusion restriction: SNPs should influence outcomes exclusively through exposure without pleiotropic effects. In this study, traits associated with MRI-derived body composition and metabolic traits were analysed for causal links to immune cells using a two-sample MR framework [15].

Data sources

Genetic instrument selection for exposure

TenMRI-derived body composition measures, which are critical indicators of overall body metrics, were selected as the exposures. These were identified using GWAS data from a cohort of 33,588 Europeans sourced from the GWAS catalogue.

Genetic instrument selection for outcomes

Hypertension: Genetic instruments for hypertension were derived from GWAS data of 31,943 individuals obtained from the GWAS catalogue.

BMI: Genetic associations for BMI were extracted from a study involving 336,107 individuals of European descent.

Lipid traits: Three lipid traits were assessed and categorised using data from three cohorts with sample sizes of 403,943, 440,546, and 441,016 individuals sourced from OpenGWAS.

Glycaemic Traits: Two glycaemic traits were assessed using data from 476,326 individuals from the MAGIC consortium.

T2D: GWAS data for T2D were obtained from the DIAGRAM consortium, which included a sample size of 149,821 individuals.

MAFLD: GWAS data for MAFLD were derived from the study by Sveinbjornsson et al., which included 9,491 individuals.

Genetic instrument selection for mediators

Summary statistics for 731 immune cell types were gathered from the GWAS catalogue with a sample size of 3,757 individuals. These genetic instruments were selected to explore the potential mediating pathways in MRs.

Genetic instrument selection

A meticulous multistep protocol was implemented to select genetic instruments, ensuring their validity and minimising bias.

Genome-Wide significance thresholds: SNPs were initially identified based on genome-wide association thresholds with stringent criteria for traits related to MRI-assessed body composition. MR

A two-sample MR was used to explore the relationship between MRI-assessed body composition and metabolic traits. To investigate the potential mediating pathways within this causal relationship, a two-step MR incorporating MRI-profiled body composition, immune cells, and metabolic traits was conducted. For primary MR, we used the MR-Egger, Weighted Median, inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method, Simple Mode, and Weighted Mode, assuming that all genetic variants were valid IVs.

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of the findings. The Weighted Median method was initially selected because of its reliability in providing causal estimates when more than half of the IVs are valid [19]. To assess heterogeneity among genetic instruments, Cochran’s Q statistic and its associated P-values were computed [20]. MR-Egger regression was used to detect potential horizontal pleiotropy by analysing the significance of the intercept term [21]. Additionally, the MR-PRESSO test was used to identify and adjust for outlier SNPs to address the potential confounding pleiotropy [22]. Weighted and Simple Mode analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency and reliability of the results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R programming software (version 4.2.1). The “TwoSampleMR” and “MR-PRESSO” packages were used to estimate causal effects and identify potential outliers. Effect sizes per standard deviation increase were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportion of mediation was calculated using the following equation: (β1 × β2)/β where β represents the overall effect from the primary analysis, β1 denotes the effect of traits related to MRI-profiled body composition on the mediator, and β2 signifies the effect of the mediator on metabolic traits. Standard errors and CI were estimated using the delta method [23].

Results

Associations of MRI-profiled body composition and metabolic traits

Obesity-related associations

We used two-sample MR to investigate the bidirectional causal relationships between MRI-assessed body composition and metabolic traits. In the forward direction, where MRI-derived body composition measures were exposures and metabolic traits were the outcomes, we identified several significant causal associations (Fig. 2, Additional file 2 [Supplementary Table S2]). Most MRI-derived body composition measures demonstrated strong positive causal effects on BMI, particularly abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (ASAT) (OR = 1.136, P = 5.97e-22) and VAT (OR = 1.136, P = 5.97e-22). Notably, liver proton density fat fraction (LPDFF), a direct marker of hepatic steatosis, showed the strongest association with MAFLD risk (OR = 1.833, P = 1.18e-07).

Lipid and glucose metabolism regulations

Distinct causal patterns were observed across the lipid profiles. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and cholesterol levels were positively correlated with numerous body components. The factors most closely associated with cholesterol levels were VAT (OR = 1.038, P = 0.000697) and abdominal fat ratio (AFR) (OR = 1.063, P = 9.52e-09). ASAT, posterior thigh muscle volume (PTMV), VAT, and weight-muscle ratio (WMR) were positively associated with LDL-C. Conversely, multiple body composition measures showed inverse causal relationships with triglycerides, with AFR demonstrating the most pronounced negative association (OR = 0.942, P = 2.31e-07). For glucose homeostasis, Anterior thigh muscle volume (ATMV) showed an inverse relationship with insulin resistance (OR = 0.948, P = 0.0266). Sensitivity analysis verified the robustness of the MR results (Additional file 3 [Supplementary Table S3]).

Reverse MR

To determine causal associations between MRI-derived body composition measurements and the metabolic traits identified above, reverse MR was conducted (Fig. 3, Additional file 4 [Supplementary Table S4]). BMI had the strongest influence on adipose tissue distribution, with marked effects on ASAT (OR = 2.09, P = 2.31E-262) and VAT (OR = 2.09, P = 2.31E-262). Among the lipid metabolism characteristics, only LDL-C was positively correlated with ATMV. Regarding glucose metabolism characteristics, T2D was negatively correlated with Anterior thigh muscle fat infiltration (%) (ATMFI), LPDFF, and Posterior thigh muscle fat infiltration (%) (PTMFI). Hypertension was positively correlated with Total thigh muscle volume z-score (TTMVz). Sensitivity analysis verified the robustness of the MR (Additional file 5 [Supplementary Table S5]).

Mediation analyses

Associations of MRI-derived body composition measures and immune phenotypes

Immune cells are crucial in metabolic diseases [24]. To further understand the effect of MRI-profiled body compartments on immune cells and their subsequent effects on metabolic traits, we conducted mediation analyses by screening 731 immune cell types as mediators. The IVW method revealed 158 causal associations between the MRI-derived body composition measures and immune phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 1, Additional file 6 [Supplementary Table S6]), of which 26 causal relationships were related to metabolic traits (Fig. 4).

Among these 26 causal relationships, AFR was associated with reduced dendritic (OR = 0.77, P = 0.015), suppressor (OR = 0.75, P = 0.041) and CD4+ T (OR = 0.79, P = 0.027) cells and elevated CD25++ regulatory T cells (OR = 1.25, P = 0.040). The strongest positive correlations with ASAT were observed in transitional B cells (OR = 1.31, P = 0.003) and CD33+CD14dim monocytes (OR = 1.35, P = 0.025), while CD14+ monocytes were suppressed in ASAT (OR = 0.82, P = 0.028). PTMFI was positively correlated with memory CD8+ T (OR = 1.20, P = 0.004) and CD33 + CD14dim (OR = 1.18, P = 0.033) cells, whereas whole-body muscle mass (WMR) had elevated HLA-DR+ T cells (OR = 1.21, P = 0.021). A significant positive causal relationship was observed between VAT and transitional B cells (OR = 1.31, P = 0.003) and CD33+CD14dim monocytes (OR = 1.35, P = 0.025). All associations remained robust after the sensitivity analyses (Additional file 7 [Supplementary Table S7]).

Associations of immune phenotypes and metabolic traits

The IVW method revealed 304 causal associations between the immune phenotypes and metabolic traits (Supplementary Fig. 2, Additional file 8 [Supplementary Table S8]), of which 20 causal relationships were related to MRI-derived body composition measures (Fig. 5). Significant causal associations were identified between specific immune cell subsets and metabolic traits. Notably, CD16⁺ monocyte subsets demonstrated potent LDL-C reduction (OR = 0.994, P = 0.0101), whereas memory B cells had paradoxically increased LDL-C (OR = 1.008, P = 0.0184). The CD25 ⁺⁺ T cells subpopulation showed a significant positive correlation with cholesterol, while CD14⁺ monocyte showed a significant negative correlation. HLA-DR+ T cells displayed a positive association with triglyceride levels, whereas CD4+ T cells exhibited an inverse relationship. Transitional B cells are most closely associated with elevated BMI (OR = 1.011, P = 0.042). The sensitivity analysis verified the robustness of the MR (Additional file 9 [Supplementary Table S9]).

Mediation analysis of immune cells in MRI-profiled body composition related to metabolic traits

To investigate the potential mechanism driving the onset and progression of metabolic traits, mediation analysis was performed to elucidate the causal pathways from MRI-profiled body composition to metabolic traits mediated by immune cells. This analysis specifically involved examining the previously identified MRI-assessed body composition and immune cells associated with metabolic traits in the two-sample MR.

We identified 28 mediating relationships (Table 1; Fig. 6). Eighteen immune phenotypes mediated MRI-assessed body composition-metabolic trait causality. Among these, ASAT and VAT had the most significant impact on BMI, and Transitional B cells showed the most significant positive mediating effect (β = 0.003, mediation proportion = 2.28%).

Mediation effect of MRI-derived body composition measures on metabolic traits via immune phenotypes

Regarding lipid metabolism, AFR showed the most significant positive causal relationship with cholesterol and CD25+ T cell played a positive mediating role (β = 0.001, mediation ratio 1.89%). CD14 monocytes were crucial in promoting the causal relationship between ASAT and cholesterol levels (β = 0.002, 6.44% mediation). As for low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-C, ASAT exerted a substantial suppression effect through BAFF-R + B cells (β=-0.003, -14.94% mediation, P = 0.046), while CD16+ monocytes mediated 5.8% of LDL-C increase (β = 0.001). In triglyceride regulation, CD4+T cells mediated 4.63% AFR protection (β = 0.003, P = 0.3 × 10⁻⁷).

Discussion

In this MR study, we demonstrated a strong causal relations hip between MRI-derived body compartment measures and key metabolic traits, emphasising their significance in diagnosing and predicting metabolic diseases. Our findings indicate that specific compartments, such as VAT, ASAT, and LPDFF, exhibit robust associations with BMI, MAFLD, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Notably, LPDFF showed a particularly strong association with MAFLD risk, whereas VAT and ASAT significantly contributed to obesity-related outcomes. Additionally, we identified immune cells, including transitional B cells and memory B cells, as key mediators of these relationships, highlighting the role of immune mechanisms in metabolic dysregulation. These results confirm the clinical utility of MRI-based body composition assessments as precise, noninvasive tools, particularly for identifying individuals at high risk of MAFLD, T2DM, and obesity.

Our study is the first to reveal a comprehensive, significant positive relationship between MRI-derived body composition measurements and BMI, indicating that any increase in body composition affects weight. Second, we observed the crucial role of LPDFF in the evaluation of MALFD. This finding aligns with prior studies demonstrating the superior accuracy of MRI in quantifying hepatic steatosis and fibrosis compared with that of traditional biomarkers or ultrasound-based methods [25, 26]. Furthermore, our study confirmed the profound diagnostic value of MRI-derived adipose tissue content measurements such as AFR, ASAT, and VAT in assessing MAFLD risk. These fats cause hepatic steatosis by increasing free fatty acid flux and pro-inflammatory signals [27]. These findings support the integration of MRI techniques into routine clinical evaluations, offering a promising approach to enhancing diagnostic precision and tailoring therapeutic strategies for MAFLD management.

MRI-derived body composition measurements were strongly correlated with lipid metabolism. However, there was an opposite correlation between LDL-C, total cholesterol, and triglycerides. Adipose tissues (ASAT and VAT) were positively associated with LDL-C and cholesterol levels, whereas their associations with triglyceride levels trended in the opposite direction. Mechanistically, LDL-C and cholesterol levels are primarily regulated by the LDL receptor-mediated cholesterol clearance pathway in the liver. Increased adipose tissue may promote LDL-C and cholesterol accumulation by affecting lipoprotein lipase activity or hepatic cholesterol metabolism [28, 29]. Triglycerides are primarily regulated through lipolysis and the metabolic pathway of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and are closely related to lipolysis [30], leading to a negative correlation with fat content.

Immune phenotypes may also mediate the effects of MRI-derived body composition measures; however, there is significant heterogeneity. Adipose tissues may activate inflammatory immune cells (such as T cells and monocytes) through adipokine-driven inflammation, amplifying the production of VLDL, causing increased LDL and cholesterol levels [31] and promoting obesity [4]. We observed specific B cell phenotypes, such as BAFF-R. B and memory B cells, which have protective effects on lipid metabolism. B cells possibly exert a protective effect on lipid metabolism by suppressing adipose tissue inflammation, enhancing lipoprotein clearance, and regulating the immune microenvironment [32, 33].

There was a correlation between the MRI-derived body composition measures and glucose metabolism. Our data clearly indicates that ATMV is negatively correlated with insulin resistance. Skeletal muscle is the primary site of insulin-mediated glucose uptake, and an increase in its volume may enhance the insulin-mediated glucose transport capacity [34]. Muscle tissue secretes various anti-inflammatory factors and myokines, reducing insulin resistance [35].

For the first time, we used a comprehensive MR framework to analyse the causal relationship between MRI-derived body composition measurements and metabolic traits, revealing the specific effects of human body composition on different metabolic diseases and traits. Furthermore, a pathway from MRI-profiled body composition to metabolic traits was constructed through a two-step MR and mediation analysis via immune phenotypes. However, this study had certain limitations. The research data were primarily sourced from populations of European descent; thus, the generalisability of the results may be influenced by differences in genetic backgrounds and environmental exposure. Similarly, MR is based on cross-sectional genetic data and cannot capture the dynamic interaction between fat distribution and metabolic characteristics such as disease progression and dynamic changes in fat distribution.

Conclusions

Our study emphasised the correlation between MRI-derived body composition measures and metabolic diseases, particularly revealing VAT, SAT, and LPDFF as key causal drivers of metabolic diseases while uncovering the pivotal role of immune phenotypes in mediating these metabolic pathways. These findings support the targeted application of MRI-based body composition profiling in high-risk metabolic diseases cohorts, where its precision significantly enhances risk stratification, potentially enabling earlier intervention and long-term cost savings. This approach provides a novel theoretical foundation for immune-targeted therapies to combat metabolic dysregulations.
