Governance frameworks and decision-making models for intensive care units in low- and middle-income countries: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Arunangshu Ghoshal, Anuja Damani, Shwethapriya Rao, Cherian Varghese V, Naveen Salins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionIntensive care units (ICUs) operate at the intersection of advanced technology, complex ethical decision-making and resource-intensive care. While many high-income countries have developed institutional governance mechanisms—such as Clinical Ethics Committees, structured triage policies and formal palliative care consultation pathways—to support ethical deliberation and accountability in ICUs, the applicability, structure and reported outcomes of such frameworks in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remain unclear and dispersed across disciplines.ObjectiveThis scoping review aims to map existing governance frameworks and ethical decision-making models guiding ICU practices globally, with particular attention to their relevance and reported application in LMIC contexts.MethodsThe review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and be reported according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) standards. Searches will be conducted across MEDLINE, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO and Global Health. Grey literature will be identified through Google Scholar, WHO Global Index Medicus and professional society websites (eg, Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine (ISCCM), Indian Association of Palliative Care (IAPC) and European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM)). Eligible sources will include empirical research, policy documents, guidelines and institutional reports published in English from 2000 onwards. Data will be charted using a structured template and synthesised through descriptive mapping and thematic analysis across domains, including structural design, decision processes, communication pathways, integration with palliative care, ethical-legal considerations and reported outcomes. The review is planned to be conducted between December 2025 and April 2026, including database searches, screening, data extraction and synthesis.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required. Introduction

Intensive care medicine represents one of the most technologically advanced and ethically complex domains of healthcare. Decisions within intensive care units (ICUs) frequently involve considerations of life-sustaining treatment, prognostic uncertainty, resource utilisation and communication with families during periods of critical illness.1 These decisions occur within institutional, legal and cultural contexts that influence how care is delivered and how ethical dilemmas are addressed.

The concept of ICU governance extends beyond clinical protocols and encompasses systems of accountability, deliberation, documentation and oversight that guide decision-making practices.2 In several high-income settings, structures such as Clinical Ethics Committees (CECs), formal ethics consultation services, triage policies and palliative care consultation pathways have evolved to support ICU teams in navigating complex decisions.2,6 Published literature suggests that such mechanisms may contribute to improved transparency, shared decision-making and support for clinicians experiencing moral distress.7 8 Evaluations of CECs in the United Kingdom and the Netherlands, for instance, have shown improvements in transparency and the ethical justification of decisions, as well as measurable reductions in ICU length of stay for patients with poor prognoses.3 9 However, these systems are resource-intensive, often embedded in robust legal and institutional infrastructures that are not readily transferable to LMICs.

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), ICUs operate within distinct constraints, including high patient volumes, limited bed availability, financial implications of care for families and variability in access to palliative care services. Reports from South Asia and other LMIC regions describe variability in admission practices, end-of-life decision-making and communication approaches.10,15 The cost of ICU care is substantial, with daily expenses in India ranging from ₹15 000 to ₹30 000, creating financial toxicity for families and straining hospital resources.16,19 National and professional guidelines addressing end-of-life decision-making and Do Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR) practices have been published in several LMICs, including India, yet the extent to which institutional governance structures exist to operationalise such guidance remains unclear.20

Existing literature on ICU ethics and decision-making is dispersed across domains such as bioethics, critical care, palliative care, health services research and policy analysis.21 22 While systematic reviews have examined ethics consultation services or communication interventions in ICUs,23 24 no comprehensive synthesis has focused specifically on governance frameworks, their structural characteristics, decision-making processes and reported outcomes, particularly in LMIC contexts.

A scoping review is therefore appropriate to map how ICU governance mechanisms are conceptualised, structured and implemented across settings, and to identify knowledge gaps relevant to LMIC healthcare systems.

Review question

What governance frameworks and institutional mechanisms guide ethical decision-making in intensive care units, and how are these reported or implemented in LMICs?

Objective

To identify and map governance frameworks and ethical decision-making models relevant to ICUs globally, with specific attention to their structure, processes and reported relevance to LMIC contexts.

Methods

Scoping review framework

In this review, ICU governance frameworks are conceptualised broadly as institutional mechanisms that structure ethical deliberation, clinical accountability and resource allocation in critical care settings. This review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR checklist.25

Data extraction

The review is planned to be conducted between December 2025 and April 2026, including database searches, screening, data extraction and synthesis. Data extraction will be conducted using a structured, piloted template developed in accordance with the JBI scoping review methodology. Two reviewers (AG and AD) will independently extract data from all included studies and grey literature documents, ensuring consistency, completeness and reliability. Discrepancies will be discussed and resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (NS). The selection process will be depicted in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Findings will be presented using thematic maps, evidence matrices and conceptual diagrams. Because institutional governance documents and policy frameworks may not be uniformly published across countries, particularly in low- and middle-income settings, the availability of grey literature may vary geographically. To address this, the review will document the source type and country of origin of included documents and will explicitly note regions where institutional documentation is sparse. This approach will allow the review to identify potential geographic and publication biases arising from uneven access to policy or governance materials.
