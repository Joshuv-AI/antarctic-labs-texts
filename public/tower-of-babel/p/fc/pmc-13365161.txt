A Qualitative Study of Family Centrality in Modified Dignity Therapy Experiences Among Korean Patients With Advanced Cancer: KCSG-PC22-10
Authors: Se-Ryun Park, Miyoung Cho, Hyun Yeb Jung, Seong-Hoon Shin, Yu-Jung Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with advanced cancer experience significant existential distress, necessitating culturally sensitive interventions to enhance psychological well-being. This study explored a modified form of dignity therapy (mDT) that incorporates a photo-collage intervention to address these needs considering the Korean cultural context, where family relationships fundamentally shape individual identity and coping processes.MethodsThirteen Korean patients with advanced cancer participated in this qualitative study, which involved integrated reflexive thematic analysis of interview transcripts and participant-generated photo-collages. The intervention combined semi-structured interviews based on mDT protocols with autobiographical image collections reflecting meaningful life experiences. Data were analyzed using an integrated approach, systematically cross-referencing verbal narratives with visual expressions.ResultsThe analysis revealed that family serves as the central organizing principle through which Korean patients interpret illness experiences. Family provided the foundation of meaning and identity, with life values gained through familial roles and sacrifices, prominently represented in images of family members. Participants emphasized reluctance to burden loved ones, and treatment decisions were filtered by family considerations.ConclusionPsychosocial interventions for Korean patients with advanced cancer benefit from addressing family dynamics as a core therapeutic component. Integrated analysis of textual and visual data revealed cultural themes not fully captured through interviews alone, supporting the development of family-centered therapeutic models tailored to Korean cultural values and collective identity structures.Trial RegistrationClinical Research Information Service Identifier: KCT0009394

INTRODUCTION

The psychological challenges faced by patients with advanced cancer include profound existential distress. As they confront their mortality, therapeutic interventions that enhance psychological well-being and improve quality of life become crucial.123 Dignity therapy (DT), a brief form of psychotherapy, has gained attention for addressing these needs.45 This individualized intervention guides participants through a reflective process of their life experiences to foster meaning and coherence.

DT aims to alleviate existential suffering and enhance quality of life at the end-of-life stage. It involves a structured interview in which patients discuss various aspects of their lives, including significant roles, achievements, and wishes for loved ones; the resulting narratives are edited into a generativity document.6 This process improves multidimensional well-being while strengthening familial bonds for a lasting legacy.7 Studies have highlighted the benefits of DT for patients with advanced cancer, demonstrating improvements in dignity, reductions in demoralization, and increases in meaning.89 The intervention has also improved quality of life and reduced emotional distress.1011 Therapies that maintain meaning in life similarly enhance spiritual well-being and reduce existential distress.1213

Art therapy using visual materials further supports patients in integrating their narratives. In a study by Keisari et al.,12 older adults who created a cohesive photo-collage representing meaningful life experiences developed a stronger sense of continuity of self, and the artwork served as a legacy for transgenerational connections. Art therapy is also used to reduce psychological distress, including depression and anxiety, in palliative care.14 Through active engagement in art-making, patients can momentarily shift attention away from personal problems and experience greater relaxation. Creative programs for end-of-life care address multifaceted distress by enabling expression of positive self-concepts through non-verbal means.

Despite these benefits, several limitations remain. Cultural variations and differing therapeutic approaches may reduce generalizability across settings.15 Therefore, qualitative studies are needed to clarify how subjective experiences and cultural factors influence the achievement of ego integrity through psychotherapeutic interventions. Recent Korean studies have identified unmet needs in end-of-life care, particularly regarding family centrality and psychosocial support.1617 Although family involvement strongly influences decision-making, communication failures and insufficient information often hinder effective engagement.16 These limitations underscore the potential benefits of culturally adapted interventions that strengthen family-based, dignity-preserving approaches, which this study explored.

The objective of this study was to analyze how modified dignity therapy (mDT) supports ego integrity in Korean patients with advanced cancer. To this purpose, in-depth narratives were collected through interviews based on the DT question framework combined with photo-collages, enabling a qualitative understanding of participants’ experiences.

The overarching research question was how patients with advanced cancer experience and find meaning in life through mDT incorporating photo-collages. Additional points of investigation were 1) how the Korean cultural context shapes the dynamics of psychotherapeutic effects, and 2) how visual elements in photo-collages and verbal narratives together illuminate the dignity-conserving effects of the therapy.

METHODS

Design and setting

This study used a phenomenological qualitative method in which reflexive thematic analysis was conducted using semi-structured interviews and participant-generated photo-collages. This approach provided holistic insights into the experiences of participants who received the mDT intervention. The method, well suited to qualitative psychological research, facilitates identifying and interpreting thematic patterns in data.1819 Photo-collages were analyzed using the stages of visual thematic analysis described by Drew and Guillemin,20 enabling integration of participant and researcher perspectives. Participants were recruited from a hospital in Busan, South Korea, and sessions were conducted face-to-face in quiet ward rooms or a hospital lounge.

Participants

Eligibility criteria included a diagnosis of advanced cancer, absence of sensory or perceptual impairments, and the ability to participate in interviews lasting more than one hour. Recruitment

All participants provided written informed consent. Before baseline measurements, they consented to the recording and publication of analyses derived from their therapy sessions.

Data collection

This qualitative study was part of a randomized controlled trial assessing the effects of mDT on Korean patients with advanced cancer (Clinical Research Information Service Identifier: KCT0009394). Fifty-two participants were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the experimental (n = 26) or control (n = 26) groups. Attrition included 13 participants in the experimental group and five in the control group due to health deterioration, withdrawal of consent, or loss to follow-up. Accordingly, 13 and 21 participants, respectively, completed the trial protocol. This article reports the in-depth qualitative analysis of the 13 participants who completed the intervention. Data were collected between July 2022 and June 2023.

A trained therapist guided all sessions. The mDT involved a photo-collage intervention and an interview session. During the photo-collage intervention, participants created autobiographical covers using thematic images that reflected meaningful experiences, cherished memories, and serene or spiritual representations (Fig. 1). Interviews addressed key life memories, personal achievements, and lessons for loved ones, following a DT protocol provided by the therapist (Table 2).

Question framework for the interview session of the modified dignity therapy

The photo-collage intervention elicited non-verbal reflections that complemented verbal mDT components. Participants selected from approximately 100 curated images organized into thematic categories (e.g., family/people, nature/landscapes, religion/spirituality, animals, food, abstract symbols). These images aligned with common life narrative elements in palliative contexts and offered cultural familiarity without requiring the preparation of own materials. Participants assembled images symbolizing their life stories and values and then verbally described their choices and symbolic meanings (e.g., sunflowers representing familial love, based on one participant’s explanation that his wife loved sunflowers). Symbolic meanings were incorporated into data analysis based on participant descriptions, without formal validation to avoid additional strain. Sessions lasted 30–45 minutes, and included minimal therapist intervention to encourage open exploration.

Therapist involvement was intentionally minimal and structured only to support task completion. Open-ended questions (e.g., “Choose an image that resonates with your life”) were used to prevent influence on participant responses. The therapist refrained from assigning favorable meanings to participants’ images or narratives.

Data analysis

All data, including transcripts and photo-collages, were analyzed using ATLAS.ti. The research team comprised oncologists with palliative care experience and DT-trained personnel who completed the online DT training program provided by the Hospice Foundation of America and engaged in both the mDT intervention and the qualitative analysis. The team maintained a reflective log throughout the coding process and held iterative meetings to examine how their perspectives might influence theme development, thereby ensuring the credibility of the thematic analysis. During these meetings, participants’ opinions and interpretations were prioritized over the researchers’ biases when analyzing transcripts and photo-collages. These strategies strengthened the transparency of the study.

The researchers conducted initial coding with emphasis on Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.18 Both the transcripts and photo-collages were reviewed multiple times for familiarization. Using a bottom-up approach, textual data were annotated with descriptive codes to capture the participants’ distress and coping strategies. Photo-collages were analyzed following Drew and Guillemin’s stages of visual thematic analysis, integrated with reflexive thematic analysis of transcripts. Visual data were examined through repeated viewing and then coded for composition, color palettes, emotional tones, and participant-described symbols (e.g., an image of a family evoking ‘legacy’). These visual codes were cross-referenced with verbal narratives to identify reinforcements (e.g., a selected image of children amplifying themes of pride) or complementary insights (e.g., non-verbal serenity symbols not articulated in interviews).

Coding was conducted collaboratively and discussed among team members to enhance reflexivity, acknowledging how their collective experiences shaped the interpretive analysis. The codes from both data sources were cross-referenced to identify connections and integrate the findings. Initial themes were conceptualized and then elaborated, reviewed, and refined through successive rounds of discussion to construct a comprehensive narrative addressing the research inquiry. Subsequently, these themes were used to develop a practical framework. To ensure the trustworthiness of the analysis, the researchers held repeated discussions and cross-referenced participant-described symbols with interview narratives to establish convergence. Although formal participant verification of symbol interpretations was not performed to minimize participant burden in the palliative care setting, the boundaries of interpretation were confirmed through the reflexive logs. Consequently, meaning was derived directly from participants’ verbal descriptions of the images rather than the researchers’ inferences. The team collaboratively evaluated data saturation through iterative discussions during coding and thematic analysis. The team observed thematic convergence after reviewing the interview with the tenth participant, and no noteworthy new insights emerged thereafter. This cooperative evaluation ensured that the data accurately reflected the range of mDT experiences among advanced cancer patients within the Korean cultural context.

The analysis of textual and visual data was expected to help researchers recognize the therapeutic characteristics of the photo-collage intervention, which differed from the life narratives elicited through symbolic non-verbal representations suggested by the previously reported mechanism of mDT and meaning-making approaches. To fully integrate both data types, visual themes were systematically cross-analyzed using the thematic framework derived from interview transcripts for each participant. This process identified areas in which the visual narrative reinforced, elaborated upon, or offered a complementary perspective to the verbal narrative, thereby yielding a richer and more nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Kosin University Gospel Hospital (IRB No. 2022-04-007). Informed consent was obtained from all the study participants. Given the sensitive nature of the discussions, safeguards were implemented to support the emotional well-being of participants. They were informed of their right to withdraw from the interview at any time without penalty. Following each session, the therapist allocated time for debriefing and reflection to promote emotional security. Furthermore, referrals for psychosocial support were arranged in advance should participants experience significant emotional distress.

RESULTS

Two overarching themes were identified in the interview and visual data: 1) family as the foundation of meaning and identity, and 2) the weight of familial burdens and responsibilities. Family served as a primary cultural factor that enhanced a sense of dignity among Korean patients with advanced cancer. It functioned as a source of identity and as the motivation to maintain hope despite worsening conditions. However, some participants struggled with the perception of burdening their family members and experienced demoralization, including a loss of meaning and purpose.

Theme 1: family as the foundation of meaning and identity

Bravo, my life: a life of devotion to family (from transcripts)

Participants reflected on their roles within their families and workplaces, recalling lives marked by daily struggles to support their loved ones. Their commitment was so intense that they worked even on Sundays, and their children often remembered them as “A dad who just sleeps at home.” After cancer diagnosis and treatment, they stepped back from work to regain balance and reflect on years spent focusing solely on the future.

When I was young and my children were growing up, I worked 12-hour shifts. On holidays, I often had to sell oil to the factory for three or four consecutive days, which meant that I worked nonstop. My life revolved around performing ancestral rites and maintaining a demanding routine. When I read my children’s diaries, they described me as always sleeping (R18).

Even if I wanted to do something selfish, I couldn’t because of my children. Even if I want to spend more money, I can’t waste it. When I was going to buy clothes for myself, I ended up buying clothes for my children instead. However, my wife told me not to do that (R36).

Some participants recounted pain experienced at home, including domestic violence and other conflicts. Despite these challenges, they chose to keep their families together rather than pursue divorce or leave. They remained committed to their roles as heads of household, striving to maintain stability for those who depended on them.

If I were to give up, how would my family, from my mother to my children, make a living? Providing for my family is my most basic responsibility in life, so I can’t afford to give up (R22).

They spoke proudly of their children, who had grown into successful and well-rounded adults because of their sacrifices. They found comfort in their children’s accomplishments, including stable jobs and sincere personalities, despite not having been able to offer them every advantage. These reflections affirmed the value of their perseverance and forward-looking efforts. Participants expressed a desire for their children to understand and appreciate the sacrifices made for them.

My sons married before they turned 30 and brought home their brides before they were 20. I helped them only slightly. They met their wives, got along well, and, now, in their 30s and early 40s, have already bought houses. They also have cars, even if those are not imported. Moreover, their children are healthy (R18).

Glowing memories with loved ones (from the photo-collage intervention)

The images enabled participants to recall moments with loved ones, the small pleasures of daily life, and significant events (Fig. 2). Images of young children being cared for by family members or playing with friends evoked memories of love and support received during childhood. Photographs of married couples holding their babies or smiling at one another reminded participants of the joy shared within their families. They also identified images of older couples sitting side by side as their children became independent, prompting reflections on their own efforts, sacrifices, and long-term dedication to their families.

Representative images of participant-generated photo-collages reflecting key themes in the modified dignity therapy.

Preparing a legacy as a familial continuation (from transcripts)

Rather than denying their cancer diagnoses or focusing solely on finding a cure, participants sought ways to live with their condition. They viewed the remainder of their lives as a “bonus” and aimed to approach it with gratitude rather than anxiety about the future. To do so, they spent time with loved ones, wrote diaries, meditated, and savored small daily joys.

When my grandchildren say, “Grandpa, buy me some sweets,” it makes me so happy. Now, I take care of them every weekend. I go to the market to buy snacks and treats for them and my daughters-in-law. It brings me the greatest joy (R11).

One participant reflected on the sudden death of her younger sister, expressing gratitude for the opportunity to prepare for her own death, an opportunity her sister did not have. After her sister’s passing, she developed a calm acceptance of mortality and helped her family come to terms with her eventual death.

Unlike my sister, I can talk to the ones I love before I die. So, I said to my mom, “Mom, she [my sister] didn’t die like that.” I feel fortunate [to be ready for the end of my life]. I keep talking to my mother, and she always responds, “That’s right.” Then, I say, “If I mention something I want to eat today, you can make it for me.” So I try to help my mother think of it in that way and appreciate it (R45).

Participants also recognized death as an inevitable part of life and developed acceptance of their earlier departure. They wished to leave a meaningful legacy by imparting life lessons to their families. They shared insights about living harmoniously with others and remaining steadfast in their beliefs despite hardships. Their reflections became sources of guidance for loved ones who would continue to live.

If I don’t want to get hurt too much, I should quickly distance myself from people whose words don’t align with their actions. My heart can’t take getting hurt. Therefore, I avoid such people and focus on devoting myself to those who are on the same page as me—good people. That’s how I think I should live (R17).

Theme 2: the weight of familial burdens and responsibilities

A reluctance to burden the family (from transcripts)

Participants often worried about becoming a burden on family members who cared for them. They felt persistent anxiety about their own well-being and guilt over the sacrifices made by their relatives, some of which affected their livelihoods. Concerned that their condition might worsen, participants frequently downplayed pain and discomfort and reassured their families that they were fine.

This time, I felt so weak that I couldn’t express my true feelings to my children. They were so happy and said they were okay, so I couldn't bring myself to tell them I was sick. I didn’t want them to worry about me (R02).

Prioritizing time with the family over treatment paths (from transcripts)

Participants evaluated whether treatment paths held existential meaning for them. Spending quality time with their families took precedence over continuing chemotherapy. Their decisions to discontinue treatment stemmed from a desire to deepen family connection rather than a thorough judgment of medical benefit.

Chemotherapy consumed time and energy that participants wished to devote to loved ones. One participant shared her frustration with repeated treatment cycles and her desire to stop treatment due to an uncertain prognosis:

I want to spend more time with my parents, and it’s frustrating to spend so much time on chemo. I don’t know when I’ll be able to finish after spending all this time like this. I don’t know how difficult it will be or when it will end, but I don’t want to go on like this. I just want it to stop (R46).

This response reflects a preference to use limited time for meaningful family connection rather than an abandonment of healing. Treatment decisions were not always fully controlled by participants. In one case, a participant’s request for discontinuation was not accepted because her children pleaded with her to continue. In another case, parents supported the participant’s decision to stop treatment, leading to relief and the opportunity to spend more meaningful time together. These examples illustrate the central role of the family in medical decision-making in the Korean cultural context, in which end-of-life treatment choices are shaped by relational harmony and familial responsibility.

DISCUSSION

This study explored mDT experiences among Korean patients with advanced cancer to identify cultural factors that shape the therapeutic components of the intervention. Family emerged as a central value that significantly influenced participants’ lived experiences and psychosocial well-being. In the Korean cultural context, family centrality structures end-of-life narratives and functions as the core element of dignity-conserving factors. In Confucian-influenced societies such as Korea, filial piety strongly shapes parent–child relationships. Whereas Western societies emphasize dignity as self-autonomy, East-Asian populations focus on fulfilling familial roles as developmental life tasks (e.g., raising successful children and financially supporting older parents). Participants expressed pride in their devotion, viewing their children’s accomplishments as reflections of successful parenting. Existing research shows that children’s filial actions enhance parental well-being and successful aging in East-Asian cultures.21222324

In Chochinov’s dignity model, family operates as an external factor that supports generativity, self-continuity, and social support. This study reframes the family within the Korean context as an internal organizational structure encompassing three dignity dimensions: personal identity rooted in familial roles, value derived from sacrifice for the family, and legacy transmitted through intergenerational harmony. This reconceptualization suggests that dignity, in the Korean context, cannot be understood solely as individual autonomy; rather, it acquires greater significance through relational harmony and the fulfillment of familial duties. Conceptual differences between the original and adapted dignity models

As revealed by this study, the role of family in shaping dignity differs across cultural contexts. In Chochinov’s model, family is positioned as one external element within the social domain and generativity, considered alongside other factors.25 Similarly, in traditional Chinese conceptualizations of dignity, family-related factors are categorized separately from caregiving or internal factors, paralleling findings in this study.2627 These elements include the performance of family roles across generations and moral convictions for familial connection, while also forming a separate component in defining the core identity of the individual. However, our findings indicate that for Korean patients, individual identity, values, and generativity are transmitted through the family, which serves as the foundational basis of meaning in life. For example, participants often concealed their pain to protect their families from the burden of caregiving, reflecting a sense of familial responsibility.

The results of this study expand Chochinov’s model of dignity by identifying the family as a fundamental element in all three domains of Korean patients with advanced cancer. This reflects the cultural values of individual identity and filial piety within the community. This integration is mapped below and visualized in Fig. 3. First, family intensified rather than merely influenced illness-related distress. Participants actively concealed symptoms to protect loved ones from caregiving burdens: “I didn’t show my true feelings to my children... I didn’t want to make them worry” (R02). Treatment decisions were filtered through family considerations, with participants prioritizing relational time over adhering to therapies: “I want to spend more time with my parents... I just want [chemo] to stop” (R46). Family thus functioned as both a source of concern and a lens through which medical decisions were evaluated.

In maintaining dignity, participants’ roles as family members formed their core identity and primary coping mechanism. Their past struggles to maintain their families provided meaning to their lives despite physical decline. “When I was young and my children were growing up, I worked 12-hour shifts... My life revolved around performing ancestral rites and maintaining that demanding routine” (R18). Self-sacrifice to support their families reinforced the sense of identity. “Even if I wanted to do something selfish, I couldn't because of my children... When I was going to buy clothes for myself, I ended up buying clothes for my children instead” (R36). Family commitment supported self-esteem when functional activities became challenging.

Regarding the social domain, family acted as a bridge for legacy transmission and social meaning-making. Life lessons were conveyed through guidance on harmonious living and familial respect: “Don’t harbor jealousy, strive to always live in harmony with your siblings, and respect your elders. When that kind of balanced living becomes a habit, that is what we call happiness. Remember, giving is truly happy—adopt the mindset, ‘Thank you for giving me the chance to help!’ It really is all about mindset.” (R11). This generativity embedded in familial connections diverges from Chochinov’s original conceptualization, in which social factors are regarded as external support rather than foundational to dignity and legacy, particularly in Korean cultural contexts. Family centrality revealed a duality in that it enhanced participants' dignity while simultaneously generating obligation not to burden relatives, risking emotional isolation and unmet personal care needs. Family involvement occasionally resulted in decision-making conflicts, where family opinions sometimes superseded patients’ wishes to discontinue treatment. The finding that family can both promote dignity and contribute to guilt and burden suggests that family-centered interventions must address positive aspects of family relationships alongside inherent risks.

Cultural norms and societal values specific to Koreans profoundly impact the psychological experiences of patients with advanced cancer. In Korean culture, a “good death” is often associated with not burdening family members.28 The belief that one should not burden others constitutes a significant psychological stressor for patients.28 Caregiving burdens placed on family members can affect mental health and reduce care quality.29 Patients’ dignity is also threatened by loss of autonomy and dependence on family for care.30

Family cultural perspectives regarding death and end-of-life care also shaped participants’ experiences. The proportion of Korean patients who voluntarily choose hospice care is approximately 20%, low compared with other populations.31 This underutilization is partly driven by societal views of family caregivers, who associate hospices with abandonment and death. For many, choosing hospice is seen as a failure of filial duty, conflicting with cultural expectations that families support treatment until the very end.32 These cultural traits affect how participants engage with psychosocial interventions such as DT, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive approaches to foster dignity and alleviate existential distress.

The sample’s diagnostic diversity, categorized by organ system (Table 1), reflects varying illness trajectories that may influence responses. For instance, aggressive trajectories in digestive or respiratory cancers often amplified urgency in family-centered meaning-making, prioritizing time with loved ones over prolonged treatment. Such prognoses heightened illness-related concerns, prompting protective behaviors (e.g., concealing pain to avoid family worry, R02). Prognosis also shaped death engagement; participants with poorer outlooks derived meaning from preparatory acts (e.g., legacy documents, R11), emphasizing acceptance and relational harmony. This variability shows how trajectory affects dignity themes, with shorter prognoses intensifying immediate family bonds and longer ones enabling broader generativity.

Integrating a photo-collage intervention into mDT offers distinct methodological and therapeutic advantages over traditional DT, which focuses on verbal elements and generativity documents. Methodologically, visual symbols in the images were integrated with guided interview responses, allowing analysis of psychological and cognitive insights conveyed in their verbal expressions (Fig. 4). For example, R02’s selection of travel images intensified tearful recollections of concealed relational burdens: “Seeing this reminds me of the island trip with my daughter... It brings tears; I didn’t show my true feelings to avoid worrying them.” R32’s flower beds represented unvoiced serenity: “Yellow flowers from my daughter come home...symbolizing familial support.” Visual data facilitated reflexive thematic analysis with complementary viewpoints and emotional tones that were not fully expressed verbally.

Therapeutically, the intervention lowered barriers to participation through preset, familiar images, promoting relaxation and accessibility for fatigued patients. Images of families enjoying weddings and outdoor activities induced pre-legacy construction in R44: “The images bring back memories of my family... Grateful for the opportunity to talk to my loved ones before dying.” Unlike traditional DT, the visual elements facilitated nonverbal expressions of legacy, fostering family-centered dignity and exploration of existential meaning, strengthening cultural empathy in the Korean context. Future research could quantify these benefits through comparative designs.

This study underscores the need to develop psychosocial programs that consider a family-centered Korean cultural context to promote ego integrity in advanced cancer patients. Rather than treating family dynamics as background features, supportive approaches engaging narratives through photo-collage and other visual elements addressing patients’ existential distress—closely related to their identity within their families—are expected to enhance dignity while preventing demoralization. This could involve interviews with family caregivers or DT frameworks exploring narratives related to patients’ self-worth and affirmation within their families. Based on cultural norms such as filial piety, interventions should focus on patient narratives tied to familial roles and lessen feelings of being a burden.

This study has limitations related to its qualitative design and scope. The small sample size (n = 13) from a single urban center limits generalizability to the broader Korean or international palliative care population. Thematic saturation was assessed collaboratively after the tenth participant, and no new insights emerged thereafter; however, this assessment may not fully capture the experiences of patients with advanced cancer across diverse cancer trajectories. Participants were drawn from an RCT that included only those who completed the experimental arm (including photo-collage), which may favor individuals who are more open to psychosocial interventions or have adequate functional status (e.g., no cognitive or sensory impairments). These limitations reflect the exploratory nature of the findings, which prioritized depth over breadth. Longitudinal and multicenter studies are needed to assess the long-term effects of the mDT on psychological well-being and validate findings in various Korean settings. Such initiatives support a holistic palliative care approach that addresses cultural, emotional, and clinical dimensions.

Despite these limitations, the findings offer practical implications for integrating mDT into palliative care. In clinical implementation, mDT proceeds sequentially with a photo-collage session facilitating autobiographical reflection through image selection, followed by a semi-structured interview adapted from DT to support meaning-making and narrative reconstruction. In this study, the interview was delivered by a DT-trained therapist who completed an officially recognized online training program provided by Chochinov et al.4 through the Hospice Foundation of America. Although mDT is informed by DT, it does not replicate the copyrighted DT protocol; rather, it extends it by incorporating visual prompts for patients who may have limited ability to articulate emotional, existential, or relational experiences verbally. The photo-collage session can be conducted using low-cost printed images and typically takes 30–45 minutes. It can be facilitated by a therapist after a 2–3-hour orientation session with minimal reflective supervision to ensure adherence to the therapeutic framework. All visual materials are drawn from publicly available, noncommercial sources and used solely for therapeutic and research purposes, ensuring ethical and copyright compliance. These features enhance the feasibility of mDT across diverse care environments, including inpatient hospices, outpatient palliative clinics, and home-based care, while preserving therapeutic depth and ethical safeguards. Collectively, the standardized and adaptable format of mDT supports its potential scalability as a resource-efficient, dignity-enhancing psychosocial intervention for patients with advanced cancer.

The mDT, which combines an interview with a photo-collage intervention, holds promise for promoting the dignity of Korean patients with advanced cancer, emphasizing family involvement in organizing palliative care experiences. Further studies should explore the distress experienced by Korean patients with cancer, with interventions designed to mitigate these challenges while incorporating the central role of family in the cultural context.
