Mass cytometry uncovers distinct blood myeloid phenotypes linked to clinical responses during gastric cancer chemoimmunotherapy
Authors: Sung Hee Lim, Minae An, You Jeong Heo, Ji Hyeon Cha, Seung Tae Kim, Jeeyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombination therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and chemotherapy has reshaped metastatic gastric cancer (GC) treatment, improving response rate, progression-free survival, and overall survival. We aimed to explore circulating immune cells and elucidate the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of pembrolizumab and capecitabine/oxaliplatin (XELOX) in patients with metastatic GC. Potential immune mechanisms in GC tumors were retrospectively examined among patients from our phase 2 chemoimmunotherapy trial. Peripheral blood samples from patients with GC undergoing first-line pembrolizumab plus XELOX therapy were monitored using high-dimensional cytometry. Matched paired-tissue single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed. Samples were collected from 24 patients at baseline, after one cycle of XELOX (FU1), and 18 weeks after pembrolizumab addition (FU2). Natural killer cell (CD3-NCAM +) and myeloid cell (CD11c + or CD14 +) subsets increased during chemoimmunotherapy.—At FU1, the proportion of PBMC monocytes was significantly higher in responders compared to non-responders. Consistent with the increase in monocyte-derived macrophages (M1-like) in paired tissues, monocytes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells increased at FU1. Immunosenescence score revealed recently mobilized monocytes infiltrating the tumor bed after chemotherapy. Gene expression analysis showed significantly upregulated CXCL8, CCL3, and CCL4 in FU1 responders. Early elevation of circulating monocytes correlated with better survival. After adding pembrolizumab, memory CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CD27 + CD28 + CD45RO +) T cells increased in responders compared to non-responders. Our results elucidate the serial immunological landscape underpinning favorable responses to first-line ICI plus chemotherapy in patients with gastric cancer. Introduction

The treatment landscape for gastric cancer (GC) has evolved significantly in the past few years, with the emergence of immunotherapy for patients with stage IV disease. Several immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been assessed in combination with standard chemotherapy in a first-line setting and have shown an improvement in the overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone.[1–3] Programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1), which is easily assessable, has been used as a biomarker in several landmark trials; however, a single biomarker is inadequate to identify patients with GC who have the potential to benefit from anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Additionally, we recently conducted a phase II first-line trial, which assessed the sequential addition of pembrolizumab to 5-FU/platinum chemotherapy for AGC to explore the mechanism of ICI over chemotherapy.[4] We found that chemotherapy induced a treatment-favorable tumor microenvironment (TME) early on, with tumor-reactive T-cell and M1-like macrophage interactions, and pembrolizumab induced increased CD8 + T-cell infiltration and the development of an immunity hub involving the tumor-reactive CXCL13 T-cells and epithelial interferon-stimulated gene programs. Although examining immune responses in the TME can be very insightful, tumor tissue is not always accessible. This emphasizes the need to identify blood biomarkers that are linked to clinical success.[5, 6] Previously, higher frequencies of naïve CD4 + T cells and lower frequencies of CD161 + Th17 and CCR4 + Th2 cells have been reported in patients with urothelial and renal cell cancer with a favorable response to ICIs.[7] Whereas increased CD4+FOXP3+ T cell density in GC tumors having a close interaction with CD8.+ T cells and increased effector and central memory CD4 + T cells in peripheral blood can be associated with good prognosis.[8]

However, the ability of noninvasive strategies such as peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) profiling to predict responses to ICI therapy remains unclear. Therefore, identifying the key responding cell types and immune systems could offer crucial insights into treatment response and resistance mechanisms. Consequently, we aimed to evaluate the potential immune mechanisms at the systemic level using mass cytometry (CyTOF) using serially collected peripheral blood samples from patients with GC treated with first-line pembrolizumab 5-FU/platinum chemotherapy.

Materials and methods

Blood collection and preparation

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 24 patients with AGC between September 2020 and April 2022 at the Samsung Medical Center (SMC). All patients were enrolled in an open-label, non-randomized, investigator-initiated phase II trial (ClinicalTrial.gov identifier: NCT04249739) and received capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (XELOX) and pembrolizumab as first-line treatments. Serial peripheral blood samples were collected from patients on day 1 before cycles 1, 2, and 7. Subsequently, PBMCs were isolated from whole blood using a Ficoll gradient. All samples were processed within 12 h after the blood was drawn and frozen in freezing medium for storage.

High-dimensional mass cytometry (MC)

At least 1 × 105 PBMCs were prepared per sample for high-dimensional mass cytometry analysis. The cells were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Initially, the cells were washed with MaxPar cell staining buffer (Standard BioTools Inc., South San Francisco, CA, USA). Cells were stained with 32 surface antibodies (Supplementary Table 1), including TIGIT antibody (Anti-Human TIGIT, MBSA43 clone), pan-keratin antibody (Anti-Human Pan-keratin, C11 clone), and a 30-marker antibody panel, Maxpar Direct Immune Profiling Panel (Standard BioTools Inc., South San Francisco, CA, USA). The cells were subsequently fixed with FA solution diluted in 16% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). After fixation, DNA was stained with iridium intercalator and incubated at 2 – 8 °C overnight to detect nuclei. EQ4 beads (Standard BioTools Inc, South San Francisco, CA, USA) were added to cells to make up the final concentration of EQ4 beads to 10% before data acquisition on CyTOF XT (Standard BioTools Inc, South San Francisco, CA, USA). Cells were analyzed using CyTOF and the data were normalized based on the events of the EQ4 beads using CyTOF software version 9.1.

Cell segmentation and cell phenotyping of high-dimensional data

Normalized data were exported in the fcs format using the CyTOF software. Data clean-up was performed using the FCS Express software version 7.18.0021 (De Novo Software, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

After cleanup, the data were pre-processed using the CATALYST R package. The data were clustered into 20 meta-clusters. First, we annotated each cluster as a T cell subset, natural killer subset, B cluster, myeloid subset, or an unclassified subset (CD45 negative subset) for the first lineage of cell clustering. Unclassified subsets were excluded from further analysis. After the first clustering, each subset was re-clustered to annotate additional details. Myeloid sub-cluster annotation was performed by examining CD14 + HLA-DR + monocytes vs CD14 + HLA-DR-MDSCs; CD11c + CD123- conventional DCs vs CD11c-CD123 + plasmacytoid DCs. The T cell subset was re-clustered and annotated into six clusters: CD8-positive and CD4-positive activated T cells, CD8 or CD4 double negative (DN) CD3-positive T cells, CD161-positive double negative (DN) T cells, NKT cells, and gamma delta T cells (Tgd cells). CD8 + T cells were reclassified to identify more details. The myeloid subsets were clustered into seven clusters. All clustering and dimensional reduction were performed using the FlowSOM and UMAP functions in the R software.

Tissue single-cell RNA-seq analysis from paired patients

Comparison of cell populations and investigation of gene expression were performed after the subset of patients from the previous study[4] matched with the PBMC. The module scores related to immune senescence [9] were estimated using the UCell algorithm.[10] Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two responding groups were analyzed using embedded functions in the Seurat package (version 4.3.0).

Random forest analysis to predict response at early treatment

Random forest classification was performing using the caret R package (version 6.0) to evaluate the importance of immune cell subsets at the FU1 timepoint in predicting clinical response. The input dataset included detailed myeloid and T cell subsets and binary response labels. Model performance was evaluated tenfold cross-validation, a standard approach balancing robustness and computational efficiency. Variable importance was calculated using the varImp function and visualized to highlight predictive immune populations.

PBMC inflammation (PI) score construction and stratification

To construct the PBMC inflammation (PI) score, we focused on myeloid lineage subsets at the FU1 timepoint. Random forest classification was performed to identify the most predictive of treatment response. Based on importance ranking, monocytes and their lineages were selected and incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model. The PI score was identified as:

PI score = 3.116 × monocytes + 3.165 × MDSCs + 7.156 × cDCs + 6.018 × pDCs + 6.170 × pre-pDCs -3.043.

This model demonstrated moderate predictive power (p = 0.026), and the coefficient significance values are provided in Supplementary Table 3. To stratify patients for survival analysis, we applied the maxstat packages (version 0.7), which identify the optimal cutoff by maximizing the standardized log-rank statistic. The best cutoff corresponded to the 12th-ranked patients, resulting in two groups (n = 12 each) categorized as high and low PI score cohorts.

T Cell subset identification and annotation

T cells were initially classified into six major subsets based on canonical surface markers: CD8 T cells (CD3 + CD8 +), CD4 T cells (CD3 + CD4 +), mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells (CD3 + CD161 +), natural killer T (NKT) cells (CD3 + CD56 +), double-negative T cells (CD3 + CD4- CD8-), and γδ T cells (CD3⁺ TCRγδ⁺). Among these, CD4 and CD8 T cells were predominant populations and were further re-clustered according to the expression of functional and differentiation markers to enable detailed phenotypic annotation. CD8 T cells were subdivided into naïve cells (CCR7 + CD45RA +), memory cells (CD45RO + CD24 + CD28 +), effector memory (TEM; CCR7- CD28-), terminally differentiated effector memory (TEMRA; CCR7- CD45RA +), and exhausted cells (CCR7- TIGIT +). Similarly, CD4 T cells were further divided into naïve cells (CCR7 + CD45RA +), memory cells (CD45RO + CD27 +), and regulatory T cells (CD25 + CD127-).

Statistical analysis

Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier plots and the Cox proportional hazards model. The log-rank test was used for p-values in the survival analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, www.R-project.org). Two independent groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. All variables were compared between pre-treatment (baseline) and on-treatment (follow-up) groups using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All p-values were two-sided, and the results were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of GC patients and study workflow

The overall study design is shown in Fig. 1a. PBMCs were collected from 24 patients with AGC at three time points. Their median age was 55 years (range, 34–75 years), and 80% of the patients were male. All patients had HER2 negative tumors with microsatellite stability (MSS). Only one patient (S21) had an Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) positive tumor, whereas 87% of the patients showed PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 (Table 1). All patients received the first cycle of XELOX chemotherapy, were treated with XELOX plus pembrolizumab from the 2nd cycle and continued until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. The overall response rate was evaluated using RECIST v1.1 as follows: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). Patients were divided into responders (CR and PR) and non-responders (SD and PD). Fifteen patients showed PR, and nine patients showed SD as their best response; the overall response was 62.5%. With a median follow-up duration of 23.0 months, the median OS was 19.9 months and median progression-free survival (PFS) was 8.3 months.

Chemoimmunotherapy remodels immune cell-type composition in PBMC

We performed high-dimensional protein profiling using MC to profile changes in the proportion of immune cells in PBMCs during chemoimmunotherapy. Broad immune cell clustering was performed by filtering data obtained from MC according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Supplementary Fig. 1a-b). After filtering, unsupervised clustering was performed using self-organizing map (SOM) algorithms. We identified five clusters using the broad immune cell markers CD45, CD3, NCAM, CD19, CD11c, and CD14. These clusters were further classified into five subsets. The first subset, CD45-negative, was classified as an unclassified subset and was excluded (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Other subsets were classified as T cell subsets (CD3 positive), natural killer cell subsets (CD3 Negative, NCAM positive), B cell subsets (CD19 positive), and myeloid cell subsets (CD11c positive or CD14 positive) (Fig. 1b, supplementary Fig. 1d, except the CD45-negative cluster). Natural killer cells increased during chemoimmunotherapy treatment (Fig. 1c, mean proportion at BL, 18.5% [range, 2.9–44.8]; mean proportion at FU1, 19.75% [range, 5.8–34.7]; mean proportion at FU2, 22.52% [range, 9.6–53.9]). Myeloid cells increased during the treatment (Fig. 1c, mean proportion at BL, 14.17% [range, 0.9–30.1]; mean proportion at FU1, 14.62% [range, 3.8–46.4]; mean proportion at FU2, 17.9% [range, 4.5–68.3]). In contrast, chemoimmunotherapy led to a significant decrease in the number of circulating B lymphoid cells (Fig. 1d, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, BL vs. FU1, p = 0.002; BL vs. FU1, p = 0.0107; BL vs. FU2, p = 0.0001). To further investigate subtype-specific immune dynamics, we incorporated Supplementary Fig. 1e, which displays longitudinal changes in immune cell composition stratified by TCGA molecular subtype (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Notably, an increase in NK cells and a decrease in B cells at follow-up were consistently observed across both subtypes. In the GS subtype, however, we identified a significant reduction in total T cells at FU2 (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) and a non-significant trend toward increased total myeloid cells. These changes were not observed in the CIN group. Further examination of T cell and myeloid subpopulations did not reveal substantial subtype-specific alterations beyond the total cell level, indicating that the observed differences are primarily confined to broader immune compartments rather than specific immune subsets.

An early increase in circulating monocytes is associated with inflammatory macrophage infiltration and reflects tumor macrophage content

In a previous study,[4] we found that early macrophage repopulation after one cycle of XELOX, and an increased M1/(M1 + M2) ratio were related to a better response to chemotherapy with anti-PD1 therapy. In the downsizing cohort, which consisted of blood-tissue paired samples (n = 24), the results were repeated (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In this context, we explored the association of monocytes in PBMC with monocytes-like macrophages (monocyte-derived macrophages), which are prematurely differentiated and may originate from PBMC. We first reclustered myeloid subsets from PBMCs and classified them into seven subsets. Monocytes and dendritic cells were classified based on CD14 and CD11c expression. Monocytes were divided into monocytes and myeloid-derived suppressive cells based on CD14 and HLA-DR expression. Subsets of dendritic cells were divided into conventional dendritic cells (cDCs), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), and premature pDCs (pre-pDCs) based on the extent of CD11c and CD123 (IL-3Rα) expression. Neutrophils and basophils were classified based on CD66b and IL-3R signals. However, these granulocytes comprised < 2% PBMCs; therefore, we excluded them from further analysis. We observed diverse changes in myeloid subtypes during one cycle of chemotherapy between different responses (Fig. 2a). While intra-group changes in monocyte proportions from BL to FU1 were not statistically significant in either responders or non-responders, the between-group difference in monocyte changes over time was statistically significant (Fig. 2b, Mann–Whitney U test, at FU1, non-responders vs. responders, p = 0.0251).

Next, we refined our tumor-associated macrophage (TAMs) cluster into four main subgroups using scRNA-seq data from patient-paired tissues. Applying the conceptual M1-like and M2-like classification, we found that resident macrophages (annotated by MARCO, C1QC, CD74) and anti-inflammatory macrophages (SPP1, APOE, APOC1) mapped to M2-like TAMs, and monocyte-like macrophages (CD14, FCGR3B, FCN1) and pro-inflammatory macrophages (IL1B, TNF) matched to M1-like TAMs (Fig. 2c). We compared TAMs from tumor tissues at baseline and FU1 and observed that the proportion of monocyte-derived macrophages at FU1 differed between the two response groups (Fig. 2d, Mann–Whitney U test, at FU1, non-responder vs. responder, p = 0.04215). Given that monocytes in PBMCs increased at FU1 and monocyte-derived macrophages increased in tissues at FU1 among responders, we sought to confirm whether recently mobilized monocytes infiltrated the tumor bed at FU1. We used several biomarker genes to define senescent immune cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Immunosenescence is characterized by low-grade inflammation and loss of proliferative ability [9]. Therefore, we first confirmed the diminished module score after one cycle of chemotherapy in the responder group (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, BL vs. FU1, p < 0.001). This suggests that the newly produced monocytes-like macrophages infiltrated the tumor tissues in the responder group. Supporting a greater enriched senescence score in the non-responder group than in the responder group at FU1 (Fig. 2e, Mann–Whitney U test, non-responder vs. responder, p < 0.01), we found an absence of a sequential influx of monocyte-derived macrophages.

We identified 537 DEGs between the non-responders and responders at FU1 (Supplementary Table 2). Among these, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8), a gene reported as a biomarker for PBMC monocyte-derived macrophages, was significantly upregulated in the responder group at FU1.[11] Additionally, CCL3 and CCL4, ligands for leukocyte recruitment and inflammation, respectively, were enhanced in the responder group (Fig. 2f).[12] While the redistribution of monocytes occurred depending on the clinical response, we observed the opposite direction of the relative distribution of pDCs, which are known to have pro-tumor activity[13] (Supplementary Fig. 2d, Mann–Whitney U test, at FU1, non-responder vs. responder, p = 0.04776).

Early elevated circulating monocytes predict chemoimmunotherapy outcomes

We analyzed the correlation between the proportion of specific immune cells and the response to chemoimmunotherapy to confirm our observation that PBMC reflects tissue immune microenvironment changes. We performed a random forest analysis to rank myeloid and T-cell subsets, which we investigated in a previous study [4]. We found that monocytes were the most important determinant of chemoimmunotherapy response (Fig. 3a). Using these important variables, we constructed a prediction model to determine responses. The PBMC inflammation (PI) score was the prediction model constructed using a regression model. The formula used was: PI score = PI score = 3.116 × monocytes + 3.165 × MDSCs + 7.156 × cDCs + 6.018 × pDCs + 6.170 × pre-pDCs − 3.043. This model showed moderate predictive performance (p = 0.026), and the significance of each variable is provided in Supplementary Table 3. We then divided the patients into two groups based on their PI scores: a high-PI group (n = 12) and a low-PI group (n = 12). The low-PI group consisted of patients with scores below the median. The highPI group showed significantly longer PFS and OS compared with the low-PI group (Log-rank test, median PFS, 11.8 months vs 5.5 months, p = 0.005; median OS, 24.4 months vs 14.4 months, p = 0.021).

Dynamics of circulating CD8 T cell levels could reflect tissue CD8 T cell status after anti-PD1 treatment

Serial CYTOF analysis was used to validate whether specific immune cells were consistent between PBMCs and tumor tissues during the seven cycles of pembrolizumab treatment. T cells were re-clustered using the SOM algorithm to further investigate T cell sub-classification. T-cells were classified into six subsets using protein markers described in Fig. 4a. Comparing the two groups of responses, no significant difference was observed in the dynamics of the specific phenotypes of T cells. Therefore, we intended to clarify whether a distinct subtype of PBMC T cells is parallel to tissue T cells, regardless of the response to chemoimmunotherapy. We found that, similar to the elevated proportion of CD8 + T cells in tissues after the addition of pembrolizumab (FU2) (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, BL vs. FU2, p = 0.0332), the CD8 + T cell proportion in PBMCs was elevated at FU2 compared to treatment-naïve (Fig. 4b, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, BL vs. FU2, p = 0.0498). In both tissue groups and PBMCs, the number of CD8 + T cells expanded from naïve to FU1 and FU2, but each patient increased with a random slope. We used a logistic regression model to simplify the level of alteration during treatment. Coefficients were positive in both biopsy methods (Fig. 4c, tissue CD8 T cell, p = 0.0165; PBMC CD8 T cell, p = 0.03934), which implies that the elevation of circulating CD8 T cells as well as tissue CD8 T cells was monitored within the duration of chemoimmunotherapy.

CD8-positive T cell clusters were re-classified to explore the various CD8 phenotypes more thoroughly associated with clinical response. Six different types of CD8 T cells, including Naïve CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CCR7 + CD45RA +), Memory CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CD27 + CD28 + CD45RO +), effector memory CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CCR7-CD28-), cytotoxic CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CCR7-CD56 +), TEMRA CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CCR7-CD45RA +), and exhausted CD8 (CD3 + CD8 + CCR7-TIGIT +) were identified. Further analysis showed that responders had larger populations of early memory CD8 + T cells after seven cycles of pembrolizumab (Fig. 4d, Mann–Whitney U test, at FU2, non-responders vs. responders, p = 0.0251), whereas non-responders had larger populations of naïve CD8 + T cells (Fig. 4e, Mann–Whitney U test, at FU2, non-responders vs. responders, p = 0.002). Thus, CD8 + T cells on non-responders might maintain their undifferentiated state and have relatively lower tumor reactivity than those in responders.

Discussion

Incorporation of ICIs plays a crucial role in GC treatment. Although tumor-related intrinsic factors that influence the response to ICIs have been elucidated in tissue samples, the immune status of tissues and the evolving immune microenvironment can be effectively reflected and monitored via peripheral blood. Therefore, in this study, we analyzed immune cell abundance during chemoimmunotherapy using MC and confirmed that circulating blood could be an alternative method to monitor the immune system reaction to anticancer therapy in patients with GC.

Previous studies have suggested that a specific tumor-infiltrating immune cell signature at baseline tumor tissue could predict the response to ICIs and survival in patients with GC[14] and that the combination of CD8+ T cells and FOXP3+CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood is a significant prognostic marker for patients with GC.[8] However, in the current study, most of the blood immune cell types at baseline did not affect the response to treatment, which is consistent with our previous study based on tumor tissues.[4] Dynamic changes were observed during treatment, with an increase in the proportion of T and NK cells in the overall population[15] and a decrease in the B cell ratio. Although the mechanism underlying B cell changes is unknown, the contribution of the adaptive immune system via B cells and B cell-mediated antibody responses might combat NK and myeloid cells and show inverse relative proportions.

We observed that first-line XELOX chemotherapy remodeled the TME, changing the distinct phenotypes of immune cells. The early repopulation of pro-inflammatory macrophages and monocyte-like macrophages in PBMCs after one cycle of chemotherapy could be a determinant of the response to sequentially combined immunotherapy (Figs. 2 and 3). The subsequent analysis of PBMC at FU2 showed accelerated CD8 T cell proportions and a high proportion of memory CD8 T cells in the responders, similar to the tissue data after adding anti-PD1 (Fig. 4). With regard to ICI treatment, the expansion of effector memory CD8( +) T cells with novel T cell receptors (TCRs) in PBMCs after ICI treatment was associated with better clinical outcomes in patients with lung cancer.[15] Increased CD8 + CD45RO + T cells have also been reported as intratumoral memory T cells in a clinical trial of anti-PD1 in patients with metastatic melanoma patients.[16] Thus, our findings in PBMCs are supported by the fact that tumor-reactive T cells are reinvigorated after pembrolizumab in responders.[17] Furthermore, high levels of infiltrating memory CD45RO + T cells have been correlated with increased survival in large-scale studies of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer[18] and gastric cancer.[19] Although further validation through CD39 and CD103 staining is necessary to clearly identify tumor-reactive CD8 + T cells, our findings suggest that circulating CD8 + T cells reflect the distinct phenotypes of tissue-infiltrating CD8 + T cells and serve as predictors of clinical prognosis.

Myeloid subsets, which include dendritic cells and macrophages, are altered during chemoimmunotherapy, and using the M1/M2 conceptual structure [20, 21]. Accordingly, divergent patterns of M1-like (pro-inflammatory) proportions were observed between non-responders and responders.[4] One novel aspect of our study was the characterization of the senescence score of the repopulated macrophages before and after XELOX treatment. We sought to assess the correlation between the enlarged monocytes observed at FU1 in PBMCs to the differentiation of inflammatory macrophages and shifts between the M1/M2 phenotypes within tumor tissues. We utilized single-cell RNA data to estimate the biological age of macrophages and identified gene-based parameters, including inflammatory chemokines (Fig. 2). Consistent with reports that circulating CD14 + classical monocytes differentiate into inflammatory macrophages[22] our data revealed that the early influx of monocytes significantly influenced subsequent clinical outcomes. Increased circulating monocytes may differentiate into monocyte-derived M1-like macrophages within the tumor microenvironment, as suggested by the elevated presence of pro-inflammatory TAMs expressing IL1B and TNF at FU1 among responders. While the definitive identification of senescent macrophages in vivo remains challenging, our interpretation is based on trajectory-informed transcriptomic states derived from tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) within gastric cancer tissues[23]. Specifically, we observed that non-responders exhibited enrichment in terminally polarized anti-inflammatory M2-like clusters, characterized by elevated expression of CD163, APOE, and SPP1, consistent with previously described dysfunctional or senescent-like TAMs in chronic inflammatory settings [4]. In contrast, responders showed increased abundance of monocyte-like and early pro-inflammatory macrophages, suggestive of a less exhausted, more plastic immune state. While we acknowledge the limitations of defining macrophage senescence based solely on gene expression, our integrative use of trajectory analysis and well-established scRNA-seq markers provides indirect yet biologically plausible evidence of differential functional polarization states, including those with senescence-like [24] features.

Further analysis is required to simultaneously integrate myeloid cells from both PBMCs and tissues using the same single-cell RNA-seq platform. Additionally, developmental trajectory analysis may be necessary.[17] Strategies to target tumor-associated macrophages for repolarization[25] include a variety of methods and targeting agents,[26] and clinical trials are ongoing.

Liquid biopsy, a minimally invasive method for the real-time detection of tumor-related information, is widely used in clinical practice.[27]. In this study, we used CYTOF to focus on immune cells and monitor the clinical outcomes. We found a strong correlation between the changes in TME proportions in liquid and tissue biopsies during treatment. Myeloid repopulation and increased monocyte numbers are indicative of subsequent chemoimmunotherapy responses. Additionally, while the number of CD8 + T cells in PBMCs increased slowly, their behavior mirrored that of infiltrating CD8 + T cells in tumors. High proportions of memory CD8 + T cells after the addition of pembrolizumab were associated with a clinical response.

In conclusion, our results indicate that monitoring PBMCs can effectively reflect changes in the tissue immune microenvironment, with the distribution of specific immune cell types being strongly correlated with clinical outcomes. This proof-of-concept study supports the use of non-invasive PBMCs to track systemic immune responses related to chemoimmunotherapy.
