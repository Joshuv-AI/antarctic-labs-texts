Concentration of volatile organic compounds within indoor dairy facilities
Authors: Afrin Jannat, Rhyse Campion, Alexa Phillips, Bianca Goncalves da Costa, Diego Manriquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVolatile organic compounds (VOCs) are important components of indoor air quality in dairy production systems, yet information on their spatial distribution and dominant compounds within enclosed barns and milking parlors remains limited. This study aimed to characterize VOC profiles and quantify concentrations across indoor locations in commercial dairy facilities operating under different housing and ventilation configurations. Air samples were collected from cow housing barns and milking parlors at two commercial dairies in Weld County, Colorado, including a mechanically tunnel‐ventilated barn with a rotary milking parlor (Farm A) and a naturally ventilated barn with an automated milking system (Farm B). Weekly integrated air samples were obtained using evacuated 6‐L stainless steel canisters deployed for a 7‐day sampling period. Canisters were positioned at three locations within each barn and at the center of each milking parlor, while temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed were recorded simultaneously. A total of 52 VOC species were consistently detected across both dairies. Gas measurements in the barns were averaged by location and ranked by concentration to represent overall conditions. Methane, ethane, and propane were the most abundant compounds in both farms’ barns and milking parlors. Hydrocarbons, including n‐butane, n‐pentane, and i‐butane, as well as acetonitrile, were consistently detected but varied in relative ranking across locations. Spatial variability within barns revealed distinct concentration patterns associated with sampling position. These findings provide characterization of VOC composition in indoor dairy environments and highlight the importance of spatial sampling for identifying dominant compounds, which might help to improve air quality.

INTRODUCTION

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are organic chemicals that easily vaporize at room temperature (Bennett & Inamdar, 2015). Because of their high vapor pressure, they readily enter the air and become part of what we breathe (Miller et al., 2020). VOCs are ubiquitous air components in livestock systems, originating from animals, feed, bedding, and manure (Grout et al., 2020). On dairy farms, diverse VOCs such as alcohols, ketones, and carboxylic acids are emitted by animals, feed, and manure. These compounds can undergo further decomposition during storage or following land application (Grout et al., 2020). Apart from these, VOC can also originate from cows’ breath or burping (Gierschner et al., 2019). Moreover, methanol, acetone, propanal, dimethyl sulfide, 4‐methylphenol (p‑cresol), and other VOC are the dominant emissions from cows and their waste (Shaw et al., 2007). Reports indicate that dairy barns emit more VOC than sheep pens, with oxygenated compounds and hydrocarbons being the dominant emissions (Haider et al., 2024). Ethanol is the predominant VOC emitted from silage, while acetic acid is the primary VOC from manure (Aizawa et al., 2022; Hafner et al., 2013).

VOCs represent an occupational health concern as they may negatively affect air quality and have been linked to health risks for humans and animals (Ancione et al., 2021). For instance, total volatile organic compounds (TVOCs) can trigger respiratory symptoms either directly or through synergistic interactions with other airborne contaminants (Basinas et al., 2017). Humans and animals exposed to VOC in barn environments can experience headaches, allergic skin reactions, nose and throat soreness, fatigue, nausea, dizziness, and respiratory symptoms such as asthma, rhinitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, due to prolong exposure to VOC in barn environments (Barbeş et al., 2024; Basinas et al., 2017; Beck et al., 2007; Li et al., 2024). Moreover, exposure to various VOC species at low concentrations over short periods has been reported to increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases (Wang et al., 2024). In addition to direct health effects, VOCs also contribute to the formation of ozone (O3) and fine particulate matter, and are a major source of unpleasant odor issues (Yuan et al., 2017). In the outdoor atmosphere, VOC oxidation in the presence of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and sunlight can generate organic peroxy radicals and hydroperoxy radicals, which convert nitric oxide (NO) to nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and subsequently promote photochemical ground‐level ozone (O3) formation (Jin et al., 2020). Therefore, VOC emitted from dairy facilities may be transported outdoors and contribute to downwind photochemical reactions, making VOC profiling relevant for evaluating the broader air‐quality implications of dairy operations (Hafner et al., 2013). The chemical reactivity of VOC also influences their potential to form O3 and secondary organic aerosols, further supporting the importance of characterizing VOC profiles in livestock production environments (Kammer et al., 2020). In dairy cattle, increases in particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) and O3 have been linked to decreased milk yield and elevated somatic cell counts in dairy cows (Beaupied et al., 2022). Moreover, fine particulate matter can be inhaled and deposited in the respiratory tract of both animals and humans, potentially leading to chronic cough, inflammation, and bronchitis (Li et al., 2024). In humans, O3 can cause respiratory symptoms like coughing, throat irritation, chest pain, shortness of breath, and airway inflammation (Nuvolone et al., 2018). Given these concerns, characterizing VOC concentrations, profiles, and variability in dairy barns is crucial to enhance air quality, occupational safety, and support animal health and productivity. Thus, this study aimed to assess the chemical profiles of VOC at various locations within dairy barns and milking parlors to identify dominant compounds. Additionally, we aimed to document spatial VOC concentrations across two dairy housing systems, considering how ventilation and management might shape VOC levels.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study sites

This study was conducted at two commercial dairy farms (Farm A and Farm B) located in Weld County, Colorado. Farm A consisted of three tunnel‐ventilated free stall barns, each housing approximately 2000 lactating cows, and a 90‐stall rotary milking parlor. Due to sampling feasibility, one tunnel‐ventilated barn was selected for inclusion in this study. The dimensions of the study barn were approximately 290 m in length and 70 m in width, while the rotary milking parlor measured 39 m in length and 35 m in width. The barn was equipped with 40 exhaust fans located on the southern end of the barn. In addition, the barn had four rows of circulation fans, with 14 fans in each row, to support internal air movement throughout the barn. The attached milking parlor was equipped with 18 exhaust fans and an additional 30 circulation fans distributed throughout the parlor and cow waiting area. During sampling, fans were operated according to the farm's routine ventilation management practices. In Farm A, manure was removed using a manure vacuum machine pulled by a tractor after each of the three daily milking sessions.

Farm B milked approximately 400 lactating cows and utilized a naturally ventilated free stall barn equipped with a centrally located automated milking system (AMS) consisting of six milking units. The housing barn measured 132 m in length and 41 m in width, and the automated milking area occupied approximately 20 m in length and 12 m in width within the barn. Farm B was a naturally ventilated free stall barn with manually curtain‐controlled sidewalls, end walls each with an opening, and a continuous open ridge running along the entire length of the barn. The ridge opening was approximately 0.3–0.5 m wide. Alleyway doors were open during the sampling period. Wind direction was recorded during VOC sampling, with a mean of 156.5° and a range from 0° to 355° with a high standard deviation (SD) of 162.21°. In Farm B, manure was removed using an automated manure collection system, where four Lely Discovery Collector manure robots vacuumed manure from the barn alleys throughout the day and night and transported it to the manure pit.

VOC sampling strategy

In both farms, weekly air samples were collected using evacuated 6‐L stainless steel canisters (Entech Instruments) coupled with CS1200E flow regulation systems (Entech Instruments). The flow control system enabled each canister to slowly fill over a 7‐day sample period at a flow rate of 0.84 L/day. Canisters were cleaned using an Entech 3100 Canister cleaning system (Entech Instruments) before field deployment by evacuating the canister to 10−2 torr and purging with ultra‐high purity nitrogen for eight cycles at 80°C. The canisters were installed at a height of 5 feet from the ground at both farms to maintain consistency in sample collection.

Environmental monitoring

During the sampling period, environmental variables, including air temperature, relative humidity, wind direction, and wind speed, were continuously recorded at on‐site weather stations (HOBO U30 Station, Onset Computer Corporation) at both farms. These data were used to provide an environmental context for the measured VOC concentrations. Reference VOC concentrations used for comparison were obtained from outdoor air measurements collected at an open‐field site in Larimer County, Colorado, by the Colorado State University Department of Atmospheric Science. These samples were collected using evacuated 6‐L stainless‐steel canisters coupled with CS1200E flow regulation systems (Entech Instruments), following the same canister‐based sampling approach used for indoor VOC measurements. These reference values were used to provide a regional outdoor background context for interpreting indoor VOC concentrations. The reference samples were not collected directly upwind of the dairy barns during the same sampling events; therefore, they should be interpreted as regional outdoor reference values rather than site‐specific upwind background concentrations.

Laboratory analysis

After sampling, the canisters were transported to the Chemical Atmospheric Laboratory at Colorado State University for VOC identification and concentration analysis. Canister air samples were analyzed using a five‐channel, three–gas chromatograph system (three flame‐ionization detectors, one electron‐capture detector, and one mass spectrometer) following published methods (Barkley Cushing, 1998; Russo et al., 2010; Zhou et al., 2009). For each sample, a 1363 cm3 aliquot was cryo‐trapped at standard temperature and pressure, warmed to 80°C, injected, and split across five streams targeting non‐methane hydrocarbons (NMHCs), halocarbons, alkyl nitrates, oxygenated volatile organic compounds (OVOCs), and reduced sulfur compounds. Whole‐air standards were analyzed approximately every 10 samples; analytical precision expressed as relative standard deviation typically ranged from 1% to 8% (NMHCs), 3% to15% (halocarbons), 3% to 8% (alkyl nitrates), 3% to 5% (sulfur), and 8% to 10% (OVOCs). Although ∼60 VOC species were quantified, downstream analyses focused on 52 compounds consistently detected at both dairies. Although methane is commonly treated separately as a greenhouse gas and excluded from regulatory definitions of VOCs due to its low photochemical reactivity, it was included in this study to support characterization of indoor dairy air composition. The objective of this work was not to quantify regulatory VOC emissions, but to identify the dominant volatile compounds present within enclosed barn and milking parlor environments. Because methane is continuously produced through enteric fermentation and manure‐related processes and often represents the most abundant volatile constituent in dairy facilities, its inclusion provides important context for interpreting compound dominance, spatial patterns, and overall indoor air quality conditions.

Data analysis

Data analyses were carried out in R (version 4.3.2). Descriptive statistics calculations were computed for VOC and the environmental wind speed, temperature, and relative humidity during the study period for both study farms. Descriptive statistics included the mean, SD, minimum, and maximum values. For barn samples, average concentrations were first calculated for each VOC within each farm, and compounds were then ranked from highest to lowest based on these average concentrations. Because VOC concentrations were reported in different units, all concentrations were converted to a common unit, ppmv, before ranking. After the top compounds were identified, concentrations were presented in the figures using conventional units for readability, with methane reported in ppmv (parts per million by volume) and other VOCs reported in ppbv (parts per billion by volume) unless otherwise noted. The full list of identified VOCs and measured concentrations from both farms are provided in Table S1 (cow housing barns) and Table S2 (milking parlors).

In Farm A, the average wind speed was 1.7 m/s with an SD of 0.6 m/s, and the minimum and maximum speeds were 0.5 and 3.0 m/s, respectively. The mean ambient temperature was 13.5°C with an SD of 6.8°C, the minimum was 0.9°C, and the maximum was 27.2°C. In Farm A, the mean wind direction was 300.3°, ranging from 286.4° to 311.7° with an SD of 5.4°, indicating relatively consistent wind direction during sampling. The average horizontal wind speed of Farm B was 0.00 m/s. The average ambient temperature throughout the study period was 13.2°C with an SD of 6.7°C, the minimum was 0.2°C and the maximum was 27.3°C. The average relative humidity was 45.6% with an SD of 16.4%, the minimum was 16.2%, and the maximum was 75.9%. In Farm B, wind direction had a mean of 156.5°, ranged from 0° to 355°, and had a high SD of 162°, indicating substantial variability in wind direction during the sampling period.

At the north entrance of the barn, methane concentration was 22.72 ppmv. Moving south toward the central part of the barn, methane concentration was similar, at 22.88 ppmv. However, methane concentration increased to 40.36 ppmv at the south end of the barn. This location corresponded to the exhaust end of the tunnel‐ventilated barn, where all exhaust fans were installed, and was also adjacent to the manure handling and processing area. In Farm A, manure was removed from barn alleys using a tractor‐pulled manure vacuum machine after each of the three daily milking sessions and transported to the manure handling area, where manure was stored and then mechanically processed through solid–liquid separation/dewatering to reduce moisture content. The processed manure solids were then loaded into trucks and transported off the farm. Therefore, the higher methane concentration observed at the south end may reflect the combined influence of manure‐associated emissions, manure disturbance during handling and processing, and airflow transport toward the exhaust end of the barn.

We found the greatest ethane concentration at the center of the barn, which was 8.00 ppbv, with 4.8 ppbv and 1.9 ppbv at the north entrance and the south end of the barn, respectively. Propane concentration followed the same trends as ethane; the greatest concentration was at the center of the barn compared to the other two locations.

Milking parlor air composition in Farm A

Within the Farm A milking parlor, the top 10 concentrated compounds included methane, ethane, propane, n‐butane, ethene, i‐butane, n‐pentane, i‐pentane, ethyne, and acetonitrile. Of these, methane (12.76 ppmv), ethane (8.21 ppbv), and propane (5.01 ppbv) were the most abundant during the sampling period (Figure 4).

VOC profiles in Farm B

Barn air composition and spatial patterns

Within Farm B's barn, the average top 10 VOCs consisted of methane, ethane, propane, n‐butane, ethene, n‐pentane, i‐butane, acetonitrile, and ethyne (Figure 3). The top three contributors were methane (25.43 ppmv), ethane (12.09 ppb), and propane (8.44 ppbv). One‐week location samples showed methane concentrations of 28.81, 24.30, and 23.20 ppmv, ethane of 14.23, 8.07, and 13.96 ppmv, and propane of 12.02, 4.85, and 8.46 ppmv across the three positions sampled within the barn (Table 1). Concentrations were highest near the east barn gate and AMS milking parlor entrance, with a clear decrease of ethane and propane at the center of the barn. In this naturally ventilated barn, this pattern may be related to differences in air exchange and animal activity across locations. The east barn gate and milking parlor entrance may have experienced greater animal congregation before milking and intermittent vehicle or equipment movement, which could elevate hydrocarbon concentrations near the entrance. In contrast, the center of the barn may have experienced greater dilution through sidewall openings and cross‐barn airflow. Although the west end showed elevated concentrations relative to the center, air recirculation was not directly measured; therefore, this pattern may instead reflect reduced local air exchange, variable wind direction, end‐zone airflow limitations, or nearby animal and manure‐related activity. Overall, these spatial contrasts suggest that end zones may function as areas of lower dilution in a naturally ventilated barn, whereas the center may act as a more effective mixing or dilution zone.

Milking parlor air composition

In Farm B's milking parlor, the major VOC detected included methane, ethane, propane, n‐butane, ethene, n‐pentane, i‐butane, acetonitrile, i‐pentane, and ethyne. The top three VOCs were methane (33.7 ppmv), ethane (13.69 ppbv), and propane (9.21 ppbv; Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

Although the compound list was identical across farms and locations, the top 10 rankings differed. In the Farm A barn, n‐hexane (1.49 ppbv) placed in the top 10, whereas it did not rank in the top 10 for the Farm B barn. Similarly, i‐pentane was found in Farm B barn, which was not among the top 10 VOCs in Farm A barn. These differences reflect variability in the relative abundances of individual compounds within indoor dairy environments, indicating that certain VOCs contributed more prominently to the measured VOC profile at specific locations during the sampling period. By characterizing the most abundant VOC across both barns and milking parlors, our study provides knowledge for understanding the drivers of VOC concentration between different housing systems. Since both study dairies operate as fully indoor systems, we generally observed higher average VOC concentrations in barns and milking parlors compared to open‐air reference values measured by the Atmospheric Science Department at Colorado State University in Larimer County, Colorado (Figures 3 and 4).

In Farm A's barn, methane and propane concentrations were 13.9 and 2.3 times greater than open‐air reference values, while ethane levels were similar to the reference (Figure 3). Conversely, Farm B's barn had methane and ethane concentrations 12.4 and 5.48 times greater than the reference value, while propane was 1.7 times greater. In the milking parlors, Farm A exhibited methane, ethane, and propane concentrations that were 6.2, 1.2, and 3.2 times greater than open‐air values, respectively. Meanwhile, Farm B's milking parlor showed methane, ethane, and propane concentrations that were 16.4, 2.0, and 5.9 times greater (Figure 4). Although these enclosed spaces consistently exhibited higher VOC concentrations than open‐air references, differences in management practices and facility design should be considered when assessing the magnitude of VOC production from each farm and its implications for the environmental footprint of dairy systems.

The VOCs are considered one of the major air pollutants from dairy farms (Place & Mitloehner, 2010) and might have important implications for both human and animal health. Farm workers are routinely exposed to a complex mixture of airborne chemicals (Damalas & Koutroubas, 2016). For instance, Kammer et al. (2020) identified over 400 VOCs in the indoor air, while Filipy et al. (2006) found 82 distinct VOCs in a farm; Guo et al. (2019) found 52 VOCs that were similar to our study.

Figure 3 summarizes the 10 most abundant VOCs measured in the barn areas of Farm A and Farm B. Methane was the single most abundant compound at both sites. In contrast, previous studies Shaw et al. (2007) reported methanol and ethanol as the major VOCs in dairy farms. Similarly, Ngwabie et al. (2008) observed that ethanol and acetic acid together accounted for the largest portion of VOC emissions from a naturally ventilated dairy barn, surpassing methanol. Concentrations were slightly greater in Farm A's larger, enclosed barn (28.6 ppmv) compared to Farm B's barn (25.4 ppmv), despite Farm A employing mechanically forced ventilation. Even though both Farms managed a stocking density close to 100%, this difference may reflect the substantially larger number of cows housed in Farm A, which could have contributed to greater overall methane generation and partially offset the dilution effect of mechanical ventilation. Farm B housed fewer cows and relied on natural ventilation, and the on‐site weather station recorded an average wind speed of 0.0 m/s during the sampling period. This low measured horizontal wind speed may indicate limited wind‐driven air exchange at the monitoring location, which could have reduced methane dilution near emission sources. However, in naturally ventilated dairy barns, airflow can still occur at low velocities due to small pressure gradients, which might explain the horizontal wind speed measurements recorded as 0 m/s. Therefore, the reported value should be interpreted as indicating minimal measurable ambient wind rather than stationary air movement conditions in the study barn.

Beyond methane, a variety of VOCs were common to both barns, including light alkanes such as ethane, propane, and n‐butane, as well as the alkene ethene. For instance, ethane concentrations in Farm B's barn reached approximately 12.1 ppbv compared to 6.4 ppbv at Farm A, while propane measured about 8.4 ppbv at Farm B versus 3.6 ppbv at Farm A. The natural ventilation at Farm B might have allowed greater intrusion of outdoor air or concentrated episodic emissions (e.g., from a tractor operating near the barn), whereas Farm A's enclosed barn with forced ventilation likely limited such incursions. Notably, n‐hexane (∼1.5 ppbv) ranked among the top 10 in Farm A's barn but did not rank in the top 10 at Farm B. Ethanol and acetic acid were not among the top contributors in the week‐long samples from either farm. Although these compounds can be associated with feed‐related sources such as silage fermentation (Hasson et al., 2013), detailed diet and feedstuff information were not available in the present study. Although silage was present in the diets of both farms, we could not determine whether silage or other feed components contributed to the observed VOC profiles. Hasson et al. (2013) were not among the top contributors in our week‐long samples.

Figure 4 illustrates the major VOC measured in air from the center of the rotary parlor at Farm A and the AMS at Farm B. In both parlors, the spectrum of VOC was broadly similar to the barns, with methane again being the most abundant component. However, the relative amounts differed markedly. Farm B's AMS parlor exhibited the highest methane level of any location (approximately 33.7 ppm), exceeding even its barn concentration. In contrast, methane in Farm A's rotary parlor was about 12.8 ppm, much lower than in its barn, and comparable to the methane level reported for tunnel‐ventilated parlors by Jannat et al. (2025). This disparity may reflect differences in parlor configuration, size, enclosure, and ventilation characteristics. Farm A's rotary parlor is a large, open structure integrated with a tunnel ventilation system, allowing frequent air exchange, and cows spend limited time on the platform. Consequently, methane exhaled or eructated by cows may be rapidly diluted or exhausted. Conversely, Farm B's AMS parlor is a small, enclosed space where methane can accumulate if multiple cows visit the robot in quick succession without active ventilation.

Our findings indicate that methane and light alkanes (ethane, propane, and n‐butane) dominated the VOC profiles in both the barn and parlor environments. This pattern contrasts with several previous studies on dairy farms, which reported oxygenated compounds as the most abundant VOC class. Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in farm operations, environmental conditions, and analytical methodologies. In this study, VOC species, including hydrocarbons, oxygenated species, aromatics, and chlorinated compounds, were quantified using three gas chromatography systems. However, variations in air sampling strategies, sample preparation, and detection techniques across studies are expected to influence VOC composition and relative abundances. For example, Shaw et al. (2007) detected primarily oxygenated metabolites in a chamber study of lactating cows, in which the greatest flux of VOC was methanol, acetone, dimethyl sulfide, and 4‐methylphenol. Similarly, Ngwabie et al. (2008) monitored a German dairy cowshed and reported emission rates dominated by ethanol and acetic acid, followed by methanol, concluding that dairy VOC emissions are largely dominated by oxygenated compounds (Ngwabie et al., 2008). A season‐long barn study in Japan also found ketones, alcohols, and volatile fatty acids accounting for >80% of TVOC mass, with acetone, 3‐pentanone, 1‐butanol, and acetic acid as the top contributors (Tanaka et al., 2019). These OVOCs, many of them fermented or silage‐related, were not among the top 10 in our study, highlighting a notable profile difference. In fact, compounds like ethanol and acetic acid, which were major VOCs in the above studies, appeared only at lower levels in our study barns. By contrast, our study revealed substantial ethane, propane, and other C2–C6 alkanes, which have been seldom highlighted in prior dairy barn analyses.

Our inclusion of methane (measured at ∼12–34 ppmv) is especially distinctive, as most dairy VOC studies either exclude methane by definition or simply report it separately as a greenhouse gas. For instance, a recent farm air quality study reported mean methane around 9 ppm in a barn and approximately 11 ppm in a milking parlor (Jannat et al., 2025), comparable to the lower end of our range, but that study emphasized TVOC measurements of ∼150 ppm without speciating the individual compounds. Thus, while methane was ubiquitously present, its prominence in our top‐ranked list underscores a broader analytical scope in our study. Notably, no other peer‐reviewed barn study to our knowledge has found methane, ethane, and propane collectively dominating the VOC mix, which appears to be a unique outcome of our measurement approach and scope. By contrast, we did observe ethene (ethylene) and n‐butane in our top 10, which aligns with Gierschner et al. (2019), suggesting that some alkenes or unsaturated hydrocarbons can be emitted during farming activities. Aromatic solvents like toluene or benzene, and halogenated hydrocarbons, have occasionally been detected in dairy environments as well, but usually at trace levels. Filipy et al. (2006) reported minor amounts of aromatic and halogenated compounds among a suite of alcohols, acids, ketones, and others. In our study, compounds of those classes (e.g., toluene and chlorinated VOCs) were present at very low concentrations, indicating they are not significant contributors in these particular farm settings. This is consistent with Haider et al. (2024), who reported that halogenated VOCs contribute <1% of total identified compounds in cattle manure gas. Kammer et al. (2020) identified toluene as a major hydrocarbon in a dairy stable; in contrast, we detected toluene only at trace levels in both study locations. Interestingly, a large‐scale dairy investigation in China found halogenated hydrocarbons to be a major VOC class in a cowshed (Guo et al., 2019), in contrast with our findings. In summary, previous findings reveal that many dairy VOC profiles are rich in oxygenated compounds (ethanol, acetone, acetaldehyde, acetic acid, and butyric acid, etc.) and certain sulfur or nitrogen compounds, whereas our profile is skewed more toward hydrocarbons. This discrepancy underscores the influence of factors such as ventilation, farm location, and, critically, differences in sampling procedures (e.g., timing, flow rate, canister collection settings, and laboratory filtering methods), all of which can affect the detection and relative abundance of VOC classes.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the VOC profiles. First, the primary objective was to characterize VOC composition and spatial variability across dairy housing environments rather than to identify or quantify specific emission sources. Therefore, detailed diet formulation and feedstuff information were not collected during sampling. This limits our ability to directly evaluate feed‐related contributions to the observed VOC profiles. Second, although ventilation characteristics were described for both farms, exact fan sizes, fan operating status at each sampling time, and ventilation rates were not measured; therefore, the influence of ventilation on VOC dilution and transport could not be quantitatively assessed. Third, outdoor VOC reference concentrations were obtained from regional open‐field measurements and were not collected directly upwind of each dairy facility during the same sampling events. Thus, these values should be interpreted as regional outdoor reference concentrations rather than site‐specific background concentrations. Finally, cow‐level air velocity and stocking density were not directly measured; therefore, interpretations related to airflow, cow numbers, and methane dilution should be considered cautiously. In Farm B, a recorded wind speed of 0.0 m/s likely reflects limitations of measurement conditions and sensor thresholds rather than the complete absence of air movement. Additionally, due to the west‐east orientation, prevailing winds may not create strong cross‐ventilation directly through the sensor location and, in naturally ventilated systems, airflow is driven by small pressure and temperature differences, which might not have been captured by our anemometer. Future studies should include simultaneous indoor, upwind, and downwind VOC measurements; detailed diet and feed composition data; ventilation‐rate measurements; and cow‐level airflow assessments to better identify source‐specific VOC contributions and environmental drivers of VOC spatial variability in dairy housing systems.

CONCLUSIONS

Different studies employ distinct analytical techniques, and variations in the reported VOC profiles partly reflect methodological selectivity (Kammer et al., 2020). This study identified and characterized VOC in barns and milking parlors of two commercial dairy farms, ranking the most abundant compounds and documenting several species within each environment. Methane was consistently the dominant compound, while hydrocarbons, OVOC, and chlorinated solvents contributed to the overall chemical profile at lower levels. By establishing baseline concentration ranges and highlighting both common and site‐specific compounds, these results provide valuable reference data for future work on air quality, emission inventories, and mitigation strategies in dairy production systems.
