Toward a comprehensive, survivor-centered measure of barriers and facilitators to sexual assault services: findings from cognitive interviews
Authors: Padideh Hassanpour, Jiwon Lee, L. B. Klein, Jeneile Luebke, Taylan T. S. Stulting, Sanika Panwalkar, Kate Walsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough medical, mental health, advocacy, and legal services exist for people who have experienced sexual assault, usage rates remain low, particularly among survivors from historically marginalized backgrounds. A review of existing measures assessing barriers and facilitators to post-assault services found that existing measures had limited inclusion of facilitators and community-level and identity-specific barriers, and were rarely developed with input from people who have experienced sexual assault. To address these gaps, we used cognitive interviews to develop and refine a preliminary measure assessing barriers and facilitators to post-sexual assault service-seeking across the social-ecological model.MethodWe conducted five iterative rounds of cognitive interviews (N = 27) via videoconference between May 2024 and March 2025 with a diverse sample of people who had experienced sexual assault. Using verbal probing techniques, we assessed participants’ comprehension, interpretation, and responses to survey items and gathered feedback on item clarity, relevance, and coverage. Interview data were analyzed using directed content analysis within a social-ecological model that combined deductive main categories with inductively identified categories and subcategories. Items were revised iteratively across survey versions based on participant feedback.ResultsParticipants identified barriers that existing measures had not adequately assessed, including lack of identity and culturally affirming services, language and geographic access barriers, social stigma, mistrust of systems, fear of repercussions, and a range of mental, emotional, and physical challenges. New facilitators included integrative and wraparound service models and trust in services. Participants also identified clarity concerns related to terminology that assumed familiarity with formal service systems and item structures that combined conceptually distinct experiences. Participant feedback informed iterative revisions to improve item clarity, reduce redundancy, and expand coverage of underrepresented barriers and facilitators.ConclusionsCognitive interviews with people who have experienced sexual assault revealed critical gaps in how existing measures assess barriers and facilitators to post-sexual assault service-seeking. Findings also highlighted how terminology and item structure can undermine response accuracy for survivors from diverse survivor backgrounds. Psychometric validation of the revised measure is underway to support its use in research and practice aimed at improving service access for underserved survivors. Finally, these findings reflect the U.S. service, legal, and sociopolitical context in which we developed the measure and may not transfer to other settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15131-8.

Sexual assault (SA) is a pervasive issue in the United States (U.S.) with one in two women and transgender or nonbinary people experiencing unwanted sexual contact in their lifetime [1–3]. Rates are also high among lesbian, gay, bisexual, and queer people [4] and among Black, Indigenous, and multiracial women [1]. SA has been associated with various consequences, including posttraumatic stress disorder, suicidality, chronic pain, sexually transmitted infections, and substance misuse [1, 5, 6]. Although medical, mental health, legal, and advocacy services exist, their usage rates are low. For instance, nationally representative U.S. data indicate that only 21% of survivors [7]1 access medical services post-assault. In a convenience sample of women who sought emergency medical services after SA, more than 75% of survivors experienced clinically significant posttraumatic stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, but less than 40% received any mental health care within the first six weeks [11]. Additionally, fewer than one-third of survivors report SA to law enforcement [12]. Use of advocacy services, such as rape crisis centers, is also low, with estimates across studies ranging between fewer than 5% and about 21% of survivors [13–16].

Barriers to seeking or accessing post-assault services include self-blame, shame, fear, retaliation concerns, lack of support from family or community, distrust in providers, and issues such as cost, transportation, confidentiality, and services that are not identity or culturally affirming [17–19]. Facilitators of service-seeking, which refer to factors that can enhance the likelihood of individuals seeking and using services after SA, include social support, positive past experiences with services, availability of specialized providers, perceived efficacy of services, and accessible information about available resources [19–21]. Survivors of color, including Black/African American, Latine, multiracial, and Indigenous individuals, encounter significant obstacles to obtaining support due to systemic biases and a lack of culturally responsive services [22–24]. For example, survivors note that cultural stigma, language accessibility issues, and mistrust due to historical and ongoing oppression prevent them from seeking or accessing services [25]. Moreover, people who experience multiple forms of oppression, such as racial, gender, and sexuality-based discrimination, report increased experiences of disbelief and negative interactions when engaging with medical and legal institutions [26]. These negative experiences can reduce the likelihood of seeking further assistance [27, 28].

Despite a large literature on barriers and a sparser literature on facilitators, few validated, survivor-informed measures to assess barriers and facilitators exist [29]. The few that do exist have been designed for specific populations including college students [30] and sexual and gender minority youth [31], and largely focus on barriers to specific services, most commonly mental health services [29]. Developing a tool to assess both barriers and facilitators across service types for survivors using a survivor-centered approach—i.e., one that prioritizes survivors’ perspectives, choices, and self-defined needs—is essential for understanding global barriers and facilitators and considering ways to improve access to and engagement with services that can support recovery [32–34]. To address these gaps, the purpose of this study was to use cognitive interviews [35–37] to develop a survivor-centered measure of barriers and facilitators across multiple service domains (medical, mental health, advocacy, and legal) and understand the comprehensiveness of the measure among individuals, particularly those with historically marginalized identities, who have experienced SA.

Review of previous measures

A recent critical review conducted by our team identified gaps in existing measures assessing barriers and facilitators to post-sexual violence services [29]. Of the 10 measures assessed, only two were developed with feedback from survivors in focus groups and interviews [36, 37], and only three assessed facilitators to service-seeking [31, 38, 39]. Gaps in assessing barriers across service areas can hinder our understanding of the wide range of challenges survivors face, such as the cumulative impact of stigmatization [40] or marginalization [41], retraumatization in legal and medical settings [42–44], financial constraints or intimidation in legal contexts [45], and limited visibility or availability of advocacy services [46]. Similarly, gaps in assessing facilitators can hinder our understanding of factors that encourage and motivate service-seeking, such as the role of social support and encouragement from others [21, 47], trust that providers will not judge survivors based on their identities or experiences [31], or awareness of available resources [39]. None of the measures reviewed included barriers and/or facilitators at each level of the social-ecological model. Barriers and facilitators to post-assault service-seeking also operate across multiple levels of influence, from structural factors such as service availability, to individual responses such as shame and fear [19]. The social-ecological model [48] provides a framework for organizing these influences across nested and interacting levels—structural/organizational, community, interpersonal, and individual—and has been widely applied in violence prevention and health services research [19, 49, 50]. Assessing barriers and facilitators across social-ecological levels, rather than concentrating on the individual level, as most measures do, can provide a more complete picture of what shapes survivors’ access to and engagement with services [29]. Lastly, service types have been defined differently across studies with some including formal (e.g., police, medical services) and informal (e.g., family, friends) sources [31] and others focusing on one or a few formal types of services [38, 51, 52]. Barriers encountered when seeking one type of formal service can also impede access to other necessary services [40].

Our review [29] consequently advanced four recommendations for the development of future measures: [1] involve people with lived experience of SA in the development and revision of measure content by eliciting their feedback on item clarity, relevance, wording, and coverage of their experiences; [2] assess facilitators of service-seeking given that few measures have assessed these factors; [3] assess barriers and facilitators across levels of the social-ecological model to capture influences beyond the individual level; and [4] assess barriers and facilitators across multiple service domains—medical, mental health, legal, and advocacy—rather than focusing on a single service type, which can contribute to a siloed understanding of post-assault service use.

Current study

Guided by these recommendations and building on the strengths of existing tools [29], we conducted cognitive interviews with a diverse group of survivors to inform the development of a measure assessing barriers and facilitators to SA services. We conducted cognitive interviews to assess whether participants, including people of color, transgender and gender diverse people, and sexual minority people (lesbian, gay, bisexual, pansexual, and other sexual minority identities), interpreted and responded to questionnaire items as intended by our team, and to gather feedback for refining and improving the questionnaire. Method

Methodological approach

We used cognitive interviewing to assess and strengthen the face and content validity of a measure assessing barriers and facilitators to post-SA service-seeking in the current study. Cognitive interviewing is a qualitative pretesting method used to evaluate how respondents interpret, process, and respond to survey items [35–37, 53–55]. It is suited to the early stages of measure development because the goal is to refine item clarity, relevance, and construct coverage with input from the intended audience—not to establish factor structure, reliability, or validity, which require larger samples and quantitative methods. We selected cognitive interviewing for this stage because we needed to understand whether survivors interpreted items as intended and whether the measure adequately captured the range of barriers and facilitators they experienced. We used the social-ecological model to organize barriers and facilitators from previous measures across four nested and interacting levels: structural/organizational, community, interpersonal, and individual [29]. This framework guided how we categorized participant feedback and structured the measure’s content.

We analyzed interview data using directed content analysis, an approach in which initial main categories are derived from existing theory or prior research, while also accommodating new categories based on feedback from participants [56]. We selected this method because our analytic goals were structured around the social-ecological model, which provided theoretically informed main categories, yet we also needed to remain open to barriers, facilitators, and clarity concerns that participants raised but that prior measures did not represent. A purely inductive approach would not have leveraged our existing framework, while a strictly deductive approach would have prevented us from assessing novel categories that participants raised. Unlike conventional content analysis [57], which derives all categories inductively without preconceived categories, directed content analysis allowed us to begin with these main categories and systematically extend or revise them as participant feedback warranted.

Questionnaire development

To develop the preliminary item pool, we mapped existing measures onto the structural/organizational, community, interpersonal, and individual levels of the social-ecological model. This ensured that the initial draft of the measure provided comprehensive coverage across these interacting levels of influence [19, 49, 50, 58]. We included items that (a) addressed a barrier or facilitator reported across multiple studies or measures in the SA literature [17–19, 29, 31, 40, 45, 59–62], and (b) could apply to people across a range of identities, geographies, and service experiences. We excluded items that were specific to a particular setting (e.g., military [39], pediatric care [51]), or specific services (e.g., understanding doctor’s orders [51]), because our goal was to develop a measure usable with a broad population of adults who have experienced SA and have varied service needs. We also drew on our clinical and advocacy experience with people who have experienced SA to generate items addressing barriers and facilitators we had observed in practice but that were not represented in existing measures. Although some items were removed or consolidated when participants identified redundancy, the preliminary item pool remained large by design. Given our goal of comprehensive coverage across ecological levels and diverse survivor populations underrepresented in existing measures, we prioritized retaining content breadth at this stage, with empirical reduction deferred to psychometric testing where item performance across a larger and more diverse sample can appropriately guide that process.

Participants

A total of 27 individuals who had experienced SA participated across five iterative rounds of cognitive interviews. Recommended sample sizes for cognitive interview studies are typically small, ranging from 5 to 30 participants across multiple rounds [35, 36, 53]. We concluded data collection when no new barrier or facilitator content was identified and feedback shifted from substantive additions to minor wording refinements, consistent with saturation in cognitive interview research [54, 63–65].

Recruitment and procedure

Human subjects’ approval was granted by the University of Wisconsin–Madison Institutional Review Board. Data were collected between May 28, 2024 and March 5, 2025. From May to September 2024, we recruited by emailing organizations that serve people who have experienced SA (e.g., rape crisis centers, domestic violence shelters, anti-violence coalitions, legal service providers, culturally specific advocacy programs) with information about the study, eligibility, compensation, and a link to a Qualtrics screener, which yielded 9 interviews. We recruited primarily through advocacy-based organizations because these agencies were most likely to have direct relationships with people who had experienced SA and the ability to share recruitment materials. We also recruited through legal service providers and professional networks that included forensic nursing professionals. Because the response rate was low, we broadened outreach from September 2024 to March 2025 by contacting more than 60 additional agencies—including local and national rape-crisis centers and shelters (e.g., Cleveland Rape Crisis Center, SafeHouse Center), state and regional anti-violence coalitions (e.g., Minnesota Coalition for Battered Women), culturally specific programs serving Indigenous, Latine, South Asian, Muslim and other minoritized communities (e.g., Sovereign Bodies Institute, South Asian SOAR, Jahajee Sisters, North American Islamic Shelter for the Abused), and tribal or pan-Indigenous organizations (e.g., Minnesota Indian Women’s Sexual Assault Coalition, Yup’ik Women’s Coalition, Wise Women Gathering Place). We targeted these organizations to reach people from racially and ethnically diverse backgrounds who are often underrepresented in research on SA and service-seeking. Each organization was asked to circulate a flyer that linked to the screener, and this strategy increased the number of participants from 9 to a total of 27. We avoided broad social-media recruitment to minimize spam and fraudulent responses [66]. To supplement agency outreach, we distributed the same flyer in local nonprofits, community centers, and other neighborhood spaces, and we sent it through advocacy listservs maintained by one of the authors as well as to selected practitioners and colleagues in our professional networks.

Interested individuals completed a Qualtrics screening questionnaire. Participants were eligible if they were at least 18 years old, lived in the U.S., and had an unwanted sexual experience since the age of 14, consistent with the Sexual Experiences Survey [67, 68]. We asked additional questions about sexuality, gender identity, race/ethnicity, and geographic location (i.e., state and city) to assist with purposive sampling. These responses allowed us to monitor participant demographics and prioritize the inclusion of individuals with diverse racial/ethnic, gender, and sexual identities. Towards the end of data collection, for example, we noted a lack of representation among Indigenous interviewees. In response, we focused recruitment efforts on Indigenous-serving organizations and were able to include four additional Indigenous interviewees. Eligible participants received brief biographies of available interviewers and a scheduling link. One day prior to their interview, we emailed participants a reminder with meeting details and the informed consent form. Interviews were audio-recorded and stored securely until they could be transcribed. We removed all identifying details (e.g., personal names, specific locations) during transcription to protect confidentiality.

Cognitive interviews

We used cognitive interviews with people who had lived experience of SA to understand how they perceived the items, whether they were understandable and relevant, and whether any important questions were missing. Participants were asked about their experiences seeking or accessing medical, mental health, advocacy, and legal services, and barrier and facilitator items were designed to apply across all four service types. Following consent, participants were asked a series of open-ended questions to explore their personal experiences with accessing services after their unwanted sexual experiences. These questions addressed the types of services used, unmet service needs, and any identity-related or cultural considerations that affected their access to or use of services. Participants were invited to share their thoughts on what made it harder or easier for them to seek or access services. Interviews ranged from 39 to 104 min (median time: 55 min), after which participants were compensated with a $50 (USD) gift card to Amazon, Target, or Walmart. All interviews were conducted via videoconference (Zoom) to allow participation across geographic locations and to enable screen-sharing of survey items during the interview. Interviews were conducted in English, the shared language of the research team and all participants, which was necessary for the iterative analytic process central to cognitive interviewing, in which the research team directly evaluates how participants reason about item wording and meaning [36]. Following each interview, participants were emailed a list of national, statewide, and culturally specific support resources related to sexual violence, including general crisis services (e.g., the Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network) and identity-specific organizations serving Indigenous, Black/African American, and lesbian, gay, transgender, queer, intersex, asexual, and Two-Spirited communities.

Four of the study authors—the first, third, fourth, and last author—conducted cognitive interviews using two tools: [1] a semi-structured interview guide (available in Supplemental 1), which provided a consistent set of open-ended questions across all interviews, and [2] the preliminary measure, which consisted of barrier and facilitator items presented to participants on slides for review and feedback. To determine what questions to include in the interview guide, we drew on the core objectives of cognitive interviewing, which focus on evaluating respondent comprehension, interpretation, and ability to respond to survey items. The guide began with contextual questions about participants’ experiences with services after SA to orient the conversation, followed by three appraisal questions applied to the survey items: [1] whether any important content was missing [2], whether any items were confusing or offensive, and [3] whether participants would know how to answer each item. The guide focused on item appraisal rather than eliciting accounts of participants’ assaults or service use histories. Service use histories were specifically assessed during screening prior to the interview.

Consistent with Willis’ cognitive interviewing approach [36], we used verbal probing techniques, including anticipated probes (i.e., pre-planned questions about item clarity, comprehension, and completeness), spontaneous probes (i.e., unscripted follow-ups initiated by interviewers to clarify participant responses), and emergent probes (i.e., questions prompted by unexpected participant feedback). We chose verbal probing rather than a think-aloud approach given the volume of items, as think-aloud would have been impractical to apply consistently across 100 + items within a single session. To minimize interview bias, the interview guide included a set of core questions that we used across all interviews, while the use of four interviewers from different disciplines (clinical psychology, social work, and nursing) helped reduce the influence of any single interpretive lens [36, 37, 69].

The interview guide remained consistent across interviews. However, the measure shown to participants was revised iteratively across five versions (V1–V5) based on participant feedback, which is the intended purpose of cognitive interviewing [36] (see Fig. 1 for item counts and participant assignments per version, and Table 2 for examples of specific revisions). Each participant reviewed only one version. Initially, we presented barrier items first to participants; however, due to participant fatigue and limited feedback on facilitator items, we tested presenting facilitators first in later versions, though this adjustment did not substantially influence the level of detail or volume of feedback participants provided. The final version (V6) reflects cumulative revisions and was not shown to participants (see Supplemental 2 for the full preliminary item pool).

Data analysis

We used a multistage process for data analysis. First, the team took notes on novel observations and met weekly to discuss new insights or item suggestions, which led to iterative revisions of the measure for subsequent interviews. Items were flagged for revision when participants indicated confusion, misinterpretation, difficulty responding, missing content, or wording concerns, or when multiple participants independently raised similar issues. In cases where interviewees provided conflicting feedback, we reviewed the transcript for context and determined as a research team if and how to revise the measure for the next set of interviews.

We conceptualized barriers and facilitators as related but distinct constructs [19, 70, 71]. Barriers were defined as factors that hindered individuals from seeking or accessing services, while facilitators were defined as factors that enabled or encouraged service-seeking [19, 70]. Although some barriers and facilitators share thematic content (e.g., cost and affordability), we did not treat facilitators as the inverse of barriers. As described in the results, several facilitator items that participants identified, such as integrated services and provider trustworthiness, were distinct from, and not merely the opposite of, a corresponding barrier. Whether this conceptual distinction holds empirically will be examined during psychometric testing.

Once we completed the interviews, we applied a primarily deductive approach to directed content analysis, while allowing for the inductive identification of novel categories identified through participants’ feedback [56]. Our approach to coding was informed by existing literature on measures assessing barriers and facilitators to service-seeking [29] and was structured around three main categories: barriers to service-seeking organized across social-ecological levels (structural/organizational, community, interpersonal, and individual), facilitators of service-seeking organized across social-ecological levels (structural/organizational, community, interpersonal, and individual), and clarity concerns. We use the term “main categories” following Assarroudi and colleagues to refer to the deductively derived top-level groupings in the categorization matrix, with “categories” and “subcategories” representing finer levels of organization within each main category [56]. Within each main category, we developed categories and subcategories to organize participant feedback at a more detailed level; examples are described below and documented in full in the codebook (Supplemental 3). Given the complexity of survivors’ experiences, we also attended to how barriers and facilitators often cut across or interact between ecological levels, rather than fitting neatly within a single category [19, 49]. This coding framework was applied consistently across all transcripts regardless of which survey version the participant reviewed. We tracked which version each participant reviewed to contextualize their feedback and iteratively incorporated new categories as participants reviewing later versions raised new patterns, consistent with our combined deductive and inductive approach.

We operationalized clarity concerns as instances where participants explicitly indicated confusion, misinterpretation, or difficulty responding to a survey item [35–37, 69]. We organized clarity concerns into two categories. Terminology issues—instances where specific words or phrases carried assumptions about survivors’ service histories, geographic contexts, or financial situations that made accurate responding difficult—were predefined based on known challenges in survey design. Item structure—instances where the format, phrasing, or logical organization of an item created difficulty, including double-barreled items, matrix formats, and response stems that did not fit the nature of the barrier being assessed—was identified inductively from participant feedback. Identified clarity concerns informed revisions to improve comprehension, response accuracy, and content relevance. Examples of each category are presented in the results and in Table 2.

We organized barriers and facilitators as main categories across four levels of the social-ecological model [19, 48, 49, 70]. Individual-level barriers were operationalized as personal factors that shaped service-seeking; categories included mental, emotional, and physical challenges, shame, and self-stigma. Interpersonal- and provider-level barriers reflected relational dynamics between participants and service providers, family members, or peers that influenced a survivor’s decision to seek help; categories included provider dismissiveness and trauma-informed care (with a subcategory of identity and culturally affirming care). Community-level barriers encompassed broader social and cultural factors that shaped perceptions of services and access to resources; categories included social stigma. Structural- and organizational-level barriers involved systemic issues such as policies, institutional constraints, and resource availability that affected service access; categories included accessibility (with a subcategory of system navigation challenges) and cost and affordability.

Individual-level facilitators reflected personal characteristics or attitudes that contributed to engagement with services [70]; categories included self-efficacy. Interpersonal- and provider-level facilitators assessed supportive relationships or positive provider interactions that encouraged service-seeking; categories included provider support (with a subcategory of trust or belief in services) and family/friend support. Community-level facilitators included social networks, cultural norms, or community resources that made service-seeking more accessible; categories included community support. Structural- and organizational-level facilitators involved institutional or systemic policies or practices that increased access; categories included affordability (with a subcategory of integrated services).

We coded transcripts in batches of four to eight with weekly meetings with team members to discuss subcategories and refine our coding framework [36, 54]. We iteratively incorporated new categories as we identified new patterns in participant responses. We generated categories based on direct participant language (e.g., “fear of retaliation from the person who caused me harm”) and then categorized them under the type of feedback and level of the social-ecological model (e.g., Interpersonal Barrier).

Four trained coders—the first, second, fourth, and last authors—independently applied the coding framework to interview transcripts using MAXQDA, a qualitative data analysis software [72]. Three of the four coders (the first, fourth, and last authors) also served as the interviewers, which allowed them to draw on contextual knowledge from the interviews during coding. The second author, who did not conduct any of the interviews, contributed an independent coding perspective and coded 8 of the 27 transcripts (approximately 30%). Our unit of analysis was verbatim participant quotes from transcribed interviews [36, 37], which were supplemented by interviewer notes taken during and after each interview. To support coding consistency, 10 of the 27 transcripts (37%) were independently coded by a second coder, who then compared their coding in side-by-side comparison spreadsheets to identify and discuss discrepancies. We double-coded a subset of transcripts to refine and stabilize the codebook, after which the remaining transcripts were single-coded. Across all 10 double-coded transcripts, the coders identified some discrepancies that required discussion. These were predominantly differences in the level of categorization rather than substantive disagreement; coders most often differed on whether a quote was best captured by a main category or a subcategory, or on which adjacent subcategory within the same category applied. Substantive disagreements, such as whether a quote reflected a barrier or a facilitator, were rare. For example, one coder placed a participant’s recommendation for more providers with relevant knowledge on Indigenous reservations under the broader category of trauma-informed care, while the other placed it under the more specific subcategory of identity- and culturally-affirming care. Discrepancies like these were resolved through a collaborative review process [35, 36] in which coders, interviewers, and the broader research team were involved at the earliest stages of analysis through weekly meetings where consensus was reached. Because there is no consensus in the literature on interrater reliability standards for cognitive interview data analysis [53], we used a negotiated consensus approach, which some qualitative methodologists argue may ensure greater validity than formal reliability metrics alone [73, 74].

The full codebook, including main categories, categories, subcategories, definitions, and examples, is available in Supplemental 3. Our study combined what Willis (2015) terms “Reparative” and “Descriptive” approaches [36], whereby we identified and revised problematic items (a Reparative approach) and also elicited new content by asking participants what was missing from the measure (a Descriptive approach), which led to the addition of new items. Next, we synthesized participant feedback into a structured summary of each category, survey item, specific issues, resulting modifications, and the corresponding level within the social-ecological model (structural/organizational, community, interpersonal, and individual). We reworded unclear items, expanded response options, and revised or removed items based on feedback.

Researcher reflexivity

Throughout the coding process, we engaged in ongoing reflexive dialogue about how our personal identities, lived experiences, and disciplinary backgrounds shaped our interpretations [75, 76]. Our seven-member research team brings a range of social identities and lived experiences, including differences in race, gender, sexual orientation, socioeconomic status, and cultural background, as well as diverse disciplinary and practice backgrounds. Some members of our team are survivors of interpersonal violence, including SA. This diversity informed our approach to data analysis by fostering both empathy and critical reflection, and guided our efforts to interpret participant feedback with care and accountability. We also drew on our collective experience in trauma-focused research to guide discussions, challenge assumptions, and document interpretive decisions throughout the analysis.

New barriers identified

Structural- and organizational-level barriers

Lack of identity and culturally affirming services

Participants highlighted the need to frame identity concerns as essential rather than preferential. This distinction—between services that feel optional to providers and services that are essential to survivors—was consistently described across interviews with participants from historically marginalized communities. One participant, a nonbinary queer person of Indigenous Caribbean descent, described this directly,…these were non-negotiables for me. I needed my cultural identity present in order to heal…talking to a counselor or a social worker, and if they don’t come from that, they have no knowledge of those communities, they don’t understand.

…these were non-negotiables for me. I needed my cultural identity present in order to heal…talking to a counselor or a social worker, and if they don’t come from that, they have no knowledge of those communities, they don’t understand.

Participants also identified gaps in how existing items framed cultural specificity. Several questioned the labels “Western” and “non-Western,” which we had used to distinguish U.S./European biomedical and psychotherapeutic approaches from other healing traditions. One participant, a genderfluid, nonbinary, bisexual man of Mexican heritage, observed that while Mexico is geographically part of the West, its approach to psychological support—commonly called plática—does not align with the U.S./European clinical paradigm, and suggested that examples would help clarify the distinction: “There is this notion of…who is Western versus who isn’t…It might be helpful to give examples of what Western versus non-Western services, healing medicine, could be like.” Across these responses, participants emphasized that cultural identity was not a preference to be accommodated but a condition for feeling safe enough to engage with services at all.

Language and geographic access

Participants identified language accessibility and geographic distance from services as distinct structural barriers that needed to be reflected in the measure. These were raised independently by multiple participants and treated as distinct from cost or transportation barriers already assessed in the measure.

On language access, participants noted that services unavailable in a survivor’s primary language constituted a fundamental barrier that existing items did not assess. One White, nonbinary, pansexual, and queer participant explicitly flagged the gap: “We haven’t really talked about language at all, so ‘[organizations] didn’t provide services in the language that I speak or my first language.’” A Chinese American, pansexual woman expanded this further, noting that the barrier applied not only to spoken services but also to written materials: intake forms and other documents inaccessible in a survivor’s primary language created additional obstacles.

On geographic access, participants distinguished between transportation barriers—already captured in the existing item pool—and the more fundamental issue of whether services exist within reach at all. One participant from a rural area described this clearly: “In rural areas, [services] don’t exist,” adding that even in urban areas, “there’s so many wait lists…it’s just this catch-22 that doesn’t matter whether it’s rural or urban, the services just aren’t there.” Geographic access, in participants’ accounts, was less about transportation than about whether services were physically present in their communities.

Community-level barriers

Social stigma

Participants described how fear of judgment, blame, and disbelief, especially within their communities or from service providers, discouraged them from seeking or using formal support after an assault. One participant described the blame survivors often encounter: “There’s blame on us. […] It’s not even that we’re not believed, but like, what did you do to cause it?” One Indigenous, bisexual woman described how stigma related to historically marginalized identities and experiences, including sex work, shaped service-seeking: “They really dismiss you when you are in sex work. They just don’t believe you or they don’t care that you were working and got hurt.” A nonbinary and transgender participant described stigma connected to transgender identity and concerns about credibility: “Will I be believed as a survivor because people have a negative idea of what it means to be trans?” These experiences pointed to measurement gaps around blame and judgment that were not assessed in the original item pool, i.e., concerns that were distinct from, though related to, existing items about differential treatment based on identity.

Mistrust of systems

Participants described how witnessing harmful outcomes for others who had sought services, not only experiencing harm themselves, contributed to their reluctance to engage with formal support systems. This form of indirect mistrust, shaped by observed consequences rather than personal experience alone, was not reflected in the existing item pool. One Black, lesbian woman described watching a peer be blamed by a school counselor after disclosing assault: “the guidance counselor was like, ‘I don’t understand why you let this keep happening to you,’ and that really stopped them from pursuing other help.” One South Asian woman described a friend’s experience in which seeking therapy led to a chain of institutional consequences, including a disrupted visa process and a family separated across borders, that the participant raised as a reason why people in their community hesitated to engage with services. In discussing this account, our research team interpreted it as evidence that systemic harms experienced by others in a survivor’s community can function as barriers even for those who have not personally experienced those harms.

Participants also described a form of mistrust that operated differently—not through fear of harm or exposure to others’ negative experiences, but through a cost-benefit appraisal in which the emotional and physical demands of engaging with formal systems outweighed the expected likelihood of getting justice. One Black, queer nonbinary person described declining to pursue legal action while weighing these costs directly against the realistic prospects of getting justice:I chose not to press charges because I care about them, and I chose not to press charges because I have forgiven them, and moved on. And I want this behind me…it was not worth the emotional and physical strain. So it just was, it was going to cost me more than the outcomes because we know more often than not there is no justice.

I chose not to press charges because I care about them, and I chose not to press charges because I have forgiven them, and moved on. And I want this behind me…it was not worth the emotional and physical strain. So it just was, it was going to cost me more than the outcomes because we know more often than not there is no justice.

These accounts pointed to gaps in how mistrust had been measured. Existing items assessed personal distrust of service systems but not the deterrent effect of observed harm to others—a distinction with particular relevance for communities where negative encounters with formal institutions are shared knowledge. Nor did existing items assess the physical and emotional toll of engaging with services as a barrier in its own right, or whether the costs of reporting felt justified when so few cases result in justice [78].

Interpersonal-level barriers

Fear of repercussions

Participants corroborated previously measured concerns about retaliation from the person who caused harm, and expanded the concept in two directions. First, they identified blackmail as a specific and unmeasured form of retaliation, distinct from the more general fear of physical harm, describing scenarios in which perpetrators who learned of service-seeking might leverage that knowledge: “Now all of a sudden your perpetrator has found out that they’re in jeopardy, and they may harm you ‘cause they want to keep you quiet.” Second, participants identified that people who caused harm may hold social or institutional power that could be used to undermine survivors’ credibility or access to services, suggesting an item to capture that dynamic as well. These accounts pointed to two distinct interpersonal barriers, coercive silencing through blackmail and the use of positional power to discourage service-seeking, that existing measures had not separated from general retaliation concerns.

Participants described fears about how disclosure—of both the assault and service-seeking—could affect intimate relationships, and emphasized that these concerns were conceptually distinct from fears about the person who caused harm. Where existing items treated “partner” and “perpetrator” as potentially overlapping categories, participants consistently indicated that these represented separate concerns that warranted separate items: a survivor might fear that a partner would leave upon learning of the assault, and separately fear that a partner would leave upon learning they had sought services. This distinction mattered practically, as the decision to seek services could feel like a separate exposure and one that might affect a current relationship even when the assault itself had not.

Individual-level barrier

Mental, emotional, and physical challenges

Participants revealed that the original item pool had significantly compressed the range of individual-level barriers to service-seeking. A single item, “I felt ashamed or embarrassed,” had been used to represent what participants described as a varied and multidimensional set of emotional, cognitive, and physical states, each of which could operate as an independent barrier.

Participants identified distinct emotional experiences, including fear, overwhelm, loneliness, and low mood, that they understood as separate from shame and from each other, and described specific functional barriers such as exhaustion and competing responsibilities that the original item did not capture. One participant described feeling “disoriented, lost, and unable to continue” because of exhaustion; another urged separating shame from fear entirely and proposed adding “I felt scared.” Others raised avoidance, competing daily demands, and a desire to move forward as reasons people might not seek services, experiences more specific than “I felt ashamed or embarrassed” could accommodate.

One participant distinguished explicitly between depression and motivation as separate mechanisms:I would frame it as my mood was so low, or I was dealing with depression. Low motivation. And you could say mood and motivation has a very different context. My mood and motivation were so low that I could not garner the strength to pursue resources or services.

I would frame it as my mood was so low, or I was dealing with depression. Low motivation. And you could say mood and motivation has a very different context. My mood and motivation were so low that I could not garner the strength to pursue resources or services.

This feedback pointed to a consistent pattern. Collapsing psychological and physical barriers into a single item obscured the distinct mechanisms through which each might discourage service-seeking and made it difficult for survivors whose primary barrier was exhaustion or competing responsibilities to find their experiences accurately represented in the measure.

New facilitators identified

Participants provided less feedback on facilitators than on barriers throughout the study. By V2, we suspected that participant fatigue may have contributed to limited feedback on facilitator items, which appeared after the longer set of barrier items. As a first step, we reordered the measure in V3 so that facilitator items appeared before barriers. When this change did not yield additional facilitator content, our team drew on clinical experience and the existing literature to generate 32 new facilitator items for V4, which we then presented to participants in subsequent rounds to check whether the items resonated with their experiences. The facilitators that participants endorsed and expanded upon extended beyond those included in prior measures [29] and reflected distinct motivations for seeking services rather than inversions of barriers.

Structural- and organizational-level facilitator

Integrative and wraparound service

Participants described the burden of navigating fragmented service systems after SA. One participant explained: “Solve your own case, solve your own mental health issues, solve your own medical needs while balancing everything else.” Another noted that retelling one’s story across providers “has completely stopped me from getting services that I need.” In contrast, participants identified coordinated or co-located services as reducing this burden: “The services [could] be more integrated [which] would be so helpful.” Another participant described the value of having a trusted navigator: “[The service] will take care of you. [The service] will connect you. And then, it’s the ‘point person’ that I can always go to…They could navigate for me in my area so I could just exist.”

Interpersonal-level facilitator

Trust or belief in services

Participants identified positive interactions with providers as encouraging service engagement. One participant emphasized the importance of providers with relevant training: “[The provider] got the training…They understand the intricacies of unwanted sexual behavior…They took the time to educate themselves to get the formal training.” Others described feeling supported when providers took time to explain options and involved them in decision-making: “Providers were committed to giving me the best possible care.”

Clarity concerns

Clarity concerns were frequently cited throughout testing and informed iterative revisions to the measure. We classified these concerns into two categories: [1] terminology issues and [2] item structure. Below, we describe each category with illustrative examples.

Terminology issues

Across interviews, participants identified terminology that made items difficult to answer accurately because it assumed knowledge, context, or experiences that not all respondents shared. The clearest example came from a White, nonbinary/woman, bisexual, and pansexual participant who flagged the verb “seek” in service-seeking items.I think putting “seek” first makes it more victim-blaming-ish. Even though this is not victim blaming at all. But I think putting “receive” first—because “seek” is like, “this is on you. So, go seek it out.” And “receive” is more like, “refer,” and I guess more community based.

I think putting “seek” first makes it more victim-blaming-ish. Even though this is not victim blaming at all. But I think putting “receive” first—because “seek” is like, “this is on you. So, go seek it out.” And “receive” is more like, “refer,” and I guess more community based.

This participant’s concern was not that the word was unfamiliar but that it framed service access as something the respondent initiated. For participants whose contact with services came through referrals, outreach, or someone else stepping in, “seeking” did not match their experience, and an item built around that verb might not register as applicable to them, even if they had in fact received the service. They suggested leading with “receive” instead, which shifted the frame from individual responsibility to something closer to how services often actually reach people.

Double-barreled items were flagged across multiple versions. No participants in V4 or V5 identified redundancy as a concern.

Discussion

Overview of feedback

Findings from cognitive interviews with U.S. adults who have experienced SA underscored that barriers and facilitators to SA services are complex and multifaceted. Participants identified barriers that are undermeasured in existing measures, including lack of identity and culturally affirming services, language and geographic barriers, social stigma, mistrust of systems, fear of repercussions, and mental, emotional, or physical challenges. New facilitators that have been undermeasured in existing measures included a need for integrative care/wraparound services and provider trustworthiness and responsiveness.

New barriers identified

Structural- and organizational-level

Lack of identity and culturally affirming care

Participant feedback emphasized that identity and culturally affirming services are not subsidiary preferences but often essential components of seeking and using formal services. This perspective challenges the common assumption in many service models that U.S./European biomedical care is the standard [79, 80]. Survivors who hold historically marginalized racial and ethnic identities in the U.S., including Black, Latine, multiracial, Indigenous, and immigrant individuals, have consistently reported feeling alienated by service systems that fail to acknowledge their cultural values, spiritual needs, or intersecting identities [22].

Language and geographic access

Although logistical barriers to seeking and receiving post-assault services are well-documented [18, 40], participant feedback highlighted dimensions that remain underassessed in existing measures. For example, only a small number of prior measures have included language-related barriers [38, 45, 51]. Whereas the item we included refers generally to language access in services, feedback from participants also included specific concerns about the accessibility of written materials, such as intake forms, in their primary language. Future measurement efforts may wish to differentiate between spoken and written language accessibility to better reflect the full range of barriers experienced by survivors with limited English proficiency.

Geographic access was also identified as a distinct and undermeasured barrier. Transportation challenges have been commonly assessed in measures assessing barriers to service-seeking post-assault [29], but few measures have directly assessed whether services are physically available or reachable in a survivor’s local area. This distinction is especially important given persistent disparities in the distribution of services for SA-specific concerns [81–83]. For example, a report from the U.S. Government Accountability Office found that several states lacked a sufficient number of SA forensic examiners to meet the demand for exams, with access particularly limited in rural communities [84]. Related research has also found that needing to travel to another community for a forensic exam is associated with delayed presentation to care [83]. Some researchers have operationalized this as “relative location” and found that survivors who received exams in a different community than where the assault occurred tended to present later than those who accessed care locally [81]. Additionally, many rural counties with critical access hospitals routinely transfer SA patients to SA nurse examination programs located hours away [82]. These transfers can delay time-sensitive treatment and forensic evidence collection, increase transportation and law enforcement burden, and potentially contribute to greater distress for SA survivors [83].

Community-level barriers

Social stigma

Participant feedback reinforced prior research identifying community-level stigma as a salient deterrent to seeking formal support [85]. Fears of being blamed or judged, both by providers and within one’s own community, can contribute to and discourage survivors from accessing needed services [86]. Research by Mellen and Hatzenbuehler found that sexual violence-related stigma was associated with higher rates of perceived past-year treatment need [87], yet qualitative work suggests that stigma can be a barrier to treatment-seeking due to fears of judgment and harm from providers [77, 88]. Importantly, participant feedback also underscored how stigma intersects with historically marginalized identities, compounding barriers for survivors engaged in sex work and those who are transgender. These observations align with broader work demonstrating the double burden of stigma among survivors who engage in sex work [89] and heightened vulnerability to distress among sexual minority survivors [90]. Taken together, these insights underscore the importance of including stigma at multiple levels of the social ecology when assessing factors that discourage post-assault service-seeking and use.

Mistrust of systems

Participant feedback emphasized how witnessing or experiencing harm during service-seeking, such as invalidation by service providers or damaging outcomes from mandated reporting, can foster mistrust in formal systems. Prior research has shown that even the possibility of law enforcement becoming involved can deter people who have experienced intimate partner violence and SA from seeking help [45, 91, 92]. Wright and colleagues documented that survivors often fear deportation, child removal, or being treated as offenders themselves when formal systems are involved [92]. While some measures account for mistrust as a barrier to seeking services (e.g., “I thought it would be hard to trust a stranger to help” [30] and “I didn’t trust the people in the system, such as police, lawyers, and/or judges” [45]), few tools assess indirect mistrust, i.e., where people avoid services based on what they have seen happen to others. Thus, it would be important to include this form of mistrust in a measure on barriers to seeking services after SA.

The participant who shared that they chose not to pursue legal action because the emotional and physical cost of doing so outweighed the likelihood of a just outcome illustrated how barriers to service-seeking can operate simultaneously at structural and individual levels, where an appraisal of systemic failure and the personal toll of engaging with legal services reinforced one another in the decision not to seek services. This appraisal was informed by a broader awareness of how rarely SA cases brought to legal authorities result in accountability [93]. Measures that assess barriers at only one level of the social ecology may risk obscuring the extent to which survivors’ decisions not to seek help reflect rational responses to systemic failure rather than individual attitudes or minimization of harm.

Interpersonal-level barrier

Fear of repercussions

Although the current study aimed to receive feedback on a preliminary set of items assessing barriers and facilitators related to SA broadly, we received feedback to expand barriers specific to intimate partner and domestic violence barriers, such as financial dependence, housing entanglements, and caregiving responsibilities. While we did add two items that were partner-specific (see Table 2) and clarified distinctions between “partner” and “person who caused harm,” we chose not to elaborate further on the many specific barriers that might arise in the context of intimate partner and domestic violence for both conceptual and practical reasons. First, several measures have been developed specifically to assess service-seeking in the context of intimate partner violence [31, 94, 95]. Second, although SA and intimate partner violence co-occur [96, 97], some risk factors and safety needs associated with partner violence (e.g., threat of losing custody of one’s children for being exposed to the very violence for which the adult survivor is seeking services) can differ meaningfully from risk factors and safety needs experienced in the context of sexual violence more broadly in ways that may warrant distinct and comprehensive assessment. Practically, including a broader set of intimate partner violence-specific barrier items could reduce the measure’s relevance for survivors whose experiences of SA did not involve a partner and increase overall participant burden.

Individual-level barrier

Mental, emotional, and physical challenges

While individual-level barriers are well-documented in prior work [30, 45, 52, 60], this was the only category in our study where participants suggested multiple new items. Most measures consolidate psychological barriers into a single item combining emotions (e.g., “I felt ashamed or embarrassed”). However, participant feedback in our study highlighted how this approach can obscure the unique and multifaceted emotional responses participants experienced. Although most measures cannot feasibly include multiple items for each emotional, cognitive, and physical barrier due to concerns about length, our context allowed for more detailed parsing. This helped reveal nuanced barriers to service-seeking that broader categories might miss.

New facilitators identified

Participants provided far more feedback on barrier items than facilitator items. Despite reordering facilitator items to the start of the interview in later versions, based on evidence that items appearing first receive more attention (the primacy effect [98]), and expanding the facilitator item pool, the volume of facilitator feedback remained lower than for barriers. This pattern may reflect that few people who have experienced SA use formal services. In a national sample of rape survivors, 40% had never sought mental health or medical services [99]. Only about one-fifth of adult rape survivors obtain medical services post-assault [7] and just 23–34% of assaults were reported to law enforcement from 2019 to 2020 [12]. Taken together, these data suggest that survivors face a greater number of factors that deter, rather than encourage, service-seeking and use. Although barriers and facilitators are often treated as opposite ends of a single continuum, access-to-care frameworks suggest they may function as distinct constructs [71]. These models conceptualize service engagement as shaped not only by obstacles that impede access but also by enabling conditions that actively support engagement. From this perspective, the absence of a barrier does not necessarily create a facilitator, and facilitators such as coordinated services, provider trustworthiness, or community support may influence service use in ways that extend beyond simply removing obstacles.

Structural- and organizational-level facilitator

Integrative and wraparound services

Facilitators identified in our study align with literature showing that structural factors, such as coordinated services, can encourage survivors’ engagement with services [100]. Coordinated services—also referred to as co-located, integrative, and/or wraparound services—are service models in which multiple types of support (e.g., forensic medical exams, legal advocacy, housing assistance, case management, and mental health counseling) are offered to service-seekers in a unified and accessible manner, often within the same physical location or through structured collaboration across agencies [101]. A systematic review of family justice center models, or one-stop services demonstrate that co-located services and warm hand-offs between providers reduce attrition and enhance perceived safety for survivors who use services [102]. Additionally, studies of SA nurse examiner programs show that when medical, legal, and advocacy services are integrated, survivors are more likely to receive follow-up care and stay engaged [103]. Participants in the current study echoed these findings and noted that a trusted point person who could guide them through the different systems would have made it easier and more motivating to engage with services.

Interpersonal-level facilitator

Trust or belief in services

Our findings reinforce prior work showing that trust in providers is a critical facilitator of service-seeking and use, particularly when it is built through respectful and validating interactions. In a qualitative study with adult survivors of child sexual abuse, Alyce and colleagues [104] found that survivors described actively scanning for signs of trustworthiness in providers, such as mutual respect, attentiveness, and warmth. Similarly, others have found that survivors perceive SA nurse examiners as “humanizing” when they provided clear explanations, offered choices, and responded compassionately [105]. Participants in our study described similar experiences and emphasized that provider behaviors such as taking time to explain options, showing genuine concern, and offering to connect them to additional resources encouraged service engagement.

Clarity concerns

Participants’ feedback about clarity of items shed light on broader challenges in measuring barriers and facilitators to post-assault service-seeking. Across survey versions, some participants pointed out that certain words or phrases did not make sense in their community or geographic context. For example, institutional terms like “rape crisis center,” “rape kit,” and “reporting” were unfamiliar to some participants, particularly those in rural areas where formal SA services were sparse or nonexistent, and financial terms like “low-cost services” carried assumptions about participants’ relationship to cost that made accurate responding difficult. Research on service-seeking and usage in the U.S. suggests that formal SA services are often designed for and most accessible to survivors who are white, urban, non-elderly, English-speaking, and without disability [25, 106]. Consequently, many survivors from historically marginalized communities may not perceive that systems were designed for them [86, 107]. These findings are consistent with cognitive interview research showing that items referencing specific healthcare systems or institutional structures can feel inapplicable or culturally incongruent to respondents whose contexts differ from those the measure was designed for [108], and that when participants struggle to interpret an item’s intent, they may rely on guessing or personal assumptions, which may compromise response accuracy [109].

These findings suggest that measures of barriers and facilitators should avoid terminology that assumes prior familiarity with formal service systems or a fixed relationship to cost. Survivors from rural or historically underserved communities may have had little or no contact with the services being assessed, as participants in this study demonstrated when they were unfamiliar with what a rape crisis center was or whether one existed in their area. Using behaviorally specific language to describe services (e.g., describing advocacy services as “someone who accompanied me to appointments or helped me understand my options” rather than referencing a service type by name) may improve response accuracy for people who have experienced SA across a range of geographic and service contexts.

Participant feedback on item structure similarly highlighted that combining conceptually distinct experiences or features into single items can obscure the specific mechanisms through which barriers operate and make it difficult for respondents to answer accurately. For example, participants drew distinctions between stable identity characteristics, such as race and gender, and fluctuating conditions, such as mental health status and substance use, suggesting that items conflating these within a single stem risk misrepresenting both. These findings are consistent with cognitive interview research showing that terms carrying more than one meaning, or items that bundle multiple concepts, can produce divergent interpretations across respondents and undermine the precision of the constructs being assessed [108, 110, 111].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Because recruitment, item content, service-system assumptions, and legal context are all embedded in the U.S. setting, these findings are specific to the U.S. and may not transfer to other national contexts. Another notable limitation is that the current study relied primary on recruitment through sexual violence organizations, which may have precluded inclusion of those who did not access services. While some community outreach was done through flyering and directed outreach to the authors’ networks, most participants had sought some type of formal services. As previously discussed, many survivors of SA do not seek formal services [7, 11, 99, 112], particularly survivors from historically marginalized backgrounds [25, 80, 86, 107], and the perspectives of those who do not seek services would have provided useful insight into the development of this measure. As such, the barriers and facilitators assessed in the current study may not fully reflect the experiences of those who are completely disengaged from services, which is a potential threat to the measure’s content validity. This is especially likely for feedback related to provider encounters (e.g., whether providers were dismissive, whether care felt identity and culturally affirming, whether participants trusted providers), since these experiences presume that someone has reached a provider in the first place. Survivors who never engage with formal services may weigh these factors differently or have concerns tied specifically to never reaching services at all, which our sample was less positioned to surface. Although we supplemented agency-based recruitment with community outreach (e.g., listservs, posting flyers across neighborhoods) and snowball sampling, future research could benefit from extended recruitment timeframes when and if feasible. For example, having a longer recruitment period could allow community-based strategies more time to reach those who are not connected to formal services and who may be less likely to encounter study materials through organizational channels. However, this approach to sampling was useful for preventing spam responses to our eligibility screener [66].

Our final sample reflected a range of lived experiences across race, gender, and sexuality. Although we did not purposively sample on the basis of financial precarity or disability, these factors were raised by multiple participants and would be important to more fully appreciate in future studies. Because all cognitive interviews were conducted via videoconference using shared PowerPoint slides, participation required stable internet access, a compatible device, and sufficient digital fluency, which may have made it easier for people who are more familiar with technology to participate. While remote interviews in the current study allowed us to speak to individuals from various geographies and eliminated the need to travel, in-person interviews could have offered participants a chance to engage more readily with the survey material. Future research could use hybrid or in-person formats when working with individuals from rural areas so as to widen access and support different modes of engagement.

A further limitation is that three of the four coders also served as interviewers, which may have introduced interpretive bias if impressions formed during the interviews shaped their coding. The second author’s independent coding of a subset of transcripts and the team’s negotiated consensus process helped minimize this risk.

Future research directions

Although cognitive interviews are an important step in measure development, they are not designed to produce generalizable results [35, 54]. Our group is currently testing the preliminary psychometric properties of the current study’s measure in a large convenience sample, with plans to do further psychometric testing in a nationally representative sample. The psychometric phase will allow us to reduce the item pool by integrating findings from an exploratory factor analysis with ongoing team discussion in which we will evaluate whether highly correlated items are assessing distinct but related experiences, whether items should be combined or removed based on conceptual overlap or redundancy, and whether the resulting structure aligns with the social-ecological model that guided item development. The exploratory factor analysis will also allow us to examine whether items correspond to distinct factors across the social ecology, whether cross-level interactions are reflected in the measure’s dimensional structure, and whether barriers and facilitators load onto separate dimensions or whether some barrier–facilitator pairs function as opposite poles of a single dimension. Scoring procedures will also be developed following psychometric evaluation, which will provide service providers with further evidence of areas to address in reducing barriers and strengthening facilitators for service-seeking among people who have been exposed to SA.

Future research should also consider conducting cognitive interviews in languages other than English to reflect the experiences of people who have experienced SA across diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds. Key constructs in the current study’s measure, such as embarrassment, shame, stigma, and trust, may carry different meanings across languages and cultures [113], and translation alone may not account for these differences.

Clinical & policy implications

Once validated, the current study’s measure could help providers identify barriers and facilitators during intake. In the meantime, clinicians can act on this study’s findings by asking survivors about past service experiences and using trauma-informed principles to guide service delivery so as not to discourage future service-seeking [114]. Clear communication about confidentiality and legal risks, along with assistance in coordinating navigation between services, may help reduce common barriers survivors reported. At the organizational level, agencies can improve access by expanding language support and adopting warm handoffs or point-person models to promote continuity and trust [102].

Conclusion

Our findings underscore that current measures of barriers and facilitators to SA services may not have captured the breadth of factors that encourage or discourage service utilization. Input from those with lived experience of SA identified critical gaps in our understanding of barriers and facilitators to service-seeking such as culturally affirming care, geographic and language access barriers, mistrust of services, and the need for coordinated services. The cognitive interview process also identified important clarity concerns, including terminology that assumed familiarity with formal service systems and item structures that combined conceptually distinct experiences. A critical next step in this work involves psychometric validation in a quantitative sample. Once validated, we aim to produce a measure that can support researchers in robustly assessing barriers and facilitators to guide policy development and best practice recommendations to improve service access, use, and safety for underserved survivors of violence.
