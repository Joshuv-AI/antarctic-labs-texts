Establishing prospective performance monitoring for real-world implementation of deep learning-based auto-segmentation in prostate cancer radiotherapy☆
Authors: Libing Zhu, Yi Rong, Nathan Y Yu, Jason M Holmes, Carlos E Vargas, Sarah E James, Lu Shang, Jean-Claude M Rwigema, Quan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Deep learning, Auto segmentation, Performance monitoring, Quality assurance

Introduction

Target and organ-at-risk (OAR) delineation is an important step in modern radiation therapy [1]. Contouring errors have been recognized as one of the high-risk failure modes for photon/electron external beam radiation therapy [2]. Inaccurate contouring could result in suboptimal radiation treatment plans [3], [4]. To improve quality and safety in radiation therapy, it was recommended that target delineation undergo peer review in a daily multidisciplinary meeting [5]. Automated quality assurance checks need to be developed to identify potential errors or inconsistencies during the review process [2].

Deep learning-based auto-segmentation (DLAS) utilizes deep learning techniques, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), to automatically segment OARs and tumors on medical images [6]. DLAS has already been implemented in clinical applications, significantly improving clinical workflow efficiency and reducing manual workload [7], [8], [9]. However, manual modifications by Radiation Oncologists (RadOnc) and dosimetrists are still required to ensure accuracy, as the accuracy of DLAS varies significantly [10], [11], [12]. Furthermore, clinical DLAS models may exhibit performance deterioration over time after clinical implementation [13] due to data drift in clinical deployment compared with the initial training phase. Specifically, the performance deterioration of DLAS models for prostate segmentation was attributed to interobserver variability, use of hydrogel spacers, CT slice thickness, MRI involvement, and intravenous (IV) contrast [13]. This poses a safety concern even though the DLAS contouring errors are usually detected and corrected manually by clinical users. It is necessary to establish a quality monitoring system capable of detecting out-of-distribution outliers [14] to alert clinicians to potential issues in clinically implemented artificial intelligence (AI) models.

Currently, in clinical practice, human operators (dosimetrists and clinicians) are responsible for verifying the AI-generated contours and editing them if necessary. An extensive manual editing process, i.e., slice-by-slice editing for some DLAS-generated structures with poor accuracy, can be more cumbersome than manual contouring from scratch. Quality deterioration in DLAS reduces efficiency and may lead to near misses or errors in contouring due to human fatigue. There is growing emphasis within regulatory science on developing methods and practical tools that can detect changes to the inputs of AI-enabled medical devices, monitor the output performance, and understand the causes of performance variations [15]. Post-deployment data drift was monitored to alert users when deviations or drifts exceeded a pre-set threshold [16]. Several studies have investigated quality assurance methodologies for DLAS [17], [18], [19]. Duan et al. developed a novel contour subregion error detection system using subregional surface distance discrepancies between manual and DLAS contours [17]. Automatic contour error detection was explored using deep learning approaches [18], [19]. Similar to other clinically implemented tools for radiotherapy, it is crucial to establish a quality assurance and continuous monitoring program that ensures clinical safety and consistency in patient treatment.

The aims of this study were twofold: (1) to evaluate the clinical performance of two DLAS models for the male pelvis; and (2) to establish a monitoring system for ongoing DLAS performance evaluation.

Material and methods

Clinically implemented DLAS model

In our clinic, a commercial DLAS, model A (AccuContour Manteia Medical Technologies Co., Milwaukee, WI, USA), was first employed as a trial product for auto-contouring. Model A was a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based 2D U-Net model [20], [21]. Subsequently, a second commercial DLAS, model B (INTContour, Carina Medical LLC, Lexington, KY, USA), was introduced in our clinic to achieve higher performance, as its network architecture was based on 3D U-Net with five encoding and five decoding layers [22], [23]. Both vendors’ built-in male pelvis models were retrained using institutional data and tested for performance comparison on the prostate, bladder, rectum, and femoral heads [9], [12]. These earlier works described model training and clinical validation using institution-specific CT images and physician-approved contours for prostate cancer. Model B demonstrated superior performance and has been adopted for clinical use since February 2023 [9]. Both DLAS models were available for dosimetrists to use at their own discretion. Manual review and editing were mandatory to ensure contour accuracy, regardless of which DLAS model was used.

Dataset

A total of 464 patients with intact prostate cancer treated from January 2022 to August 2023 were included in this study, covering two clinical implementations of DLAS models in our clinic as shown in Fig. 1(a). 294 cases from January 2022 to January 2023 were contoured using commercial DLAS model A. The remaining 170 cases from February 2023 to August 2023 represented the initial clinical implementation of model B. Dosimetrists were required to use only model B for every intact prostate case they processed. Dosimetrists and genitourinary (GU) RadOncs modified the AI-generated contours for clinical approval. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (or Ethics Committee) of Mayo Clinic (protocol code 24-012370 and date of approval: 27 November 2024).

Performance monitoring workflow for AI auto-segmentation: (a) patient selection and control limit creation and (b) organ-specific AI performance monitoring.

In this study, the original AI contours from model A were not archived. Therefore, all CT datasets were re-segmented using model B to generate a consistent set of AI contours for comparison. The clinically approved contours edited from model A outputs (January 2022–January 2023) and those edited from model B outputs (February 2023–August 2023) were then compared against the model B-generated contours to assess temporal performance and practice changes. The clinical contours edited from model A were used to establish the baseline of performance monitoring (details explained in section 2.3), using 150 cases from January 2022 to July 2022. A total of 314 cases (144 from model A, August 2022–January 2023, and 170 from model B, February–August 2023) were used to test the monitoring system. Results from both DLAS-generated contours and clinically approved contours after manual edits were stored and exported from Aria database for this study analysis. Five physicians and nine dosimetrists were involved in model A. There were six physicians and eight dosimetrists involved in model B. A total of four physicians and six dosimetrists participated in both models.

DLAS performance monitoring

We employed process behavior charts [24] for DLAS performance monitoring, using 150 cases from January 2022 to July 2022 to establish the baseline behavior. A process behavior chart was a quality control (QC) approach used to monitor performance by identifying discrepancies from the expected process behavior. This method continuously evaluated new data against the established control limit (CL). If the data metric fell within the CL, the performance was considered stable (in-control); otherwise, instances outside the CL were flagged as potential outliers or edge cases (out-of-control), as shown in Fig. 1(b). Three commonly used metrics for contour evaluation were Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Hausdorff Distance 95 percentile (HD95) and Surface Dice Similarity Coefficient (SDSC) with 1 mm/2 mm tolerance [25], [26]. Upper and lower control limits (UCL and LCL) were calculated using two and three standard deviations of the monthly mean performance [27], as shown in Eq. (1). The two-standard-deviation limit was more sensitive and could detect shifts earlier. A case was flagged if any of the three metrics (DSC, HD95, or SDSC) exceeded the predefined control limits. Additionally, SDSC was employed to estimate the extent of manual editing likely required by clinicians, based on acceptance tolerances of 1 mm and 2 mm.

Two control charts were developed based on 2σx¯ and 3σx¯ to monitor monthly performance degradation or overdependence on AI by clinicians. The baseline CLs were established using a selected cohort (n = 150) from model A and validated with a retrospective cohort (n = 314) from both models A and B. Flagged outliers were subsequently analyzed to identify potential causes such as metal artifacts. For monthly control limits, we used the monthly mean values (X¯ chart) [28], and the variability of monthly mean was denoted by the standard deviation. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test [29] was utilized to evaluate the deviation between two models, with a p-value smaller than 0.05 being considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11 with pandas 2.1.1 and matplotlib 3.8.0. The code is available at https://github.com/zhulibing2023/Code-to-calculate-quantitative-metrics-for-DLAS-QA.git.

Results

DLAS performance monitoring

The temporal trends of DSC, HD95, and SDSC metrics were visualized in Figs. 2 and S1. Distinct performance differences were observed across the five organs. Notably, DSC, HD95, and SDSC 1 mm/2 mm values showed clear differences between the two DLAS models for the prostate, rectum and bladder, whereas only SDSC 1 mm/2 mm exhibited significant deviations between models for the two femoral heads. For DSC and HD95, all five organs demonstrated highly significant differences between the two DLAS models (p < 0.001), except for right femoral head HD95 (p = 0.19). SDSC metrics showed highly significant differences (p < 0.001) for all organs.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of a retrained DLAS model for prostate cancer and introduced a novel process behavior chart-based framework for monitoring segmentation quality. Using retrospective data, the framework successfully identified temporal variations in DLAS performance and validated its stability across clinical implementation. No evidence of AI degradation or clinician overreliance was observed. Model B showed significant improvement in DSC, HD95, and SDSC 1 mm/2mm with reduced variance among all five organs compared to model A.

The metrics and thresholds defined in this study could be adapted to monitor performance in other anatomical sites or cancer types, especially where DLAS models were already employed. Our QA model automatically calculated the performance metrics of DLAS contours and clinically approved contours for each case. Further investigation is planned to integrate the scripts into the treatment planning system. We recommend implementing ongoing QA with at least a monthly evaluation. If a performance drop was identified and linked to data shift such as changes in CT scanner hardware, scanning protocols, or the adoption of devices such as rectal balloons or hydrogel spacers, then immediate validation and retraining of the AI model was required. This ensured that the model remained aligned with evolving clinical practice. Although this study focused on the male pelvis regions, the monitoring framework had significant potential for broader applications.

An improvement in DSC and HD95 was observed between the real-world implementation stage and the testing stage. The mean DSC increased from a range of 0.82–0.96 during testing to 0.89–0.97 during clinical practice, while the HD95 decreased from 5.0–9.6 mm to 2.5–5.7 mm across all organs of interest. Specifically, the mean DSC/HD95 obtained using the retrained model B was 0.89 ± 0.07/4.6 ± 3.2 mm for prostate in real-world monitoring, whereas during the testing stage, model B achieved 0.82 ± 0.07/6.6 ± 3.0 mm for prostate [9]. This difference occurred because, during the implementation of model B, radiation oncologists and dosimetrists modified the DLAS-generated contours, whereas in the testing stage, the ground-truth prostate contours were directly delineated by RadOncs.

Although the DSC, HD95, and SDSC values for model B were superior to those for model A, this improvement did not necessarily imply that model B intrinsically performed better. In 2023, the metrics were calculated by comparing model B contours with clinician-edited model B contours, whereas in 2022, the comparison was between model B contours and clinician-edited model A contours. This suggested that physicians’ contours may have inherently aligned more closely with model B, possibly reflecting greater physician trust or familiarity with the new model.

In our study, the control charts did not show any obvious degradation trend. However, pronounced deterioration was reported for prostate cancer auto-segmentation [13]. In their study, a notable decline in exponentially weighted moving average (EMA) curves of DSC was observed for the prostate and rectum, while bladder contours remained stable [13]. This could be attributed to the limited timeframe investigated in our study (two-year range). Additionally, we utilized the monitoring method with a σx¯-based threshold for the DLAS performance while EMA curves [13] or novel contour subregion error detection systems [17] were used by other researchers. Our proposed method was simple to implement and adapt to other clinics and other anatomical sites using institution-specific data.

In our study, AI-generated contours were used as the baseline to investigate the interobserver variability and overdependence of clinicians on AI. Among the prostate outliers, five cases were associated with fiducials and six with hip implants. However, it is important to note that many other patients with fiducials remained within acceptable performance thresholds, as metal-artifact reduction was implemented in our clinical practice. This indicated that fiducials themselves were not the direct cause of AI segmentation failures. Instead, they may have acted as a contributing factor in certain cases. A detailed failure scenario analysis was provided in a previous study [26]. Rectum and bladder outliers were attributed to metal artifacts and limitations in DLAS generalization, where unforeseen cases occurred in clinical application. The metal artifacts caused by hip prostheses contributed to the failure of femoral head segmentation. In Fig. 2, Fig. 4, both daily and monthly performance exhibited temporal drift and minor fluctuations, even while remaining within the control limits. Beyond image differences, variations in physicians’ contouring guidelines may also have contributed to this drift. Therefore, we analyzed the performance of individual physicians after implementing DLAS guidance.

There were a few limitations regarding this study. Firstly, the investigated dataset was limited in consistency evaluation because our retrained DLAS model was clinically implemented in 2023. Secondly, the 2σx¯ criterion might be too general to detect all outliers when taking all RadOnc contours together and could be limited in cases with interobserver variability. The control charts could be further expanded to an individual RadOnc assessment. However, this method was simple and efficient to monitor DLAS performance. Additionally, data drift remained a persistent challenge in the post-implementation phase of DLAS model. By employing statistical process control, our framework effectively identified out-of-distribution outliers, serving as an early warning mechanism to prevent performance degradation. Future research should explore deep learning-based data input detection to further mitigate drift effects and maintain high accuracy over time. Model A was introduced for discretionary use during a clinical trial period, and the evaluation may have been subject to user selection and editing bias. However, in clinical practice dosimetrists and physicians were responsible for producing clinically acceptable contours, which naturally constrained the variability. Moreover, the data reflected contributions from multiple dosimetrists and physicians, so averaging across users helped mitigate individual bias. Finally, model A’s performance appeared consistent during the first period of evaluation.

Our current monitoring system was cohort- and site-specific. For this feasibility study, we focused on the male pelvis and outlier detection, while overreliance monitoring was not included. Quantile-based methods, such as using 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as thresholds could be applied for over-reliance monitoring in future studies. To extend the approach to other anatomical sites, real-world data should be collected to establish control limits for DSC, HD95, and SDSC metrics. Because contouring guidelines vary across institutions, the σx¯-based tolerances could be adjusted to align with each physician’s standards. Overall, the proposed methodology can be readily adapted by other hospitals, provided they incorporate their own data and contouring practices. Three different performance metrics illustrated complementary effectiveness for outlier detection. Cases flagged by all three metrics demonstrated major segmentation errors, whereas those identified by only one metric highlighted their complementary nature, as each assessed different segmentation performance: DSC for overall volume overlap, HD95 for boundary deviation, and SDSC for clinically relevant surface agreement.

The monitoring system using process behavior charts effectively detected out-of-distribution outliers for prostate, bladder, rectum, fem_head_l, and fem_head_r, providing a reliable and easy-to-use framework for quality assurance and early detection of clinical practice change. No obvious performance degradation of clinically implemented DLAS or overdependence of clinicians on AI was found in the male pelvis. Future work will focus on the expansion of DLAS performance monitoring in other anatomical sites.
