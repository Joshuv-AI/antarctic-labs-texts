Long‐Term Hydrophilic, Anti‐Clotting, and Anti‐Fibrotic Dynamic Covalent Silicone‐Based Biomaterials
Authors: Norma A. Garza Flores, Eva Mueller, Robert Bui, James C. Fredenburgh, Ruiqi Yin, Rawaa Hussain, Rida A. Malik, Thomas Kalab, Dabeer Abdul‐Azeez, Fei Xu, Michael Brook, Jeffrey I. Weitz, Todd Hoare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) has been widely applied in biomedical devices and implants, its interfacial hydrophobicity and poor blood compatibility limit its long‐term effectiveness. Existing approaches to enhance the surface hydrophilicity and reduce the interfacial biological responses of PDMS implants often result in a gradual loss of hydrophilicity due to the chemical instability of the coating and/or the hydrophobic recovery of the low surface energy silicone. Herein, dynamic covalent crosslinking of a Schiff base‐crosslinked silicone elastomer (PDMSS) with an anti‐fouling hydrazide‐functionalized poly(oligoethylene glycol methacrylate) (OEGMA‐Hzd) or zwitterionic sulfobetaine (DMAPS‐Hzd) polymer is demonstrated to improve the long‐term hydrophilicity and anti‐fouling properties of PDMS. Dip coating PDMSS into a hydrazide‐functionalized polymer solution significantly improved both the interfacial and bulk properties of the material, increasing its tensile and compressive strength while preventing hydrophobic recovery over at least 100 days, significantly longer than most reported approaches. Blood‐contacting studies show the material resists clotting, while subcutaneous implants indicate a significant reduction in immune cell density at the tissue‐implant interface as well as a decrease in capsule thickness and collagen deposition compared to PDMSS and Sylgard PDMS. This dynamic covalent crosslinking approach can thus address a key barrier to the fabrication of tissue‐compatible and long‐term hydrophilic PDMS‐based biomedical implants.

Introduction

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is an elastomer characterized by its high optical transparency [1, 2], chemical resistance [1], thermal stability [3], oxygen permeability [4], and tunable elastic behavior [5, 6]. Coupling these properties with its ease of fabrication, PDMS has been used for the development of a range of biomedical devices such as intraocular lenses [7], microfluidic devices [8], dialysis membranes [9], finger joints [10], pacemaker lead coatings [11], and implants for plastic and reconstructive surgery [12, 13]. However, the effectiveness of PDMS‐based implant devices can be limited by their poor blood compatibility and potential to induce fibrotic responses [12]. Both these issues are tied directly to the high interfacial hydrophobicity of PDMS [1, 14]. In microfluidic biosensor applications, the hydrophobicity of PDMS leads to flow resistance to aqueous solutions and non‐specific adsorption of hydrophobic analytes that impedes sensor accuracy [4, 15]. In implant applications, studies on the occlusion of ventricular catheter drains showed that extracellular fluid, serum, and cerebrospinal fluid proteins are adsorbed on PDMS within seconds of implantation, leading to immune cell attachment and a multi‐faceted response led by microglia and astrocytes to attempt to clear the implant from the body [16]. Similar findings have been reported for silicone breast implants, in which local reactions to silicone exuding from the implants (i.e., gel bleed) or released by ruptures [17] can lead to implant failure [12, 18].

Different approaches have been explored to mediate these challenges by enhancing the surface hydrophilicity of PDMS implants and devices [19] including plasma treatment [20, 21, 22], corona discharge [23], ultraviolet irradiation [24, 25], chemical vapor deposition [26], coating or grafting hydrophilic polymers onto the PDMS surface [27, 28, 29] and/or hybrid approaches [19], with plasma treatment being the most commonly applied in practical applications. Although each of these methods can significantly improve the short‐term wettability of PDMS, most (in particular plasma treatment, corona discharge, and UV irradiation) result in hydrophobic recovery, in which the very low surface energy PDMS chains gradually migrate back to the interface to undo the benefit of the surface modification [19]. Among the approaches that can enable better long‐term hydrophilicity (typically those involving polymer‐based coating/grafting) [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36] most suffer from one or more of the need for complex and/or hard to scale chemistry, a loss of transparency, and/or a required compromise in the desirable mechanical properties of PDMS that can significantly impact other aspects of the material's application performance [37, 38].

Among polymer‐based modification methods, polyethylene glycol (PEG)‐based and zwitterionic polymer coatings have gained particular attention due to their anti‐fouling properties, which are tied to their very strong water‐binding capacity. In the context of PEG‐based coatings, Gu et al. developed a UV‐curable methacrylated PDMS/PEG methylether methacrylate blend coating that increased the material surface hydrophilicity while also reducing protein and bacterial adsorption [39]. Similarly, silanol‐terminated PDMS films were mixed with a trifunctional oximinosilane crosslinker and coated with PEG‐based amphiphiles of different chemistries (e.g., alkyl, alkyl/aryl) that significantly reduced water contact angle and improved fouling resistance [40]. In the context of zwitterionic polymer coatings, Zhang et al. reported the covalent grafting of allyl carboxybetaine to Sylgard 184 PDMS via a hydrosilylation reaction in the presence of Karstedt's catalyst which enabled a significant reduction of protein and bacteria adsorption while increasing wettability for up to 3 months post‐modification [41]. Covalent photografting and photocrosslinking of sulfobetaines and carboxybetaines onto commercial PDMS has also been explored through the activation of the silicone surface with benzophenone and the use of different crosslinkers (e.g., poly(ethylene)glycol diacrylate or poly(ethylene glycol) dimethacrylate) and photoinitiators (e.g., 1‐[4‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)‐phenyl]‐2hydroxy‐2‐methyl‐1‐propane‐1‐one (HEPK) or Irgacure 2959), enabling the prevention of bacterial and fibroblast adhesion in vitro as well as reduced fibrotic capsule formation in vivo [42, 43]. However, these approaches all require the use of additives such as crosslinkers and photoinitiators as well as the application of ultraviolet light, which can limit scalability. Other developments have focused on the bulk modification of PDMS to impart improved surface properties. For example, a PDMS‐based zwitterionic polyurethane‐urea copolymer incorporating sulfobetaines into the silicone structure significantly reduced fouling and hemolysis in vitro [44], albeit at a cost of optical transparency.

Dynamic crosslinking represents a class of reversible crosslinks that can dynamically form and break in a suitable environment, allowing for structural reorganization of the material over time. Diverse types of self‐healing and dynamic covalent crosslinked PDMS networks have been reported based on (1) Diels‐Alder crosslinking of hydroxyl‐terminated PDMS, diol, and isophorone diisocyanate with maleimides [45], (2) disulfide crosslinking between amino‐modified PDMS, 2(6‐isocyanatohexylaminocarbonylamino)‐6‐methyl‐4[1H] pyrimidinone and 2,2′ ‐dithiodibenzoic acid (which also introduced multiple types of hydrogen bonds to enhance crosslinking) [46], (3) boroxine crosslinking between aryl‐boroxine and bis(3‐aminopropyl)terminated poly(dimethylsiloxane) [47], (4) hindered urea‐based crosslinking between isocyanate and piperazine [48], (5) siloxane equilibration of polysiloxane catalyzed by potassium silanolate groups [49], (6) vinylogous urethane crosslinking between aminated silica and 3‐aminopropyl trimethoxysilane in the presence of (2‐acetoacetoxy)ethyl methacrylate [50], (7) hydrogen bonding‐based interactions between α,ω‐aminopropyl poly(dimethylsiloxane) and ethylene carbonate [51], and (8) imine crosslinking between aminopropyl‐functionalized polydimethylsiloxane and aromatic aldehydes [3]. Similar types of crosslinking approaches have been used to form anti‐fouling hydrogels [52, 53, 54, 55, 56], including our previous work fabricating hydrazone‐crosslinked anti‐fouling hydrogels based on poly(oligoethylene glycol methacrylate) (POEGMA) [55] and zwitterionic sulfobetaines (PDMAPS) [56] that showed effective anti‐fouling properties against both protein adsorption and cell adhesion. Leveraging a common dynamic covalent chemistry (Schiff base formation) between the silicone network and the anti‐fouling polymer may thus offer the potential to form long‐term stable anti‐fouling silicone networks; more specifically, adding hydrazide‐functionalized anti‐fouling polymers to an imine‐crosslinked PDMS network will enable dynamic restructuring of the network that favors the formation of the more thermodynamically stable hydrazide‐aldehyde (hydrazone) bond, enabling effective post‐modification of pre‐formed PDMS elastomers to enable anti‐fouling capacity.

Herein, a Schiff‐base silicone elastomer (PDMSS) [3] is dynamically crosslinked with hydrazide (Hzd)‐functionalized polymers based on either (oligoethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate monomer (OEGMA‐Hzd) or a zwitterionic sulfobetaine monomer (DMAPS‐Hzd) via a one‐step dip coating process to improve the long‐term hydrophilicity and anti‐fouling properties of silicone‐based implants (Figure 1). Relative to other reported coating strategies, the key innovations of our approach include (1) avoiding the need for additional crosslinking agents or modifications, enabling long‐term (>3 month) maintenance of hydrophilicity via a scalable dip coating step; (2) the potential to improve not only the surface but bulk properties of the material (e.g. tensile and compressive strength); and (3) significantly improved anti‐clotting properties that are to our knowledge unique among reported silicone‐based materials. These benefits are demonstrated both in vitro as well as via in vivo subcutaneous implant studies that show that the dynamic covalent silicones provide significantly improved anti‐fouling/anti‐fibrotic properties, inducing reduced immune cell density at the tissue‐implant interface while also decreasing capsule thickness and collagen deposition compared to both unmodified PDMSS and commercial PDMS (Sylgard 184).

Experimental

Materials

[2‐(Methacryloyloxy)ethyl]dimethyl‐(3‐sulfopropyl)ammonium hydroxide (DMAPS, 95%), di(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (M(EO)2MA, Sigma Aldrich, 95%) and oligo(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate with a number average molecular weight of 475 g/mol (OEGMA475, Sigma Aldrich, 95%) were purified by passing over a column of basic aluminum oxide (Sigma Aldrich, type CG‐20) to remove the methyl ether hydroquinone (MEHQ) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) inhibitors. N‐(2,2‐dimethoxyethyl) methacrylamide (DMAEAm, Sigma Aldrich, 98%) was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure [55]. Acrylic acid (AA, Sigma Aldrich, 99%), adipic acid dihydrazide (ADH, Alfa Aesar, 98%), N′‐ethyl‐N‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)‐carbodiimide (EDC, Carbosynth, Compton CA, commercial grade), 2,2‐azobisisobutyric acid dimethyl ester (AIBMe, Wako Chemicals, 98.5%), Sylgard 184 (Sigma Aldrich, 100%), 3‐(aminopropyl)methylsiloxane‐dimethylsiloxane) (AMS‐152, Gelest, 4500–6000 g/mol), terephthalaldehyde (Sigma Aldrich, 99%), chloroform (Sigma Aldrich, ≥99%,), and PrestoBlue (TermoFisher) were purchased and used without further purification. Pooled normal plasma (PNP) was collected from donors after written consent in compliance with the Faculty of Health Sciences Research Ethics Board via a procedure approved by McMaster University (Research Ethics Protocol 07–396T) [57]. Calcium chloride (CaCl2, Sigma Aldrich), HEPES buffer (Gibco, pH 7.4), and z‐GGR‐AMC thrombin substrate (Z‐Gly‐Gly‐Arg‐AMC, Bachem, Switzerland) were used as received. Milli‐Q grade distilled deionized water (DIW) was used for all experiments.

Polymer Synthesis

Hydrazide‐functionalized POEGMA (OEGMA‐Hzd) targeting 30 mol% hydrazide groups was synthesized by free radical polymerization by adding AIBMe (37 mg), TGA (7.5 µL), M(EO)2MA (3.1 g), OEGMA475 (0.9 g), acrylic acid (0.55 g) and dioxane (20 mL) to a 100 mL flask, purging with nitrogen for 30 min, and polymerizing at 75°C for 4 h under magnetic stirring. Subsequently, the acrylic acid residues were functionalized with hydrazide groups via the addition of ADH (2.65 g, 15.2 mmol, 5× molar excess) and EDC (1.18 g, 6.2 mmol, 2.5× molar excess), maintaining a stable pH at 4.75 over the 4 h grafting time. The polymer was then purified via dialysis (6+ h for 6 cycles), lyophilized, and stored in a 20 wt.% solution in PBS at 4°C.

DMAPS‐hydrazide polymer (DMAPS‐Hzd) targeting 30 mol% hydrazide groups was synthesized via free‐radical polymerization by dissolving DMAPS (4 g) in 10 mL of Milli‐Q water in a 100 mL round‐bottom flask. Acrylic acid (0.44 g), TGA (10 µL), APS (40 mg), and 20 mL of Milli‐Q water were added, after which the flask was purged with nitrogen for 30 min under magnetic stirring and placed in a 75°C oil bath overnight. Next, ADH (5.35 g) and EDC (2.38 g) were added to convert the acrylic acid moieties to hydrazide groups, with an additional 70 mL of Milli‐Q water added to fully dissolve the ADH and EDC prior to conducting the grafting reaction at pH 4.75 for 4 h. The polymer was then purified via dialysis (6+ h for 6 cycles), lyophilized, and stored in a 20 wt.% solution in 0.9% saline in MilliQ water at 4°C.

Polymer Characterization

Polymers were characterized by using aqueous gel permeation chromatography (GPC) and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR). GPC was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II GPC system with an Agilent 1260 Infinity refractive index detector and a Superpose 6 increase 10/300 GL (GE Healthcare) column maintained at 30°C. A mobile phase consisting of 1× PBS with 0.05% sodium azide at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min was used for all polymers analyzed, and the system was calibrated with narrow PEG standards (molecular weights 3–60 kDa). The degree of hydrazide functionalization on OEGMA‐Hzd was determined using 1H NMR (600 MHz, Bruker) by comparing the intensities of the −CH2 group on ADH (0.89‐1.06 ppm), the −OCH3 signal on POEGMA (3.45 ppm), and the −CH3 signal on the backbone of OEGMA (0.81 ppm) using dimethyl sulfoxide (d6‐DMSO) as solvent. Similarly, for DMAPS‐Hzd the degree of functionalization was quantified based on the intensity of the −CH2 signal of the DMAPS monomer (4.45 ppm) relative to the −CH2 signal from the functionalized ADH (1.55 ppm) using deuterium oxide (D2O) as solvent. See Supplementary Information Figures S1–S3 and Table S1 for full polymer characterization results.

Fabrication of Sylgard 184 Silicone (Sylgard PDMS)

Sylgard 184 (Sylgard PDMS) silicone was prepared by manually mixing the commercial base and curing agent at a 10:1 ratio, after which the mixture was put under vacuum for 1 h and transferred to an 80°C oven overnight for curing. Once cured, the silicones were cut into 2 mm thickness and 5 mm diameter discs for characterization. Sylgard PDMSPLASMA samples were prepared by plasma‐treating the surface of Sylgard PDMS discs using a handheld corona treater (BD‐20AC, Electro‐Technic Products) for 2 min.

Fabrication of Schiff‐Based Silicone (PDMSS)

Bulk PDMSS elastomers were fabricated by dissolving terephthalaldehyde (0.43 g) in chloroform (∼10 mL) and mixing with 4%–5% aminopropylmethylsiloxane (AMS‐152, 9 g), maintaining a stoichiometric amine:aldehyde ratio. The mixture was stirred until homogeneous, poured onto a Petri dish, and allowed to cure overnight at room temperature to ensure complete chloroform evaporation. After curing, PDMSS elastomers were cut into discs (2 mm thickness, 5 mm diameter) for characterization.

Fabrication of Anti‐Fouling Silicone

PDMSS discs were dip‐coated in 5, 10, 15 or 20 wt.% aqueous solutions of OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd under gentle shaking at room temperature for 1 h. Following, the samples were washed 3× with Milli‐Q water and dried overnight at room temperature.

Chemical Characterization of Anti‐Fouling Silicone

The chemical composition of the anti‐fouling silicones was assessed via attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR‐FTIR) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). ATR‐FTIR was conducted using a Bruker Vertex 70 spectrometer with a resolution of 2 cm−1 and a scan range of 500–4000 cm−1. XPS analysis was performed using a Physical Electronics Quantera II Scanning XPS Microprobe operating at 25 W/15 kV with a pass energy of 224 eV. Spectra were collected at three different points for each sample using a take‐off angle of 45°, a survey step size of 0.8 eV, and an elemental step size of 0.1 eV.

Penetration of Anti‐Fouling Polymer into Pre‐Formed Silicone Discs

The penetration of the anti‐fouling polymer into the silicone elastomer was assessed via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Nikon) using FITC‐labeled anti‐fouling polymer. Briefly, FITC (5 mg) was reacted under magnetic stirring with 1 g OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd (15 w/w%) DIW for 12 h, with all reaction flasks and storage containers covered in aluminum foil to minimize photobleaching (target ∼1 mol% functionalization with FITC). Post‐reaction, the OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd polymer solutions were dialyzed for a minimum of 6 h × 6 cycles, lyophilized, and stored at 20 wt.% in 1× phosphate buffered saline (1× PBS, pH = 7.4) or 0.9 wt.% saline, respectively, both at 4°C away from light. Confocal z‐stack images (3D view) were collected by scanning planes at 5 µm intervals from the bottom to the top of silicone discs using an excitation wavelength of 495 nm and an emission wavelength of 519 nm. ImageJ was used for analysis of the fluorescence intensity as a function of penetration depth.

Interfacial Hydrophilicity of Anti‐Fouling Silicones

The surface hydrophilicity of the anti‐fouling silicones was assessed via water contact angle measurements against Sylgard PDMS and Sylgard PDMSPLASMA as controls using an Optical Contact Angle (OCA35s) instrument and SCA software (Dataphysics). Droplets (∼5 µL) droplets of distilled deionized water were applied on each surface, with the contact angle tracked over a 5 min period to assess the wettability of the tested surfaces at room temperature.

Mechanical Properties of Anti‐Fouling Silicones

The compressive moduli and rheological properties of the anti‐fouling silicones were analyzed using a Mach‐1 micromechanical tester (Biomomentum, Laval, QC) by compressing the samples by 25% of their original height at a rate of 0.03 mm/s with a flat indenter (12.5 mm). Rheological properties were evaluated using a strain sweep from 0.1%−100% strain at 0.5 Hz to identify the linear viscoelastic region of the materials. Based on a selected strain within the linear viscoelastic region, a frequency sweep was subsequently performed from 1 to 100 rad/s at an amplitude of 1.0 Hz to measure the shear elastic (G′) and loss (G′′) moduli. Tensile strength measurements on PDMSS, PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd, and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd anti‐fouling elastomers prepared by dip coating in a 20 wt.% anti‐fouling polymer solution were performed using a Instron 5900 series Universal Mechanical Tester using a 50 N load cell at a constant strain rate of 5 mm/min. All mechanical measurements were conducted at 25°C.

Self‐Healing Properties of Anti‐Fouling Silicones

The self‐healing properties of anti‐fouling silicones were assessed by dip‐coating the pre‐formed PDMSS elastomer with OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd polymers as described above and pinning two strips of material in partial contact with each other using 30G needles, with the excess length of each sample used to clamp the samples to the mechanical test frame. The self‐healing process was allowed to proceed for 24 h prior to testing at room temperature, after which a tensile test was performed using the same protocol as described above. The ultimate tensile strength and Young's modulus were calculated based on the maximum tensile strength and stress‐strain curves in the linear region as measured using an Instron Universal Mechanical Tester, converting the measured force versus distance curve to a modulus based on the interfacial area between the two samples. Cell Culture

NIH 3T3 fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin to ∼80% confluency at 37°C and 5% CO2 before use.

Cytotoxicity of Polymers and Anti‐Fouling Silicones

The cytocompatibility of OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd polymers and leachates from the dynamic silicone materials was quantified using a Presto Blue cell viability assay. For polymer cytotoxicity testing, hydrazide‐functionalized polymers were prepared at concentrations of 500–5000 µg/mL and sterilized by filtering through a 0.2 µm syringe filter. Cells were plated at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells per well in a 96‐well plate and incubated in 100 µL DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. Following, the plate was washed with 10 mM PBS, after which 100 µL aliquots of fresh DMEM containing OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd were added at different concentrations for 24 h. The medium was then removed, replaced with 90 µL of fresh DMEM and 10 µL of PrestoBlue reagent, and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Cell viability was assessed by fluorescence (560 nm excitation/590 nm emission) using a plate reader (Biotek Cytation5). For leachate testing, Sylgard PDMS, PDMSS, PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt%, and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% silicone discs (5 mm diameter, 2 mm thickness) were fabricated as described above, placed in cell culture inserts (40 µm pore size) inside 6‐well plates, and incubated in 10 mM PBS (8 mL per well) for 24 h at 37°C. Following, the leachates were collected and filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter. The cytocompatibility test was then performed following the same procedure used for the precursor polymer test, replacing the 100 µL aliquot of the polymer solution with 100 µL of the leachate solution. Results were normalized to the cell‐only wells in which no polymer or silicone leachate was added to estimate relative cell viability.

Anti‐Clotting Testing of Anti‐Fouling Silicones

To determine the anti‐clotting properties of the materials, pre‐formed silicone discs (5 mm diameter, 2 mm thickness) were cut and placed in individual wells of a 96‐well microtiter plate. An aliquot of 70 µL of human plasma and 20 µL of 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) was then added to each well and incubated for 15 min at 37°C before adding 20 µL of 260 mM CaCl2 to initiate clotting. Absorbance was then monitored for 60 min at 405 nm using a THERMOmax plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA), and the clotting times were recorded as the time to half‐maximum absorbance as determined by the instrument software.

To assess the corresponding thrombin generation associated with the clotting response [59], pre‐formed silicone discs were loaded into individual wells of a 96‐well black flat‐bottom plate to which 3.33 µL of phospholipid vesicles (PCPS), 70 µL of plasma, and 10 µL of 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) were added. The solution was incubated for 15 min at 37°C before adding a master mix containing 15 mM CaCl2, 20 mM of HEPES buffer (pH 7.4), and 1 mM of the thrombin substrate Z‐GGR‐AMC. Fluorescence intensity was correlated with thrombin generation using a standard curve and was monitored using an excitation wavelength of 360 nm and an emission wavelength of 460 nm over a 1.5 h period using a M3 plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA), with the resulting data used to measure the thrombin generation profile and peak thrombin concentration relative to empty wells (negative control) and Sylgard PDMS and Sylgard PDMSPLASMA controls.

Tissue Responses to Subcutaneous Anti‐Fouling Silicone Implants

To evaluate the anti‐fouling properties, anti‐fibrotic properties, and long‐term stability of the anti‐fouling silicones, silicone discs (2 mm height, 1 cm diameter) were sterilized via cobalt‐60 gamma irradiation at a dose rate of 280 mGy/s and a total irradiation dose of 25 KGy for 25 h and then implanted subcutaneously in BALB/c mice (n = 4 mice per formulation). All animal procedures were performed according to protocols approved by the Animal Research Ethics Board of McMaster University (protocol #24‐48), consistent with the Animals for Research Act of the Province of Ontario and the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines. Mice were anesthetized, and the incision site was shaved and sterilized before making a small (1‐2 cm) incision through the skin with a scalpel. Two silicone discs were inserted in the subcutaneous site, after which the wound was closed with staples. The incision site was monitored daily to ensure closure and prevent infection. At pre‐determined time points (7 and 28 days for acute and chronic timepoints respectively), mice were sacrificed and the area around the implant was resected for histological analysis of inflammatory responses (H&E staining), macrophage population (F4/80 staining), neutrophil population (Ly6g staining), and fibrotic capsule formation (Masson's trichrome staining). The number of immune cells per unit area at different distances from the implant margin was assessed based on the total purple area on the H&E‐stained histology samples, while the total collagen deposition area at different distances from the implant was measured based on the total blue area on the Masson's trichrome‐stained samples (both as quantified using ImageJ). Collagen deposition was also analyzed via a qualitative scoring by four blinded independent observers using the scale 0 = no significant collagen deposition, 1 = low density, thin, and disorganized collagen deposition, 2 = moderate density and thickness collagen deposition with somewhat organized collagen fibers, and 3 = high density, thick, and highly organized collagen fibers, with the reported scores representing the average of the four independent observations.

Tissue Adhesion and Anti‐Fouling Silicone In Vivo Degradation

To assess the degradability of the anti‐fouling silicones in vivo, a second set of experiments was conducted in which the silicone discs were explanted, the dimensions were measured, and the compressive mechanical properties of the discs weremeasured to assess the stability of the silicones in vivo. Implant adhesion to the surrounding skin and muscle was scored at the chronic timepoint during the explant process, randomizing the sequence of groups evaluated to minimize bias, using the qualitative scale of 0 = no adhesion, disc easily extracted with tweezers with no resistance to separation, 1 = loose adhesion to tissue, disc easily extracted with tweezers using light force with only mild resistance to separation, 2 = moderate adhesion to tissue, some cutting and moderate force required for extraction, and 3 = strong adhesion to tissue, cutting needed for extraction. The compressive modulus of the extracted samples (which, for more strongly adhering samples, may also contain skin, implant and muscle) was then measured using the same protocol described for non‐implanted samples to assess implant degradation over time.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software, with the data presented as mean ± standard deviation of measurements on three independent replicate samples (*p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, **** p ≤ 0.0001). Statistical significances were assessed via one‐ or two‐way ANOVA at 95% confidence plus Dunnett's T3 or Tukey's multiple comparisons corrections, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Surface Chemical Characterization

To assess the potential of the hydrazide‐functionalized anti‐fouling polymers to effectively functionalize PDMSS, ATR‐FTIR was used, with the results shown in Figure 2A for OEGMA‐Hzd and Figure 2C for DMAPS‐Hzd. The strong ester signal (∼1700 cm−1) from the methacrylate backbone of both OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd was visible following PDMSS coating with all concentrations of polymer dip‐coating solutions tested, confirming the successful attachment of the anti‐fouling polymers to the Schiff‐base crosslinked silicone. No clear trend in ester signal intensity versus dip coating polymer concentration was observed with the OEGMA‐Hzd polymers, while higher polymer concentrations resulted in significantly more apparent surface modification for the DMAPS‐Hzd polymers. We attribute this result to the significantly higher water binding capacity of the DMAPS‐Hzd polymers that may require a higher concentration gradient to enable significant diffusion of the more hydrophilic DMAPS‐Hzd polymer into the hydrophobic PDMS elastomer matrix. In parallel, XPS results generally indicated an increase in carbon content coupled with a decrease in silicone content when higher concentrations of either OEGMA‐Hzd (Figure 2B) or DMAPS‐Hzd (Figure 2D) were used in the dip coating solution, consistent with effective concentration‐dependent PDMSS functionalization.

Surface Penetration of Anti‐Fouling Polymers

To assess the distribution of the anti‐fouling polymer within the PDMSS elastomer, OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd were covalently functionalized with a fluorescein fluorescent probe to enable analysis of polymer diffusion depth via confocal microscopy; note that the irreversible covalent tethering of the fluorescein fluorophore to the anti‐fouling polymers results in the fluorescent signal being directly correlated with the location of the anti‐fouling polymer within the pre‐formed dynamic silicone network, allowing for direct visual tracking of OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd penetration into PDMSS. For both OEGMA‐Hzd (Figure 2E) and DMAPS‐Hzd (Figure 2F), the anti‐fouling polymer appears to penetrate into the bulk of the PDMSS elastomer, with a significant fluorescence signal measured well below the surface (depth >300 µm). Hence, the formation of the more stable hydrazone bond between the anti‐fouling polymers and the aldehyde groups of the initially imine‐crosslinked PDMS material drives diffusion of the anti‐fouling polymers into the silicone phase despite the relatively unfavorable mixing enthalpies between the anti‐fouling polymer and the silicone phase, enabling effective bulk and not just surface modification. Consistent with the AT‐FTIR results, significant in‐diffusion of OEGMA‐Hzd was observed at all tested polymer concentrations while substantial DMAPS‐Hzd functionalization was only achieved at higher polymer concentrations. This potential for at least partial bulk penetration is consistent with shifts observed in some of the FTIR peaks associated with the silicone precursors (Figure 2A,C) as Schiff base interactions between the silicone precursor materials are partially replaced with hydrazone bonds with the anti‐fouling polymers and/or new hydrogen bonds form between the silicone precursors and the anti‐fouling polymers within the near‐surface AT‐FTIR measurement volume. All the dip‐coated samples maintained high optical transparency (Figure 2G–I), a particular advantage in some applications (e.g. microfluidic chips, contact lenses) that is sometimes challenging to maintain using other PDMS modification techniques.

Mechanical Properties

The compressive moduli of PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd are shown in Figure 3A,C. While the PDMSS samples in general have significantly lower compressive moduli relative to the Sylgard PDMS control, the addition of either anti‐fouling polymer significantly increased the compressive modulus as a function of the anti‐fouling polymer concentration consistent with the increased in‐penetration of the anti‐fouling polymers at higher polymer concentrations shown in the confocal microscopy images. This result demonstrates that the dynamic crosslinking between the silicone elastomer and the hydrazide‐functionalized anti‐fouling polymer results in effective bulk crosslinking of the PDMSS elastomers via the formation of more stable hydrazone bonds upon exchange of the pre‐existing imine crosslinks with either hydrazide‐functionalized anti‐fouling polymer. The shear storage moduli (Figure 3B,D) show a similar trend, with higher shear storage moduli achieved in samples prepared with higher OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd concentrations. Similarly, comparing only the 20 wt.% polymer dip coating solutions, dip coating with either OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd increased both the Young's modulus and the ultimate tensile strength of the PDMSS materials (Figure 3E,F), with functionalization using the OEGMA‐Hzd polymer that has an apparently higher miscibility with the PDMSS phase (Figure 2E) resulting in significantly higher increases in both the Young's modulus and ultimate tensile strength. As such, the dip coating process not only maintains but also enhances multiple aspects of the mechanical properties of the materials.

Self‐Healing Properties

The self‐healing capacity of the anti‐fouling PDMSS materials was evaluated via dry tensile tests following pinned contact of two separately formed strips over 24 h at room temperature. Figure 4A–D shows pictures of the tensile strain response of each sample following self‐healing. All PDMSS samples showed some degree of self‐healing, in contrast to conventional Sylgard PDMS for which no self‐healing was observed and the strips immediately separated at very low applied strains (Figure 4A). Conversely, PDMSS and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd samples did not fracture at the self‐healed interface but rather within the single strip sections (i.e. cohesive rather than adhesive failure was observed) (Figure 4B,D). PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd strips also showed a degree of self‐healing; however, instead of the eventual cohesive failure observed for PDMSS and PDMSS‐DMAPS, the self‐healed interface of PDMSS‐OEGMA gradually slipped without fracturing as strain was applied (Figure 4C), resulting in a significantly higher Young's modulus and ultimate tensile strength for PDMSS‐OEGMA despite its likely lower practical utility in real applications (see Figure 4E for representative raw stress versus strain data, Figure 4F for the percentage of the ultimate tensile strength recovered following self‐healing, and Figure S4 for tensile modulus and ultimate tensile strength data). In this context, the potential for zwitterion fusion between the DMAPS zwitterion groups may provide a secondary self‐healing mechanism in addition to imine/hydrazone bond formation to enhance the self‐healing properties of the material, which is highly beneficial for preventing failure in long‐term biomedical implants.

Surface Hydrophilicity

To assess the influence of the OEGMA/DMAPS dip coating process on the surface hydrophilicity of PDMSS, water contact angles of anti‐fouling silicones prepared using 5–20 wt.% dip coating solutions were measured and compared to those measured for PDMSS, conventionally plasma‐treated PDMS (Sylgard PDMSPLASMA), and untreated Sylgard PDMS at days 1, 7, 14, 28, 56, 84, and 100 post‐fabrication (Figure 5). After 1 day, Sylgard PDMS, Sylgard PDMSPLASMA, and PDMSS exhibited contact angles of 96○, 50○ and 83○ respectively after 5 min of water exposure; in comparison, PDMSS‐OEGMA20wt% (Figure 5A) and PDMSS‐DMAPS20wt% (Figure 5B) showed contact angles of ∼65○ and ∼35○, respectively consistent with the hydrophilicity introduced by the surface‐bound OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd polymers. No significant difference in the contact angle was observed as a function of the concentration of either dip‐coating polymer solution, with all modified surfaces showing rapid wettability. After 100 days (Figure 5C), Sylgard PDMS and PDMSS maintained a similarly high contact angle while Sylgard PDMSPLASMA underwent its typical hydrophobic recovery, with the contact angle increasing back to ∼90°. In contrast, both PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% retained their initial contact angles of ∼50° and ∼20°, respectively, consistent with the high stability of the hydrophilic modification enabled by the dynamic covalent crosslinking‐based technique.

Cell Viability of Anti‐Fouling Polymers and Silicones

To evaluate the cytocompatibility of the OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd polymers and anti‐fouling silicones, a Presto Blue cell viability assay was conducted on NIH 3T3 mouse fibroblasts over a 24 h incubation time. The cell viabilities for both polymers over all polymer concentrations tested (500–5000 µg/mL) were >95% (Figure 6A), while leachates collected following 24 h soaking of the dynamic silicones prepared with and without OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd in PBS maintained cell viabilities of >70% as required by ISO 10993–5:2009 [60] even without dilution (Figure 6B). As such, both the anti‐fouling polymers and the resulting modified silicones are cytocompatible.

Anti‐Clotting Properties

To analyze the anti‐clotting properties and mechanism of anti‐fouling silicone elastomers, recalcification studies and thrombin generation assays in human plasma were performed in vitro on PDMSS samples dip‐coated in 20 wt.% of anti‐fouling polymer solutions, with the 20 wt.% modified samples chosen due to their highest apparent functionalization efficacy as assessed via both chemical (Figure 2) and mechanical (Figure 3) characterization coupled with their maintained transparency (Figure 2G–I). PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% exhibited significantly longer clotting times relative to any of the Sylgard PDMS, PDMSS, Sylgard PDMSPLASMA, or polystyrene surface controls (Figure 6C), reflective of improved hemocompatibility. More significantly, in thrombin generation assays, both PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt showed dramatically reduced peak thrombin (Figure 6D) and impaired thrombin generation (Figure 6E) relative to the control materials, with the PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% material almost eliminating thrombin generation. Based on these observations, the incorporation of the anti‐fouling polymer coatings disrupts the clotting cascade triggered by material contact, thereby significantly reducing clot formation associated with both conventional Sylgard PDMS and the PDMSS silicone materials and resulting in strong anti‐clotting performance; potentially broader anti‐thrombogenic properties will be further investigated in future mechanistic studies.

Inflammatory and Fibrotic Responses to Subcutaneous Implants

The inflammatory response to cell adhesion and fibrotic capsule formation around implants was evaluated in a BALB/c mouse implant model at acute (7 days) and chronic (28 days) timepoints. Note that the anti‐clotting properties of the anti‐fouling silicones were preserved after sterilization by gamma irradiation before in vivo implantation (Figure S5). H&E staining of the recovered tissue (Figure 7A,D) showed that PDMSS implants displayed the highest inflammatory cell density, while PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% implants showed significantly fewer inflammatory cells surrounding the implant, with the latter displaying the lowest and most significant reduction in inflammatory cell number at both the acute and chronic timepoints. Correspondingly, local collagen deposition at the silicone‐tissue interface (as assessed via Masson's trichrome staining, Figure 7E) demonstrated that while conventional Sylgard PDMS exhibited a lower collagen density and degree of collagen organization relative to those observed with unmodified PDMSS, both PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% induced significantly less collagen deposition relative to any control PDMS materials; indeed, there was no significant increase in collagen concentration around the implanted disc for either PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% relative to the native tissue after 28 days in vivo (Figure 7E). Immunohistochemical analysis of the macrophage population evaluated via F4/80 staining at acute and chronic timepoints (Figure 7B,F) confirms a significantly lower macrophage density at the tissue interface with PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% implants relative to Sylgard PDMS and PDMSS, with PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% maintaining particularly low macrophage densities at the chronic timepoint. Correspondingly, both PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% significantly reduced neutrophil populations (as assessed via Ly6g staining) at both acute and chronic timepoints compared to Sylgard PDMS and PDMSS (Figure 7C,G). Adhesion scoring between the implanted silicone discs and the surrounding tissue at the chronic time point further illustrated the significantly reduced fibrotic response associated with the anti‐fouling silicones (Figure 7H). Conventional Sylgard PDMS implants showed strong adhesion to the surrounding tissue (score = 3) while both PDMSS (score = 1.6) and PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% (score = 1.75) exhibited moderate adhesion; in contrast, the PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% discs (score = 0.13) were fully non‐adhered to either skin or muscle within the implant site and could be freely moved around in the subcutaneous space even after 28 days of implantation with minimal applied force.

To supplement the visual analysis of histological images, the inflammatory responses were further quantified based on immune cell density at the tissue‐implant interface at both the acute (Figure 8A) and chronic (Figure 8B) timepoints. Consistent with the visual observations, PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% showed a significantly lower inflammatory cell density compared to all other tested groups at the acute timepoint (Figure 8A) and all groups except PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% at the chronic timepoint (Figure 8B). As such, while the DMAPS‐Hzd coating appears to provide particular advantages in terms of suppressing local inflammatory responses, both anti‐fouling polymer coatings significantly reduced interfacial inflammatory responses to the implants. Correspondingly, measurement of the fibrotic capsule thickness around the implants (Figure 8C) indicated that the PDMSS implants resulted in the thickest capsule while both PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% and PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% implants resulted in significantly thinner capsules, with PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% performing significantly better than even PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% with an error bar that intersects with the x‐axis (i.e. no capsule is present); this result is consistent with the adhesion scoring that showed free movement of the implanted PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% discs under the skin even after one month of implantation. To assess the potential degradation of the dynamically‐crosslinked silicones upon in vivo implantation, the compressive moduli of recovered discs (which may contain not only the implant itself but also adhered skin and/or muscle) were measured and compared to the mechanics of implants pre‐implantation to examine the implant stability over 28 days in vivo (Figure 8D). All materials remained fully intact in vivo over 28 days, with no significant change in disc dimensions (diameter or thickness) observed. Similarly, no significant change in compressive modulus was observed over the chronic implantation period for Sylgard PDMS, PDMSS and PDMSS‐OEGMA‐Hzd20wt% implants; PDMSS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% implants exhibited a significant decrease in compressive modulus between pre‐implantation and the acute timepoint but thereafter retained their mechanical properties over the remainder of the 28‐day implantation period. The strong hydration capacity of the DMAPS‐Hzd polymer coupled with the apparent bulk modification of the dynamically‐crosslinked silicone (Figure 2F) induces localized swelling that reduces the local compressive modulus without compromising the overall bulk stability of the material; in this context, the decrease in compressive modulus observed between 0 and 7 days would be attributed to hydration rather than degradation, consistent with the modulus not further changing over the following 3‐week implantation period.

Overall, although the incorporation of the anti‐fouling OEGMA‐Hzd and DMAPS‐Hzd polymers was demonstrated to enable stable and long‐term surface and (at least to some extent) bulk modification of imine‐crosslinked silicones, the improved penetration of OEGMA‐Hzd into the silicone even at lower polymer concentrations (Figure 2E) coupled with the enhanced tensile strength of OEGMA‐Hzd modified materials (Figure 3E,F) suggests improved miscibility of OEGMA‐Hzd with PDMSS; in contrast, the potential of DMAPS‐Hzd to support secondary zwitterion fusion interactions enables the significantly enhanced self‐healing properties observed (Figure 4). Clear differences were also apparent in the biological tests, with DMAPS‐Hzd polymers enabling significantly better anti‐adhesion and anti‐fibrotic properties (Figures 7 and 8) (although both polymers resulted in substantially improved performance relative to all PDMS control samples). The apparent bulk swelling of the PDMS‐DMAPS‐Hzd20wt% sample (Figure 8D) is also notable in that this material appears to behave at least in part as a hydrogel in the physiological environment, opening other potential application areas beyond conventional PDMS elastomer applications. As such, the choice of anti‐fouling polymer coating can be made rationally depending on the properties to be prioritized in any specific application.

While diverse polymer‐based modification methods have been successful in modifying the surface of silicones to promote anti‐fouling properties, the single‐step dip coating approach proposed herein avoids the need for additional crosslinkers, photoinitiators, UV light, and/or plasma pre‐treatment typically required to develop anti‐fouling and long‐term stable polymer coatings. In addition, the dip coating modification method preserves the silicone's optical transparency, a key feature for many biomedical applications, while better preserving the long‐term hydrophilicity and enhancing the material's self‐healing capacity compared to other reported modification techniques [31, 32, 33, 38, 41, 42, 43] (Figure S6). The key drawback of this approach is the reduced mechanics of the Schiff base material in general relative to conventional PDMS, which is likely not due to the Schiff base crosslinking approach but rather the small molecule terephthalaldehyde crosslinker used. While we anticipate this challenge can be overcome with macromolecular crosslinkers, the mechanics achieved here (modulus on the 30–170 kPa scale depending on the testing modality used) are still highly appropriate for many applications of silicones in medical implants, with silicone implants for plastic and reconstructive surgery representing particularly relevant applications given the dramatically reduced tissue adhesion observed that will lessen external mechanical stresses on the implanted materials. The replacement of (more labile) imine bonds with (more stable) hydrazone bonds upon anti‐fouling polymer functionalization also enhances the mechanics of the material while reducing the reversibility of the network, mitigating potential network stability concerns around the use of a dynamically‐crosslinked base material. We would also note that, while PDMS is not typically used blood‐contacting applications, the main reason it is often avoided in such applications is the typically strong pro‐clotting properties of most PDMS‐based materials [61]; indeed, persistent inflammation and foreign body responses to PDMS implants can lead to device malfunction and reduced hearing quality for cochlear implants [61] and the need to replace breast implants within 10–20 years due to fibrotic capsule contracture [12]. As such, the combination of the elastic properties and anti‐clotting properties of Schiff base‐PDMS may open new practical applications of PDMS‐based materials in biomedical applications.

Conclusions

Imine‐crosslinked silicone elastomers can be rendered anti‐fouling via dynamic covalent crosslinking of hydrazide‐functionalized OEGMA‐Hzd or DMAPS‐Hzd polymers. The dynamic crosslinking of both the silicone network and the anti‐fouling polymers results in not only surface but also bulk modification of the dynamic elastomer, resulting in long‐term interfacial hydrophilicity over at least 100 days while also increasing the tensile and compressive strength of the silicone as a function of anti‐fouling polymer concentration. The anti‐fouling silicone elastomers significantly delayed clot formation and dramatically reduced thrombin generation when in contact with recalcified human plasma, showing strong anti‐clotting properties; in parallel, subcutaneous implantation of the anti‐fouling silicone elastomers resulted in significantly reduced inflammatory responses and, particularly for the DMAPS‐Hzd‐modified silicones, significantly reduced immune cell density, collagen deposition and suppression of fibrotic capsule formation over the 28‐day implantation time. Overall, we anticipate these dynamic silicone materials to significantly improve long‐term outcomes of the use of silicone‐based biomedical devices, particularly those subjected to long‐term implantation and/or subjected to complex biological fluids (e.g. biosensors).
