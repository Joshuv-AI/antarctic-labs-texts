Self‐Powered Flexible Triboelectric‐Gated Ion‐Gel Transistor for Neuromorphic Tactile Sensing and Human Activity Recognition
Authors: Hanseong Cho, Seoyeon Park, Youngmin Lee, Sejoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWearable and flexible neuromorphic devices capable of accurately emulating synaptic behaviors while autonomously responding to mechanical stimuli hold great promise for intelligent sensing and bio‐inspired computing. Here, we present a fully flexible synaptic device in which a graphene‐channel ion‐gel‐gated transistor (g‐IGT) fabricated on a plastic substrate is directly driven by a poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐trifluoroethylene)‐based triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG), enabling self‐powered tactile sensing and analog weight storage. The device emulates the multistore memory hierarchy through sensory–short‐term–long‐term memory transitions, characterized by synaptic decay times of ∼70 ms (sensory), 0.2–0.45 s (short‐term), and >2.0 s (long‐term). Furthermore, rate‐coded learning is demonstrated through spike‐rate‐dependent plasticity, enabling frequency‐selective potentiation and depression. When the experimentally measured weight‐update profiles are implemented in a single‐layer perceptron for human activity recognition, the neural network system achieves >88% classification accuracy, even under 1.1 MPa bending stress. Furthermore, the network system maintains relatively stable performance (>75%) even under the extreme environment with a high noise level. These results establish the TENG‐driven g‐IGT as a viable route toward mechanically compliant, battery‐free neuromorphic platforms capable of sensing, learning, and adapting in situ.

Introduction

The rapid progress of wearable electronics has intensified efforts to design novel devices capable of seamless interaction with biological systems [1, 2, 3]. In this context, neuromorphic devices that emulate the functionalities of biological neural networks have emerged as promising candidates for efficient [4, 5, 6], low‐power [7, 8, 9], and intelligent interfaces for health monitoring and multimodal sensing [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Central to this pursuit is the ability to reproduce key synaptic operations, such as signal transmission and plasticity, that enable artificial neural networks to process and adapt to sensory inputs in a brain‐like manner [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Among the available device architectures, the graphene‐channel ion‐gel‐gated transistor (g‐IGT) stands out due to the exceptional carrier mobility and electrostatic transparency of graphene, combined with the flexibility and electric double‐layer functionality of ion gels. These features allow operation at low voltages (<2 V) and analog synaptic weight modulation that closely mimics ionic dynamics in biological synapses [21, 22, 23]. Recent studies have reported sub‐100 fJ synaptic events, linear conductance tuning, and large‐scale fabrication compatibility in g‐IGTs. For example, Kim et al. demonstrated impressive mobilities of 203 ± 57 cm2/V·s (holes) and 91 ± 50 cm2/V·s (electrons), while maintaining stable operation under bending stress [24]. Likewise, Liu et al. realized a side‐gated g‐IGT that exhibited diverse synaptic functions in both flat and bent states, and even performed a Pavlov's‐dog experiment under combined electrical and optical stimuli [25]. Despite these advances, most reported g‐IGTs still rely on external power supplies and rigid substrates, limiting their practical use in fully integrated wearable neuromorphic systems.

Human tactile perception originates from mechanoreceptors that sense even minute mechanical disturbances and convert them into neural spikes. To replicate this process electronically, materials are required that can directly transduce mechanical motion into electrical outputs. Approaches based on capacitive [26] and piezoelectric [27, 28, 29] mechanisms have been reported, and most recently, triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) [30, 31, 32] have emerged as a particularly effective alternative since their introduction by Wang et al. in 2012 [33]. TENGs are distinguished by their ability to harvest energy from subtle body movements, vibrations, or touch, while exhibiting sensitivity down to sub‐milli‐newton forces [34, 35]. During contact electrification, TENG devices generate sharp voltage pulses that can exceed 100 V and deliver currents in the tens of microampere range [36, 37]. These outputs are sufficiently strong to modulate low‐power synaptic transistors and simultaneously drive their conductance. Despite these advantages, the intrinsically high output impedance and spike‐pulsed nature of TENG signals complicate direct use with conventional electronic circuits [35]. To overcome this issue, several studies have coupled TENGs with rectifying or charge‐storage circuit modules to operate sensors or memory devices [38]. However, producing a stable DC output from the raw TENG waveforms still requires additional power‐conditioning components, which hinders full integration.

Considerable efforts have been devoted to integrating TENGs with IGTs. For instance, Yu et al. reported a TENG‐gated MoS2 IGT that achieved an ultralow switching energy of 11.9 fJ per event on a rigid SiO2/Si substrate [39]. Later, Zeng et al. demonstrated triboelectrically driven synaptic functions on a flexible platform; however, their device still required an external drain bias, and thus was not fully self‐powered [40]. More generally, most flexible TENG–IGT studies remain focused on device‐level performance, without extending to system‐level demonstrations such as end‐to‐end simulations or task benchmarks [41, 42]. These findings suggest both the promise and the limitations of the current technology, bringing into focus four unresolved challenges that must be overcome for practical sensory‐neuromorphic applications: (i) faithful emulation of the multistore memory hierarchy, (ii) achieving linear and stable synaptic weight updates, (iii) implementing spike‐rate‐dependent plasticity (SRDP) under electrical and triboelectric stimuli as well as during mechanical bending, and (iv) demonstrating system‐level relevance through dynamic motion‐recognition tasks. Specifically, for (i), reproducing the temporal and hierarchical transition of ‘sub‐100 ms sensory memory (SM) → a few hundred milliseconds short‐term memory (STM) → multi‐second long‐term memory (LTM) states is essential. For (ii), the transient and non‐Gaussian nature of TENG outputs often results in asymmetric potentiation/depression and rapid conductance decay. For (iii), SRDP, a key frequency‐dependent Hebbian learning rule, has not yet been demonstrated in a TENG‐driven IGT. Finally, for (iv), translating triboelectric spikes into real‐time human activity recognition remains an unexplored but crucial step toward validating system‐level functionality for dynamic motion‐tracking inference.

Driven by the need for battery‐free and mechanically‐compliant neuromorphic platforms, we developed a self‐powered TENG‐driven g‐IGT on a polyethylene‑terephthalate (PET) substrate that unifies tactile energy harvesting with synaptic operation. After establishing the device architecture and electromechanical behavior, we explore pulse‐resolved synaptic dynamics including excitatory/inhibitory responses, paired‐pulse plasticity, and rehearsal‐driven SM–STM–LTM transition. We further confirm the SRDP functionality under both electrical and triboelectric stimulation, even under mechanical bending, demonstrating robust and stable frequency‐dependent learning activity in a self‐powered mode. Finally, by integrating hardware‐derived long‐term potentiation/depression (LTP/LTD) kernels into a neuromorphic inference framework, we validate system‐level functionality through human activity recognition (HAR), linking triboelectric synaptic plasticity to real‐time on‐body computation.

Results and Discussion

Device Architecture and Fundamental Device Characteristics

Figure 1a compares the biological tactile pathway (upper left) with its electronic analogue (lower left). In the human arm, mechanoreceptors transduce skin deformation into spike trains that propagate along afferent neurons and are weighted at chemical synapses. Mimicking this process, two TENGs are coupled to a g‐IGT: the gate‐side TENG supplies pre‐synaptic spikes, whereas the drain‐side TENG generates post‐synaptic spikes. During contact–separation, the poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF‐TrFE)) layer accumulates opposite triboelectric charges. The resulting voltage pulses drive 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium (EMIM+) and bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (TFSI−) ions within the ion‐gel to migrate toward or away from the graphene channel, thereby modulating the postsynaptic current (ΔPSC) in a manner analogous to neurotransmitter‐induced synaptic current in biology. Since both spikes are harvested directly from ambient touch, the circuit operates entirely without an external battery while maintaining the causal pre/post ordering required for SRDP. The device's structural flexibility is essential for wearable applications, and this is visually and experimentally validated through bending tests and optical imaging (Figure 1b). Fabrication details are provided in Figure S1, and Raman scattering and X‐ray diffraction data confirming monolayer graphene and the β‐phase of P(VDF‐TrFE) are shown in Figure S2.

Figure 1c illustrates the schematic of the measurement setup for evaluating the fundamental memory characteristics. In this configuration, the graphene channel is separated from the gate by an ion‐gel layer that acts as an electrostatic synaptic cleft. Figure 1d compares the drain current vs. gate voltage (I D–V G) transfer curves, measured by double sweep at three scan rates (0.20, 0.10, and 0.05 V/s, left to right). At the relatively fast scan rate of 0.20 V/s (Figure 1d, left), the forward and reverse traces diverge significantly, producing a memory window (ΔV M) of 1.23 V when the sweep voltage (V sweep) range of V G was ±3 V (see also Figure S3). Operational insights into ionic distributions under gate modulation, including electron and hole accumulation within the graphene channel, are comprehensively presented in Figure S4. Reducing the scan rate to 0.05 V/s (Figure 1d, right) allows the EMIM+/TFSI− ion pairs to approach equilibrium at each bias step. As a result, the residual interfacial charge is smaller and the hysteresis loop narrows to 0.69 V at V sweep = ±3 V. This rate‐dependent broadening confirms that synaptic hysteresis in the graphene channel is governed by the charging and relaxation dynamics of ions in the ion‐gel electrolyte.

To further verify the spatial uniformity of the fabrication process, we assess cycle‐to‐cycle and device‐to‐device consistency by measuring nine discrete devices across the chip, all of which displayed consistent ambipolar behavior and hysteresis evolution (see Figure S5). When performing 20‐cycle double‐sweep transfer measurements on these multiple discrete samples at each scan rate (0.20, 0.10, and 0.05 V/s; Figure S6), the devices exhibit highly reproducible switching behavior, maintaining consistent hysteresis loop shapes and window widths throughout the cycles. Specifically, the average hysteresis width systematically decreases as the scan rate slows (from 1.1 V at 0.20 V/s to 0.85 V at 0.05 V/s). This confirms that the response is governed by predictable ion dynamics rather than instability. Additionally, the devices exhibited long‐term stability during 1000 consecutive set/reset switching cycles, demonstrating stable conductance states with negligible fatigue (see Figure S7).

Figure 1e presents the drain voltage (V D)‐dependent transfer characteristics measured at V G(sweep)​ = ±2 V under V D​ ranging from 1 to 100 mV. Increasing V D enhances the lateral field, lowers the injection barrier at the graphene/ion‐gel interface, and amplifies the hysteresis window. The on‐state current rises from 0.69 µA at V D​ = 1 mV to 17.2 µA at 100 mV, demonstrating highly analog, V D‐tunable channel‐conductance modulation. Output characteristic curves (I D–V D at V G​ = −2.0 to +2.0 V; Figure S8) remain linear and symmetric, confirming Ohmic contacts and stable channel conductance across the full synaptic voltage window.

In the case of the TENG, the vertical contact–separation mode (Figure 1f) converts mechanical impact into triboelectric charge through repeated contact and release of the PET/Al and P(VDF‐TrFE)/Al layers [43] (see also Figure S9). To verify the dominant charge generation mechanism, we initially conducted control experiments comparing the contact‐separation (triboelectric dominant) and continuous compression (piezoelectric only) modes. As shown in Figure S10a,b, the piezoelectric contribution under 1.5 N was found to be negligible (∼0.25 V) compared to the triboelectric output (∼2.4 V). This confirms that the sharp voltage spikes driving the synaptic plasticity are primarily attributed to the contact electrification and electrostatic induction during the separation phase. Here, precise independent control of contact force and frequency was achieved using a feedback‐controlled linear motor system (Figure S11).

Figure 1g shows the voltage pulses generated when the contact–separation force is varied from 0.25 to 2.0 N. Each cycle produces a bipolar pulse pair: a positive spike (V TENG+) during contact and a negative spike (V TENG−) upon release. Both amplitudes increase monotonically with the impact force. The trend is summarized in Figure 1h, where V TENG+​ increases from 0.6 V at 0.25 N to 3.0 V at 2.0 N, and V TENG−​ increases from |−1.01 V| at 0.25 N to |−5.3 V| at 2.0 N. The high determination coefficient (R2 = 0.99) was obtained from the relationship between V TENG and contact–separation force, confirming that the TENG output provides a mechanical force‐ and/or velocity‐encoded voltage stimulus. To further verify the charge delivery capability, we also evaluated the output current characteristics of the TENG device (Figure S12). Consistent with the voltage behavior, the output current (I TENG) that flows across the load resistance of 1 MΩ exhibited a force‐dependent increase in I TENG, reaching a peak value of approximately 2.91 µA at 1.5 N. In addition to plausible output performance, the TENG demonstrated excellent mechanical durability and electrical stability, maintaining consistent voltage amplitudes over 1000 continuous operating cycles (see Figure S13). Furthermore, the TENG device displayed stable output characteristics under bending‐induced tensile stress (Figure S14). For instance, even at σ f = 0.32 MPa, the pulse train remained stable with only ∼15% peak‐amplitude variation compared to the flat state, which is consistent with the expected reduction of effective contact areas under bended states (i.e., when curvature ↑, contact area ↓).

Pulse‐Resolved Synaptic Dynamics and Memory Transition

After establishing the structural design and fundamental electrical behavior (Figure 1), we next examined the synaptic plasticity of the fabricated g‐IGT. As shown in Figure 2, excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic current (EPSC/IPSC) responses were systematically characterized under varying pulse amplitudes (V pulse) and durations (t pulse), providing direct insight into biologically relevant dynamics. Synaptic transients clearly differentiate between EPSC (Figure 2a, red) and IPSC (Figure 2b, blue) when the negative and positive pulses (V pulse = ±0.5–1.5 V, t pulse = 10–100 ms) are applied to the g‐IGT, respectively. For both EPSC and IPSC, the amplitudes of ΔPSC increased progressively with longer t pulse and larger V pulse (Figure 2c,d). To further analyze the dependence of the updated postsynaptic current (ΔPSC) on these pulse parameters, we extracted the synaptic decay time (τ decay) from each dataset. The τ decay values were obtained by fitting the transients with the stretched exponential function (SEF), which was originally introduced to describe capacitor discharge and dielectric polarization decay [44, 45]. This function has been widely applied to describe time‐dependent phenomena in diverse physical systems [46, 47, 48]. The SEF is expressed as (1) It=I∞+Ipeak−I∞·e−t−t0τdecayβ where I(t) is the output current, I peak is the peak EPSC (or IPSC), I ∞ is the final value after decay, t 0 is the time of the presynaptic spike, and β is the stretching exponent ranging from 0 to 1. To determine the optimal β, EPSC and IPSC data recorded at t pulse = 20 ms under various V pulse were fitted with β values from 0.3 to 1.0. The extracted τ decay exhibited a bell‐shaped dependence on β (see Figure S15a,b). Namely, τ decay increased continuously from β ≈ 0.3, reached the maximum (≈ 270 ms for EPSC and ≈ 220 ms for IPSC) at β ≈ 0.7, and then became saturated. In contrast, the R2 (i.e., coefficient of determination) peaked at a slightly lower exponent of β ≈ 0.5 (see Figure S15c,d). To balance the physical relevance of long τ decay with the statistical fidelity of the fit, we fixed β at 0.6, which is an intermediate value that preserves more than 95% of the maximum τ decay while keeping R2 within 1% of its optimum. This choice ensures accurate modeling of both short‐term and long‐term decay characteristics of the synaptic device, thereby bridging the timescales of biological synaptic relaxation with the quantitative fidelity required for hardware‐based neuromorphic simulations. Figure 2e,f shows that the τ decay as a function of t pulse for EPSC/IPSC. The g‐IGT exhibits τ decay values ranging from approximately 70 to 450 ms for EPSC and 70 to 300 ms for IPSC, both of which increase with higher |V pulse| (|−0.5| to |−1.5 V| for EPSCs; 0.5 to 1.5 V for IPSCs) and longer t pulse (10–100 ms). These dynamic changes in τ decay correspond well to the multistore memory hierarchy. At t pulse = 10 ms, the device yields τ decay of ∼70 ms for both EPSC and IPSC, which fall within the SM regime and closely match the decay characteristic of the N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor (τ decay (SM) = 60–100 ms) [49, 50]. For longer pulses (>> 10 ms), τ decay drastically increases up to approximately 300–450 ms for EPSC and 200–300 ms for IPCS. This depicts that the g‐IGT extends its memory state into the STM regime with longer τ decay(STM) values, reflecting enhanced synaptic integration. By reproducing these biologically relevant timescales and amplifying decay times under stronger input stimuli, the g‐IGT captures essential features of neural plasticity and provides a tunable temporal response for diverse neuromorphic applications.

To further investigate the transition from SM to STM, we examined paired‐pulse plasticity in the g‐IGT. For this measurement, two consecutive voltage pulses were applied with varying pulse‐to‐pulse intervals (t inter), and the resulting EPSC and IPSC responses were recorded. Namely, two consecutive pulses (i.e., V pulse = +1 V with t pulse = 20 ms for paired‐pulse facilitation (PPF) and V pulse = −1 V with t pulse = 20 ms for paired‐pulse depression (PPD)) were applied while t inter varied from 10 to 500 ms. As shown in Figure 3a,b, the second pulse in each pair elicited a change in ±ΔPSC, corresponding to PPF and PPD, respectively (see also the t inter‐dependent PPF/PPD transients in Figure S16). These characteristics were quantified by comparing the updated response amplitude at the second pulse (A 2) relative to the first (A 1), as defined in Figure 3c,d. The maximum values of the PPF and PPD indices reached approximately 75%. Both indices exhibited an exponential decrease with increasing t inter. The t inter‐dependent PPF and PPD behaviors were fitted using the following double‐exponential function [52, 53, 54]: (3) ft=y0+A1exp−Δtτ1+A2exp−Δtτ2 where τ 1 and τ 2 represent the characteristic relaxation times of the fast and slow phases, respectively [55]. In our synaptic g‐IGT, τ 1 and τ 2 were estimated to be 73 and 344 ms for PPF, respectively, and 74 and 214 ms for PPD. These values are consistent with those reported for biological synapses [56]. To better understand the effect of t inter on the SM–STM transition, we analyzed the decay times τ PPF(A2) and τ PPD(A2) associated with the second A 2 response (Figure 3e,f). When t inter << τ decay(STM) (= 200–450 ms for EPSC/IPSC), both τ PPF(A2) and τ PPD(A2) increase with increasing t inter. For such short intervals, ions lack sufficient time to fully migrate and stabilize at the ion‐gel/channel interface; consequently, incompletely diffused ions take additional time during the increased t inter to reorganize and establish a stable EDL, thereby prolonging the decay time of the second A 2 response. In contrast, when t inter ≥ τ decay(STM), τ PPF(A2) and τ PPD(A2) decrease sharply with increasing t inter, as ions accumulated at the EDL gradually diffuse back into the bulk ion‐gel electrolyte because of the limited τ decay. These findings suggest that t inter should remain below τ decay(STM) to effectively mediate the hierarchical SM–STM–LTM transition.

Figure 4 illustrates a rehearsal‐induced transition from STM to LTM in the g‐IGT, achieved by adjusting the number of incoming spike pulses (n pulse). Successive potentiating bursts progressively elevate the ΔPSC (Figure 4a). As a result, the LTP gain (= A n/A 1) increases with n pulse (Figure 4b). For instance, at t inter = 20 ms, the LTP gain rises from 4.08 at n pulse = 8 to 11.9 at n pulse = 128. In parallel, the τ LTP extends from 0.4 to 2.3 s (Figure 4c), reflecting a rehearsal‐driven progression from STM to LTM through increasing n pulse. LTD exhibits a similar trend (Figure 4d), yielding a maximum gain of 8.7 and τ LTD of 2.4 s at n pulse = 128 (Figure 4e,f). This transition is dictated by how the spike train modulates ion kinetics at the ion‐gel/graphene interface. In short, each spike injects EMIM+ or TFSI− ions that populate the EDL and enhance channel‐conductance. For example, at intervals shorter than the ion relaxation time, incompletely diffused ions gradually stabilize during consecutive n pulse, thereby increasing charge accumulation in the graphene channel and producing large LTP and LTD gains. In contrast, relatively longer intervals allow the accumulated ions in the EDL to diffuse back to the bulk electrolyte, percolate into nanometer‐scale gel pores [57], become trapped at residual poly(methyl methacrylate) sites [58], or participate in slow redox processes at graphene edges and van der Waals gaps [59, 60, 61], thereby reducing the conductance increment. For instance, as t inter increased from 20 to 100 ms, the LTP gain at n pulse = 64 decreased from 9.1 to 4.8 (Figure 4b) and the LTD gain from 8.1 to 4.3 (Figure 4e). In contrast to the LTP and LTD gains, τ LTP and τ LTD exhibited the opposite dependence on t inter. As discussed earlier, choosing an appropriate t inter below τ decay provides an EDL stabilization period. Thus, as t inter increased from 20 to 100 ms, τ LTP at n pulse = 64 increased from 1.4 to 2.2 s (Figure 4c) and τ LTD from 1.3 to 2.0 s (Figure 4f). Consequently, the inverse correlation between gain and retention originates from the competition between faster interfacial charging and slower ionic migration/chemistry. By adjusting t inter and n pulse, therefore, the device can switch from STM to LTM through simultaneous modulation of dynamic range and data retention (see also Figure S17), thereby effectively reproducing rehearsal‐driven consolidation in an energy‐efficient, time‐adaptive neuromorphic platform (see Figure S18).

Spike Rate‐Dependent Plasticity Driven by Electrical and Triboelectric Stimuli

In neuromorphic devices, SRDP encodes long‐term plasticity as a function of pre‐ and post‐synaptic firing rates, representing a fundamental learning and memory mechanism. Specifically, SRDP describes how synaptic weights are modulated by varying the frequency of spike trains (i.e., action potentials) applied to the pre‐synapse (f pre) and post‐synapse (f post) [62, 63, 64, 65]. To evaluate the SRDP characteristics of our g‐IGT, we therefore systematically investigated synaptic responses as a function of both f pre and f post ​(Figure 5). First, we evaluated the electrically‐driven SRDP characteristics by applying a gate pulse (V pulse(G) = +0.4 V, 20 ms) to the pre‐synapse and a drain pulse (V pulse(D) = +0.5 V, 20 ms) to the post‐synapse. As shown in Figure 5a, the f pre‐dependent SRDP characteristics were examined by varying f pre from 15 to 45 Hz while maintaining f post at 25 Hz. Figure 5b presents the f pre​‐dependent ΔPSC, clearly revealing SRDP behavior. In the absence of V pulse(G), the application of +V pulse(D) alone induced a pronounced LTP response. This effect arises because +V pulse(D) can also facilitate the accumulation of interfacial TFSI− ions, forming an EDL within the ion‐gel electrolyte and thereby modulating the charge population at the graphene channel (see Figure 6a). However, the dynamic range of the LTP‐related ΔPSC decreased when +V pulse(G) was applied to the pre‐synapse at a relatively low f pre = 15 Hz. This reduction occurs because the EDL induced by +V pulse(G) counteracts the EDL generated by +V pulse(D), as the two possess opposite electrical dipoles (see Figure 6b). In other words, V pulse(D) accumulates interfacial charge, while V pulse(G) serves as a neuromodulator that resets and biases the interfacial state [66, 67]. When f pre = f post = 25 Hz, the device entered a firing‐rate parity condition, yielding no observable ΔPSC update. At higher f pre values above 35 Hz, surprisingly, the ΔPSC response shifted into the LTD regime, with the depression becoming particularly pronounced at f pre = 45 Hz. This LTP‐to‐LTD transition can be attributed to the increased IPSC generated when +V pulse(G) is applied at higher f pre​ (> f post), which counterbalances the EPSC induced by +V pulse(D) (see Figure 6c). These findings demonstrate that both the polarity (LTP vs. LTD) and the magnitude of synaptic weight changes can be systematically tuned by adjusting the rate difference (Δf = |f pre − f post|), thereby confirming the ability of g‐IGT to faithfully reproduce SRDP. Consequently, the observed SRDP behaviors allow the g‐IGT to function as a tunable analog frequency filter that can convert relative spike rates into bidirectional synaptic weight updates.

Beyond electrical stimulation, it is equally important to determine whether SRDP can be realized when the device operates in a fully self‐powered mode. To verify this, we evaluated triboelectrically‐driven SRDP using TENG inputs, with the post‐synapse held at a constant frequency and the pre‐synapse varied across different rates. As shown in Figure 5c, TENG outputs were applied to both synaptic terminals: the drain (post‐synapse) received a constant f post of 10 Hz, while the gate (pre‐synapse) was driven at variable f pre ranging from 5 to 20 Hz. Consistent with the electrically stimulated SRDP, the device exhibited clear TENG‐driven SRDP (Figure 5d), with ΔPSC evolving systematically with f pre. In the absence of V pulse(G), the V pulse(D) alone induced strong LTP due to the accumulation of interfacial TFSI− ions. Introducing V pulse(G) at f pre = 5 Hz partially suppressed this potentiation. When f pre equaled f post (10 Hz), the EDLs were nearly balanced inside the electrolyte, resulting in negligible ΔPSC variation. Increasing f pre to 15 Hz inverted the ELSC/IPSC balance and initiated weak LTD, while further raising f pre to 20 Hz produced pronounced LTD. Notably, the device maintained consistent SRDP functionality even under mechanical bending stress up to 1.1 MPa (Figure 5e,f), demonstrating its robustness and stable neuromorphic performance. Furthermore, other g‐IGT devices also exhibited the identical features of TENG‐driven SRDP even under flex (see also Figure S19). Additionally, extended tests over a stress window of 0.39–1.1 MPa yielded constant ΔPSC values across pulse trains at 1–100 Hz (Figure S20a), and the endurance tests over 1000 bending cycles at a higher tensile stress of 1.1 MPa displayed stable ΔPSC values (Figure S20b). These depict the robustness and stability of interfacial ion dynamics in the g‐IGT and its associated synaptic plasticity under mechanical deformation.

To evaluate how the g‐IGT converts physical stimulation into synaptic weight changes, we extracted the updated conductance (ΔG) values at each pulse and plotted them as a function of n pulse, yielding LTP and LTD curves with respect to n pulse (Figure 7). These curves clearly differentiate strong and weak synaptic modifications induced by three types of input: pure electrical pulses (Figure 7a), TENG pulses under the flat state (Figure 7b), and TENG pulses under flex (Figure 7c). The lower panels in Figure 7d–f summarize the dynamic range index (G max/G min) and non‐linearity factor (NL) as a function of Δf (= f pre – f post), which were extracted from the following equations [68, 69, 70]: (4) GLTP=A1−e−npulseNL+Gmin (5) GLTD=−A1−enpulse−nmaxNL+Gmax (6) A=Gmax−Gmin/1−e−nmaxNL where G LTP and G LTD are the conductance values at the LTP and LTD states, respectively, and A is a fitting constant used to normalize the conductance range. Across all stimulation modes, the G max/G min ratio exhibits a V‐shaped dependence: it decreases to about 0.9 at rate parity (Δf ≈ 0) and increases to nearly 40 as |Δf| widens. For Δf‐dependent NL, electrical pulses provide the most linear updates with values ranging from 1.3 to 2.3 (Figure 7d). TENG pulses under the flat state show a slightly broader range (1.5–2.4, Figure 7e), while comparable values (1.5–2.5) are retained even under flex at a bending stress of 1.1 MPa (Figure 7f).

Inference Tasks for Human Activity Recognition

The SRDP characteristics demonstrated above enable frequency‐dependent modulation of synaptic weights, providing a natural foundation for temporal pattern learning. Building on this principle, we next evaluated whether the g‐IGT's SRDP‐driven plasticity can support sequence learning and HAR. To evaluate whether device‐level plasticity can support sequence learning, we constructed a conceptual neuromorphic HAR system that mimics how physical movement data are processed within a simplified neural architecture. For this, as illustrated in Figure 8a, we used time‐dependent acceleration signals from wearable waist‐mounted sensors, extracting triaxial streams (x‐, y‐, and z‐axes) for six representative activities: walking (Walk), walking upstairs (Up), walking downstairs (Down), sitting (Sit), standing (Stand), and lying (Lay). These data are provided by the publicly available UCI HAR dataset (see also Methods: HAR Training Dataset) [71, 72, 73]. The raw acceleration data were segmented into 2.56 s windows (128 timesteps at a 50 Hz sampling rate) and converted into feature tensors that preserve both temporal continuity and directional variance. Each segmented waveform was then fed into a single‐layer perceptron with 384 input nodes (128 timesteps × 3 axes), fully connected to six output neurons (Figure 8b). To isolate the contribution of synaptic device characteristics to HAR performance, we deliberately adopted a single‐layer perceptron architecture, excluding hidden layers that could obscure the influence of device‐level plasticity. This streamlined configuration ensures that classification outcomes are governed solely by the temporal transformations encoded in the synaptic weight update dynamics.

To bridge the gap between algorithmic learning models and experimentally observed synaptic behaviors, we incorporated a g‐IGT‐based artificial synapse into the HAR simulation framework. As shown in Figure S21 (Supporting Information), the normalized ΔG and n pulse‐dependent LTP/LTD curves obtained under electrical and triboelectric modes (under both flat and flex) were fitted with a double‐exponential function (Equation (3)). The extracted parameters were then used to initialize a synaptic‐transformation module placed on top of the device model. Specifically, two independent parameter sets (y 0, A 1, τ 1, A 2, and τ 2) for potentiation/depression were transferred from the g‐IGT fits to the higher‐level learning algorithm. For each 384‐element input vector, temporal derivatives (Δx) were computed, and either the LTP or LTD kernel was selectively applied depending on the sign of each element (i.e., potentiation for Δx ≥ 0, and depression for Δx < 0), thereby replicating the g‐IGT's synaptic behavior at each time step. The transformed feature vector was then passed to a six‐class SoftMax output layer, where the probability of each class i was computed as [74, 75]: (7) Pi=ezi∑j=16ezi with z i denoting the logit corresponding to class i. All parameters were optimized via back‐propagation. The network was trained on 5881 labeled sequences and validated on an independent set of 1471 examples (80:20 split). With this configuration, the network rapidly adapted to the hardware‐derived synaptic dynamics. As shown in Figure 8c, the learning curves reveal fast convergence in all cases. After 200 epochs, the classification accuracy reached 89.04% (ideal), 88.78% (electrical), 88.46% (TENG under flat), and 88.05% (TENG under flex). These values differ by only ≤1%, confirming that self‐powered operation and moderate mechanical strain do not significantly erode inference accuracy. Confusion matrices at the first and 200th epochs for the TENG‐driven synapses under the flat state evolve from near‐random to sharply diagonal (Figure 8d), evidencing robust recall of every class. As shown in Figure 8e, the corresponding input‐weight heatmaps show that the initially featureless distribution segregates into six well‐defined clusters. A qualitatively identical progression is observed for the TENG‐driven synapses under flex (Figure 8f,g), indicating that neither triboelectric waveform jitter nor flexural stress compromises long‐term inference performance. Taken together, Figure 8 demonstrates that the triboelectric gating scheme converts stochastic, self‐harvested mechanical energy into synaptic weight updates that are sufficiently reproducible to sustain >88% recognition accuracy in a realistic HAR benchmark.

Building on this result, we next examined robustness against common signal distortions expected in practical wearable operation (Figure 9). Specifically, we introduced four perturbation types to emulate realistic hardware and environmental instabilities (see Figure S22): (1) Gaussian noise (σ = 0–0.2, normalized) to account for thermal and environmental background; (2) signal dropout (0.5%–2.0%) to simulate intermittent contact/interface failures; (3) gate–drain misalignment (temporal delays of 1–4 time steps) to model mechanical lag induced by complex deformation; and (4) amplitude fluctuation (1%–15% variation) to represent irregular human impact forces and bending‐induced changes in the effective contact area (see Figure S14). These artifacts were jointly scaled across five intensity levels (Lv. 0–Lv. 4) to quantify the evolution of training accuracy (see Table S1). As shown in Figure 9a, after 200 epochs, the model achieved the maximum accuracy of 88.05% under the noise‐free condition (Lv. 0), whereas increasing the noise intensity to Lv. 4 reduced the accuracy to 54.8% (see also Figure 9a, inset). Notably, the model maintained relatively stable performance (>75%) up to Lv. 2 (high noise level), indicating resilience against moderate signal perturbations. The corresponding distorted input feature maps, confusion matrices, and synaptic weight distributions at Level 1–4 are summarized in Figure 9b–e, showing that static activities remain largely distinguishable, while severe distortions (Lv. 4) primarily hinder the classification of dynamic activities and blur the learned feature patterns. Overall, these results confirm robustness against realistic noise levels expected in wearable operation, while highlighting performance trade‐offs under extreme signal degradation. Together with the HAR results (Figures 8 and 9), this system‐level robustness supports practical deployment of triboelectrically gated synaptic transistors for on‐body intelligence.

Conclusions

We demonstrated a fully flexible, self‐powered neuromorphic synapse on a PET substrate, where a P(VDF‐TrFE) TENG directly gates a graphene‐channel IGT. The device achieves (i) biologically relevant multistore memory hierarchy spanning its synaptic decay times from ∼70 ms (SM), 0.2–0.45 s (STM), to >2 s (LTM), (ii) highly linear synaptic weight updates (NL ≈ 1.3–2.5), and (iii) SRDP functioning as an analog frequency comparator. Notably, triboelectric pulses harvested from mechanical contact directly modulate synaptic conductance without any external power conditioning. Moreover, the device maintains stable performance under 1.1 MPa bending stress. When implemented in a single‐layer perceptron, the experimentally measured LTP/LTD kernels enable >88% accuracy in six‐class human activity recognition, which is only ∼1% below the ideal software model. Additionally, the relatively high accuracy (>75%) was recorded when adopting a high‐level noise environment. Collectively, these findings bridge the gap between proof‐of‐concept triboelectric gating and system‐level neuromorphic inference, paving the way toward wearable artificial intelligence that translates ubiquitous body motion into meaningful on‐body computation.

Experimental Section

Materials

P(VDF‐TrFE) (density: 1.74 g/mL at 25°C), poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (average Mn 575, density: 1.12 g/mL), and 2‐hydroxy‐2‐methylpropiophenone (Mw: 164.20 g/mol, density: 1.077 g/mL) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich and used as received. For the ion‐gel electrolyte, [EMIM][TFSI] (Mw: 391.31 g/mol) was obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. Single‐layer graphene was synthesized on a copper foil via chemical vapor deposition. Commercially available PET films were utilized as flexible substrates for both the TENG and the transistor.

g‐IGT Fabrication

Monolayer graphene was synthesized on a Cu foil by chemical vapor deposition and subsequently transferred onto a PET substrate (t PET = 100 µm). The graphene film was patterned by photolithography, and the unwanted regions were removed through O2 plasma etching to define the channel area (channel length L = 300 µm; channel width W = 100 µm). Source and drain contacts and an in‐plane gate were then deposited on the PET sheet using e‐beam evaporation. The ion‐gel electrolyte was prepared from the ionic liquid [EMIM][TFSI], the photo‐curable monomer poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate, and the photoinitiator 2‐hydroxy‐2‐methylpropiophenone in an 88:8:4 mass ratio. After spin‐coating, the ion‐gel layer thickness was approximately 10 µm and was patterned by selective UV exposure (365 nm, 100 mW cm−2, 10 s). The illuminated regions cross‐linked, while the unexposed portions were removed with deionized water, leaving the final gate dielectric structure.

TENG Fabrication

PET sheets were first cleaned, after which aluminum was thermally evaporated to form both the bottom and top electrodes. The triboelectric layer was prepared by dissolving P(VDF‐TrFE) (10 wt.%) in N,N‐dimethylformamide, followed by spin‐coating the solution onto the Al‐coated substrate at 1000 rpm for 60 s. The coated films were then annealed at 140°C for 2 h in a dry oven to induce the ferroelectric β‐phase of P(VDF‐TrFE). After cooling, the P(VDF‐TrFE) layer was laminated with the top Al electrode, completing the friction‐pair stack. The effective contact area of the TENG was 3 cm × 3 cm, and the overall device footprint was 5 cm × 5 cm.

TENG Actuation Setup

The triboelectric output performance was evaluated using a custom‐built measurement system. A programmable linear motor (P01‐23×80/60×120‐LC, LinMot) was employed to apply periodic vertical contact‐separation motions to the TENG device with precise control over frequency and displacement. The normal force applied to the device was monitored in real time using an in‐line force gauge (M5IE, Mark‐10) mounted coaxially with the motor shaft. Prior to each measurement session, the force gauge was zeroed and calibrated according to the manufacturer's procedure), and the peak force during each contact event was used as the reported “force.” The electrical output signals were recorded using a digital oscilloscope (EDUX1052A/G, Keysight). The open‐circuit voltage of the TENG (V TENG) was measured using high input impedance, while the output current (I TENG) was determined by measuring the voltage drop across a connected external load resistance.

TENG Stimulation Protocol for SRDP

For SRDP, the actuation force was first calibrated to a target peak force of 1 N by tuning the motor acceleration and verified using the in‐line force gauge. After calibration, the stroke and acceleration profile were kept fixed to ensure identical tapping conditions, and only the f pre was adjusted to set the actuation frequency (5–20 Hz). Unless otherwise noted, the TENG spike waveform used in these experiments had a fixed pulse width (t pulse = 20 ms), while the repetition period (1/f) was varied accordingly.

Materials Characterization

The crystalline phase of P(VDF‐TrFE) was characterized by X‐ray diffraction (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The structural integrity of the graphene layer was analyzed using Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA, Japan).

Device Characterization

The electrical performance of the g‐IGT and TENG was evaluated using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keysight B1500A/B1530A). Standard device metrics were obtained from transfer (V G–I D) and output (V D–I D) characteristics. Synaptic functions were examined with the instrument's waveform generator/high‐speed measurement unit, which applied programmed voltage pulses to the gate while monitoring the resulting drain current in real time. This setup enabled systematic extraction of EPSC/IPSC, PPF/PPD, LTP/LTD, and SRDP characteristics.

HAR Training Dataset

The UCI HAR dataset was employed for system‐level validation. It contains triaxial accelerometer and gyroscope signals recorded from 30 participants performing six daily activities, with a Samsung Galaxy S2 smartphone mounted at the waist. The dataset is publicly available through the UCI Machine Learning Repository.
