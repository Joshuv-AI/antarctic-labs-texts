Rapid detection and quantification of sweet potato storage roots using ground penetrating radar
Authors: Mika Tei, Jiaqi Liu, Christopher Gomez, Takayoshi Ishii, Yusaku Uga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sweet potato is a nutritionally valuable crop that contributes to food security, owing to its storage roots rich in starch, sugars, and antioxidants, while requiring minimal cultivation inputs. Estimating its yield based on visible above-ground traits remains challenging due to weak and inconsistent correlations between shoot biomass and storage root development. Therefore, direct assessment of underground biomass is essential. In this study, we demonstrate the field application of ground penetrating radar (GPR) for non-destructive detection and yield estimation of sweet potato. GPR is a geophysical technique that typically transmits ultra high frequency radio waves into the soil and records reflections from subsurface objects. Electromagnetic wave simulations within the soil–root system revealed GPR signals that strongly correlate with root length, forming the basis for yield quantification. We developed an image-processing pipeline comprising static correction, gain adjustment, noise filtering, and hyperbola segmentation via the Hough transform to enable semi-automated storage root detection from GPR data. By integrating detection and quantification approaches, a linear regression model predicting sweet potato yield from GPR signals achieved moderate accuracy (R2 = 0.567, normalized RMSE 0.190). We established a non-destructive and low-labor approach for monitoring root systems, providing a foundation for rapid, scalable, and field-ready yield estimation in sweet potato and other root and tuber crops.

Introduction

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas Lam) is one of the most important root and tuber crops worldwide. Its resilience to drought and poor soil conditions, together with its high yield potential and harvest index, enables stable food production on marginal lands [1]. The superior biomass productivity of sweet potato makes it valuable for ensuring food security on Earth and beyond [2,3]. Accordingly, sweet potato has been selected as a candidate crop for Environmental Life Support Systems (ELSS) intended for cultivation in space, including on the lunar and Martian surfaces [4]. Although traditionally regarded as a staple food, sweet potato has also gained attention as a “superfood” due to its high content of dietary fiber and antioxidants, making it an increasingly important target for breeding and crop improvement [5].

Plant root systems play a fundamental role in crop productivity, serving as the harvested portion in root and tuber crops and contributing indirectly to all crops through nutrient acquisition, water uptake, anchorage, and symbiotic interactions with rhizosphere microbiota. Despite their critical importance, investigating root systems remains a major challenge due to the opaque, dense, and heterogeneous nature of soil, which severely limits visibility and accessibility.

Traditional excavation-based approaches, involving soil coring and root washing [[6], [7], [8], [9]], have long been the cornerstone of root research. While these methods allow direct visualization, they are labor-intensive, destructive, and inherently ex situ, restricting throughput and precluding dynamic observation of root development.

To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed a range of non-invasive imaging techniques. Transparent growth containers–such as rhizotrons [10], rhizotubes [11], rhizoboxes [12,13], and EcoFABs [14]–allow direct observation of superficial roots but are limited in field-of-view and lack three-dimensionality. Advanced tomographic approaches, including X-ray computed tomography (CT) [15,16] and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [17,18], provide high-resolution visualization of intact root systems within soil. Emerging technologies such as electrical impedance tomography [19] and fiber optic sensors [20] further enhance dynamic tracking with high temporal resolution. Despite these advances, most non-invasive applications remain largely confined to indoor environments.

Proximal sensing technologies offer promising avenues for large-scale, in situ assessment of plant root systems. Conventional approaches, such as spectral imaging, have been successfully applied to predict commercial yield in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) based on canopy traits [21]; however, similar approaches have proven unreliable for sweet potato [22,23], likely due to weak correlations between above-ground biomass and storage root development [24]. This highlights a critical need for direct, non-invasive sensing of root traits in sweet potato. Several proximal sensing techniques have been explored for subsurface characterization, including electrical resistivity tomography [25,26], acoustic imaging [27,28], and electromagnetic induction [29]. However, these methods rely on conductive, moisture-rich soils as the sensing medium [[30], [31], [32]]. In agricultural environments where irrigation is limited and soils are often dry and weakly conductive, ground-penetrating radar (GPR) is particularly well suited for below-ground sensing applications.

GPR employs electromagnetic waves–typically in the range of 100 MHz to 2 GHz– to penetrate soil and sense subsurface structures based on variations in wave propagation velocity. The velocity V is governed by the relative electric permittivity (ϵr) and the magnetic permeability (μ) of the medium:(1)V=cμrϵr,where c is the speed of light in a vacuum. At an interface between dissimilar materials, part of the incident wave is reflected while the remainder is transmitted. Under normal incidence, the reflection coefficient R depends on the electromagnetic impedance Z1, Z2 of the two materials:(2)R=Z2−Z1Z2+Z1≈μ2ϵ2−μ1ϵ1μ2ϵ2+μ1ϵ1.Because plant tissues generally contain more moisture and thus exhibit higher permittivity than surrounding soil, their location and physical properties can be inferred from reflected wave patterns [33]. Historically, GPR applications have focused on tree root systems [[34], [35], [36], [37]], but recent studies have extended its use to crop roots, including cassava [[38], [39], [40]], wheat [41], peanuts [42], sorghum [43], maize [44], and potatoes [45,46]. Nevertheless, detecting finer and softer crop roots remains more challenging than identifying woody tree roots.

In this study, we evaluated the potential of GPR as a non-invasive tool for detecting and estimating sweet potato yield. To establish a quantitative basis for root biomass estimation, we first simulated electromagnetic wave propagation within a soil–root system and identified signal features that correlate with storage root size. These findings were validated through experiments conducted in both controlled and natural field environments. In parallel, we developed an image-processing pipeline incorporating the Hough transform, enabling automated detection and localization of storage roots within GPR data. By integrating signal analysis with image-based detection, the proposed framework facilitates scalable, non-destructive estimation of storage root biomass in sweet potatoes cultivated under field conditions.

Plant materials and site description

Field experiments on sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L., cultivar Beniazuma) were conducted using 20 biological replicates. Sweet potato cuttings were planted vertically on flat ground by burying the three basal nodes at approximately 1 m intervals and cultivated at the Arid Land Research Center (ALRC), Tottori University, from July 10 to October 28, 2024. Beniazuma, registered by the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO) in 1984, is known for its sweet, powdery flavor and accounts for approximately 10% of Japan's total sweet potato production. This cultivar is adapted for late planting with an optimal harvesting cycle of 100 to 120 days. Basal fertilizer was applied at rates of 15.6 g N m−2, 19.2 g P m−2 and 19.2 g P m−2, 14 days before and one month after planting. Irrigation was provided using a low-pressure rotating sprinkler system installed at a height of 1 m, with a sprinkler spacing of 3.3 m and a wetted radius of 1.6 m. Water was applied at a flow rate of 43 L min −1 for 20 min per irrigation event, conducted two to three times per week. To prevent adventitious rooting, sweet potato vines were lifted at 30, 47, 64, 85, and 110 days after planting.

The ALRC is located at 35.53°N, 134.21°E, adjacent to the Tottori Sand Dune (Fig. 1a). The soil consists of 96% sand, 1% silt and 3% clay [51]. Average volumetric moisture content was 6.1% near the ground surface (depth ≤0.1 m) and 7.7% at 0.3-0.4 m depth. To minimize soil moisture interference, a rain-out shelter was used to dry the field for at least 24 h prior to each measurement. Validation using artificially buried storage roots

Under the rain-out shelter at the ALRC, soil was excavated to a depth of approximately 0.2 m. Spindle-shaped sweet potato storage roots, spanning a range of sizes from multiple cultivars, were individually buried at 0.5 m intervals along three parallel rows for subsequent GPR-based mensuration of storage root size and weight (Fig. 2). The spindle-shaped morphology is representative of the majority of sweet potato cultivars cultivated in Japan, accounting for more than 80% of total national production [52]. Similar spindle-shaped storage roots are also commonly observed in sweet potato varieties cultivated in other major producing countries, including China and the United States [53,54]. Each storage root was placed horizontally, with its longest axis aligned along the scan trajectory. Six sensors were installed in the central row at depths of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 m to monitor soil moisture. GPR surveys were conducted using a MALÅ ProEx system (Guideline Geo, SC, USA) with a shielded 800 MHz antenna. 800 MHz was selected because preliminary experiments demonstrated that it produced sufficiently strong reflection signals to reliably detect the smallest marketable sweet potato storage roots.

The A-scan in GPR represents a time-series measurement of the electric field, capturing the amplitude, phase, and arrival time of reflected electromagnetic waves. Each A-scan was recorded using a 20 ns time window. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, 16-stacking was applied, whereby the GPR system acquires 16 consecutive traces at the same location and averages them to produce a single A-scan.

The B-scan, constructed from sequential A-scans, is visualized as an image where the horizontal axis corresponds to the transmitter trigger index, and the vertical axis represents the receiver's recording time. For each sample, three B-scans were acquired above the target location with approximately 100 mm lateral spacing. The trigger index corresponding to the 0.01 m interval was set by the odometer wheel attached to the GPR antennas. The scan showing the strongest hyperbola response was selected as the representative measurement for subsequent analysis.

B-scans were processed using Reflexw software (Sandmeier Geophysical Research, Karlsruhe, Germany) following (1) DEWOW filtering to suppress high- and low-frequency noise; (2) static correction to align time zero with the ground surface; (3) background removal to eliminate horizontal banding and enhance hyperbolic reflections; and (4) conversion of travel time to depth.

Field GPR surveys for sweet potato cultivation

In situ measurements were conducted using a PulseEkko system equipped with 1000 MHz antennas (Sensor & Software Inc., ON, Canada). The higher transmitter frequency, compared to the 800 MHz system used in the individual storage root experiments, was selected to enhance sensitivity to the growth of storage roots in developing plants with unknown and potentially small sizes. The transmitter and receiver antennas were mounted on a skid plate with a separation of 0.15 m and operated in reflection mode with broadside polarization. Each A-scan recorded the time-resolved electric field at the receiver, including a short pre-event window and a 20 ns acquisition window, with 16-stacking. B-scans were collected at 0.005 m intervals using an integrated odometer.

Image-processing and hough transform

Automated image-processing pipeline was implemented using a custom MATLAB script (MathWorks, MA, USA). For static correction, the ground surface was identified by the arrival time of the maximum amplitude wavelet in each A-scan. The data above this point were subsequently removed to confer time zero. To compensate for signal attenuation with depth (d), a sigmoid gain function was applied:G(d)=a⋅dndn+Kn+b,where a, b, K, and n are empirical parameters. This function enhances deeper signals while preserving near-surface features. High-pass filters were applied to emphasize reflection signals, with cutoff frequencies of f ≥ (3 ns)−1 in the time-frequency domain and k ≥ (0.5 m)−1 in the spatial frequency domain. Subsequently, low-pass filters with cutoff frequencies of f ≤ (0.2 ns)−1 and k ≤ (0.02 m)−1 were used to suppress instrumental noise and motion-induced artifacts, effectively implementing band-pass filtering for background removal. A Hough transform was implemented in Python to locate the reflection sources using the hyperbolic model:y=a⋅(x−w)2with a = −0.01, w = 50, and a detection threshold of 0.2.

The image-processing scripts are publicly available at https://github.com/mtei1/GPRScript.

Extraction of apex pixel intensity in a B-scan

In a GPR B-scan, the apex of a hyperbola typically indicates the location of a subsurface object [55,56], while its curvature is influenced by the object's size, burial depth, and the wave propagation velocity of the surrounding medium. In this study, we used a simplified approach was adopted in which a fixed hyperbolic morphology was used to identify subsurface objects without analyzing curvature parameters. Instead, the pixel intensity at the hyperbola apex–representing the peak amplitude of the reflected wave–was extracted and used as a proxy for estimating sweet potato storage root yield.

When multiple storage roots develop in close proximity, interference among reflected waves may occur, along with appearance of a clustered hyperbolic signatures. Based on the B-scan overlaid with the segmentation output, an apex pixel with a relatively strong intensity within the cluster of hyperbolae was manually selected as a representative measurement for the entire plant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB (MathWorks, MA, USA). The relationship between GPR-derived peak reflection amplitude and storage root traits for individual storage roots was evaluated using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Linear regression models were fitted using the MATLAB function polyfit, and the goodness of fit was assessed by the coefficient of determination (R2). Confidence intervals for the fitted regression lines were calculated using the MATLAB function polyconf. The root-mean-square-error (RMSE) was normalized by the range of the observed sample values. All analyses were conducted using default MATLAB settings unless otherwise specified.

Results

GPR simulation with variable root length

To estimate sweet potato biomass from GPR signals, we simulated the electric field response for storage roots of varying size positioned directly beneath the midpoint between the transmitter and receiver. A simplified two-dimensional model was employed, where the horizontal and vertical dimensions of a rectangular cuboid were systematically varied (Fig. 3a and 4a). While biomass is generally defined as the product of storage root volume and density, this model considers only size and excludes density. Although density could theoretically influence permittivity and permeability, both soil and sweet potato are typically non-magnetic (μsoil = μroot = 0). Furthermore, intra-species variation in root permittivity is negligible compared to the pronounced contrast between soil and sweet potato root (ϵsoil ≈ 5 and ϵroot ∼ 55). In the simulation, the electromagnetic pulse emitted by the transmitter propagates through the soil and reflects at the storage root surface. The reflection from the storage root's upper surface (the “primary reflection”) undergoes a 180° phase-shift, whereas the reflection from the lower surface (the “secondary reflection”) does not. This phase behavior is governed by the relative wave velocities of the interfacing media: when the wave transitions from soil to root (ϵsoil < ϵroot), it experiences a 180° phase reversal; conversely, when traveling from root back to soil (ϵroot > ϵsoil), the wave retains its original phase.

The amplitude of reflected waves varied with changes in the root's horizontal dimension (Fig. 3). The primary reflection increased proportionally with length up to 130 mm, beyond which it began to saturate, reaching a maximum near 200 mm (Fig. 3g). This saturation effect can be explained by diffraction behavior when the object size is comparable to the wavelength:λ=cfϵsoil≈134mm for f=1000MHz and ϵsoil=5.We hypothesize that when the root length is below this threshold, a substantial portion of the wavefront diffracts around the root, reducing the reflected signal. Conversely, when the root length exceeds the wavelength, the incident wave is predominantly reflected or refracted, with minimal diffraction.

The secondary reflection also increased proportionally with root length up to about 200 mm (Fig. S1). However, instead of saturating, its amplitude subsequently decreased, reaching a minimum near 300 mm. This nonmonotonic relationship likely arises from two competing mechanisms. On one hand, increasing root length enhances the secondary reflection from the lower surface, similar to the primary reflection. On the other hand, larger storage roots introduce greater internal reflections and electromagnetic absorption, particularly at the upper surface, which attenuates the wave reaching the receiver.

The vertical dimension of the storage root strongly influences the timing of the secondary reflection but has only a marginal effect on peak amplitude (Fig. 4). The time delay of the secondary reflection increases proportionally with root length, whereas the amplitude remains nearly constant with slight attenuation as length increases (Fig. 4g–S2). The slope of the regression line corresponds to half the propagation velocity of the electromagnetic wave within the sweet potato storage root, given by:v2=c2μrootϵroot≈20 mm⋅ns−1,accounting for the two-way travel time of the wave traversing the root length before reflecting from the lower surface.

Artificially buried storage roots

An experimental setup mirroring the two-dimensional simulation model was established using individual storage roots with a range of sizes (Fig. 2). Sweet potato storage roots were manually buried in ALRC soil, and GPR scans were conducted directly above each storage root to evaluate size- and weight-related signal characteristics. Storage root lengths ranged from 90 to 310 mm, with corresponding weights between 70 and 640 g. Fig. 5 shows the relationship between the GPR-derived peak reflection amplitude and both storage root length and weight. Overall, peak reflection amplitude increased with storage root size, exhibiting a particularly strong dependence on storage root length. Statistically significant correlations were observed between peak amplitude and storage root length (r = 0.86, P < 0.01, RMSE 26.45) and between peak amplitude and root weight (r = 0.48, P < 0.05, RMSE 138.38). The stronger correlation with root length likely occurs because reflection amplitude is more directly influenced by the horizontal extent of the object, as shown in the simulation results. Although storage root weight can be inferred primarily from length, it also depends on density and overall volume, introducing variability that weakens the correlation in a simple linear regression model based solely on reflection amplitude.

The saturation behavior observed in the in silico experiments for storage root lengths exceeding 200 mm was not conclusively confirmed in the controlled burial experiments. Although the peak reflection amplitude exhibited an apparent saturation between 280 and 310 mm, this range lies beyond the saturation domain predicted by the simulations. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in the effective pulse frequency between the simulated and experimental systems. In practice, the empirical center frequency of the GPR systems used in the burial experiments was approximately 500 MHz rather than the nominal 1000 MHz assumed in simulations, corresponding to a longer effective wavelength. This likely extended the linear relationship between reflection amplitude and storage root length, thereby shifting the onset of signal saturation.

At 0 DAP, no sweet potato roots were present; therefore, the B-scan represents baseline conditions, capturing both instrumental characteristics and the underlying geological features of the ALRC field (Fig. 6a). The first 2 ns has almost no signal, attributed to the pre-event window during which the receiver is activated prior to signal transmission from the transmitter. A strong direct coupling (DC) signal is observed at approximately 2.0–3.5 ns. The DC signal refers to the wave traveling directly from the transmitter to the receiver and overlaps with the surface reflection due to the close proximity of both antennas to the ground surface. The DC waveform resembles a Ricker wavelet with three peaks at an empirical center frequency of approximately 670 MHz. Most of the background subsurface reflections associated with baseline geological features, such as clay-rich clusters, are suppressed by the Hough transform-based segmentation. However, a few strong reflections, particularly beyond 6 ns (approximately 0.40 m depth), remain evident in both the baseline and subsequent scans.

The chronological sequence of B-scans reveals a progressive increase in subsurface reflections between 4 and 6 ns, corresponding to the active zone of storage root development located approximately 0.15 m below the ground surface. Notably, at 64 DAP, rapid root proliferation is observed, as indicated by a marked increase in hyperbola detections following Hough-based segmentation (Fig. 6g). Interestingly, a rapid increase in vine growth was observed prior to the root proliferation, occurring between 30 and 47 DAP based on aboveground inspection (Fig. S3).

Yield estimation from pixel intensity

At 110 DAP, sweet potato roots at ALRC were excavated following acquisition of the final GPR B-scan. Root size varied substantially across replicates, indicating that storage root formation was strongly influenced by environmental conditions. Some plants developed predominantly fibrous and pencil-like roots, with few or no marketable storage roots (Sample # 9, 14, 18, and 19). Among plants that produced storage roots, all exhibited a spindle-shaped morphology, with individual root weights ranging from 70 to 460 g. The number of storage roots per plant ranged from 0 to 8, with a mean of 3.5 and a standard deviation of 2.28. Total fresh root weight per plant, including fibrous and pencil roots, ranged from 20 to 1470 g, with a mean of 761 g and a standard deviation of 438 g.

In a GPR B-scan, the observed clusters of subsurface reflections can be attributed to storage roots originating from the same plant (Fig. 6f). A representative pixel from each cluster was selected as a proxy measurement for an individual plant.

A linear regression model was then used to convert apex intensity into storage root weight, resulting in R2 = 0.567 with RMSE and normalized RMSE of 266.03 and 0.190, respectively (Fig. 7). Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of using GPR for non-invasive detection and yield estimation of sweet potatoes under field conditions. By validating a proxy for storage root size across progressively complex environments–including in silico simulations, controlled burial experiments, and field surveys of cultivated plants–we establish a robust framework for underground biomass quantification.

Simulation results revealed that GPR reflection amplitude and timing are strongly influenced by storage root dimensions, particularly the horizontal and vertical length. The primary reflection amplitude increases with horizontal root length until reaching a saturation point, consistent with the diffraction limit of the radar system. For L-band electromagnetic waves, the wavelength may exceed the typical length of sweet potatoes. In such cases, yield estimation based on reflection amplitude is more effective than relying on reflected imagery, which is inherently constrained by lower spatial resolution. Secondary reflections, influenced by vertical root length, provide additional information on subsurface geometry.

The controlled burial experiments corroborated these findings, showing statistically significant correlations between peak reflection amplitude and both root length and weight. While storage root weight is agronomically important, its dependence on both volume and density introduces variability that weakens its linear relationships with reflection amplitude. Nevertheless, the observed correlations support the use of GPR as a proxy for estimating root biomass.

An automated image-processing pipeline for field surveys (Fig. S4) enables efficient B-scan analysis, facilitating subsurface structure identification and monitoring of root development over time. The observed increase in reflection complexity and hyperbola detections is consistent with expected sweet potato growth patterns. The in-field model performed better for estimating cultivated sweet potato yield than for individual storage root weight. This is likely due to the more homogeneous density and moisture content in live plants compared to post-harvest storage roots. However, a key challenge remains with asymmetric root development: roots extending beyond the antenna's coverage may be missed or attenuated, leading to yield underestimation (Fig. 7, S5). Implementing grid-based survey designs, rather than line scans, could improve spatial coverage and detection accuracy.

A linear regression model based on a single hyperbola apex value extracted from GPR scans was used to estimate sweet potato yield. This simplified approach does not fully capture important phenotypic traits such as storage root number, spatial arrangement, or size distribution. Although the image-processing pipeline may partially detect these features through hyperbola segmentation, the assignment of individual hyperbolas to specific storage roots was not established in the scope of this study. Future work should integrate multi-dimensional, image-driven approaches to better capture root system architecture across cultivars with diverse phenotypes.

Other groups have explored non-invasive sweet potato storage root detection using acoustic sensing [31]. However, such methods perform effectively only in the water-saturated soils, which differ from typical agricultural conditions. In drier soils, more representative of sweet potato cultivation, acoustic sensing shows large error margins and weak correlations with root size. To the best of our knowledge, this study presents the first successful demonstration of non-invasive sweet potato yield estimation in its native agricultural setting. The proposed model achieved moderate to strong accuracy, with correlation coefficients of r = 0.86 for individual storage root length, r = 0.48 for individual storage root weight, and R2 = 0.567(r = 0.75) for estimating total root fresh weight for the cultivated sweet potatoes.

These results are comparable to or exceeded those reported in previous GPR-based studies across various crops, including cassava (R2 = 0.51-0.77) [[38], [39], [40]], wheat (R2 = 0.0-0.54) [41], peanut (R2 = 0.39-0.51) [42], sorghum [43], maize (R2 = 0.02-0.9) [44], and potato (R2 = 0.64) [45,46]. Unlike most prior studies, which rely on frequency-domain features, the present approach uses peak reflection amplitude in time domain. While modern commercial GPR systems often employ frequency-modulated waveforms, such as stepped-frequency continuous wave (SFCW) or linear frequency-modulated signals, this study utilizes a pulse-based system.

An advantage of the proposed approach is that peak reflection amplitude is relatively robust to lateral discontinuities caused by uneven terrain and imperfect antenna movement during data acquisition. However, a key limitation is its sensitivity to depth-dependent attenuation and soil dielectric properties, particularly moisture content. These factors can vary spatially and temporally, potentially limiting model transferability across sites.

Furthermore, interference among reflected waves–originating from the upper and lower boundaries of storage roots or from multiple roots in close proximity–can lead to constructive or destructive signal interactions. The current model does not explicitly account for these effects or for variations in root morphology. While manual selection of a strong reflection within clusters of hyperbolas may partially capture these interactions, the process is inherently subjective, inconsistent, and not scalable. These limitations, together with the constraints associated with line-scans and linear modeling, underscore the need for more advanced approaches. Incorporating frequency-domain features and automated, data-driven analysis could improve robustness and enhance model transferability across diverse field conditions.

Compared to traditional excavation-based methods, GPR offers a scalable, non-destructive alternative for root phenotyping. While previous studies focused on woody root systems, our work extends applicability to softer roots like sweet potatoes. Successful detection and quantification in sandy soil highlight GPR's potential for broader agricultural use, especially in low-conductivity soil environments.

In conclusion, this study establishes GPR as a promising tool for monitoring and quantifying sweet potato storage root development under field conditions. By enabling rapid, high-throughput assessment of underground root traits, GPR has the potential to advance precision agriculture, support data-driven crop management, and accelerate breeding programs focused on root traits. Further improvements in signal processing algorithms, along with the integration of higher-dimensional spatial and frequency-domain analyses, are expected to enhance accuracy, robustness, and scalability. These advancements may ultimately unlock the broader potential of automated root phenotyping across diverse crops and soil environments.
