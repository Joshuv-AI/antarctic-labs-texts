Violations of the breast milk substitutes act-2013 and its rules − 2017 in Bangladesh
Authors: S. K. Roy, Khurshid Jahan, Saria Tasnim, Soofia Khatoon, Nurul Alam, Tofail Md. Alamgir Azad, Shahidul Islam, Khadijatul Cubra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInadequate breastfeeding practices increase the risk of mortality and morbidity among infants and young children. Recent global estimates suggest that inadequate breastfeeding is responsible for approximately 16% of child deaths worldwide each year, highlighting the ongoing need to strengthen support systems to improve infant and young child feeding practice in Bangladesh, 55% of infants under six months of age are exclusively breastfed. To improve breastfeeding rates and reduce infant mortality, the Government of Bangladesh enacted the Breast-milk Substitute (BMS) Act, 2013, and its Rules in 2017 to regulate the marketing of breast-milk substitutes.ObjectivesThe study aimed to assess violations of the BMS Act, 2013, and Rules, 2017, focusing on the types, actors, and settings of such violations, while also raising awareness of the Act among key stakeholders and the public.MethodWe carried out a cross-sectional survey among 818 retail outlets and pharmacies; 969 baby-friendly hospitals; 969 doctors, nurses, and other health professionals; and 315 mothers with children aged 0–24 months across eight divisions of Bangladesh from August 2016 to December 2019. Data were collected through structured interviews, direct observations, and document reviews using a modified International Baby Food Action Network–International Code Documentation Centre (IBFAN–ICDC) protocol and the BFHI Hospital External Assessment Tool.ResultsAll retail outlets surveyed (n = 818) sold unregistered BMS products, and all displayed promotional materials. These retail outlets also sold BMS products labeled with health and nutrition claims. In hospitals, 25.5% of doctors (n = 247) and 21.3% of nurses and other health professionals (n = 206) promoted BMS without medical indication. Additionally, 32.0% of health facilities (n = 300) reported providing financial incentives to health professionals to participate in events sponsored by BMS manufacturers. Furthermore, BMS manufacturers violated the BMS Act, 2013 by establishing breastfeeding corners in 164 hospitals (17.5%).ConclusionThis study identified extensive violations of the BMS Act 2013 and its Rules 2017 in Bangladesh, revealing unethical promotion and distribution of BMS. Health professionals in BFHI-trained hospitals played a significant role in these breaches, contributing to the erosion of breastfeeding practices. Urgent monitoring and enforcement are needed to protect infant health and uphold national regulations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01382-y.

Introduction

The contribution of breastfeeding to child survival, health, and development, as well as to maternal health and human capital development, is well documented. Inadequate structural and societal support for recommended breastfeeding practices contributes significantly to child mortality and morbidity worldwide. Recent global estimates suggest that inadequate breastfeeding is responsible for approximately 16% of child deaths each year, highlighting the ongoing need to strengthen support systems to improve infant and young child feeding practices [1]. According to the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey, the exclusive breastfeeding rate in Bangladesh was 65% in 2017–18, but declined to 55% in 2022 [2].

The International Code of Marketing of Breast Milk Substitute (BMS) was adopted by the World Health Assembly (WHA) in 1981 in response to rising infant mortality associated with the promotion of BMS product. Since then, the WHA has endorsed several subsequent resolutions to clarify and further strengthen the Code. Collectively, the International Code and subsequent WHA resolutions are referred to as the Code [3].

As of March 2024, 146 (75%) of the 194 World Health Organization (WHO) Member States have adopted legal measures to implement at least some provisions of the Code, covering 91% of global annual births. Among these countries, 33 are substantially aligned with the Code, providing protection against unethical marketing practices for 44% of global births annually. A further 40 countries are moderately aligned, 73 have adopted some provisions, and 48 countries have no legal measures in place [4] (The Status of the Code Report 2024).

In Bangladesh, the Government initially promulgated an Ordinance in 1984, which was later strengthened and updated through the enactment of the BMS Act, 2013, followed by the BMS Rules, 2017 [5–8]. These legal advancements were achieved through the combined efforts of the Bangladesh Breastfeeding Foundation, civil society organizations, and United Nations agencies working in Bangladesh to align national legislation with the Code [3, 9].

The study aimed to assess violations of the BMS Act 2013 and Rules 2017, focusing on the types, actors, and settings of violations, while also raising awareness about the Act among stakeholders and the public.

Methods

This study used a cross-sectional design to detect violations of the BMS Act, 2013, and its Rules, 2017. The study was conducted between August 2016 and December 2019 across Bangladesh.

The survey was carried out in 11 City Corporations within the eight divisions of Bangladesh and included urban municipalities “Pourashavas” from all 64 districts, 343 administrative division (upazila) headquarters, and Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI)–trained health facilities. Monitoring activities covered both market settings (City Corporations and municipalities) and BFHI-trained health facilities.

A total of 969 health facilities were randomly selected from 1,095 BFHI-trained hospitals nationwide. These facilities had previously received a four-day BFHI training and a two-day refresher training, which included detailed sessions on the BMS Act, 2013, and its Rules, 2017 [5–8]. Hospital authorities and healthcare providers had already been sensitized to the provisions of the BMS Act and its Rules through these trainings.

Across the eight divisions, the monitored health facilities included 26 government maternal and child health (MCH) facilities, 52 medical college hospitals, 67 district (sadar) hospitals, 343 Upazila (administrative division) Health Complexes, 53 Mother and Child Welfare Centres, five tertiary government hospitals, 422 private hospitals, and one private chamber that had received BFHI training. The study population included BFHI-accredited health facilities, retail outlets and pharmacies, mothers of children aged less than 24 months, and healthcare providers working in both public and private BFHI-trained facilities throughout Bangladesh.

Two Divisional Officers (DOs) from the Bangladesh Breastfeeding Foundation (BBF) were assigned to each of the eight divisions. Monitoring activities were conducted three days per week, with two days allocated for BFHI hospital monitoring and one day for market monitoring. The monthly target for market monitoring was 52 sites across the eight divisional cities. Each DO assessed two to three public and private hospitals per week and identified violations of specific clauses of the BMS Act, 2013, and its Rules, 2017 [5–8].

Monitoring followed the methodology developed by the International Baby Food Action Network (IBFAN) and the International Code Documentation Centre (ICDC) for the assessment and monitoring of the International Code of Marketing of BMS [10, 11]. The Code Monitoring Kit (2015 and 2019) includes a manual and nine standardized forms for monitoring compliance with the International Code, relevant World Health Assembly resolutions, and guidance on ending the inappropriate promotion of foods for infants and young children.

A “Quick and Easy” BMS Act violation monitoring form [10 (p.56), 11 (p64), 12] and the BFHI Hospital External Assessment Tool [13] were used for data collection. The questionnaires included 11 questions for mothers, 11 questions for healthcare professionals, and 14 questions for market monitoring. Data were collected through a combination of structured interviews, direct observations, and photographic and video documentation.

Hospital monitoring involved physical visits to health facilities and included interviews with the head of the facility, three doctors, and three nurses, as well as interviews with at least three pregnant women and three postnatal mothers. Observations were conducted on BFHI committees, breastfeeding corners or Lactation Management Centres, and potential violations of the BMS Act, 2013. Photographs, interview notes, and observation findings were documented, and feedback was provided to the heads of health facilities and the BBF Central Office.

During health facility monitoring, specific BMS Act compliance checks included the presence of BMS products within hospital premises; the display of promotional materials such as posters, leaflets, or booklets from BMS companies; the establishment of breastfeeding corners by BMS companies; the presence of BMS company representatives in health facilities; the provision of free samples, gifts, money, or other incentives to mothers; and participation of health professionals in seminars or conferences organized by BMS companies. Corrective actions taken and advice provided by monitoring officers were also documented.

Market monitoring was conducted in City Corporations, Pourashavas, municipalities, divisional cities, district towns, and selected upazila headquarters across all eight divisions of Bangladesh. A total of 818 points of sale were assessed, including supermarkets, pharmacies, retail outlets, bakeries and confectioneries, fruit retail outlets, cosmetic stores, and other shop types selling BMS products.

Observational assessments were conducted at all points of sale and health facilities. Label of all BMS and commercially prepared infant food products within the scope of the BMS Act, 2013, were reviewed in formal markets across the eight divisions. Label compliance was assessed based on the presence of a product registration number, list of ingredients, product composition or analysis, storage conditions, batch number, and expiry or “best before” date, taking into account local climatic and storage conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 20). Appropriate descriptive and inferential statistical tests were applied to examine differences between groups and associations between variables.

Table 2 presents the reasons reported by mothers for providing BMS products to their infants. A substantial proportion of BMS use was attributed to promotion by health professionals 25 (7.4%) and health workers 11 (3.3%). Among 336 mothers, 124 (36.9%) reported feeding BMS products to their children while admitted to BFHI hospitals. Of those, 25 mothers used BMS products based on a doctor’s prescription without medical justification, and 35 mothers (10.4%) used BMS products following advice from doctors provided on unsigned slips of paper rather than formal prescriptions. In addition, 5 (1.5%) of families reported choosing BMS products based on recommendations from shopkeepers. The distribution of reasons for BMS use differed significantly by the child’s age (χ² = 188.736; p < 0.05), underscoring the need for stricter regulation of BMS promotion and age-sensitive interventions.

Promotion in pharmacies was also common, with 687(84%) of cases violating the Act.

Challenges in implementing the BMS Act, 2013, and rules, 2017

A major challenge in implementing the BMS Act in Bangladesh is the presence of contradictory government regulations [14]. The Essential Commodity Control Order, 1981 (Clause 22(1) and 22(2)(i)) issued by the Ministry of Commerce permits the sale, distribution, and promotion of BMS and other baby foods without requiring IPHN registration, thereby undermining the supremacy of the BMS Act, 2013 (Clause 3) (Annexure 1 and 2).

As a result, shop and pharmacy owners can easily obtain and renew licenses from District Commissioner offices, enabling them to legally promote and sell BMS products as food. This regulatory inconsistency allows pharmacies, retailers, distributors, and BMS companies to continue marketing, selling, and distributing BMS products while violating the BMS Act.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to evaluate the extent and nature of violations of the BMS Act, 2013, and its accompanying Rules, 2017 [5–8], which were enacted to protect breastfeeding practices and regulate the marketing of BMS. Specifically, the study assessed the number and types of violations, identified key actors responsible for these violations, and determined the settings in which violations most frequently occurred. The findings provide valuable insights into existing gaps in the implementation and enforcement of the BMS Act. Notably, the study revealed that many mothers used BMS products for their children following advice from health professionals working in BFHI-trained hospitals.

Healthcare professional involvement and institutional non-compliance

A key finding of the study was the high prevalence of BMS promotion by healthcare professionals. Doctors frequently recommended BMS products using informal slips of paper rather than formal prescriptions. According to the BMS Rules, 2017 (Clause 4), BMS products may be prescribed only by registered medical officers under life-saving circumstances [5–8]. Therefore, inappropriate advice or recommendation of BMS products by health professionals constitutes a clear violation of the BMS Act.

Comparable findings have been reported in other countries. Mothers reported receiving advice to use BMS from health professionals at rates of 3% in Laos and 41–47% in Nepal, with an average of 13% across eight countries (Indonesia, Vietnam, Senegal, Cambodia, Nigeria, China, Laos, and Pakistan) [15]. In Cambodia, midwives and nurses were most frequently involved in violations, whereas in Pakistan, doctors were the primary violators. Studies conducted in these eight countries documented the proportion of mothers who recalled receiving at least one recommendation from health professionals to use CMF [15]. Additionally, BMS manufacturers have been shown to promote their products through financial incentives to health professionals [16].

Conflicts of interest among health professionals

The findings confirm that conflicts of interest among health professionals were widespread. In the present study, approximately one-fifth of health professionals in both public and private health facilities were involved in conflicts of interest. According to the WHO NetCode comparative analysis, 24.7% of health professionals in Nigeria (n = 275), 20.0% in Lao PDR (n = 40), and 10.4% in Pakistan (n = 1,202) reported receiving at least one BMS sample[15]. Although the direct distribution of BMS samples to health professionals was not documented in the present study, evidence from India shows that two private hospitals in Bengaluru participated in company-sponsored clinical trials involving infants, raising serious ethical concerns [17].

Violations in registration and labeling

This study found that all BMS products sold in retail outlets were unregistered, indicating that manufacturers, importers, distributors, and retailers violated the BMS Act requirement for mandatory registration prior to marketing. This also suggests inadequate regulatory oversight by the registration authority, as stipulated in Clause 5 of the BMS Act, 2013. Failure to register implies non-compliance with international standards, including requirements related to the Codex Alimentarius Commission, radioactive compound testing, and other safety certifications. Instead, retailers relied solely on local trade licenses issued by District Commissioner offices, which authorize the sale of milk food [14]. This regulatory inconsistency highlights the need for urgent coordination between the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Commerce [14].

Furthermore, a substantial proportion of BMS product labels contained health and nutrition claims, violating Clause 6 of the BMS Act, 2013. Such claims can mislead parents and caregivers. Similar findings have been reported in Mexico, where more than 90% of CMF labels included information or imagery idealizing infant formula use, 30% included promotional images or text, and nearly all labels contained health and nutrition claims. CMF products were available in almost all retail outlets, pharmacies, and convenience stores in Mexico [18].

Aggressive marketing and free sample distribution

BMS manufacturers employ diverse marketing strategies across countries. In Singapore, formula milk manufacturers have engaged in aggressive marketing practices, including sponsorships and payments to private hospitals to distribute products and cultivate premium brand images [19]. Consistent with these findings, the present study documented the distribution of free BMS samples to mothers in hospitals, including BFHI-designated facilities. Free samples are a well-established marketing tool known to undermine breastfeeding.

Studies across eight countries (Bangladesh, China, Mexico, Morocco, Nigeria, South Africa, the United Kingdom, and Vietnam) reported that mothers received free samples of formula milk from health workers inside or outside hospitals. In China and Vietnam, 46% (n = 1050) and 35% (n = 1050) of mothers, respectively, reported having received at least one free BMS sample [16]. Evidence also suggests that increases in CMF sales are associated with declines in breastfeeding rates, underscoring the need to prioritize breastfeeding promotion and public awareness campaigns highlighting the disadvantages of formula milk [17].

Corporate sponsorship of hospital infrastructure

The study identified that a major BMS manufacturer established and decorated approximately one-quarter of breastfeeding corners and nutrition libraries in public and private hospitals, clinics, and private chambers in Bangladesh, often displaying promotional posters. Additionally, BMS companies rented prominent shelf space in grocery stores, pharmacies, and hospital pharmacies, branding these areas as “nutrition corners” to promote BMS products. Globally, similar practices have been reported. By the end of 2014, Nestlé had established 192 crèches or breastfeeding rooms’ worldwide, actions that have been criticized for masking conflicts of interest and undermining breastfeeding [21].

Although Nestlé has implemented workplace policies supporting motherhood such as extended maternity leave, breastfeeding rooms, childcare facilities, and flexible postnatal reintegration programs [22] the company has also been reported to violate WHA Resolution 34.22 (1981) through aggressive marketing practices and free sample distribution, thereby promoting conflicts of interest among health professionals [23].

Incentives and gift-based promotion

BMS manufacturers frequently use gift-based incentives to attract consumers, including discount coupons, loyalty points, and promotional gifts linked to the purchase of baby products such as strollers, nappies, bottles, and toys. Similar practices have been widely documented in China, Mexico, the United Kingdom, and Vietnam. Reports from organizations such as the Access to Nutrition Initiative (ATNI) and the WHO have repeatedly highlighted these promotional strategies [20, 24].

In the present study, approximately half of health professionals in BFHI facilities reported being influenced by BMS manufacturers. Health professionals acknowledged receiving commissions from sales, research funding, promotional gifts, free samples of infant and specialized formula products, and invitations to seminars, conferences, and events, thereby engaging in conflicts of interest [20]. Such ethical deterioration among health professionals is directly detrimental to infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices and child health outcomes. Sponsorship of healthcare professional associations forms part of the broader CMF marketing system, wherein financial support, corporate-backed science, and the medicalization of infant feeding practices are used to influence professional behavior [17].

Call for institutional reform

Despite being bound by professional ethics and national guidelines, BFHI-trained staffs were observed to be active participants in violations of the BMS Act. This reflects not only institutional failure to enforce existing standards but also broader deficiencies in professional accountability. The findings underscore the urgent need for legal action and administrative reform. Without regular monitoring of violations and the application of appropriate legal measures, non-compliance will continue to increase, thereby jeopardizing public health interests and undermining the objectives of the BMS Act, ultimately undermining infants’ and children’s health and rights.

Conclusion

The findings of this study reveal widespread violations of the BMS Act, 2013, and its Rules, 2017, indicating unethical practices in the promotion and distribution of BMS in Bangladesh. The magnitude of these violations is alarming and requires urgent monitoring and corrective action. The involvement of health professionals in BFHI-trained hospitals further exacerbates the problem, undermining breastfeeding practices and misleading parents through false health claims, aggressive marketing, and the distribution of free samples.

Recommendations

To address this situation, immediate and coordinated actions are essential, including strict enforcement of the BMS Act, active surveillance, and legal penalties for offenders. Priority must also be given to strengthening breastfeeding promotion and implementing widespread public awareness campaigns on the disadvantages of formula milk. Educating both health professionals and the general public about the benefits of breastfeeding and the risks associated with formula feeding is critical for protecting infant and young child health.
