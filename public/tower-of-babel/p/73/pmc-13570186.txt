Immune features of graft‐derived mucosal‐associated invariant T cells predict gastrointestinal graft‐versus‐host disease
Authors: Gao Mengge, Cheng Rongqi, Kong Jun, Sun Yuqian, Wang Zhidong, Sun Hui, Chang Yingjun, Huang Xiaojun, Zhao Xiaosu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGastrointestinal acute graft‐versus‐host disease (GI aGVHD) remains a major complication after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT), and early risk identification and intervention are essential for improving outcomes. Mucosal‐associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are mucosa‐enriched unconventional T cells with major histocompatibility complex class I‐related protein 1 (MR1)‐restricted, major histocompatibility complex (MHC)‐independent recognition, suggesting a potentially reduced risk of alloreactivity. Our previous work showed that higher graft MAIT‐cell levels were associated with improved post‐transplant MAIT‐cell reconstitution and a lower incidence of GI aGVHD. Single‐cell ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing (sc‐RNA‐seq) and murine models revealed their functional heterogeneity in immune regulation, tissue repair and chemotaxis—supporting their role as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets. In this prospective study, spectral flow cytometry was used to characterize MAIT‐cell phenotypes in peripheral blood stem cell grafts. higher graft MAIT‐cell abundance was associated with more robust early post‐transplant MAIT‐cell reconstitution and a lower risk of GI aGVHD. A three‐marker predictive panel based on MAIT‐cell functional markers (C‐C chemokine receptor type 2 [CCR2], interleukin‐4 [IL‐4], interleukin‐17A [IL‐17A]) achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.80, increasing to 0.85 after adjustment for clinical covariates. These findings identify graft‐derived MAIT cells as a predictive immune‐associated biomarker for GI aGVHD, enabling pre‐transplant risk stratification and supporting precision prevention strategies. INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal acute graft‐versus‐host disease (GI aGVHD) remains a major clinical challenge in allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) and is a leading cause of non‐relapse mortality. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Despite advances in donor selection and GVHD prophylaxis, approximately 40% of allo‐HSCT recipients still develop GVHD, with GI involvement accounting for nearly 40% of cases. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 GI aGVHD often presents with severe complications, including bleeding, perforation and secondary infections, which can significantly worsen patient outcomes. Early identification and timely intervention are therefore critical. However, GI aGVHD continues to be associated with poor therapeutic responses and high mortality, highlighting the urgent need for reliable early predictive strategies to guide clinical management and improve transplant outcomes.

Currently, prediction of GI aGVHD mainly relies on the measurement of serum biomarkers before or at disease onset. 5 , 6 , 7 However, this approach has several limitations in clinical practice. First, the onset of GI aGVHD is difficult to predict and disease progression can be rapid, making optimal sampling time points challenging. Second, biomarker levels can be influenced by confounding factors such as infections or systemic inflammation, reducing their specificity. Third, commonly used detection methods, including enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assaybased assays, are technically demanding, costly and lack standardized clinical thresholds, resulting in variable predictive performance across studies. These limitations underscore the need to identify novel biomarkers and develop more precise predictive models through translational research.

Mucosal‐associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are an evolutionarily conserved subset of innate‐like T cells with distinct biological characteristics and functions. In humans, MAIT cells comprise 1%–10% of circulating T cells, 1%–10% of intestinal T cells and up to 20%–40% of hepatic T cells. 8 , 9 , 10 These cells are activated via their T‐cell receptor (TCR) upon recognition of microbial‐derived metabolites from the riboflavin (vitamin B2) synthesis pathway 5‐(2‐oxopropylideneamino)‐d‐ribitylaminouracil (5‐OP‐RU) presented by the major histocompatibility complex class I‐related protein 1 (MR1) molecule. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Upon activation, MAIT cells exhibit tissue repair properties by secreting growth factors and epithelial growth factors, playing a critical role in mucosal immunity. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Furthermore, MAIT cells highly express chemokine receptors such as C‐C motif chemokine receptor CCR5, CCR6, C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor CXCR3 and CXCR6, as well as chemokines including C‐C motif chemokine ligand CCL3, CCL4 and CCL20, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 which collectively regulate their migration and localization, thereby contributing to immune modulation in inflammatory and infectious settings.

Due to their MR1‐restricted rather than major histocompatibility complex (MHC)‐dependent antigen recognition, MAIT cells generally do not induce GVHD. 19 , 20 Previous studies have demonstrated that the MAIT‐cell number in donor grafts influences early post‐transplant MAIT‐cell reconstitution in recipients, as well as the abundance of gut‐specific microbiota, and is associated with a lower incidence of GI aGVHD. 21 Further investigations using sc‐ribonucleic acid (RNA)‐seq, in vitro functional assays and humanized mouse transplantation models have revealed a potential protective role of MAIT cells in GI aGVHD. These studies suggest that MAIT cells may mitigate disease through multiple mechanisms, including immune regulation, tissue repair and chemotactic control, highlighting their functional heterogeneity and therapeutic potential. 22

Based on these insights, we propose that graft‐derived MAIT cells contribute to a clinically relevant immune signature associated with the risk of GI aGVHD. In this study, we aim to validate novel MAIT‐cell–associated predictive biomarkers through a prospective clinical trial cohort and integrate donor–recipient clinical characteristics to establish an MAIT‐cell–based GI aGVHD prediction score. This predictive score may provide a practical tool for early risk assessment. Ultimately, we hope that this research will facilitate the translation of mechanistic findings into clinical applications, improve transplant outcomes and advance the clinical potential of MAIT cells in transplantation immunology.

METHODS

Study cohort

From January to November 2024, 165 granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF)–mobilized peripheral blood stem cell (G‐PBSC) grafts were prospectively collected for spectral flow cytometric analysis of MAIT cells and their functional markers. Peripheral blood (PB) samples were additionally obtained from 70 recipients around day 90 post‐transplant for downstream analyses.

Spectral flow cytometry

MAIT cells were defined as cluster of differentiation (CD)3+CD161hiVα7.2+ T cells, with frequency calculated as the proportion of CD161hiVα7.2+ cells among total CD3+ T cells (Figure S1). Conventional T cells (CD3+CD4+CD25+), Tregs cells (CD3+CD4+CD25+CD127−) and γδ T cells (CD3+TCRγ/δ+) were also analysed, with frequencies expressed as percentages of CD3+T cells. Antibodies used are listed in Table S1.

Transplantation protocol

All transplant recipients received busulfan‐cyclophosphamide (Bu‐Cy) myeloablative conditioning regimens. 23 , 24 The G‐CSF (5 μg/kg/day) was administered to donors from day −3 for five consecutive days, and the first apheresis was started on the morning of the fourth day after the start of the G‐CSF administration. 25 , 26 All patients received PB as a graft source.

Diagnosis, management and reclassification of acute GVHD

The diagnosis of GI aGVHD depends on histopathological diagnosis provided by rectal or colonoscopy. aGVHD was diagnosed and graded based on the established criteria. 27 , 28 , 29 The conditioning regimen and GVHD prophylaxis were previously reported in detail. 30 For GVHD prevention, all subjects received ciclosporin (CSA), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and methotrexate (MTX).

Statistical analysis

Patient and transplant characteristics were summarized as counts and percentages for categorical variables, and as medians with ranges for continuous variables. To minimize the influence of outliers, expression levels of MAIT‐cell biomarkers were log10‐transformed prior to analysis.

Risk factors and cumulative incidence of GI aGVHD were analysed using the Fine–Gray method with competing risks, and group comparisons were conducted using Gray's test. Variables with p < 0.05 and clinical relevance were included in multivariable models. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). All analyses were performed with R (v4.2.0) and GraphPad Prism (v9), with p < 0.05 considered significant. Detailed methods are provided in the Supporting Information.

RESULTS

Patients and transplantation characteristics

After excluding samples with >10% missing data, 149G‐PBSC grafts were analysed. Detailed information on the enrolled donors and their corresponding recipients is provided in Table S2. Among them, 111 (74.5%) underwent haploidentical HSCT, and 38 (25.5%) underwent sibling‐identical HSCT. All patients received PB grafts. A total of 46 patients (30.9%) developed aGVHD: 18 (12.1%) with skin‐only, 24 (16.1%) with GI involvement (median onset: 21 days, range 12–90) and 7 (4.7%) with liver involvement (median onset: 36 days, range 18–90) (Table S3).

Early MAIT‐cell reconstitution correlates with graft abundance

T‐cell subset recovery within 100 days post‐transplant showed substantial interpatient variability. Early CD3+ T‐cell reconstitution did not correlate with graft CD3+ levels (Figure 1A). Recipients exhibited decreased CD4+ and increased CD8+ proportions relative to grafts, resulting in an inverted CD4/CD8 ratio (Figure 1A). GVHD‐associated T‐cell subsets were generally reduced post‐transplant, with heterogeneous reconstitution patterns (Figure 1B). Notably, MAIT, CD161+, γδ and conventional T cells showed positive correlations with graft frequencies, with MAIT cells showing the strongest correlation (Figure 1C). In contrast, CD4+, CD8+ and Treg cells showed no significant correlation, suggesting host‐dependent recovery (Figure 1C; Figure S2).

Low graft MAIT‐cell abundance associates with GI aGVHD

Grafts were stratified by post‐transplant aGVHD organ involvement into four groups: No events, GI aGVHD, Skin aGVHD and Liver aGVHD. Total CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell frequencies were similar across groups (Figure S3A). However, MAIT‐cell frequency was significantly lower in GI aGVHD grafts compared with no‐event and skin‐aGVHD grafts (p = 0.02 and p = 0.016, respectively), whereas other T‐cell subsets showed no differences (Figure 2A). MAIT‐cell levels did not correlate with aGVHD grade, indicating a GI‐specific association (Figure S3B).

In the graft and PB at +90 day, the GI aGVHD group consistently showed significantly lower proportions of MAIT cells at both time points (G‐PBSCs: p = 0.005; +90 days: p = 0.016), and minimal reconstitution was observed at day +90 (Figure 2B). CD161+T‐cell frequencies in grafts were also lower, and CD8+T cells were higher in PB at day +90 in the GI aGVHD group. Other subsets showed no significant differences (Figure 2B; Figure S4A). As a control, T‐cell dynamics in patients with or without skin aGVHD were similar, with no significant differences in MAIT‐ or other T‐cell subsets in grafts or day +90 PB (Figure 2C; Figure S4B).

These findings suggested that GI aGVHD markedly affected early MAIT‐ and CD8+ T‐cell reconstitution. The GI aGVHD inflammatory milieu may drive CD8+ T‐cell expansion, whereas reduced MAIT‐cell levels in the graft may hinder MAIT‐cell reconstitution and increase GI aGVHD susceptibility.

Functional MAIT‐cell marker reconstitution and their association with GI aGVHD

Building on our prior work linking MAIT‐cell chemotactic, reparative, tissue‐resident and immunoregulatory functions to GI aGVHD, we examined early post‐transplant reconstitution of functional MAIT subsets. Regarding key chemokine receptors, CXCR3 expression on peripheral MAIT cells was significantly upregulated early after transplantation in patients without GI aGVHD compared to graft levels, whereas no such increase was observed in the GI aGVHD group (Figure 3A). Expression levels of C‐C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) and CCR7 were not significantly different between groups in either grafts or early PB. However, CCR2 expression was consistently higher in both grafts and PB in the No GI aGVHD group, and CCR7 expression in grafts was also higher (Figure 3A). These results indicated that CCR2 and CCR7 could be reconstituted to graft levels early after transplantation.

(A, B) Comparison of frequencies of chemokine receptors and functional markers of MAIT cells in G‐PBSCs and early post‐transplant PB between patients with (n = 10) and without GI aGVHD (n = 60). Statistical significance was determined using one‐way ANOVA and paired two‐tailed t‐tests (A, B). *p < 0.05; ****p < 0.0001.

Notably, CCR5 was markedly upregulated and CXCR6 was significantly downregulated in both groups after transplantation, regardless of GI aGVHD status. These consistent trends imply that CCR5 and CXCR6 reflect intrinsic migratory characteristics of MAIT cells, relatively independent of GI aGVHD development.

For functional markers associated with tissue repair, residency and immune tolerance, early reconstitution in the No GI aGVHD group was characterized by upregulation of CD114 and interleukin‐4 (IL‐4), and downregulation of CD69 and interleukin‐17A (IL‐17A). However, these markers showed no significant changes in the GI aGVHD group (Figure 3B). These findings suggested that the occurrence of GI aGVHD may disrupt the normal reconstitution of these functional molecules, or alternatively, that their early reconstitution status may contribute to the pathogenesis of GI aGVHD.

Collectively, our results revealed distinct early reconstitution patterns of MAIT cells expressing various functional markers and their potential associations with GI aGVHD. The occurrence of GI aGVHD may interfere with the normal reconstitution of CXCR3, CD69, CD114, IL‐17A and IL‐4. CCR2 and CCR7 may serve as potential markers associated with GI aGVHD, while CCR5 and CXCR6 likely represent intrinsic chemotactic features of MAIT cells that are less influenced by GI aGVHD.

MAIT‐cell–based biomarker panel for GI aGVHD

To identify functional MAIT‐cell markers predictive of GI aGVHD, we performed Fine–Gray analyses to evaluate the association between log‐transformed MAIT‐cell markers and the GI aGVHD. All analyses were adjusted for eight pre‐transplant clinical covariates: donor age and sex, recipient age and sex, donor–recipient ABO blood type compatibility, sex matching status (mismatch vs. match), conditioning regimen (BU‐CY+anti‐thymocyte globulin vs. others) and transplant type (sibling vs. haploidentical) (Table S4). In univariate analysis, only recipient age was significantly associated with GI aGVHD (p = 0.023) (Table S4). Given the strong clinical relevance of transplant type, it was included alongside recipient age in subsequent multivariate models.

Among T‐cell subsets and 13 MAIT‐cell–associated markers detected in G‐PBSCs, the frequency of MAIT cells and 11 individual MAIT‐cell markers was significantly associated with GI aGVHD (p < 0.05; Table S4). Following variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis to address multicollinearity (VIF<5; Table S5), 10 markers remained for candidate selection (Figure 4A).

ROC curve analysis was then conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of the panel. The combined three‐marker panel achieved an AUC of 0.80, outperforming MAIT‐ cell frequency alone (Figure 4B). To assess clinical applicability, we evaluated the biomarker panel in multivariate models with and without clinical covariates; incorporation of covariates increased the AUC to 0.85, underscoring its potential for risk stratification (Table 1).

MAIT‐cell–based risk score robustly predicts GI aGVHD

Based on these findings, we constructed a risk score using the MAIT‐cell–based biomarker panel: 1.8 × (−log10CCR2) + 1.4 × (−log10IL‐4) + 1.9 × (−log10IL‐17A) (Figure 5A). Risk scores were significantly higher in patients with GI aGVHD or grade II–IV aGVHD compared with those without (p < 0.0001), with the difference most pronounced for GI aGVHD (Figure 5B,C). Adjustment for clinical covariates further strengthened these associations, whereas no difference was observed for skin aGVHD (Figure 5D). Using the median score (6.8) to stratify patients, those in the high‐risk group had increased incidence of GI aGVHD and grade II–IV aGVHD, an effect amplified when using a cut‐off of 7.2 after covariate adjustment (Figure 6A,B). The panel outperformed single MAIT‐cell frequency, particularly for GI aGVHD, but did not predict skin aGVHD (Figure 6C).

Internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples showed good calibration of the panel score for predicting GI aGVHD at days 30, 60 and 90 post‐transplant (Figure S5A). Incorporation of clinical covariates further improved predictive performance, notably on days 30 and 60, as reflected by reduced mean absolute and 0.9 quantile prediction errors (Figure S5B). In addition, we performed a time‐based internal validation by dividing the cohort into a training set for marker selection (Table S6) and model construction and an independent temporally separated test set for validation. The final model derived from the training cohort demonstrated consistent predictive performance in both the training and test datasets, supporting its robustness and stability (Figure S5C). These results demonstrate that the MAIT‐cell–based biomarker panel, particularly when combined with clinical covariates, enables robust individualized risk prediction for GI aGVHD and provides superior predictive accuracy compared with single MAIT‐cell frequency.

DISCUSSION

GI aGVHD remains a major complication of allo‐HSCT due to its early onset, intense inflammation, overlap with intestinal infections and poor response to first‐line therapies, contributing to inferior outcomes. Here, we developed a predictive model based on donor MAIT‐cell profiles in G‐PBSCs, integrating IL‐4, IL‐17A and CCR2, enabling early identification of high‐risk recipients and pre‐transplant risk stratification.

We observed that higher frequencies of IL‐17A+, IL‐4+ and CCR2+MAIT cells in G‐PBSCs correlated with a reduced risk of GI aGVHD, suggesting functional heterogeneity within the MAIT‐cell population. Specific subsets may participate in regulating intestinal immune homeostasis through both immunoregulatory and tissue repair mechanisms, thereby influencing disease progression. Distinct from other T‐cell subsets, MAIT cells are enriched for a wide range of chemokine receptors, particularly CCR1, CCR2, CCR5, CCR6 and CXCR6. Among them, CXCR6 is a well‐established mediator of MAIT‐cell homing. 17 , 22 , 31 Our prior sc‐RNA‐seq analysis revealed that CXCR6 is the most abundantly expressed chemokine receptor on MAIT cells in G‐PBSCs, with significantly higher expression in G‐PBSCs infused into recipients who did not develop GI aGVHD. Furthermore, humanized mouse transplant models confirmed that CXCR6 mediates MAIT‐cell migration to the gut and may play a protective role against GI aGVHD. 22 In this study, the frequency of CXCR6+MAIT cells in early post‐transplant PB was consistently low across recipients, regardless of whether they developed GI aGVHD, suggesting rapid CXCR6‐mediated tissue homing. However, CXCR6 expression in G‐PBSCs and PB at 90 days post‐transplantation, as measured by flow cytometry, showed no significant correlation with GI aGVHD.

These findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between gene transcription, protein expression and active cellular migration. One possible explanation is that elevated transcriptional levels of CXCR6 may indicate a pre‐programmed tissue‐homing profile rather than real‐time migratory activity. Under steady‐state conditions, messenger ribonucleic acid expression does not necessarily translate into immediate or functional protein production, which may explain the discrepancy between transcriptional and protein‐level CXCR6 expression in relation to GI aGVHD. This view is supported by prior studies showing that MAIT cells acquire a tissue‐resident phenotype, including high expression of CCR2, CCR8 and CXCR6, during thymic development. 32 , 33 , 34 Animal models further underscore the complexity of CXCR6's role. In a pulmonary Francisella tularensis francisella tularensis live vaccine strain infection model, CXCR6 was dispensable for initial recruitment of MAIT cells to the lungs but essential for their long‐term retention in the airway lumen. 31 In contrast, in a full‐thickness skin excision model, CXCR6 was required for MAIT‐cell migration to injured skin, 17 although it remains unclear whether this reflects an intrinsic migratory ability or inflammation‐induced recruitment. Taken together, these data suggest that CXCR6 may reflect a general tissue‐residency programme rather than serve as a dynamic predictor of GI aGVHD risk, potentially accounting for its limited predictive value in our flow cytometry‐based dataset.

CCR2 appears to play a more direct role in inflammation‐driven peripheral trafficking of MAIT cells. As a key mediator of transendothelial migration, CCR2+MAIT cells exhibit superior extravasation capacity and are considered ‘first responder’ cells among memory T‐cell subsets. 35 , 36 , 37 Under inflammatory conditions, CCR2 enables efficient rolling, adhesion and transmigration through activated endothelium. In a patient‐derived appendix organoid (PDAO) model, CCR1, CCR2 and CCR4 collectively guided MAIT‐cell recruitment to inflamed tissue, 38 further supporting the central role of CCR2 in inflammation‐driven migration. Additionally, CCR5 may act synergistically with CCR2, while CCR5 blockade does not reduce overall MAIT‐cell migration, it delays migration onset and compromises efficiency. 35 In our study, CCR2 expression on MAIT cells rapidly recovered to graft levels in early post‐transplant blood and was included in the predictive panel, suggesting its early activation in response to the transplant‐associated inflammatory milieu. This may facilitate gut homing and contribute to local immune regulation and tissue repair, potentially mitigating GI aGVHD.

IL‐4 and IL‐17A expression reflect the immunosuppressive and tissue‐repairing functions of MAIT cells respectively. IL‐4 is a key regulator of immune tolerance, promoting Th2 polarization, inhibiting Th1 differentiation and inducing immunoregulatory features in MAIT cells. 22 , 39 , 40 Consistent with these functions, our previous work confirmed that IL‐4 is critical for MAIT‐mediated immune suppression. 22 IL‐17A is essential for epithelial repair. In type 1 diabetes models, reduced expression of IL‐17 and IL‐22 compromises mucosal barrier integrity and increases gut permeability. 41 , 42 , 43 In murine models, residual MAIT cells help maintain intestinal barrier function via IL‐17 secretion, alleviating GI aGVHD. 44

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although bootstrap and time‐based internal validation supported the robustness of the model, the limited number of GI aGVHD events may still introduce overfitting risk, and independent multicentre validation is required to confirm generalizability. In contrast to currently established biomarkers, whose predictive performance may be affected by infection, inflammatory status and treatment‐related heterogeneity, our MAIT‐cell–based model enables rapid pre‐transplant risk stratification using a single flow cytometry analysis and may serve as a complementary strategy to existing biomarker systems.

CONCLUSIONS

Graft‐derived MAIT cells define a biologically coherent immune signature characterized by coordinated expression of CCR2, IL‐4 and IL‐17A, reflecting tissue homing, immunoregulation and tissue repair. This graft‐intrinsic signature was consistently associated with the risk of GI‐aGVHD and demonstrated reproducible predictive performance in internal validation analyses. Although prospective multicentre validation is required, incorporation of this MAIT‐cell–derived immune signature into pre‐transplant assessment may improve risk stratification and support risk‐adapted clinical management.
