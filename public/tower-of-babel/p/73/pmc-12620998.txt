Motoric cognitive risk syndrome: prevalence and associated sociodemographic and clinical factors in primary care settings in Brazil
Authors: Lílian Viana dos Santos Azevedo, Alice Moreira dos Santos Marques, Geraldo Alves Silva‐Júnior, Giselle Stephanie Sousa de Santos, Laura Pereira Faria, Maria Luíza Taveira, Marina Nascimento, Maira Tonidandel Barbosa, Paulo Caramelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMotoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR) is a pre‐dementia condition characterized by subjective memory complaints and slow gait, and it is associated with increased risk of dementia and mortality. This study investigated MCR prevalence in a Brazilian community sample and its associations with sociodemographic and clinical factors.METHODSCross‐sectional study conducted in four public primary care settings. Sociodemographic, clinical, motor, and cognitive aspects were assessed.RESULTSThe sample included 355 individuals (mean age = 71.4 years, 69.6% women). The prevalence of MCR was 11.3%. Univariate analysis showed associations with older age, lower education, inactivity, falls, polypharmacy, depression, stroke, poor Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination‐Revised (ACE‐R) performance, and higher Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale, subsection III (UPDRS‐III) scores. Multivariate analysis confirmed significant associations with falls (odds ratio [OR] = 14.4, 95% CI: 5.5 to 37.4), polypharmacy (OR = 4.3, 95% CI: 1.8 to 10.3), and UPDRS‐III scores (OR = 1.1, 95% CI: 1.03 to 1.17).DISCUSSIONThe prevalence of MCR was 11.3% in Brazilian older adults and was linked to polypharmacy, falls, and parkinsonian signs, highlighting its value for early and low‐cost identification in primary care.Highlights Prevalence of MCR was 11.3% in Brazilian older adults.MCR was independently associated with falls, polypharmacy, and parkinsonian signs.Findings highlight low‐cost clinical markers for early dementia risk identification.Results support integration of MCR screening into primary care in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Keywords: cognitive impairment, gait, motoric cognitive risk syndrome

BACKGROUND

Population aging is a global phenomenon, with the proportion of individuals aged 60 years and older increasing rapidly worldwide. It is estimated that in 2022, this population totaled ∼1.1 billion people, and that number is projected to nearly double by 2050. 1 In Brazil, the number of older adults increased from around 10 million in the year 2000 to over 32 million in 2024, and by 2050, one‐third of the Brazilian population is expected to be aged 60 years and older. 2

Aging is the main risk factor for cognitive decline. Furthermore, motor changes, such as gait disturbances, have been identified as predictors of accelerated cognitive deterioration and the development of dementia syndromes. 3 , 4

Considering the predictive potential of gait abnormalities in relation to cognitive decline, Verghese et al. proposed the concept of motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR). The diagnosis of this pre‐dementia syndrome is based on the presence of subjective cognitive complaints, slow gait speed (defined as one standard deviation or more below the age‐ and sex‐adjusted mean), preserved activities of daily living, and absence of dementia. Individuals with MCR have been found to present higher rates of conversion to dementia. 5 , 6

Unlike mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the diagnosis of MCR is more accessible and resource‐efficient, as it does not require formal neuropsychological testing or complex assessments, which are often difficult to access in low‐ or middle‐income countries. Additionally, MCR is less influenced by an individual's educational background. 6

A systematic review that included 17 studies reported a pooled MCR prevalence of 10% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 8% to 12%, I 2 = 98.4%). 7 Nevertheless, studies on MCR in the Brazilian population and in other Latin American countries remain limited, particularly those employing more robust clinical methodologies, which include comprehensive clinical examination, cognitive and mood assessment, gait analysis, and neurological evaluation with emphasis on parkinsonian signs. 8 , 9

In this context, the present study aimed to estimate the prevalence of MCR among Brazilian older adults who receive care in primary healthcare settings, as well as to examine the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics associated with an increased risk of this pre‐dementia syndrome. Identifying individuals at risk and understanding the factors related to MCR can provide important insights for the development of preventive strategies and interventions aimed at reducing the likelihood or delaying the onset of dementia.

METHODS

Study participants and design

This cross‐sectional study was carried out in four primary healthcare public units located in the city of Belo Horizonte, Brazil, including both urban and peri‐urban areas. The research protocol received formal approval from the Research Ethics Committees of both the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais and the Municipal Health Department of Belo Horizonte (SMSA‐BH). Data collection was conducted over a period spanning from November 2022 to June 2024. All participants were informed about the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

The sample size calculation indicated that it would be necessary to include at least 310 individuals to achieve adequate statistical power. Eligible participants were men and women aged 60 years or older who were capable of walking independently, including those who required the use of assistive devices. The exclusion criteria comprised a previous clinical diagnosis of dementia, the presence of severe orthopedic impairments, significant visual or hearing deficits, or current residence in long‐term care facilities.

A total of 411 older adults were initially recruited for participation. Among these, individuals were excluded for the following reasons: prior diagnosis of dementia (n = 43), inability to perform gait assessment (n = 4), age below 60 years (n = 6), and severe visual impairment (n = 3). After the application of these exclusion criteria, the final analytical sample consisted of 355 individuals aged 60 years or older.

MCR criteria

The diagnostic criteria for MCR were based on the presence of (1) subjective memory complaint, (2) slow gait, defined as walking speed one standard deviation or more below the age‐ and sex‐specific mean for the population, (3) preserved functional performance, and (4) absence of dementia. 5 , 6

Subjective memory complaint was assessed through the standardized question: “Do you feel that your memory is worse compared to other individuals of your age group?” A positive response was considered indicative of a subjective cognitive complaint.

Gait speed was evaluated along a flat, well‐lit 14‐m walkway. At the beginning and the end of the track 2 m were excluded from the measurement to account for acceleration and deceleration phases. Participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace, and the use of assistive devices was permitted when necessary.

Participants were divided into different sex and age groups and cut scores for defining slow gait speed were: for men <0.855 m/s for the age group 60 to 69 years, <0.778 m/s for the age group 70 to 79 years, and <0.714 m/s for the age group ≥80 years; for women <0.764 m/s for the age group 60 to 69 years, <0.714 m/s for the age group 70 to 79 years, and <0.519 m/s for the age group ≥80 years

A quantitative gait assessment was also conducted, using the APDM Mobility Lab inertial sensor system (APDM Inc., Portland, OR, USA). Three Opal sensors were used, one placed on the trunk at the lumbosacral junction and one on the dorsum of each foot. The data were automatically transmitted to a computer, processed using the Mobility Lab software, and stored in a local database. This system has demonstrated good reliability for gait assessment in older adults. 10 However, the results of the quantitative gait analysis were not used to define MCR diagnosis and were not included in this study, given that the main objective was to investigate the prevalence of MCR and to identify factors associated with this diagnosis.

Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables

Sociodemographic data, including age, sex, educational level, and socioeconomic status, were collected through a structured interview. Socioeconomic classification was assessed using the Brazilian Economic Classification Criteria, 11 which use seven socioeconomic levels, from A (higher) to E (lowest). To assess comorbidities, participants were interviewed regarding the presence or absence of selected medical conditions, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, heart disease, neurological disorders, orthopedic conditions, depression, anxiety, medication use, and history of falls within the past year.

Lifestyle‐related factors were also evaluated, including obesity, physical activity level, alcohol consumption, and smoking status. Obesity was assessed using the body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). The cutoff values established for the older adult population. Overweight was defined as BMI > 27 kg/m2 and underweight as below 22 kg/m2. 12

Motor function was assessed using the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale, subsection III (UPDRS III), 13 and global cognitive function was evaluated with the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination‐Revised (ACE‐R). 14

Physical activity was assessed using the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which estimates energy expenditure based on light, moderate, or vigorous physical activity performed for at least 10 continuous minutes during a typical week. Participants were categorized into five groups according to weekly frequency and duration of physical activity: very active, active, irregularly active A, irregularly active B, and sedentary. 15

Current alcohol consumption was evaluated based on the number of standard drinks consumed per week. One standard drink was defined as 14 g of alcohol, equivalent to one can of beer (350 mL), one shot of liquor (45 mL), or one glass of wine (150 mL). Participants were categorized as follows: nondrinkers (0 drinks/week), light drinkers (0.1 to 7 drinks/week), moderate drinkers (7.1 to 14 drinks/week), and heavy drinkers (>14 drinks/week). 16

Smoking status was classified as nonsmokers, former smokers, and current smokers, based on self‐reported quantity and duration. Current smokers were those who smoked regularly at least once a day for >6 months, while former smokers were those who had quit smoking at least 6 months prior to the study.

Additionally, the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)‐15 was used to screen for depressive symptoms. This scale is widely used in older adult populations and has demonstrated valid and reliable psychometric properties. 17 Fall history was assessed by asking: “Have you fallen in the past year?” Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications. 18

All assessments were carried out by a multidisciplinary team of trained healthcare professionals who possessed extensive prior experience in the clinical evaluation of older adults. This expertise contributed to the consistent application of standardized procedures throughout the study, thereby enhancing the reliability, accuracy, and overall quality of the data collected.

Statistical analysis

Initially, the characteristics of the total sample and the subgroups of individuals with and without MCR were described. Subsequently, univariate binary logistic regression was performed to investigate the association between each independent variable and the presence of MCR (present vs absent). Odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Variables with a p value < 0.20 in the univariate analysis were selected for inclusion in the multivariate binary logistic regression model. The multivariate analysis was conducted using a stepwise selection method. Variables with p ≥ 0.05 were progressively removed from the model, beginning with the variable with the highest p value. Variables with p < 0.05 or whose exclusion would compromise the model's fit were retained in the final model.

To assess the impact of excluding non‐significant variables, the likelihood ratio test was applied to determine whether their removal significantly altered the association with MCR.

A nomogram was developed based on the final multivariable logistic regression model to assist in the clinical interpretation of the statistical findings, providing an individualized estimate of MCR risk using the data obtained from this sample.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Among the 355 participants included in the final analysis, a total of 40 individuals met the diagnostic criteria for MCR, corresponding to a prevalence of 11.3% in this sample of community‐dwelling older adults.

The results highlight that women predominated in both groups. The mean age was higher among individuals with MCR compared to those without (73.6 vs 71.1 years), and a greater proportion of MCR participants were aged ≥80 years (27.5% vs 14.6%). DISCUSSION

This study identified a prevalence of 11.3% of MCR among older adults seen in primary healthcare settings in Brazil. This figure is consistent with global estimates, which range from 2.6% to 14.7%, with an average of ∼10%. 7 Our findings are also in line with previous studies conducted in Latin America, which generally reported higher prevalence rates compared to high‐income countries. 8 , 9

In a recent study conducted in Brazil, the prevalence of MCR was 4.3% in a cohort of 4677 older adults aged ≥60 years, with a predominance of individuals aged between 60 and 69 years (56.3%). 19 In contrast, the higher prevalence found in our study may be explained by the older age of the participants, considering that MCR is more frequent with advancing age. 6 , 20 Age is widely recognized as the primary risk factor for cognitive decline. 3

This previous Brazilian investigation, although analyzing data from a representative population‐based study, did not evaluate important clinical correlates of MCR, such as global cognitive performance and motor function (i.e., parkinsonian signs). 19

Moreover, our work focused on older adults attending primary care clinics in Brazil, a vulnerable population with high multimorbidity, less education, and frequent public healthcare utilization, thereby providing clinically meaningful insights for early detection of dementia in resource‐limited contexts, a different scenario from most previous community‐based studies investigating MCR prevalence.

Beyond estimating prevalence, we also investigated potential clinical and sociodemographic features associated with MCR. No significant differences were observed regarding race or sex, which is consistent with earlier findings in other populations. 6 However, we found significant associations between MCR and advanced age, lower educational attainment, and lower socioeconomic status.

A low educational level has been consistently identified in the literature as an important risk factor for the development of MCR. 6 , 19 , 20 In contrast, higher levels of education are thought to function as a protective factor not only against MCR but also against various forms of dementia, a relationship often attributed to the concept of increased cognitive reserve. This reserve, built up over years of formal education and cognitive stimulation, may enhance the brain's ability to compensate for age‐related or pathological changes, thereby delaying the onset or reducing the severity of clinical symptoms. 21

We also observed significant differences between participants with and without MCR in variables, such as physical inactivity, history of stroke, depressive symptoms, polypharmacy, and falls within the past year. These findings are aligned with prior research, which highlights unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, comorbidities, and polypharmacy as key risk factors for MCR. 22

A recent multicenter meta‐analysis showed that MCR risk factors varied between high‐ and low‐income countries. In this context, our findings may contribute valuable insights for the development of targeted public health strategies and interventions in low‐ or middle‐income settings. 22

Global cognitive function evaluation in our study was performed using the ACE‐R scale, which showed statistically significant differences between individuals with and without MCR. Although individuals with MCR may demonstrate cognitive performance within the expected range for their age and educational level, they are at increased risk of developing dementia, including Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia. 5 , 6 Furthermore, subjective memory complaints combined with reduced gait speed may precede measurable neuropsychological deficits by several years. 6 , 23

Individuals with MCR have also been shown to perform worse in global cognitive assessments compared to those without the syndrome. 24 A longitudinal study involving 1095 participants from the LonGenity cohort (mean age = 75.4 years), followed for up to 12 years, demonstrated that MCR was associated with greater cognitive decline, particularly in visuospatial, executive, and language domains. 24

Our findings reveal a higher frequency of falls in the past year among individuals diagnosed with MCR. This observation is consistent with evidence from a recent study indicating that MCR serves as a significant predictor of fall risk in older adults. 25 Furthermore, a longitudinal investigation involving three independent prospective cohorts demonstrated that MCR was an independent predictor of future falls over a 4‐year follow‐up period, even after adjusting for potential confounding variables. These results reinforce the importance of identifying MCR in clinical practice, as its presence may highlight individuals who could benefit from targeted fall‐prevention strategies. 26

In the present study, we observed that individuals diagnosed with MCR exhibited higher scores in motor assessment when evaluated using the UPDRS‐III scale. This observation is consistent with findings reported in previous research, particularly in a study conducted with a cohort of 580 older adults who were followed for an average period of 30 months. The authors of that investigation concluded that even subtle or mild parkinsonian signs could serve as potential predictors for the future development of MCR. Furthermore, such signs may play an important role in the underlying pathogenesis of the syndrome and contribute meaningfully to improving the early detection and identification of individuals at increased risk for MCR. 27

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Although the sample size exceeded the initial estimate based on sample size calculation, assessments were conducted in different settings but restricted to a single city, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, due to the cross‐sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established and should be further explored in future prospective studies.

Given the rapidly aging population in Brazil and the increasing demand for cost‐effective screening strategies, understanding the clinical correlates of MCR in primary care settings may inform prevention and early intervention strategies within the public health system. Although our sample was drawn from urban and peri‐urban areas of a single city, we consider that these results are relevant, given that the first contact with the Brazilian public health system occurs predominantly at the primary care level.

The evaluation of a simple question regarding cognitive complaints combined with gait speed takes less than 5 min. This approach is particularly suitable for primary care settings, where high patient demand and a limited number of specialized professionals are common. Early identification of these patients allows for timely referral to specialized centers.

CONCLUSION

This study investigated the prevalence of MCR in the primary health care setting and identified associations with sociodemographic and clinical factors previously described as risk factors for this condition. In our sample, MCR was associated with polypharmacy, falls in the past year, and parkinsonian signs in multivariate analysis. The identification of factors associated with MCR in the context of primary care is essential for developing early prevention and intervention strategies. In this sense, this is the first study to provide a nomogram for prediction of MCR diagnosis, which may be a useful tool for clinical settings, especially at the primary care level.

The use of simple tools, such as gait assessment and subjective cognitive screening, can facilitate the early detection of individuals at risk, enabling the implementation of actions aimed at delaying or preventing progression to dementia – especially in low‐ or middle‐income countries, where access to comprehensive neuropsychological evaluations may be limited.
