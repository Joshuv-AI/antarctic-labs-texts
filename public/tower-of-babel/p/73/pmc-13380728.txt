Plastic particle risk is shaped by transformation history
Authors: Swaroop Chakraborty
Published: 2026

The environmental risk of plastic particles is not determined solely by polymer type, particle size, and concentration. It is also shaped by transformation history: the ordered sequence of atmospheric priming, aqueous/soil system conditioning, and biotic encounter that determines the state presented to the next compartment. This paper argues that treating transformation history as a primary exposure descriptor can make environmental monitoring, treatment, and risk assessment more realistic.

A useful organising lens is the environmental transformation hierarchy: the idea that plastic particles undergo sequential changes across environmental compartments, for example, atmosphere → aquatic or soil system → biota, and that this sequence determines which processes dominate and which particle state is passed to the next environment [1]. Applying this hierarchy to micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs) shifts the core question from “how much plastic is present in water?” to “what transformation state has arrived, how was it produced, and which state variables control fate, mixture chemistry, and biological exposure?” Here I outline a minimal, hierarchy-aligned workflow and reporting framework to make MNP studies more measurable and comparable across water and soil systems.

Transformation history as exposure logic

The environmental transformation hierarchy is not a call for ever-more complexity. Rather, it reduces complexity by identifying commit points: stages where irreversible or strongly conditioning changes occur (Fig. 1). This hierarchy is also size- and organism-sensitive. Nanoplastics are governed more strongly by colloidal stability, surface interactions, and membrane-scale uptake, whereas microplastics are shaped more by morphology, settling, and ingestion mechanics. Likewise, zooplankton, fish, and benthic organisms do not encounter the same transformed state in the same way. Each regime is controlled by different drivers—ultraviolet radiation, oxidants, and humidity cycling in air; ions, dissolved organic matter, and sunlight in water; organismal feeding modes and gut residence times in biota—and therefore different controlling variables.

Atmospheric priming sets surface reactivity before water entry

Water science often begins its MNP story at the point of aquatic detection. But for many important sources, that story begins in air. Mechanical recycling is a clear example: shredding and related processes generate airborne MNPs at high concentrations and within respirable size ranges [2]. These particles can then deposit onto surfaces and enter water systems through wet cleaning, runoff, and industrial wash streams.

This matters because atmospheric exposure can impose chemical priming before any aqueous interaction occurs. Ageing studies consistently show that abiotic weathering can introduce oxygen-containing functional groups, increase surface roughness and cracking, and create chemical heterogeneity, or patchiness, across a particle surface. Once particles reach water, these changes can alter aggregation, pollutant sorption, and additive leaching. Crucially for water monitoring, ageing can also affect analytical recovery and comparability, as many methods and reference materials are validated on pristine plastics [3].

From an environmental risk perspective, the implication is that “polymer type + size + mass” does not uniquely define exposure. Sequence-aware reporting should treat the ageing state as a primary descriptor. At minimum, this means tracking oxidation proxies, such as the carbonyl index, heterogeneity indicators, and fragmentation or roughness metrics alongside conventional concentration measures.

Aqueous and soil system conditioning creates coronas, biofilms, and reactive interfaces

Once MNPs enter water and soil systems, they are rapidly redefined by interfacial layers and aqueous/soil chemistry. Two transformations are particularly consequential for fate and mixture toxicity: eco-corona formation and biofilm colonisation.

Eco-coronas as state-dependent coatings. In natural waters, dissolved organic matter (DOM), proteins, polysaccharides, and inorganic colloids can adsorb onto plastic surfaces, forming eco-coronas that change surface charge, stability, aggregation, and interaction energies [4]. A widely cited review argues that coronas are a key determinant of MNP risk and should be explicitly considered in safety assessments. Empirical evidence also shows that corona formation is not uniform, but sensitive to prior ageing. For example, work on photooxidised plastics has quantified how organic matter adsorption differs between virgin and ultraviolet-aged polymers, and how organic mixtures can alter adsorption outcomes [5]. More generally, eco-corona formation and organic sorption are expected to vary with polymer type and transformation state. Polymers differ in hydrophobicity, polarity, crystallinity, roughness and surface charge, particularly after ageing. These differences can be seen across low-density polymers such as polyethylene and polypropylene, higher-density polymers such as polyvinyl chloride and polyamide, and chemically resistant fluorinated polymers. Organic molecules also differ in polarity, ionisation, and functional groups. Sorption may therefore involve hydrophobic partitioning, electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces and retention within roughened or biofilm-coated interfaces [6].

Biofilms as ecological rematerialisation. Plastic surfaces host microbial communities, often termed the plastisphere, that can restructure surface chemistry and govern pollutant interactions and transport [7]. Distinct microbial communities on plastic debris are well established, and syntheses highlight that biofilm formation can alter MNP physicochemical properties, pollutant adsorption potential, fate, and ecotoxicity. Biofilms are therefore not simply a confounder. They may control whether a contaminant is retained during transport, released under changing conditions, or transferred during ingestion by organisms.

These conditioning processes also help explain why laboratory studies using pristine spheres in simple media may fail to capture environmentally conditioned behaviour, especially when ageing, coronas, biofilms, and co-contaminants alter the particle state presented to water treatment systems and organisms.

This framework is intended to integrate, rather than replace, existing strands of evidence. Weathering and ageing studies explain how particle surfaces are chemically reworked before water entry; eco-corona and plastisphere studies explain how particles are redefined in water; and colloid and interface science explain how charge, roughness, ionic strength, and organic matter control aggregation, transport, and capture. The transformation hierarchy brings these literatures into a single sequence-aware question: which transformed particle state is actually presented to monitoring systems, treatment trains, and biota? MNPs as mixture carriers and mixture generators

Transformation history matters because it governs particle behaviour and mixture chemistry: what MNPs carry, what they release, and what they help transform.

Photochemical and reactive-oxygen-species pathways. MNPs can participate in sunlight-driven processes that modify polymer surfaces and influence the transformation of co-occurring pollutants, often through reactive oxygen species [9]. In practical terms, the light regime and water chemistry (DOM, ions, oxidants) can shift a system between sorption- and transformation-dominated behaviours—another reason why state variables and exposure conditions must be reported for comparability.

Additive leaching and transformation-enabled release. A key pathway linking transformation to exposure is the leaching of plastic-associated additives and degradation products. Reviews of additive leaching in aquatic environments highlight the importance of phase equilibrium and kinetics in controlling release, while ageing-focused syntheses explicitly note that abiotic ageing can affect additive and monomer leaching by altering the polymer matrix and surface properties [10]. From an environmental transformation hierarchy perspective, leaching is not simply a function of polymer type but of the history that sets porosity, cracking, oxidation, and corona or biofilm state.

This reframing also matters for bioaccessibility and dose in an environmentally relevant sense. In aquatic systems, bioaccessibility is more appropriately considered for plastic-associated chemicals, additives, leached species, and transformation products than for the particles as a whole. In practice, the bioaccessible fraction is often the fraction that is freely dissolved, weakly bound and exchangeable, or mobilised during biological encounter, such as ingestion and gut processing. Even without detailed measurements in physiological fluids, water-relevant studies can estimate this fraction by combining time-resolved leaching, passive sampling, speciation measurements where relevant, and organismal uptake and depuration experiments.

This structure enables comparability by specifying the sequence of environmental compartments included, the state variables that define the resulting particle state, and how those variables map to fate and effect endpoints.

A simple example shows why sequence matters. A pristine polyethylene particle tested directly in clean laboratory water may appear weakly interactive and reasonably removable. The same polymer, after atmospheric ultraviolet or oxidative priming followed by dissolved organic matter conditioning and biofilm growth, may arrive with different roughness, charge, aggregation behaviour, contaminant loading, and biological retention potential. The hierarchy therefore changes the risk question from “how much plastic is in water/soil?” to “which transformed state has arrived, and what does that state imply for removal and exposure?”

Implications for monitoring and management in water systems

A transformation-history lens also clarifies where water interventions can be most effective.

Monitoring. If ageing affects detectability and quantification, method development should test aged and conditioned particles, not only pristine standards. In drinking-water contexts, reviews report wide variation in measured concentrations and stress the need for standardised sampling and analytical approaches—an issue that transformation history likely exacerbates [11].

Treatment and removal. Coronas and biofilms can change density, aggregation behaviour, and surface charge parameters that directly influence coagulation, filtration, and sedimentation. Treating MNPs as clean particles may mispredict removal efficiencies when most particles are conditioned composites. This is especially relevant for wastewater treatment plants, where efficient removal from the water phase does not eliminate risk if transformed particles are concentrated in sludge pathways [12]. The wastewater-to-soil transfer pathway is also relevant to irrigated agricultural systems, where reclaimed water, sludge-derived amendments, or contaminated runoff may transfer transformed MNPs into soils. In this setting, the exposure pathway extends into a soil–water–plant continuum, where aged particles, eco-coronas, biofilms, and sorbed contaminants may interact with soil organic matter, rhizosphere chemistry, and root surfaces, with possible implications for crop exposure and food-chain transfer [12,13].

Risk ranking. The practical value of a hierarchy lies in prioritisation. High-risk trajectories are those where transformation history increases sorption capacity for co-contaminants, additive leaching, or ingestion and retention by biota. Reporting the minimum descriptor set above provides a mechanistic basis for defining and comparing these trajectories across catchments and engineered systems.

Closing perspective

MNPs in water/soil systems are not a single entity, but a spectrum of transformation states. Atmospheric generation and ageing can set the initial surface reactivity and heterogeneity; aqueous/soil conditioning then builds eco-coronas and biofilms that redefine interfacial chemistry; and biotic encounter translates these states into bioaccessibility and internal dose. Treating transformation history as a primary exposure descriptor—and reporting a small, hierarchy-aligned set of state variables—offers a practical route to more comparable monitoring, more realistic estimates of treatment performance, and more defensible risk ranking in water/soil systems.

This Commentary does not seek to establish a standard protocol; rather, it proposes a sequence-aware conceptual framework to guide more comparable future studies.
