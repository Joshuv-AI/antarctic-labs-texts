Psoriatic arthritis and obesity: a review of clinical burden and considerations for comprehensive care
Authors: Norman Madsen, Laura C Coates, Joseph F Merola, Wayne Ho, Willa Hsueh, Uta Kiltz, Veena K Ranganath, Atul Deodhar, Orrin M Troum, Carlos Diaz, Andris Kronbergs, Natalia Bello, Philip J Mease, Alexis Ogdie, Christopher T Ritchlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterised by joint involvement, significant impact on quality of life and frequently linked with obesity, metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular comorbidities. Despite advances in PsA management, obesity remains a critical factor influencing disease severity, treatment response and patient outcomes. We examine the interplay between PsA and obesity, the current clinical guidelines, gaps in clinical practice, research on shared inflammatory pathways, treatment implications and the potential of weight-loss interventions to modify disease progression. This comprehensive narrative review uses a non-systematic search of PubMed, Embase and Scopus databases and analyses literature on PsA, obesity, cytokine signalling and therapeutic outcomes, with priority to clinical trials, meta-analyses and large cohort studies. Obesity exacerbates PsA through systemic inflammation, adipokine dysregulation and mechanical joint stress, contributing to worse clinical outcomes, increased comorbidity and poorer response to biologics. Weight management improves disease activity and therapeutic efficacy, although its effects on PsA pathophysiology remain underexplored. Our review highlights that obesity should be recognised as a risk factor and comorbidity in PsA for the integral management of patients. Further research is needed to elucidate the cytokine and molecular changes induced by weight management, generate more clinical data and optimise personalised management strategies. While clinical guidelines recommend a coordinated care approach to optimising the management of PsA in patients with obesity, emerging clinical data support further focus on the role of targeting inflammation, obesity and metabolic dysfunction to improve long-term PsA outcomes and quality of life, mitigate complications and provide better cardiovascular and overall health to patients.Keywords: Arthritis, Psoriatic; Interleukin-17; Psoriasis

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects the joints and is often associated with cutaneous psoriasis.1 Both psoriasis and PsA result from a combination of genetic predisposition, immune system dysregulation and environmental triggers. About one-third of individuals with psoriasis will develop PsA.1 Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterised by immune-mediated hyperproliferation of keratinocytes, leading to erythematous scaly plaques primarily on the skin.2 PsA is characterised by joint pain, stiffness and swelling, which can lead to joint damage if left untreated. PsA can affect multiple joints in the body, and its symptoms can vary from mild to severe, potentially leading to significant physical disability.3 Enthesitis, or inflammation at the attachment sites of ligaments, tendons and joint capsules to bone, is common, and many patients develop diffuse digit swelling or dactylitis. Psoriatic disease (PsD) refers to its various clinical manifestations, which include but are not limited to psoriasis affecting the skin and PsA affecting the joints.4 Patients with psoriasis and PsA frequently exhibit associated comorbidities such as metabolic syndrome (MetS (hypertension, hypertriglyceridaemia, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL), diabetes and obesity)), liver dysfunction and cardiovascular disease (CVD).2

Obesity (defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2, with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 considered overweight) is defined by the Obesity Medicine Association as a chronic, progressive, relapsing and treatable multifactorial neurobehavioural disease, in which an increase in body fat promotes adipose tissue dysfunction and abnormal fat mass physical forces, resulting in adverse metabolic, biomechanical and psychosocial health consequences.5 The condition poses significant health risks, leading to various well-recognised complications such as type 2 diabetes (T2D), CVD, sleep apnoea and many others including psoriasis and rheumatic diseases.6,8 Obesity is notably more prevalent among individuals with PsD compared with the general population.4 The prevalence of obesity in patients with PsA is significantly high, with each unit increase in BMI in patients with psoriasis elevating the risk for the development of PsA by 5.3%. Patients with obesity and psoriasis are three times as likely to develop PsA. Studies conducted in the USA showed that the prevalence of psoriasis increased by 4%–8% in patients with obesity.6 7 The increased prevalence of obesity in these patients is associated with a more severe disease course and greater comorbidity burden.9 Adipose tissue is not merely a fat storage site but an active endocrine organ that secretes various bioactive molecules, including adipokines, which influence systemic metabolism and inflammation.10 11 Obesity is expected to reach its maximal levels in the USA (44% of individuals) and Europe (24%–37% of individuals) between 2026 and 2054.12

The pathogenic pathways in psoriasis, PsA and obesity are intertwined, involving common inflammatory mediators and signalling pathways. The interplay between adipokines, cytokines and immune cells creates a complex network that drives chronic inflammation and disease progression in these conditions.2 7 10 The excess adipose tissue in individuals with obesity acts as an active endocrine organ, secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines that may enhance the inflammatory milieu characteristic of PsA and contribute to worse clinical outcomes.9 11 13

Patients with PsA have a higher prevalence of MetS compared with patients with psoriasis or the general population.9 More than 50% of patients with PsA have at least one comorbidity and around 40% of patients have three or more comorbidities.9 Understanding the link between obesity and PsA is crucial because obesity is not just a comorbidity but a globally rising modifiable risk factor that influences disease onset, severity and treatment response. This review (1) explores how adipose tissue, adipokines and systemic inflammation contribute to PsA pathogenesis; (2) assesses the impact of obesity on response rates to biologic and non-biologic therapies and how obesity can lead to reduced drug effectiveness; (3) provides insights into how obesity influences disease severity, comorbidity burden and long-term health risks in patients with PsA; (4) discusses the role of lifestyle interventions, pharmacological approaches and bariatric surgery in improving PsA outcomes and (5) reviews current guidelines and offers a comprehensive framework for clinicians to optimise treatment strategies by incorporating weight management into PsA care. By integrating these perspectives, this review underscores the importance of comprehensive disease management in PsA, emphasising that obesity should be a key consideration in both prevention and treatment strategies (figure 1).

Methods

We identified relevant literature through a non-systematic search of PubMed, Embase and Scopus databases up to October 2025. Keywords included “obesity”, “psoriatic arthritis”, “psoriasis”, “inflammation”, “cytokines”, “biologic therapies” and “weight loss/management”. Priority was given to peer-reviewed clinical trials, meta-analyses and large cohort studies, while only three of the 23 screened case reports and small observational studies were considered (fewer than 50 participants) if they provided novel insights. We manually screened reference lists of key articles for additional sources. The non-systematic narrative approach may introduce selection bias, which was mitigated by prioritising higher-quality evidence (clinical trials, meta-analyses and large cohort studies). Overall, 49 studies were included in this review, comprising meta-analyses and systematic reviews (n=3); narrative reviews (n=17); randomised controlled trials (n=3); case-control studies (n=3); cross-sectional studies (n=9); prospective cohort studies (n=1); mechanistic in vitro/animal reviews (n=7) and guidelines and epidemiological or expert opinions (n=6).

Risk factors and pathogenesis underlying psoriasis, PsA and obesity

Genetic

Genetic predisposition may explain part of the development of psoriasis, PsA and obesity. Variants in the fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) gene (rs1558902, rs1121980, rs9939609, rs9941349) and the hypothalamic melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) gene (rs17782313, rs12970134) are among the most significant genetic factors associated with obesity and psoriasis.14 The FTO and MC4R genes can influence food intake and energy expenditure, thereby contributing to obesity.14 Obesity-related variants in genes like leptin receptor (LEPR) (rs1137101) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (rs925946) are also associated with early-onset psoriasis.14 LEPR variants affect leptin signalling, which plays a role in regulating immune responses and metabolic processes—both critical in obesity and inflammatory diseases like psoriasis. Variants in BDNF, a gene involved in energy homeostasis and neural development, are also linked to higher BMI and have been associated with an increased risk of psoriasis.14

Environmental

Consumption of high-calorie, unhealthy foods rich in fats and sugars can lead to obesity and an increased incidence of psoriasis and PsA, mediated by pro-inflammatory effects of adipose tissue.15 A diet high in vegetables, lean proteins and whole grains is recommended to help manage these conditions.15 Sedentary behaviour is associated with increased body weight and fat accumulation, which can worsen inflammatory processes in these diseases. Conversely, regular physical activity can reduce systemic inflammation, lower production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, improve cardiovascular health and help maintain a healthy weight. Engaging in moderate to vigorous physical activity is recommended to help control inflammation and maintain overall health in patients with PsA.16 Short-term weight management in patients with PsA positively correlated with improvement in Disease Activity Score in 28 joints with C reactive protein (DAS28-CRP) (p=0.002), with the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)20 response rate from baseline to 6-month visit significantly higher (71% vs 30%, p=0.008) in patients with weight loss ≥18.6%.17 The percentage reduction in waist circumference also showed a significant correlation with the decrease in DAS28-CRP (p=0.019). In the Dietetic IntervEntion in psoriaTicArthritis (DIETA) trial with 97 patients with PsA, participants after a 12-week hypocaloric intervention showed a fourfold improvement (over baseline) in joint activity by DAS28-CRP (p=0.004) and a twofold improvement in axial involvement by Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (p=0.001) compared with the baseline.18

Immunological

Psoriasis and PsA are chronic inflammatory diseases with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-17 and IL-23. TNF-α promotes the activation and migration of immune cells to the skin and joints, leading to the inflammation and tissue damage observed in psoriasis and PsA. IL-17 plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis and PsA by driving the production of other pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and matrix metalloproteinases, which contribute to the inflammatory milieu.19 20 IL-17 is primarily produced by T-helper (Th)17 cells, with additional important cellular sources being mast, γδ T, αβ T and innate immune cells. IL-23 has a key role in the maintenance and expansion of Th17 cells and is elevated in the skin and synovium of patients with psoriasis and PsA.19 20

These cytokines, produced by infiltrating immune cells within adipose tissue, are also called adipokines and lead to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state. Changes in the adipokine secretion profile of leptin, adiponectin, resistin and visfatin by the excess adipose tissue in obesity drive pro-inflammatory pathways in patients with psoriasis.2 Leptin, for instance, promotes the recruitment of macrophages, the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-17 and TNF-α and the differentiation of Th17 immune cells.2 Conversely, adiponectin generally has anti-inflammatory properties and the levels of high molecular weight adiponectin are reduced in obesity.2 The chronic inflammation in PsA and obesity shares common pathways, with the overproduction of TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23 and IL-6 being the hallmark of all these conditions.3 Levels of pro-inflammatory TNF-α and IL-6 are positively correlated with and may help elevate levels of adipokines such as leptin, resistin, visfatin and chemerin, which lead to insulin resistance, increased food intake and immune cell infiltration into adipose tissues that exacerbate related comorbidities seen in MetS.2

The altered secretion profile of adipokines in obesity contributes to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state (table 1), which can increase the risk and severity of inflammatory diseases like PsD.21 22 Patients with obesity have early-onset PsA, more severe PsA symptoms such as tuft resorption, plantar and Achilles spurs and pelvic enthesitis and are less likely to achieve remission,23 24 but weight management improves remission in patients receiving biologic therapy.25

Individual adipokines involved in psoriasis and PsA

Adipokines, bioactive peptides secreted by adipose tissue, play significant roles in the regulation of metabolic and inflammatory processes. In the context of psoriasis and PsA, several adipokines (and antibodies against progranulin) have been implicated in the pathogenesis of these diseases, particularly due to their hypothesised pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory properties (table 1).21 22

Leptin

Leptin, a hormone predominantly produced by adipocytes, is known for regulating appetite suppression, mitochondrial activation and efficient energy utilisation.2 Persistently elevated leptin levels in obesity, thought to be due to leptin resistance, exhibit pro-inflammatory properties. It is hypothesised that this could exacerbate psoriasis and arthritis symptoms. Elevated leptin levels correlate with increased disease severity in psoriasis,2 and women with PsA have been reported with higher serum leptin levels than women with psoriasis (mean (SD), 3.1 (0.8) vs 2.8 (0.8) log(ng/mL); p=0.04).26 Leptin also enhances naïve T-cell differentiation into pro-inflammatory Th1 cells.27 Leptin has been associated with the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 8, TNF-α and IL-6, which in turn lead to elevated IL-17/IL-23 levels that are important in the pathogenesis of PsD.28

Chemerin

Chemerin, a chemotactic protein secreted primarily from adipocytes, is elevated in inflammatory and metabolic diseases like MetS, diabetes, obesity and psoriasis, and its levels correlate with high BMI. Chemerin attracts dendritic cells and macrophages to sites of inflammation, thought to be contributing to the chronic inflammatory state observed in PsD.2

Progranulin

Progranulin, which is elevated in obesity, promotes anti-inflammatory action by binding to TNF receptors and antagonising TNF-α. However, elevated levels of antiprogranulin antibodies are found in patients with PsA but not in patients with psoriasis alone. Antiprogranulin antibodies lower plasma progranulin levels, leading to more frequent enthesitis and dactylitis in PsA.29

Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1

TNF-α increases the production of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, which recruits osteoclast precursors from circulating monocytes to sites of bone erosion in patients with PsA.30 It also recruits macrophages, which are essential in the initiation of enthesitis.

Zinc-alpha-2-glycoprotein

Zinc-alpha-2-glycoprotein (ZAG), an adipokine with anti-inflammatory properties, is decreased in individuals with psoriasis and PsA. ZAG has been shown to inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and may have a protective role against inflammation.31 ZAG is also known to protect against obesity by upregulating anti-inflammatory adipokine adiponectin and lipolytic genes (FXR, PPARα, etc) while downregulating lipogenic genes (SREBP-1c, LXR, etc).31

SERPINE1

The adipokine SERPINE1, also known as plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, is elevated in obesity and is associated with a prothrombotic and pro-inflammatory state. High levels of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 contribute to the chronic inflammation observed in psoriasis and PsA, promoting both thrombosis and inflammation and increasing cardiovascular risk.6 32

Resistin and visfatin

Resistin and visfatin are adipokines associated with insulin resistance and inflammation. Both adipokines are significantly elevated in patients with psoriasis and both increase the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6.2

Adiponectin, omentin-1 and vaspin

Adiponectin, omentin-1 and vaspin are adipokines with anti-inflammatory effects. Adiponectin protects against insulin resistance and exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting the production of TNF-α and IL-6. However, the adiponectin levels often decrease in obesity, which can lead to a pro-inflammatory state that exacerbates psoriasis and PsA. Lower levels of adiponectin, omentin-1 and vaspin contribute to increased inflammation and elevated obesity (higher BMI) and PsD severity (higher Psoriasis Area and Severity Index scores).33

Obesity-associated PsA disease activity and its impact on treatment response

Patients with obesity and PsA experience more severe symptoms, greater disease activity and have greater morbidity compared with patients without obesity.9 22 This is attributed to the chronic inflammatory state induced by excess adipose tissue, which further fuels the inflammatory processes in PsA.9 Moreover, obesity is linked to poorer clinical outcomes and reduced treatment response in patients with PsA,6 23 while weight management leads to improvement in patients with PsA and obesity.17 34

Effective pharmacological management of PsA in patients with obesity should consider the unique challenges posed by obesity to optimise treatment outcomes. This includes careful consideration of drug dosing, as standard doses may be less effective in patients with obesity. Additionally, emerging obesity drug therapies and their potential for positive impacts on PsA disease activity and other comorbidities warrant consideration for co-therapy. Adjusting treatment plans to include weight-management strategies alongside pharmacological interventions may enhance overall treatment efficacy and improve patient outcomes.3 9 24 35

Conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) are first-line therapy for PsA. Such csDMARDs as methotrexate can elevate the risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in patients with PsA.36 TNF inhibitors (TNFis) are recommended as first-line biologics and are commonly prescribed for the treatment of PsA, but their efficacy is compromised in patients with obesity.36 This decreased response is associated with TNFi pharmacokinetics, where adipose tissue can alter drug distribution. Furthermore, weight management has been shown to improve treatment outcomes.17 34 36 Consequently, patients with obesity may require higher TNFi doses adjusted for BMI by weight-based dosing in intravenous formulations or alternative therapeutic approaches.36

IL-17 inhibitors such as ixekizumab, secukinumab and bimekizumab are approved as PsA therapies and also used as first-line biologics depending on the clinical scenario. There are few studies on secukinumab with mixed results on the impact of obesity.36 In one real-world study, ixekizumab has shown consistent clinical benefits across weight categories36 and independent of sex, duration of PsA or BMI in clinical trials.37 While ustekinumab inhibits the p40 subunit of both IL-12 and IL-23, guselkumab and risankizumab are specific to IL-23p19. Ustekinumab uses weight-based dosing.36 Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) like tofacitinib and upadacitinib have shown promise in treating PsA and are approved in the USA and European Union post-TNFi therapies. ORAL Surveillance Study found that JAKis can elevate lipid parameters, weight and may increase the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in smokers and patients with rheumatoid arthritis, aged ≥65 years.3638,40

Besides increasing mechanical stress, particularly in weight-bearing joints, obesity is associated with systemic chronic low-grade inflammation.9 In patients with obesity, the pro-inflammatory burden is heightened due to increased systemic levels of pro-inflammatory adipokines and cytokines, which further amplify osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption.1 The receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL)-dependent and RANKL-independent pathways drive bone resorption by promoting the differentiation of osteoclasts from progenitor cells. In PsA, elevated RANKL expression by synovial fibroblasts and B cells, as well as increased serum RANKL levels, strongly contribute to bone degradation.1 TNF-α and IL-17 enhance osteoclastogenesis in synergy with RANKL, but IL-6 can also drive this process in a RANKL-independent manner.1 This effect is particularly relevant in enthesitis, where inflammatory infiltration at tendon and ligament insertions drives pathological bone remodelling in the general population with obesity.17 These changes underscore the importance of weight management in patients with PsA to mitigate the impact on joint integrity.17

Obesity is a major risk factor for various cardiometabolic comorbidities within MetS, which complicates PsA management.35 Incidence of MetS, hypertension and dyslipidaemia is higher in patients with PsA than those with psoriasis. Around 16% of patients with PsA have insulin resistance, while 6%–20% have T2D. Patients with PsA have a 43% higher risk of developing CVD than the general population.35 PsA also poses a risk for cardiovascular events, independent of the classical risk factors such as diabetes, dyslipidaemia and smoking. Morbidity risks for myocardial infarction and cerebrovascular diseases were increased by 68% and 22%, respectively, in patients with PsA.35 Presence of these comorbidities necessitates a comprehensive treatment approach that addresses both joint and skin manifestations of PsA as well as associated metabolic and cardiovascular risks.2 35

Impacts of obesity on disease burden in PsA

Obesity exacerbates the pain and fatigue experienced by patients with PsA, significantly diminishing their health-related quality of life (QoL). Excess body weight places additional stress on weight-bearing joints, aggravating inflammation and joint pain in PsA.17 Additionally, obesity is linked to a systemic inflammatory state, further intensifying pain. This interplay of factors results in a more severe disease burden for patients with obesity and PsA compared with those with normal weight. Sleep disorders are also associated with obesity, diabetes and CVD. A meta-analysis of six studies that used the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index revealed that 72.9% and 59.8% of patients with PsA and psoriasis, respectively, experienced poor sleep quality compared with 26.9% of healthy controls.41 Patients with obesity experience fibromyalgia as do patients with PsA, leading to worse sleep quality.41 Another study found that sleeping disorders were associated with poor QoL and intense fatigue in 67.7% and 57.7% of patients with PsA and psoriasis, respectively, compared with 14.6% in the control group.8

Obesity contributes significantly to the psychosocial burden in patients with PsA, increasing the prevalence of depression, anxiety and other mental health issues. The chronic pain and fatigue associated with PsA, compounded by the psychological distress of obesity, create a vicious cycle that severely impacts mental health. Studies have shown that patients with obesity and PsA are more likely to experience depression and anxiety, further impairing their QoL and complicating disease management. Patients’ perceptions of musculoskeletal involvement play an important role in sleep disturbances and fatigue in patients with PsA, and fatigue is strongly associated with anxiety and depression.42

The economic burden of managing PsA is compounded by obesity and its comorbidities due to increased healthcare costs, frequent medical visits, lost productivity and reduced life expectancy.43 44 Patients with comorbidities often require more frequent and intensive medical care, including higher medication doses or additional therapies, leading to increased healthcare expenditures. A work disability study in patients with PsA revealed 14% absenteeism and 46% work productivity loss. Additionally, the presence of any PsA-associated comorbidities resulted in higher unemployment rates, further exacerbating the economic impact.45

Recommendations

A multidisciplinary, comprehensive and personalised approach tailored to the individual is universally recommended to address the diverse manifestations, disease severity and comorbidities of PsA. The Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA) particularly emphasises weight management and lifestyle modifications as a key component of treatment, which is also supported to some extent by the other guidelines.

European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology

The 2023 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations provide a comprehensive overview for the management of PsA with pharmacological therapies.38 These recommendations emphasise the importance of a personalised treatment approach based on disease severity, comorbidities (obesity, MetS, CVD, depression) and patient preferences (table 2). Cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities such as obesity play an important role in the JAKi risk profile; hence, JAKis are proposed as second-line therapies. Despite the emerging data on the influence of obesity on PsA outcomes and treatment, guidance on how to manage PsA comorbidities such as MetS and obesity is lacking.

Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis

GRAPPA recommendations, updated in 2021, align closely with EULAR recommendations but provide additional guidance on managing specific PsA manifestations, such as axial disease, enthesitis and dactylitis.46 GRAPPA emphasises a multidisciplinary approach, involving dermatologists, rheumatologists and primary care providers, to effectively manage both skin and joint symptoms. GRAPPA recommendations caution against the use of leflunomide and methotrexate in obesity, glucocorticoids in congestive heart failure and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and JAKis in CVD, while they suggest avoiding the use of TNFis in congestive heart failure. The guidelines advocate for patients to maintain a healthy weight and suggest regular assessment of disease activity; however, there are no specific dietary, lifestyle or pharmacological guidelines for combating obesity (table 2), and the recommendations of weight management to improve outcomes in patients with PsA are either absent or conditional at best.

American College of Rheumatology/National Psoriasis Foundation

The American College of Rheumatology/National Psoriasis Foundation (ACR/NPF) guidelines, last updated in 2018, provide a framework for treating PsA, emphasising the importance of early diagnosis and intervention to prevent joint damage and improve long-term outcomes.47 The ACR guidelines recommend the use of TNFi as first-line therapy followed by oral small molecules and IL-17i or IL-12/IL-23i for patients with moderate to severe disease.

All clinical guidelines highlight the importance of patient involvement via patient education, shared decision-making and adherence to treatment to improve outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Weight management in patients with psoriasis and/or PsA

Obesity is a significant modifiable risk factor in psoriasis and PsA, with a notable impact on disease activity and treatment efficacy. Effective, sustainable weight management by lifestyle/dietary interventions and pharmacological management is crucial for managing obesity and improving clinical outcomes in patients with PsA (figure 3). Lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes and exercise, are essential for managing obesity in patients with psoriasis or PsA.15 16 48 Dietary interventions play a critical role in managing obesity, psoriasis and PsA by reducing systemic inflammation—a hallmark of both conditions. Although data are limited, anti-inflammatory diets, omega-3 fatty acids and caloric restriction may reduce disease activity and improve overall health outcomes in patients with PsA by modulating inflammatory pathways. Rich in vegetables, legumes, nuts and olive oil, the Mediterranean diet has demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects and may reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are elevated in patients with PsA.15

Weight management has been consistently linked with improvements in PsA disease activity.17 34 The excess adipose tissue in patients with obesity secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate PsA symptoms.2 9 28 Reducing body weight alleviates these inflammatory processes and enhances the efficacy of pharmacological treatments.9 Research shows that even modest weight management (5%–10% of body weight) can lead to significant improvements in disease activity and physical function in patients with PsA.9 17 34 48 In cases of severe obesity, when patients have not achieved success with lifestyle modifications alone, bariatric surgery has been associated with substantial weight management, a protective effect on PsD development and a decreased risk of PsA in patients with psoriasis.35

Given the challenges many patients face in achieving significant weight management through lifestyle interventions alone, pharmacological treatment options are increasingly used. Before the advent of incretin-based therapies, several obesity management medications were developed, each with its own mechanism of action and associated risks. These older drugs include orlistat, phentermine/topiramate extended release, naltrexone/bupropion and phendimetrazine, all of which had mixed results in terms of safety and efficacy (table 3).

Incretin-based therapies: GLP-1 RAs, such as liraglutide and semaglutide, mimic the effects of GLP-1, a hormone that promotes insulin secretion, delays gastric emptying and increases satiety.44 These drugs were originally developed for diabetes management but have shown significant efficacy in reducing body weight and improving psoriasis severity.49 These agents have also demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, which may be beneficial in managing PsD.49

The GLP-1 RAs and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 RAs are more effective in promoting sustained weight management than older medications and have been approved for the management of obesity (in addition to T2D). Clinical trials of semaglutide, a GLP-1 RA approved for the management of obesity, reported an average body weight management of 15.3% after 68 weeks of treatment at a dose of 2.4 mg, significantly higher than that typically seen with older agents that resulted in <10% weight loss.44 48 Tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 RA approved for the management of obesity, has shown (SURMOUNT trials) an average weight loss of 20.9% after 72 weeks of treatment.48 50 In a recent head-to-head trial (NCT05822830), treatment with tirzepatide (20.2% average weight loss) resulted in superior weight management to semaglutide (13.7%) in patients with obesity.51 Several other incretin-based molecules are in development for weight management and have shown efficacy in phase II trials: the dual GLP-1/glucagon RA survodutide (4.8 mg dose) showed a 14.9% average weight loss in 46 weeks; 6 mg mazdutide induced 11.3% weight loss at 24 weeks; 420 mg maridebart cafraglutide, a GLP-1 RA/GIP receptor antagonist, showed 14.5% average weight loss at 12 weeks and 12 mg retatrutide, a triple GIP/GLP-1/glucagon RA, showed 24.2% average weight loss at 48 weeks in phase II trials.50 Monotherapy with 4.5 mg cagrilintide (amylin receptor agonist) induced 10.8% weight loss, and when combined with 2.4 mg semaglutide, the weight loss was 15.4% after 26 weeks.50 Orforglipron, an oral GLP-1 RA, showed an 11.2% decrease in average weight at 36 weeks in phase III trials along with improvements in waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, triglyceride levels and non-HDL cholesterol levels.50

GLP-1 RAs and the GIP/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits in patients with T2D.50 A meta-analysis of seven cardiovascular outcome trials showed that GLP-1 RAs reduced the incidence of major cardiovascular events and mortality by 12% in patients with T2D.50 In the SELECT trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced major adverse cardiovascular events versus placebo in patients with CVD and obesity without diabetes.50 The use of semaglutide in the FLOW trial reduced the risk of death from CVD and kidney disease by 24%.50 Composite renal disease risk was reduced by 17% with tirzepatide in the SURPASS-4 trial, showing less glomerular filtration rate decline and reduced urine albumin-to-creatinine ratios in patients with diabetes and high cardiovascular risk.50 The SURPASS-CVOT trial evaluated tirzepatide versus dulaglutide in patients with T2D and established atherosclerotic CVD, and demonstrated that tirzepatide was cardioprotective in participants with established atherosclerotic CVD and T2D.52

Although GLP-1 RAs and other dual-acting and triple-acting incretin therapies can be associated with nausea, diarrhoea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal pain, dyspepsia, injection site reactions, fatigue, hair loss and, very rarely, pancreatitis, severe gastrointestinal, renal or gallbladder effects, and carry a warning about thyroid tumours,44 50 they generally have fewer systemic and neuropsychiatric side effects compared with older drugs. There is also no risk of stimulant-related side effects, which are common with sympathomimetic agents like phentermine and phendimetrazine.53 Daily moderate exercise is recommended to prevent muscle loss and osteoporosis that can occur with rapid or significant weight loss irrespective of the medicines used.

Long-term studies have shown that weight management with GLP-1 RAs and GIP/GLP-1 RA can be maintained for extended periods with continuous use.44 50 GLP-1 plays an important role in the treatment of obesity and T2D and has emerging potential in psoriasis and PsA.44 49 GLP-1 RAs, GIP/GLP-1 RA and other incretins, originally developed for the management of T2D, have shown promise in promoting weight management, improving metabolic profiles and reducing fatty liver, a common comorbidity in patients with obesity and PsD.49 50 While incretin-based therapies are promising adjunctive strategies, PsA-specific outcome data remain limited and their role is still evolving.

Combining a biologic and incretin-based therapy for patients with PsA and obesity may offer several benefits for patients in addition to treating solely with a biologic and support of a comprehensive lifestyle approach to the management of these patients with the goal for better overall outcomes. Recent results from the phase IIIb TOGETHER-PsA trial (NCT06588296) provide evidence supporting combined metabolic and anti-inflammatory treatment strategies in PsA. This 52-week randomised trial evaluated ixekizumab plus tirzepatide versus ixekizumab alone in 271 adults with active PsA and overweight or obesity with one or more weight-related comorbidities. Significantly more participants receiving dual treatment achieved the primary end points of ACR50 and ≥10% weight loss at week 36 than those who received ixekizumab alone (31.7% vs 0.8%; p<0.001). Combination therapy also resulted in significantly higher ACR50 response rates (33.5% vs 20.4%; p=0.02), along with improvements in minimal disease activity, skin outcomes and patient-reported outcomes, including physical function and fatigue.54 These findings highlight the potential for integrated immunological and metabolic treatment approaches to improve both joint and systemic outcomes in patients with PsA and obesity.

Conclusion

The intersection of obesity and PsA represents a significant area of interest in both clinical management and patient outcomes (figure 4), underscored by novel therapies for obesity management. The strong association between obesity and PsA highlights its critical role in disease severity, treatment response and overall patient outcomes. Obesity is a chronic disease and a well-known risk factor for the development of PsA and significantly complicates its management. The chronic systemic inflammation driven by excess adiposity is theorised to drive PsA pathogenesis, leading to more aggressive disease and increased comorbidities such as CVD and MetS. Additionally, obesity is linked to poor response to several PsA therapies, markedly lower functional drug efficacy and an increased likelihood of multidrug failure. Patients with obesity and PsA experience greater pain, reduced physical function, worse patient-reported outcomes and a limited likelihood of achieving remission or low disease activity in addition to other obesity-related complications. These factors contribute to a prevalent subset of patients with PsA who are particularly complex to manage, requiring a more individualised management approach. Given the substantial impact of obesity on disease burden, therapeutic efficacy and overall patient and health outcomes, addressing weight management should be a key component of comprehensive PsA care.

Weight management plays an important role in the therapeutic strategy for PsA, as it has been shown to improve disease activity and patient-reported outcomes. Weight-management interventions, including lifestyle modifications, bariatric surgery and pharmacological approaches, can lead to significant improvements in PsA symptoms, response to therapy and overall QoL.3 17 18 34 36 Given the well-established link between obesity and PsA, a comprehensive approach to patient care is essential, integrating rheumatological management with strategies to address obesity and its associated cardiometabolic comorbidities. Rheumatologists can apply current evidence-based treatments by incorporating weight management into routine PsA care, using interdisciplinary collaboration to tailor treatment strategies. In this context, GLP-1 RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 RAs, with moderate daily exercise, present a promising adjunctive option for patients with PsA who are struggling with weight management by lifestyle modification. Therefore, expanding access to these therapies in patients with PsA and obesity may enhance long-term patient outcomes by improving comorbidities through weight management, while further data are needed to show the overall potential impact of these therapies in PsA.

Future directions

Current treatment paradigms, including the treat-to-target approach, have improved outcomes for many patients, yet a significant proportion continues to experience inadequate disease control due to the burden of comorbidities such as obesity. The timely and effective management of these comorbidities is critical and aligns with recommendations from EULAR, GRAPPA and ACR/NPF guidelines.

Future research should focus on characterising the impact of weight-loss interventions on cytokine profiles and molecular changes in patients with PsA and other autoimmune diseases. Understanding these mechanisms at a deeper level could pave the way for targeted therapies that address both PsA and obesity, potentially modifying disease progression and improving treatment outcomes.

Healthcare providers should adopt a comprehensive approach to treating patients with PsA by considering the presence of comorbidities and tailoring treatment plans accordingly. Managing obesity as a key comorbidity in PsA requires a multidisciplinary strategy that integrates dietary modifications, structured physical activity and pharmacological interventions. We call on the scientific, medical and healthcare service community to prioritise the management of comorbidities in PsA and to invest in research that will lead to more effective and personalised treatment strategies. By addressing these factors, we can help improve the QoL for patients with PsA and pave the way for better health outcomes.

Lastly, more longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the long-term impact of obesity on PsA progression and treatment response. These studies could provide valuable insights into how best to manage PsA in the context of obesity and inform future clinical guideline updates.

In summary, optimising PsA management requires an integrated approach that concurrently addresses obesity and inflammation, with ongoing research and clinical adaptations being crucial to improving patient outcomes. We urge collective action from researchers, clinicians and policymakers to advance knowledge, refine treatment strategies and ensure comprehensive care for patients with PsA and obesity towards improving their QoL and health outcomes.
