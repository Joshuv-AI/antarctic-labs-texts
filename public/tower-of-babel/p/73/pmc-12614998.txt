Incidence of Respiratory Syncytial Virus-Associated Hospitalization Among Adults in Ontario, Canada, 2017–2019
Authors: Sarah A Buchan, Jenna Alessandrini, Samantha S M Drover, Melissa K Andrew, Susan E Bronskill, Kevin A Brown, Nick Daneman, Shelley L Deeks, Jonathan B Gubbay, Jennie Johnstone, Timothy M Karnauchow, Samantha Lee, Allison J McGeer, J Dayre McNally, Shelly A McNeil, Samira Mubareka, Michelle Murti, Marek Smieja, Sarah E Wilson, Jeffrey C Kwong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes substantial morbidity and mortality among adults. Given recent RSV vaccine authorizations, data on groups at highest risk are needed to support vaccine program decision making.MethodsWe identified adults aged ≥ 18 years hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed RSV and hospitalizations with RSV-related diagnostic codes in Ontario, Canada (2017–2019). We calculated incidence of hospitalization with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using Poisson regression stratified by demographic and clinical risk factors, and substratified by age. We reported secondary outcomes including the proportion of individuals with fatal outcomes.ResultsOver 2 respiratory virus seasons, we identified 3928 RSV-associated hospitalizations. Incidence increased steadily with age from 2.0 (95% CI, 1.8–2.3) per 100 000 for those aged 18–49 years to 43.7 (95% CI, 41.0–46.6) per 100 000 for those aged 70–79 years, with a sharp increase to 134.7 (95% CI, 128.6–141.1) per 100 000 for those aged ≥ 80 years. Incidence was higher for those with comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease (receiving dialysis) (494.7; 95% CI, 410.7–595.8) and transplant recipients (370.9; 95% CI, 318.0–432.6), as well as for those living in lower (22.4; 95% CI, 21.1–23.7) versus higher-income neighborhoods (11.8; 95% CI, 10.8–12.8). Among those hospitalized, 10.3% (n = 403) died within 30 days of admission, and 93.1% of deaths occurred in those aged ≥ 60 years. Of survivors, 44.6% of community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years had functional decline requiring formal supports at discharge.DiscussionWe found a substantial burden of RSV among older adults, particularly among those with preexisting medical conditions and those of lower socioeconomic status. These results will inform equitable vaccine recommendations for adults.Keywords: RSV, adult, hospitalization, administrative data, risk factors

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is increasingly recognized to cause serious illness in older adults and those with comorbidities. Along with influenza, and now severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), RSV causes significant morbidity, mortality, and health care system strain. RSV is well recognized as a highly important pathogen in infants and young children, but fewer data are available on the burden of RSV in adults. The risk of RSV-related hospitalization and death among adults increases with age, with a progressively more rapidly increase beyond 65 years [1–10]. Furthermore, certain comorbidities such as immunocompromise, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and heart failure (HF) have been identified as risk factors for severe disease, with RSV also attributed to exacerbations of these conditions [6, 10–16].

Since 2023, three RSV vaccines have been authorized for older adults in Europe, the United States, and Canada [17]. While severe RSV disease among adults has recently been explored, population-level burden estimates have been limited to smaller subsets of risk factors and age groups [6, 18–23]. Having detailed information about the incidence of RSV hospitalization by a wide variety of demographic and clinical characteristics will not only inform vaccine recommendations and policy, but also help clinicians with patient education and targeting of priority populations for vaccination. To address current gaps in the literature, we aimed to estimate the incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization among adults in Ontario, Canada across finely stratified age groups, various comorbidities, and indicators of socioeconomic status (SES) and frailty, as well as to explore secondary complications among those with RSV-associated hospitalizations.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective cohort study to estimate the mean annual incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization in Ontario adults over 2 northern hemisphere respiratory virus seasons (1 September 2017 to 31 August 2018 and 1 September 2018 to 31 August 2019). All Ontario residents aged ≥ 18 years who had a valid provincial health insurance number (approximately 12 million) were assessed for RSV hospitalization. Datasets included in the analysis were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES. ICES is an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. We obtained ethics approval from Public Health Ontario's Ethics Research Board.

RSV-Associated Hospitalizations

We identified RSV-associated hospitalization, our primary outcome, from 2 sources of data. First, we used the Canadian Institute for Health Information's Discharge Abstract Database to identify RSV hospitalizations using a validated algorithm based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes [24]. This included anyone who, on the first visit of a hospitalization episode, had RSV-related disease identified by 1 of 4 ICD-10 codes (J12.1, J20.5, J21.0, B97.4) in any of the 25 diagnostic fields. Second, we included anyone who had laboratory-confirmed RSV associated with a hospitalization (for any cause), defined as a test yielding RSV from a sample obtained within 7 days before to 4 days after a hospital admission. We used the Ontario Laboratories Information System to identify individuals with a laboratory-confirmed RSV infection, supplemented with data collected from Public Health Ontario Laboratories and select individual hospital laboratories, as described elsewhere [25]. Individuals could have 1 RSV-associated hospitalization per season (ie, up to 2 during the study period).

Secondary outcomes among those with RSV-associated hospitalizations included hospital length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) admission, receipt of mechanical ventilation, and death within 30 days of admission (via Ontario's Registered Persons Database). Using the Discharge Abstract Database, we also identified disposition at time of discharge (discharged home, discharged home with support services [ie, in-home supports or referral to further services], discharged to a long-term care [LTC] home or residential care facility, death, or other) for those aged ≥ 60 years.

Covariates

We identified covariates using a variety of administrative datasets housed at ICES including various ICES-derived disease cohorts and other validated registries and algorithms. These were used to create population denominators by identifying individuals with these covariates as of 1 September of each applicable season (index), with the exception of age for RSV cases, which was defined at hospitalization. We used the Registered Persons Database to define sex and age (18–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, ≥ 80 years), and to obtain postal codes to derive neighborhood-level income as well as rurality of residence based on Statistics Canada's 2016 Census. We classified individuals as having no identified comorbidities or as having 1 or more conditions, including asthma, autoimmune disorders, cancer (active or past 5 years), chronic heart disease (other than HF), chronic kidney disease (CKD; with and without dialysis), COPD, cirrhosis, dementia, diabetes mellitus, HF, hypertension, immunocompromising conditions, stroke, and transplant recipient. We identified frailty as a binary measure using the Johns Hopkins ACG System version 10, and as a continuum using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score, with individuals categorized as having a low (score < 5), moderate (score 5–15), or high (score > 15) frailty risk [26, 27]. We classified all individuals by dwelling type/care requirements based on the highest level of care received (through publicly funded services) in the 12 months prior to index (LTC resident [highest]; community-dwelling resident receiving chronic home care [ie, in-home maintenance and long-term supportive care]; or community-dwelling resident not receiving chronic home care [lowest]). Detailed definitions of outcomes and covariates can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

For the overall study population and by listed covariates, we calculated the mean annual incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization, incidence rate ratios (IRRs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using Poisson regression, with the log of each subgroup-specific population as the offset. We repeated our analysis of incidence further stratified by age group. Missing data were minimal (< 1% for most covariates) and all individuals remained in our cohort with incidence estimates presented for nonmissing categories only.

For those with an RSV-associated hospitalization, baseline characteristics and the proportion of secondary outcomes were estimated overall and by age. We calculated the proportion of those with an RSV-associated hospitalization who had RSV listed as their most responsible diagnosis (MRDx; ie, most significant condition responsible for the greatest proportion of a patient's hospital stay) overall and by age. For those who did not have our primary outcome, RSV, or other acute respiratory illnesses, such as influenza (Supplementary Table 2), listed as their MRDx, we tabulated the top 3 most frequent MRDx codes.

RESULTS

Across the 2 respiratory virus seasons included in this study, we identified 3928 RSV-associated hospitalizations among Ontario adults aged ≥ 18 years (maximum of 2 per person, ie, 1 per season). Of these hospitalizations, 1252 (31.9%) were identified using only ICD-10 codes and 2676 (68.1%) were laboratory confirmed, of which 1698 also had an RSV ICD-10 code (Supplementary Figure 1). Of all RSV-associated hospitalizations, 984 (25.1%) had RSV listed as the MRDx (Supplementary Table 3); this varied by age group and was lowest for those aged 60–69 years (18.9%) and highest for those aged ≥ 80 years (28.5%). Among the 2085 (53.1%) hospitalizations that did not have RSV or another acute respiratory illness identified as the MRDx, the most commonly coded conditions listed as the MRDx were COPD (J44; n = 944), HF (I50; n = 169), and asthma (J45; n = 97).

Most (85.8%) of the RSV-associated hospitalizations occurred in those aged ≥ 60 years, with 45.3% occurring in those aged ≥ 80 years (Table 1). Approximately three-quarters occurred in December (20.0%), January (35.0%), or February (18.8%). Nearly all (96.1%) hospitalized individuals had at least 1 comorbidity identified; 43.9% of patients had at least 4. Furthermore, 24.5% were classified as moderately frail and 12.4% as highly frail. In the 12 months prior to the start of the respiratory virus season, 8.4% of patients with RSV-associated hospitalizations were LTC residents and 28.8% had received chronic home care. Among adults aged ≥ 80 and 18–49 years with RSV-associated hospitalizations, 1.2% and 21.9% had zero comorbidities, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). Overall, the most prevalent comorbidities among those with RSV were hypertension (77.7%), COPD (45.3%), and diabetes (40.0%) (Table 1); however, this varied by age group; for example, asthma (41.8%) was most prevalent among those aged 18–49 years whereas hypertension (92.1%) was most prevalent among those aged ≥ 80 years (Supplementary Table 4). Young adults (18–49 years) were the least frail while older adults (≥ 80 years) were the most frail.

The mean annual incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization increased with age, with a sharp rise for those aged ≥ 80 years (134.7 per 100 000; 95% CI, 128.6–141.1 per 100 000; Figure 1 and Table 1). Among all adults, higher rates per 100 000 were observed for those with any comorbidity than for those with no identified conditions (1.2; 95% CI, 1.0–1.4); the highest rates were among those with CKD receiving dialysis (494.7; 95% CI, 410.7–595.8), transplant recipients (370.9; 95% CI, 318.0–432.6), HF (232.1; 95% CI, 219.6–245.3), and dementia (187.5; 95% CI, 174.4–201.5). The incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization per 100 000 adults increased with the number of comorbidities identified (from 7.3 [95% CI, 6.7–8.0] for those with 1 comorbidity to 212.6 [95% CI, 202.8–222.9] for those with 4 or more). RSV incidence also increased for persons with increasing frailty (high, 394.3 [95% CI, 360.9–430.9] vs low, 36.0 [95% CI, 34.2–38.0]) and was higher for LTC residents (197.7; 95% CI, 177.5–220.3) and persons receiving chronic home care (279.4; 95% CI, 263.6–296.2). We observed an inverse relationship between RSV-associated hospitalization and neighborhood-level income quintile; people in the lowest income quintile had almost double the incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization (22.4; 95% CI, 21.1–23.7) as those in the highest (11.8; 95% CI, 10.8–12.8).

The impact of underlying medical conditions was similar in age-stratified analyses, with higher incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization for each condition (compared to those with no comorbidities identified), and estimates increasing by age overall across most comorbidities (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 5). Despite some overlap in confidence intervals, adults aged 18–49 years with each condition had similar or higher incidence compared to adults aged 70–79 years with no conditions; adults aged 18–49 years with cancer (whether past or active), immunocompromising conditions, stroke, HF, other chronic heart diseases, cirrhosis, CKD (whether or not receiving dialysis), or who were transplant recipients had higher rates than adults aged ≥ 80 years with zero comorbidities (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 5). Incidence rate ratios comparing rates for those with conditions to those with no comorbidities increased with the number of comorbidities within each age group (Supplementary Table 6). For most conditions, the IRR was greatest for those aged 18–49 years and declined monotonically across increasing age groups; adults aged 18–49 years with any condition (except for asthma) had substantially higher IRRs compared to those aged ≥ 80 years. Among highly frail adults, persons receiving prior chronic home care, and LTC residents incidence was very high (> 140 per 100 000) across all age groups, although rates did increase with age among recipients of chronic home care (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 5).

The median LOS in hospital among those with RSV-associated hospitalizations was shortest for those aged 18–49 years (5 days; interquartile range [IQR], 2–11) and longest for those aged ≥ 80 years (7 days; IQR, 4–13) (Table 2). The proportion of individuals admitted to the ICU varied by age, with 23.4%–27.5% of those aged 18–79 years admitted, and a lower proportion for those aged ≥ 80 years (12.0%). Overall, 6.4% of individuals received mechanical ventilation. The proportion of adults who died within 30 days of admission was 10.3%, with 93.1% of these deaths occurring in those aged ≥ 60 years.

Among adults aged ≥ 60 years who resided in the community without chronic home care prior to their RSV-associated hospitalization, 169 (8.4%) had a fatal outcome (Supplementary Table 7); of survivors, only 49.9% were discharged home without formal supports, a further 36.3% were discharged home with support services, and 8.3% were discharged to long-term or residential care (ie, 44.6% requiring formal supports at discharge). The remaining 5.5% were discharged elsewhere (eg, transferred within the facility to alternate level of care).

DISCUSSION

Our study highlights the significant burden of RSV hospitalization among adults, and the variability of this burden across age and risk groups. We found that individuals aged ≥ 80 years had the highest incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization, and incidence was higher for those with identified comorbidities, those who are more frail, those requiring prior care, and those living in lower-income neighborhoods. Various comorbidities played an influential role in shaping severe RSV disease burden among adults of all ages, including those aged 18–49 years. Less than half of community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years with no prior chronic home care were discharged home without formal supports.

Our results are consistent with other studies from high-income countries that have reported increased RSV hospitalization incidence [3–7, 10, 11, 18], LOS, and case fatality [2, 28] with increasing age. We found the incidence in those aged ≥ 80 years was substantially higher than younger age groups, including those aged 70–79 years; this trend is similar to results from the United States, England, and New Zealand [5, 7, 10]. Our results are also similar to a recent study from Ontario; however, our estimates are higher when comparing overlapping study years, likely as a result of incorporating laboratory data into our outcome definition in addition to using the same ICD-10–coded outcomes [9].

As observed in our cohort, RSV hospitalization frequently occurs among adults with preexisting conditions [9, 10, 28, 29]. We found a higher incidence in those with each comorbidity compared to those without underlying conditions, a trend well documented in recent literature [6, 10, 12, 14, 15]. Similar to our findings, a study conducted in the United States from 2017 to 2020 found that among adults aged ≥ 18 years hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed RSV, incidence rates in patients with asthma, diabetes, and COPD were 2.0–3.6, 2.4–11.4, and 3.2–13.4 times the incidence compared to those without each respective comorbidity [10]; IRR estimates in the current study are likely higher due to use of a reference group that had no identified comorbidities. A separate US study further found CKD (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 4.4; P < .001), HF (aOR = 2.1; P < .001), and stroke (aOR = 2.0; P = .045) to be significant predictors of hospitalization among adults with RSV [14]; these 3 comorbidities were associated with substantially higher incidence in our study. Although dementia was one of the highest risk conditions in our study, it has not been as commonly explored as other comorbidities. Further research clarifying which conditions contribute to the highest risk of severe RSV-related disease will be helpful to inform recommendations. As comorbidities also typically increase in prevalence with age, and approximately 96% of our study population had at least 1 comorbidity identified, we evaluated the incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization by number of comorbidities. Our results demonstrate that the accumulation of conditions is also associated with much higher incidence across all ages.

Interestingly, and similar to existing literature [11, 23], the presence of many underlying conditions was associated with similar or greater incidence of RSV hospitalization among younger adults compared to older healthier adults. Despite incidence also being higher among immunocompromised individuals, our work highlights the striking importance of other comorbidities in RSV disease progression among young adults in particular. While rates were substantially higher among adults aged ≥ 80 years regardless of comorbidity status, our identification of comorbidities (as opposed to age alone) as the driving force of risk among younger adults (including those up to aged 79 years for certain conditions) is of key importance to inform vaccination recommendations.

In our study, among all ages, severity of frailty, prior chronic home care, and LTC residence were associated with increasing incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization, similar to other studies [30, 31]. We also observed a clear inverse gradient between hospitalization rate and neighborhood-level income. This trend of health disparities among individuals with lower SES has been observed for a wide range of respiratory pathogens including SARS-CoV-2 [32], and more recently, RSV [33–36]. SES influences access to resources, environmental exposures (eg, pollution, crowding), and health-promoting behaviors [37], all factors that influence risk of severe RSV disease. This may be a helpful framework to identify priorities for vaccination beyond age and comorbidities, including targeting those of lower SES.

Importantly, we also identified persistent functional decline requiring formal supports (including in-home supports/LTC) as important sequelae of RSV hospitalization in older adults. Whereas hospitalization and death rates were highest in the oldest age group, we observed lower proportions of adults aged ≥ 80 years receiving mechanical ventilation and being admitted to the ICU compared to younger adults (albeit still remaining high). While this may not be intuitive given that older adults were at higher risk than their younger counterparts, this trend likely reflects service eligibility and advance care directives such that intensive interventions may be avoided for older patients. Lower hospitalization rates among LTC residents with increasing age may similarly be due to goals of care and may also reflect higher fatality following RSV-related disease before the possibility of ICU/hospital admission. This further emphasizes the importance of RSV infection prevention among these high-risk groups to mitigate such outcomes.

Our study has some limitations. Although we included both laboratory-confirmed and ICD-10–coded validated outcomes, and the majority of Ontario laboratories were contributing to the Ontario Laboratories Information System during our study period, we still likely underestimated the true burden of disease in hospitals. We relied on routinely collected data, resulting in lower estimates compared to active surveillance studies in Canada [38]. Underestimation is likely due to a lack of routine testing (particularly for atypical presentations), reduced sensitivity of RSV ICD-10 codes and laboratory tests among adults (due to lower viral loads in older adults at presentation [39, 40]), as well as the likelihood that some laboratory outcomes were not identified partially due to nonstandardized data entry practices in the Ontario Laboratories Information System. Burden estimates are also likely largely dependent on testing patterns, which may vary greatly by age and risk status; despite this, a relatively large percentage of young adults hospitalized with RSV were considered low-risk, for example (21.9% with zero comorbidities and 88.0% nonfrail). We did not adjust for underdetection (ie, undertesting or test sensitivity), but in one modelling study that did account for underascertainment, the adjustment more than doubled the estimated hospitalization rate among adults aged ≥ 65 years [4]. As not all cases were laboratory confirmed (with the timing of infection thus unknown), some nosocomial infections may have been included. There may have also been some misclassification of comorbidities as not all conditions were identified using validated definitions (with our frailty risk score only validated in those aged ≥ 75 years), and some individuals may have developed a condition between 1 September (when the presence of comorbidities was ascertained) and their hospitalization date. We used administrative database definitions for comorbidities, which may not include mild forms of some conditions and does not include all immunosuppression caused by therapy, for example. Finally, given that most comorbidities were not treated as mutually exclusive, and we were unable to identify private care services and distinguish retirement home residents from community-dwelling adults, it is difficult to disentangle the direct influence of each level of care and individual comorbidity. Future work should continue to explore additional lifestyle behaviors and indicators of SES beyond income to better understand the inequities present as they relate to severe RSV disease among adults.

Our study has several notable strengths. Due to the inclusion of all Ontario adults, we were able to provide a comprehensive assessment of the burden of RSV-associated hospitalization across the entire adult population prior to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. The large sample permitted a degree of stratification not always present in other studies from Ontario [9, 41] and other jurisdictions [6, 11, 20, 42], including by presence and number of comorbidities and other characteristics of interest such as neighborhood-level income, while further stratified by age groups across the entire adult lifespan. As RSV vaccine recommendations and programs are continuously evolving and are highly contingent on age, care setting, and comorbidity status [17], these stratifications offer timely insights. Due to the nature of the administrative data holdings, we were also able to identify discharge setting/formal supports after hospitalization; given the increasing understanding of the role of acute respiratory illness on functional decline [43, 44], this may be particularly helpful to understand the longer-term impacts of RSV disease and the benefits of prevention. We were also able to look at outcomes such as ICU admission and death, which will be helpful for modelling the potential impact and cost-effectiveness of RSV vaccination programs. An additional strength was the use of laboratory-confirmed infections in addition to ICD-10–coded infections, allowing for a more accurate representation of the burden of RSV hospitalization in Ontario [9]. Furthermore, we included any diagnostic code of RSV on the discharge abstract; had we limited to the MRDx, we would have dramatically underestimated RSV-associated hospitalizations by 75.3% and 71.5% among young (18–49 years) and older (≥ 80 years) adults, respectively.

CONCLUSION

Our work highlights the substantial burden and important sequelae of RSV-associated hospitalizations, particularly among older individuals, those with comorbidities, and those with lower SES. We further illustrate key age-specific risk factors for consideration, including the importance of comorbidities in shaping RSV disease patterns in adults of all ages. Given the recent authorization of RSV vaccines for older adults, these results will be helpful as decision-makers consider vaccine recommendations for publicly funded programs, and will support clinician counselling and patient decision-making related to RSV prevention. These findings can also serve as a baseline to assess the impact of vaccination programs on RSV hospitalizations among adults.
