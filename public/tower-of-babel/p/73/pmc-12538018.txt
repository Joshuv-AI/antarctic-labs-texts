Pulmonary inflammation and immune responses induced by nanocellulose: Insights from in vivo and in vitro models
Authors: Katsuhide Fujita, Sawae Obara, Junko Maru, Yuka Kawai, Shigehisa Endoh, Akihiro Moriyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Nanocellulose (NC), including cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs) and cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), is an emerging class of bio-based materials derived from plant cellulose. Owing to their exceptional mechanical properties, NC particles have been increasingly applied in industrial and consumer products as sustainable alternatives to petroleum-based materials. For instance, CNFs are > 5-fold stronger than steel, while being significantly lighter, highlighting their potential as alternative materials (Kangas et al., 2013, Sharma et al., 2019).

CNFs and CNCs have gained particular attention for applications in cosmetics, packaging, coatings, and biomedical materials. However, their expanding use raises concerns about potential human exposure, particularly through inhalation, and their potential respiratory toxicity. NC particles fall within the respirable size range (<10 µm), and smaller fractions (<4 µm) can reach the alveolar region, potentially causing adverse pulmonary effects (Oberdörster et al., 2005). However, inhalation toxicity data for NC materials remain limited, and their long-term health implications are not fully understood (Stoudmann et al., 2020).

Several animal studies have investigated the pulmonary effects of the inhalation of CNFs, using oropharyngeal aspiration and intratracheal instillation models (Catalán et al., 2017, Hadrup et al., 2019, Ilves et al., 2018, Park et al., 2018). These studies reported that CNF-induced pulmonary inflammation tends to be transient and relatively mild compared to that caused by carbon nanotubes (CNTs) (Ilves et al., 2018, Park et al., 2018). CNTs are widely used as reference materials in nanotoxicology owing to their well-characterized fibrous morphology and potent pulmonary toxicity. Although CNFs are bio-based and composed of cellulose, and CNTs are synthetic and carbon-based, both share high aspect ratios and fibrous structures, key factors that influence their deposition, clearance, and biological interactions in the respiratory tract (Park et al., 2018, Fujita et al., 2021).

Our previous study used rat models to show that different types of CNFs, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl radical (TEMPO)-oxidized and mechanically fibrillated, induced varying degrees of pulmonary inflammation. These effects resolved within 90 d and were milder than those induced by multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) (Fujita et al., 2021). The differences observed likely reflect the physicochemical properties of CNFs, such as fiber size and surface chemistry. For instance, more finely fibrillated CNFs, such as TEMPO-oxidized ones, primarily deposit in the alveoli. In contrast, coarser, bundled mechanically fibrillated CNFs tend to accumulate in the terminal bronchioles and are associated with granuloma formation (Fujita et al., 2021, Fujita et al., 2025). Furthermore, endotoxin contamination in CNF suspensions has been shown to contribute minimally to these inflammatory responses (Fujita et al., 2025).

Considering the variability in physicochemical properties and clearance profiles of these nanomaterials, it is crucial to elucidate their immunological-induced responses to better inform their safe use. Previous studies have shown that CNFs may induce distinct immune responses and are poorly cleared from the lungs, potentially leading to persistent immune activation (Ilves et al., 2018, Park et al., 2018, Fujita et al., 2025). However, the long-term immunological consequences of NC exposure remains poorly understood, particularly under repeated low-dose exposure conditions, which are more relevant to occupational settings.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to comprehensively assess the pulmonary and immunotoxic potential of CNFs and CNCs using both in vivo and in vitro models. Specifically, lung inflammation, histopathological changes, and immune responses were evaluated 28 d after intratracheal instillation of these NC in rats. In parallel, we conducted in vitro analyses in NR8383 rat alveolar macrophages of CNC-induced cytotoxicity, inflammatory cytokine production, morphological alterations, and gene expression changes.

The physicochemical properties of NC and carbon-based nanomaterials, particularly fiber dimensions and dispersion state, are critical determinants of their biological behavior and pulmonary distribution. However, accurately characterizing anisotropic nanomaterials such as CNFs, CNCs, and MWCNTs presents several challenges. For instance, dynamic light scattering (DLS), is influenced by particle shape and polydispersity, and the resulting size values reflect dispersion behavior instead of precise particle dimensions. Mechanically defibrillated CNFs are particularly difficult to measure using atomic force microscopy (AFM) owing to their broad width distribution, irregular morphology, and entangled structures. Therefore, TEM was performed for morphological assessment and DLS for dispersion analysis, enabling consistent and comparative evaluation of the physicochemical properties of all test materials.

This study provides mechanistic insights by integrating immunophenotyping with histopathological and inflammatory assessments. Through the direct comparison of the effects of CNFs and CNCs with MWCNTs, our findings advance the understanding of NC-specific biological responses and support risk assessment efforts aimed at ensuring the safe use of these bio-based materials in industrial and consumer applications.

Materials and methods

Materials preparation

TEMPO-oxidized CNFs (10 mg/mL in water) were obtained from Nippon Paper Industries Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) and dispersed for 30 min at 2000 rpm using a planetary centrifugal non-foaming mixer (ARE-310, THINKY CORPORATION, Tokyo, Japan). Mechanically fibrillated CNFs (30 mg/mL in water), derived from softwood bleached kraft pulp, were sourced from the Daio Paper Corporation (Tokyo, Japan) and dispersed for 3 min using an ultrasonic mixer (PR-1; THINKY CORPORATION) with an ultrasonic oscillation output of 70 W per transducer (two transducers: side and bottom) at a frequency of approximately 40 kHz, under simultaneous centrifugation at 300 rpm. These dispersions were designated as CNF1 and CNF2, respectively.

Standard CNCs (52 mg/mL in water), sulfuric-acid-hydrolyzed CNCs (50 mg/mL in water), and desulfurized CNCs (42 mg/mL in water) were purchased from Cellulose Lab Inc. (Toronto, Canada) and dispersed for 30 min using the ARE-310 mixer. These dispersions were designated as CNC1, CNC2, and CNC3, respectively.

Before testing, CNF and standard CNC slurries were pre-irradiated with gamma rays at ∼ 1.0 kGy (Koga Isotope Ltd., Shiga, Japan), a dose shown to effectively inactivate microorganisms without altering the properties pf the CNFs (Horie et al., 2023). Concentrations of the CNFs and CNCs were then determined using the dry weight method (JISC, 1992), and stock dispersions were diluted with UltraPure™ DNase/RNase-free distilled water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to 2.0 mg/mL for in vivo testing and 1.0 mg/mL for in vitro cell-based assays.

Bulk NC7000® Nanocyl-MWCNTs (Nanocyl SA, Sambreville, Belgium), with a manufacturer-specified average diameter of 9.5 nm and length of 1.5 µm, were dispersed in 10 mg/mL bovine serum albumin solution (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) using a Branson 1510 J-DTH ultrasonic bath (Branson Ultrasonics Corp., Danbury, CT, USA), with an ultrasonic oscillation output of 70 W per transducer at a frequency of approximately 42 kHz. The dispersion was then filtered through a 40-μm nylon mesh cell strainer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to remove agglomerates. Concentration of MWCNTs were measured using UV–vis absorption spectroscopy (600–800 nm, UV-2550 spectrophotometer, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), and the suspensions were diluted to 2.0 mg/mL (hereinafter “MWCNT”).

Physicochemical and biological characterization of NC and MWCNT dispersions

The morphology of the stock dispersions was analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi H-7100, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 75 kV. The diameters and lengths were measured from approximately 200 or 1000 images acquired using a JEM-1010 TEM (JEOL, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 100 kV. Hydrodynamic size of the stock dispersions was determined using DLS with a Zetasizer Pro (Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, UK). The pH and zeta potential of the stock and F-12 K cell culture medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) dispersions were measured using an F-72 M pH meter (HORIBA, Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) and Zetasizer Pro (Malvern Panalytical), respectively.

Microbial contamination (bacterial, fungal, and yeast) was assessed using Petrifilm® Aerobic Count Plates and Rapid Yeast and Mold Count Plates (Neogen Corporation, Lansing, MI, USA). Endotoxin and (1,3)-β-D-glucan levels were quantified using Pyrochrome™ with Glucashield™ Buffer (Associates of Cape Cod, Inc., East Falmouth, MA, USA) and the Endosafe® nexgen-PTS™ system with β-glucan cartridges (Charles River Laboratories International, Inc., Wilmington, MA, USA), respectively.

Intratracheal instillation study in rats

Ethical approval

This study was conducted at Trans Genic, Inc. (Iwata, Japan). The study design was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology, Tsukuba, Japan (approval 2024–0446), and Trans Genic Inc., IWATA Research Institute, Iwata, Shizuoka, Japan (approval 24–0096A). All procedures adhered to the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986, and relevant guidelines, and animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Trans Genic Inc. operated in compliance with AAALAC International standards, as outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, NRC 2011, maintaining full accreditation.

Animals and housing

Male Crl:CD (Sprague–Dawley [SD]) rats (6 weeks old; Jackson Laboratories Japan, Yokohama, Japan) were acclimated for 1 week prior to the start of the study. SD rats were selected owing to their widespread use in toxicological research, well-characterized physiology, and availability of historical control data.

Rats were housed in groups of two to three per plastic cage (W 34.5 × D 40.3 × H 17.7 cm), with bedding (ALPHA-dri™, Shepherd Specialty Papers Inc., Watertown, TN, USA) under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 20–26 °C, humidity: 35–70 %). Rats had ad libitum access to standard rat chow (Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Each cage was equipped with a polycarbonate tunnel (inner diameter: 76 mm; Bio-Serv, Flemington, NJ, USA) for environmental enrichment. Cages, bedding, feeders, and polycarbonate tunnels were cleaned and replaced weekly. Following acclimation, rats were randomly assigned to experimental groups (CNF, CNC, and MWCNT) or to the vehicle control groups (distilled water), with six rats per group.

To minimize hormonal variability and ensure consistency with our previous inhalation toxicity studies (Fujita et al., 2025, Horie et al., 2023), treatments initiated at 7 weeks of age. At this time, body weights ranged 233.2–262.8 g. Although 7-week-old rats are post-pubertal, only male subjects were used in this study to minimize potential confounding effects associated with estrous cycle-related hormonal fluctuations in females. This decision was made to reduce biological variability and ensure consistency with previous inhalation toxicity studies, as discussed in detail in the Discussion section.

Study design

The effects of CNFs, CNCs, and MWCNTs on pulmonary inflammation and systemic immune responses were assessed 28 d post-instillation, a timepoint selected to assess subacute effects and potential recovery following exposure. The study included two CNF-administered groups (CNF1 and CNF2), a CNC-administered group (CNC1), an MWCNT-administered group (MWCNT), and a Control group.

Each rat received an intratracheal instillation of 1.0 mL/kg body weight of the test substance dispersion, corresponding to a dose of 2.0 mg/kg body weight. This dose was selected based on previous findings showing no irregular respiration or mortality (Fujita et al., 2021). In contrast, intratracheal instillation of 5.0 mg/kg CNF1 or CNF2 resulted in irregular respiration and mortality in a few animals (data not shown).

Dispersions were gently agitated and administered intratracheally under isoflurane (Viatris Inc., Tokyo, Japan) using a feeding needle (Natsume Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The needle tip was positioned near, but not in direct contact, with the tracheal carina to ensure accurate placement.

Cyclophosphamide (CP; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used as a positive control for immunophenotyping and administered by forced oral gavage using a plastic syringe and Teflon gastric probe at 5.0 mL/kg for 28 d (day 0–27).

Following instillation, animals were monitored daily, and body weights were recorded at baseline, on days 1, 3, and 7 post instillation, and weekly thereafter until day 28. No unexpected deaths occurred during this period. Macroscopic examination of the lungs was conducted after sacrifice, 28 d after instillation.

Histopathological analysis

Left lungs were fixed in 10 % buffered paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Digital images of each lung section were captured to evaluate histopathological changes in the alveoli, alveolar walls, bronchioles, and blood vessels. Severity scores were assigned to individual animals based on comprehensive histopathological assessment using the following criteria: normal, slight, moderate, or marked. These scores were determined by evaluating the extent and distribution of histopathological changes in comparison to the control group, as we previously described (Fujita et al., 2025).

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis

Animals were euthanized by exsanguination through abdominal aorta transection under deep isoflurane anesthesia, prior to bronchoalveolar lavage. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected from the right lung by instilling 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C, followed by four aspiration cycles. The collected BALF was centrifuged, and supernatants were analyzed for total protein and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity using a multiparameter biochemical autoanalyzer (LAbOSPECT006; Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Total protein was determined by the pyrogallol red method, and LDH activity was measured using the UV method.

Cell pellets were resuspended in PBS for total leukocyte count, including normal and atypical macrophages, neutrophils, lymphocytes, eosinophils, and basophils, using Diff-Quik staining (Sysmex Corporation, Hyogo, Japan).

Pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in BALF were quantified using the MILLIPLEX® Rat Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel (Merck KGaA), the Rat Kidney Toxicity Magnetic Bead Panel 1, and a Luminex 200 System (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA). The analyzed cytokines included interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α), macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-18 (IL-18), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), and secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1, also known as osteopontin), selected because of their critical roles in lung inflammation.

TEM imaging of NCs and MWCNTs in alveolar macrophages

BALF samples were fixed in 1.25 % (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 30 min at 4°C, post-fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 2h, dehydrated using a graded ethanol series, and embedded in Epon 812 (TAAB Laboratories Equipment Ltd., Aldermaston, UK). Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were collected on copper grids and double stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The intracellular distribution of NCs and MWCNTs in alveolar macrophages was analyzed using TEM (H-7100, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 75 kV.

Lymphocyte immunophenotyping

Spleen and thymus samples were collected at necropsy and processed into single-cell suspensions using 50 mL of Hank’s solution and a 70-µm cell strainer. The number of nucleated cells (×103/mm3) in the suspensions was determined, using the white blood cell count as the total nucleated cell count, assessed in a hematology analyzer (ADVIA 120, Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany).

Cell suspensions were stained with different antibodies: PE-labeled anti-rat CD3 (clone: 1F4; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), RB780-labeled anti-rat CD4 (Clone: OX-38, BD Pharmingen™, Becton, Dickinson and Company), Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rat CD8a antibody (Clone: OX8, BD Pharmingen), BV421-conjugated anti-rat CD45RA (Clone: OX33, BD Pharmingen™) and BV711-conjugated anti-rat NKR-P1A (clone 10/78; BD Pharmingen™). Samples were then hemolyzed, washed, and fixed using a BD FACS™ Lyse Wash Assistant (Becton, Dickinson and Company) and analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACSLyric™, Becton, Dickinson and Company). Gating strategy and definitions of lymphocyte subsets are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.1–S1.3. These tables provide detailed information on phenotypes, abbreviations, calculation methods, and corresponding immune cell types, serving as a reference for data interpretation.

In vitro cell-based assays

Cell culture and exposure to CNC

CNC stock dispersions (1.0 mg/mL) were diluted tenfold in Ham’s F-12 K (Kaighn’s) culture medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; MP Biomedicals, Inc., Santa Ana, CA, USA), 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (F-12 K + FBS medium). NR8383 rat alveolar macrophages (ATCC® CRL-2192™) were used for in vitro assays because of our know-how from previous studies regarding their responses to exposure to various nanomaterials (CNFs, MWCNTs, SWCNTs, and graphene) on cytotoxicity, cytokine production, ultrastructural changes, and transcriptomic alterations (Fujita et al., 2025, Fujita et al., 2020, Fujita et al., 2015, Fujita et al., 2018). The use of NR8383 enables direct comparison of CNC effects with existing data and provides a reproducible model for pulmonary toxicology.

The maximum CNC concentration of 100 μg/mL was selected for 24h exposure, based on stock preparation feasibility and absence of aggregation nor precipitation, allowing direct comparison with our previous studies using NR8383 cells. Cells were pre-cultured in 75-cm2 cell culture flasks at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95 % air and 5 % CO2 and then seeded in 96-well culture plates (Corning, New York, NY, USA) for viability assays or 6-well plates 3 × 105 cells/mL for other assays (that is, morphological changes, CNC uptake, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, RNA extraction, and DNA microarray analysis). In both cases, before seeding, pre-cultured cells were centrifuged and the original F-12 K + FBS medium was replaced with the CNC dispersions. Cellular effects were assessed at 24 and 48h to evaluate both early and delayed responses. All experiments were conducted using four technical replicates (n = 4), measured in quadruplicate wells within a single plate using the same sample under identical conditions.

Cell viability

The WST-1-based assay with 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)–2H-tetrazolium monosodium salt (WST-1; Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) was used to evaluate mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity. Briefly, cells were treated with CNC medium dispersions in 96-well culture plates (in quadruplicate) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 or 48h. Following incubation, WST-1 was added to each well at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio, and cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 90–180 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Model 680 microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Data were normalized to control cells. Mitomycin C (100 μM; Merck KGaA) and pyocyanin (100 μM; Merck KGaA) were used as positive controls.

Morphological changes and CNC uptake

NR8383 cells were exposed to 100 μg/mL CNC for 24h, then fixed with 1.25 % (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 1h at 4 °C, followed by secondary fixation with 1 % osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 2h. Following fixation, cells were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in Epon 812 (TAAB Laboratories Equipment Ltd., Aldermaston, UK), sectioned into 70 nm thick sections, and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Morphology and CNC uptake were examined using TEM (75 kV, H-7100, Hitachi).

Pro-inflammatory cytokines

The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), IL-1α, IL-1β, interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-18, MCP-1, MIP-1α, and TNFα, were measured in NR8383 cell suspensions. Cytokine levels were assessed in quadruplicate using the MILLIPLEX® Rat Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel (Merck KGaA) and quantified with a Luminex 200 System (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX USA).

RNA extraction and DNA microarray experiments

Total RNA from CNC-exposed cells was extracted using the QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively. Cyanine-3-labeled complementary RNA (cRNA) was synthesized using a Low Input Quick Amp Labeling Kit (Agilent Technologies), purified with a RNeasy column (Qiagen), and hybridized to a 4 × 44 K G2519F#14879 Whole Rat Genome Oligo Microarray for 17h at 65 °C. Following hybridization, microarray slides were washed following the manufacturer’s instructions and scanned at 5 μm resolution using a G2565BA DNA Microarray Scanner (Agilent Technologies). Scanned images were analyzed using Agilent Feature Extraction ver. 12.0.3.1 (Agilent Technologies).

Microarray data analysis

Normalized data were analyzed using GeneSpring GX ver. 14.9.1 (Agilent Technologies). Log-fold changes (FCs) were calculated relative to vehicle control samples. Genes with log FC values > 1 were considered upregulated, whereas those with log FC values < − 1 were considered downregulated. The focus was placed on differentially expressed genes involved in inflammatory response, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. Data are available under the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) accession GSE289408, a dataset that contains the transcriptomic profiles of rat alveolar macrophages exposed to cellulose nanocrystals, resultant from this study. Volcano plots were generated to visualize overall transcriptomic trends for each CNC condition (thresholds set at p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at *p < 0.05 or **p < 0.01. For in vivo assays, quantitative variables (body weight, BALF cell count, and BALF biochemical test values) were first assessed for variance equality using a two-tailed F-test at a 5 % significance level. Depending on the outcome, either a Student’s t-test (for equal variance) or a Welch’s t-test (for unequal variances) was performed for comparisons.

Results

Characterization of test materials

TEM analysis (Fig. 1) revealed distinct morphological differences among the nanomaterials. CNFs exhibited a fibrillar structure with a high aspect (length-to-diameter) ratio (Fig. 1, A1 and B1), whereas CNCs appeared as shorter fibrous crystals (Fig. 1, C1, D1, and E1). MWCNTs exhibited a curved, multi-walled tubular morphology, distinguishing them from both CNFs and CNCs (Fig. 1, F1). Based on geometric mean values, aspect ratios were approximately 78.0 for both CNF1 and CNF2, reflecting similarly elongated fibrillar structures despite differences in diameter and length. In contrast, CNC1, CNC2, and CNC3 exhibited lower aspect ratios of 30.6, 22.4, and 25.5, respectively, whereas MWCNTs showed an aspect ratio of approximately 89.7.

Hydrodynamic size analysis revealed significant differences in dispersion behavior. CNF2 showed the highest hydrodynamic diameter (1804.0 nm), which is indicative of significant fiber aggregation, whereas CNC1 and CNC2 remained well-dispersed (136.8 and 134.7 nm, respectively). CNC3 (desulfurized) exhibited a moderately increased hydrodynamic size (248.0 nm), potentially caused by altered surface interactions. MWCNTs displayed an intermediate hydrodynamic diameter (213.1 nm).

Zeta potential analysis indicated that both CNFs and CNCs possessed highly negative surface charges, which is indicative of good colloidal stability. CNF1 (−63.2 mV) exhibited a higher negative charge than CNF2 (−21.1 mV), possibly because of the introduction of carboxylate by TEMPO oxidation. In addition, CNC1 and CNC2 exhibited strong negative charges (−50.5 mV and − 51.1 mV, respectively), whereas CNC3 exhibited a lower negative potential (−38.9 mV) following desulfurization. MWCNTs displayed a lower zeta potential (−28.3 mV), indicating reduced dispersion stability. In the CNC medium suspension (F-12 K), CNCs show a reduced negative charge (−14.1 to − 16.0 mV), likely deriving from interactions with medium components.

Regarding pH, CNFs showed mildly acidic pH values (6.6 for CNF1 and 6.2 for CNF2), whereas CNCs exhibited more acidic conditions (pH 5.0–5.6). MWCNTs displayed a nearly neutral pH of 7.1. In the CNC medium suspension (F-12 K), the pH was acquired to a more basic value of 8.1, reflecting the buffering capacity of the medium.

Bacterial contamination was detected in CNF2 (82 culture-forming units [CFU]/mL), CNC2 (1.3 × 103 CFU/mL), and CNC3 (6.0 × 103 CFU/mL), with CNC3 exhibiting the highest contamination, including fungal and yeast cells (43 CFU/mL). No contamination was detected in CNF1 and CNC1. Endotoxin levels were the highest in CNF2 (171.4 EU/mL), followed by CNF1 (99.9 EU/mL), whereas CNCs exhibited lower levels (2.9–37.2 EU/mL). In addition, (1,3)-β-D-glucan, a marker of fungal and bacterial cell wall components, was highly detected in CNF2 (191,905 pg/mL), whereas CNCs contained negligible amounts (< 1000 pg/mL). Importantly, no viable bacterial, fungal, or yeast cells were detected in CNC1, CNC2, or CNC3 groups after incubation in the antibiotic-supplemented cell culture medium (100 units/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin).

General condition of animals following NC instillation

Respiratory irregularities were observed in one of the six animals in the CNF2 group immediately after instillation (upon awakening from anesthesia); however, the animal fully recovered within 24h.

Body weight was monitored over 28-d period (Table 2) in all experimental groups. Body weight progressively increased over time in all groups. However, the CNF2 group showed a statistically significant transient decrease in body weight on day 1 post-instillation compared with the Control group (*p < 0.05). This difference resolved and body weight was then comparable across groups. No significant differences in body weight were observed at any time point for the CNF1, CNC1, or MWCNT groups.

At day 28 post-instillation, the weights of total and left lung did not significantly differ among groups. Analysis of BALF collected 28 d post-instillation revealed group-specific differences in total protein and LDH levels (Fig. 5). Total protein levels, an indicator of alveolar-capillary barrier integrity, were significantly higher in the CNF1 group compared to the Control (**p < 0.01). The MWCNT group showed an even more notable increase compared to the Control (##p < 0.01), whereas no significant changes were observed in the CNF2 and CNC1 groups.

MWCNT-treated alveolar macrophages exhibited a more notable response, with substantial intracellular deposition of the test substances (Fig. 8, A5). Furthermore, macrophages appeared to be highly activated, with abundant number of cytoplasmic inclusions and irregular cell morphology, revealing a stronger inflammatory or phagocytic response compared to the NC groups.

Effects of CNCs exposure on cell viability

Cell viability of NR8383 cells was assessed following 24 and 48h of exposure to CNCs (Fig. 10). At 24h, cell viability remained close to or slightly above 100 % for most CNC treatments, showing no alterations in viability. Slight reductions were observed with 100 µg/mL CNC1 (91.2 %) and 100 µg/mL CNC3 (89.8 %), whereas exposure to 10 µg/mL CNC2 increased cell viability (111.8 %). After 48h, cell viability remained stable for most conditions, with a slight decrease upon exposure to 100 µg/mL CNC2 (91.3 %) and a significant reduction with 100 µg/mL CNC3 (74.9 %), revealing a time-dependent cytotoxic effect for CNC3. Positive controls, Mitomycin C (5.1 % at 24h and 1.1 % at 48h) and pyocyanin (41.5 % at 24h and 17.8 % at 48h), caused significant reductions in cell viability. These findings indicate that CNCs at lower concentrations have a minimal impact on NR8383 cell viability, whereas high doses of CNC3 induce mild-to-moderate cytotoxicity.

Inflammatory response genes, including Arg1, Ccl2, Ccl3, Ccl4, Ccl5, Ccl7, Ccl9, Ccl22, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, Il1rn, Nos2, and Tnf, showed varying levels of upregulation, with CNC2 generally promoting the strongest effects, especially on Ccl5 and Ccl12. In addition, genes associated with oxidative stress and apoptosis, such as Gpx1, Gpx3, Hmox1, Sod1, Sod2, Casp1, and Serpine1, showed differential responses to CNCs, with CNC2 strongly upregulating Hmox1, Serpine1, and Sod2. Overall, the exposure to CNC1, CNC2, and CNC3 modulated the expression of genes involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and ECM degradation, with CNC2 eliciting the most robust transcriptional response.

Discussion

Comparative pulmonary inflammatory responses among NC types

CNF1 induces a mild inflammatory response

The severity of inflammation appeared to correlate with fiber dimensions and aggregation behavior. Exposure to CNF1 promoted a mild but notable increase in alveolar macrophages and infiltration of inflammatory cells. BALF analysis revealed elevated levels of total protein and LDH, which is indicative of mild alveolar damage. These findings reveal that CNF1, which is composed of finely fibrillated and well-dispersed fibers, was efficiently phagocytosed by macrophages, while still eliciting a measurable immune response. Cytokine analysis further revealed elevated levels of MIP-1α and IL-18 in CNF1-treated animals, supporting cytokine-mediated immune activation. These observations are consistent with previous findings (Fujita et al., 2021, Fujita et al., 2025), which showed that TEMPO-oxidized CNFs tend to deposit in alveolar regions and induce transient, mild inflammation.

CNF2 promotes granuloma formation and limited inflammation

In contrast, CNF2 exposure resulted in granuloma formation and fiber deposition in the terminal bronchioles, without significant changes in BALF inflammatory markers. These granulomas likely represent a defensive mechanism to sequester biopersistent fibers, preventing their migration into surrounding tissues, consistent with previous observations for MWCNTs or single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) (Fujita et al., 2015, Porter et al., 2020, Fujita et al., 2015, Fujita et al., 2016, Fujita et al., 2022). The persistence of coarser, aggregated CNFs in lung tissue appears to drive localized granulomatous inflammation. However, granuloma formation in the terminal bronchioles may obstruct airflow and impair gas exchange in downstream alveolar regions. Chronic granulomatous inflammation may also contribute to long-term structural remodeling and compromised respiratory function.

CNC1 induces more significant pulmonary immune responses

Histopathological evaluation supported these findings: lungs from CNC1-treated rats showed more extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in alveolar spaces and walls, as well as in perivascular and peribronchiolar regions than CNF2. The relatively short length and crystalline structure of CNC1 are likely to enhance its uptake by alveolar macrophages and promote the activation of inflammatory pathways (Yanamala et al., 2014, Joseph et al., 2021). Cytokine analysis showed elevated MIP-1α and IL-18 levels in CNC1-treated animals, whereas IL-1β was increased only in the CNC1 group, indicating stronger cytokine-mediated immune activation by CNCs.

Despite these histological differences, BALF inflammatory markers, including total protein, total leukocyte counts (including neutrophils and macrophages), and selected cytokines such as IL-1α and TNF-α, did not significantly differ between CNC1 and CNF2, indicating a divergence between BALF biomarkers and tissue-level histopathological changes. Although BALF analysis provides valuable insight into pulmonary responses, it may not fully reflect localized or cell-specific effects evident in lung tissue. Therefore, a combined assessment using both BALF biomarkers and histopathological analysis is crucial to accurately evaluate the pulmonary effects of nanomaterials.

NCs elicit distinct pulmonary inflammatory responses

CNF1 and CNC1 induced comparable alterations in BALF inflammatory markers, although CNC1 was associated with more significant histopathological changes. CNF2 exposure resulted in granulomas with minimal non-significant changes in BALF markers, revealing weaker pulmonary inflammatory effects compared to CNC1 and CNF1. These findings indicate that fiber size and deposition patterns directly influence pulmonary responses and may determine both the location and severity of inflammation.

CNF2 fibers, mechanically fibrillated and coarser than TEMPO-oxidized CNFs, preferentially deposited in bronchiolar regions, limiting alveolar exposure and associated inflammation. Histopathological findings confirmed minimal CNF deposition in alveolar macrophages, consistent granuloma formation, and negligible alterations in BALF inflammatory markers.

Although CNF1 and CNF2 have similar aspect ratios, they elicited distinct inflammatory responses. The smaller diameter (14.1 nm), shorter length (1.1 µm), and more negative zeta potential, resulting from TEMPO-mediated carboxylation of CNF1, potentially enhanced colloidal stability, dispersion, and alveolar distribution. In contrast, the larger and longer CNF2 fibers aggregated more and localized primarily in terminal bronchioles.

These findings indicate that the biological activity of NC depends on aspect ratio as well as fiber dimensions, aggregation, deposition site, and the efficiency of macrophage-mediated clearance. These factors likely contribute to their distinct pulmonary distribution patterns and biological effects.

MWCNTs induce more significant inflammatory responses than NCs

In vitro and in vivo studies have reported that various carbon-based nanomaterials, including MWCNTs, SWCNTs, submicron-diameter carbon fibers, and exfoliated graphene, elicit diverse biological responses depending largely on their physical properties such as shape, diameter, and length (Fujita et al., 2020, Fujita et al., 2015, Fujita et al., 2018, Fujita et al., 2016, Fujita et al., 2022, Honda et al., 2017). Among these, MWCNTs used in this study exhibited strong cytotoxicity toward alveolar macrophages and markedly induced transcriptional activation of genes associated with inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and tissue remodeling (Fujita et al., 2020).

Compared to MWCNTs, CNFs, and CNCs elicited milder inflammatory responses, as evidenced by lower lung weights, reduced BALF total protein levels, LDH activity, leukocyte counts, and cytokine concentrations (IL-1α, IL-1β, MIP-1α, MCP-1, and SPP1), as well as diminished tissue infiltration.

These differences may be attributed to the lower rigidity, distinct aspect ratios, and surface chemical properties of CNFs and CNCs relative to MWCNTs. Such factors likely influence their interactions with pulmonary cells and the extent of inflammatory activation. Although NCs may be classified as nanomaterials with relatively low pulmonary toxicity, their poor biodegradability reveals the potential for prolonged retention in lung tissue (Fujita et al., 2025). Therefore, long-term exposure studies and mechanistic investigations are required to establish a comprehensive safety profile. In addition, these insights are critical for guiding the safe-by-design development of next-generation nanomaterials.

Immunotoxicity assessment of NC

Lymphocyte subset analysis showed no significant systemic immunotoxicity from CNFs or CNCs, with only a modest increase in NK cells in the CNF1 group. These findings indicate minimal immune system disruption, further supporting their relatively low immunotoxicity. However, the short observation period (28 d) and single-dose administration may not fully recapitulate cumulative exposure, which may limit understanding of long-term effects. Future studies should consider longer observation periods or repeated dosing to better assess the potential long-term immunotoxic effects.

Cytotoxicity and cellular responses of CNCs

Cytotoxicity profile of CNCs

CNCs exhibited minimal cytotoxicity toward NR8383 cells at lower concentrations, with cell viability remaining close to or slightly above 100 % after 24h of exposure. Slight reductions were observed with 100 µg/mL CNC1 and CNC3, whereas 10 µg/mL CNC2 increased cell viability. After 48h, cell viability remained stable for most conditions, although a significant reduction was observed with 100 µg/mL CNC3, revealing a time- and dose-dependent cytotoxic effect specific to CNC3 at higher concentrations. These findings indicate that CNCs generally have low cytotoxicity; however, CNC3 may induce mild-to-moderate cytotoxicity under prolonged and high-dose exposure.

Intracellular localization of CNCs

TEM revealed that CNC1 localized within vacuole-like compartments in NR8383 cells, consistent with our previous findings that CNFs bind to recognition receptors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and scavenger receptors, and are subsequently internalized via endocytosis or phagocytosis (Fujita et al., 2025). Therefore, the localization of CNC1 within vacuole-like structures indicating that its uptake may occur through similar mechanisms. Furthermore, similar intracellular localization was observed in alveolar macrophages isolated from rats exposed to CNCs, revealing similar in vitro and in vivo uptake mechanisms.

Gene expression changes

CNC exposure upregulated genes associated with inflammation response (Ccl5 and Ccl12), oxidative stress (Hmox1 and Sod2), apoptosis (Casp1), and ECM degradation (Mmp3 and Mmp9). Among the tested materials, CNC2 elicited the most notable gene expression changes, indicating a higher potential for triggering inflammatory and oxidative stress responses. In addition, these findings indicate that oxidative stress may be a key mechanism underlying CNC-induced cytotoxicity. However, direct measurements of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative damage markers, such as lipid peroxidation and protein carbonylation, were not conducted. Future studies incorporating these assays are essential to elucidate how gene expression changes translate into tissue-level damage.

Influence of surface chemistry on cytotoxicity

The effects of CNCs are strongly influenced by surface chemistry and functional groups resulting from their manufacturing processes. CNC1 represents a standard CNC, whereas CNC2 is produced through sulfuric acid hydrolysis and retains sulfonic acid groups, and CNC3 is a desulfurized variant of CNC2 without these groups. Despite having similar dimensions, CNC2 exhibits a highly negative surface charge and enhanced colloidal stability, which may promote cellular uptake and elicit stronger inflammatory responses. In contrast, the reduced surface charge of CNC3 may alter its interactions with lung cells. The increased cytokine production and oxidative stress observed in macrophages treated with CNC2 and CNC3 underscore the critical role of surface modifications in cytotoxicity and immune activation. These findings highlight the importance of considering manufacturing-induced surface chemistry when evaluating the inhalation toxicity of CNCs. Further studies measuring zeta potential and surface functionalization are warranted to clarify structure–activity relationships.

Our previous study showed that CNFs with different surface modifications, such as TEMPO oxidation and phosphorylation, elicit distinct macrophage responses (Fujita et al., 2025). In the present study, CNCs similarly exhibited surface chemistry-dependent cytotoxicity and immune activation, thereby reinforcing that physicochemical properties should be characterized to ensure biological safety of NC materials.

Implications for occupational and environmental health

Fiber morphology and pulmonary toxicity

NC materials (CNFs and CNCs) exhibited weaker pulmonary and systemic effects than high-aspect-ratio fibrous nanomaterials such as MWCNTs. Based on the observed biological responses, they are considered to pose relatively low occupational risks when appropriate exposure controls are in place.

These findings underscore the importance of fiber morphology in the pulmonary toxicity of NCs. CNF1, highly fibrillated and composed of fine, well-dispersed structures, induced only mild inflammatory responses. In contrast, CNF2, characterized by lower fibrillation and a bundled morphology, tended to aggregate and deposit in the terminal bronchioles, leading to the formation of localized granulomas. CNC1 induced changes in inflammatory markers in BALF similar to those of CNF1, but triggered more extensive histopathological changes, particularly increased cellular infiltration and BALF pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Surface chemistry modulated the cytotoxic effects of NCs. Compared to the standard CNC1, CNC2, and CNC3 induced greater cytokine production in macrophages and upregulated several genes associated with inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and ECM. These findings confirm the role of surface modifications in immune activation and emphasize the importance of considering manufacturing-related surface chemistry when evaluating the inhalation toxicity of CNCs, supporting case-by-case evaluation.

Endotoxin contamination and its impact

Microbial and endotoxin contamination in CNF and CNC dispersions may elicit inflammatory responses unrelated to the intrinsic toxicity of the materials, acting as confounding factors in toxicity assessments. Gamma irradiation and the use of antimicrobial agents have been shown effective against microbial contamination (Ogura et al., 2025, Sai et al., 2020). However, complete endotoxin removal is challenging since it usually requires methods such as dry heat, which may compromise the physicochemical properties of the materials. Even trace amounts of endotoxins can trigger immune responses, highlighting the need for careful quantifications. However, because of the practical difficulty of obtaining truly endotoxin-free NCs, conducting toxicity evaluations under conditions with trace levels of endotoxins, accompanied by accurate quantification and cautious interpretation, represents a realistic approach.

Previous studies have shown that endotoxin levels in CNF dispersions exert limited effects on inflammatory responses, indicating that biological effects attributable to the material itself can be distinguished from those caused by contamination (Fujita et al., 2025). Furthermore, workplace environmental monitoring data show that airborne CNF concentrations are typically below 2 µg/m3, which is comparable to or lower than the occupational exposure limits established for other nanomaterials such as carbon nanotubes and titanium dioxide nanoparticles (Ogura et al., 2020). Under such low-exposure conditions, the contribution of endotoxins is considered negligible.

When NC dispersions are intended for medical applications or other high-safety products, even trace amounts of endotoxins can activate the immune system, potentially causing inflammation or fever. Therefore, rigorous endotoxin testing and management protocols are essential to ensure biocompatibility and patient safety, particularly for products administered directly into the body, such as injectables or implants. Moreover, to maintain the validity and reproducibility of hazard evaluations, endotoxin control remains a critical issue in future NC toxicity research.

Occupational health management and environmental interactions

Because of the inflammatory potential of certain NC materials, appropriate occupational health measures are essential to ensure worker safety. These measures include engineering controls, personal protective equipment, routine environmental monitoring, and health surveillance of exposed personnel. Although specific occupational exposure limits for NCs have not yet been established, existing guidelines for other nanomaterials, such as those from NIOSH, OSHA, and ECHA, provide a useful regulatory framework (Dahm et al., 2015, OSHA fact Sheet, 2013, Guide, 2021). Our findings support the need for substance-specific exposure limits tailored to the unique properties of NCs.

In addition to direct material effects, potential interactions between NCs and environmental stressors warrant consideration. Previous short-term intratracheal instillation studies demonstrated that MWCNTs may impair recovery from inflammation in a chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) mouse model, whereas CNFs did not exacerbate pulmonary inflammation under similar conditions (Fujita et al., 2023). However, persistent NCs may interact with airborne pollutants, tobacco smoke, ozone, hypoxia, or particle overload, potentially amplifying oxidative stress and inflammatory responses.

To ensure comprehensive risk assessment and effective occupational safety strategies, future studies should incorporate realistic co-exposure scenarios that reflect actual workplace conditions. Understanding how NCs behave in complex environmental contexts will be critical for developing evidence-based guidelines and ensuring the safe implementation of NCs in occupational and consumer settings.

Study limitations and future directions

Although these findings provide important insights into the pulmonary effects of morphologically distinct NCs, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the exposure doses used in this study were substantially higher than estimated occupational levels. Although such doses are useful for identifying potential hazards, they may overestimate actual risks under typical workplace conditions, which involve lower and chronic exposures. Second, a single or short-term exposure model was used in this study, whereas real-world occupational scenarios are more likely to involve repeated or long-term exposures. Therefore, the persistence and cumulative effects of NCs in the lungs warrant further investigation using sub-chronic or chronic exposure models.

These considerations align with our latest review (Fujita and Moriyama, 2025), which emphasized the critical roles of fiber morphology, macrophage-mediated clearance, and the necessity of standardized frameworks for evaluating NC inhalation toxicity. Future studies should incorporate realistic exposure scenarios and account for potential interactions with environmental stressors and biological contaminants to ensure accurate risk assessment and the safe implementation of NCs in occupational and consumer settings.

Sex differences and future female studies

The estrous cycle in female rodents is known to influence pulmonary inflammatory responses through hormonal fluctuations, particularly those involving estrogen and progesterone. These hormones modulate immune cell activity and cytokine production, thereby affecting both the severity and the nature of lung inflammation (Card and Zeldin, 2009). In addition, changes in hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity across estrous cycle stages influence stress-related inflammatory pathways (Power and Iremonger, 2021).

Previous studies have reported that female mice exposed to respirable CNCs exhibited higher pulmonary toxicity than males, suggesting substantial sex-based differences in biological responses (Shvedova et al., 2016). Furthermore, recent findings demonstrate that the estrous cycle phase significantly modulates pulmonary responses to environmental pollutants. Fuentes et al. reported that female mice exposed to ozone during the follicular phase showed markedly higher expression of inflammatory genes and greater airway resistance compared with those exposed during the luteal phase, underscoring the importance of hormonal status in lung inflammation (Fuentes et al., 2019).

In light of these biological variables and to minimize potential confounding effects, the present study included only male subjects. The study design also adhered to the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) in animal research, particularly regarding the reduction of animal numbers, while ensuring scientific validity and consistency with previous inhalation toxicity studies. Nevertheless, given the well-documented sex-based differences in pulmonary and immunotoxic responses, future studies incorporating female subjects will be essential to fully elucidate biological variability and enable a more comprehensive risk assessment of NC materials.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively evaluated the pulmonary and immunological effects of various NC materials, including CNFs and CNCs, with differing morphologies and surface chemistries. Our findings demonstrate that fiber dimensions, aggregation behavior, and surface modifications strongly influence NC localization, inflammatory responses, and cytotoxicity in lung tissue. Well-dispersed CNFs and rigid CNCs generally elicited mild inflammation and immune activation, whereas aggregated or bundled fibers caused localized granuloma formation, prolonged lung retention, and more notable macrophage responses.

In vitro assays using NR8383 alveolar macrophages showed that CNCs generally exhibited low cytotoxicity under 24h exposure at concentrations up to 100 μg/mL. However, CNC3 induced mild to moderate cytotoxicity following 48h exposure at 100 μg/mL, promoting the expression of genes associated with inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and ECM degradation. Surface chemistry emerged as a key determinant of these effects, emphasizing the need for detailed physicochemical characterization in risk assessment. Furthermore, potential interactions with environmental stressors and the presence of endotoxin contamination underscore the complexity of evaluating NC safety under realistic exposure conditions.

Collectively, these results indicate that NCs should not be regarded as a uniform material class; rather, their biological effects should be assessed individually based on physicochemical attributes and potential contaminants. To support safe use in occupational and consumer settings, future studies should incorporate long-term and repeated exposure models, mechanistic investigations, and rigorous endotoxin control. Appropriate occupational safety measures and case-by-case evaluation of each NC variant are essential to mitigate potential health risks, particularly for materials with limited biodegradability and prolonged pulmonary retention.
