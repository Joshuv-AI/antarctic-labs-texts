Endosteal Button Fixation for Latissimus Dorsi Tendon Rupture in a Bodybuilding Athlete
Authors: Jasraj S Raghuwanshi, Jelle P van der List, Nichole M Perry, Alan W Reynolds, Nicholas A Trasolini, Brian R Waterman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Bodybuilders are uniquely at risk of shoulder injuries given training intensity, frequency, and heavy overhead loads through a full arc of motion. Latissimus tears are rare injuries that have increased incidence in overhead and weightlifting athletes, following forceful hyperabduction/adduction during sport-specific activities. Humeral avulsions of the latissimus tendon are treated with endosteal button fixation.Indications:Endosteal button repair is indicated for acute or chronic latissimus tears up to 2 years after injury, in patients who are unable to return to their desired level of competition or activities of daily living. Video Transcript

Weightlifting sports include powerlifting, bodybuilding, and Olympic weightlifting, and these athletes are at risk of unique injuries. Acute latissimus dorsi tendon rupture is a rare yet devastating injury that affects this population. Other concurrent shoulder injuries may further limit their return to sport. We present the case of a bodybuilder who suffered an acute latissimus dorsi tendon rupture, and our best practices for endosteal button fixation.

Background

Latissimus tendon rupture occurs after resisted adduction or hyperabduction. While this injury is rare in the general population, overhead and weightlifting athletes are at increased risk, given their explosive training using provocative overhead movements. Co-occurrence of other shoulder injuries is common in bodybuilders, 9 with rotator cuff tears in 7%. This limits strength and ability to compete, and increases the complexity of surgical management.

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old bodybuilder presented acutely after hearing a pop, with left axillary pain during a pull-up. He also had a remote history of ipsilateral rotator cuff repair and biceps tenodesis, with suspected chronic retear and positive provocative maneuvers. On examination, there was axillary contour asymmetry with a palpable tendon stump.

Imaging

Our patient demonstrated a humeral avulsion of the latissimus tendon with 4 cm of retraction into the axilla, best appreciated on lateral chest wall magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which has a larger field of view than a standard shoulder MRI. 5 There is no critical threshold for tendon retraction, as intraoperative mobilization is the best measure of whether the tendon is amenable to primary repair. The teres major was intact. Notably, the patient also had a massive retracted supraspinatus tear and upper subscapularis tear, with minimal muscle atrophy and cuff tear arthropathy.

Indications

Given that the patient's strength deficit limited him from returning to competitive bodybuilding at the same intensity, we proceeded with endosteal button fixation of the latissimus tendon. Subscapularis repair and biologic tuberoplasty were also performed, given the patient's concurrent rotator cuff injuries.

Technique Description

Patient Positioning

We use the lateral decubitus position with a dynamic limb positioner. Slight adduction takes tension off the lat, and maximal internal rotation rotates the footprint toward the incision.

Incision and Landmarks

Previously, a 2-incision approach was used, but we find that a single 8 to 10–cm curvilinear incision along the posterior axillary fold is sufficient to locate the tendon and deliver it to the footprint. The posterior brachial cutaneous (PBC) nerve crosses the distal incision. The interscalene block is the anesthetic of choice, although this may incompletely cover the skin innervated by the thoracic spinal nerves.8,10 Local anesthetic can also be delivered subcutaneously along the incision. Using a 10-blade followed by electrocautery to complete the incision may decrease the risk of C. acnes infection.

Superficial Dissection

Superficial dissection uses a push-and-spread technique. A fasciocutaneous layer is elevated anteriorly, and traumatic seroma is evacuated, if present. Anterior retractor placement and gentle scraping with a Hurd may be used to facilitate dissection.

Deep Dissection

The interval is between the teres major and the deltoid superficially, with the long head of triceps deeply. Along with the radial nerve, the motor branches to the triceps, the PBC, and the brachial artery can be injured in this approach. The branch to the long head has a common origin with the PBC approximately 2.5 cm proximal and deep to the inferior border of the teres major. 1 An Army- Navy retractor can be used to retract the deltoid and triceps superiorly. Intermuscular adhesions between the latissimus and the teres major are released. The latissimus muscle is followed distally until locating the tendon stump, anterior to the teres major.

Preparing the Latissimus Tendon

Two Alice clamps are used to deliver the tendon. The tendon is sutured in running, locking fashion with 2 multistrand polyethylene suture tapes. Each suture tape should be passed approximately 10 times from opposite ends of the tendon and meet in the middle, allowing for control of tendon rotation. If the teres major is also ruptured, its tendon may be repaired in a side-to-side fashion with the lat tendon. One may also add another anchor. Tendon mobility is assessed. Immobile tendons can be bridged with an Achilles or dermal allograft or reduced to the footprint with a transosseous technique. As long as the footprint can be reached, the latissimus usually stretches to accommodate. Previous studies have demonstrated successful primary repair up to 2 years after injury.

Humeral Footprint Exposure

The dorsoepitrochlearis, a musculofibrous slip connecting the lat tendon and triceps in most patients, must be released to expose the humerus. Other anatomical variants are listed here. 7 The latissimus dorsi insertion is located through a combination of palpation and direct visualization. The radial nerve courses posteriorly over the brachial artery distal to the teres major. Also be cognizant of the axillary nerve and circumflex vessels at the proximal margin of the lat footprint.

Endosteal Button Fixation

The repair bed is prepared with periosteal elevation—care is taken to avoid complete decortication, which could risk fracture. The relationships between the latissimus dorsi, subscapularis, and teres major have previously been characterized in cadaveric studies. We use these measurements to guide the locations of the endosteal buttons. The footprint is 3.5 cm long and should be anterior and overlapped with the teres major and inferior to the subscapularis. 2 We restore the native 90° rotation anteriorly around the teres major, which is believed to play a role in antagonistic stabilization. The turn is clockwise in the right shoulder and counterclockwise in the left. The anterior fibers become the superior, and the posterior fibers become the inferior. A 3.2-mm bit is used to drill 2 unicortical pilot holes—1 at each end of the footprint. The trajectory is anterior/oblique to avoid breaching the cortex. Drilling the proximal pilot hole first prevents intraosseous bleeding from obscuring the view. The suture tape from the tagged tendon is loaded into the button eyelets, as shown. The tails are clamped, and the process is repeated for the second button. A Bovie may be inserted into the pilot hole as a placeholder to aid in trajectory. The inserter is used to pass the button into the pilot hole. Upon contacting the far cortex, the bevel toggles the button, and the rod at the end should be turned and withdrawn for deployment. The inserter is withdrawn to engage the near cortex, and suture limbs are tested for pull-out. The process is repeated for the second button. Sequential alternating traction to each limb of suture—known as the tension-slide technique—reduces the tendon back to its footprint. Wrapping the suture around a blunt instrument or using an arthroscopic knot pusher may apply further tension. The button is secured with 4 to 6 half-hitches. The final repair is shown. Irrigation and vancomycin powder are applied, and a layered closure is performed.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

To address this patient's rotator cuff pathology, the patient was placed in a beach-chair position, and standard arthroscopic portals were placed with accessory anterolateral and percutaneous portals. Diagnostic arthroscopy revealed a subscapularis tear and a massive retracted supraspinatus tear with a bare greater tuberosity.

Subscapularis Repair

Our approach for subscapularis repair is to examine the footprint, skeletonize the subcoracoid space, and approximate the tendon back to the footprint using knotless anchors. The Lafosse classification is a helpful diagnostic algorithm, based on the width of the tear, which also guides management. The viewer is referred to Denard and Burkart 3 for a comprehensive overview of subscapularis injury classification and repair.

Discussion

Biologic Tuberoplasty

Biologic tuberoplasty is used to treat massive cuff tears and uses an acellular dermal allograft to prevent impingement between a bare greater tuberosity and acromion. It was proposed by Dr Mirzayan after patients with failed superior capsular reconstructions maintained function and pain control if the tuberosity was left covered. Steps involve creating a lateral portal, preparing the greater tuberosity, placing 2 rows of anchors, and directing the graft into position. The viewer is referred to Mirzayan and Bouz 9 for the technique guide.

Postoperative Care

The senior author's (B.R.W.) rehabilitation protocol for latissimus repair is shown with integration of rotator cuff-specific therapy for biologic tuberoplasty (Appendix). The shoulder is immobilized for the first 6 weeks, with pendulum exercises and passive range-of-motion exercises. Active range of motion and strengthening are initiated at 6 weeks, and sport-specific conditioning may begin at 3 months. Lightweight lifting was permitted, followed by a gradual increase in intensity until painless shoulder range of motion and symmetric strength were achieved.

Complications

Here, we list complications addressed in this video. Notably, inadequate tendon mobility or rupture of the myotendinous junction indicates conversion to latissimus tendon reconstruction. Injury to the radial nerve and brachial artery can be avoided by maintaining dissection planes proximal and lateral to the neurovascular bundle. The shoulder should also be maximally internally rotated to turn the footprint toward the surgeon.

Results

Lat repair has demonstrated excellent patient-reported outcomes and return to elite competition for professional overhead athletes.4,6 Even for amateur athletes or nonathletes, positive outcomes have been achieved for patients who are unable to compete at their desired level or complete their activities of daily living. At 1-year follow-up, our patient had restoration of the posterior axillary contour and full latissimus strength and was able to return to bodybuilding.
