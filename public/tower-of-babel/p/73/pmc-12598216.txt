Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair Technique Incorporating Remnant Tendon Insertion
Authors: Renato Miyadahira, Andrew S Bi, Arden Shen, Gregory Nicholson, Grant E Garrigues
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRotator cuff repair (RCR) in massive tears often is limited by excessive repair tension and inadequate biological healing. Traditional techniques involve debriding the greater tuberosity and resecting remnant tendon tissue. However, this may eliminate valuable biological and structural elements. This Technical Note describes an arthroscopic approach for large RCR that includes the lateral remnant tendon into a double-row construct. The goal is to reduce repair stress while preserving native enthesis and vascularity. After conventional diagnostic arthroscopy and subacromial decompression, the medial cuff is mobilized and secured to a medialized footprint using double-loaded anchors. The lateral residual tendon is retained and incorporated into the "pants-over-vest" configuration. A lateral-row suture bridge compresses the natural insertion point. Preserving the remaining tendon stump can maintain vascular supply and potentially transfer progenitor cells to the healing site. This approach shortens the tendon-to-footprint distance, allowing for low-tension anatomic restoration. Previous animal and histology studies have demonstrated the therapeutic effects of remnant preservation. This method for transtendinous RCR is biologically beneficial and reduces tension, potentially increasing healing and functional outcomes. Further research is needed to determine its long-term clinical efficacy.

Repair of massive rotator cuff tears has proven to be a challenge for shoulder and elbow surgeons, in particular getting the tendon to heal to its native footprint and to improve the postoperative functional response for patients. It has been well established that there are 2 primary requirements for successful rotator cuff healing in addition to adequate tissue quality; adequate biology/vascularity at the tendon-bone interface and an avoidance of excessive tension on the repair. In response, orthobiologics augmentation, marrow venting with the crimson duvet, and medialization of the tendon footprint have all been used in rotator cuff repair (RCR).1,2 There have been various modern RCR techniques designed to maximize rotator cuff tendon footprint compression, stability, and biology, such as the double-row transosseous-equivalent developed by Park et al.,3 structural or bioinductive patch augmentation, and recently enthesis augmentation.4,5 Often as a part of standard RCR technique, the greater tuberosity insertion is debrided of remnant tendon and decorticated to allow for vascularity and tendon to bone compression.

A less-frequently investigated avenue involves preservation of this remnant tendon insertion as both added (and free) biology, structural support, and enthesis preservation. Huang et al.6 demonstrated the presence of multipotent cells in the remaining tendon attached to the rotator cuff footprint, with the potential for differentiation into chondrocytes and tenocytes, depending on the stress imposed. Su et al.7 demonstrated in a rabbit model that preservation of native enthesis during RCR allowed for superior healing on histologic analysis, and Sun et al.8 demonstrated superior biomechanical properties in a remnant tissue-preserving RCR technique compared to remnant tissue removal, similarly in rabbit models. Applying excessive tension during the repair of a retracted rotator cuff to its footprint may result in failure and must be avoided.9,10 Excessive tension beyond physiological limits can contribute to inadequate biological healing and increased risk of ischemia.11 Preservation and incorporation of the remnant tendon insertion can allow for footprint medialization of the anchors, while incorporating the remnant tendon as the lateral tissue and insertion. Therefore, this technical note demonstrates an arthroscopic double-row RCR in which the remaining tendon insertion is incorporated into the repair, in order to provide a more favorable environment for tissue healing, with an improved blood flow, and a lower tension construct.

Surgical Technique

Preoperative Planning and Patient Positioning

Rotator cuff tears amenable to this technique can be identified on preoperative MRI, with tears more medial than the insertion, with remnant footprint tendon and tear location similar to Cho type II retears (Figs 1 and Fig 2A). The patient is positioned in beach-chair with the upper extremity placed in a pneumatic arm holder (Tenet T-Max Beach Chair and Spider arm positioner; Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN).

Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Subacromial Decompression

A standard posterior viewing portal is created to assess the glenohumeral joint. The long head of the biceps tendon and the glenoid labrum are evaluated and addressed as needed. The articular side of the rotator cuff is examined with the arm forward elevated and externally rotated and tagged with a suture if small. If necessary, an anterior working portal is created through the rotator interval to aid in assessment and treatment of any pathologies. The subacromial space is then examined for bursitis and large subacromial spur. A lateral portal is used as the working portal, allowing for bursectomy and acromioplasty if needed. The rotator cuff tear is then reassessed, using a grasper to evaluate medial tendon mobility and ability to reduce to its footprint, up to the remnant tendon. The medial border of the rotator cuff is mobilized and released from any adhesion so it can reach the lateral stump and its footprint freely. The remnant distal insertion is assessed to ensure adequate tendon quality, preservation of the insertional enthesis, and great care is taken to preserve as much of this tissue as possible.

Rotator Cuff Repair with Lateral Remnant Tendon Incorporation

The lateral remnant tendon is first elevated using a traction suture placed via a suture passer and held with a clamp out of the lateral portal (Fig 2B). An arthroscopic electrocautery device is then used to elevate the articular sided insertion partially off the greater tuberosity. The greater tuberosity footprint is then gently decorticated with a burr, medializing the footprint by 3-4 mm by removing lateral articular cartilage. Two 4.5-mm double loaded suture anchors (HEALIX ADVANCE; Mitek, Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) are then placed along the medial row, and all sutures are passed in mattress fashion using a suture passer (EXPRESSEW, Johnson & Johnson) through the medial rotator cuff, lateral to the musculotendinous junction. These sutures are then tied, reducing the medial tendon the medialized footprint (Fig 2C). The remaining sutures are then passed through the lateral remnant tendon in mattress fashion and tied, creating a “pants over vest” augmented repair. Following this incorporation of the remnant tendon, the sutures are then loaded in a suture-bridge configuration into two 4.75-mm lateral row suture anchors (SwiveLock, Arthrex, Naples, FL) for added footprint compression over the intact tendon enthesis (Fig 2D, Fig 3, and Fig 4).

Postoperative

Postoperatively, the shoulder is immobilized in a sling with a triangular abduction pillow for six weeks, after which rehabilitation with physical therapy begins to recover range of motion. Strengthening begins three months after surgery.

Discussion

Transtendinous rotator cuff tears are defined as tears with the presence of tendon still attached on footprint and with at least 1 cm of length.12 As described by Millett et al.,13 these tears can be classified as the following: type A, tendon on footprint with a short but adequate medial tendon; type B, tendon on footprint with a deficient medial tendon and healthy muscle; and type C, tendon on footprint laterally but with a deficient medial tendon, and retracted, atrophied muscle with fatty infiltration. In this classification, the authors suggest performing tendon-to-tendon repair with a double-row construct for type A, a bridging repair with graft augmentation for type B, and superior capsular reconstruction for type C. For type B and C treatments, they recommend resecting the lateral tendon stump and debriding the greater tuberosity footprint to a bleeding surface, to allow for healing of the graft to greater tuberosity.

Although the aforementioned techniques focus on a tension-free repair using an allograft or xenograft, in contrast, we believe the lateral remnant tendon stump is a better augmentation tissue than the allo/xenograft. The blood supply to the supraspinatus mainly comes from the posterior humeral circumflex artery.14, 15, 16, 17 Because of that, the remnant tendon has a robust vascular supply, creating an environment favorable for healing.18, 19, 20 Huang et al.6 additionally demonstrated the presence of multipotent cells in the remaining tendon attached to the rotator cuff footprint, with the potential for differentiation into chondrocytes and tenocytes. Previously, Bi et al. revealed that precursor tendon cells are present in tendon tissue and constitute 1% to 4% of the total nucleated cell population.19 Therefore, we believe that resecting the remnant tissue unnecessarily removes potential autologous multipotent mesenchymal cells and vascular supply.

Excessive removal of the lateral remnant tissue may also increase the distance of the torn tendon to the bone bed, resulting in increased tension required to achieve an anatomic repair, or the need for a medialized, non-anatomic repair. However, if the remnant tissue is preserved, the distance between the rotator cuff and the reattachment site is reduced, allowing for a lower-tension, and anatomic repair.13,21,22 In addition, the natural tendon enthesis is stronger than tendon-bone interface healing tissue,6, 7, 8,22, 23, 24 and may add benefits to shoulder proprioception, as the remnant tendon has been shown to contain a greater number of mechanoreceptors than scar tissue.25 Further investigation is warranted to explore the potential benefits in preserving the lateral rotator cuff tendon stump in these type A transtendinous rotator cuff tears, since current evidence suggests that its presence creates a more favorable healing environment. Given these potential benefits, we propose a reproducible, familiar, arthroscopic surgical technique to incorporate the lateral remnant tendon as autograft augmentation tissue, with benefits of a lower tension anatomic repair, preservation of native enthesis and vascular supply, and a possible improved functional outcome (Table 2).
