Doppler ultrasound diagnosis of anterior nutcracker syndrome presenting with isolated urinary abnormalities
Authors: Michele Marchini, Matteo Trezzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNutcracker syndrome is a vascular compression disorder caused by entrapment of the left renal vein between the abdominal aorta and the superior mesenteric artery, leading to renal venous hypertension and a spectrum of urinary and pelvic symptoms. We report the case of a 19-year-old male referred for incidental microscopic hematuria and mild proteinuria detected during routine laboratory screening. Ultrasound demonstrated marked dilation of the left renal vein proximal to the aortomesenteric crossing, with focal narrowing at the compression site. Pulse-wave Doppler evaluation revealed significant flow acceleration within the stenotic segment, with a peak velocity of 178 cm/s and an estimated trans-stenotic pressure gradient of 12.8 mmHg. These combined morphological and hemodynamic findings strongly supported the diagnosis of anterior Nutcracker syndrome without the immediate need for additional imaging studies. The patient was managed conservatively because urinary abnormalities were mild and no pain, gross hematuria, or pelvic symptoms were present. This case highlights the pivotal role of Doppler ultrasound as a first-line, noninvasive imaging modality capable of providing both anatomical and functional assessment.Keywords: Nutcracker syndrome, Left renal vein compression, Doppler ultrasound, Proteinuria, Hematuria, Abdominal imaging

Introduction

Nutcracker syndrome is a vascular compression disorder caused by entrapment of the left renal vein between the abdominal aorta and the superior mesenteric artery, leading to increased renal venous pressure and subsequent venous congestion to the left kidney. This hemodynamic alteration may result in a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from isolated urinary abnormalities to flank pain, pelvic congestion, and orthostatic proteinuria. This condition is often overlooked and underdiagnosed due to its nonspecific presentation. Imaging plays a vital role in establishing the diagnosis, with Doppler ultrasound representing a valuable first-line, noninvasive modality capable of demonstrating both anatomical narrowing and hemodynamic impact. We report a case of anterior Nutcracker syndrome presenting with isolated urinary abnormalities in a young male, highlighting key sonographic features and the role of quantitative Doppler assessment.

Case report

A 19-year-old male was referred to our attention following the incidental finding of intermittent microscopic hematuria (150 RBC/µL) and proteinuria (albumin-to-creatinine ratio [ACR] 95 mg/g), detected during routine urinalysis performed together with other laboratory tests for sports eligibility. At clinical evaluation, the patient had no family history of kidney disease, reported normal growth and development, was normotensive, and denied episodes of gross hematuria. His body mass index (BMI) was 17.8 kg/m², and he was not taking any medications. Laboratory investigations showed normal serum creatinine, cystatin C, electrolytes, complete blood count, total protein levels, serum protein electrophoresis, immunoglobulins, and complement levels. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA), extractable nuclear antigens (ENA), and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), was negative. Renal ultrasonography revealed normal renal morphology, with symmetric kidneys and well-preserved cortical thickness for age. Incidentally, a dilated left renal vein (measuring 0.87 cm) was observed (Fig. 1), with focal narrowing (measuring 0.11 cm) at the point where it crosses between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery (Fig. 2). The hilar-to-aortomesenteric diameter ratio was 7.9. Pulse-wave Doppler assessment demonstrated a peak velocity of 178 cm/s at the site of compression, with an estimated pressure gradient (PG) derived from the simplified Bernoulli equation of 12.8 mmHg (Fig. 3). The trans-stenotic pressure gradient was estimated using the simplified Bernoulli equation and should be regarded as a hemodynamic estimate. Peak velocity at the hilar segment was not recorded. Taken together, these findings strongly supported the diagnosis of anterior Nutcracker syndrome. Given the absence of pain, gross hematuria, pelvic symptoms, or other clinical manifestations beyond mild urinary abnormalities, a conservative ``wait-and-see'' approach was adopted with periodic monitoring through blood and urine tests, and the patient was advised to gain weight. At twelve-month follow-up, renal function remained normal and the mild urinary abnormalities persisted without the development of symptoms.

Discussion

Nutcracker syndrome (NCS) is characterized by extrinsic compression of the left renal vein (LRV), most commonly between the abdominal aorta and the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), resulting in renal venous hypertension and collateral formation. This renal venous hypertension may lead to urinary abnormalities, most commonly microscopic hematuria and proteinuria. A posterior variant of nutcracker syndrome has also been described, in which, due to an anatomical variation, the left renal vein passes between the aorta and the lumbar spine and may be compressed at this level, producing clinical and laboratory features similar to those of the classic anterior form. The condition was first described in detail in the radiologic literature as a vascular entrapment phenomenon responsible for hematuria and flank pain [1,2]. The pathophysiology of NCS is related to elevated venous pressure within the LRV, which may lead to rupture of thin-walled septal and fornical veins, explaining the presence of microscopic or gross hematuria. Venous congestion may also alter glomerular hemodynamics, contributing to orthostatic or persistent proteinuria [2,3]. Clinical manifestations range from incidental urinary abnormalities to severe pelvic congestion syndrome, varicocele, or debilitating flank pain, reflecting the broad hemodynamic spectrum of the disease [3]. The distinction between Nutcracker phenomenon and Nutcracker syndrome remains controversial. In the present case, the presence of microscopic hematuria and proteinuria suggested a clinically relevant form of LRV compression despite the absence of pain, gross hematuria, or pelvic symptoms. Imaging plays a central role in diagnosis. While computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) allow detailed evaluation of the aortomesenteric angle and collateral pathways, Doppler ultrasound is widely considered the first-line diagnostic modality because it is noninvasive, readily available, and capable of providing both anatomical and hemodynamic information [1,3]. Typical sonographic findings include marked dilation of the LRV proximal to the compression site, focal narrowing at the aortomesenteric portion, and increased peak systolic velocity at the stenotic segment. Several Doppler criteria have been proposed, including a significant ratio between prestenotic and stenotic diameters and elevated peak velocity ratios, which strengthen diagnostic confidence [1,3]. In our case, Doppler ultrasound clearly demonstrated both morphological compression and substantial flow acceleration, allowing estimation of a trans-stenotic pressure gradient. Although not currently included among standard diagnostic criteria, Doppler-derived pressure estimates may provide additional functional information regarding the hemodynamic significance of LRV compression. The integration of structural and hemodynamic findings provided strong evidence supporting the diagnosis without the need for further invasive studies. Management strategies depend on symptom severity and patient age. Conservative treatment is generally recommended in asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic young patients, as spontaneous improvement may occur with weight gain and changes in retroperitoneal fat distribution [2,3]. Endovascular stenting or surgical interventions are reserved for patients with persistent, severe, or disabling symptoms [3]. This case highlights the importance of Doppler ultrasound as a comprehensive diagnostic tool in suspected NCS, particularly in young individuals with incidental urinary abnormalities, where early recognition may prevent unnecessary invasive investigations.

Conclusions

This case underscores the central role of Doppler ultrasound in the diagnosis of Nutcracker syndrome by providing simultaneous anatomical visualization and hemodynamic evaluation. Recognition of characteristic sonographic features, including left renal vein dilation, focal compression, and flow acceleration, allows prompt noninvasive diagnostic assessment. Early identification through ultrasound is essential to guide appropriate management, avoid unnecessary invasive investigations, and ensure adequate follow-up.
