Author Response: Methodological Reflections Regarding the LASSO-derived AAGC Model for In-hospital Mortality Prediction in Sepsis
Authors: Hemant Gulia, Mohit Suhag, Nikhil Bagal, Prity R Deshwal, Sandeep Kumar, Rahul K Joshi
Published: 2026

Response to the Letter to the Editor regarding “A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-derived AAGC model for in-hospital mortality prediction in sepsis incorporating age, APACHE II score, Glasgow coma scale, and creatinine: A prospective study”.

We thank Soni and colleagues for their interest in our study and for their thoughtful methodological comments. We appreciate the opportunity to clarify aspects of our model development process and discuss the limitations inherent in exploratory prediction modeling.

First, regarding the concern that the AAGC model incorporates overlapping information by including age, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and creatinine alongside the APACHE II score, we acknowledge that APACHE II contains components related to age and physiological variables. Our primary objective, however, was predictive modeling rather than causal inference or the estimation of independent biological effects. To identify variables with the greatest predictive contribution, we employed LASSO regression, which is specifically designed to address high-dimensional data and correlated predictors through penalization and variable selection. Despite potential overlap, age, the APACHE II score, GCS, and creatinine were retained by the LASSO procedure using the more conservative λ1se criterion, suggesting that these variables provided incremental predictive information within our study population. Nevertheless, we agree that coefficient interpretation should be approached cautiously in the presence of correlated predictors and have already acknowledged this limitation in the discussion section of our article.

Second, the authors raise concerns regarding the categorization of continuous variables. We agree that preserving continuous variables generally improves statistical efficiency and avoids loss of information. As noted by Harrell and Steyerberg, the categorization of continuous predictors may reduce statistical power and obscure nonlinear relationships. However, clinically meaningful categorization remains common in bedside risk stratification tools when interpretability and ease of implementation are important considerations.1,2 However, it is important to clarify that although categorized versions of age and the APACHE II score were included among the candidate predictors explored during model development, neither variable was retained in the final LASSO-derived AAGC model. The final prediction model incorporated age, the APACHE II score, creatinine, and GCS in their original continuous forms. Therefore, the performance metrics reported for the AAGC model were derived from continuous predictors rather than categorized representations.

Third, regarding the concern that refitting a standard logistic regression model after LASSO selection may reduce the shrinkage benefit of penalized regression, we acknowledge that alternative approaches that retain penalization throughout model development have been proposed. In our study, LASSO was primarily used as a variable selection method to identify the most informative predictors. The use of LASSO for variable selection followed by multivariable logistic regression for model development and nomogram construction is a widely adopted approach in clinical prognostic modeling.3,4 The selected variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to facilitate the construction of a clinically interpretable nomogram and the estimation of predictor effects.

Fourth, concerns were raised regarding the sample size and the number of candidate predictors. Our sample size was prospectively determined using the framework proposed by Riley et al., incorporating anticipated model performance, target shrinkage, and expected mortality prevalence.5 In addition, LASSO regression was selected specifically to reduce model complexity and mitigate overfitting in the presence of multiple candidate variables. Nevertheless, we recognize that the relatively small sample size and single-center design remain important limitations. As stated in our manuscript, the model should be considered exploratory until externally validated in larger multicenter cohorts.

Finally, regarding the inclusion of APACHE-predicted mortality, the purpose of the AAGC model was to develop a parsimonious prognostic tool rather than to estimate the independent causal effects of individual predictors. In predictive modeling, correlated predictors may coexist within a model provided they contribute to overall discrimination and calibration.1,2 Therefore, the presence of overlap between the APACHE II score and selected clinical variables should be interpreted primarily in the context of prediction rather than etiologic inference.

We fully agree with the authors that external validation is essential before widespread implementation of the AAGC model. Indeed, our manuscript explicitly acknowledged potential optimism, possible predictor overlap, and limitations related to internal validation. We view the present study as an initial step toward the development of a simplified prediction tool for sepsis mortality and hope that future investigations will further evaluate, refine, and validate its performance across diverse clinical settings.

We thank the authors once again for their valuable comments and for contributing to a constructive scientific discussion regarding prediction modeling in sepsis.

Sincerely,
