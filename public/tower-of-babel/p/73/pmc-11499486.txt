Modernizing Newborn Screening in the Genomic Era: Importance of Health-Related Quality of Life
Authors: Ellen Kim DeLuca, Ann Chen Wu, Kurt D Christensen, Davene R Wright, Jennifer Yeh, Hadley Stevens Smith
Published: 2024

Introduction

Since their establishment in the 1960s, state-based newborn screening programs in the US have expanded the number of screened conditions alongside technological advances. For example, the development of tandem mass spectrometry has allowed for simultaneous testing of many additional conditions from single blood spot samples. The US Department of Health and Human Services Secretary’s Advisory Committee on Heritable Disorders in Newborns and Children (ACHDNC) receives nominations for conditions to be added to the Recommended Uniform Screening Panel (RUSP) and is charged with making recommendations regarding whether a nominated condition should be added. While evidence on benefits and risks of early disease detection is key to the review process, it is often lacking given the rarity of most nominated conditions. The challenges that evidence gaps pose for decision-making are exemplified by the recent prolonged deliberation process regarding the inclusion of Krabbe disease to the RUSP [1], which spanned nearly 15 years [2]. Implementation of newborn population-based genome-scale sequencing, which is currently being researched around the world [3], could enable detection of an even broader spectrum of rare genetic conditions at birth. Consequently, policymakers will face a rapid increase in the number and complexity of decisions that will be exacerbated by the lack of systematic evidence on the value of screening for each potentially detectable condition at birth. Ensuring data collection on patient-centered outcomes, including health-related quality of life (HRQoL), will be key to supporting these decisions moving forward in the genomic era.

When the ACHDNC receives nominations for conditions to be added to the RUSP, a structured review process is conducted to summarize the available evidence on the benefits and harms of newborn screening for nominated conditions [4, 5]. Decision analysis is part of this procedure, and any published evidence on cost-effectiveness of screening that is identified as part of the systematic review process is also evaluated, although published studies are rarely available [6]. A significant challenge to the development of decision analytic and cost-effectiveness models of newborn screening is incorporating HRQoL effects for infants and children with rare genetic diseases, which primarily stems from a lack of data. Without sufficient HRQoL data, the uncertainty of the value of screening for rare diseases is increased, which leads to contentious debates and confusion among policymakers on how to weigh the benefits and harms of screening and prevents this key patient-reported outcome from being considered in the decision process. In this commentary, we discuss the importance of HRQoL data in rare disease populations for newborn screening policy in the genomic era and highlight the need for more data collection that leverages existing infrastructure and the establishment of collaborative partnerships between patient organizations, clinical experts, and outcomes researchers. Such interdisciplinary stakeholder engagement will help ensure that patients’ and their families’ experiences with disease and treatment are accurately and reliably integrated in the policymaking process.

Significance of HRQoL for Newborn-Screened Conditions

There are many ways to operationalize the effectiveness of newborn screening programs. Most newborn-screened conditions are rare, affect multiple organ systems, and have high heterogeneity in presentation of symptoms, making it difficult to meaningfully assess screening and treatment benefits using only traditional clinical markers. HRQoL, “the value assigned to duration of life as modified by impairments, functional states, perceptions, and social opportunities that are influenced by disease, injury, treatment, or policy” [7], is thus arguably one of the most important outcomes for individuals with newborn-screened conditions. The significance of HRQoL for patients with newborn-screened conditions arises from the chronic nature of these conditions, for which clinical interventions are often limited to therapeutic options rather than curing disease. Accordingly, HRQoL serves as a critical measure to understand the disease impacts and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions from the patient’s perspective.

There are various approaches to measuring HRQoL. Preference-based measures are one method and are necessary to calculate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which are a standard metric of health benefit recommended for use in cost-effectiveness analyses [8]. A major advantage of QALYs is that they enable diverse health outcomes to be expressed using a common metric where both losses and gains in HRQoL can be aggregated over time and compared across conditions with varying clinical endpoints. As a result, these measures can be instrumental in evaluating the tradeoffs inherent in newborn screening programs. For example, the central benefit of newborn screening is early diagnosis to enable timely treatment, resulting in HRQoL gains, especially since current treatments are generally most effective when initiated before symptom onset or in early stages of disease. Screening at the population level, however, will inevitably lead to false positives, including individuals who never develop the disease due to reduced penetrance and the identification of asymptomatic individuals with genetic variants of unknown significance or genotypes suggestive of later-onset forms of disease. Research has shown that these false or inconclusive screening outcomes can result in HRQoL losses for patients and their families [9]. Utilizing preference-based HRQoL data can help quantify such tradeoffs and promote transparency regarding the tradeoffs under consideration to ultimately enhance our understanding of more comprehensive population-level impacts of newborn screening decisions.

Another advantage of preference-based HRQoL measures is their ability to combine health outcomes across populations. This is particularly important within the framework of newborn screening given the familial nature of rare genetic diseases, which can have extensive implications for the health and well-being of both affected individuals and their family members. For example, newborn screening not only identifies infants with a condition, but can also lead to identifying at-risk relatives via cascade testing. Family members may also experience HRQoL spillover effects from the child’s illness [10]. Burden from caregiving may impact parents’ own HRQoL. Non-caregiving family members may also experience emotional stress, guilt, and grief [11]. Positive family spillover effects are also possible, though less frequently assessed. Preference-based HRQoL measures allow for these family-level outcomes to be considered alongside the individual patient’s experience in decision analytic models.

Valuing health is complex, thus there are limitations to existing preference-based HRQoL measures. For example, “generic” measures, such as the EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) [12], are often used to estimate health utilities to calculate QALYs. It is generally assumed that these instruments are applicable to all interventions and patient populations. However, generic instruments have been found to be inappropriate or insensitive to use with certain medical conditions [13]. Moreover, most existing generic instruments were primarily developed for adult populations, thus may be less applicable to pediatric conditions. In such instances, alternative techniques may be appropriate, including condition-specific measures or direct valuation of health states using vignettes. However, condition-specific measures are currently available for only a limited number of diseases, and utilizing direct valuation methods may be less feasible given that they are cognitively burdensome, resource-intensive, and may lead to loss of comparability across studies due to the wide variability in methods [14]. There are also alternative approaches to QALYs, including disability-adjusted life years, equity weighting, healthy years in total, and equal value of life years gained [15–17], which may be feasible and/or potentially more suitable depending on context. However, further research is needed to validate these approaches, particularly in children and rare disease populations. Despite these challenges, the importance of accurately measuring HRQoL remains critically important for keeping patient-centered outcomes at the forefront of newborn screening policy and clinical practice. Continued research and innovation to refine existing instruments and to develop new, context-specific tools will help ensure that the unique health states associated with newborn-screened conditions are captured effectively.

Addressing the HRQoL Data Gaps

As mentioned previously, there are numerous challenges associated with measuring HRQoL in children and families with newborn-screened conditions. There are special considerations for children that pose a number of additional challenges when valuing child health compared with valuing adult health. For example, young and cognitively impaired children are unable to value health directly, but there are potential biases associated with using proxy respondents [18, 19]. Such methodological challenges have led to a paucity of child-specific preference-based measures and substantial variability in valuation methods, which are key reasons why data gaps exist [18–21]. Nevertheless, there have been recent advancements in the tools and methodologies available to researchers. For example, the EuroQol Toddler and Infant Populations (EQ-TIPS) is a recently developed HRQoL measure for children aged 0–3 years and is one of the very few instruments applicable to infants and young children [22]. The Child Health Utility 9D instrument (CHU-9D) and Health Utilities Index (HUI) are also being validated for children as young as 2 years old [23, 24]. Though preference-based scoring systems are currently not available for the younger age groups in some these measures, future work is planned to fill this gap. The EuroQol 5-Dimension Youth (EQ-5D-Y) is also steadily gaining international prominence, with value sets published for nine countries and more studies underway [25, 26]. A preference-based scoring system for the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), the PedsUtil scoring system, is also currently under development for children 2–18 years [27]. Table 1 summarizes some of these child-specific preference-based measures, and previous reviews provide an overview of available HRQoL measures for children [20, 28–31]. While these measures are subject to further development and testing, they demonstrate the potential to elicit preferences for HRQoL for children of all developmental stages. Relatedly, methodological research on measuring spillover effects is also advancing. Most studies to date have used the EQ-5D to estimate these effects [10]. Although these measures are still being refined, their application can help address the scarcity and comparability of preference-based HRQoL data for current and future newborn-screened conditions.

Ongoing prospective trials of newborn genomic sequencing and pilot newborn screening programs, along with existing disease registries, offer unique opportunities to leverage existing infrastructure to collect preference-based HRQoL data for patients and families with rare genetic conditions for both screened and unscreened cohorts [48–50]. These research activities also provide a chance to gain further insights into the validity of emerging instruments for patients with rare genetic diseases and their families. Registries and prospective studies are especially well-positioned to assess parent/caregiver HRQoL to quantify spillover effects, as parents and/or caregivers are often already engaged in providing patient-level data for their child. Furthermore, collecting HRQoL data would offer a comprehensive and consistent catalog of preference-based values to support economic modeling efforts aimed at evaluating pediatric genomic interventions while research on improving child health valuation methods continues.

Though critical to representing the voices of patients and their family members, HRQoL is oftentimes overlooked as an outcome of interest when measuring the impacts of newborn screening and genomic sequencing. Addressing this oversight and emphasizing the importance of HRQoL data for decision-making requires concerted efforts, particularly through collaborative partnerships between patient organizations, clinical experts, and outcomes researchers. Patient organizations play a vital role in data collection initiatives and research engagement in rare disease communities [51]. Consequently, one possible strategy to foster greater collaborations is by assembling scientific advisory boards (SABs) of rare disease patient organizations with researchers of diverse methodological expertise, as many SABs are predominately limited to clinical experts. Such partnerships would not only advance rare disease research but would also create opportunities for patients to share their perspectives with larger research networks and more broadly influence the healthcare decision-making process. These partnerships would also help to cultivate long-term relationships that are built on trust and respect between researchers and patients [52, 53], which may be essential in the context of collecting preference-based HRQoL data [54]. Another approach is to integrate health economic and outcomes working groups into prospective trials and pilot newborn screening programs [55]. These working groups should strive to engage with each other to improve the harmonization of HRQoL measurement.

Conclusions

Decisions around the expansion of newborn screening programs will only become increasingly difficult with advancements in genomic technology, especially as the original Wilson and Jungner screening criteria [56], which were initially established to guide the selection of conditions suitable for population-based screening, may no longer provide the most relevant guide [57, 58]. The rapid emergence of genome-based technologies, coupled with the evolving landscape of modern medicine and the growing importance of ethical, legal, and social considerations in policymaking, underscores the need for updated approaches. Within this context, policymakers require methods to effectively assess the benefits and harms of screening for many heterogeneous rare genetic diseases. Preference-based HRQoL data are a practical tool for confronting some of these tradeoffs and help capture the multifaceted experiences of patients and families that could be considered alongside other types of evidence. Collaborative partnerships between patient organizations, clinical experts, and outcomes researchers can help facilitate data collection efforts and promote patient-centered research. Investigators leading trials of newborn genomic sequencing and pilot newborn screening programs must also recognize HRQoL data collection as a priority. There is an urgent need to address the scarcity of HRQoL data if we want policymakers to be able to make robust, evidence-based decisions regarding the efficient and equitable implementation of genomics into newborn screening programs.
