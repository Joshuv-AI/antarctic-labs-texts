Splenic Artery Hemorrhage as a Rare Delayed Complication of Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy
Authors: Joshua E. Pagán-Busigó, Ethan Shamsian, Rohan Karkra, Ayyad Mohammed, Kranthi Mandava, Salil Chowdhury, Gokturk Suut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPercutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) is commonly used for long-term enteral access in patients with dysphagia. We report a 67-year-old man with laryngeal carcinoma, prior stroke, and severe dysphagia who developed delayed hemorrhage from a branch of the splenic artery 7 days after PEG placement. He presented with abdominal pain, bleeding through the PEG tube, profound anemia, and hemodynamic instability. Imaging showed hemorrhage in the lesser sac, and angiography confirmed active splenic artery branch bleeding. Endovascular embolization achieved hemostasis. This rare complication is notable for its delayed presentation and favorable outcome after prompt intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) is one of the most frequently performed procedures for establishing long-term enteral access in patients with impaired swallowing and poor oral intake. In the United States alone, approximately 150,000 to 200,000 PEGs are placed annually, and the worldwide numbers are substantially higher.1 Indications for PEG placement primarily include disorders that impair the swallowing mechanism. The most common indications are neurologic dysphagia (such as stroke, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and traumatic brain injury), head and neck malignancies, and conditions in which patients are unable to meet their caloric requirements due to chronic medical illnesses or prolonged critical care needs. PEG is also indicated in cases of anatomical, inflammatory, or functional obstruction of the upper gastrointestinal tract, where adequate oral intake is not feasible.2,3

Although PEG is generally considered safe, it is not without risk. Overall complication rates range from 8% to 30%, with major complications reported in fewer than 3% of cases.4 Common minor complications include peristomal infection, tube leakage, and dislodgement. Major adverse events, although rare, include gastrointestinal perforation, buried bumper syndrome and vascular or visceral injury.5 Intra-abdominal hemorrhage has been reported in fewer than 1% of cases, and bleeding of arterial origin is exceedingly rare but potentially fatal.6 This report presents a case of delayed splenic artery hemorrhage after PEG placement, underscoring the need for clinical vigilance and rapid multidisciplinary management in the setting of unexplained abdominal pain, anemia, or bleeding after PEG.

CASE REPORT

A 67-year-old man with a history of laryngeal carcinoma status postradiotherapy, emphysema, asthma, and prior ischemic stroke presented with a 4-day history of weakness, productive cough, and fever and was found to have sepsis secondary to aspiration pneumonia with bacteremia. After treatment with intravenous antibiotics and fluids, a modified barium swallow demonstrated severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, and a PEG was placed for long-term enteral nutrition. Before PEG placement, hemoglobin was 9.7 g/dL, compared with a baseline of 12.0 g/dL. Platelet count ranged from 250 to 350 ×103/µL and international normalized ratio was 1.1, consistent with the patient's baseline. He was receiving aspirin 81 mg daily and prophylactic enoxaparin, with no history of chronic liver disease or therapeutic anticoagulation. A computed tomography scan obtained on admission before PEG placement showed nonspecific esophageal mural thickening, with no bowel obstruction, free air, or reported vascular abnormality. However, no dedicated preprocedural vascular imaging had been performed.

The morning after PEG placement, he had one self-limited episode of small-volume blood-streaked emesis without recurrence. Over the following week, hemoglobin decreased and fluctuated between 7.5 and 8.5 g/dL before acutely dropping to 5.4 g/dL 7 days after PEG. At that time, he developed diffuse abdominal pain, blood clots in the PEG tube, and profound hypotension. Examination was notable for blood within the PEG tube and tenderness around the insertion site. The abdomen was nondistended with preserved bowel sounds, and mental status remained at baseline. At the time of deterioration, coagulation studies remained at baseline and platelet count was preserved without thrombocytopenia. He required 3 units of packed red blood cells, 1 unit of fresh frozen plasma, intravenous fluids, vasopressor support, and intensive care unit admission. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated intra-abdominal hemorrhage in the lesser sac (Figure 1), and mesenteric angiography showed active bleeding from a branch of the splenic artery (Figure 2), which was successfully embolized. He subsequently stabilized, tolerated enteral feeding, and was discharged after a 16-day hospitalization.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights a rare presentation of delayed hemorrhagic shock after PEG placement, caused by splenic artery branch injury. Although PEG is widely performed and generally safe, vascular complications can be life-threatening and may present days after the procedure. In this patient, CT angiography was essential in the rapid identification of intra-abdominal bleeding, while endovascular intervention successfully repaired the iatrogenic injury. According to the adverse events in gastrointestinal endoscopy classification, this adverse event is most consistent with grade IVa severity because the patient developed hemorrhagic shock requiring vasopressor support and intensive care unit-level care, in addition to radiologic intervention for definitive hemostasis.7

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of delayed hemorrhagic shock from rupture of a splenic artery branch following PEG placement that was successfully managed with angiographic embolization. Previously reported PEG-associated vascular complications have occurred predominantly in older adults and have most often presented as hemoperitoneum or severe hemorrhage with shock, illustrating the potential for clinically significant injury involving both arterial and venous structures.8–16 Reported vascular injuries have involved the gastroepiploic, gastric, superior mesenteric, splenic, and abdominal wall vessels. Across published cases, the timing of hemorrhage has ranged from within the first hour to several days after PEG placement, and management has varied from endoscopic therapy and exploratory laparotomy to transcatheter arterial embolization, with outcomes ranging from successful hemostasis to death. Notably, previously reported splenic artery involvement was associated with early fatal hemorrhage, whereas our patient developed a later presentation from a splenic artery branch injury and survived after prompt recognition, imaging localization, and transcatheter embolization. Clinical presentation may be nonspecific and include hypotension, tachycardia, abdominal pain, intraluminal bleeding, or an abrupt hemoglobin decline. In our case, isolated blood-streaked emesis on postprocedural day 1 was initially felt to represent minor mucosal irritation given its small volume and lack of recurrence, although an early sentinel bleed cannot be completely excluded. This case demonstrates that delayed hemorrhage can occur even 1 week after PEG placement and should remain in the differential when unexplained anemia, abdominal pain, or hemodynamic instability develops. Prompt imaging, resuscitation, and endovascular intervention may be lifesaving in these rare but life-threatening complications.17–19
