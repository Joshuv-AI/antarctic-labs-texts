When enough is enough: Understanding saturation in qualitative research
Authors: H Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite its widespread use in qualitative research, saturation is often improperly stated or loosely used in the methods section and cited as a methodological checkbox to persuade editors and peer reviewers that research methodology is robust. This narrative review sheds light on this multifaceted concept, tracing its origin in grounded theory to its current, diluted applications across qualitative methodologies. Four conceptually distinct forms of saturation are synthesized: theoretical saturation, where researchers can no longer develop category properties due to lack of new data; inductive thematic saturation, where more data yield no new codes or themes; a priori thematic saturation, where sufficient examples exist for predefined categories in an existing theory; and data saturation, where new information merely restates what has already been heard, without offering new perspectives. Empirical evidence indicates saturation is often achieved within 9 to 17 In-Depth Interviews and 4 to 8 Focus Group Discussions, yet these are estimates, not fixed rules, and parameters such as study goals, population, and sampling strategy influence when saturation is reached. Most qualitative researchers stop data collection not because saturation is truly achieved but because of budgetary and time constraints. Saturation should not be regarded as a rigid universal rule but must align with the goals of the research, guiding, not dictating, qualitative inquiry. This review emphasizes defining saturation properly, aligning it with analytical and epistemological methods, and reporting it separately in the protocol and publication for transparency rather than retrofitting the label to satisfy editors and peer reviewers.

Introduction

Qualitative research has emerged as the preferred method for biomedical researchers in recent years. Qualitative research is essential in studies, particularly when dealing with health-related outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, or understanding perspectives, behaviors, and lived experiences. The primary reason for its popularity is the ability of qualitative research to explore and provide deeper insight into real-world problems from a holistic perspective.[1]

Essentially, rather than relying only on numbers or quantitative data, qualitative research uses qualitative data collected through In-Depth Interviews (IDIs) and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs). A skilled qualitative researcher then uses several techniques to record, evaluate, and analyze these IDIs and FGDs to explore and uncover the hidden, deeper meaning or truth, such as ‘how’ the phenomenon occurs, ‘what’ the participant’s perspectives are, and ‘why’ such behavior exists.[2]

On the one hand, quantitative research focuses on sample size determination using various formulae and power analysis, whereas qualitative research relies on data saturation to determine when to cease collecting data through IDIs and FGDs. Despite the frequent use of the data saturation approach in qualitative research, the term saturation is either improperly stated or loosely used in the methods section without a clear grasp of how much data are considered saturated and how to establish whether the data are saturated.[34] Furthermore, ‘data saturation’ is often casually used during manuscript writing to persuade editors and peer reviewers that research methodology is robust.

Through this lens, we want to shed some light on the concept of ‘data saturation’ commonly used in qualitative research.

The origin of saturation: Theoretical sampling and its link to grounded theory

The concept of saturation first appeared in the book “The Discovery of Grounded Theory: Strategies for Qualitative Research” by Barney G. Glaser and Anselm L. Strauss in 1967. The word was employed in the context of grounded theory, in which the primary purpose was to build an empirically grounded theory to explain social processes rather than simply describe them.[5]

Glaser and Strauss coined the term ‘theoretical sampling’ to describe a joint data collection, coding, and analysis procedure to be used in qualitative research, which simultaneously determined what data are missing, what data need to be collected further, and where to look for those data. This approach was used to collect data to develop the theory as it emerges.[5]

In this approach, the researcher switches between data collection and analysis for generating categories and their properties, and sampling is based on the data needs being studied. Thus, when categories appear to be undeveloped, fresh data are sought to fill the gaps. This coordinated procedure offers researchers momentum and purpose. Data collection and analysis occur concurrently as a ‘joint process,’ with the evolving theory controlling the data rather than the researcher determining the numbers. As a result, the procedure is flexible and iterative, with researchers unable to determine at the outset how many participants, new IDIs, or FGDs are required.[5]

Within this framework, theoretical saturation is the moment at which the qualitative researcher can no longer develop the properties of a category due to the lack of new data. In other words, a recurring pattern or stance is repeatedly noticed at this moment. This gives researchers empirical confidence that the category is sufficiently dense, well defined, and saturated to contribute to the emerging theory and that additional data collection will be redundant and unnecessary for the development of the theory.[5]

How other researchers see saturation in qualitative research

With its first appearance in 1967, the concept of saturation had come a long way, with different authors viewing it from different angles but converging on a similar core meaning, such as Sandelowski (2008) viewing it as the point at which information begins to become redundant or repeated, thus naming it ‘information redundancy,’ and Rowlands et al. (2016) as a point of diminishing returns, where data become so saturated that any additional interviews will yield diminishing benefits.[34]

With all of the terminology existing for a single concept, it is critical to have a thorough understanding of the conceptual types and numbers of IDIs and FGDs required before even hoping to reach saturation.

Conceptual types of saturation

The concept of “saturation” is not a single, rigid, all-encompassing idea, but rather a multifaceted concept with various applications, as demonstrated by the subsequent advancement of methodologies by other qualitative researchers.[6]

In 2018, Saunders et al.[6] attempted to clarify the nature, purpose, and use of saturation after observing uncertainty and inconsistencies in the conceptualization of saturation in several qualitative studies. Depending on the degree of inductive and deductive reasoning as well as the relative significance of data collection, analysis, and theorizing, they identified four different approaches to saturation.

They proposed that the operationalization of saturation should align with the research question(s), theoretical position, and analytic framework adopted and identified four distinct models of saturation[6]:

Theoretical saturation

The first model is similar to what Glaser and Strauss demonstrated in 1967; it is concerned with the emergence of theoretical categories in an emerging theory within the framework of grounded theory. Here, the researcher switches between data collection and analysis to develop a category of an emerging theme based on the data requirements for that category. Once the properties of the categories are fully developed to play a meaningful role in the emerging theory, the researcher moves on to the next category and repeats the process.[6]

Here, you gather and analyze the data to create category attributes and then alternate between data collection and analysis to fill in any gaps in the categories until you have a final theory. It is like filling the gaps of a puzzle by moving back and forth.

Inductive thematic saturation

The second model is comparable as well, but it focuses on the inductive method of finding new codes and themes in the qualitative data and determines saturation. As a result, the analysis phase is the main focus, and inductive thematic saturation happens when more data yield no new codes or themes.[6]

Here, you collect data, identify patterns (codes or themes), and gradually keep on adding new codes and themes as you gather data till you reach the end of your research.

A priori thematic saturation

In this case, the qualitative researcher adopts the reverse approach; instead of concentrating on a newly emerging theory or improving upon it, the researcher begins with a pre-existing theory and gathers data to provide examples and prove its validity.[6] Here, you already have a flawless theory; all you need to do is gather data that fit the individual categories of the existing theory in order to demonstrate or validate the theory. Thus, in this case, saturation does not occur when no new codes or themes are found; rather, it occurs when there are sufficient examples for each premade category in an existing theory to provide its validity or evidence.

Data saturation

Finally, there is data saturation, which is the most common and most cited occurrence among qualitative researchers. It simply describes a moment in which any new information in the form of IDIs and FGDs merely restates what has already been heard without offering any new perspectives. Studies with limited time and resources are more likely to cite this type of saturation.[6]

From the existing literature, it is evident that while theoretical saturation, on the one hand, aligns with grounded theory and emerging theorizing, inductive thematic saturation, on the other hand, suits exploratory designs where patterns are allowed to emerge freely from data. Conversely, a priori thematic saturation is most suitable for theory-testing studies where predefined categories guide data collection. Finally, data saturation, which is the most commonly cited type, is best suited to applied or descriptive qualitative work. Researchers should align their choice of saturation types with their epistemological position and analytical framework rather than defaulting to ‘data saturation’ as a generic shortcut. Empirical evidence on when saturation occurs

While understanding the conceptual differences between different forms of saturation is crucial, qualitative researchers also need to know how many IDIs and FGDs are needed before anticipating saturation. Several empirical studies and reviews have addressed these questions.[6]

Guest et al. (2006)[7] conducted a study to determine how many interviews were required to obtain a valid sense of thematic exhaustion and variability in their data set. They studied interviews in batches of six to determine whether six interviews are sufficient to provide as much useful information as 12, 18, 24, or 30 interviews. With a codebook containing 109 codes, they observed that approximately 73% of the original codes were detected within the first batch of six interview transcripts, increasing to 92% in the subsequent six batches of interview transcripts. Thematic discovery was achieved almost entirely, that is, saturation occurred almost entirely within the first 12 interview transcripts, whereas adding further interviews after 12 interviews provided only a marginal benefit of up to 8%. New themes occurred only infrequently after the saturation threshold of 12 interviews. They also determined that for reasonably homogeneous groups and narrowly defined research questions, 12 interviews are typically sufficient to understand perspectives and experiences. However, they also cautioned researchers and the scientific community not to assume that 6 to 12 interviews are always sufficient to achieve a particular research purpose or to justify “quick and dirty” research.

Hennink and Kaiser (2022) conducted a comprehensive assessment of 23 published articles, with 17 studies using empirical data and 6 using statistical models to determine saturation. They discovered that in studies with a homogeneous population and narrowly stated study objectives, saturation is often achieved within a limited range of 9 to 17 interviews, whereas FGDs require 4 to 8 sessions. The paper also highlights a critical gap in empirical studies: there is a lack of information about how parameters such as study goal, nature of the study population, sampling strategy used (i.e. inductive vs. fixed sampling), type of data, saturation goal, and other influences affect the total number of interviews to be conducted.[8]

One of the key components of any research plan, that is, sampling strategy, remains a critical, underexplored determinant of when saturation is reached in qualitative research, for example, maximum variation sampling, which is frequently employed by qualitative researchers to identify and broaden the range of variation or differences in perspectives/opinions. This method involves recruiting participants with a variety of important characteristics such as age, gender, geographic location, or disease status.[910] This approach delays saturation because diverse perspectives and opinions from such populations tend to generate a wider range of initial codes, requiring a greater number of interviews before saturation is reached.

In a similar direction, Squire et al. (2024)[11] carried out a secondary analysis of interview data from five studies funded by the Food and Drug Administration that were conducted via web-based platforms among patients with underlying medical conditions or healthcare providers offering primary or specialty care services to the patients. These studies used a sample size that varied from 30 to 70 interviews. Following data preparation, all five data sets were analyzed using a systematic approach to determine true and near saturation by calculating saturation independently for each study’s data set. Near saturation was defined as “90% of all applied codes being used,” while true saturation was defined as “100% of all applied codes being used.”

In simple terms, the first step included calculating new codes that had never appeared before for each interview. The second step involved adding each code appearance cumulatively (running total) for each interview till all codes were ultimately used. Finally, the interviews were marked when the cumulative total reached either 90% (near saturation) or 100% (true saturation).[11]

The authors found that near saturation was obtained after 33% to 60% (n = 15–23) of planned interviews, while true saturation was attained after 91% to 100% (n = 30–67). Additionally, the authors note that studies using a more structured interview guide or a deductive coding approach reached true and near saturation earlier.[11]

Code versus Meaning saturation: Which requires more interviews?

Hennink, Kaiser, and Marconi (2017) divided the concept of saturation into two approaches: Meaning Saturation and Code Saturation. Twenty-five interviews were subjected to a secondary data analysis to ascertain the point at which code or meaning saturation was attained. While code saturation indicates when researchers have ‘heard it all,’ that is, when no additional issues are identified and the codebook begins to stabilize (i.e. list of codes stop growing, indicating all the basic codes are identified), the meaning saturation is required to ‘understand it all,’ that is, when the author fully comprehends issues and no additional dimensions, nuances, or insights of issues can be found (i.e. it goes beyond the list of codes to full understanding).[12]

To illustrate the distinction between the two concepts, consider a study on the barriers to antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence. Code saturation is reached when the researcher has identified and documented all the barriers such as ‘cost,’ ‘stigma,’ or ‘pill burden,’ and the codebook stabilizes because no new barriers such as ‘ease of accessibility’ are emerging. Meaning saturation, however, extends beyond the identification and documentation of all the barriers to ART. It requires deeper understanding of the psychosocial determinants underlying those barriers, not merely that stigma is a barrier, but how internalized stigma interacts with social support, disclosure fears, and patient–provider relationships to shape adherence behavior.

The authors note that code saturation occurred as early as nine interviews, whereas meaning saturation required 16-25 interviews to completely comprehend and establish a rich, textured picture of these thematic issues. The authors also noted that when considering especially complex or sensitive clinical topics like stigma or end-of-life care, the number of interviews required increases further.[12]

Beyond saturation: Information power as an alternative framework

Malterud, Siersma, and Guassora (2016) proposed “information power,” a more practical substitute for the saturation technique in sample size planning. The authors noted that the idea of saturation is inconsistently defined and implemented in the majority of qualitative studies, despite being strongly tied to a certain methodology. Therefore, they suggested that fewer participants would be required for the study if the sample contained more information pertinent to the actual investigation; this idea is known as information power.[13]

The aim of the study, sample specificity, use of an established theory, dialog quality, and analysis approach are the five elements that determine this sample size’s adequate information power.[13] Each of the elements is described as follows:

The aim of the study

How narrow or broad is your research question? The narrower the study issue, the fewer participants/interviews are required to address it because they have more experience or perspectives to give.[13]

Sample specificity

How well do your participants match your research topic of interest? The more aligned the participants are with the research topic, the richer and more textured information they can provide to gain a deeper knowledge of the issue, requiring fewer participants/interviews.[13]

Use of established theory

This element focuses on where the research starts and whether it commences from an established theory to offer a more structured inquiry format or it starts from scratch without any guidance. Fewer participants and interviews will be required if the research is based on earlier findings, but more participants and interviews will be required if it is starting from scratch in order to develop an understanding of a certain topic or issue.[13]

Quality of dialog

This element deals with the extent and quality of interaction between the participant and researcher. The better the quality and extent of interaction, the fewer participants/interviews are required. Moreover, the probing between the interview will increase the chances of gathering rich and textured information to understand the issue.[13]

Analysis strategy

This element relates to the kind of analysis a researcher wants to do. An in-depth case analysis will require fewer participants.[13]

Therefore, the concept of information power is highly desirable when the qualitative researcher works with fewer but highly selective/specific samples such as when working on research topics such as rare disease, a specific age group, or any marginalized communities where the information provided to each participant will be inherently rich due to their depth of experience in their lived condition or environment.

Critiques and limitations of saturation

O’Reilly and Parker (2012) state that saturation has recently emerged as a “gold standard” or a way to assess the quality of qualitative research. Hence, the majority of studies report saturation in order for the methodology to be deemed robust. The idea of saturation, which first emerged in grounded theory as “theoretical saturation” with a more precise meaning than simply “no new themes appearing,” “new data start repeating,” or “no new fresh codes, categories, and insights start appearing,” is now included in quality criteria checklists to boost the reliability of qualitative research. However, the authors contend that saturation is not appropriate for all qualitative approaches and that researchers have begun to apply the idea in a broader and looser way, that is, ‘unsatisfactory saturation.’[14]

Beyond the issue raised by O’Reilly and Parker, a persistent real-world challenge is that researchers often retrofit saturation claims after the data collection is already complete in order to retrospectively justify a sample size that was determined by researchers’ own discretion or due to logistical constraints rather than methodological rigor. This practice undermines the iterative, concurrent nature of saturation as originally conceived. Saturation, properly applied, is not a post-hoc label but an active, ongoing assessment during data collection and analysis. Treating it as a methodological myth, that is, a box to check for peer reviewers, rather than a practical, reflective tool risks eroding the credibility of qualitative evidence.

Over time, the definition of saturation has evolved, becoming less specific and focusing on more generic thematic/data saturation. Furthermore, the authors argue that while selecting the sample size for their research, researchers should examine the depth of data rather than just the frequency of data (i.e. repetition and no new data appearing). Additionally, the researcher should choose “samples” of participants who are most appropriate for the research topic and who can offer rich, detailed data to gain a thorough grasp of a range of experiences without repetition. Therefore, saturation should not be used generically or as a one-size-fits-all criterion in all qualitative research. Instead, authors encourage researchers to employ quality standards that align with their particular methodology and to provide a detailed explanation of how they selected the sample.[14]

Conclusion

Saturation remains the core concept in qualitative research, yet its origins in grounded theory have become diluted over time. In the current era, the concept of saturation is invoked broadly and loosely under a variety of names, depending on the knowledge and intent of the researcher. This dilution has led to widespread misuse, with researchers retrofitting saturation claims after data collection is complete, or citing it merely as a methodological checkbox rather than a genuine, iterative process. The reality is that most qualitative researchers stop data collection not because saturation is truly achieved but because of budgetary and time constraints, difficulties in finding participants, and tight deadlines. Critics rightly argue that saturation should not be regarded as a rigid universal rule or a benchmark for methodological rigor; rather, it must align with the goals and objectives of the research in question. Alternative frameworks such as information power offer more nuanced and context-specific guidance for sample size determination, and empirical estimates remain just estimates of when to expect saturation, not a strict endpoint for halting data collection. In short, saturation should guide, not dictate, qualitative inquiry.
