Recurrent pulmonary embolism with haemoptysis, a challenging case
Authors: Nilay Tripathi, Jyoti Bajpai, Pravesh Vishwakarma, Sourabh Kumar, Akshyaya Pradhan
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Pulmonary embolism is said to be the third leading cause for cardiovascular mortality (after the myocardial infarction and stroke) and the most common source of pulmonary thrombo-emboli are deep venous plexus of lower extremity or pelvic veins. The acute pulmonary embolism (APE) survivors, if remain inadequately monitored for the thrombolytic treatment, are at risk of developing chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) in almost 20% to 50% patients.[1] In long run the CTEPH lungs will be associated with adaptation to the pulmonary vascular obstruction and very limited studies have reported haemoptysis. We share our experience of successfully managing a patient of haemoptysis during APE on starting anticoagulation.

A 37-year young male reported to emergency department of Cardiology centre with complaints of exertional shortness of breath (NYHA-IV) and unconsciousness. The patient had history of APE and at that time pulmonary embolus was detected in main pulmonary artery (PA), left lower lobe and right upper-middle lobes with bilateral pleural effusion. The patient received 3 months of warfarin therapy, which completely resolved his symptoms.

In emergency, he had tachycardia (heart rate-120 bpm), hypotension (ABP-80/63 mmHg) and hypoxemia (arterial blood PaO2-63.4 mmHg). Electrocardiogram showed right ventricle (RV) strain (S1Q3T3) pattern. Echocardiography report, of dilated pulmonary artery, akinesia of right free wall with apical sparing (McConnells’s sign), dilated right atrium and ventricle without thrombus with poor TAPSE of 7 mm, pulmonary acceleration time (54 msec), suggested severe pulmonary hypertension. Simplified PE severity index was 3 with left leg DVT. The patient was managed by intravenous streptokinase (250,000 units in 30 min) and non-invasive ventilation. His respiration and haemodynamic status (HR-89; ABP-100/70 mmHg) improved. However, patient developed haemoptysis (200 ml) which responded to fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and was discharged on oral apixaban (5 mg 12 hourly).

Next-day patient returned with massive haemoptysis and worsening of dyspnoea. We gave two units of FFP and packed red blood cells. Another remarkable blood test was hyperhomocysteinemia (50 µmol/l). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography thorax revealed non enhancing linear filling defects in right pulmonary artery with extension into its lobar and segmental branches along with segmental branches of left upper and lower lobes suggestive of APE [Figure 1a, b].

CT findings (a) Non enhancing filling defect seen in right pulmonary artery extending to lobar and segmental branches along; (b) Similar filling defects segmental branches of the left and upper lobe; (c) Abnormal parenchymal blush right and left common bronchial artery, left IC artery, and 3,4 Right intercostal artery

To address this after taking informed and written consent under full aseptic precautions right femoral artery access was taken using 5 Fr sheath. Angiogram revealed abnormal blush suggesting breach in right bronchial and left common bronchial, left intercostal artery and right 3rd and 4th intercostal artery [Figure 1c and Video 1]. Coil embolization was conducted in various arteries-common bronchial (2 coils), right intercostal-3 (1 coil) and right intercostal-4 (1 coil), followed by polyvinyl alcohol particle (300 m) embolization [Figure 2a and Video 2]. It corrected abnormal blush in the target vessels [Figure 2b and Video 3].

(a) White arrow pointing the tip of catheter and the coil placed the common bronchial artery and the right 3rd and 4th intercostal arteries; (b) Anormal parenchymal blush got corrected after successful embolization. (c) White arrow pointing position of inferior vena cava filter placed below both the renal veins with intact blood flow

To avoid the recurrence of PE, we also placed inferior vena cava (IVC) filter [Aegisy catheter (6 French); Lifetech scientific products] under full aseptic precaution below the level of renal veins and above common iliac. The blood flow was recorded [Figure 2c and Video 4]. Patient improved gradually without any recurrence of haemoptysis, dyspnoea, or syncope. Three months later IVC filter was carefully removed. Patient is on lifelong anticoagulant therapy.

The incomplete resolution by anticoagulant therapy or recurrences due to hypercoagulation state due to hyperhomocysteinemia in young adult may result in CTEPH and impaired quality of life in long run.[2] It was confirmed by angiography in our patient showing dilated bronchial and intercostal arteries with abnormal blush presenting as haemoptysis. Its embolization was effective in our patient as being recommended treatment of choice.

The secondary adaptive remodelling of small peripheral pulmonary vessels contribute for increased total pulmonary resistance resulting in high proximal PA pressure and reduced post-obstructive PA pressure.[3] The high bronchial arterial pressure in long run is likely to open the pre-existing aberrant broncho-pulmonary arterial connections.[4] In the background of anticoagulants, the haemoptysis in CTEPH is more challenging to manage than the haemoptysis of other lung diseases; bronchiectasis, lung cancer, and tuberculosis. In our patient haemoptysis was in large volume with fresh blood and triggered by starting of anticoagulant to treat PE was more suggestive of non-infective origin well fitting into CTEPH. The IVC filters have shown promising results to protect lungs by trapping venous emboli released during thrombolytic therapy.[5] Our patient too did well on continued oral Apixaban without recurrence after placement of IVC filter.

In summary, our patient management emphasized that the adolescent patient presenting with APE should be investigated for the uncommon high-risk factors of hypercoagulation and the CTEPH features. CT-Angio is very handy to document bronchial to pulmonary dilated connections. IVC filter was also useful to prevent recurrent PE during anticoagulation therapy in DVT patients.
