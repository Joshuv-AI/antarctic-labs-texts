Association between first-trimester chorionic bump and early pregnancy loss: a two-center retrospective study with exploratory radiomics analysis
Authors: Jie Shen, Yipeng Zhao, Zhina Wu, Yizhi Wu, Bishi He, Zhe Xu, Nannan Chen, Junmiao Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe chorionic bump (CB) is a rare sonographic finding defined as an irregular protrusion from the choriodecidual surface into the gestational sac. Its prognostic significance regarding pregnancy loss remains controversial. This study aimed to evaluate the association between first-trimester CB and pregnancy outcomes and to investigate whether ultrasonographic radiomic features can support early risk stratification.MethodsThis two-center retrospective study included 184 women diagnosed with a CB and 1052 matched controls between January 2020 and December 2024. All participants underwent transvaginal ultrasound at 5–10 weeks of gestation. Radiomic features were extracted from regions of interest using PyRadiomics. Furthermore, logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF)—alongside SHAP analysis, were employed to analyze the ultrasonographic radiomic features.ResultsSonographically, CB exhibited variable echogenicity and an absence of internal blood flow on color Doppler flow imaging. While no significant differences were found in other secondary pregnancy outcomes, CB was strongly associated with early pregnancy loss. Following feature selection, the optimized RF_Sel model achieved an AUROC of 0.573, accuracy of 0.538, and a precision of 0.539. SHAP analysis identified “original_firstorder_Minimum” (first-order minimum intensity) as the feature with the highest decision-making value. Furthermore, lower grayscale minimum intensity on ultrasound, smaller gestational sac (GS) volume, and a higher CB-to-GS volume ratio were identified as significant risk factors for pregnancy loss within the CB population.ConclusionsA first-trimester CB is a significant high-risk factor for early pregnancy loss. However, the radiomics-based model demonstrated limited predictive performance, indicating that radiomic features alone are currently insufficient for clinical risk stratification and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Introduction

The chorionic bump (CB) is defined as an irregular, convex protrusion extending from the choriodecidual surface into the gestational sac [1]. Sonographically, it exhibits variable echogenicity, often presenting as a hyperechoic mass or a hypoechoic center with a hyperechoic rim. Crucially, CB lacks internal blood flow, a characteristic that distinguishes it from subchorionic hematomas [2]. This rare finding is detected in approximately 0.15% to 0.70% of first-trimester pregnancies [3, 4]. Although its exact etiology remains unconfirmed, current theories suggest that a CB represents a hematoma within the intervillous space. This hematoma likely expands toward the path of least resistance within the confined chorionic layer, eventually bulging into the gestational sac [4]. Since Harris et al. [1] first characterized the CB in 2006 as a sign of guarded prognosis (with live birth rates below 50%), research interest in its clinical impact has grown. However, the correlation between CB and pregnancy loss remains controversial. Some researchers, such as Sepulveda et al. [5], argue that a CB detected between 11 and 13 weeks is often a transient, incidental finding with minimal clinical significance. Similarly, Arleo et al. [6] suggest that its presence does not inevitably indicate a poor obstetric outcome. Conversely, a majority of studies suggest that a first-trimester CB significantly elevates the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [3, 7], and meta-analyses confirm that first-trimester CB is associated with an increased risk of miscarriage and a reduced live birth rate [8, 9]. Consequently, identifying a CB early in gestation is vital for assessing pregnancy risk and determining the necessity of preventive interventions [10].

Despite growing interest, most existing literature consists of case reports or small series, and no study to date has integrated specific ultrasonographic parameters to predict pregnancy outcomes in CB-affected pregnancies. This study aims to evaluate the association between first-trimester CB and broader pregnancy outcomes, and to investigate whether distinct ultrasonographic characteristics—analyzed through a radiomics-based approach—correlate with the risk of pregnancy loss in patients presenting with a chorionic bump.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This two-center retrospective study analyzed clinical and sonographic records of 184 pregnant women diagnosed with a CB at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University and Rui’an Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. Eligible participants were aged ≥ 18 years with ultrasound-confirmed intrauterine singleton pregnancies (5–10 weeks’ gestation). A control group of 1052 women without CB or subchorionic hematoma (SCH) was selected from all singleton pregnancies examined at both centers during the same period using frequency matching by maternal age (± 2 years) and gestational age at initial ultrasound (± 1 week), at an approximate ratio of 1:6. Exclusion criteria included: (1) concomitant SCH; (2) voluntary termination of pregnancy; (3) systemic comorbid diseases and severe infections or reproductive system malformations; (4) loss to follow-up or incomplete data; (5) multiple pregnancies; and (6) poor ultrasound image quality. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in Tokyo 2004), informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Definitions

The primary outcome was the pregnancy loss rate, defined as any spontaneous termination of pregnancy before 24 weeks of gestation, including embryonic demise and stillbirth. Ongoing pregnancy was defined as fetal survival confirmed by ultrasound beyond 24 weeks of gestation. In our study, all ongoing pregnancies ultimately resulted in live births. Secondary outcomes comprised the rates of full-term and preterm birth, as well as various obstetric morbidities, including placental abruption, placenta previa, oligohydramnios, fetal distress, gestational diabetes (GDM), intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), fetal growth restriction (FGR), and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Furthermore, birth outcomes—specifically the mode of delivery, gestational age at delivery, and birthweight—were assessed. CB was sonographically defined as an irregular protrusion from the choriodecidual surface into the gestational sac. These lesions typically exhibit heterogeneous internal echogenicity (hyperechoic, isoechoic, or mixed) and lack internal vascularity on color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) [11]. Gestational age was determined by the last menstrual period or first-trimester ultrasound per standard guidelines [12]. he volumes of both the CB and the gestational sac (GS) were calculated using the standard ellipsoid formula: volume (cm3) = anteroposterior diameter (cm) × longitudinal diameter (cm) × height (cm) × 0.52. Subsequently, the CB/GS volume ratio was determined by dividing the calculated CB volume by the GS volume.

Instrumentation and procedure

Demographic and clinical data—including maternal age, body mass index (BMI), reproductive history, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), gestational age at the time of examination, and pregnancy outcomes—were retrieved via telephone follow-ups and electronic medical record systems. Two-dimensional ultrasound examinations were performed on all participants presenting with a CB by certified obstetric and gynecologic ultrasonographers. Scans were conducted using a Voluson E8 ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) equipped with a 5–7 MHz transvaginal probe (model BT21). With the patients placed in the lithotomy position, the lesions were comprehensively scanned from multiple angles. The recorded parameters included the location, size (anteroposterior, longitudinal, and height diameters), and echogenicity of the CB, as well as the dimensions of the gestational sac. All obtained images were subsequently stored in the system in DICOM format for further analysis.

Image segmentation and feature extraction

Captured ultrasound images were converted into JPG format and normalized to minimize variability arising from different equipment parameters. Two senior sonographers manually delineated the regions of interest (ROIs) using the X-AnyLabeling software. The segmented ROI images were subsequently imported into the open-source platform PyRadiomics for the extraction of radiomic features (Fig. 1).

Feature selection, model construction, and evaluation

Following Z-score normalization, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was utilized to evaluate the consistency of the manual segmentations, and features with an ICC ≥ 0.8 were retained. Subsequently, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied for dimensionality reduction, and features with non-zero coefficients were selected to form the optimal radiomic feature subset for model development. Three machine learning algorithms—Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF)—were employed to construct predictive models, which were internally validated using 5-fold cross-validation. Among these, the RF algorithm demonstrated optimal performance. Furthermore, an RF_Sel model was developed utilizing the top 30 features that exhibited the highest contribution to the model following feature selection. Diagnostic performance was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. To enhance model interpretability, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis was performed on the final 20 radiomic features to evaluate feature importance.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as the median with the interquartile range (Q1, Q3) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages). Comparisons between groups were performed utilizing Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous data, the Mann–Whitney U test for skewed data, and the Pearson χ² test for categorical variables. The diagnostic performance of the predictive models was evaluated using ROC curves and the Area Under the Curve (AUC). A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics and pregnancy outcomes

A total of 1236 participants were enrolled in this study, comprising 184 pregnant women diagnosed with a chorionic bump (CB group) and 1052 matched controls (non-CB group). The mean age was 30.8 ± 5.4 years in the CB group and 31.0 ± 4.9 years in the non-CB group, while the mean BMI was 22.4 ± 3.4 kg/m² and 22.2 ± 3.5 kg/m², respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding baseline maternal age, BMI, obstetric history, or history of miscarriage (all P > 0.05).

Regarding pregnancy outcomes, the incidence of pregnancy loss was significantly higher in the CB group compared to the non-CB group (93/184 [50.5%] vs. 267/1052[25.4%]; P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were found between the groups across other secondary obstetric and birth outcomes, including preterm birth, FGR, pregnancy-induced hypertension, preeclampsia, GDM, ICP, placenta previa, placental abruption, oligohydramnios, fetal distress, rate of cesarean section, birthweight, and gestational age at delivery (Table 1).

Subgroup analyses

The presence of a CB demonstrated a significant association with an increased risk of.

pregnancy loss (OR = 3.00; 95% CI: 2.18–4.14; P < 0.001). To further investigate the relationship between CB and pregnancy loss, subgroup analyses were performed. No significant interactions were detected across any of the subgroup variables, including maternal age, BMI, obstetric history, or history of miscarriage. These findings suggest that the association between CB and pregnancy loss is consistent and independent of baseline clinical characteristics, as demonstrated by subgroup analyses (Table 2) and multivariable-adjusted models (Supplementary Table 1).

Comparison of characteristics between pregnancy loss and ongoing pregnancy in pregnancies with a chorionic bump

To further investigate the factors influencing pregnancy loss in patients presenting with a CB, baseline clinical characteristics and ultrasonographic parameters were compared between the pregnancy loss and ongoing pregnancy groups. The analysis revealed no significant differences regarding maternal age, BMI, obstetric history, history of miscarriage, maximum diameter of the CB, or absolute CB volume (all P > 0.05). However, significant differences were observed in the GS volume and the CB-to-GS volume ratio (P < 0.05). Specifically, a smaller GS volume and a higher CB/GS volume ratio were significantly associated with an increased risk of pregnancy loss (Table 3). Collinearity analysis confirmed acceptable collinearity among maximum CB diameter, CB volume, GS volume, and CB/GS volume ratio (VIF < 5), supporting their simultaneous inclusion in the model (Supplementary Table 2).

Radiomic feature-based model construction

Three machine learning algorithms—logistic regression, SVM, and RF—were utilized to construct predictive models. Among these, the RF algorithm demonstrated the optimal performance, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.513. Following feature selection to identify the top 30 features with the highest model contribution, the performance of the optimized RF_Sel model improved, with the AUROC reaching 0.573 (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Radiomic feature selection and interpretability analysis

A total of 102 radiomic features were extracted, comprising 18 first-order, 9 shape, and 75 texture features. SHAP analysis was performed on the final 20 radiomic features to evaluate their contribution to the predictive model. The original_firstorder_Minimum (first-order minimum intensity) was identified as a critical feature for determining pregnancy outcomes. SHAP analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between the greyscale minimum and the risk of adverse outcomes. In the digital ultrasound matrix, extremely low greyscale values correspond to anechoic or hypoechoic regions, which likely represent liquid components or acute hemorrhage within the chorionic bump. Conversely, higher minimum values suggest organized or fibrotic hematomas, which are associated with greater lesion stability. Beyond extreme values, original_firstorder_Skewness and various texture features significantly influenced model decision-making. High skewness (reflecting asymmetrical greyscale distribution) and increased texture intensity were positively correlated with risk (Fig. 3). Quantitative analysis identified a trend indicating that when the normalized internal grayscale minimum of the lesion fell below approximately − 100 (Z-score normalized value), the predicted probability of adverse outcomes increased sharply (Fig. 4). This suggests that a critical proportion of liquid or fresh hemorrhagic components serves as a primary driver of poor pregnancy prognosis. However, this threshold is derived from normalized data and represents a relative rather than absolute measurement; its clinical applicability requires prospective validation with standardized imaging protocols.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the presence of a CB is a significant risk factor for pregnancy loss. Within the CB group, a smaller GS volume and a higher CB-to-GS volume ratio were associated with a greater risk of miscarriage. Although the radiomics-based prediction model showed limited predictive performance (AUROC 0.573), the first-order minimum intensity (original_firstorder_Minimum) emerged as the most informative feature. Skewness and selected texture features also contributed meaningfully to outcome prediction.

First-trimester ultrasound screening is essential for prenatal care [13], In rare cases, a CB may be detected, appearing as an irregular protrusion from the choriodecidual surface into the gestational sac. It is clinically important to distinguish a CB from a SCH. A CB represents bulging of the choriodecidual membrane into the sac, whereas an SCH is a blood collection between the chorion and decidua [14, 15]. On ultrasound, a CB is typically located along the margin of the chorionic frondosum next to the gestational sac and ranges in size from a few millimeters to several centimeters. Its internal echogenicity is variable and may appear hyperechoic, hypoechoic, isoechoic, or mixed. Color Doppler imaging characteristically shows no internal blood flow. In our cohort of 184 cases, most lesions were either uniformly hyperechoic or showed a hypoechoic center with a hyperechoic rim, consistent with prior descriptions.

We observed that CB was strongly associated with early pregnancy loss. Among 184 women with a CB, all 93 losses occurred before 14 weeks of gestation. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a higher risk of miscarriage in pregnancies complicated by CB, including in vitro fertilization cohorts. Prior research has also described a guarded prognosis, with live birth rates below 50% in some series, and meta-analyses confirm an increased risk of first-trimester miscarriage [3]. No CBs were detected beyond 14 weeks in our cohort, suggesting that their clinical course—resolution or pregnancy loss—typically occurs in early pregnancy. Although rare late-pregnancy cases have been reported [16], CB is generally considered a first-trimester finding. It is possible that later cases are underrecognized because clinicians do not routinely look for CB beyond early gestation. Importantly, among women whose pregnancies continued past the first trimester, birth outcomes were comparable to those without a CB. This indicates that if the early high-risk period is overcome, subsequent pregnancy outcomes are generally favorable. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing no association between CB and later obstetric complications [3]. However, this observation is limited to our study population and does not exclude the possibility of CB persisting or presenting de novo in later gestation, as rare late-pregnancy cases have been reported. Systematic evaluation of CB beyond the first trimester was beyond the scope of this study.

We observed no significant differences in the absolute size or volume of the CB between the ongoing pregnancy and pregnancy loss groups, consistent with previous reports. One study found no significant difference in CB volume between outcomes (P = 0.198) [8]. Similarly, the mean CB diameter did not differ significantly between live births (10.4 mm, range 5.7–25.3 mm) and miscarriages (10.9 mm, range 5.7–19.3 mm; P = 0.42) [4]. In contrast, we found that a smaller GS volume and a higher CB-to-GS volume ratio were significantly associated with pregnancy loss. Although the CB/GS ratio is mathematically derived from GS volume, collinearity analysis confirmed acceptable collinearity between these variables (VIF < 5). The CB/GS ratio is retained as a clinically intuitive metric reflecting the relative space occupied by the lesion within the gestational sac, which may better capture the functional impact on embryonic development than either absolute measurement alone. One possible explanation is that a relatively large CB within a small gestational sac reduces the effective space needed for normal embryonic development. These volumetric patterns may also reflect impaired placental perfusion or early placental dysfunction.

The cause of CB remains unclear, but it is widely thought to represent an intervillous hematoma. Pregnancy loss may result from underlying bleeding related to abnormal chorionic vascular development or impaired microcirculation [17]. Our radiomics findings support this hypothesis. The most important predictive feature, original_firstorder_Minimum, reflects the lowest grayscale value within the lesion. Lower minimum values, which correspond to anechoic or low-signal regions on ultrasound, were associated with worse outcomes. These areas likely represent fluid or acute hemorrhage, consistent with a bleeding-related mechanism. However, this interpretation requires pathological or prospective validation, as the relationship between grayscale intensity and hemorrhagic content has not been confirmed histologically in our study. Other studies have proposed alternative explanations, including chromosomal abnormalities or abnormal villous development [18–20]. Larger prospective studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths, including a relatively large sample of CB cases and complete pregnancy outcome data. The use of ultrasound-based radiomics allowed us to identify imaging features associated with pregnancy loss. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, data on laboratory markers such as homocysteine and autoantibodies were unavailable, and treatment details were incomplete, preventing adjustment for potential confounders. Third, some patients lacked serial ultrasound follow-up, so the exact timing of CB resolution could not always be determined. Fourth, genetic or pathological data were not available, despite prior reports linking CB with fetal chromosomal abnormalities [18, 19, 21]. Additionally, the optimized RF_Sel model achieved an AUROC of only 0.573, indicating poor discriminative ability close to random classification. Moreover, feature selection was performed prior to rather than within the cross-validation loop, which may further overestimate this already limited performance. These findings confirm that radiomic features alone are insufficient for clinical prediction of pregnancy loss. Future research should integrate clinical variables, biological markers [22], and genetic markers to enhance model performance. These limitations highlight the need for standardized diagnostic criteria and systematic evaluation of CB in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, a chorionic bump is a significant risk factor for first-trimester pregnancy loss. Within affected pregnancies, a smaller gestational sac volume and a higher CB-to-GS volume ratio are associated with increased risk. Radiomic analysis identified first-order minimum intensity as the most informative imaging feature; lower minimum grayscale values were linked to adverse outcomes and may reflect hemorrhagic changes. These findings underscore the importance of early detection and careful risk assessment of CB. Prospective studies and external validation are needed to guide clinical management.
