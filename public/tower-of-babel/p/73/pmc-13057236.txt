Impact of central venous pressure trajectories on prognosis in ICU patients with sepsis
Authors: Jiayi Chen, Shuhao Que, Guangyong Jin, Buqing Ma, Hongwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated host response to infection. Optimal fluid resuscitation remains a challenge in sepsis management, and central venous pressure (CVP) is often used as a surrogate marker to guide therapy. However, the prognostic value of dynamic CVP changes remains unclear. This retrospective study utilized the MIMIC-IV database to investigate the association between CVP trajectories in the first 24 h of ICU admission and outcomes in septic patients. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify distinct CVP change patterns. Patients were stratified into four trajectory groups. Baseline characteristics, survival outcomes, and logistic regression analyses were conducted. A total of 3,068 patients were included. Four CVP trajectory groups were identified. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significant differences in ICU, in-hospital, and long-term mortality (28-day, 60-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year) across groups (log-rank p < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression revealed that patients in the highest CVP trajectory group had significantly increased mortality compared to the lowest group. Subgroup analyses confirmed a consistent trend, with elevated CVP associated with worse prognosis. Distinct CVP trajectories during the early ICU period are associated with varying mortality risks in septic patients. Persistently elevated CVP trajectories may was associated with poor outcomes and warrants cautious fluid management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41213-y.

Introduction

Sepsis is a clinical syndrome characterized by physiological and biological abnormalities resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection. Approximately 20% to 30% of patients with sepsis require admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for ongoing treatment1. Fluid therapy is a cornerstone in the management of critically ill patients with sepsis. Early volume resuscitation to counteract shock has become a widely accepted therapeutic principle. Although early small-sample studies suggested that early goal-directed therapy(EGDT) might be beneficial, subsequent large randomized controlled trials failed to demonstrate any additional advantage2–4. Current treatment practice emphasizes individualized resuscitation strategies rather than fixed goal-directed approaches5,6.

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection,typically indicated by an increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score by ≥ 2 points, which correlates with an in-hospital mortality rate exceeding 10%7. A meta-analysis primarily based on high-income countries estimated an incidence of 288 cases per 100,000 person-years, with in-hospital mortality rate ranging from 17 to 26%. In contrast, in low- and middle-income regions, the incidence can reach up to 4,000 cases per 100,000 person-years, accounting for over 50% of total deaths8.

Current sepsis guidelines recommend an initial intravenous infusion of 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid, followed by individualized fluid administration guided by ongoing assessment of perfusion and risk–benefit evaluation9. Although static CVP values have historically been used to guide resuscitation, they poorly predict fluid responsiveness and have been associated with factors such as right ventricular dysfunction or venous congestion rather than intravascular volume status8,10. This study utilized data from the MIMIC database to investigate CVP trajectory patterns within the first 24 h after ICU admission in septic patients using GBTM, aiming to explore whether distinct CVP trajectories—representing different hemodynamic states—are associated with clinical outcomes.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were obtained from the publicly available MIMIC-IV 3.0 database (website: https://mimic.mit.edu/), released in July 2024. The database includes data from a total of 364,627 patients admitted between 2008 and 2022, accounting for 546,028 hospital admissions, of which 94,458 were intensive care unit (ICU) stays. Our research team has received approval to use this database from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 0403000206) and the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2001-P-001699/14)9. This study adhered to all ethical regulations concerning data research. All researchers involved have completed the necessary ethical training and obtained authorization to access the MIMIC database.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Patients diagnosed with sepsis who had available CVP data in the database. CVP measurements within the first 24 h after ICU admission were divided into six time intervals: 0–4 h, 4–8 h, 8–12 h, 12–16 h, 16–20 h, and 20–24 h. The maximum CVP value from each time interval was extracted.

Exclusion criteria: Patients younger than 18 years old, those with ICU stays shorter than 24 h on initial admission, and patients with missing CVP values in any of the six time intervals were excluded.

GBTM was then applied to identify subgroups with similar CVP dynamic trajectories, resulting in four distinct groups (Group 1, 2, 3, and 4). Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed for all patients in this study. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD) if normally distributed, or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) if non-normally distributed. Comparisons among categorical variables, normally distributed continuous variables, and non-normally distributed continuous variables were conducted using the chi-square test, t-test, and Kruskal–Wallis test, respectively.

The number of trajectory groups was determined using the traj package in STATA. To identify the optimal model structure, an initial baseline model without covariates was established, and polynomial orders (linear, quadratic, cubic) were evaluated. Model selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), entropy, and the proportion of the sample in each trajectory group. Participants were assigned to trajectory groups if their average posterior probability was at least 70%, and each group comprised more than 5% of the total sample.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, missing data were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) method and multiple imputation techniques. After imputation, we compared ICU mortality differences among four patient groups.

Second, Winsorization was applied to truncate the original CVP data at the 5th and 95th percentiles, resulting in a CVP range of 5–32 mmHg. GBTM clustering was then re-performed with the truncated data to identify the optimal number of groups.

Finally, multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between CVP and ICU mortality, using the modified dataset. These methods were used to test the sensitivity of our findings and ensure the robustness of our conclusions.

Samples with more than 20% missing data were excluded, and missing values were imputed using the mode. Outliers were addressed using the winsor command in STATA to cap values at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Based on clinical expertise, CVP values greater than 30 mmHg were replaced with a clinically reasonable threshold of 30 mmHg. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was ICU mortality. Secondary outcome measures included in-hospital mortality and mortality at 28 days, 60 days, 90 days, 180 days, and 1 year.

Results

Characteristics of CVP trajectories

The optimal grouping process of GBTM is shown in Supplementary Table 1. Based on the model fit indices (AIC, BIC, and entropy) and clinical interpretability, the four-group model was identified as the optimal solution. The average posterior probabilities (AvePP) for Groups 1–4 were 0.84, 0.82, 0.87, and 0.92, respectively. Fig. 2 displays the central venous pressure (CVP) trajectories during the first 24 h after ICU admission. Group 1 exhibited a persistently low and slightly declining CVP trend (low-level declining group), Group 2 showed moderate initial levels with a gradual decline (moderate-level declining group), Group 3 maintained a higher CVP range that gradually decreased (high-level declining group), whereas Group 4 showed persistently elevated and rising CVP levels (high-level rising group). The dynamic distributions of CVP values across time points in each trajectory are visualized in Fig. 3.

Baseline characteristics

This study included data from a total of 3,068 patients, among whom 1,920 (62.6%) were male. The median age was 68 years (IQR 18–96), and the overall median BMI was 28.5 (IQR 10.8–71.5). According to the GBTM method, patients were classified into four groups: Group 1:low-level declining group; Group 2:moderate-level declining group; Group 3: high-level declining group; and Group 4:high-level rising group. Baseline characteristics for these groups are presented in Table 2. Patients in Group 4 had the highest severity scores, with median APSIII, SOFA, and SAPSII values of 57, 10, and 47, respectively, compared with 41, 6, and 39 in Group 1 (all P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis

After imputing missing values using K-Nearest Neighbor and multiple imputation, we investigated ICU mortality differences among four patient groups. Compared to Group 1, Group 3 and Group 4 exhibited significantly higher ICU mortality risk (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4–10). Subsequently, we applied Winsorization to truncate the original CVP data at the 5th-95th percentiles, yielding a CVP range of 5–32 mmHg. Repeating GBTM clustering with the truncated data still identified four groups as the optimal solution. Multivariate logistic regression analysis for ICU mortality confirmed consistent results with the main text, again showing significantly higher risk in Group 3 and Group 4 versus Group 1 (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 11–12). These findings collectively demonstrate the robustness of our research conclusions.

Discussion

This study identified distinct CVP trajectory patterns during the first 24 h of ICU admission in patients with sepsis, and found that persistently elevated or rising CVP trajectories were independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality and increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI).These associations persisted across multiple sensitivity analyses and key clinical subgroups, and the dynamic CVP trends may be related to underlying cardiopulmonary pathophysiology beyond simple fluid status.

Physiological interpretation of CVP and Its determinants

CVP should not be regarded as an independent causal determinant of hemodynamics but rather as a dependent variable reflecting the interaction between venous return, right heart function, and intrathoracic pressure. In critically ill patients, particularly those with sepsis, this relationship becomes nonlinear and context-dependent. According to Guyton’s venous return model, venous return is governed by the pressure gradient between the mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP) and right atrial pressure (approximated by CVP), divided by the resistance to venous return13. Thus, CVP represents the back-pressure opposing venous return, not simply a surrogate of volume load. When right ventricular afterload increases or venous compliance decreases—as in sepsis with elevated pulmonary vascular resistance or capillary leak—CVP rises even without true hypervolemia. This conceptual framework is consistent with the observation that elevated CVP in our study was associated with worse outcomes, which could represent systemic congestion and impaired venous return rather than excessive fluid administration4,14,15.

The relationship between fluid resuscitation and CVP

Fluid resuscitation is a cornerstone of sepsis management, aimed at restoring effective circulatory volume and organ perfusion.However, our findings emphasize that CVP elevation following fluid therapy does not necessarily indicate adequate preload. In the presence of pericardial constraint, increased intrathoracic pressure, or pressure dissociation between the right atrium and ventricle—conditions common in septic shock—the correlation between CVP and right ventricular end-diastolic pressure becomes unreliable10,16,17.

From a mechanistic standpoint, elevated CVP signifies the point at which venous return curves intersect the cardiac function curve—beyond this equilibrium, additional volume fails to enhance stroke volume and instead increases venous congestion. This aligns with the Frank–Starling principle and the concept of critical closing pressure, beyond which microcirculatory flow becomes compromised18. Our data support this physiological interpretation: patients with persistently high or rising CVP trajectories demonstrated higher mortality, which may be attributable to impaired venous return and increased systemic filling pressures rather than volume overload per se. However, given the lack of direct measurements of cardiac output or venous return in this dataset, these mechanisms remain hypothetical interpretations of the observed statistical associations.

Dynamic CVP trajectories and right heart function

The observed CVP trajectories are consistent with evolving states of right heart performance and venous compliance. The “high-rising” group may be associated with progressive right ventricular dysfunction and venous congestion, whereas the “low-declining” group may correspond to preserved cardiac adaptability and more favorable preload responsiveness.This perspective links dynamic CVP patterns to right ventricular afterload and systemic venous tone beyond a simple statistical association. Previous echocardiographic studies corroborate this interpretation, showing that septic patients with elevated CVP exhibit right ventricular dilation, reduced contractility, and increased pulmonary vascular resistance—all of which predict higher mortality19–21.

In the subgroup analysis, elevated CVP was consistently associated with adverse outcomes across strata of endotracheal intubation, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. Septic patients requiring intubation often present with ARDS or severe respiratory failure, conditions that increase intrathoracic pressure and may reduce venous return22. These physiological changes can aggravate right ventricular afterload, elevate CVP, and worsen hemodynamic instability.Previous studies in ventilated ARDS patients have shown that a conservative fluid strategy significantly reduces mortality, supporting this mechanism23. In atrial fibrillation, irregular ventricular contraction reduces cardiac efficiency and may impair venous return, further elevating CVP24,25. Moreover, right ventricular dysfunction may contribute to elevated CVP and worse prognosis by reducing forward flow and promoting systemic congestion4,26,27. These interactions underscore that CVP integrates the combined effects of cardiac, pulmonary, and venous factors, in addition to intravascular volume status.

Therefore, CVP trajectories may represent the integrated cardiopulmonary response over time rather than a static measurement of filling pressure. The prognostic value of CVP in our study appeared to be influenced not only by its absolute level but also by the dynamic pattern, which could be related to hemodynamic adaptation or decompensation.

CVP levels and the risk of acute kidney injury

We observed that the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) increased in higher CVP trajectory groups. Other studies have shown that in patients with septic shock, maintaining CVP within the range of 6–12 cmH2O on the first day after resuscitation is associated with the lowest mortality. If the time-weighted average CVP (TWA-CVP) exceeds 12 cmH₂O and persists for more than 5 h, the risk of mortality and acute kidney injury (AKI) significantly increases, primarily manifested by reduced urine output rather than elevated serum creatinine10. In patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (AKI), a CVP below 13 mmHg has been associated with reduced mortality21. Similarly, studies have reported that higher standardized CVP load correlates with increased 28-day mortality, driven primarily by the intensity of elevated CVP28.

These observations align with our results, and persistent venous congestion could contribute to renal dysfunction in sepsis through both hemodynamic and microcirculatory mechanisms. Clinically, careful monitoring of CVP trends can help prevent excessive preload and minimize the risk of renal injury.Nevertheless, without direct data on renal venous pressure or intra-abdominal pressure in our cohort, the exact pathway linking CVP trajectories to AKI requires further investigation.

Interpretation of sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analyses (including multiple imputation and Winsorization) were designed to test robustness, not to redefine physiological limits. The Winsorization threshold of 30 mmHg was selected to minimize the influence of extreme outliers that likely represent measurement artifacts or transient mechanical effects (e.g., ventilator cycling). Although the truncation might exclude a small number of physiologically valid observations, the consistency of results across truncated and imputed datasets supports the stability of our findings.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the distinction between the trajectory shape and absolute CVP level remains important for interpretation. While the high-rising and high-declining groups both showed increased mortality, their differing baseline characteristics (e.g., heart failure prevalence, urine output, and organ support requirements) may point to distinct pathophysiological pathways—namely, impaired cardiac adaptation versus static volume overload. Future work integrating echocardiographic and hemodynamic indices (e.g., MSFP or pressure–volume analysis) could better differentiate these mechanisms.

Implications for clinical practice

Our findings reinforce the importance of interpreting CVP within the broader framework of venous return physiology rather than as an isolated target. Early ICU management should aim to maintain adequate perfusion while avoiding venous congestion. CVP should not be used as the sole guide for fluid resuscitation but rather in conjunction with dynamic assessments of venous return, right ventricular function, and tissue perfusion. Recognizing that CVP is a dependent variable shaped by the cardiopulmonary system helps reconcile the conflicting results across previous studies and provides a mechanistic foundation for understanding why dynamic CVP trajectories are strongly associated with outcomes in sepsis.However, these data do not support using CVP trajectories alone to dictate fluid restriction or administration without comprehensive clinical assessment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was designed as a retrospective observational analysis, which may be subject to potential selection bias and confounding factors, thereby limiting the ability to infer causal relationships. Second, due to restrictions inherent in the data source, important variables such as fluid input volume, right heart function assessment, and echocardiographic parameters were unavailable. This absence data constrains a deeper mechanistic exploration of the relationship between CVP and prognosis and prevents us from definitively distinguishing whether elevated CVP is a cause of organ dysfunction or a marker of severe underlying cardiac/pulmonary pathology. Third, as this study was based on a single public database without external validation, the generalizability and robustness of the findings remain limited.Additionally, the MIMIC-IV database primarily reflects practices in a specific academic medical center, which may not be representative of all healthcare settings. Future research should include multicenter, large-sample prospective cohort studies, and ideally randomized controlled trials (RCTs), to more rigorously evaluate the causal relationship between CVP levels and outcomes in patients with sepsis. Furthermore, extending analyses to diverse populations and healthcare settings will help determine whether the associations observed in this study are broadly applicable.

Conclusion

This exploratory study,identified a significant association between dynamic CVP trajectories within the first 24 h of ICU admission and mortality risk in patients with sepsis. These findings are observational and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. Rather,the results are consistent with the view that CVP is influenced by multiple hemodynamic factors—including right heart function, venous compliance, and intrathoracic pressures—rather than being solely a measure of volume status. While CVP alone is insufficient to guide fluid resuscitation, continuous and dynamic monitoring of CVP trends may may offer supplementary information for individualized hemodynamic optimization in critically ill septic patients.Future studies incorporating direct hemodynamic monitoring are needed to validate these trajectory patterns and clarify their utility in guiding therapeutic interventions.
