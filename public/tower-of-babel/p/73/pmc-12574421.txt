Membranous Labyrinth Position in the Lateral Semicircular Canal: Considerations for Gene Therapy Delivery
Authors: Koffi L Lakpa, Maya N Matabele, Rafael da Costa Monsanto, Maurizio Falcioni, Nevra Keskin‐Yilmaz, Brevin Miller, Jozef Mierzwinski, Arnaldo Rivera, John P Dahl, Andrew J Fishman, Sebahattin Cureoglu, Michael D Puricelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFenestration of the lateral semicircular canal has been proposed to improve intracochlear gene therapy delivery, but the relationship between the membranous and bony labyrinths at the canal apex has not been systematically defined.MethodsWe examined 62 human temporal bones, including 31 unaffected specimens and 31 with chronic otitis media, using histologic review to assess the membranous‐to‐bony relationship at the apex.ResultsIn all specimens, the membranous labyrinth directly contacted the outer circumferential bony wall.ConclusionThis consistent finding, independent of disease state, indicates that fenestration at the lateral canal apex carries a high risk of membranous injury. Potential consequences include hearing loss, vestibular dysfunction, and reduced therapeutic effect. Inadvertent entry into the endolymphatic compartment might not adequately support perilymphatic flow and compromise therapeutic delivery. Because fenestration of the outer circumference of the lateral semicircular canal is histologically unfavorable, histologic assessment of proposed fenestration sites is necessary.Keywords: inner ear gene therapy, lateral semicircular canal, membranous labyrinth, sensorineural hearing loss, temporal bone

Congenital sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) affects 1 to 3 per 1000 newborns, with genetic causes accounting for 65% of cases in developed countries. 1 While hearing aids and cochlear implants offer functional improvement, they do not restore normal hearing. Recent advances in gene therapy offer promising strategies to restore cochlear function. 2 , 3

In 2022, the first intracochlear gene therapy injection resulted in hearing improvement. 3 Since then, multiple clinical trials have been launched in the United States. However, gene therapy delivery to the inner ear remains anatomically and technically challenging. The round window membrane (RWM) is the most common entry site, yet the cochlea's enclosed bony structure and membranous compartmentalization limit egress and predispose to pressure fluctuations. 4 Disruption of the membranous labyrinth during fenestration may also cause vestibular dysfunction or hearing loss. 5 To mitigate these risks, secondary fenestration has been proposed to relieve pressure and improve vector distribution.

Animal models have explored various fenestration sites. 4 , 6 In humans, RWM access is feasible, and the lateral semicircular canal (LSCC) is more surgically accessible than the posterior semicircular canal (PSCC). Clinical trials have therefore combined RWM injection with LSCC or oval window fenestration. 4 While prior histologic studies examined anatomy relevant to the RWM and oval window, 7 the LSCC apex has not been systematically evaluated.

Traditional fistula classification diagrams, such as those proposed by Dornhoffer and Milewski, depict the membranous labyrinth centered within the bony canal, suggesting low surgical risk. 8 , 9 In contrast, some reports 10 show the membranous labyrinth closely approximating the lateral wall while still allowing space for perilymph between it and the bony endosteum. Finally, chronic otitis media (COM) alters inner ear cell populations, but its effect on membranous semicircular canal anatomy is unknown. 11 , 12 , 13

Therefore, this study characterizes the relationship between the membranous and bony labyrinths at the LSCC apex in human temporal bones with and without COM.

Methods

Specimen Preparation

Human temporal bones were obtained from the Paparella Otopathology Laboratory (University of Minnesota). Specimens were processed by standard histologic protocol: formalin fixation, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) decalcification, celloidin embedding, 20 µm horizontal sectioning, and hematoxylin–eosin staining of every 10th section. 14 Institutional review board exemption was granted (University of Minnesota ID: STUDY00003249 and Children's Mercy STUDY00003823).

Sample Selection

A total of 31 unaffected specimens and 31 with COM were examined. Specimens were excluded if the region of interest was incompletely represented or if a significant artifact was present, including removal, sectioning, processing, cutting, mounting, or compression defects.

LSCC Apex Identification and Analysis

The LSCC apex was defined histologically as the midpoint between the ampullated and non‐ampullated limbs (Figure 1). To minimize sectioning or selection error, adjacent slides, one above and one below, were also reviewed (Figures 2 and 3). Digital images were obtained with a Nikon Eclipse E400 microscope using NIS‐Elements software. The minimal membranous‐to‐bony distance at the apex was measured, and the smallest value was recorded in cases of discrepancy. Measurements were performed independently by two observers using the same slide set, with complete agreement across all specimens. All analyses were descriptive, as membranous contact was present in all specimens.

Results

All 62 specimens were suitable for evaluation. In both groups (31 COM, 31 unaffected), the membranous labyrinth contacted the outer bony wall at the LSCC apex (measured distance 0 mm), with no difference observed between groups.

Discussion

This study represents the first human histologic analysis of membranous‐to‐bony relationships at the LSCC apex. Membranous contact with the bony wall was consistently observed, independent of disease status.

These findings have important implications for LSCC fenestration procedures, including intracochlear gene therapy delivery. The high rate of membranous contact at the LSCC dome indicates that fenestration at this site risks endolymph leakage, vestibular dysfunction, and hearing loss. 15 , 16 , 17 In a study assessing DB‐Oto for inherited deafness, fenestration was performed at the dome of the LSCC and the primary endpoint (pure tone audiometry average threshold of 70 decibel hearing level or less at week 24) and an auditory brainstem response at or below 90 dB normalized hearing level were seen in 75% of patients. Of the 75% who responded to treatment, a complete response was seen in one‐third (25% of those treated), and an incomplete response, as evidenced by the need for ongoing amplification, was observed in two‐thirds of patients (50% of those treated). One individual suffered a serious adverse event (grade 3 walking instability). While no persistent adverse vestibular findings were observed, compensation could result in the absence of ongoing symptoms. 18 In a study of AAV1‐hOTOF gene therapy administered through the round window and ventilated via stapes footplate fenestration, a response was seen in five of six patients with thresholds falling to 38 to 55 dB at 26 weeks. The only grade 3 event was decreased neutrophil count. 3 Variation in outcomes is likely multifactorial due to technical and clinical challenges including the mechanism of delivery, size of OTOF gene, which could impact integration and expression, dose delivered, vector proportion, expression efficiency, patient age, and immune responses. 19

Accessing the perilymphatic compartment from an outer circumferential approach may require a superior or inferior extreme approach, or deeper drilling/puncture through the endolymphatic compartment. In cases where LSCC fenestration is considered for conductive hearing loss with endosteal preservation, the close approximation of the membranous labyrinth to the endosteum suggests that even superficial violation may cause iatrogenic injury. 16 , 17 , 20 These observations also apply to semicircular canal occlusion and related procedures.

The eccentric membranous position further indicates that existing classifications of semicircular canal fistulas may not fully capture the histologic relationships, and the risk of membranous injury likely varies with fistula location. 9 , 10 , 21 , 22 Reassessment of current schemes may therefore be warranted.

Limitations include the use of two‐dimensional histologic sections to evaluate a three‐dimensional fluid‐filled structure, which may introduce spatial distortion. However, examining the apex along with adjacent sections minimized this risk. Importantly, human temporal bone histology remains the gold standard for inner ear microanatomy. 23

Future studies should evaluate membranous labyrinth position relative to both inner and outer circumferences across all semicircular canals as well as other potential targets to refine the understanding of these relationships and identify potential alternative fenestration sites that can be systematically studied to optimize intracochlear treatment delivery. Such efforts are consistent with the calls of other authors who recognize the need for delivery optimization. 19 Combining high‐resolution histology with advanced imaging, such as micro computed tomography, may further enhance anatomical and physiologic insight. 7

Conclusion

In all specimens, the membranous labyrinth contacted the outer bony wall at the LSCC apex, independent of disease state. These results question the safety of fenestration at this site and underscore the importance of histologically guided fenestration approaches for gene therapy.
