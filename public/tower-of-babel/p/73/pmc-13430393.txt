From linear methylation to spatial metabolic control: Rethinking DNMT1 targeting in MASLD: Letter to the editor on “DNMT1 facilitates the progression of MASLD by impeding transcription mediated by HNF4α and PPARα”
Authors: Yao Liu, Qitai Song, Muhu Chen
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The authors are to be congratulated on their sophisticated interrogation of the epigenetic landscape underlying metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [1]. Their study provides a profound contribution to molecular medicine by elucidating the DNMT1-HNF4α-PCK1 axis, offering an elegant framework for how DNA hypermethylation, particularly within the C1 cluster of regulatory regions, silences essential metabolic programs. This work represents a significant milestone in our understanding of hepatocyte plasticity.

While the mechanistic link between hypermethylation and transcriptional silencing is robustly supported, evidenced by the EPIC BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) analysis and verified through false discovery rate-corrected differential methylation studies, several forward-looking considerations regarding genomic architecture and systemic physiology warrant discussion from an interdisciplinary perspective.

First, from a genomics and data science standpoint, the focus on promoter-proximal methylation may overlook the broader impact of 3D chromatin architecture. DNA hypermethylation frequently disrupts CCCTC-binding factor binding, which is essential for maintaining topologically associating domains [2,3]. Although the study demonstrates that 5-aza-4′-thio-2′-deoxycytidine (Aza-TdC) treatment can restore HNF4α occupancy, it remains to be determined whether pharmacological demethylation alone can re-establish the physical loops between distal enhancers and the PCK1 promoter. In the era of machine learning, purely linear epigenetic maps may lack the predictive power to capture these hierarchical structural collapses. Future investigations utilizing Hi-C or Micro-C are necessary to determine if the structural “scaffold” of the liver genome is truly restored [4].

Second, as clinicians, we must address the “PCK1 paradox.” The authors’ use of CRISPR-mediated epigenetic editing to demonstrate that PCK1 promoter methylation directly leads to significant triacylglycerol accumulation is highly persuasive. However, as PCK1 is a rate-limiting enzyme for gluconeogenesis, its therapeutic restoration may inadvertently increase hepatic glucose output. Given the high prevalence of comorbid type 2 diabetes in MASLD populations, integrated metabolic flux analysis is essential to ensure that fixing hepatic lipid metabolism does not inadvertently trade one metabolic crisis for another, regardless of the strong Pearson correlation between methylation and steatosis severity [5-7].

Third, by integrating single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) with clinical pathology, the study hints at a potential “point of no return” or epigenetic scarring [8-10]. From a data science perspective, identifying the stochastic noise and temporal thresholds where chromatin accessibility becomes permanently restricted, perhaps through repressive histone marks that persist after DNMT1 inhibition, is a prerequisite for effective patient stratification. Developing “digital twin” models that simulate these zonal transitions will be vital for clinical translatability.

To translate this into clinical management, we propose the development of patient-specific “digital twin” models constructed via pseudotime trajectory inference and RNA velocity analysis [11]. Rather than relying on static snapshots, these computational simulations can map the dynamic transition probabilities of hepatocytes under DNMT1 inhibition, predicting whether a patient’s liver will revert to a healthy metabolic state or transition into a “demethylated but distinct” metastable phenotype [12]. Such in silico modeling would serve as a crucial decision-support tool, identifying non-responders prior to therapy and defining the specific epigenetic boundaries that separate reversible plasticity from permanent damage.

In summary, this landmark study has provided an invaluable map of the MASLD methylome. Bridging the remaining gaps to clinical therapy will require a transition from “mean methylation” to a nuanced, interdisciplinary understanding of the liver’s structural and spatial epigenetic memory. We thank the authors for this significant and thought-provoking contribution.
