Genetic diversity and population structure of sour passion fruit in Brazil
Authors: Carolina Sampaio Araponga, Gonçalo Santos Silva, Cláusio Antônio Ferreira de Melo, Margarete Magalhães Souza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ISSR markers revealed high intraspecific polymorphism in Passiflora edulis populations.•Five populations were identified among analyzed accessions.•Most molecular variance (55%) was detected within rather than between populations.•Field-collected populations showed greater diversity than commercial seed sources.•Results support the strategic use of genetic structure for passion fruit breeding.Keywords: ISSR markers, Genetic variability, Molecular diversity, Passiflora edulis, Principal coordinates, Population genetics

Introduction

The sour passion fruit (Passiflora edulis Sims) is the most significant species within the genus Passiflora L., both agronomically and commercially. In 2023, Brazil produced 711,279 tons, with commercial production systems, primarily managed by small and medium farmers in the Northeast region, achieving a yield of 15,540 kg/ha. Ceará and Bahia remain the most important producing states1.

P. edulis produces commercially valuable fruits for the food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries. The fruits exhibit phenotypic variations, ranging from yellow to purple coloration2. In the wild, P. edulis appears in two forms, P. edulis f. flavicarpa O. Deg. and P. edulis Sims f. edulis, distinguished by qualitative and quantitative morphological traits3.

Over the years, the sour passion fruit has been genetically improved through germplasm flow, enhancing the genetic diversity of commercial populations. Additionally, P. edulis is allogamous and self-incompatible. Therefore, heterogeneous orchards should be required for fruit production and high productivity4. Commercial passion fruit orchards typically consist of materials chosen by the producers or cultivars developed by public and private research institutions. The cultivars are developed to enhance quality, productivity, and resistance to key diseases and pests affecting the crop5. The producer typically selects the finest fruits annually to establish new orchards, while orchards using cultivars are created using seeds and seedlings bought from certified nursery6.

Molecular markers provide the quickest and most informative method for assessing genetic diversity. Random molecular markers have been informative for studying genetic diversity (the number of genetically different individuals, due to inheritable and/or environmental factors, within a species). Genetic diversity has been evaluated in several populations of different species, from many parts of the world. Important vegetable crops such as Cucumis L. 7, Capsicum L. 8 and Triticum L. 9 were evaluated using molecular markers, including ISSR. Studies on genetic diversity in passion fruit have primarily used phenotypic markers, which are incorporated into breeding programs to select and develop superior plants10, 11. However, different molecular markers have been used in passionfruit to estimate genetic variability such RAPD12, AFLP13, ISSR14, SSR15, SNP16, SRAP17 and RGA18.

ISSR markers, pioneers in analyzing variability in wild and cultivated germplasm19, have been used in genetic improvement to select plants20 and identify promising parents for hybridization21. In intra- and interspecific analyses, random molecular markers enabled the distribution and grouping of wild genotypes compared to commercial varieties, as well as the identification of hybridizations22. Estimation of genetic diversity, population genetics and genetic structure (distribution of genes among individuals and subpopulations) using ISSR23 or associated with other markers24, 25 have been successful in Sonneratia26 and Lathyrus L. 27.

The use of ISSR markers to characterize Passiflora species proved effective, successfully amplifying over half of the tested markers and highlighting the genus's diversity28. The intraspecific analysis of P. edulis genotypes has revealed distinctions among plants based on their origin, including those from breeding (half-siblings of crosses), the Active Germplasm Bank, the Working Collection, or hybridization14. ISSR markers were selected for this study due to their high reproducibility, cost-effectiveness, and ability to detect polymorphisms across the genome without prior sequence knowledge, making them particularly suitable for genetic diversity studies in non-model species like passion fruit5.

The aim of this study was to analyze the diversity and genetic structure within and between three cultivars and two populations of sour passion fruit used as parent plants in Brazilian orchards, to better understand the variability and structuring of the populations examined.

Materials and methods

History of plant material

The current cultivars were developed using various breeding techniques including mass selection, intraspecific crosses, and backcrosses by institutions such as the IAC, the EMBRAPA, and Viveiros Flora Brasil. In 1999, IAC introduced the first hybrid cultivars of the series (IAC-273, IAC-275, and IAC-277) to the Brazilian market, selected for their quality and productivity29. Through recurrent selection and classification of intra- and interspecific hybrids, EMBRAPA Cerrado in Federal District, Brazil, developed the BRS-RC Rubi do Cerrado30. The private company Viveiros Flora Brasil (Araguari − MG, Brazil) has been conducting genetic improvement research since 1985, and as a result of this work, two commercial passion fruit cultivars, FB200 − Yellow Master and FB300 − Araguari, were made available, which were registered in 200831.

Plant material

Twenty-five genotypes from each of the five P. edulis populations were evaluated. Three populations were sourced from commercial seeds, and two were collected in the production area (Table 1). The seeds were sown in Styrofoam trays filled with potting soil as a substrate. After germination and the appearance of true leaves, the seedlings were moved to 1.5 L black polyethylene bags. For populations sourced from small producers' commercial areas, propagation occurred through cuttings. The cuttings, sourced from the middle sections of the branches, were standardized to include two nodes and two leaves, the latter halved in sand. They were cut at diagonal angle (bisel), treated with synthetic auxin (IBA), and then inserted into black polyethylene bags. Each bag, with a capacity of 0.5 L, was filled with washed sand to facilitate rooting. After rooting, the plants were transferred to 1.5 L black polyethylene bags with substrate composed of a mixture of topsoil and organic fertilizer in a 1:1 ratio and kept in a protected environment with 50 % shading at the Passiflora Working Collection, UESC, Ilhéus, Bahia, Brazil (39° 10′ W longitude, 14° 39′ S latitude, 78 m above sea level). Monthly fertilization, proportionally calculated for 1.5 L bags from the dosage for 25 L, was carried out with 39 mg of CH4N2O (Urea), 4.1 mg of B (Boron), 2.0 mg of Cu (Copper), 0.12 mg of (NH4)6Mo7O24 (Ammonium Molybdate), 6.0 mg of Zn (Zinc), and 4.6 mg of Mn (Manganese).

DNA extraction

The genomic DNA was extracted32 and the procedure involved macerating young leaves in liquid nitrogen and incubating them in 2 % CTAB extraction buffer [1.5 M NaCl; 20 mM EDTA; 100 mM Tris-HCl; 0.2 % β-Mercaptoethanol] at 65 °C for 40 min. Nucleic acids were isolated using a chloroform:isoamyl alcohol solution (24:1), and DNA was resuspended in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl; 1 mM EDTA). The genomic DNA concentration was verified by comparing it to 100 ng of Lambda DNA (Promega) on a 1.2 % agarose gel stained with SYBR Safe (Invitrogen). Genomic DNA was diluted to a concentration of 10 ng/µl using TE buffer.

Amplification of ISSR markers

The ISSR amplifications were conducted using 28 UBC ISSR primers (Table 2). The reactions were conducted with a final volume of 20 µl, containing 10 × buffer, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 pmol primer, 10 mM dNTP, and 1 U of Taq DNA Polymerase. The amplifications were conducted using a thermocycler (Eppendorf Mastercycle). The program included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min, followed by 31 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C for denaturation, 45 s of annealing at temperatures between 52 °C and 60 °C depending on the primer (Table 2), and a 2 min extension at 72 °C. A final extension was performed at 72 °C for 10 min. The fragments amplified in the ISSR reaction were stained with SYBR Safe (Invitrogen) and separated by electrophoresis on a 2 % agarose gel, using 90 V for 50 min with 1 × SB buffer (H3BO3 100 mM; NaOH 150 mM). The amplified fragments for each primer were treated as dominant markers and evaluated based on the presence (1) or absence (0) of a band. The ISSR technique provided patterns that were used to evaluate genetic parameters including population structure, polymorphism content, genetic diversity, and effective population number.

Statistical analysis

The binary matrix, indicating the presence (1) and absence (0) of bands, was created using Microsoft Excel. The missing data was identified based on the programs used for computation. The number of Pa, I, He, Na, Ne, uHe, %P, the number of private bands for each population and the AMOVA and PCoA were obtained using the software GenAlEx 6.533. To assess the similarity among individuals within each population, NTSYS-pc 2.1 software34 was used to calculate the Jaccard similarity coefficient35. The dendrogram was constructed using the UPGMA clustering method. The cophenetic correlation coefficient was used to compare the dendrogram with the original similarity matrix. The genetic structure among different populations was analyzed using a Bayesian model. The genetic structure was inferred using STRUCTURE software version 2.3.436. The admixture ancestry model was chosen, and the number of clusters tested (K value) ranged from 1 to 10. All runs were conducted using 400,000 replications following a burn-in period of 100,000 interactions. STRUCTURE Harvester v. 0.6.9337 was used to analyze the STRUCTURE output file, and the ideal K value was calculated using the ad hoc ΔK statistic38.

The binary matrix was imported into RStudio version 4.3.2, and the following R packages were used: vegan (version 2.6–4) for multivariate statistics and distance matrices, dplyr (version 1.1.4) for data manipulation, ggplot2 (version 3.4.4) for data visualization, and reshape2 (version 1.4.4) for data restructuring. These packages were employed for the calculation of the PIC for each marker, Ht, Hs and Gst for dominant markers. Gene flow was estimated as Nm = 0.5 (1 − Gst)/Gst, representing the number of migrants per generation. Marker utility parameters were evaluated through the EMR, calculated as the product of the number of polymorphic loci and the fraction of polymorphic bands, and the MI, computed as the product of EMR and expected heterozygosity. All analyses were performed using custom R scripts implementing diversity indices for dominant markers.

Results

The 28 primers successfully amplified across all P. edulis populations, producing 452 bands of high analytical resolution, averaging 16.14 bands per primer (Fig. 1). The marker system exhibited high efficiency, with an EMR of 13.99, a MI of 3.39, and a DI of 0.607. The total number of bands varied from 6 (UBC-835) to 21 (UBC-841), and the number of polymorphic bands ranged from 5 (UBC-835) to 21 (UBC-841), indicating effective polymorphism detection with high level of polymorphism observed (92.9 %). The PIC values across all ISSR primers ranged from 0.1978 to 0.2733, with a mean PIC of 0.247 (Table 2). The %P varied across populations, ranging from 47.79 % in BRS-RC to 63.50 % in LV (Table 3). The He and I ranged from He = 0.166 and I = 0.249 for BRS-RC to He = 0.203 and I = 0.308 for LV (Table 3). Na, Ne, uHe and Pa showed higher values (1.493, 1.342, 0.207 and 11, respectively) for LV and lower values for BRS-RC (1.215, 1.285, 0.170 and 2, respectively) (Table 3). When comparing expected heterozygosity, Shannon diversity, and polymorphic proportions, the LV and RJ populations exhibited the highest values (Fig. 2).

The genetic relationships and population structure identified above were quantified through analyses of overall population genetic parameters. The results revealed an Ht of 0.307. The average Hs was 0.178. The Gst was 0.421, indicating that 42.1 % of the total genetic variation is distributed among populations, a finding consistent with the AMOVA results. This moderate to high level of population structure was supported by a Nm of 0.343 migrants per generation, which is below the threshold considered necessary to prevent population divergence. The pairwise genetic distances between individuals, based on Jaccard's coefficient, ranged from 0.100 to 0.773, reflecting the wide spectrum of genetic similarity observed. The mean Nei's gene diversity and mean diversity index across all populations were 0.178 and 0.607, respectively.

Discussion

ISSR molecular markers are valuable tools for understanding population dynamics, adaptation, and evolutionary patterns, crucial for species conservation and agronomic improvement. In agronomically important species worldwide, genetic variability often correlates positively with geographical distance among related populations39. These markers facilitate parental selection in breeding programs40 and provide a scientific foundation for future research on variety selection, germplasm evaluation, and resource conservation40, 41. Furthermore, studies involving diverse populations can reveal potential genetic resources for prospecting and preserving vital plant resources24.

ISSR markers are widely used in the identification of germplasm resources and genetic diversity determination41. In this study, molecular data derived from ISSR markers facilitated the analysis of polymorphism across five sour passion fruit (P. edulis) populations, yielding a substantial number of polymorphic bands, which is typical in intraspecific studies using dominant markers in Passiflora42. The high level of polymorphism demonstrates the effectiveness of ISSR markers to detect genetic variation within P. edulis populations, confirm the presence of substantial genetic diversity in the studied germplasm, and ultimately help in planning effective plant breeding strategies43.

The mean PIC value of 0.247 across all ISSR markers indicated moderate discriminatory power, as PIC values exceeding 0.25 are typically considered highly informative for plant genetic diversity studies. The narrow range of PIC values suggests consistent marker performance, beneficial for reliable population genetic analyses. Furthermore, the EMR, MI, and mean diversity index values confirmed the efficiency and informativeness of the ISSR marker system, enabling a robust assessment of genetic structure. The low number of monomorphic bands identified underscores the high intraspecific genetic variability in P. edulis14, providing a broad genetic base essential for breeding programs.

In contrast to other dominant molecular markers, such as RAPD in P. cincinnata Mast., which exhibited less polymorphism, ISSR markers appear to reveal more genomic region variations in intraspecific diversity analyses44. Among the primers used, UBC-841 not only revealed the highest number of polymorphic bands21 but also has a dinucleotide CT SSR locus annealing site, demonstrating the abundance of this microsatellite sequence in the P. edulis genome. In plants, microsatellite dinucleotides are more common than tri-, tetra-, and pentanucleotide tandem sequences45. This wide distribution can be used in strategies to study genetic diversity, enabling the selection of more informative primers for future analyses.

The I measure population genetic diversity, with values closer to zero indicating lower diversity46. In Kenya, 22 passion fruit genotypes from various production regions exhibited a lower I, ranging from 0.0388 to 0.163, indicating limited genetic diversity in those populations17. In this study, it was observed that the two populations collected from the agricultural production areas (LV and RJ) exhibited higher I compared to those derived from commercial seeds, which ranged from I = 0.249 to I = 0.308. These indices demonstrate genetic diversity in Brazilian populations compared to those observed in other countries. This finding is further supported by the moderate to high level of Nei's Hs (0.178) and the total Ht (0.307). The I above zero is desirable because the genetic diversity of the population suggests an ability to adapt to changes in environmental conditions and demographic fluctuations, both in the short and long term26.

For the five populations of P. edulis, 28 ISSR markers were used, and 420 polymorphic bands were obtained out of 452 bands, reaching percentage of polymorphic loci from 47.79 % in the BRS-RC population to 63.50 % in LV. This high level of polymorphism indicates that these populations maintain significant genetic diversity, which is essential for breeding programs. The variation in polymorphism levels among populations suggests different breeding histories and selection pressures14, with commercial cultivars (BRS-RC, FB300, IAC) showing somewhat lower diversity compared to populations from production areas (LV, RJ).

The genetic structure was clearly defined, with a significant proportion of the total genetic diversity (42.1 %, Gst = 0.421) distributed among populations. This moderate to high level of genetic differentiation is consistent with the estimate of low gene flow (Nm = 0.343), which is below the threshold of one migrant per generation considered necessary to counteract genetic divergence caused by genetic drift. The population structure analysis revealed clear genetic differentiation among the five populations, with the STRUCTURE analysis supporting both K = 2 and K = 5 as optimal clustering solutions38. This hierarchical structure suggests that the populations can be grouped into two major genetic lineages, which then subdivide into five distinct populations. This information is crucial for breeding programs as it indicates the genetic relationships among available germplasm and can guide crossing strategies to maximize heterosis and genetic gain47.

The genetic similarity coefficients, calculated according to Nei's48, ranged from 0.785 to 0.869. This range of Jaccard distances (0.100 to 0.773) between individuals underscores the wide spectrum of genetic relationships present, from very closely related to highly divergent individuals within the species. This indicates that while populations are related, they maintain sufficient genetic differentiation to be useful in breeding programs. The closest relationship between FB300 and LV populations (similarity = 0.869) suggests these could be used as complementary parents in breeding programs, while the more distant relationship between BRS-RC and IAC (similarity = 0.785) indicates these populations could be crossed to maximize genetic diversity in offspring47.

The reproductive characteristics of the species directlyinfluence the structure of genetic diversity within populations. P. edulis exhibits sporophytic self-incompatibility49, which makes maintaining a broad genetic base essential for establishing productive orchards, as the diversity of incompatibility alleles is crucial for efficient pollination and fruit set50. The distribution of genetic variance observed in our study, with 45 % of the variation occurring between populations (AMOVA), is consistent with other studies in Passiflora and aligns perfectly with the differentiation coefficient (Gst) of 0.421. For instance, an analysis of P. setacea using ISSR markers found a comparable distribution, with 57 % of the variation occurring within populations, reinforcing the importance of population differentiation in the genus51.

The PCoA analysis data confirmed the overall population distribution observed in the dendrogram. However, in the PCoA, the RJ and IAC populations were distributed differently, showing that the interpretation of genetic structure can vary slightly depending on the similarity/dissimilarity coefficient used. This pattern of population-specific clustering is comparable to results found in P. cristalina Vanderpl. & Zappi, where individuals from two populations remained isolated within their respective groups, possibly due to a geographical barrier that could impede gene flow52. Similarly, the five P. edulis populations analyzed in our study remained distinct within their respective groups, a pattern likely influenced by the geographical barriers imposed by the distances between the locations where these cultivars are produced. The low gene flow estimate (Nm) provides a quantitative measure supporting this hypothesis of restricted genetic exchange. This isolation promotes the fixation of unique gene variants arising from mutation and genetic drift8. However, it is important to note that geographic origin does not always dictate genetic structure, as occurred in Lathyrus accessions that were not influenced by their geographical distribution27.

Information on the species genetic diversity and population structure is of utmost priority for its sustainable use, conservation, and management plans24. The analysis of the genetic structure in our study identified five groups (ΔK = 5), a finding that was corroborated by the clustering patterns observed in both the dendrogram and the PCoA. The STRUCTURE analysis revealed clear genetic boundaries between populations, with minimal admixture between most groups, indicating strong population structure and limited gene flow among the studied populations.

The analysis results generally indicate that the five populations are genetically heterogeneous, with each population maintaining its distinct genetic identity while sharing a common evolutionary background. The five major groups revealed distinct population identities, each with its own structure and genetic diversity, showing minimal intermixing. This clear genetic differentiation facilitates the traceability of these plants for certification and quality control purposes. Furthermore, our results are comparable to findings in P. cristalina, where microsatellite markers also separated individuals into their respective populations, confirming a distinct population structure52.

In conclusion, this study successfully utilized ISSR markers to elucidate the genetic diversity and population structure of Passiflora edulis cultivars and wild populations. Our findings confirm substantial genetic variability within the germplasm, with commercially sourced populations exhibiting lower diversity compared to those from production areas, suggesting potential breeding bottlenecks. The detailed insights into genetic differentiation and relationships among these populations provide a crucial foundation for the strategic selection of parental lines in future P. edulis breeding programs. This knowledge will facilitate the development of new varieties with enhanced fruit quality, adaptability, and resilience, thereby contributing significantly to the sustainable improvement of passion fruit cultivation.
