Transcriptomic Analysis Reveals the Heterogeneous Role of Conducting Films Upon Electrical Stimulation
Authors: Nicholas B. Lawler, Uditi Bhatt, Vipul Agarwal, Cameron W. Evans, Priya Kaluskar, Sebastian E. Amos, Kai Chen, Yin Yao, Haibo Jiang, Yu Suk Choi, Minghao Zheng, Dino Spagnoli, Irene Suarez‐Martinez, Per B. Zetterlund, Vincent P. Wallace, Alan R. Harvey, Stuart I. Hodgetts, K. Swaminathan Iyer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral nervous system (CNS) injuries and neurodegenerative diseases have markedly poor prognoses and can result in permanent dysfunction due to the general inability of CNS neurons to regenerate. Differentiation of transplanted stem cells has emerged as a therapeutic avenue to regenerate tissue architecture in damaged areas. Electrical stimulation is a promising approach for directing the differentiation outcomes and pattern of outgrowth of transplanted stem cells, however traditional inorganic bio‐electrodes can induce adverse effects such as inflammation. This study demonstrates the implementation of two organic thin films, a polymer/reduced graphene oxide nanocomposite (P(rGO)) and PEDOT:PSS, that have favorable properties for implementation as conductive materials for electrical stimulation, as well as an inorganic indium tin oxide (ITO) conductive film. Transcriptomic analysis reveals that electrical stimulation improves neuronal differentiation of SH‐SY5Y cells on all three films, with the greatest effect for P(rGO). Unique material‐ and electrical stimuli‐mediated effects are observed, associated with differentiation, cell‐substrate adhesion, and translation. The work demonstrates that P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS are highly promising organic materials for the development of biocompatible, conductive scaffolds that will enhance electrically‐aided stem cell therapeutics for CNS injuries and neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction

The human brain is a complex and intricately wired system in which electrical polarizations facilitate neuronal communication and developmental processes. These electric potentials are fundamentally important in neuronal function, and can influence cellular dynamics and fate during early neuronal development.[ 1 , 2 ] Electrical signals within the brain can be detected to monitor neuronal activity, and more recently electrical stimulation has been explored as a clinical tool to affect activity and influence cellular processes. In particular, electrical stimulation has been implemented in deep brain stimulation for the treatment of advanced Parkinson's disease, neuromodulation for spinal cord injury, and for guiding regenerative cell therapies.[ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ]

Regenerative stem cell therapies have emerged as a promising approach for improving the cellular microenvironment and repairing tissue architecture at the site of injury to restore function.[ 6 ] This is particularly important for neurodegenerative diseases and central nervous system (CNS) injuries that have high rates of permanent dysfunction due to the general inability of CNS neurons to naturally regenerate.[ 7 ] Cell populations at the site of injury can be restored using chemical, biological, and physical factors to synergistically regulate stem cell differentiation outcomes.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] One such physical factor, electrical stimulation, is a highly promising tool in regenerative therapeutics that allows a high degree of control over voltage, current, frequency, and duration to influence differentiation outcomes and cell function.[ 2 , 11 ] Electrical stimulation can also regulate the direction of migration and neurite and axon growth.[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ] Achieving this degree of control over differentiation hinges on the design of suitable electrode materials.

Electrodes that interface with biological tissues have been developed to facilitate both diagnostic and therapeutic applications. Traditional inorganic electrode materials such as gold or platinum have disadvantageous properties for implementation in vivo and can result in the induction of inflammation and tissue damage, and may require ongoing surgical replacement due to fouling.[ 17 , 18 ] Consequently, advanced materials have been developed to enhance electrode flexibility, conductivity, and biocompatibility. These materials, including conductive polymers and graphene‐based nanomaterials, have been shown to enhance neuronal differentiation with electrical stimulation, demonstrating their regenerative potential.[ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 ]

In the brain, there is notable heterogeneity in neuronal structure and function, characterized by differences in gene expression and complex regulation of the transcriptome during differentiation.[ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] Consequently, it is important to consider the full extent of transcriptomic changes induced by electrically stimulated differentiation using these advanced electrode materials and distinguish between the role of the material and electrical stimulation. Herein, we implement three classes of materials, a polymer/reduced graphene oxide nanocomposite (P(rGO)), poly(3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), and indium tin oxide (ITO) as conductive films for electrical stimulation. We systematically highlight material‐ and electrical stimuli‐associated transcriptomic alterations during the differentiation of neuron‐like SH‐SY5Y cells. This study demonstrates that these advanced materials are highly promising for use as electrodes for electrically‐aided regenerative therapies and can induce unique transcriptomic effects in the presence or absence of electrical stimulation.

Results

Initially, we prepared P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO thin films on glass slides for cell growth and stimulation. PEDOT:PSS is an organic conjugated polymer that is a mixed ionic/electronic conductor and is biocompatible and highly stable in neural environments.[ 30 , 31 ] Reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is an electronic conductor that is a commonly used intermediate between highly conductive, hydrophobic graphene and the hydrophilic but poorly conducting graphene oxide, and was integrated in a P(rGO) nanocomposite to facilitate the preparation of robust and flexible films.[ 32 , 33 , 34 ] On the other hand, ITO is an inorganic n‐type degenerate semiconductor that is often used in cellular electrical stimulation.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ]

The P(rGO) film was prepared by drop‐casting at ambient temperature followed by thermal annealing,[ 39 , 40 , 41 ] resulting in a film thickness of 20.0 ± 1.74 µm (mean ± SEM). In contrast, the PEDOT:PSS film was prepared by spin coating[ 42 ] to produce a 1.54 ± 0.56 µm thick film, and the ITO was sputter coated by the manufacturer as a 134 ± 8.22 nm thick layer on the glass slide (Figure 1a; Figure S1, Supporting Information).

The stiffness of the substrates on which cells grow is well documented to affect cell growth, function, and fate, and the high stiffness of inorganic electrodes has been linked to inflammation.[ 43 , 44 ] Stiffness of the three films was characterized by atomic force microscopy force (AFM) curves, with Young's moduli of 3.63 ± 1.60 MPa, 96.7 ± 30.2 kPa, and 6.32 ± 2.90 MPa for the P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO, respectively (Figure 1b). The topology was also characterized by AFM and the root mean square (RMS) roughness determined as 101 ± 30.2, 5.15 ± 3.37, and 1.99 ± 1.36 nm for P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO, respectively (Figure 1c).

We evaluated the potential for these materials as biocompatible scaffolds by initially testing the viability of cells grown on the film surface. SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were cultured and treated with a combination of serum reduction, retinoic acid and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) on each of the surfaces, as a model of neuronal differentiation (Figure 1d). Cell viability exceeding 85% was maintained for differentiating SH‐SY5Y cells on each of the conductive films as well as the plastic differentiation control (Plastic) of SH‐SY5Y cells differentiated in standard cell culture‐treated well plates with the same biochemical differentiation process and no electrical stimulation (Figure S2, Supporting Information).

Next, the differentiation of the SH‐SY5Y cells on the films alone, without electrical stimulation, was assessed via immunocytochemistry. Cells were stained with antibodies for nestin and βIII‐tubulin to evaluate the protein expression levels and cellular morphology after the full 10 d differentiation (Figure 1e). Differentiation toward a neuron‐like phenotype was observed on all three films, characterized by the formation and extension of neurites, and strong βIII tubulin and low nestin immunofluorescence. Notably, SH‐SY5Y cells growing on each of the three conductive films prior to retinoic acid and BDNF treatment did not exhibit any visible morphological changes associated with differentiation and thus were not examined further.

Differentiation on the films alone, without electrical stimulation, and the plastic differentiation control was examined at the RNA level via quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Gene expression levels were determined relative to undifferentiated SH‐SY5Y cells maintained in growth medium (Figure 1f). Significantly increased expression of the differentiation‐associated tubulin beta 3 class III (TUBB3) and microtubule‐associated protein tau (MAPT) genes was observed for each of the films and the plastic differentiation control, relative to the untreated cells. The levels of nestin (NES) gene expression were largely unchanged, consistent with previous reports of SH‐SY5Y differentiation (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[ 45 ] Material‐associated effects of the films are evident in the expression of TUBB3 and MAPT. For both genes, differentiation on all three films resulted in lower expression than the plastic differentiation control (p < 0.0001).

P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO Films Alter Gene Expression

A wide range of transcriptomic changes occur during differentiation. The substrate on which cells are grown has also been shown to influence gene expression and cellular function, for example stiffness can affect differentiation outcomes.[ 46 ] Therefore, it is highly possible that the conductive films may uniquely affect RNAs associated with a range of cellular processes. Consequently, we used RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) to investigate changes in the transcriptome during the differentiation of SH‐SY5Y cells on each of the conductive films, denoted as P(rGO)/BDNF, PEDOT:PSS/BDNF, and ITO/BDNF (Figure S3, Supporting Information), compared to the plastic differentiation control. The plastic differentiation control demonstrated large‐scale transcriptomic modifications relative to undifferentiated SH‐SY5Y cells, with 3262 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, corresponding to genes altered during neuronal differentiation (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

All three conductive films induced transcriptomic modifications relative to the plastic differentiation control, with 2309 DEGs for P(rGO)/BDNF, 1622 for PEDOT:PSS/BDNF, and 1790 for ITO/BDNF (Figure 2a,b). Interestingly, for all three of the materials ≈63% of these genes had higher expression than the plastic differentiation control, while 37% were decreased. Of these DEGs, 984 are shared between all three films, while the remainder are unique to a subset.

Consistent with the qPCR data, the transcriptomes of the cells differentiated on all three conductive films are more similar to the plastic differentiation control than the undifferentiated SH‐SY5Y cells (clusters i and ii) (Figure 2c). However, for a subset of differentiation‐associated genes the expression levels for P(rGO)/BDNF, PEDOT:PSS/BDNF, and ITO/BDNF are between the plastic differentiation and undifferentiated controls (clusters iii and iv), indicating that differentiation on the films results in a less mature neuronal outcome. This was particularly apparent when considering the expression of genes typically used as “undifferentiated” and “neuronal” markers (Figure 2d). On all three films, the SOX2, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, VIM, and NEUROD1 “undifferentiated” marker genes that are associated with undifferentiated SH‐SY5Y cells, and the MKI67 proliferation marker are downregulated relative to the undifferentiated control.[ 47 ] The NTRK2, SYP, GAP43, MAPT and MAP2 “neuronal” genes are upregulated, consistent with neuronal maturation and previous studies of SH‐SY5Y differentiation.[ 45 ] However, for all three films most of the “undifferentiated” genes had higher expression than the plastic differentiation control and “neuronal” genes were lower, indicating that differentiation on the electrically conducting films is not as complete as in the cell‐culture treated plate.

Electrical Stimulation Caused Transcriptome‐Wide Effects on P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO Conductive Films

To assess the effect of electrical stimulation (ES) on differentiation using the P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO films, SH‐SY5Y cells were electrically stimulated with a 0.1 VP‐P (from 0 to +0.1 V), 1 Hz electrical potential for 8 h d−1 for the final 4 days of differentiation in conjunction with the incubation with BDNF‐supplemented media (Figure 3a,b). The conductivity of the thin films was initially determined via 4‐point probe, with the greatest sheet resistance identified for the P(rGO), 482.3 ± 1.62 kΩ square−1, followed by PEDOT:PSS, 5.32 ± 0.06 kΩ square−1, and ITO, 39.80 ± 0.16 Ω square−1 (Figure 3c).

The electrically stimulated cells were βIII‐tubulin positive and nestin negative for all three conductive films as observed through immunofluorescence, and qPCR revealed electrical stimulation‐associated effects on the expression of TUBB3 and MAPT genes for P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS (Figure 3d,e). For the P(rGO), electrical stimulation increased the expression of both TUBB3 and MAPT to levels more similar to the plastic differentiation control (no electrical stimulation). The electrically stimulated cells on PEDOT:PSS exhibited decreased TUBB3 expression (p < 0.0001), while there was no significant change in cells on ITO, highlighting the material‐dependence of the electrical stimulation effects. During stimulation, no significant change in cell viability was observed for PEDOT:PSS and ITO, and viability decreased to 57% for P(rGO) (Figure S2, Supporting Information).

Transcriptome analysis with RNA‐seq revealed electrical stimulation induced further significant differential gene expression for all three conductive films. Electrical stimulation of cells differentiating on the P(rGO), denoted as P(rGO)/BDNF+ES, induced the greatest transcriptomic effects, resulting in 615 DEGs, whereas 88 DEGs were identified for PEDOT:PSS/BDNF+ES and 71 for ITO/BDNF+ES (Figure 4a,c). The electrical stimulation‐induced DEGs are largely unique to each material, with only 44 genes shared by any two conditions and 4 genes differentially expressed on all three films with electrical stimulation.

In general, electrical stimulation resulted in decreased expression of “undifferentiated” and increased neuronal “activity” marker genes compared to the films alone, corresponding to improved neuronal differentiation (Figure 4b). This was most pronounced for the P(rGO)/BDNF+ES sample, with decreased expression of SOX2, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, MKI67, VIM, NEUROD1, and GAP43, and increased expression of the FOS, FOSB, JUN transcription factors that have been associated with neuronal function, as well as NR4A1.[ 48 , 49 ] The expression and activation of FOS and JUN in neurons are induced by neuronal activity, including Ca+2 flux through voltage‐sensitive calcium channels.[ 50 , 51 , 52 ] Electrical stimulation can activate ion channels by altering the cell membrane potential,[ 53 , 54 ] leading to the expression of these calcium‐regulated transcription factors. Both PEDOT:PSS/BDNF+ES and ITO/BDNF+ES similarly downregulated many of the “undifferentiated” markers, and upregulated most neuronal “activity” markers, but not as many or to the same extent as the P(rGO)/BDNF+ES. Together, these results show that electrical stimulation on all three conductive films promoted improved differentiation toward a neuronal outcome compared to the films alone.

Furthermore, electrical stimulation promoted a large‐scale transcriptomic shift toward the plastic differentiation control, further indicating improved or accelerated neuronal differentiation on these conductive films. For the P(rGO) and ITO, 79.0 and 71.8% of genes, respectively, that were upregulated on the films alone relative to the plastic differentiation control had decreased expression upon electrical stimulation, and 67.5 and 76.9% of the genes that are downregulated on the films alone were then increased by electrical stimulation (Figure 4d,e). On PEDOT:PSS this was less pronounced at 59.0 and 54.2%, respectively. This resulted in greater similarities between films with electrical stimulation and the plastic differentiation control than for the films alone, with 1275 fewer DEGs between P(rGO)/BDNF+ES and the plastic differentiation control than P(rGO)/BDNF and plastic differentiation control, 137 less for PEDOT:PSS, and 498 for ITO.

To explore the functional implications of these changes to gene expression, Gene Ontology analysis was performed on the DEGs associated with the film materials and electrical stimulation (Figure 5 ; Figure S6, Supporting Information). For all three conductive films without electrical stimulation, neurogenesis, neuronal projection, axonogenesis, and metabolic activity pathways were significantly altered relative to the plastic differentiation control, consistent with the reduced expression of neuron‐associated genes. Cellular components and functions involved in neuronal differentiation, such as actin filaments, distal axon components, growth cones, and microtubules were similarly affected. Furthermore, several pathways associated with cell‐substrate interactions were identified, including focal adhesion, cell‐substrate junctions, and cadherin binding, demonstrating that the cellular interactions with the substrate were altered on all three conductive films relative to the plastic differentiation control.

In comparison, the pathways associated with electrical stimulation were less consistent across the three films. P(rGO)/BDNF+ES induced the greatest number of differential pathways, consistent with the number of DEGs. Microtubule and cytoskeletal pathways were enriched for P(rGO)/BDNF+ES, while focal adhesion and cell‐substrate junction pathways were significantly altered for both P(rGO)/BDNF+ES and ITO/BDNF+ES, as were ribosomal and translation‐associated processes. In comparison, cell cycle and mitosis pathways were altered for PEDOT:PSS/BDNF+ES and P(rGO)/BDNF+ES.

Previously, the effects of electrical stimulation have been associated with membrane potential depolarization and altered ion channel conductance, resulting in activation of voltage‐gated ion channels and a flux of charged ions across the cell membrane.[ 53 , 54 ] However, in addition to the charged ions that are important in propagating the action potentials and regulating cellular functions, many functionally relevant biomolecules are also charged. BDNF is one such molecule that is highly positively charged and essential to SH‐SY5Y differentiation.[ 55 ] We hypothesized that the charged BDNF molecule may interact with the applied electric field, possibly affecting its function and thus the differentiation process. We performed computational modeling of the BDNF/NT4 heterodimer to examine if the experimentally applied electric field may affect the structure of the molecule and thus its potential interactions with Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB). Expression of NTRK2 mRNA, which encodes TrkB, was increased >29‐fold for each of the films, both with and without electrical stimulation, as well as the plastic differentiation control, relative to the undifferentiated control. TrkB is known to be expressed in response to the retinoic acid stage of differentiation and the addition of BDNF can then induce Trk autophosphorylation,[ 56 ] highlighting the importance of the BDNF‐TrkB interaction during the differentiation. The molecular dynamics simulations showed the electric field conditions used experimentally induced no change in the structure of BDNF, however stronger electric fields may significantly deform the molecule (Figure S7, Supporting Information).

Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated successful neuronal differentiation of SH‐SY5Y cells on three conductive films, P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO, prepared for use as biocompatible scaffolds for electrical stimulation. In addition to morphological changes and altered expression of neuronal marker genes, RNA sequencing revealed significant transcriptome‐wide modifications associated with each of the three film materials. Electrical stimulation of the differentiating SH‐SY5Y cells further altered the transcriptome and enhanced neuronal differentiation, demonstrating that electrical stimulation using these materials may improve stem cell‐based regenerative therapies.

A key hurdle in the development of electrodes for use in the brain is the disparity between the stiffness of traditional inorganic electrodes and soft brain tissues. All three materials used in this study have significantly lower Young's moduli than most metals and silicon (50–200 GPa),[ 57 ] and thus will result in a smaller mechanical mismatch with biological tissues to reduce adverse effect such as inflammation and glial cell activation.[ 58 ] Furthermore, these materials were prepared as thin films on glass, which itself has a Young's modulus >50 GPa.[ 57 ] When implemented as flexible films in a soft tissue environment, the P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS may have decreased stiffnesses, leading to improved integration with in vivo systems.

Visualization of the cells on each of the conductive films via immunofluorescence revealed high βIII‐tubulin and low nestin expression, with characteristic neurite growth that was consistent for each of the films, both without and with electrical stimulation, as well as in the plastic differentiation control, indicative of neuronal differentiation. Decreased levels of “undifferentiated” and increased expression of “neuronal” marker genes relative to the undifferentiated control were also consistent with neuronal differentiation. Transcriptome‐wide investigation via RNA‐seq revealed characteristic differences due to both the different film materials and electrical stimulation. Without electrical stimulation, the altered expression of “undifferentiated” and “neuronal” marker genes relative to untreated cells was not as pronounced as the plastic differentiation control, corresponding to less mature neuronal differentiation at the transcriptional level (Figure 2). The significant differences in neuronal development, neurite growth, and axonogenesis pathways on each of the films are similarly associated with less mature differentiation on the conductive films than the plastic. Focal adhesion genes are upregulated on all three conductive films and contribute to cell‐substrate interactions, cell adhesion, and migration processes that are involved in differentiating, rather than differentiated cells. There were many genes differentially expressed on only a subset of the conductive films, which were also associated with neuronal differentiation and focal adhesion pathways. Few alternate pathways are identified in these gene subsets, indicating that the predominant effects of the films are on neuronal differentiation and cell adhesion.

Electrical stimulation has previously been shown to promote neuronal differentiation both in vitro and in vivo,[ 2 , 11 ] and in this study, we also observed improvement using P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO films as electrodes. For each of the conductive films, electrical stimulation induced a large‐scale transcriptomic shift toward the plastic differentiation control and was more pronounced for P(rGO)/BDNF+ES and ITO/BDNF+ES. Similarly, decreased “undifferentiated” and increased neuronal “activity” markers by electrical stimulation indicated improved neuronal differentiation. The increased expression of neuronal activity‐associated transcription factors and simultaneously altered ribosomal and translational pathways is consistent with previous work describing widespread translational remodeling during neuronal differentiation.[ 59 ] Considerable differences in the resistivity of the conductive films were observed, which is attributed to the conduction modes of the materials. ITO is an n‐type degenerate semiconductor with metal‐like conductivity arising from free electrons,[ 35 ] whereas conductivity of rGO is associated with mobile π electrons, and PEDOT:PSS is a mixed ionic and electronic conductor. Doping of PEDOT with PSS and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) reduces the energy gap and improves conductivity. Conversely, the oxygen groups on the rGO increase the density of sp3‐hybridised carbons in the lattice, reducing conductivity relative to pristine graphene, however are important in increasing the hydrophilicity of the material to improve cell adhesion.[ 33 , 42 , 60 ] Interestingly, we did not observe a strong correlation between the sheet resistance of the films and the effect of electrical stimulation. The P(rGO) had the highest sheet resistance but unexpectedly induced the greatest number of DEGs and caused the largest change in the expression of differentiation‐associated genes with electrical stimulation. On the other hand, ITO had the lowest sheet resistance and resulted in fewer DEGs than P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS, but caused a similar transcriptome‐wide shift toward the plastic differentiation control as P(rGO). This highlights the complex nature of the interactions between these conductive materials and cells, and the importance of large‐scale characterization techniques such as transcriptome‐ and proteome‐wide analysis to identify the full extent of effects linked to the material properties.

Overall, the favorable material properties of the conductive P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS films and the high viabilities of cells grown and differentiated on these materials highlight their potential for implantable conductive scaffolds with a variety of biological applications, including deep brain stimulation. Compared to traditional inorganic electrodes, P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS both have lower stiffness and higher flexibility, which is linked to decreased tissue inflammation and glial activation.[ 58 ] Furthermore, electrical stimulation using the conductive films resulted in enhanced neuronal differentiation and the expression of neuronal activity markers. Here, the applied electric field was perpendicular to the thin film. Parallel fields have been shown to induce directional migration and axonal growth,[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ] representing an additional mechanism by which electrical stimulation along these conductive films may be able to regulate neuronal differentiation. Consequently, P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS represent promising materials for use in regenerative stem cell therapies for CNS and peripheral nerve injuries, as well as neurodegenerative diseases, to induce targeted differentiation of stem cells into neurons. In this study, the materials were implemented as conductive thin films which have potential applications as brain surface electrodes and nerve guidance scaffolds.[ 61 , 62 , 63 ] Both PEDOT:PSS and graphene‐based materials have also been implemented as conductive 3D scaffolds such as hydrogels, foams, and microstructures, significantly expanding the potential applications of these materials.[ 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 ]

Electrical stimulation has been linked to a number of interaction mechanisms within cells, including the activation of voltage‐gated ion channels and the flux of ions within cells.[ 53 , 54 ] In this work, we considered the potential interactions of electric fields with the structure of the highly charged biomolecules involved in cellular differentiation. While no significant change in BDNF structure was observed under experimental conditions, stronger electric fields induced significant deformation of the molecule, and molecular modification due to the electric field may represent an additional mechanism by which electrical stimulation can affect cellular function. Many of the functionally relevant molecules within the cell are charged, such as nerve growth factor (NGF), histones, and nucleic acids,[ 70 , 71 ] and our results indicate that sufficiently high potentials may modify molecular structures, which can affect their interactions with receptors and other molecules. While the individual BDNF structure was not significantly altered, this warrants further investigations as to how receptor interactions may be affected, as well as the implications for other charged molecule dynamics, such as DNA‐histone binding and ion channel activity. Given that applied electric fields can alter the flux of charged ions within cells, it may be interesting to also consider how electric fields affect the distribution of these charged molecules and thus their functions within cells.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated and compared two biocompatible materials that are highly suitable for implementation as conductive scaffolds for electrical stimulation. The material properties of the P(rGO) and PEDOT:PSS thin films are closer to that of biological tissues in comparison to commonly used metallic materials and thus should result in improved integration in vivo. Successful growth and neuronal differentiation of SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were demonstrated on both conductive films and electrical stimulation further improved differentiation. Thus, polymer/reduced graphene oxide nanocomposite and PEDOT:PSS are highly promising materials for the development of biocompatible conductive scaffolds for deep‐brain stimulation and electrically‐aided stem cell therapeutics for CNS injuries and neurodegenerative diseases.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Polymer/GO Nanocomposite

The poly(styrene‐stat‐n‐butyl acrylate)/graphene oxide/SDS aqueous latex (P(GO)) nanocomposite was prepared using miniemulsion polymerisation as previously reported.[ 39 , 40 , 41 ]

Briefly, the as‐received aqueous GO dispersion (Graphenea, 4 mg mL−1, ≈600 nm by dynamic light scattering (DLS)[ 41 ]) was diluted in Milli‐Q H2O to obtain the final concentration of 5% (w/w) rel. to total monomers and ultrasonicated at a 70% amplitude on ice for 10 min (Branson Digital Sonifier). The sonicated GO dispersion was then added to a 25 mL glass vial containing a 1:1 mixture of styrene (St) and n‐butyl acrylate (nBA) (0.525 g each, 7% (w/w) rel. to water), hexadecane (HD, 0.0525 g, 5% (w/w) rel. to total monomers), azobis(isobutyronitrile) (AIBN, 0.05 g, 0.25 m rel. to HD and total monomers), and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 11.525 mg, 1% (w/w) rel. to organic phase), and magnetically stirred at room temperature for 15 min. Next, a miniemulsion was prepared by ultrasonicating the mixture on ice at 70% amplitude for 10 min. The miniemulsion was degassed under nitrogen for 20 min on ice prior to polymerization at 70 °C for 24 h under constant stirring.

P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO Film Preparation

ITO‐coated slides were purchased from SPI Supplies (06401‐CF).

PEDOT:PSS thin films were prepared on clean glass slides by spin coating.[ 42 ] The PEDOT:PSS (Sigma–Aldrich, 483095) was doped with 5% w/w DMSO (Sigma–Aldrich, D2438) and sonicated for 20 min then filtered (0.45 µm). 750 µL of the solution was spin‐coated on each glass slide (2000 rpm, 60 s). Samples were annealed at 120 °C for 15 min.

The P(GO) aqueous dispersion was uniformly spread onto 2.5 cm × 6.5 cm sections of clean glass slides (35 µL cm−2) and dried for 24 h at 25 °C to allow film formation. The obtained film was then thermally annealed at 160 °C for 24 h to reduce the graphene oxide (forming P(rGO)) and render the film electrically conductive.

Samples were prepared for cell culture experiments by affixing 4‐ and 8‐well chamber slides on top of the conductive films.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

Substrate cross‐sections were visualized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to determine the thickness. SEM images were recorded on an FEI Helios Nanolab G3 CX DualBeam FIB/SEM using an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a probe current of 21 pA.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

Sample stiffness was measured using an MFP‐3D atomic force microscope (Asylum Research) equipped with 200 µm long gold‐coated pyramidal‐tipped pyrex‐nitride cantilevers (NanoWorld, PNP‐TR) as previously described.[ 72 ] Briefly, samples were immersed in PBS and probed with 2 nN indentations using approach and retraction velocities of 2 and 10 µm s−1, respectively. All indentations were repeated in triplicate to ensure a stable reading. The linear portion of the contact‐generated force curves was analyzed with custom code in Igor Pro to derive Young's Modulus. Each sample was indented at ten sites that were consistent between conditions. All measurements were taken at 24.5 °C.

AFM roughness measurements were performed using a Dimension Icon SPM (Bruker) equipped with a Nanoscope V controller (Bruker). The measurements were performed using the peak force tapping mode with a SCANASYST probe (Bruker) and a scan size of 5 µm. The peak force setpoint and the feedback gain were adjusted to optimize tracking of the sample surface as required, without introducing significant feedback noise. The image resolution was set to 512 pixels line−1 and analyzed using Gwyddion (version 2.61) software.

4‐Point Probe

The sheet resistances of the P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO films were evaluated via 4‐point probe (Ossila, T2001A3) that was calibrated before each measurement to an ITO‐coated glass standard.

Cell Culture

SH‐SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells (ATCC, CRL‐2266) were cultured in growth media consisting of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium/Nutrient Mixture F‐12 (DMEM/F12) (Gibco, 11320‐033) supplemented with 10% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099‐141). Cells were maintained at 37 °C, 5% (v/v) CO2 in a humidified incubator during maintenance and for all experiments and passaged at 80–90% confluency with 1x TrypLE Express (Gibco, 12604‐013). Cell culture medium was replaced every 4 days, and cells used for experiments between passages P4‐P10.

Differentiation

Prior to differentiation experiments, the conductive films (P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, and ITO) and 12‐well plates used for the plastic differentiation control were coated with poly‐L‐lysine (PLL) and laminin. Briefly, samples were incubated with 0.05 mg mL−1 PLL (Sigma–Aldrich, P6282) for 1 h at room temperature then washed with sterile, double‐distilled H2O (×2) and dried. Samples were then incubated with 10 µg mL−1 laminin (Gibco, 23017) in DMEM/F12 at 37 °C, 5% (v/v) CO2 for 24 h. Media was removed, and samples air‐dried for 1 h before use.

SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded at 15625 cells cm−2 (immunocytochemistry) and 31250 cells cm−2 (qPCR and RNA‐seq) on PLL and laminin‐coated P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, or ITO films in normal growth media and maintained for 2 d. Serum was reduced to 5% FBS on day 2, 2.5% on day 3 and 1% on day 5. 100 U mL−1 penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140‐122) was added on day 2. With the reduction of FBS, 10 µm retinoic acid (Sigma–Aldrich, R2625) was added to the culture on day 3 and maintained until day 6. On day 6 media was replaced with DMEM/F12 supplemented with 50 ng mL−1 brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (Sigma–Aldrich, B3795) and maintained for 4 d.

Electrical Stimulation

Electrical stimulation was performed using a two‐electrode setup described in Figure 3b. A platinum wire was suspended in solution above the cells and used as the anode, while the conductive P(rGO), PEDOT:PSS, or ITO film acted as the cathode. Cells were stimulated with a periodic, 0.1 VP‐P (from 0 to +0.1 V), 1 Hz, square wave potential for 8 h d−1 for the final 4 days of differentiation using a Grass Instrument Co. S88B.

Live/Dead Assay

SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded at 15625 cells cm−2 in 8‐well chamber slides on each conductive film or in 12‐well cell culture plates for the plastic differentiation control and undifferentiated control samples. Cells were differentiated and electrically stimulated as described above. Following the final electrical stimulation, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead assay (Thermofisher, L3224) as per the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, media was removed and the cells gently washed with PBS. Cells were then incubated with 2 µm calcein‐AM and 4 µm ethidium homodimer‐1 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted microscope.

Immunocytochemistry

Differentiation of cells was observed via immunofluorescence with antibodies to βIII‐tubulin and nestin.

SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded at 15625 cells cm−2 in 8‐well chamber slides on each conductive film or on coverslips in a 12‐well cell culture plate for the plastic differentiation control and undifferentiated control samples. Cells were differentiated and electrically stimulated as previously described. Following the final electrical stimulation, cells were prefixed with 4% w/v paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Scharlau, PA00950500) for 2 min at room temperature. The media was removed, and cells washed with PBS (×2). Cells were then fixed with 4% w/v PFA for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were washed with PBS (×2) and then permeabilized with 0.2% v/v Triton X‐100 (Sigma–Aldrich, T8787) in PBS for 10 min. The cells were washed with PBS (×2) and then blocked with 4% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma–Aldrich, A9418) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with 1 µg mL−1 mouse monoclonal anti‐nestin (Abcam, ab6320, [196908], lot: GR3280465‐1) and 1 µg mL−1 rabbit polyclonal anti‐βIII‐tubulin (Abcam, ab18207, lot: GR3410986‐2) diluted in 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.1% (v/v) Tween‐20 (Sigma–Aldrich, P9416) in PBS for 16 h at 4 °C. Cells were washed with 0.1% (v/v) Tween‐20 in PBS (×3) and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with secondary antibody solution containing 4 µg mL−1 donkey anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam, ab150105), 4 µg mL−1 goat anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (ThermoFisher, A11037, lot: 1420978) and 1 µg mL−1 Hoechst 34580 (Sigma–Aldrich, 63493) diluted in 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.1% (v/v) Tween‐20 in PBS. Cells were then washed with 0.1% (v/v) Tween‐20 in PBS (×3) and PBS (×2) before being coverslipped using Fluoromount‐G mounting media (Invitrogen, 00‐4958‐02).

Microscopy

Immunocytochemically stained samples were imaged using a confocal microscope (Nikon C2), imaging cell nuclei (Hoechst 34580), Nestin (AF488), and βIII‐tubulin (AF594). For each antibody, identical acquisition settings were used for each sample.

Images were processed using FIJI software (2.1.0),[ 73 ] by maximum intensity z‐projections.

RT‐qPCR

SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded at 31250 cells cm−2 in 8‐well chamber slides on each conductive film or in a 12‐well cell culture plate for the plastic differentiation control and undifferentiated control samples. Cells were differentiated and electrically stimulated as described above. The undifferentiated control was maintained in growth media for the full duration of the experiment, with media replaced every 4 days. After the final electrical stimulation, cells were harvested with 1×TrypLE and RNA extracted and purified using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106) with on‐column DNase digestion using RNase‐Free DNase Set (Qiagen, 79254). RNA was then reverse transcribed using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, 205313). cDNA was analyzed using QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, 208056) to assess the expression of TUBB3 (tubulin beta 3 class III), MAPT (microtubule associated protein tau), and NES (nestin) genes. GAPDH (glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase) and ACTB (β‐actin) housekeeping genes were used to normalize gene expression. Primers were acquired from Qiagen (QT00079247, QT01680476, QT00083713, QT00017199, QT00235781). qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 II (Roche).

A comparative delta threshold cycle (ΔΔCT) method was used to determine changes in gene expression, normalized against both housekeeping genes.

RNA‐Sequencing

Cells were harvested from two biological replicates using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106) with on‐column DNase digestion using RNase‐Free DNase Set (Qiagen, 79254). Quantification and quality control were performed using Qubit (Invitrogen, Q32852) and Bioanalyzer. RNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, E7490S) along with NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7760S) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB, E6440S). Libraries were sequenced using an Illumina Hiseq Xten (50 × 106 reads per sample, 150 bp PE reads) by Macrogen Oceania.

Data Analysis

Quality control of the fastq files was performed with FastQC. Reads were aligned to GRCh38.p13/hg38 using STAR.[ 74 ] Release 43 (GRCh38.p13) of the Gencode gene annotation was used. Reads were assigned to genes and counts generated using HTSeq.[ 75 ] Genes with fewer than 10 counts were omitted. Differential gene expression was analyzed via EBSeq in R,[ 76 ] and genes identified as significantly differentially expressed at an FDR < 0.05. Gene ontology enrichment analysis was conducted on significantly differentially expressed genes with FDR < 0.05 using the clusterProfiler package.[ 77 ]

Molecular Dynamics

Molecular dynamics was performed on the BDNF/NT4 heterodimer (PDB, 1B8M) using LAMMPS.[ 78 ] The molecule was modeled at 310.15 K in 0.5 fs timesteps for 75 ps using the CHARMM force field and NVE ensemble. An electric field of 0.1 V over 2 mm was applied using the fix_efield command.
