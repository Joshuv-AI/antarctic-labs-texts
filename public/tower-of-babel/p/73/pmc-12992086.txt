CSPG4+ extracellular vesicles carry fibronectin and interleukin-11 and reflect radiographic severity in knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Xin Zhang, Lindsey Adair MacFarlane, Janet L. Huebner, Jason Jinkun Tao, Jeffrey Neil Katz, Virginia Byers Kraus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4)+ plasma extracellular vesicles (EVs) as a novel biomarker for knee osteoarthritis (OA) severity across independent study cohorts.MethodsRadiographic and symptomatic OA severity were evaluated in participants from two separate institutions. EVs were isolated from plasma (discovery cohort, n = 51; validation cohort, n = 92) and synovial fluid (SF) (n = 61) from 204 OA patients and characterized by morphology, surface markers, and pathogenic cargo, and evaluated for associations with knee OA severity. Single-cell RNA sequencing datasets (GSE152805 and GSE216651) were used to elucidate the joint tissue localization of the genes of interest.ResultsAcross both discovery and validation cohorts, plasma CSPG4+ EV frequencies showed positive correlations with knee radiographic OA severity scores, reflecting joint structural damage, but not with symptomatic OA severity. In OA joint tissues, particularly in damaged cartilage-derived chondrocytes, the CSPG4 gene was co-expressed with FN1 and IL11 genes, which encode the OA pathogenic mediators fibronectin (FN1) and interleukin-11 (IL-11). Consistent with this, OA SF CSPG4+ EVs carried higher proportions of FN1 and IL-11 compared with CSPG4- EVs, identifying these proteins as enriched pathogenic cargo of the CSPG4+ EV subset in OA.ConclusionsWe identified CSPG4+ EV subpopulations in plasma as novel biomarkers of knee radiographic OA severity, reflecting underlying structural joint damage. In SF, CSPG4+ EVs were enriched in FN1 and IL-11, potent mediators of matrix remodeling and inflammatory signaling in OA, consistent with a role for CSPG4+ EV cargo in joint deterioration.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), the most common form of arthritis, affects nearly 600 million people worldwide and is the leading cause of physical disability among older adults, substantially diminishing their quality of life [1]. Beyond its clinical impact, OA contributes to age-related morbidity and mortality and imposes more than $130 billion in annual healthcare and productivity costs [2]. Despite its burden, current treatments for OA only alleviate symptoms without halting structural progression. Biomarkers to detect early OA and risk of structural progression are needed urgently. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), nanosized biofluid-based particles that carry molecular signatures of their parent cells, are emerging as promising biomarkers in many fields [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. EVs are stable, widely distributed, and reflect physiological and pathological states [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

EVs from patients with knee OA are highly heterogeneous, varying in size from small (≤100 nm) to large (up to 6000 nm), and carrying diverse surface markers and pathogenic cargo [6,[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. While most studies have focused on small EVs (SEVs), such as exosomes, our group has taken a broader approach by examining the full EV size spectrum, including SEVs, medium (MEVs), and large EVs (LEVs) [10,14,15]. This comprehensive analysis is critical, as the roles of MEVs and LEVs in OA remain largely unknown. We identified multiple SEV, MEV, and LEV subpopulations, particularly those with immune-related surface markers, that correlate with radiographic OA (rOA) severity and predict disease progression [6,10,14]. These pathogenic EVs hold promise not only as prognostic biomarkers but also as novel therapeutic targets in OA.

Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4, also known as nerve/glial antigen 2 [NG2]), a transmembrane protein well-studied in cancer, interacts through its chondroitin sulfate chain with extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as fibronectin (FN1), integrins, collagen, and matrix metalloproteinases to promote cell adhesion, migration, MMP activation, and ECM degradation [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Based on discovery proteomic analyses by mass spectrometry in patients with knee OA, we identified chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4) as one of the key surface markers of pathogenic synovial fluid (SF) EVs [6]. In end-stage OA joints, we observed enriched CSPG4 gene expression in chondrocytes from damaged cartilage [6]. We also observed that CSPG4 peptides in SF EVs had strong positive correlations with over 75 % of pathogenic SF EV peptides associated with knee rOA severity, including numerous peptides derived from FN1 [6]. Notably, the FN1 gene is expressed ubiquitously by OA joint tissue cells [10], and yielded an area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve > 0.9 for discriminating individuals with knee OA from healthy controls in two external datasets, as previously reported [22].

While CSPG4+ SF EVs may reliably indicate OA-related joint structural changes, SF collection is limited by bleeding and infection risks. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate CSPG4+ EVs in plasma, a minimally invasive biospecimen, as biomarkers of structural joint damage in knee OA. We pursued this objective in two independent cohorts and further characterized the cargo of CSPG4+ EVs isolated from osteoarthritic SF. We hypothesized that CSPG4+ EV subpopulations contribute to OA pathogenesis by transferring pathogenic cargo, such as FN1 fragments, known inducers of cartilage degeneration and joint damage [23]. To test our hypothesis, we profiled EVs from plasma and SF of knee OA participants enrolled in independent studies at two separate academic institutions. Focusing on validating CSPG4+ EVs as biomarkers of structural severity, we also characterized their pathogenic cargo. Building on prior evidence that cytokine concentrations differ between endo-EV (EV-enriched, containing EV corona, membrane, and cargo) and exo-EV (EV-depleted) fractions of SF and plasma [9,14,15], we quantified OA-relevant effectors, including FN1, interleukin (IL)-11, IL-17A/F, IL-21, and IL-22 [[6], [15], [23], [24], [25], [26]], thereby extending our previous analyses of Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF)-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and Interferon (IFN)-γ [15].

Methods

Study cohorts

Participants were enrolled at Duke University and Brigham and Women's Hospital (BWH); demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Two cohorts were included in the plasma EV analysis: (1) The Discovery knee rOA cohort (n = 51; K/L > 1) was recruited at Duke University; (2) The Validation knee rOA/symptomatic (sx) OA cohort (n = 92; summed OST ≥1 or summed joint space narrowing (JSN) ≥1; summed Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) pain score ≥11.1) was drawn from the prospective Osteoarthritis Registry of Biomarkers and Imaging Trajectories (ORBIT) study [27] at BWH. Participants in both cohorts provided plasma samples and underwent standardized study radiographs using a Synaflexor frame. Knee rOA structural severity was assessed using Kellgren-Lawrence (K/L) grade, JSN, and osteophyte (OST) scores, graded using standard atlases [28,29] for both cohorts. Knee sxOA severity was evaluated using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) [30] for the Duke cohort, and the KOOS [31] for the BWH cohort; KOOS pain scores were not inverted, so higher scores indicate worse symptoms. A separate knee rOA cohort (n = 61; K/L ≥ 1), also recruited at Duke University, contributed SF samples. Among these, forty-five SF samples were anonymized waste specimens collected during therapeutic aspiration or total knee replacement. The 61 SF samples were used in EV biomarker studies in our prior publications, including 43 SF samples for measuring endo-EV and exo-EV concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, IFN-γ and surface markers (Cluster of Differentiation 81, CD9, CD63, CD29, CD4, CD8, CD68, CD14, CD56, CD15, CD19, CD235a, CD41a, CD34, CD31, Human Leukocyte Antigen [HLA]-ABC, HLA-DRDPDQ, and HLA-G) [15], 16 SF samples for EV proteomic anlysis [6,10], 25 SF samples for evaluation of transmembrane and intravesical TNF-α [16], and 10 SF samples for OA SF EV-specific surface markers (CSPG4, CD109, V-Set and Immunoglobulin Domain Containing 4, CD163, Macrophage receptor MARCO, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1, Neuropilin-1, Biglycan, and protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type S) [6]. None of these results overlap with the findings reported in the current study.

All samples and data were collected under IRB-approved protocols with informed consent or as anonymized waste.

EV isolation and characterization

As previously described [6,10,16], freshly collected whole EDTA blood and SF biospecimens were centrifuged (3000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) to remove cells, platelets, and debris, then aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C. All SF specimens used in this study were light yellow and free of blood contamination. Before EV isolation, frozen plasma and SF samples were thawed and centrifuged (2000×g, 10 min, 4 °C) to remove debris. Thereafter, EVs were isolated from the cleared plasma and SF with SF digested in hyaluronidase from Streptomyces hyalurolyticus (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA), 10 unit/mL, 1 h, 37 °C in an Eppendorf ThermoMixer C dry block with shaking at 300 rpm. Recognizing that commercial EV isolation methods can present trade-offs between yield and specificity, often enriching for SEVs while co-isolating non-EV material [32], we used ultrafiltration with a 100 kDa filter (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) followed by high-resolution flow cytometry. This approach enabled marker-specific analysis across the full EV size spectrum in SF and plasma without requiring purified EV isolates, while maximizing EV yield and preserving size heterogeneity from small-volume clinical samples [6,7,10,[14], [15], [16]]. Following our reported protocols [9,15], we also applied polymer-based precipitation (ExoQuick, System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA) to isolate endo-EV and exo-EV fractions of SF for ELISA assays.

Isolated EVs were then characterized for morphology, size, concentration, surface markers, and cargo effectors in accordance with Minimal information for studies of extracellular vesicles (MISEV2023) recommendations, as extensively described in our previous studies [6,7,9,10,[14], [15], [16],33,34].

Negative staining and transmission electron microscopy imaging

Formvar/carbon-coated 400 mesh copper grids (Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA) were glow discharged for 2 min, then floated on 20 μL EV samples for 10 min. Grids were briefly washed twice with deionized water, stained with 2 % aqueous uranyl acetate for 1 min, and dried with filter paper. Samples were imaged using a JEOL JEM-1230 transmission electron microscope (80 kV; JEOL USA, Peabody, MA, USA) with a Gatan Orius SC1000 CCD camera and Gatan Microscopy Suite v3.10.1002.0 (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA).

ELISAs

As previously described [15], isolated EV pellets from OA SF were lysed in NP40 lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in volumes matching the corresponding EV-depleted supernatants. Concentrations of endo-EV and exo-EV FN1 and cytokines were quantified by ELISAs using a Human Fibronectin ELISA kit (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) and a custom multiplex panel including IL-11, IL-17A/F, IL-21, and IL-22 (Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

High-resolution multicolor flow cytometry

Following our previous protocols [10,15,16], EV pellets isolated from 20 μL plasma and SF were resuspended in double-filtered PBS (df-PBS, 100 nm filters [EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA]) and stained with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies against human proteins (CD9, CD81, CD63 from Thermo Fisher Scientific; CSPG4, APOA1 from Bio-techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA; FN1 from BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA; IL-11 from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). Samples were diluted to 300 μL (1:15 dilution) with df-PBS for acquisition. Negative controls included df-PBS and unstained EVs; UltraComp™ eBeads Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for compensation and gating. EV size distribution was estimated using non-fluorescent reference beads (100 nm, 1000 nm, and 6000 nm, Thermo Fisher Scientific), defining LEVs (∼1000–6000 nm), MEVs (∼100–1000 nm), and SEVs (≤100 nm). Notably, because of differing refractive indices between EVs and reference beads, our EV size estimates are approximate. Samples were acquired on a Sony MA900 high-resolution flow cytometer (Sony Biotechnology, San Jose, CA, USA) at ∼1 μL/s with a backscatter threshold of 0.02 % to exclude debris while capturing SEVs. The percentages of EVs carrying each tested marker were analyzed using FCS Express 5 software (De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA, USA).

Analysis of CSPG4 gene expression in human tissue cells

Building on our recently published method [16], we analyzed CSPG4 expression across 55 × 106 human tissue cells representing 62 tissue types across the whole body, using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the CZ CELLxGENE Discover database (cellxgene.cziscience.com; accessed July 25, 2025) [35], a rapidly expanding scRNA-seq resource. At the time of access, the database did not include gene expression data for specific joint tissue cells, such as chondrocytes or synoviocytes.

Secondary analyses of published gene expression data in OA joint tissue cells

We analyzed our published scRNA-seq data [36] (NCBI GEO GSE152805) from human end-stage knee OA joint tissues, including chondrocytes from damaged and intact cartilage and synoviocytes (11,579, 14,613, and 10,640 cells, respectively). Genes encoding our markers of interest and their co-expression with CSPG4 were examined and visualized with violin and scatter plots. Validation was performed using published scRNA-seq data from infrapatellar fat pad and synovium [37] (GSE216651), downloaded from GEO on 09/20/2024.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and JMP 17 (JMP Statistical Discovery, Cary, NC, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the D'Agostino & Pearson test to determine the appropriateness of parametric versus non-parametric statistical tests. Primary outcomes included knee rOA (summed K/L, JSN, OST) and sxOA (summed WOMAC, KOOS pain) severity scores. Continuous demographic and clinical variables were compared between the Duke and BWH cohorts using the Mann-Whitney test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons, and categorical variables were compared using the Fisher's exact test. Multivariable linear regression, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, evaluated associations among CSPG4+ EV frequencies and knee rOA and sxOA severity scores. Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests compared pathogenic protein frequencies between CSPG4+ and CSPG4- EV subpopulations, as well as endo-EV and exo-EV concentrations of effectors. Spearman correlation assessed associations between CSPG4+ EV frequencies and cargo proteins in OA SF, including FN1 and pathogenic cytokines measured in this study (IL-11, IL-17A/F, IL-21 and IL-22) and prior study (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ [15]). Additionally, using our published datasets of comprehensive EV marker profiling [10,14,38], Spearman correlation assessed associations between CSPG4+ EV frequencies and cell-specific markers in OA plasma. Significance was set at p < 0.05 or FDR q < 0.05, where applicable.

Results

OA cohort characteristics

For plasma EV analysis, the discovery cohort consisted of 51 participants with knee rOA (mean age: 66.1 ± 13.0 years; 28 females, 23 males) enrolled at Duke University. The validation cohort included 92 participants with both knee sxOA and rOA (mean age: 64.3 ± 9.9 years; 57 females, 35 males) enrolled at BWH. Plasma samples were collected from all participants. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two cohorts for age (q = 0.31), sex (p = 0.48), BMI (q = 0.63), or summed JSN (q = 0.06); however, the Duke cohort exhibited higher summed K/L (q = 0.015) and OST (q < 0.0001) scores.

Additionally, 61 SF specimens were collected from 61 knee rOA participants at Duke University (mean age: 66.1 ± 11.6 years; 32 females, 29 males), including 45 anonymized waste samples with limited demographic information (age and sex only).

The characteristics of the participants, including demographics and clinical variables, are summarized in Table 1.

Characterization of OA EVs

As described in our previous studies [6,10,[14], [15], [16]], OA SF and plasma-derived EVs isolated using polymer-based precipitation or 100 kDa ultrafiltration were comprehensively characterized using nanoparticle tracking analysis, dynamic light scattering, mass spectrometry-based proteomics, and high-resolution multicolor flow cytometry, following MISEV2023 recommendations. These EVs consistently comprised three major subsets (LEVs, MEVs, and SEVs), exhibited PKH26+ lipid bilayer structure, carried various cargo including mitochondria and cytokines (e.g. TNF-α, IL-27, IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-11, IL-17A/F, IL-21, IL-22), canonical EV surface markers (CD9, CD81, CD63), and additional markers reflecting distinct cell/tissue origins (e.g., CSPG4, TLN1, CD29, FGA, FGB, FGG, AMBP, CD4, CD8, CD19, CD56, CD29, HLA-ABC, HLA-DRDPDQ, CD31, CD235a, CD41a and CD34). We also evaluated their associations with knee sxOA severity, using WOMAC scores in the discovery cohort and KOOS pain scores in the validation cohort. In multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, sex, and BMI [6,10], the frequency of CSPG4+ LEVs was significantly positively associated with knee K/L grades (p = 0.0060; Estimate 1.86; t-ratio 2.88) in the discovery cohort (Fig. 2B). We confirmed the significant positive association between CSPG4+ LEV frequency and K/L grades (p = 0.0358; Estimate 1.15; t-ratio 2.13) in the validation cohort (Fig. 2C). Additionally, in the validation cohort, the frequency of CSPG4+ total EVs was positively associated with K/L grades (p = 0.0286; Estimate 0.23; t-ratio 2.23), while the frequencies of CSPG4+ total EVs (p = 0.0064; Estimate 0.37; t-ratio 2.79), LEVs (p = 0.0169; Estimate 1.69; t-ratio 2.43), and MEVs (p = 0.0404; Estimate 0.21; t-ratio 2.08) were positively associated with JSN scores (Fig. 2D). There were no significant associations of CSPG4+ plasma EVs of any size with OST, or pain scores (WOMAC or KOOS) in either cohort. These findings support CSPG4+ EV frequencies as novel biomarkers of knee OA-related structural severity.

Interestingly, scRNA-seq data from the CZ CELLxGENE Discover database [35] (accessed July 25, 2025) showed CSPG4 expression in only a small proportion of cells in most tissues, including tendon of the semitendinosus (3.2 %), nervous system (2.5 %), heart (2.1 %), liver (0.25 %), and blood (0.01 %) (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, our previous scRNA-seq analysis of OA knee joint cells revealed high CSPG4 expression in chondrocytes from damaged cartilage (56.1 %) intact cartilage (43.67 %), and synoviocytes (22.89 %) [6] (Supplementary Fig. 1). Together, these findings suggest that OA joint tissue cells, particularly chondrocytes from damaged cartilage, may be the predominant source of CSPG4+ plasma EVs.

To further explore the potential cellular origin of CSPG4+ plasma EVs, we performed Spearman correlation analysis using data from a parallel study with comprehensive EV marker profiling, as reported in our previous publications [10,14,38]. Plasma CSPG4 levels were strongly correlated with the joint-enriched marker TLN1 (r = 0.8059, [95 % CI: 0.6212–0.9058) but showed minimal correlation with immune cell markers including CD4 (r = 0.3286, [−0.04709–0.6228]), CD68 (r = 0.2355, [−0.1473–0.5569]), CD14 (r = 0.3247, [−0.05142–0.6201]), and CD19 (r = 0.2349, [−0.1479–0.5565]). These results support a likely joint-derived origin of CSPG4+ EVs, though definitive identification of the cell source will require further validation, which we plan to address in future studies.

FN1 and IL11 are novel pathogenic cargo in CSPG4+ SF EVs in OA

To identify pathogenic candidates underlying the positive association between plasma CSPG4+ EV abundance and knee OA-related structural severity, we next evaluated the potential cargo of CSPG4+ EVs derived from joint tissues. Using scRNA-seq data (GSE152805), we analyzed co-expression of CSPG4 with FN1 and IL11 genes in OA joint tissue cells (Fig. 3A). Because EVs reflect the molecular composition of their cells of origin, we hypothesized that CSPG4+ OA SF EVs carry FN1 and IL-11 proteins as pathogenic cargo. Consistent with our previous findings that CSPG4 peptides in OA SF EVs correlated positively with FN1 peptides [6], high-resolution flow cytometry revealed that in knee OA SF, FN1 expression was 93-fold higher in CSPG4+ EV subpopulations (mean 7 % [95 % CI: 3.9–10.3 %]) than in CSPG4- EVs (0.08 % [0.05–0.08 %]) (Fig. 3B). Similarly, in knee OA SF, IL-11 expression was 92-fold higher in CSPG4+ EVs (65.1 % [59.5–72.5 %]) than in CSPG4- EVs (0.07 % [0.06–0.07 %]) (Fig. 3B).Fig. 3FN1 and IL-11 were identified as novel pathogenic effectors of CSPG4+ SF EVs in OA. Our previous mass spectrometry-based proteomics identified two CSPG4 peptides (sequences: APLEVPQALGR and LVLGQEELR) that were highly enriched in OA SF EVs and significantly positively correlated with 175 and 137 FN1 peptides, respectively [6]. In contrast, key OA-related cytokines were not detected by proteomics due to their low abundance. To further quantify OA-relevant effectors associated with SF EVs, we isolated and characterized the endo-EV (EV-enriched, containing EV corona and cargo) and exo-EV (EV-depleted) fractions of OA SF as previously described [15]. The concentration of FN1 in the endo-EV fraction (95 × 106 pg/ml [80–101 × 106]) was 122-fold higher than in the exo-EV fraction (0.8 × 106 pg/ml [0.4–0.7 × 106]) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, concentrations of IL-11 (37.5 pg/ml [20.3–33.9]), IL-17A/F (0.25 pg/ml [0–0.25]), and IL-21 (2.5 × 106 pg/ml [2–2.7]) were 7-, 5.4-, and 1.5-fold lower, respectively, in the endo-EV compared with the exo-EV fraction, while IL-22 was undetectable (Fig. 4B). Exploring cellular origins of pathogenic cargo-containing EVs in OA joints

Our previous studies identified expression of the following: FN1 [10] and IL11 [36] in OA chondrocytes and synoviocytes; TNF in OA synoviocytes and infrapatellar fat pad [16]; and IL1B and IL6 in OA synoviocytes [36]. In the current study, extended this analysis by comprehensively visualizing the expression patterns of ten effector genes of interest, including FN1, IL11, TNF, IL1B, IL6, IFNG, IL17A, IL17F, IL21, and IL22, in OA joint cells from individual OA patients using two independent scRNA-seq datasets (GSE152805 and GSE216651) (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). We confirmed ubiquitous FN1 expression across all cell types, IL11 enrichment in chondrocytes derived from damaged cartilage, and elevated expression of TNF, IL1B, IL6, and IFNG in synoviocytes. In contrast, IL17A, IL17F, IL21, and IL22 were either minimally expressed or undetectable in OA joint tissue cells in both datasets (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). These findings suggest that osteoarthritic chondrocytes are the primary source of IL-11+ EVs, whereas synoviocytes predominantly contribute TNF-α+, IL-1β+, IL-6+, and IFN-γ+ EVs. Conversely, IL-17A/F+ and IL-21+ EVs are likely derived from circulating immune cells that subsequently migrate into the joint. Future cell-based analysis is required for validating the cellular sources of these EVs carrying pathogenic cytokines in OA joints.

Discussion

We previously identified CSPG4 as a key surface marker of pathogenic SF EVs [6]. In the present study, we extend this finding by detecting CSPG4 on the surface of plasma EVs from individuals with knee OA in both a discovery and an independent validation cohort. A higher abundance of CSPG4+ plasma EVs was positively associated with knee rOA severity, indicating greater structural joint damage, but was not associated with sxOA severity. We further identified FN1 and IL-11 as pathogenic cargo carried by CSPG4+ SF EVs, with FN1 concentration markedly enriched in the endo-EV fraction compared to the exo-EV fraction. Collectively, these findings support CSPG4+ plasma EVs as biomarkers of burden of disease according to the BIPEDS criteria [39]. The minimally invasive nature of plasma-derived EV biomarkers provides a practical and scalable approach for monitoring joint structural damage and enabling early therapeutic intervention in OA.

Based on scRNA-seq data, CSPG4 expression was markedly elevated in OA knee joint tissue cells, particularly in chondrocytes from damaged cartilage (56.1 %) [6] compared to intact cartilage and synovium, but low in other tissues (<3.5 %) [6]. These findings suggests that CSPG4+ plasma EVs are likely derived predominantly from OA joint tissue cells. CSPG4 has been studied extensively in cancer and serves as a target for CAR-T cell therapy in various tumors [17]. The extracellular domains of CSPG4 interact with extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including FN1, integrins, collagens II, V, and VI, lectins, and perlecan, promoting cell adhesion and migration [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. CSPG4 activates MMP2 (also known as type IV collagenase) on tumor cell surfaces, promoting invasion through basement membranes and connective tissue by degrading type IV collagen [21]. In OA, ECM degradation by matrix-degrading enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases is a key effector of disease progression [40]. Moreover, the binding affinity of CSPG4 to its extracellular cartilage ligands—type VI collagen and FN1—correlates with OA progression [41]. Further investigation is needed to clarify the potential role of CSPG4 and CSPG4+ EVs not only as biomarkers but also as mediators of ECM degradation and joint structural damage in OA.

Remarkably, CSPG4 peptides enriched in SF EVs compared with matched plasma EVs were positively associated with multiple pathogenic SF EV peptides in OA, including FN1, matrix-degrading enzymes MMP2, MMP3, MMP9, and ADAMTS13 [6], suggesting CSPG4+ EVs may carry these pathogenic effectors as their cargo. In this study, we confirmed that CSPG4+ SF EVs contain FN1 and IL-11 proteins among their cargo. These CSPG4+ EVs may exacerbate OA severity by delivering these pathogenic effectors to recipient cells [42], similar to how cancer cell-derived EVs transfer FN1 to induce malignant transformation [43]. Consistently, FN1 fragments account for nearly 20 % of pathogenic peptides in OA SF EVs and show a strong correlation with CSPG4 peptides enriched in SF EVs [6]. FN1 fragments promote MMP and catabolic cytokine production, worsen cartilage damage, and induce OA-related genes such as IL11 in normal chondrocytes [23,44,45]. IL11 gene expression is likewise linked to knee OA progression [24], and a large genome-wide association meta-analysis (489,975 OA cases, 1,472,094 controls) identified a significant association between IL11 and total hip replacement, with IL11 variant aggregation conferring increased OA risk [46].

In addition to delivering their cargo, CSPG4+ EVs may modulate recipient cell behavior through signaling mediated by the CSPG4 intracellular domain. This short domain activates ERK1/2 and MAPK signaling pathways [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]], which are known to regulate key processes in tissue remodeling and degeneration. The MAPK pathway is a proposed therapeutic target in OA due to its regulation of multiple mediators of joint tissue destruction [40]. Notably, FN1 fragments interact with integrins, triggering their endocytosis and sustained activation of ERK and MAPK signaling, which drives continued cartilage matrix degradation [47]. These endosomes can further mature into multivesicular bodies, leading to the release of SEVs [48]. Collectively, these findings suggest that CSPG4+ SF EVs, which carry OA-related pathogenic cargo such as FN1 and IL-11, may propagate tissue damage in OA joints, a hypothesis that warrants confirmation through functional validation assays.

CSPG4+ cells can release EVs through three main biogenesis pathways: (1) direct shedding from the plasma membrane via outward budding, (2) formation of multivesicular bodies followed by fusion with the plasma membrane to release EVs [48], and (3) generation of apoptotic bodies, a subtype of LEVs through membrane blebbing during late-stage apoptosis [49]. Because nearly all cell types can release EVs into biofluids both in vivo and in vitro, earlier observations of CSPG4 secretion merit reinterpretation within the context of EV biology. For example, a prior study reported increased CSPG4 shedding into the culture medium of primary human periodontal ligament fibroblasts undergoing FN1-induced anoikis, a form of ECM detachment-induced apoptosis [50]. Only the extracellular domain of CSPG4 was detected, which was initially attributed to proteolytic cleavage [50]. However, this may instead reflect release of CSPG4+ EVs during apoptosis. In another study, inhibition of MMP13, a known OA mediator [51], reduced retention of CSPG4's extracellular domain in the ECM but not on mandibular fibrochondrocyte surfaces [51], suggesting MMP13 activity may facilitate CSPG4+ EV release from chondrocytes. Together, these findings support EV-mediated mechanisms as plausible routes for CSPG4 mobilization and delivery in OA, consistent with our data implicating CSPG4+ EVs as both pathogenic mediators and candidate biomarkers. Nonetheless, further studies are needed to define specific EV biogenesis pathways responsible for generating CSPG4+ EVs in joint tissues.

This study has several limitations. Most SF specimens were obtained as anonymized waste samples from OA joints, without accompanying clinical data or medical data. Consequently, we were only able to assess associations of plasma CSPG4+ EV subpopulations and OA severity, but not SF CSPG4+ EV subpopulations. Addressing these gaps remains an important priority for future research. Another limitation of this study is that healthy controls and patients with other joint conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, anterior cruciate ligament injury, or meniscal injury) were not included. Therefore, we cannot determine whether elevated CSPG4+ EV levels are specific to OA or represent a more general marker of joint injury or inflammation. Additionally, our findings are based on associative analyses and do not establish a causal role for CSPG4+ EVs in the pathogenesis of OA. Functional experiments are needed to determine whether these EVs directly promote cartilage matrix degradation or inflammatory responses. Despite these limitations, our observations indicate that CSPG4+ plasma EVs are positively associated with knee OA structural damage, and CSPG4+ SF EVs carry pathogenic cargo, supporting the potential of CSPG4+ EVs to serve as both biomarkers and mediators of tissue damage and structural severity in OA joints. This study did not include functional assays; further investigation is needed to elucidate the pathogenic effects of CSPG4+ EVs on human chondrocytes and their broader biological roles in OA.

In summary, our study identifies CSPG4+ EV subpopulations in OA plasma as a novel biomarker of knee rOA severity. Cells expressing CSPG4 in OA joint tissues co-express FN1 and IL11 genes, and CSPG4+ SF EVs from knee OA patients carry FN1 and IL-11 proteins, both established mediators of OA pathogenesis. Additionally, OA SF EVs contain pathogenic cytokines, including IL-17A/F and IL-21. These findings provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies to define the role of CSPG4+ EVs in OA pathogenesis and progression.
