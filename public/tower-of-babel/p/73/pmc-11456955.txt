Genotyping and phylogeographic dynamics of coxsackievirus A16
Authors: Xia-Nan Chu, Pir Tariq Shah, Zi-Hui Ma, Yan Wang, Li Xing
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoxsackievirus A16 (CV-A16) is one of the major pathogens of Hand, Foot and Mouth disease. Here, we analyzed 287 full-length genome sequences of CV-A16 found worldwide from 1994 to 2019 to see the genomic evolution characteristics. Full-length genome-based phylogenetic tree divided the viruses into five different genotypes, G-a to G-e. The CV-A16 strains circulating in China dominate G-a and G-c, but can also be found in other genotypes including G-b and G-e. Phylogeographic analysis showed a high diversity of CV-A16 distribution. In addition, recombination was shown to drive the genomic evolution of CV-A16 during past decades. However, the structural proteins still remain relative conserved while there is extensive genomic recombination. This study updates the phylogenetic and phylogeographic information of CV-A16 and provides insights into the genetic characteristics of CV-A16.Keywords: CV-A16, Phylogenetic, Phylogeographic, Recombination, Genotyping

Introduction

Hand, Foot, and Mouth disease (HFMD) is a common acute infectious disease, which predominantly affects children under 5 years of age and is characterized by transient fever, minor blisters or small ulcers on the hands, feet, and mouth [1,2]. HFMD is caused by a certain member of enterovirus groups A and B. The coxsackieviruses A6 (CV-A6), CV-A10 [3], CV-A16, and enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) in enterovirus group A are the main viruses associated with HFMD. The coxsackieviruses B2 (CV-B2), CV-B3 [4,5], CV-B5 [6], and Echoviruses [7] in enterovirus group B were also reported to causally associate with HFMD. But over the years, the most common viruses causing HFMD were CV-A16 and EV-A71 [8].

CV-A16-caused HFMD was discovered for the first time in 1957 in New Zealand [9], then outbreak was reported in Canada (Toronto) [10], UK (Birmingham) [11], and USA (California) [12]. So far, CV-A16-caused HFMD is still reported in many countries in Europe, America, and Asia, including Spain [13], Italy [14], Germany [15], USA [16], India [17], Japan [18], and China [19] etc.

CV-A16 belongs to the Picornaviridae family [20]. The only known natural host of this virus is human being [21]. CV-A16 virion is a non-enveloped icosahedral particle, approximately 30 nm in diameter, and contains a single-strand, sense, polyadenylated viral RNA genome (～7.4 kb) [22]. The genome contains an open reading frame (ORF) encoding a large polyprotein precursor, a 5′-untranslated region (5′-UTR), and a poly-adenosine-tailed 3′-UTR. The polyprotein is processed into structural protein P1 and nonstructural protein P2 and P3 [23]. P1 can be further processed by virus-encoded proteases to produce the viral capsid subunit proteins VP0, VP1, and VP3. VP0 self-cleaves to generate VP2 and VP4. Nonstructural proteins P2 and P3 are processed into seven nonstructural units including 2A, 2B, 2C, and 3A, 3B, 3C and 3D, respectively, which are responsible for the regulation of virus replication and virus-host interactions [22].

Before the 21st century, researchers identified different types of enterovirus by neutralization experiments with antibodies, and the VP1 protein contains many important neutralization sites [24]. Since the 21st century, sequencing of the 5′-UTR of enterovirus genome and typing based on the VP1 gene sequence [25] have become routine methods to distinguish enterovirus genotypes. Like most enteroviruses, in previous studies, the genetic diversity of CV-A16 was investigated by analyzing the gene sequences of VP4 or VP1 capsid proteins [26]. CV-A16 was initially divided into two large phylogenetic groups, A and B. Genotype B was further divided into B1 and B2. The majority of CV-A16 strains isolated worldwide from 1997 to 2007 formed the B1 sub-genotype, and the nine strains isolated from Japan and Malaysia between 1981 and 2000 formed B2 sub-genotype [27]. Later, genotype B1 was further divided into B1a to B1c [28]. From 1999 to 2008, B1a and B1b viruses were identified in many countries including China, Malaysia, Thailand, and Vietnam et al. [29]. Genotype C was suggested by several studies based on VP4 gene sequence [30,31], but not confirmed by analysis of VP1 sequences. In 2009, a virus identified in Peru was designated as genotype D based on the VP1 gene [28,32]. Since then, CV-A16 has been classified into four main genotypes A, B (B1 and B2), and D based on the VP1 gene, and C based on VP4 gene.

EV-A71 and CV-A16 cause similar clinical symptoms in infected people. At present, the vaccines against EV-A71 are available in different forms including inactivated vaccines, virus like particle (VLPs) [33], and attenuated live vaccine [34], etc. However, no vaccine against CV-A16 has been successfully licensed while the potential CV-A16 vaccine candidates are currently under investigation, such as an inactivated vaccine [35], attenuated live vaccine [35,36], and virus-like particle vaccines [37].

In 1981, the first CV-A16-caused case of HFMD was reported in Tianjin city, China [38]. An outbreak of HFMD occurred in Fuyang city, Anhui province of China in the spring of 2008. A total of 6049 HFMD cases were reported, including 3023 hospitalizations (50 %), 353 severe cases (6 %) and 22 deaths (0.4 %). It was confirmed that the main pathogen of the epidemic was the recombinant virus formed by cross species recombination of EV-A71 and CV-A16, the recombinant strain was named EV-A71/Fuyang.Anhui/17.08 [29]. In the same year, China has established a national system for monitoring HFMD [29]. In the first decade of the 21st century, EV-A71 was the main strain of HFMD in China, which was distributed in Shandong [39], Shenzhen [30], Shanghai [40], Hong Kong [41] and other places. Almost all provinces in China Mainland have reported the HFMD epidemic. However, since the second decade of the 21st century, CV-A16 started becoming the major pathogen of HFMD in China, and was reported in Beijing [42], Shanghai [43], Shenzhen [44], Guangzhou [45] and other densely populated cities. It was also reported in Taiwan [46] of China. Until now, a large number of CV-A16 full-length genomic sequences are being accumulated and required to be systemically reviewed to get insight into the genetic diversity and evolutionary characteristics, which are informative for efficient surveillance or formulating control strategy.

Methods

Dataset

The available of 287 complete genomic sequences of CV-A16 identified between 1994 and 2019 were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database, among which 236 were from China, and 51 from eight other countries, including Vietnam (n = 7), India (n = 10), Malaysia (n = 21), the United States (n = 9), Thailand (n = 2), Finland (n = 1), and South Korea (n = 1). CV-A16 strains identified in China were mainly from Beijing (n = 96), Yunnan (n = 17), and Shenzhen (n = 17). In addition, the amino acid sequences of these 287 viruses-encoded polyproteins were also retrieved from NCBI GenBank. The viruses were identified by their GenBank ID, virus name, country/region, and year of isolation or report.

Construction of phylogenetic tree

All the nucleotide sequences were aligned with ClustalW multiple sequence alignment tool in the MEGA11 software [47]. The sequences were manually curated using the BioEdit v7.2.5 [48]. The maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees based on the full-length genomic sequence, VP4 ORF sequence, or VP1 ORF sequence were constructed using the IQ-TREE multicore version 1.6.12 (http://www.iqtree.org/release/v1.6.12) based on 1000 bootstrap replicates and best-fitting model GTR + F + I + G4, TNe + G4, or SYM + G4, respectively [49]. The final tree was visualized and modified using FigTree v1.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). In addition, the pairwise distance among the sequences was calculated using the MEGA11 software, while the TempEst v1.5.3 was used to conduct root-to-tip regression analysis to determine the temporal signal and relationship between divergences from tree root and sample dates [50].

Phylogeographic network analysis

To visualize the regional level distribution and genetic relationships of the full-length genome sequences, a minimum spanning network (MSN) was applied using the PopArt v1.7 (https://popart.maths.otago.ac.nz/) [51]. The MSN phylogenetic network contains all the full-length genome sequences included in the phylogenetic tree construction. In addition, the nucleotide diversity, number of segregating sites, and Tajima's D statistic (D) data were also recorded.

Recombination analysis

The 287 full-length nucleotide sequences of CV-A16 were analyzed using Recombination Detection Program 4 software (RDP4, http://web.cbio.uct.ac.za/∼darren/rdp.html) [52]. The recombination events were examined by each of the seven algorithms, including GENECONV, RDP, SiScan, Bootscan, 3seq, MaxChi, and Chimaera embedded in the RDP4. A real recombination event was accepted when confirmed by at least four of the above algorithms. The potential recombination events were further characterized using phylogenetic analysis to verify the recombination authenticity.

Amino acids variability analysis

The full-length polyprotein sequences of CV-A16 were separately retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database and were translated into amino acid sequences using the MEGA11 software. The amino acids variability was determined using the Wu-Kabat variability coefficient implemented by the Protein Variability Server (PVS, http://imed.med.ucm.es/PVS/) [53]. The Wu-Kabat variability coefficient was determined using formula V= N∗k/n, where V represents the variability, n denotes the frequency that the most commonly recognized amino acid at that position, k represents the number of different amino acids at a given position and N indicates the number of sequences in the alignment [54].

Results

Phylogenetic view of CV-A16

Previously, most studies on the classification of CV-A16 genotypes were based on the VP1 gene sequences [55,56]. To precisely explore the genetic relatedness, the full-length genome sequence-based ML phylogenetic tree was constructed using the IQ-TREE v1.6.12 with the best-fitting model GTR + F + I + G4 [49]. As shown in Fig. 1, Supplement Table S1, and Supplement Fig. S1, CV-A16 was divided into five major clades, i.e. G-a to G-e. Almost all CV-A16 strains circulating in China were from G-a and G-c genotypes, and a few in G-b (n = 1) and G-e (n = 2). Five strains in the G-e genotype were far from the other four groups. G-a genotype was dominant in our classification. Forty-five virus strains that were previously defined as B1 genotype [56] were distributed in different genogroups in our ML phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1A and Supplement Fig. S1), among which one fell in G-a (16E4/USA/2016, GenBank ID: KY674980.1), 8 in G-b, 20 in G-c, and 16 in G-d. The virus strains that were previously defined as A, C, or D genotype according to VP1 gene sequences were also marked accordingly in Fig. 1 and Supplement Figs. S1, S2, and S3.

The pairwise distance analysis indicated that all the strains were genetically closer to each other (distance less than 20 %), except the G-e clade strains that appeared distinctly far (distance more than 40 %) (Fig. 2A). Similar results were shown by the root-to-tip divergence analysis, where all the strains diverged from a closer ancestor, while the G-e strains appeared distant from all the remaining strains. The common ancestor was predicted to merge around 1900s (Fig. 2B). These results are consistent with ML phylogenetic tree, further validating the ML method of phylogeny (Fig. 1 and Supplement Fig. S1).

(A) The pairwise distance among all the full-length genomes indicates the G-a to G-d groups are genetically closer to each other, while the G-e appears as distanced strains. (B) The root-to-tip diversion analysis of all the full-length genomes of CV-A16, showing the departure of G-e strains from the remaining CV-A16 strains. The pairwise distance was calculated using MEGA11 software, while the root-to-tip diversion was determined using the TempEst v1.5.3. The green lines represent the ancestor traces.

The ML phylogenetic trees were also constructed based on the VP4 gene or VP1 gene. In VP4 gene-based ML tree (Fig. 1B and Supplement Fig. S2), G-a genotype virus strains are divided into three main groups (one small group and two large group). CVA16-DS6756 (GenBank ID: KU163608.1, China) and CVA16-DS7028 (GenBank ID: MZ671909.1, China) belong to the G-a group according to the full-length genome-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1A and Supplement Fig. S2), but become relatively independent and far from other G-a viruses in VP4 gene-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1B and Supplement Fig. S2). VP4 genes of G-b or G-d viruses still form independent groups. Most of VP4 genes of G-c still cluster together with the exception of VP4 gene of one virus strain shzh00-1 (GenBank ID: AY790926.1), which is genetically closer to that of group G-b viruses. Among five VP4 genes of G-e, three of them including FY18 (GenBank ID: EU812514.1, China), CV-A16-genotypeA (GenBank ID: JQ746659.1, Malaysia), and G-10 (GenBank ID: U05876.1, Finland) still cluster together, but other two forms an independent branch (10-2952-1, GenBank ID: MH118027.1, India; SH-HP-16-51, GenBank ID: MG957117.1, China) (Fig. 2 and Supplement Fig. S2). In VP1 gene-based ML tree (Fig. 1C and Supplement Fig. S3), VP1 genes of G-a, G-b, or G-d still separately cluster together. VP1 genes of G-c were divided into 2 separate groups. VP1 genes of G-e were separated into 2 groups.

Fig. 1D shows our collection of 287 CV-A16 viruses, depicting the distribution of different genotypes from 1994 to 2019. The earliest full-length virus sequence collected in 1994 belongs to the G-e group (G-10, GenBank ID: U05876.1, Finland). Then until 2005, only G-c group virus strains were detected. Since 2005 until 2019, all five genotypes were detected, but the prevalence of G-a strains dominated, which constitutes the major threat to the public health.

Phylogeographic network of the complete genome of CV-A16

To further view the transmission of CV-A16 at the regional level, a phylogeographic network of whole genome sequences of CV-A16 strains included in phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Minimum Spinning Network (MSN) method implemented by PopArt v1.7 [51]. The results showed that CV-A16 distribution was highly diverse, containing multiple major network Clusters (Fig. 3). The viruses identified in China can be found in almost all the major mutational branches whereas the viruses identified in other countries show the geographic characters. Interestingly, a virus PM-14660-97 (GenBank ID: JQ746665.1) identified in Malaysia in 1997 can give rise to viruses of G-a, G-b, and G-d by longer mutational steps. Noticeably, viruses of G-e seem to originate from two different mutational branches of G-a.

Phylogeographic network of 287 full-length CV-A16 sequences worldwide from 1994 to 2019. Phylogenetic networks were inferred using MSN networks implemented with PopArt v1.7. G-a strains dominated the network and formed several major branches. G-c virus strain PM-14660-97 isolated in Malaysia in 1997 (GenBank ID: JQ746665.1) is genetically connected with G-a, G-b, and G-d genotypes through varied mutation steps. Viruses identified in different countries are indicated with different colors.

In addition, the phylogeographic network analysis also resulted in a nucleotide diversity (pi) of 0.0682143, indicating a moderate level of genetic variation within the population. The network identified 3719 segregating sites, highlighting the presence of multiple genetic variants in the population. Among these sites, 2998 were parsimony-informative sites, indicating informative genetic variation for phylogenetic analysis. Furthermore, Tajima's D statistic was indicated as −0.556975, suggesting a slight deviation from neutral evolution. The p-value associated with D being greater than or equal to −0.556975 was 0.689494, indicating that the observed deviation is not statistically significant. These findings contribute to our understanding of the population genetics and evolutionary history of CV-A16.

Recombination of CV-A16 genome

To explore the mechanisms underlying the high genetic heterogeneity and variation of CV-A16 gene, we performed a recombination analysis of 287 CV-A16 full‐length genomes using seven algorithms (RDP, Geneconv, BootScan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, and 3Seq) implemented in the recombination detection program 4 (RDP4) [52]. A potential recombinant was not acceptable until at least 4 of 7 assays show the positive identification. The results are presented in Supplement Table S2. Between 1994 and 2019, a total of 13 potential natural recombination events were detected, among which 8 in China, 4 in India and 1 in Vietnam. There were 8 inter-type recombination events (events 1,2,3,4,5,8,9, and 13) and 5 intra-type recombination events (events 6,7,10,11, and 12). We also identified several strains participating in multiple recombination events simultaneously. For example, SH-HP-16-51 (GenBank ID: MG957117.1, G-e) participates in events 1 and 4; and CV-A16-M02-BLR-IN (GenBank ID: KY792583.1, G-b) participates in events 7, 8, and 11.

The recombination breakpoints identified are shown in Fig. 4. The breakpoints of seven recombination events were mainly located at the 5′ and 3′ ends of virus genome (events 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, 9, and 12), suggesting that the 5′ and 3′ ends were the hot spots of recombination. In addition, 8 recombination events (events 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, and 12) occurred within P1 coding region, indication that the structural protein coding region is another recombination hotspot. P1 protein coding sequence contains recombination break points in the regions encoding VP4, VP2, and VP1 proteins.

The identified potential recombination events were further evaluated by constructing Neighbor-Joining phylogenetic trees based on different genomic regions of CV-A16 strains involved in recombination (Supplement Fig. S4). For example, in recombination event 12, recombinant BJ14-4 (GenBank ID: KU254598.1, China) is closer to the minor parent BJ14-3 (GenBank ID: KU254597.1, China) in the NJ tree of nt 5255–6709 genomic region (Supplement Fig. S4B), but closer to the major parent CVA16/DS2965/BJ/2015 (GenBank ID: MZ671879.1) in the NJ tree of nt 400–2121 (Supplement Fig. S4A). In recombination event 13, recombinant shzh00-1 (GenBank ID: AY790926.1) is closer to its minor parent MAV (GenBank ID: KC695830.1) in the NJ tree of nt 5255–6709 genomic region of (Supplement Fig. S4B), but closer to its major parent PM-15922-00 (GenBank ID: JQ746667.1) (Supplement S4B). Similar results were observed for other recombination events. The results of phylogenetic tree analysis were consistent with that of recombination analysis, further supporting the detected recombination events.

Amino acid variability landscape of full-length CV-A16 polyprotein

One genetic feature of CV-A16 is the amino acid variability of viral polyprotein. The Wu-Kabat coefficient of variation was determined using PVS server and 287 full-length protein sequences inferred from the coding sequences of CV-A16 identified worldwide. The polyprotein contains a total of 2193 consensus amino acids and is split into protein VP1-VP4 (P1 region) and non-structural protein (P2 and P3) regions during virus replication. The results showed that there was wide variability in all regions, with aa 1725–1875 as the most variable region encoding N-terminus of RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) (the highest recorded value of 9) (Fig. 5). The structural proteins including VP1 to VP4 are relatively more conserved than the non-structural proteins.

Amino acid variability landscape of full-length CV-A16 polyproteins. The plot represents amino acid variations in aa 1–750 position (A), aa 751–1501 (B), and aa 1501–2193 (C). The ORF nucleotide sequences were used to acquire their consensus amino acid sequence using the Wu-Kabat variability coefficient implemented by PVS. Y-axes represent the Wu-Kabat variability coefficient values, where the estimation limit is 1. Above the limit of 1 represents variations. X-axes represent the amino acid positions. The variability coefficient was calculated as following equation: variability = n∗k/N. The n denotes the number of sequences in the alignment, k denotes the number of different amino acids at a given position, and N is the time available for the most frequently identified amino acid.

Discussion

Viruses can generate genetic diversity to become highly adaptive to the environment. In this study, we provided a comprehensive description of the epidemiological and molecular characteristics and geographical differences of CV-A16 by analyzing the phylogenetic tree, and phylogeographic network of 287 CV-A16 genomes isolated worldwide from 1994 to 2019.

In previous studies of CV-A16 outbreaks and sporadic outbreaks, researchers mostly studied VP1 sequences, and in a few years [58,59] or longer [19,60] to centrally collect hospital case samples and analyze the VP1 sequence divergence to obtain the epidemiological and geographical characteristics of the virus for HFMD surveillance. However, only SH-HP-16-51 (GenBank ID: MG957117.1, China) in G-e group was clearly documented to be involved in the HFMD outbreak in Shanghai, China in 2008 [57].

In this study, we classified all the CV-A16 genome sequences collected and also reclassified them according to the VP1 and VP4 sequences (Fig. 1A, B, C, and Supplement Figs. S1, S2, and S3). A comparison of three different analyses revealed that viruses that were close to each other in the phylogenetic tree of complete genome sequences were far apart in VP1 or VP4 sequences. For example, shzh00-1 (GenBank ID: AY790926.1) belongs to G-c in the whole genome analysis, but were close to G-a in the VP1 and VP4 sequences-based phylogenetic trees. This suggests that the classification on the basis of single gene sequence segment may not accurately reflect the natural relatedness of virus genomes.

CV-A16 strains identified in China dominate the dataset in this report and can be found in nearly all the genotypes except G-d that was isolated only in USA (Fig. 1). G-a and G-c contain most of virus strains identified in China, which are corresponding to previously defined B1 viruses in China [56]. In particular, a large number of viruses were reported in Beijing (n = 98 strains), followed by Shenzhen city (n = 17 strains) and Yunnan province (n = 17 strains). Beijing as the capital of China has large floating population, which may facilitate the spreading of virus to other provinces. The viruses identified in South Asia seem to contribute to the transmission of virus worldwide as shown in phylogeographical network (Fig. 3). In addition, different viruses isolated from the same country were geographically closer to each other, especially for Malaysia, Vietnam, India, and the USA.

Genomic recombination of enterovirus was originally identified in poliovirus [61]. Genetic recombination can generate new viral variants during virus evolution, which may have higher infectivity, pathogenicity, or adaptability to specific environments [62]. Recombination may have played a key role in the evolution of CV-A16. In this paper, eight intertype recombination events (events 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9 and 13) were found. In another paper, researchers found that CV-A16 contained sequences from other EV-A donors in the 5′ UTR region and the P2 and P3 nonstructural protein-coding regions. It indicates that the evolution of CV-A16 may involve genetic recombination with other enterovirus type A (EV-A) [63], suggesting the varied adaptability, transmissibility, and host immune escape ability of the virus. In contrast to the negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses that have a lower recombination rate [64], enteroviruses generate more recombinants. By thoroughly analyzing all the available full-length CV-A16 genomes, we identified at least 13 potential recombination events, which confirm that recombination drove the genomic evolution during past decades. However, at the amino acid level, the structural proteins of CV-A16 seem relatively conserved, which may facilitate the development of vaccines with sustainable preventive effects.

Limitations of the study

Only the full-length genomic sequences of CA-V16 are included in this study and the partial genomic sequences are excluded.
