Examining school mental health policy support for teachers diagnosed with mental illness through ecological and stress vulnerability frameworks
Authors: Thembi Nkomo, Mokoko Percy Kekana, Mabitsela Hezekiel Mphasha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTeachers diagnosed with mental illness remain an under-recognised and under-supported group within the education workforce, yet the policies intended to support them within school systems often lack coherence, depth, or alignment with contemporary mental health frameworks. This study aimed to critically analyse existing South African School mental health policies that support teachers diagnosed with mental illness and those battling mental illness relapse, using Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory and the Stress-Vulnerability Model to identify strengths, gaps, and opportunities for improved mental health support within educational settings.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative document analysis design using the READ approach to critically analyse existing South African School mental health policies related to the management and support of teachers diagnosed with mental illness. A theory-driven data extraction tool was developed, informed by Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory and the Stress Vulnerability Model, to assess the extent to which policies address multi-layered environmental influences, workplace stressors, mental illness relapse management, referral pathways, and reintegration procedures.ResultsThere is no policy or framework that is specifically designed for teachers diagnosed with mental illness. Only four South African Department of Basic Education policy documents met the study’s inclusion criteria. Thematic analysis revealed three key themes: (1) teachers as secondary stakeholders in school mental health policies, reflecting how policies provide minimal attention to teachers’ mental health despite their mandated role in supporting learners’ mental health; (2) Policy gaps in addressing mental illness, and (3) leave provisions for teachers diagnosed with mental illness. Although some policies and frameworks reference teacher wellbeing, they all position teachers as implementers and secondary beneficiaries. None of the policies and frameworks provides comprehensive, coordinated, or ecologically aligned guidance for managing mental illness, preventing mental illness relapse, or supporting reintegration.ConclusionThis study advocates for a policy to be specifically designed for teachers diagnosed with mental illness and for teachers’ mental health to be integrated into the existing School mental health policies. Strengthening policy integration, explicitly incorporating ecological and stress-vulnerability principles, and formalising relapse-prevention pathways are urgently needed to support teacher wellbeing and sustainable workforce functioning. Strengthening teacher mental health support requires multi-level, ecologically aligned policy approaches that integrate prevention of mental illness relapse, early identification of mental illness, and reintegration mechanisms post mental illness relapse within schools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27813-3.

Introduction

Mental illness among teachers has emerged as a critical public health and occupational concern, with implications for both educational outcomes and workforce sustainability. Globally, one billion people have mental illness, the most common being depression and anxiety [1]. Among teachers, the global prevalence rate of depression ranges from moderate rates of 4% to severe rates as high as 77%, while anxiety rates range from moderate rates of 38% to severe rates of 41.2% [2]. In Ethiopia, the prevalence of depression among teachers is reported at 44.7% [3], and in Egypt, the prevalence of anxiety among teachers is 67.5% and 23.2% depression [4]. In South Africa, general prevalence rates of depression and anxiety vary from moderate rates of 14.7% to severe rates of 38.8% [5], and depression affects 49.87% of South African teachers [6].

Worldwide, the teaching profession is associated with high levels of psychological distress, often resulting in absenteeism, reduced productivity, mental illness and early career exits. In South Africa, these challenges are worsened by systemic pressures, including limited mental health resources and inadequate workplace support [7]. School mental health promotion policies prioritise learner well-being but overlook teachers with existing mental illness and those grappling with mental illness relapse [8]. Several policies, frameworks and guidelines are in place to prevent mental stressors and enhance mental health [9–11]. Global policies, guidelines and frameworks are increasing emphasis on preventive and promotional mental health programs; however, these efforts overlook mental illness relapse management [12]. Similarly, existing literature largely examines teacher stress, burnout, psychological well-being, and intervention outcomes [13–15] with little to no focus on critically analysing policies that govern institutional support, accommodations, relapse prevention, and sustained mental health care for teachers diagnosed with mental illness. This reveals a substantial gap in both local and international scholarship regarding the adequacy, implementation, and comprehensiveness of school mental health policies specifically tailored to teachers’ mental health vulnerabilities.

In an attempt to care for persons with mental illness in South Africa, the Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002 provides guidelines for care, treatment, and rehabilitation [16]. The South African Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995 mandates reasonable accommodations for employees with mental illness [17, 18]. However, it also does not put measures in place for mental illness relapse. Furthermore, the South African Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993 emphasises the creation of safe work environments, including provisions for mental health support services [19]. However, it also does not have measures in place for mental illness relapse. Despite efforts like South Africa’s Employee Health and Wellness program in the Department of Basic Education, a significant gap remains in addressing mental illness relapse among teachers [20]. Existing support structures often provide broad employee wellness services but rarely establish policy-specific mechanisms for managing recurring mental illness episodes, workplace reintegration, ecological supports, or vulnerability reduction for teachers with chronic or relapsing psychiatric conditions [7].

Consequently, policy analysis focused specifically on teachers diagnosed with mental illness remains underdeveloped, particularly within South Africa, where educational, labour, occupational health, and mental health policies intersect but may not cohesively address teachers’ sustained mental health care needs. This policy neglect is particularly concerning given the essential role teachers play in educational continuity, learner outcomes, and national development [21]. Without robust policy frameworks that explicitly prioritise prevention, accommodation, recovery, and relapse mitigation for teachers, mental illness may continue to compromise teacher retention, occupational functioning [22], and the broader educational system. Therefore, a critical examination of existing school mental health policy support is urgently needed to identify structural gaps, inform evidence-based reforms, and strengthen sustainable mental health support systems for teachers with mental illness and grappling mental illness relapse within South Africa’s educational landscape.

Theoretical framework

This study aimed to critically analyse existing South African School mental health policies that support teachers diagnosed with mental illness, using Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24] to identify strengths, gaps, and opportunities for improved mental health support within educational settings. From Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory perspective [23], the focus of this study is on the exosystem and macrosystem, where education policies, labour legislation, and institutional structures are developed and enforced. These systems indirectly but powerfully influence teachers’ day-to-day experiences by determining the availability of workplace support, accommodations, and protections for those with mental illness. The Stress Vulnerability Framework [24] complements this perspective by explaining how the absence of adequate protective mechanisms increases the likelihood of mental illness relapse. Within this framework, inadequate policy support, stigma, and lack of organisational responsiveness function as stress-amplifying factors, undermining recovery and sustained work participation [7]. In so doing, this study sought to answer the research question: How do existing South African School mental health policies support teachers diagnosed with mental illness and those battling mental illness relapse in the workplace?

Understanding these dynamics is crucial to fostering inclusive and recovery-oriented educational environments. This paper argues that strengthening school mental health policies, enhancing leadership capacity, and fostering supportive environments are critical steps toward creating psychologically safe and enabling schools where teachers diagnosed with mental illness can not only return to work but thrive in it.

Methodology

Study design

A qualitative document analysis approach was used to systematically examine and interpret existing school mental health policies that address the support of teachers diagnosed with mental illness and battling mental illness relapse. Document analysis, as defined by Morgan [25], is used to elicit meaning, gain understanding, and develop empirical knowledge about a phenomenon. This approach was appropriate for identifying how current policies conceptualize, structure, and operationalize support for teachers experiencing mental illness within the school system. This study was guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24]. Furthermore, this study sought to answer the research question: How do existing South African School mental health policies support teachers diagnosed with mental illness and those battling mental illness relapse in the workplace?

Sampling

A purposive, comprehensive sampling strategy was used. Palinkas et al. [26] define a purposive, comprehensive sampling strategy as a non-probability sampling approach that intentionally selects all documents relevant to the research question. This sampling strategy was used to identify and include all national and provincial policy and framework documents relevant to teacher mental health and workplace well-being. This sampling approach ensured that only documents directly addressing, or implicitly related to, the management and support of teachers diagnosed with mental illness were included [26].

The inclusion criteria were (a) official national or provincial government and Department of Basic Education policies and framework documents, (b) the policy and framework documents should address teacher health and mental health, employee well-being or wellness in the workplace, (c) policies and framework documents related to workplace support or retention of employees with mental health conditions, (d) relevant policy and framework documents that were in active use at the time of the study.

The exclusion criteria were (a) policy and framework documents that only talk about learner health and well-being without reference to teachers, (b) documents that were not policy or frameworks, such as opinion pieces, without policy authority, (c) policy and framework documents that do not talk about teacher mental health, (d) policy and frameworks that were outdated, superseded or not officially endorsed by the South African Department of Basic Education.

The search process identified 7 policy and framework documents. Following screening and application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, four documents met the criteria and were included as the final number of relevant documents that were used to extract data and answer the research question. The four sampled documents were: the Integrated School Health Policy [17], the National School Safety Framework [27], the Employee Health and Wellness Strategic Framework [28], and the Policy and Procedure on Incapacity Leave and Ill-health Retirement [29], met the inclusion criteria.

Data collection

A systematic search strategy was used to identify relevant policy and framework documents in accordance with the study’s inclusion criteria. Documents were sourced from the official South African government [28] and South African Department of Basic Education websites [17, 27], including the Department of Public Service and Administration [28, 29]. Targeted searches were conducted using general web-based search engines, including Google and Google Scholar. Keywords and combinations used were: teacher mental health, teacher workplace wellness, teachers diagnosed with mental illness, South African school health policy, leave policies, and guidelines for teachers diagnosed with mental illness. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search, such as teacher AND mental health AND policy AND South Africa.

Each identified policy and framework was reviewed to determine how it addresses teachers’ mental health concerns, with specific attention to school-based support for teachers diagnosed with mental illness, leave entitlements, and return-to-work procedures after mental illness relapse. Data were extracted from policies and frameworks using a data extraction tool developed for this study, in accordance with Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress Vulnerability Model [24]. The data extraction tool included guiding questions on the availability, accessibility, and adequacy of workplace mental health support mechanisms, including referral pathways to Employee Health and Wellness (EHW) services, relapse management, reintegration strategies, and preventive interventions. Themes and subthemes

Table 1 presents the analysed qualitative data resulting from critiquing School mental health policies and frameworks that support teachers diagnosed with mental illness in the workplace. Guided by the Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress Vulnerability Framework [24], this led to the identification of three themes and six subthemes, with each theme capturing a broad area of support and the corresponding gaps. From an ecological systems perspective [23], the findings demonstrate how existing school-based mental health policies operate across different system levels, particularly the macrosystem and exosystem, and how these policy intentions are reflected within the microsystem of the school environment where teachers encounter everyday occupational demands. When viewed through the Stress Vulnerability Framework [24], the results highlight how existing policies frame teacher roles, responsibilities, and expectations in relation to mental health support, while indicating the extent to which stressors and protective supports are addressed within the policy landscape. Together, these theoretical lenses provide a structured framework for presenting the identified themes and subthemes, which outline the forms of support available to teachers and the areas where policy guidance is limited within the South African Department of Basic Education.

Theme 1: teachers as secondary stakeholders in school mental health policies

Subtheme 1.1: limited inclusion in policies

This subtheme explores the extent to which teachers are only marginally included in School mental health policies and frameworks, often as secondary stakeholders. Drawing on Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems theory [23], this analysis examines how multi-layered environmental systems influence the support provided to teachers. While teachers are key to implementing these frameworks and policies, the policies and frameworks tend to focus primarily on learners, with limited attention to teachers’ specific needs and well-being.

A cross-policy critique revealed that while the national frameworks governing school health and safety are comprehensive in scope, they are designed to support learners rather than teachers, offering limited provisions specifically aimed at teachers’ mental health. From the Stress Vulnerability Framework [24] perspective, this highlights that the policies provide minimal mechanisms to buffer teachers’ vulnerabilities or mental health stressors, potentially leaving them exposed to challenges associated with managing their own mental health while having to fulfil their professional responsibilities. Policies such as the Integrated School Health Policy [17] and the National School Safety Framework [28] demonstrate a commitment to ensuring schools remain supportive, safe, and health-promoting environments; however, they are framed in ways that position teachers as implementers rather than beneficiaries of support. For instance, the Integrated School Health Policy [17] states:

“Staff also need to be primarily designated as school health staff, which becomes their core responsibility, rather than as an add-on to other duties.” (page 10).

“Staff also need to be primarily designated as school health staff, which becomes their core responsibility, rather than as an add-on to other duties.” (page 10).

The above policy quote further highlights that learners are the primary beneficiaries of the policy. Moreover, the above policy quote further supports one of its objectives, which is:

“To support the school community in creating a safe and secure environment for teaching and learning.” (page 11).

“To support the school community in creating a safe and secure environment for teaching and learning.” (page 11).

This implies that teachers will be supported in creating a safe and secure school environment; however, the policy does not specify how, when, or by whom this support will be provided. Similarly, the National School Safety Framework [27] states that:

“The overall aim of the National School Safety Framework is to create a safe, violence and threat-free, supportive learning environment for learners, educators, principals, school governing bodies, and administration.” (page 3).

“The overall aim of the National School Safety Framework is to create a safe, violence and threat-free, supportive learning environment for learners, educators, principals, school governing bodies, and administration.” (page 3).

Similarly, with the Integrated School Health Policy [17], the National School Safety Framework [28] prioritises the learners, with limited direct inclusion and interventions for the teachers because it states that:

“A safe school is dependent on adequately trained and equipped educators and management. It is thus critical to provide educators and management with training that will assist them in identifying and dealing with safety and security threats.” (page 15).

“A safe school is dependent on adequately trained and equipped educators and management. It is thus critical to provide educators and management with training that will assist them in identifying and dealing with safety and security threats.” (page 15).

Subtheme 1.2. Absence of direct provisions for teacher mental health

This subtheme highlights a critical gap across existing school health and safety policies: the lack of direct provisions addressing teacher mental health. This absence suggests that teachers are expected to support learners while receiving limited institutional support themselves. Guided by the Stress Vulnerability Framework [24], this gap indicates that current policies and frameworks do not take into consideration the vulnerabilities of teachers being exposed to learners’ mental health challenges and how that can negatively impact the teachers’ mental health.

The Integrated School Health Policy [17] focuses on promoting learners’ health and well-being through initiatives such as health education, screenings, and access to health services. It aims to ensure that learners are physically and mentally healthy. The Bron Pérez-Jorge et al. [31] report that this is essential for their learning and development. Analysis from Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] indicates that policies and frameworks consider the different ecological systems levels when attempting to support learners’ mental health. While the ISHP [17] offers provision for learner health and mental health, it lacks direct provision for teacher mental health, as it states that it will ensure:

“More emphasis is being placed on the provision of health services (as opposed to screening and referral) in schools, with a commitment to expanding the range of services provided over time. Mechanisms for ensuring that learners who are assessed as requiring additional services receive these services need to be in place.” (page 7).

“More emphasis is being placed on the provision of health services (as opposed to screening and referral) in schools, with a commitment to expanding the range of services provided over time. Mechanisms for ensuring that learners who are assessed as requiring additional services receive these services need to be in place.” (page 7).

The Integrated School Health Policy [17] further states that:

“Although the ISHP will initially target the most disadvantaged schools, sequenced plans for progressive implementation aim to ensure that all learners are reached.” (page 7).

“Although the ISHP will initially target the most disadvantaged schools, sequenced plans for progressive implementation aim to ensure that all learners are reached.” (page 7).

Sadly, the ISHP [17] does not emphasise teachers as it does with the learners; it only provides direct provisions for learner mental health, as it mentions that:

“Implementation of the ISHP at the school level is the responsibility of the School-Based Support Team (SBST), which also includes the teachers, under the guidance of the school principal. Teachers are also required to ensure that all components of the ISHP package are provided to all learners” (page 20).

“Implementation of the ISHP at the school level is the responsibility of the School-Based Support Team (SBST), which also includes the teachers, under the guidance of the school principal. Teachers are also required to ensure that all components of the ISHP package are provided to all learners” (page 20).

Theme 2: policy gaps in addressing mental illness

Subtheme 2.1: limitations in policy implementation for teacher mental health support

This subtheme investigates the impact of health and safety legislation, and support programs on teachers’ mental health. Guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24], it also considers how interactions across multiple environmental systems, as well as the presence or absence of workplace supports, influence teachers’ vulnerability to stress and overall well-being. Additionally, this subtheme assesses the integration and impact of both legislative requirements and support programs in creating a safe and healthy work environment for teachers.

The Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA) [19] mandates employers to protect employees’ health and psychological well-being, including those in educational settings. OSHA [19] requires every work environment to implement health and safety policies that mitigate workplace hazards and promote a supportive work environment, which can directly influence teachers’ mental health.

In further response to the Occupational Health and Safety Act [19], the Department of Public Service and Administration (2023) has implemented the Employee Health and Wellness Program Strategic Framework [28], previously known as the Employee Assistance Program. This direct service initiative aims to enhance teacher productivity and effectiveness by offering support, resources, and interventions for stress management, burnout prevention, and mental health issues.

The Employee Health and Wellness Program Strategic Framework [28] further states that one of the bases for process indicators is to:

“Develop programmes which recognise mental health needs; Raise awareness of self and other mental wellbeing; identify and address factors affecting mental health in the workplace; Provide support options which are confidential and non-stigmatising.” (page 78).

“Develop programmes which recognise mental health needs; Raise awareness of self and other mental wellbeing; identify and address factors affecting mental health in the workplace; Provide support options which are confidential and non-stigmatising.” (page 78).

Subtheme 2.2: employee wellness and support programmes

This subtheme explores employee wellness and the available support programmes to enhance and support teachers’ mental health. Using Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24], this subtheme considers how workplace wellness initiatives interact with environmental systems and teachers’ individual vulnerabilities to buffer stress and promote mental well-being.

A previous study conducted in South Africa found that the strength of the Employee Health and Wellness Programme is that it provides an approach to employee wellness and aims to host workshops to help promote overall well-being and early detection of health issues. However, the weakness is that it may lack the depth and accessibility needed for consistent mental health support, particularly in rural or under-resourced areas [32].

In contrast to the Employee Health and Wellness Strategic Framework [28], Finland’s Occupational Health Care (OHC) [25] provides a robust Occupational Health Care system for teachers, as mandated by the Occupational Health Care Act No. 1383/2001. Finland’s OHC [33] offers extensive mental health services, including direct access to mental health professionals and structured rehabilitation programs. Furthermore, Finland’s OHC [33] emphasises preventive services and early intervention, with regular health check-ups and risk assessments. This approach is consistent with broader European occupational health and social protection systems, where preventive care, income protection, and structured return-to-work programmes are integrated within national frameworks [34, 35]. These systems typically combine employer responsibility with state-supported sickness benefits, enabling more comprehensive and sustained support for employees experiencing mental illness. In contrast, the United States adopts a less integrated approach to occupational mental health. While policies such as the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) provide job-protected leave for employees with serious health conditions, including mental illness, they do not typically include structured preventive or rehabilitation-focused occupational health services [36]. South Africa’s EHWSF [28] has limited interventions and structured plans. For instance, the framework highlights one of the output indicators concerning the management of mental health, as when:

“A Toolkit is developed and implemented for Mental Health Promotion in the workplace, which looks at practical steps for addressing mental health.” (page 78).

“A Toolkit is developed and implemented for Mental Health Promotion in the workplace, which looks at practical steps for addressing mental health.” (page 78).

Theme 3: leave provisions for teachers diagnosed with mental illness

Subtheme 3.1: Temporary Incapacity Leave (TIL) and categories

This subtheme examines the provisions for Temporary Incapacity Leave (TIL) as outlined in the Policy and Procedure on Incapacity Leave and Ill-health Retirement [28]. It includes a detailed analysis of the criteria for short-term and long-term TIL, the documentation required, and the discretionary nature of the leave approval process. It also differentiates between short-term and long-term TIL, exploring how each category is applied and the requirements for medical proof and periodic reviews.

Guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24], this subtheme considers how systemic policies and leave procedures interact with teachers’ vulnerabilities, stressors, and ability to manage mental health challenges within the workplace. This subtheme further explores the sick leave cycle that teachers are entitled to, what guides the allocation of the sick leave cycle, the process to follow when applying for sick leave, and the usage limits.

The Department of Basic Education is guided by the Policy and Procedure on Incapacity Leave and Ill-health Retirement [29], which is a policy developed in accordance with the principles and provisions of the Labour Relations Act 66 [37], among other relevant legislation. The PILIR [29] provides comprehensive procedures for applying for incapacity leave, the required medical documentation, the duration of leave, and the process for considering ill-health retirement. It aims to ensure that these processes are fair, consistent, and supportive of employees’ health and well-being, while also considering the needs of the employer or institution. For extended sick leave due to mental health issues, teachers can apply for additional leave under the temporary incapacity leave provision. This requires thorough medical documentation and is subject to approval by the DBE. The policy outlines what incapacity leave is, by stating that:

“Incapacity leave is not an unlimited amount of additional sick leave days at an employee’s disposal. Incapacity leave is additional sick leave granted conditionally at the Employer’s discretion.” (page 7).

“Incapacity leave is not an unlimited amount of additional sick leave days at an employee’s disposal. Incapacity leave is additional sick leave granted conditionally at the Employer’s discretion.” (page 7).

It further outlines the criteria for applying for incapacity leave by mentioning that:

“An employee who has exhausted his/her normal sick leave, referred to in the leave determination, during the prescribed leave cycle and who, according to the treating medical practitioner, requires to be absent from work due to a temporary incapacity, may apply for temporary incapacity leave with full pay on the applicable application forms.” (page 7).

“An employee who has exhausted his/her normal sick leave, referred to in the leave determination, during the prescribed leave cycle and who, according to the treating medical practitioner, requires to be absent from work due to a temporary incapacity, may apply for temporary incapacity leave with full pay on the applicable application forms.” (page 7).

Moreover, it guides the teachers on the required documents that must accompany the temporary incapacity leave forms, by stating that:

“The employee must provide medical proof that he/she is not in a functional position to work.” (page 7).

“The employee must provide medical proof that he/she is not in a functional position to work.” (page 7).

Subtheme 3.2: impact of leave processes on teachers’ mental health

This subtheme assesses the impact of leave policies on teachers’ mental health, focusing on the advantages and limitations of the Policy and procedure on Incapacity Leave and Ill-health Retirement [29]. It examines how the policy’s provisions for sick leave and incapacity leave affect teachers’ stress levels, recovery, and overall well-being. It also discusses the need for additional support systems, such as structured rehabilitation and therapy, to enhance the effectiveness of leave policies in supporting teachers with mental health issues.

The pros of the PILIR [29] are that it provides necessary extension beyond standard sick leave, accommodating the often-lengthy recovery periods associated with mental health conditions [38], as stated in its overview statement:

“The leave dispensation provides for normal sick leave of 36 working days in a sick leave cycle of three years. If an employee has exhausted his/her normal sick leave, the Employer may, at his/her discretion, grant additional incapacity leave, i.e., temporary incapacity leave and, where applicable, permanent incapacity leave.” (page 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to critically analyse existing South African School mental health policies that support teachers diagnosed with mental illness and those battling with mental illness relapse, using Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24] to identify strengths, gaps, and opportunities for improved mental health support for teachers within educational settings. Guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23], the findings demonstrate that current School mental health policies predominantly operate at the exosystem and macrosystem levels, focusing on institutional safety, governance, and regulatory compliance, with limited attention to the microsystem where teachers directly experience psychological stress, support, and recovery within their immediate work environments.

Results indicated that the Integrated School Health Policy [17] and the National School Safety Framework [27] align with the requirements of the South African Occupational Health and Safety Act [19], which mandates employers to protect the health and psychological well-being of workers and to have health and safety policies in place. This is significant because a safe and secure environment minimises the risks and incidents that can cause stress and anxiety for teachers [40]. Therefore, by mitigating these concerns, teachers can focus on their teaching duties without the added burden of fear for their safety or well-being. This is crucial because evidence shows that the threat to teachers’ safety at school and experience of violence or bullying, either between learner to learner or leaner to teacher, has a negative impact on the teachers’ mental health, which leads to low productivity as it causes long-term psychosocial and professional harm to the teachers [41].

However, despite the benefits of enabling a safe and healthy work environment for teachers, both the ISHP [17] and the NSSF [27] fail to provide direct interventions, instead expecting teachers to drive the school health and safety policy and framework. Over and above the failure of the ISHP [17] and NSSF [27] ability to provide direct interventions for teacher’s mental well-being, they both indirectly benefits teachers, and this is because a healthier leaner is likely to be more engaged in learning [42], exhibit fewer behavioural issues, and require less disciplinary intervention [43], thus reducing stress and workload for teachers [43].

Additionally, the policy’s emphasis on a holistic school environment that promotes mental health can foster a more supportive and less stressful work environment for teachers [44]. From an ecological perspective, the Integrated School Health Policy and the National School Safety Framework primarily address exosystem-level determinants by promoting structurally safe and regulated school environments. However, their limited focus on direct teacher mental health support reflects a gap at the microsystem level, where daily interpersonal interactions, confidentiality, and emotional safety play a critical role in mental illness recovery.

The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisation (UNESCO) [45] and other partners developed the Teacher Support and Motivation Framework for Africa. This framework aims to enhance the overall working conditions and professional support for teachers in Africa, thereby indirectly promoting their mental health by fostering a positive and supportive work environment. However, like the national frameworks, it falls short of providing direct mental health services specifically for teachers and for teachers diagnosed with mental illness and battling mental illness relapse. Using the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24], the absence of direct mental health interventions within School mental health policies represents a critical limitation, as unmanaged workplace stressors may interact with individual vulnerability, increasing the risk of mental illness relapse among teachers. While environmental safety reduces certain stressors, the lack of structured psychological support and relapse prevention strategies weakens protective factors necessary for sustained recovery.

The Employee Health and Wellness Strategic Framework [27] has a limited direct step-by-step process that must be followed in assisting teachers diagnosed with mental illness, and this is because it groups mental illness with other non-communicable diseases. It lacks measures to assist teachers battling mental illness relapse and ensure their psychological safety in the work environment. Within the Stress-Vulnerability framework [24], the grouping of mental illness with other non-communicable diseases limits the framework’s responsiveness to relapse-sensitive conditions. Mental illness requires proactive stress reduction, early intervention, and continuous psychosocial support, rather than the largely reactive approach currently embedded in the Employee Health and Wellness Strategic Framework.

A study in the Free State Province, South Africa, identified challenges in implementing the Employee Health and Wellness Program, including limited reach to rural teachers, budget limitations, lack of senior management commitment, insufficient support from other departmental sections, and a reactive rather than proactive approach to workplace issues [32]. This situation emphasises the need for a more comprehensive and strategic approach to ensure that the Employee Health and Wellness Strategic Framework [27] provides visible teacher psychological safety in the workplace, especially for teachers diagnosed with mental illness and battling mental illness relapse. South Africa’s EHWSF [27] lacks the systematic preventive and rehabilitation approach seen in Finland’s OHC [33]. Finland’s OHC [33] emphasis on preventive services, structured mental health support, and seamless integration with schools provides a strong model for holistic teacher wellness. This approach aligns with broader European occupational health systems, where preventive care, early intervention, and coordinated return-to-work programmes are embedded within national social protection framework [34, 35] In contrast, in the United States, workplace mental health support is less centrally coordinated; while policies such as the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) provide job-protected leave for employees experiencing mental health conditions, they do not typically incorporate structured preventive or rehabilitation-focused occupational health services [36] South Africa’s EHWSF [27], while beneficial, could enhance its impact by expanding mental health services and improving accessibility, particularly in under-resourced areas [32].

The Policy and Procedure on Incapacity Leave and Ill-health Retirement (PILIR) [28] outlines clear categories for incapacity leave, including normal sick leave and temporary incapacity leave (TIL). The policy is aimed at supporting teachers who are unable to perform their duties due to ill health or mental illness relapse, as in the case of teachers diagnosed with mental illness. While these provisions appear to offer a structured framework for teacher support, there is still a gap in the policy’s practical accessibility. The findings show that although temporary incapacity leave is available, it is subject to employer discretion and a complex application process, often requiring detailed medical reports and lengthy investigations. This creates barriers to timely access, especially for teachers experiencing mental illness relapse, who may not have the psychological or administrative capacity to navigate such a demanding system while unwell. This then highlights the need for the policy to have exceptions for mental illness regarding the timeframe for submission of the completed form and the required documents.

The cons of the Policy and Procedure on Incapacity Leave and Ill-health Retirement (PILIR) [28] are that the application form is lengthy and requires extensive specialists’ documentation at the teachers’ expense, which can be stressful for teachers diagnosed with mental illness issues. Since the approval is at the discretion of the health risk management, there is no guarantee that leave will be granted, which can also lead to uncertainty and additional stress for the teachers. Moreover, the policy indicates that if an employee refuses to accept the adapted duties or to move to alternative employment, which is more suitable for his/her incapacity, the Employer may, subject to due process being followed, terminate the services of the employee concerned on grounds of operational reasons. This could create a stressful situation for teachers with existing mental illness, potentially exacerbating their condition. While the policy allows for essential recovery time and alternative work recommendations, it lacks comprehensive support systems, such as return-to-work programs and ongoing mental health support.

Despite the complexities of the PILIR [28], it offers significant advantages over Sierra Leone’s system. In Sierra Leone, if a teacher’s absence due to sickness exceeds six months, their salary is halved, and after one year, they are removed from service [46]. In contrast, PILIR [28] provides a more supportive framework, allowing for extended short term temporary incapacity leave and the possibility of long-term temporary incapacity leave based on comprehensive medical assessments. This offers greater security and decreases psychological distress [39]. Therefore, South Africa’s PILIR [28] represents a significant step forward in supporting teachers with mental health issues, offering a structured yet flexible approach to incapacity leave and ill-health retirement. However, when compared to higher-income contexts, both strengths and limitations become apparent. In the United States, the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) provides up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave for employees with serious health conditions, including mental illness, ensuring continuity of employment through a clear legal entitlement. While this offers stronger job protection, it is typically unpaid, limiting its accessibility for individuals requiring prolonged recovery [36]. In contrast, many European countries implement comprehensive sickness benefit systems that combine paid sick leave with state-supported income protection and legally mandated return-to-work programmes [34]. These systems often provide more generous financial support and place greater responsibility on employers to facilitate reintegration into the workplace.

However, to fully meet the needs of teachers facing mental health challenges, the policy must evolve to include mandatory mental health support and rehabilitation for teachers with mental health issues or mental illness. Colizzi et al. [47] support this by relating that it will limit poor long-term outcomes. Together, Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress-Vulnerability Model [24] reveal that while South African School mental health policies demonstrate structural intent to support teacher well-being, they insufficiently address the dynamic interaction between workplace stressors, individual vulnerability, and recovery needs, particularly at the interpersonal and organisational levels where relapse prevention is most critical.

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations as the study relied on interpreting existing data rather than conducting stakeholder interviews. Consequently, the analysis reflects the intended policy design, rather than how these policies are enacted or experienced by teachers diagnosed with mental illness and those battling mental illness relapse in practice. In addition, the study focused on a purposive selection of national and provincial policies, and it is possible that some relevant documents were not identified or included. Future studies should complement document reviews with qualitative interviews or focus groups involving teachers, school administrators, and policymakers to bridge this gap between policy intent and lived implementation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a critical policy gap in the South African Department of Basic Education system, specifically the absence of a dedicated, coherent policy framework that recognises and supports teachers living with mental illness or battling mental illness relapse. While existing School mental health policies emphasise learner wellbeing and position teachers as implementers and secondary beneficiaries, they systematically overlook teachers as primary recipients of mental health protection and support. As a result, teachers are expected to sustain emotionally demanding roles without structural safeguards for their own mental health [7].

Viewed through Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [23] and the Stress Vulnerability Framework [24], the findings of this study highlight how systemic, organisational and policy-level factors actively undermine recovery and increase vulnerability to mental illness relapse among teachers with existing mental illness. The lack of school-based support and structured return-to-work support illustrates how institutional environments may inadvertently perpetuate stress, stigma, and exclusion rather than promote recovery and sustainability within the teaching workforce [7]. The implications for public health are substantial as teachers’ mental well-being is not only a workforce issue but a population-level concern that directly influences educational quality, learner outcomes, and long-term system resilience. Without policy reform that explicitly prioritises teacher mental health, the education sector risks reinforcing cycles of relapse, absenteeism, early exit from the profession, and compromised teaching capacity. This study, therefore, calls on the South African Department of Basic Education to move beyond learner-centred mental health policies and adopt a comprehensive, teacher-inclusive wellbeing strategy. Such a strategy should explicitly address mental illness relapse, provide clear guidelines for workplace accommodations, ensure supportive leadership practices, and embed mental health protection across policy, school management, and occupational health structures. Positioning teacher wellbeing as a public mental health priority is essential for sustaining a healthy, effective, and resilient education system.
