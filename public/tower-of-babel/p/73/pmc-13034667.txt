Automated abdominal aortic calcification and trabecular bone score independently predict incident fracture during routine osteoporosis screening
Authors: Abadi K Gebre, Marc Sim, Syed Zulqarnain Gilani, Afsah Saleem, Cassandra Smith, Didier Hans, Siobhan Reid, Barret A Monchka, Douglas Kimelman, Mohammad Jafari Jozani, John T Schousboe, Joshua R Lewis, William D Leslie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal aortic calcification (AAC), a marker of subclinical cardiovascular disease, has previously shown to be associated with low BMD and fracture. However, it remains unclear whether AAC is associated with trabecular bone score (TBS), a gray-level textural measure, or whether it predicts fracture risk independent of this measure. Here, we examined the cross-sectional association of AAC scored using a validated machine learning algorithm (ML-AAC24) with TBS, and their simultaneous associations with incident fractures in 7691 individuals (93.4% women) through the Manitoba BMD Registry (mean age 75.3 yr). The association between ML-AAC24 and TBS was tested using generalized linear regression. Cox proportional hazards models tested the simultaneous relationships of ML-AAC24 and TBS with incident fractures. At baseline, 41.3% of the study cohort had low (<2), 32.4% had moderate (2 to <6), and 26.3% had high (≥6) ML-AAC24. Compared to low ML-AAC24, high ML-AAC24 was associated with a 0.81% lower TBS in the multivariable-adjusted model. Independent of each other and multiple established fracture risk factors, ML-AAC24 and TBS were each associated with an increased risk of incident fractures. Specifically, high ML-AAC24 (HR 1.41, 95% CI: 1.15-1.73, compared to low ML-AAC24) and lower TBS (HR 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05-1.22, per SD decrease) were associated with increased relative hazards for any incident fracture. High ML-AAC24 and lower TBS were also associated with incident major osteoporotic fracture (HR 1.48, 95% CI: 1.18-1.87 and HR 1.15, 95% CI: 1.06-1.25, respectively) and hip fracture (HR 1.56, 95% CI: 1.05-2.31 and HR 1.25, 95% CI: 1.08-1.44, respectively). In conclusion, high ML-AAC24 is associated with lower TBS in older adults attending routine osteoporosis screening. Both measures were associated with incident fractures. The findings of this study highlight high ML-AAC24, seen in more than 1 in 4 of the study cohort, and lower TBS provide complementary prognostic information for fracture risk.

Introduction

Vascular calcification is an active and highly regulated process involving a progressive pathological build-up of calcium within the arterial walls.1 The abdominal aorta is one of the first arteries where calcification is observed and abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) is an established marker of subclinical cardiovascular disease (CVD).2 Abdominal aortic calcification can be readily identified from vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) images obtained using DXA and radiographs.3

Compared to individuals with low AAC, those with higher AAC have greater risks for muscle strength decline,4 incident rapid weight loss,5 and falls.6,7 Higher AAC is also associated with lower BMD, accelerated bone loss,8 and higher fracture risk,9 supporting a vascular-bone nexus. This link between calcification in the blood vessels and decline in bone health is likely due to multiple factors, including loss of conduit function for nutrient exchange and altered blood flow,10 shared genetic, lifestyle factors, and signaling mechanisms.1

Reliance on time-consuming manual scoring of AAC by highly trained experts has been automated using a machine learning algorithm (ML-AAC24),11 significantly improving its prospect for routine clinical utility.3 Two prospective studies have reported an association between higher ML-AAC24 and increased fracture risk: one involving 1024 community dwelling older Australian women,12 and another including 11 013 older adults from the Manitoba BMD Registry.13 These analyses accounted for a range of established risk factors, including previous fracture and FN BMD.13 However, other aspects of skeletal health, such as bone texture and microarchitecture, also known to provide useful information beyond BMD, were not considered.14

Trabecular bone score (TBS), a textural measure derived from LS DXA images, is associated with fracture risk independent of BMD and fracture probability (eg, FRAX)14 and its clinical utility is widely recognized. However, to the best of our knowledge, only one cross-sectional study involving 146 dialysis patients has reported a weak association between greater AAC and lower TBS.15 The small sample sizes and the inclusion of only chronic kidney disease patients limit generalizability of these findings, warranting further investigation.

While AAC and TBS can be opportunistically captured using DXA during routine osteoporosis screening, it is unclear whether ML-AAC24 and lower TBS are related to each other or whether each can independently predict fracture risk. Additionally, despite multiple reports demonstrating an association between AAC (assessed by imaging experts) and lower BMD,9 it remains unclear whether ML-AAC24 is related to lower BMD, especially in people attending routine clinical bone density assessment. The present study sought to examine correlations between ML-AAC24 and TBS, and their independent associations with incident fractures using data from the Manitoba BMD Registry. It also reports the association between ML-AAC24 and BMD at multiple skeletal sites.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This study included a cohort of 7691 older adults from the Canadian province of Manitoba who underwent VFA as part of BMD testing between March 2010 and December 2017 (Figure S1). Eligibility for VFA was determined from international criteria and a cost-effectiveness analysis.16,17 Briefly, the criteria include, T-score of ≤−1.5 at the LS, TH, or FN, and (1) age ≥70 yr, or (2) age 50-69 yr plus more than 5 cm of historical height loss (difference between recalled young adult and current height) or measured height loss >2.5 cm, and (3) exposure to glucocorticoids for ≥3 mo over the past year. Notably, health services in Manitoba, including DXA testing, are delivered through a single, integrated provincial program that serves almost all residents. Established in 1997, the Manitoba BMD Registry maintains a comprehensive database of DXA results, with over 99% completeness and accuracy. Importantly, this database is linked to other population-based databases through a unique and anonymized personal identifier. The study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Board of the University of Manitoba.

Baseline assessment

BMI (kg/m2) was determined from height (m) and weight (kg) measurements. Smoking status was collected through a standardized questionnaire during the clinical visit for DXA testing. History of parental hip fracture was obtained from linked hospitalization records and self-reported data. Medical history prior to the DXA testing, including fractures, rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, secondary causes of osteoporosis, hypertension, myocardial infarction, and cerebrovascular disease, was obtained through linked health records (hospital discharge abstracts and outpatient medical claims diagnoses) as previously described.18 Age and area of residence (rural vs urban determined based on postal code) were obtained from linked population registry. Income (lower two quintiles vs upper three quintiles) level was also included.19 The history of dispensed medications—including anti-osteoporosis treatment (including systemic estrogen), statins, beta blockers, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, thiazide diuretics, beta-blockers, and calcium channel blockers (CCBs) (≥6 mo of use) or glucocorticoid (≥3 mo of use)—in the year prior to DXA testing, was obtained from a linked, province-wide retail pharmacy database that captures all dispensed prescription medications in Manitoba. Average spine tissue thickness (in cm), which closely agrees with sagittal abdominal diameter measured from CT (Pearson r = 0.95), was automatically measured and recorded by the densitometer software from the spine DXA image (GE enCORE version 14.×).20,21

Machine learning assessment of abdominal aortic calcification 24-point scores (ML-AAC24)

Abdominal aortic calcification was scored using a novel machine learning algorithm applied to de-identified unlabeled VFA images derived from DXA scans (Lunar Prodigy or iDXA, GE Healthcare). The machine learning algorithm was developed based on the original 24-point semiquantitative scoring method described previously.7,11–13 Briefly, the algorithm was trained on lateral spine images from the Hologic-4500A dataset (n = 1914) and then independently tested on images from the Hologic Horizon (n = 508). To help the algorithm adapt GE (Lunar Prodigy and iDXA models) bone density machine scans, it was fine-tuned on 2331 (90%) iDXA and Lunar Prodigy scans from the Manitoba BMD Registry and subsequently cross validated on the remaining 259 (10%) of the GE images. These VFA images had a human expert assessed AAC24 scores. The cross-validation process was repeated for each 10% random sets. This algorithm was then applied on unlabeled VFA images to estimate AAC scores. Based on established AAC severity categories, ML-AAC24 was classified as low (ML-AAC24 score < 2), moderate (ML-AAC24 score 2 to <6), and high (ML-AAC24 > 6). Details of the datasets and their sample sizes used to train/test/validate the machine learning algorithm, and their performances were extensively described previously.11

TBS and BMD measurements

Lumbar spine TBS measurements (L1-L4 without vertebral body exclusions) were performed in the Bone Disease Unit at the University of Lausanne, Switzerland (TBS iNsight Software, Version 3.03, Medimaps Group) blinded to clinical outcomes using de-identified spine DXA files from Manitoba BMD Registry. No TBS phantom was available for scanner cross-calibration given the retrospective study design. Detailed information is available in previous publications.22,23 In this analysis, TBS was considered as a continuous measure (per SD decrease) as well as in tertiles operationalized based on previously developed cut-points.24 Specifically, TBS was categorized as tertile 1 (high fracture risk, TBS < 1.23), tertile 2 (intermediate fracture risk, TBS 1.23-1.31) and tertile 3 (low fracture risk, TBS > 1.31).

BMD at the LS, TH, and FN was determined using DXA (Lunar Prodigy or iDXA, GE Healthcare). All densitometers used were carefully monitored by a medical physicist and showed stable long-term performance (phantom coefficient of variation < 0.5%). Cross-calibration procedures showed negligible between-scanner differences (<0.1 T-score). Short-term (different day, mean 1 wk) test–retest measurements in 96 Prodigy and 50 iDXA scan-pairs that involved all DXA technologists gave 95% least significant change (LSC) of 0.080 for TBS, 0.050 g/cm2 for LS BMD, and 0.030 g/cm2 for TH BMD.25

Incident fractures ascertainment

Any incident fracture was defined as having hip, clinical vertebral, forearm, humerus, pelvis, or other fractures (excluding head/neck, hands/feet, and ankle), occurring between the date of DXA visit and VFA up to March 31, 2018, and was obtained from linked health records. Specifically, (1) hospital discharge abstracts with diagnoses and procedures coded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-9-CM] before 2004 and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Canadian Enhancements [ICD-10-CA] from 2004 onward, as well as (2) physician billing claims (coded using ICD-9-CM) were used to identify the incident fractures. Hip, proximal, humerus, distal radius, and clinical vertebral fractures were collectively classified as major osteoporotic fractures. Fractures involving hip and forearm required additional site-specific fracture reduction, fixation, or casting codes. Further details on fracture ascertainment are available in prior publications.13,26

Statistical analysis

Unadjusted mean differences in TBS and BMD by ML-AAC24 extent were assessed using one-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc between-group comparisons using Tukey Honest Significant Difference test for unequal sample sizes. To test for multivariable-adjusted differences in TBS and BMD by ML-AAC24 extent, generalized linear regression models were employed, including (1) Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, and (2) Model 2, which included Model 1 as well as BMI, socioeconomic status, smoking status, previous fracture, and parental hip fracture; secondary causes of osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, hypertension, myocardial infraction, and cerebrovascular disease; medication use, including antiosteoporosis treatment, statins, beta-blockers, ARBs, ACE, thiazide diuretics and CCBs, and prolonged glucocorticoid use. All the variables included in Model 2 showed variance inflation factors of less than 2, suggesting no multicollinearity. Given previous reports suggesting that excess spine tissue thickness may artifactually reduce TBS27 and is associated with lower BMD and fracture,28,29 we evaluated the associations between ML-AAC24, TBS, and BMD after accounting for average tissue thickness in Model 3 (ie, Model 2 plus spine tissue thickness). Results are presented as estimated marginal means and 95% CI. Percentage differences in TBS and BMD at multiple skeletal sites were calculated by subtracting the estimated mean in the low ML-AAC24 group (the reference group) from the estimated mean in the moderate and high ML-AAC24 groups. These differences were then divided by the mean TBS or a site-specific BMD in the reference group and multiplied by 100.

Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression was used to determine the association of ML-AAC24 extent and lower TBS (per SD decrease), simultaneously as exposures of interest, with incident fractures. Two-way interaction testing was performed between ML-AAC24 extent and TBS (per SD decrease) for incident fractures using Cox regression analysis in Model 1. We performed additional sensitivity analyses using: Model 4, which included Model 2 plus FN T-score; and Model 5, adjusted for Model 2 plus spine tissue thickness and FN T-score. In this particular model, the variance inflation factors for BMI and spine average tissue thickness were slightly over five as the variables were correlated. As a rule of thumb, variance inflation factors less than ten suggest no serious multicollinearity. In Models 1-5, mutually adjusted associations of ML-AAC24 extent and TBS tertiles with the fracture outcomes were determined. Model 5 was used to generate 5-yr cumulative incidence curves evaluated at the covariate means. Log-likelihood chi-square statistic, as described in previous studies,7,13 was used to evaluate importance of each variable included in Model 5 by assessing the impact of removing individual predictors from the model. For all the analyses, p-values <.05 was considered statistically significant.

The majority (93.4%) were women, almost 60% had moderate or high ML-AAC24, and 27.1% had previous fracture. The mean TBS of the study population was 1.235 ± 0.102.

Association of ML-AAC24 with TBS and BMD

Compared to patients with low ML-AAC24, those with moderate and high AAC24 had 1.28% (0.16 SD) and 1.85% (0.23 SD) lower unadjusted TBS, respectively. These differences remained significant in both Models 1 (1.20% and 1.67%, respectively) and 2 (0.89% and 0.81%, respectively). However, after further accounting for spine tissue thickness, the mean difference in TBS between high versus low ML-AAC24 was non-significant (Table 2). Those with moderate and high ML-AAC24 had 1.37% (0.10 SD) and 3.87% (0.29 SD) lower unadjusted TH BMD, respectively, compared to patients with low ML-AAC24. They also had 1.44% (0.12 SD) and 3.14% (0.26 SD) lower unadjusted FN BMD. These associations were confirmed in Models 1, 2, and even after adjustment for spine tissue thickness (Model 3). Compared to low ML-AAC24, 1.57% (0.10 SD) and 4.39% (0.28 SD) higher unadjusted LS BMD were observed in individuals with moderate and high ML-AAC24, respectively. Furthermore, high ML-AAC24 remained significantly associated with higher LS BMD in Models 1, 2, and 3 (Table 2).

Individual associations of ML-AAC24 and TBS with incident fractures

Over a mean follow-up period of 3.7 ± 2.2 yr, 8.9% (n = 685) of the study cohort experienced any fracture, 7.0% (n = 542) experienced a major osteoporotic fracture, and 2.5% (n = 189) experienced a hip fracture. In separate models without accounting for each other, high ML-AAC24 and lower TBS (per SD decrease) were each associated with any incident fracture (HR 1.43, 95% CI: 1.16-1.75 for high ML-AAC24; 1.14, 95% CI: 1.06-1.23 for TBS), major osteoporotic fracture (HRs 1.50, 95% CI: 1.19-1.89 and 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06-1.26, respectively) and hip fracture (HRs 1.61, 95% CI: 1.09-2.38 and 1.26, 95% CI: 1.09-1.45, respectively) in Model 2. These associations remained significant for incident any fracture (HRs 1.39, 95% CI: 1.13-1.70 and 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01-1.18, respectively) and major osteoporotic fracture (HRs 1.45, 95% CI: 1.15-1.83 and 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01-1.20, respectively) even after adjustment for FN BMD, but were attenuated (non-significant) for hip fracture (Table S1).

Mutually adjusted associations of ML-AAC24 and TBS with any incident fracture

No significant interaction was found between ML-AAC24 and TBS for any incident fracture (p-value for interaction = 0.832). Additionally, no interactions of sex with ML-AAC24 and TBS for any incident fracture and other considered outcomes were observed. Compared to individuals with low ML-AAC24, those with moderate (HR 1.23, 95% CI: 1.02-1.48) and high ML-AAC24 (HR 1.57, 95% CI: 1.29-1.90) had a significantly greater relative hazard for any incident fracture, independent of TBS in Model 1. Each SD decrease in TBS was associated with an increase in relative hazard for any incident fracture (HR 1.17, 95% CI: 1.09-1.26). Although these effect sizes were modestly reduced with multivariable adjustments, the associations remained statistically significant for high ML-AAC24 and lower TBS (per SD decrease) even in Models 4 and 5 (Tables 3 and 4). Considering the relative importance of the variables included in Model 5, age (χ2 = 30.9), FN T-score (χ2 = 29.1), previous fracture (χ2 = 26.1), ML-AAC24 (χ2 = 9.7), parental hip fracture (χ2 = 7.4), TBS (χ2 = 6.2, per SD decrease), and prior cerebrovascular disease (χ2 = 9.7) were the top seven contributors to fracture risk (Figure 1).

Mutually adjusted associations of ML-AAC24 and TBS with incident major osteoporotic fracture

No significant interaction was found between ML-AAC24 and TBS for a major osteoporotic fracture (p-value for interaction = 0.719). In Model 1, individuals with moderate (HR 1.29, 95% CI: 1.05-1.59) and high ML-AAC24 (HR 1.66, 95% CI: 1.34-2.07) had a significantly greater relative hazard of experiencing a major osteoporotic fracture compared to those with low ML-AAC24. Each SD decrease in TBS was also associated with an increase (HR 1.18, 95% CI: 1.09-1.29) in relative hazard for a major osteoporotic fracture. Albeit slightly reduced, results remained statistically significant after multivariable adjustments (Tables 3 and 4). The top seven contributors to major osteoporotic fracture risk included FN T-score (χ2 = 27.4), age (χ2 = 21.3), previous fracture (χ2 = 13.6), ML-AAC24 (χ2 = 9.8), rheumatoid arthritis (χ2 = 6.6), parental hip fracture (χ2 = 6.3), and TBS (χ2 = 5.3, per SD decrease) (Figure S2).

Mutually adjusted associations of ML-AAC24 and TBS with incident hip fracture

No statistically significant interaction was observed between ML-AAC24 extent and lower TBS (per SD decrease) for hip fractures (p-value for interaction = 0.084). In Model 1, individuals with high ML-AAC24 (HR 1.87, 95% CI: 1.29-2.72) had a significantly higher relative hazard for a hip fracture, compared to those with low ML-AAC24. Each SD decrease in TBS was also associated with an increased relative hazard of hip fracture (HR 1.29, 95% CI: 1.12-1.48). In Models 2 and 3, high ML-AAC24 and lower TBS (per SD decrease) remained significantly associated with an increased risk of hip fracture, independent of each other (Table 3). However, these associations were non-significant in Models 4 and 5 (Table 4). The top seven contributor for hip fracture risk in Model 5 included FN T-score (χ2 = 46.8), age (χ2 = 26.7), rheumatoid arthritis (χ2 = 5.0), previous fracture (χ2 = 4.8), BMI (χ2 = 4.3), parental hip fracture (χ2 = 4.1), and ML-AAC24 (χ2 = 3.1) (Figure S3).

Compared to low ML-AAC24 and highest TBS tertile, high ML-AAC24 and the lowest TBS tertile were associated with an increased relative hazard of any incident fracture (HRs 1.56, 95% CI: 1.28-1.89; 1.57, 95% CI: 1.26-1.95, respectively), major osteoporotic fracture (1.66, 95% CI: 1.33-2.06; 1.56, 95% CI: 1.22-1.99, respectively) and hip fracture (1.87, 95% CI: 1.29-2.73; 1.70, 95% CI: 1.11-2.60, respectively) in Model 1, independent of each other. The results remained similar for any incident fracture (HRs 1.41, 95% CI: 1.15-1.73 and 1.46, 95% CI: 1.17-1.83, respectively) and major osteoporotic fracture (HRs 1.48, 95% CI: 1.18-1.86 and 1.47, 95% CI: 1.15-1.89, respectively) with multivariable adjustment (Model 2) and further adjustment with spine average tissue thickness, FN T-score or both (Table S2). Moreover, they exhibited higher 5-yr cumulative incidence of any fracture compared to their respective counterparts (Figure S4). However, significant associations of both high ML-AAC24 and the lowest TBS tertile with hip fractures were observed only in Model 1 (Table S2).

Discussion

In this large clinical registry of patients undergoing VFA as part of routine clinical bone density assessment, high ML-AAC24 was associated with lower TBS. However, the association was weak, and attenuated after adjustment for spine tissue thickness. High ML-AAC24 was consistently associated with lower TH and FN BMD, as well as higher LS BMD, independent of multiple factors. No statistically significant interaction was observed between ML-AA24 and TBS for fracture outcomes, suggesting that high ML-AAC24 predicts fracture equally well irrespective of TBS levels. More importantly, we have demonstrated for the first time that high ML-AAC24 and lower TBS provide complementary information for identifying individuals at high risk of fracture, independent of each other and of traditional fracture risk factors, including FN T-score. Surprisingly, in the analysis of the relative variable importance, ML-AAC-24 extent and lower TBS (per SD decrease) made larger contributions to the prediction of any fracture and major osteoporotic fracture than established risk factors, including smoking, glucocorticoid use, secondary causes of osteoporosis, and diabetes. Given that ML-AAC24 can be opportunistically captured during routine osteoporosis screening and provides prognostic information for fracture risk independent of established risk factors, its inclusion alongside TBS and other risk factors into fracture risk prediction models may improve risk prediction and management.

To date, one study has reported an investigation on the association between AAC and TBS in 149 patients receiving dialysis (mean age 48 ± 13 yr, 40% women). This study reported a weak cross-sectional association (β = −.160) between AAC and lower TBS.15 Additionally, a modest inverse association (β = −.473) between carotid intima-media thickness, a marker of subclinical CVD, and TBS has been reported in 131 patients with type 2 diabetes (mean age 60.0 ± 7.3 yr, 58% women).30 However, no association was observed in 265 non-diabetic participants (58.9 ± 7.8 yr, 60% women). These studies considered only a few potential confounding factors and had relatively small sample sizes. In our study, high ML-AAC24 was associated with lower TBS independent of multiple factors. However, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for spine tissue thickness in the multivariable adjusted model. Collectively, the present study along with the existing evidence suggest a weak association between AAC and lower TBS. It is possible that AAC overlying the LS may introduce a bias in the assessment of TBS, although TBS is relatively insensitive to other artifacts, such as spinal osteoarthritis and compression fractures.31,32

A few studies have reported correlations between TBS and some of the parameters of bone microarchitecture such as connectivity density.33–35 Vascular calcification has also been reported to be inversely related to bone microarchitecture parameters assessed with HR-pQCT. Specifically, in 693 men and women, AAC was weakly correlated with radial trabecular number (Spearman’s correlation ρ = −0.17) and trabecular separation (ρ = 0.18) in men over 50 yr.36 However, no correlation was observed in women and younger men, nor with other bone microarchitectural parameters, such as trabecular bone thickness and bone volume-to-total volume ratio (BV/TV).36 It is important to note that this particular study only considered the radius, a peripheral skeletal site; thus the influence of AAC may differ at more central skeletal sites (eg, hip and spine), which may be directly perfused by branching arteries of the abdominal aorta. Supporting this hypothesis, another study of 648 older women found no cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between AAC and pQCT assessed bone structure and estimated strength of the radius and tibia.37 Nevertheless, it is important to recognize that any impairment of skeletal blood and nutrient supply can adversely affect trabecular bone remodeling and healing after injury.38

In this study, we also identified associations between high ML-AAC24 and lower BMD at the TH and FN. These findings are consistent with those of a recent meta-analysis, which demonstrated the presence of any or advanced AAC was associated with lower standardized TH BMD (16 studies, n = 20 277) and FN BMD (10 studies, n = 6981).9 The association between AAC, BMD, and TBS may be attributable to shared risk factors and underlying mechanisms, as previously discussed,9 yet these relationships remain poorly understood and warrant future investigation. In contrast to findings of a previous meta-analysis (20 studies, n = 17 260),9 higher ML-AAC24 was associated with higher LS BMD. Additionally, a few other cross-sectional studies have reported higher LS BMD in individuals with higher AAC.39,40 These results may be related to the uncertain reliability of DXA for LS BMD assessment in older adults with overlying AAC.41–43 While a few studies reported negligible effects,43,44 the abdominal aorta is located anterior to the LS and calcification of this large artery and age-related degenerative changes of the spine may lead to artifactual elevation in BMD using DXA,45 indicating that higher lumbar BMD in individuals with high AAC may not reflect “true” BMD values. Spine osteoarthritis, which can artifactually elevate spine BMD, may also partly explain the observed BMD findings, as it is also related to higher AAC.46,47

Importantly, we demonstrated that both high ML-AAC24 and lower TBS were independently associated with incident fracture risk. In a recent meta-analysis (10 studies, n = 16 129), advanced AAC (assessed by imaging experts) was associated with 51% increased risk for incident fracture.9 We also demonstrated that high ML-AAC24 was associated with 57% increased relative hazard for clinical fracture risk in a cohort of 1024 community-dwelling older women,12 independent of several fracture risk factors, including BMD. In the Manitoba BMD registry, high ML-AAC24 was associated with incident fractures independent of prevalent vertebral fractures and other traditional fracture risk factors, such as BMD,13 with hazard ratios (HR 1.36, 95% CI: 1.14-1.62 for major osteoporotic and HR 1.55, 95% CI: 1.15-2.09 for hip fractures) similar to the present study. Collectively, the present study reinforces existing evidence that AAC is consistently associated with incident fracture,12,13 not only independent of BMD and previous fractures, but also after accounting for TBS and other risk factors. The clinical utility of TBS in providing skeletal health information for fracture assessment, beyond traditional fracture risk factors, has been already established.48,49 The findings of the present study underscore that higher AAC and lower TBS are both important factors to be considered for fracture risk prediction to improve skeletal health. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the “added value” of incorporating AAC imaging results and TBS into fracture risk assessment tools to improve predictive performance.

Our study has limitations including the observational nature of the investigation meaning causality cannot be inferred as the potential influence of residual confounding (eg, unmeasured spine osteoarthritis when examining the association between ML-AAC24 and LS BMD) cannot be avoided. However, we accounted for multiple risk factors to minimize the influence of confounding bias. Over 90% of the study cohort included were older White women undertaking routine osteoporosis screening, in conjunction with further eligibility criteria (described in methods) for VFA. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to healthy low risk individuals, younger adults, older men or other ethnic groups. Nevertheless, the study cohort are likely to be representative of older adults attending osteoporosis screening and VFA as part of routine clinical practice. Moreover, fracture was ascertained through linked health records without review of medical history or radiology reports. However, we have shown that the linked health record identifies incident clinical fractures with good accuracy in the Manitoba Bone Density Registry (positive and negative predictive values 95%).50 This study has also several strengths. First, the study is the largest to date exploring the association between ML-AAC24 and TBS and their independent associations with incident fracture. Second, the DXA testing and VFA images were captured as part of routine clinical practice indicating the potential clinical utility of AAC for fracture risk prediction that are likely reflective of real-world clinical populations. Third, a range of potential fracture risk factors (eg, FRAX components, spine tissue thickness, and medication use) were included as covariate adjustments, and this study is the first study to demonstrate that high level of ML-AAC24 and TBS can provide prognostic information about fracture risk independent of each other, BMD and other established fracture risk factors.

In conclusion, high ML-AAC24 was associated with lower TBS and BMD at the hip and FN in predominantly older women undergoing routine osteoporosis screening. Both high ML-AAC24 and lower TBS predicted incident any fracture, major osteoporotic fracture and hip fracture, independent of each other and multiple established clinical risk factors for fracture. This underscores the potential of these measures to provide complementary information about risk of fracture beyond BMD. However, further studies are needed to better understand on the added value of ML-AAC24 to improve fracture risk prediction and skeletal health, and whether the current armamentarium for fracture prevention are effective in people with high AAC.
