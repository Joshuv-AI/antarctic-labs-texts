Evaluation and Comparison of Antibiotic Prescribing Practices and Treatment Outcomes in Children with Pneumonia Admitted to Public and Private Hospitals in Pakistan: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Ayesha Naseem, Furqan K. Hashmi, Zikria Saleem, Huma Javaid, Zobia Afzal, Nimra Shakeel, Bisma Tariq, Nawaal Akbar, Ayesha Asghar, Rida Zahid, Hamid Saeed, Mohsin Ali, Zia Hasnain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumonia remains a leading cause of mortality among children under five years worldwide, particularly in Pakistan.AimThis study aimed to evaluate antibiotic prescribing practices, diagnostic utilization, and treatment outcomes among hospitalized children with pneumonia in public and private hospitals in Lahore, Pakistan.MethodologyA retrospective observational study was conducted using medical records from a public and private hospital between January and December 2021. Children aged ≤ 59 months with clinically diagnosed pneumonia were included. Pneumonia severity was classified according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, and antibiotic appropriateness was assessed using WHO guidelines in combination with the Gyssens classification system.ResultsAmong the study population, 68.7% had severe pneumonia, 17.4% non-severe pneumonia, and 13.9% very severe pneumonia. Diagnostic support was limited with chest radiographs performed in 84.2% of cases; whereas microbiological testing was low 10.2%. Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most commonly identified pathogen. Antibiotic therapy was predominantly empirical (89.2%), particularly in public hospitals. Ceftriaxone was the most frequently prescribed antibiotic (84.4%), often prescribed regardless of disease severity. Only 2.8% of prescriptions adhered to WHO treatment guidelines, while Gyssens analysis showed that 97.3% of prescriptions were inappropriate, primarily due to incorrect duration and suboptimal antibiotic selection. Treatment outcomes indicated that only 29.2% of patients were successfully discharged, while 70.8% left against medical advice (LAMA). A significant difference in outcomes was observed between public and private hospitals, with a higher discharge rate in private settings and increased LAMA in public hospitals.ConclusionThis high LAMA rate likely reflects socioeconomic constraints, caregiver-related factors, and healthcare system limitations. These findings major gaps in guideline adherence, diagnostic capacity, and rational antibiotic use. Implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs, improved diagnostic facilities, and strict adherence to WHO guidelines are essential to optimize clinical outcomes and reduce antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Pneumonia is a leading cause of mortality in children under five years of age worldwide [1, 2]. An estimated 150–160 million cases of childhood pneumonia occur globally each year, with a substantial proportion necessitating hospitalization, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3, 4]. Pakistan remains among the countries with a high pediatric pneumonia burden, where access to timely diagnosis, appropriate antibiotic therapy, and adherence to guidelines remain limited [5, 6]. Despite sustained national and global efforts to reduce childhood mortality, pneumonia remains a major contributor to preventable deaths in Pakistan, reflecting persistent gaps in healthcare infrastructure and quality of care.

Pneumonia is an acute infection of the lung parenchyma caused by a variety of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, or atypical organisms [6]. The most common pneumonia-causing bacteria are Streptococcus pneumoniae followed by Haemophilus influenzae and Staphylococcus aureus [7], while respiratory viruses such as respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) also play a significant role particularly in younger children [8, 9]. The relative contribution of viral and bacterial pathogen varies depending on age, geographical region, and disease severity, with bacterial infections more frequently associated with severe clinical outcomes and hospitalization [10].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), childhood pneumonia based on clinical severity into non-severe, severe and very severe categories using a standard clinical criteria [11, 12]. WHO case management guidelines, recommend oral amoxicillin for non-severe pneumonia and parenteral ampicillin plus gentamicin for severe cases, emphasizing early empirical therapy followed by targeted therapy when microbiological data are available [11, 13]. In many LMICs including Pakistan, clinical practice is largely guided by WHO-based recommendations, however, implementation remains inconsistent due to resource limitations and variability in clinical practice [14].

Despite the availability of effective antimicrobial agents, the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a significant global health threat. Inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, particularly the excessive use of broad-spectrum agents and deviation from standard treatment guidelines, is a major cause of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [15, 16]. Resistance among clinically significant pathogens, particularly Streptococcus pneumoniae and Gram negative bacteria, is increasing in LMICs, causing complication in treatment outcomes and increasing healthcare burden [17]. In pediatric population, the misuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics have been widely reported and is associated with increased risk of multidrug-resistant infections, treatment failures and prolonged hospitalization [18, 19]. Several studies conducted in LMIC settings including Pakistan, have demonstrated substantial deviation from WHO treatment guidelines [20, 21].

Given these concerns, evaluating antibiotic prescribing practices in relation to established clinical guidelines is essential to optimize treatment outcomes and limit the spread of AMR. However, comparative evidence assessing prescribing patterns across public and private healthcare settings in Pakistan remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate antibiotic prescribing practices and their appropriateness among children aged ≤ 5 years hospitalized with pneumonia in public and private sector hospitals in Lahore, Pakistan, using WHO treatment guidelines and the Gyssens classification system as a structured framework for assessment.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective observational study of patients diagnosed with pneumonia from one public and one private tertiary care hospital. The study evaluated routinely collected clinical data without any direct patient interaction, reflecting real-world prescribing practices. The study period (January to December 2021) covered four seasonal patterns of pneumonia incidence. A consecutive sampling approach (complete enumeration) was used, whereby all eligible pediatric pneumonia cases meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included.

Study Population

A total of 420 pediatric patients with pneumonia were initially identified. After excluding incomplete records, 380 patients were included in the final analysis. The study population comprised children aged ≤ 59 months admitted with a primary diagnosis of pneumonia. Only patient received at least one antibiotic prescription were included in the antibiotic appropriateness analysis.

Inclusion Criteria

Children aged ≤ 59 months admitted with clinically diagnosed pneumonia between January and December 2021.

Exclusion Criteria

Children aged ≥ 5 years and those with comorbidities excluded from WHO pneumonia algorithms (e.g., meningitis, HIV, severe malnutrition, chronic cardiorespiratory disease) were excluded. Records with incomplete data were also excluded. Missing data were not imputed.

Diagnostic Criteria and Severity Classification

Pneumonia diagnosis was based on World Health Organization (WHO) 2014 clinical criteria for children aged ≤ 5 years. Diagnosis included cough and/or fever, age-specific tachypnea: ≥50 breaths/min (2–12 months) and ≥ 40 breaths/min (12–59 months). Additional clinical signs included chest indrawing and general severe signs such as inability to feed, convulsions, lethargy or unconsciousness [11]. Disease severity was classified to WHO criteria: Non-severe pneumonia: fast breathing only. Severe pneumonia: presence of chest indrawing. Very severe pneumonia: presence of general danger signs [22]. Chest radiographs and laboratory tests (complete blood count and sputum culture) were used as supportive diagnostic tools where available. However, diagnosis and classification remained primarily clinical.

Assessment of Antibiotic Appropriateness (WHO Guidelines and Gyssens Classification)

Antibiotic appropriateness was evaluated using WHO 2014 pneumonia treatment guidelines as the reference standard, in combination with the Gyssens classification system [11, 23]. Two independent clinical pharmacists evaluated the medical records. Each prescriptions were evaluated for indication (based on WHO severity classification), antibiotic selection (recommended 1st line therapy), dose and dosing interval, route of administration and duration of therapy. A prescription was considered adherent only if all parameters were consistent with WHO recommendations.

In parallel, the Gyssens classification system was applied as a structure hierarchical method to evaluate prescribing appropriateness. Each prescription was assigned to a single mutually exclusive category:

Category 0 (appropriate therapy); Categories I-V: inappropriate therapy due to issues related to timing, dose/route, duration, antibiotic selection, or indication; Category VI: insufficient data [23].

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 [24]. Descriptive analysis of the data was expressed as percentages and frequencies. Association between categorical variables was assessed using the Chi-square test. Statistically significant was set at p < 0.05. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with outcome variables of the patients.

Ethics Approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Punjab University College of Pharmacy (Approval No: D/31/FOP ; Date: 08-04-2026) and confirmed its scientific merit, ethical compliance with applicable guidelines Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Patient consent was waived by the Institution Review Board due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of anonymized data. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/).

Results

A total of 380 pediatric patients diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia met the inclusion criteria after exclusion of incomplete records. Of these, 276 (72.6%) were admitted to the public sector hospital and 104 (27.4%) to private sector hospital. Male children predominated (245, 64.5%) compared to females (135, 35.5%). The majority of patients were aged 2–11 months (245, 64.5%), followed by 12–59 months (73, 19.2%), and < 2 months (62, 16.3%). A marked seasonal variation was observed, with the highest number of cases during winter (243, 63.9%), followed by autumn (72, 18.9%), summer (38, 10.0%) and spring (27, 7.1%) as shown in Table 1. Half of the patients (190, 50.0%) had a documented vaccination history. A large proportion (271, 71.3%) required prolonged hospitalization (5–14 days), while 109 (28.7%) had shorter hospital stay (1–4 days).

The most common clinical features were cough (97.6%), fever (96.3%), and tachypnea (93.7%). Chest indrawing was observed in 76.3% of cases, while inability to feed (23.4%) and convulsions (2.6%) were less frequent but indicative of severe disease.

Complete blood count (CBC) was performed in 95.3% of patients, and chest radiographs in 84.2%. However, sputum cultures were performed in only 10.3% of cases. Among culture-positive cases (n = 39), Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most commonly isolated pathogen (41.0%), followed by Haemophilus influenzae (23.1%), Escherichia coli (20.5%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (10.3%), and Streptococcus aureus (5.1%).

According to WHO classification, severe pneumonia accounted for the majority of cases (68.7%), followed by non-severe pneumonia (17.4%) and very severe pneumonia (13.9%). Only 111 (29.2%) patients were successfully discharged, whereas a substantial proportion of patients (269, 70.8%) left against medical advice (LAMA).

Antibiotic prescribing pattern and WHO Guideline Adherence (n = 360)

Out of 380 patients, 360 (94.7%) received antibiotics. Among these, 75.0% received single antibiotic therapy, 19.7% received multiple antibiotics and 5.3% received no antibiotics. The most commonly prescribed antibiotic was ceftriaxone (84.4%), followed by azithromycin (4.2%), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (2.8%). Most prescriptions were empirical (89.2%), while only 10.8% were culture guided. Only 10 prescriptions (2.8%) adhered to WHO guidelines, while 350 (97.2%) were non-compliant (Table 4). Only 2.7% of prescriptions were categorized as appropriate (Category 0), indicating adherence to WHO pneumonia treatment guidelines as shown in Table 5. A high prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic use was observed. The most frequent deviation was related to incorrect duration of therapy (Category III), observed in 120 (33.3%) prescriptions, mainly due to treatment course exceeding recommended lengths followed by inappropriate antibiotic selection (Category IV) in 110 (30.6%) cases and dose related errors (Category II) occurred in 60 (16.7%) prescriptions.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of antibiotic prescribing practices in pediatric pneumonia and identifies major gaps in guideline adherence, diagnostic utilization and rational antibiotic use. Overall, only 2.8% of prescriptions were compliant with WHO guidelines (Table 4), while 89.2% of cases were managed empirically, indicating a strong reliance on non–evidence-based prescribing. Ceftriaxone was the most frequently used antibiotic (84.4%) prescribed widely irrespective of disease severity. According to WHO recommendations oral amoxicillin is preferred for non-severe pneumonia, while severe cases should be treated with parenteral ampicillin plus gentamicin [11]. The observed deviation toward third-generation cephalosporins suggests inappropriate antibiotic selection, a known driver of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Similar patterns of poor adherence have been reported in other low-middle income countries [16, 25, 26], where a large proportion of pediatric pneumonia cases were managed with non-recommended antibiotics. Inappropriate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics has also been associated with poor clinical outcomes and increased resistance burden [27].

This high reliance on empirical therapy (89.2%) is closely linked to limited diagnostic support, with only 10.3% of cases undergoing microbiological testing. This restricts the ability to initiate targeted therapy and contributes to continued reliance on broad-spectrum agents. Although private hospitals demonstrated relatively higher diagnostic utilization, inappropriate prescribing persisted in both settings. This suggests that diagnostic availability alone is insufficient to improve prescribing without stewardship implementation. Barriers to microbiological testing in LMICs include cost constraints, limited laboratory capacity, and delayed reporting [28, 29].

Furthermore, analysis using the Gyssens classification revealed that inappropriate prescribing was primarily driven by incorrect antibiotic selection and inappropriate duration, followed by dose and timing errors. These findings are consistent with global evidence identifying duration and selection errors as major contributors to irrational antibiotic use [23, 30]. Inappropriate treatment duration may adversely affect outcomes and accelerate resistance development. Evidence from recent antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) show that structured interventions, such as guideline implementation, audit and feedback and clinical education can significantly improve prescribing practices and patient outcomes [30–32]. Similarly, stewardship initiatives targeting community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) have improved guideline adherence [33].

Significant differences were observed between healthcare settings. Most patients (72.6%) were managed in public hospital reflecting greater accessibility and affordability. However, public hospital had higher rate of patients leaving against medical advice (LAMA) (81.2%) as compared to private hospitals (43.3%). Multivariable analysis identified hospital type as a significant predictor of treatment outcomes, with patients in public hospital more likely to leave earlier. Higher LAMA rate limits treatment completion and outcome assessment. The higher proportion of LAMA cases is consistent with other findings from other LMICs [34, 35] and reflects underlying systemic and socioeconomic challenges in healthcare settings. Financial burden, overcrowding, and limited patient–provider communication are likely contributing factors. In contrast, better discharge rates in private hospital likely reflects improved resource availability and patient satisfaction.

Gender was also found to be a significant predictor, with female patients more likely to leave against medical advice. Additionally, infants aged 2–11 months constituted the majority of pneumonia cases (Table 1) indicated high vulnerability, consistent with previous studies [14]. Disease severity was not significantly associated with outcomes, indicating that non-clinical factors play a greater role on patient management decisions.

Seasonal variation was observed, with higher incidence during winter months (63.9%), consistent with known epidemiological trends. Clinically, cough, fever, and tachypnea were the most common presentations, as consistent with WHO diagnostic criteria. However, low microbiological testing limits confirmation of etiological agents. Previous studies indicate that Streptococcus pneumoniae, respiratory viruses and Haemophilus influenzae are common causes [7], but limited diagnostic data restrict etiological interpretation in this study.

These findings are consistent with studies from Bangladesh and Vietnam, where high rates of antibiotic prescribing in pediatric pneumonia have been reported [36, 37]. In contrast, high-income countries demonstrate improved guideline adherence and reduced broad-spectrum antibiotic use, largely due to established antimicrobial stewardship programs [38, 39]. This contrast highlights the importance of strengthening stewardship systems in LMICs.

At a broader level,, inappropriate antibiotic use contributes significantly to the global burden of antimicrobial resistance. If AMR is not controlled, estimates suggest that it could reduce global GDP by 1.1% to 3.8% annually by 2050 [40]. Overall, the findings strongly support the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship interventions in hospital settings. Strategies including guideline adherence, prescription audits, clinical training and improved diagnostic utilization are essential to promote rational antibiotic use. However, implementation in LMICs remains inconsistent and insufficiently enforced [41]. Therefore, strengthening national and institutional stewardship frameworks is critical to mitigate antimicrobial resistance in Pakistan.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The retrospective design limits assessment of real-time clinical decision-making. Inclusion of only two hospitals restricts generalizability. The high proportion of LAMA cases limited outcome assessment due to lack of follow-up data. Additionally, low microbiological testing constrained evaluation of antibiotic appropriateness based on etiological evidence. Potential confounders such as socioeconomic status and geographic variability could not be assessed, which may have influenced the findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights poor adherence to WHO guidelines, high empirical therapy, and extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly ceftriaxone. Most prescriptions were inappropriate due to incorrect duration and selection. A significant difference in outcomes was observed between public and private sector hospitals having increased LAMA cases in public hospitals. Therefore, strengthening antimicrobial stewardship programs, improving diagnostic capacity, and enforcing national/international treatment guidelines are essential to optimize antibiotic use and combat antimicrobial resistance.
