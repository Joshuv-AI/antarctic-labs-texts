Chronic widespread pain and the risk of cardiovascular disease-a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ann-Sofie Rönnegård, Tessa Schillemans, Björn Äng, Katja Boersma, Johan Ärnlöv, Elena Tseli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

1. Introduction

Recent research suggests a close link between 2 of the world's major health conditions: pain and cardiovascular disease. Pain 1 year after a myocardial infarction seems to be a stronger risk factor for mortality than smoking,7 and previous research has suggested an association between chronic pain, defined as pain >3 months, and cardiovascular disease.18 Among individuals with chronic pain, those with chronic widespread pain (CWP) seem to be at the highest risk of cardiovascular disease.32

Chronic pain is recently acknowledged with a specific diagnostic code and definition in ICD-11.38 Spreading of pain may increase over time, hence spreading is associated with pain duration and is also an indicator of pain burden.20 Chronic widespread pain has a prevalence of 10% in the general population28 with large societal implications. It is the core symptom in fibromyalgia, as defined by the 1990 American College of Rheumatology (ACR90) criteria,54 and also occurs in other pain conditions categorised in ICD-11.38 The definition of widespreadness has evolved in response to the need for greater diagnostic precision, progressing from the ACR90 criteria to the WP 2019 definition,50 which offers a more detailed characterisation of pain distribution and extent.

Cardiovascular disease such as stroke and ischemic heart disease are the leading causes of global disability,47 and cardiovascular mortality is among top 3 causes of death because of noncommunicable diseases.19

Potential causal pathways underlying the association between CWP and cardiovascular disease are not yet clarified. Although causality has not been proven, long-term stress, physical disability, obesity, depression, sleep disturbances, and low socioeconomic status are all associated with chronic pain9,23 and could contribute to a more rapid progression of atherosclerosis and increased risk for overt cardiovascular disease.18 Another hypothesis is that certain common medications in the treatment of chronic pain such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) might increase the risk of future cardiovascular events.5

Given the high global prevalence and disease burden of CWP and cardiovascular disease, an association between them may have substantial public health implications. The current international guidelines on cardiovascular prevention46 do not include CWP as a disease state with high cardiovascular risk nor do pain guidelines emphasise cardiovascular disease prevention.27 Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analyses on current state-of-the-art of the association between CWP and cardiovascular risk could form a basis for future revisions of both cardiovascular prevention guidelines and pain guidelines.

To our knowledge, there are no systematic reviews on the association of CWP and nonfatal incident atherosclerotic diseases and heart failure. The previous reviews3,25,35,39 on CWP and cardiovascular mortality have reported nonsignificant associations. However, these reviews do not cover recent research. Therefore, this study aimed to summarise the current evidence on the association between CWP and atherosclerotic diseases, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality and perform a meta-analysis to clarify if CWP is a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases.

2. Methods

This systematic review follows the PRISMA guidelines30 and a protocol was registered a priori in Prospero (CRD 42024481408).

2.1. Eligibility criteria

Longitudinal, peer-reviewed studies on adults with CWP/fibromyalgia and a comparison group without CWP/fibromyalgia were included if they investigated any of the following outcomes: incidence of atherosclerotic diseases (myocardial infarction and stroke), heart failure, cardiovascular mortality, or any combination of these. Chronic pain was defined as pain >3 months.38 Widespread pain was defined according to (1) ACR90 definition of fibromyalgia54 (axial and upper and lower segments and left- and right-sided pain), (2) the ACR 2010 and 2016 fibromyalgia definitions51,52, or (3) the widespread pain (WP) definition from 201950 (pain in 4 of 5 main pain regions (axial plus the upper and lower quadrants) and pain in at least 7 of 15 subsites). We also accepted studies with slightly less specific definitions of chronicity and widespreadness to capture as much evidence as possible. Excluded were studies with a cross-sectional design, case studies, languages other than English, secondary sources (ie, reviews), studies with pain exposure caused by cancer, inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, and studies with only haemorrhagic stroke as outcome.

2.2. Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed with a research librarian to identify studies examining the relationship between CWP and the risk of incident cardiovascular disease and cardiovascular mortality. For details on the search, see Appendix A (supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473). The search strategy was the result of iterative searches with combinations of free text and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) describing CWP (eg, fibromyalgia, chronic widespread pain, chronic multisite pain) and cardiovascular outcomes (such as myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality). On January 9, 2024, we performed searches, covering from inception date, of Cochrane, EMBASE, MEDLINE (via Ovid) and Web of Science, and stored the search results in Endnote. An update search was performed on November 16, 2024. We also performed forward and backward citation searches on the included studies.

2.3. Study selection

In the selection process, the Covidence systematic review software (Covidence systematic review software; Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia, available at www.covidence.org) was used. All studies were screened independently by 2 reviewers (E.T., A.S.R.), with competence covering both chronic pain (E.T.) and cardiovascular disease (A.S.R.). Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, irrelevant studies were sorted out by reading the title and abstract, and full-text papers were retrieved for the remaining studies for further screening, also performed independently by E.T. and A.S.R. Disagreements were solved through discussions and consensus involving 2 senior researchers with expertise in chronic pain (K.B.) and cardiovascular diseases and prevention (J.Ä.). A record of the number of included and excluded studies (see list in Appendix A, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473) was kept at each stage of the process and was presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.31

2.4. Data extraction

One author (A.S.R.) extracted the data from each included study, and a second author (E.T.) independently validated the data. Disagreements were resolved by the senior researchers as described above.

The following data were extracted and presented in a table: study (first author, year, reference); population description (name of register/database, country, type of population targeted); sample (number included and number CWP/fibromyalgia); follow-up time (years, mean) and period; age (mean); percentage of females included; pain phenotype; cardiovascular outcome, number of events, and measures; confounders accounted for (list); association unadjusted or adjusted for nonmodifiable confounders only (such as age and sex) (minimally adjusted model); association adjusted for remaining confounders (maximally adjusted model).

2.5. Quality assessment

The Quality In Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies. The assessment of risk of bias was performed independently by 2 researchers (E.T., A.S.R.). Disagreements were resolved through discussions involving 2 senior researchers (K.B., J.Ä.). In QUIPS, there are 6 domains: study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor measurement (CWP in this review), outcome measurement, study confounding, and statistical analysis and reporting. We ranked the studies within each domain, then discussed reasonable cut-offs for high/moderate/low risk of bias within each domain, and finally, the total score until consensus was reached. The risk of bias assessment was visualised using the Robvis tool (see Appendix B, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473) showing domain-level judgements for each study and weighted bar plots of the distribution of risk of bias judgements within each QUIPS domain.

2.6. Data synthesis and analysis

We performed a narrative synthesis and a meta-analysis. The outcomes were sorted under 3 categories and presented as atherosclerotic diseases (myocardial infarction and stroke), heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality. The rationale for this categorisation is that the pathophysiology of heart failure may differ from atherosclerotic diseases. Cardiovascular mortality, on the other hand, is an indistinct outcome because it includes a broad spectrum of cardiovascular diseases not only caused by atherosclerosis, such as heart failure, arrhythmias, valve disease, and cardiomyopathies. Moreover, the true cause of death may be difficult to firmly assess, particularly as autopsies are less commonly used to confirm the cause of death. For each study, we presented minimally and maximally adjusted models when available. The minimally adjusted models were used to investigate CWP as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease, and the maximally adjusted models to investigate whether any observed association could be explained by additional adjustment for established cardiovascular risk factors.

2.7. Meta-analysis

Study pooling: Study estimates and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were extracted from each of the selected studies. We selected estimates from both a minimally adjusted model and a maximally adjusted model to gain insights in the importance of potential confounders and mediators. Only the minimally adjusted model was used for sensitivity analyses to investigate heterogeneity and outliers because the maximally adjusted model had more incongruity between studies because of different selection of confounders in the included studies. If several studies used overlapping study populations, we only included the study with the highest quality in the meta-analysis (presented in Appendix C, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473). All study estimates (hazard ratios, HR; odds ratios, OR; risk ratios, RR; mortality rate ratios, MRR; mortality risk ratios, MRiR; standardised mortality ratios, SMR) were pooled in the meta-analysis under the following assumptions: (1) HR, MRR, and OR approximate RR and MRiR if the outcome is rare and effect sizes are small; (2) the general population, which is used as a reference category to calculate SMR, is similar to the control populations that were used to calculate the other study estimates; (3) allowing heterogeneity in the meta-analysis and carefully investigating heterogeneity and influential studies; and (4) avoid unnecessary study exclusions or stratifications by study estimates as only few studies were available and exclusions could also introduce biased estimates.

We performed a random-effects meta-analysis on the log-transformed extracted estimates and 95% CIs, which accounts for both within-study and between-study variations and provides a more conservative estimate of the pooled effect size. The inverse variance method8 was used to pool data, weighting studies by their precision. The confidence intervals for the random effects estimate were calculated using a quantile of the standard normal distribution.14 The restricted maximum-likelihood estimator was used to estimate between-study tau and tau-squared.43 Confidence intervals for tau and tau-squared were calculated using Q-profile method.45 Results were visualised in forest plots.

Heterogeneity was investigated using the I-squared statistic (<25 low, 25-75 moderate, >75 high heterogeneity)22 and Cochran's Q test (P < 0.05 indicates significant heterogeneity).12 In addition, we included a prediction interval for the overall effect size estimate, which provides a comprehensive range of plausible effects expected in future studies. The prediction interval was calculated based on t-distribution with k-1 (k corresponds to n studies) degrees of freedom.42 In the sensitivity analyses, we investigated potential sources of heterogeneity and influential studies by performing outlier analyses and inspecting Baujat and leave-one-out plots.17 We explored potential publication bias by inspecting funnel plots and performing tests of funnel plot asymmetry (Egger tests; P < 0.05 indicates the presence of publication bias or small-study effects).17 We used trim-and-fill to simulate potential missing studies because of publication bias16 and limit meta-analysis to adjust for small study effects.33 It is of note that for the cardiovascular disease meta-analysis, there were too few studies to have a reliable Egger test result, and we therefore only use it as an additional guidance. Based on QUIPS assessments, we additionally presented meta-analysis results after removal of studies with high risk of bias. We used R version 4.4.2 and R packages “meta,”6 “metasens,”34 “metafor,”44 and “dmetar.”21 The R-code is available in Appendix D (supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C474).

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) was used to assess the overall certainty of evidence (see Appendix E, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473).

3. Results

3.1. Screening process

In our literature search, we found 4483 studies after removing duplicates. In the title and abstract screening, 4435 studies were excluded, and full-text screening was performed on the remaining 48 studies. After excluding 30 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria (see list of reasons for exclusion in Appendix A, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473), 18 were included in the review (Fig. 1). Of these 18 studies, 7 reported on atherosclerotic diseases, 2 on heart failure and 12 on cardiovascular mortality (some studies reported several outcomes).

There were 3 main types of studies with slightly different characteristics: population-based register studies,1,2,4,11,13,24–26,29,32,37,48 patient-based studies,15,49,53 and studies based on insurance registers.36,40,41 The definition of CWP was based on surveys in most population- and patient-based studies except 1 study that relied on clinical examination.15 The studies on insurance registers defined fibromyalgia from the presence of ICD 9 diagnoses (729.0 [unspecified rheumatism and fibrositis] or 729.1 [unspecified myalgia and myositis]).36,40,41

The proportion of women varied between studies. The population-based studies reported around 60% of females in the CWP group,4,25,29,32,37 and a Norwegian study2 slightly higher, 69%. The patient-based studies15,49,53 had around 95% female participants with CWP. Follow-up time of the studies varied between 4 and 25 years.

Two studies26,29 did not fully meet the pain duration criteria for chronic pain, but after an overall judgement by the reviewer team, these studies were still deemed as appropriate for CWP because the prevalence of widespread pain in these studies equalled the anticipated prevalence of CWP, and that inclusion of studies with a lower precision of the pain duration criteria would likely not inflate the risk estimates and the risk of reporting false-positive associations.

The statistical analysis models varied, from crude models stratified on age to models including established cardiovascular risk factors, socioeconomic factors, medication and lifestyle factors. The 3 studies that reported sex-specific estimates on CVD mortality2,4,15 and 1 study on a composite outcome (myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality)32 showed considerable heterogeneity, limiting firm conclusions. Two studies had estimates with asymmetrical confidence intervals: the odds ratio for the association between fibromyalgia/CWP and mortality because of diseases of the heart49 and the hazard ratio for the association between CWP and stroke in the maximally adjusted model.48 Because we did not receive a sufficient explanation for this discrepancy after contacting the authors, we excluded these specific risk estimates from the meta-analyses.

3.3. Quality assessment

A summary of the QUIPS quality assessment is provided in Appendix B (supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473). Of the 18 studies included, 14 were assessed as having a low-to-moderate risk of bias, whereas 4 were rated as having a high risk of bias. Among the 5 studies included in the meta-analysis examining atherosclerotic disease outcomes, 4 were assessed as having a low-to-moderate risk of bias, and 1 as having a high risk. In the meta-analysis of cardiovascular mortality outcomes, 8 studies were assessed as having a low to moderate risk of bias, and 2 were rated as high risk. Two patient-based studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias because of small sample sizes and a limited follow-up time of only 3.9 years,15 and unclearness in exposure/outcome measurements in different subgroups and low reliability of outcome measurement.53 A third study26 attained a high risk of bias because few confounders were included in the multivariable model, no crude data were presented, and the presentation was sparse. Finally, a fourth study41 was considered as high risk of bias because of an inexact definition of fibromyalgia and limitations in the multivariable modelling. Altogether, most included studies had a moderate risk of bias.

3.4.2. Chronic widespread pain and heart failure

Only 2 studies11,32 with data from the same population-based register explored the association between CWP and heart failure and reported a nearly doubled risk after adjusting for established risk factors (Table 1).

3.4.3. Chronic widespread pain and cardiovascular mortality

Twelve studies reported the association between CWP and cardiovascular mortality (Table 1 and Fig. 3A). The patient-based studies15,49,53 found no significant associations between CWP and cardiovascular mortality in the minimally adjusted models. These studies did not report any adjusted models. Most population-based studies reported a significant association between CWP and cardiovascular mortality in the minimally adjusted models,1,2,13,25,29,32,37 but in the maximally adjusted models, these associations were no longer significant. The studies examining individuals exposed to the most widespread pain, WP 201913 and “pain all over the body”,25,32 displayed significant associations in the maximally adjusted models.

3.5. Meta-analysis

3.5.1. Chronic widespread pain and atherosclerotic diseases

The 10 study estimates selected for meta-analysis on the association between CWP and atherosclerotic diseases are described in Figure 2A. After excluding studies using overlapping study populations (see Appendix C for details, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473), 6 study estimates remained in the final meta-analysis. The overall RR for the minimally adjusted model was 1.94 (95% CI: 1.56-2.41), which indicates a significant positive association between CWP and atherosclerotic diseases (Fig. 2B). However, the between-study heterogeneity variance was high (I-squared = 91%; Cochran's Q P-value < 0.05). The prediction interval ranged between 1.02 and 3.71, indicating that significant associations would be expected to be found in future studies. For the maximally adjusted model, study estimates (RR = 1.53 [95% CI: 1.27-1.86]) and heterogeneity (I-squared = 76%) were considerably lower but remained significant (Fig. 2C, Table 2). Subgroup analyses were conducted separately for the outcomes of myocardial infarction and stroke (see Appendix F, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473). In these analyses, risk estimate for myocardial infarction appeared higher compared with the risk estimate for stroke, but the limited number of studies and partially overlapping confidence intervals precludes firm conclusions. Separate analyses by exposure definition indicate slightly higher estimates when CWP was defined using questionnaire-based criteria compared with definitions based on ICD-9 codes, RR 2.17 (95% CI: 1.73-2.71) (n studies = 4) vs 1.67 (95% CI: 1.14-2.41) (n studies = 2) in the minimally adjusted models. In the maximally adjusted models, the corresponding estimates were RR 1.64 (95% CI: 1.38-1.95) (n studies = 2) and RR 1.53 (95% CI: 1.03-2.29) (n studies = 2), respectively.

The 14 study estimates selected for CWP and cardiovascular mortality are described in Figure 3A. After excluding studies using overlapping study populations (see Appendix C for details, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473), 11 study estimates remained in the final meta-analysis. The overall RR for the minimally adjusted model was 1.61 (95% CI: 1.24-2.08), which indicates a significant positive association between CWP and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 3B). Heterogeneity was high (I-squared = 91%; Cochran's Q P-value < 0.05) and the prediction interval was large, ranging between 0.64 and 4.02. The maximally adjusted model had lower, nonsignificant estimates (RR = 1.12 [95% CI: 0.93-1.34]) and heterogeneity (I-squared = 78%) (Fig. 3C, Table 2). When excluding the 2 studies that did not meet the ≥3-month chronicity criterion,26,29 the estimates stayed consistent both in the minimally and maximally adjusted models (RR 1.62 [95% CI: 1.27-2.05] and 1.16 [95% CI: 0.93-1.43] respectively). The excluded studies did not materially alter the pooled estimates, supporting the stability of the findings. One study with the outcome cardiovascular mortality used clinical examination to define CWP15 (Dreyer); all the other studies had a questionnaire-based definition of CWP. Analysis excluding Dreyer slightly attenuated the RR from 1.61 (95% CI: 1.24-2.08) to 1.54 (95% CI: 1.19-2.00) in the minimally adjusted model. In the maximally adjusted model for cardiovascular mortality all studies used questionnaires for exposure definition.

3.5.3. Sensitivity analyses: outliers and publication bias

The minimally adjusted model was used for all sensitivity analyses. The Baujat plot indicated Tseng41 and Ronnegard32 as the most influential to effect size and heterogeneity for atherosclerotic diseases and McBeth,29 MacFarlane,26 Ronnegard,32 and Asberg4 for cardiovascular mortality (see Appendix G, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473, Fig. 1). The leave-one-out analysis highlighted the same studies as the largest contributors to effect estimates and heterogeneity (see Appendix G, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473, Fig. 2). Outlier removal (studies whose 95% CI do not overlap with the 95% CI of the pooled effect): Tseng41 for atherosclerotic diseases and Macfarlane,26 Ronnegard,32 and Asberg4 for cardiovascular mortality increased overall study estimates and reduced the heterogeneity for both atherosclerotic diseases and cardiovascular mortality outcomes (Table 2).

However, the trim-and-fill and small study adjustment models both affected the overall results, particularly for cardiovascular mortality (Table 2). The trim-and-fill models lowered the RR from 1.94 to 1.51 for atherosclerotic diseases (P < 0.05, Table 2), whereas cardiovascular mortality was reduced from 1.61 to 1.15 (P = 0.425). The adjustment for small study effects remained significant for atherosclerotic disease models and borderline for cardiovascular mortality models (P < 0.05, Table 2), but the atherosclerotic disease results were not weakened much (G-squared = 13%, P < 0.05), whereas those of cardiovascular mortality were (G-squared = 100%, P = 0.051). In addition, the funnel plot for atherosclerotic diseases did not show strong indications for asymmetry (Fig. 4A), whereas the funnel plot for cardiovascular mortality indicated asymmetry (Egger test P-value < 0.05, Fig. 4B). Results remained significant upon removal of studies with high risk of bias for both outcomes (Table 2).

3.5.4. Grading of recommendations assessment, development, and evaluation

The GRADE assessment for certainty of evidence for the outcomes of atherosclerotic diseases was rated low and that for cardiovascular mortality was very low (see Appendix E, summary of findings-table, supplemental digital content, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C473).

4. Discussion

4.1. Principal findings

We identified 18 longitudinal studies reporting on the association between CWP and incident cardiovascular diseases, of which 7 included outcome data on atherosclerotic diseases, 2 on heart failure, and 12 on cardiovascular mortality. Individuals with CWP had an almost doubled risk of atherosclerotic disease and a 1.6-fold risk for cardiovascular mortality in the minimally adjusted models. In the maximally adjusted models, associations with atherosclerotic disease were somewhat attenuated albeit still significant, but for cardiovascular mortality, the estimates became nonsignificant. Sensitivity analyses suggest that the association between CWP and cardiovascular mortality was stronger when outlier studies or studies with higher levels of bias were removed from the analyses. There were indications of publication bias for the mortality studies and limited study sample size for atherosclerotic diseases.

Taken together, this systematic review and meta-analysis provide support for a clinically relevant increased cardiovascular risk in patients with CWP for incident atherosclerotic diseases, and that this risk is largely independent of age, socio-economy, and established cardiovascular risk factors. The association between CWP and cardiovascular mortality was less pronounced.

4.2. Comparison of previous reviews

There is, to our knowledge, no meta-analysis on the association between CWP and nonfatal atherosclerotic diseases. A previous meta-analysis has presented data on the association between chronic (but not widespread) pain and nonfatal atherosclerotic diseases,18 reporting significant associations between chronic pain and nonfatal cardiac and cerebrovascular diseases in minimally adjusted models (pooled OR 1.73 [95% CI: 1.42-2.04] and 1.81 [95% CI: 1.51-2.10], respectively). These estimates are slightly lower compared with the present study, maybe because studies on widespread pain were not included in that meta-analysis. In addition, cross-sectional studies were included, a study design that may introduce bias because of reverse causation.

The association between CWP and cardiovascular mortality has been examined in 3 systematic reviews published between 2013 and 2017,3,25,35 collectively encompassing 5 studies (of which 1 did not report on CWP) and in a more recent review from 202339 with 3 included studies (2 of which have an overlap in population). None of these reviews reported any significant associations between CWP and cardiovascular mortality, although 1 meta-analysis on crude models35 found a borderline but still nonsignificant association, MRR 1.63 (95% CI: 0.98-2.70). Our results, based on 10 studies—4 of which are not included in any previous meta-analyses—represent the most comprehensive analysis to date and demonstrate that individuals with CWP have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular mortality in the minimally adjusted model. Previous reviews may have included studies using less stringent definitions of CWP, potentially leading to a dilution of the observed associations. The reviews that performed meta-analyses on adjusted models3,25 report, like this review, no analysis of separate confounders because the included studies have heterogeneous models for adjustment, combining established cardiovascular risk factors, psychiatric and lifestyle factors, and socio-economic measurements in different patterns.

4.3. Strengths and limitations

A strength of the study is the comprehensive and systematic review process, adhering to contemporary state-of-the-art review methodology and quality criteria.

An overall limitation of the evidence is the relatively small number of studies published in this area, especially studies on the outcomes of atherosclerotic diseases and heart failure. In an update search on October 27, 2025 (Embase and MEDLINE via OVID), we found only 2 new original studies10,55 both based on data from a population already well represented in this review and meta-analysis (UK Biobank), confirming our conclusions but not providing new evidence. Separate meta-analyses by sex and gender were not conducted because of insufficient data availability. An important strength of our review is that the broad spectrum of studies included provides a relevant overview of the research field.

Given the complex bidirectional interplay between CWP and many of the variables included in the multivariable modelling, it is not so straightforward whether these factors should be considered as confounders or mediators. The proposed directed acyclic graph (DAG) in Figure 5, together with the table in Appendix H summarising the variables and their potential roles in the association between CWP and the development of cardiovascular disease, illustrates these considerations. Based on the GRADE assessment (where experimental designs are prerequisites for attaining high level and observational studies low level of evidence), the certainty of the evidence on the outcome of atherosclerotic diseases was low, and cardiovascular mortality was very low. We are aware that the causal inference based on observational studies must be interpreted with caution. Still, we would like to point out that associations were robust even after excluding studies with a higher risk of bias, which suggest that our approach did not provide inflated risk estimates or an increased risk of reporting false-positive findings.

The longitudinal design of the studies diminishes the risk of reversed causation, but because prevalent cardiovascular events at baseline are not excluded in all studies, some level of reversed causation might still be present. The differences between those who take part in a cohort study and nonresponders, often referred to as the healthy volunteer effect, might affect the generalizability to the general population.

4.4. Clinical implications

Today CWP is not specified as a cardiovascular risk factor in clinical cardiovascular prevention guidelines46 nor is assessment of cardiovascular risk recommended in pain guidelines.27 This review indicates that individuals with CWP might have an almost doubled risk for atherosclerotic diseases such as myocardial infarction and stroke, compared with individuals without CWP; however, the certainty of this evidence was rated as low according to GRADE. CWP was associated with cardiovascular mortality only in the minimally adjusted models, with very low certainty of evidence (GRADE).

Interestingly, mental disorders—despite uncertainties around underlying causal mechanisms—are acknowledged in the recent European Society of Cardiology (ESC) prevention guidelines46 as high-risk states for cardiovascular disease. The guidelines recommend increased cardiovascular risk screening for affected individuals on account of this elevated risk profile alone. Based on our results, it is reasonable to believe this approach would also be relevant to individuals with CWP.

5. Conclusion

Our meta-analyses on longitudinal studies suggest that chronic widespread pain is a robust risk factor for atherosclerotic diseases and also may be a risk factor for cardiovascular mortality. Thus, individuals with chronic widespread pain may represent an until now underrecognized high-risk group that could benefit from more proactive risk assessment and preventive strategies. We hope our results will help guide future research to determine whether individuals with CWP should be considered in the development of cardiovascular prevention guidelines. There is also a need for studies that provide sex-specific estimates and investigations designed to clarify causal relationships.
