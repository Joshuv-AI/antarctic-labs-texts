Comparative efficacy of intraoperative wound irrigation with aqueous chlorhexidine versus normal saline to prevent surgical site infections: a double-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Metasist Kultanaruangnonth, Lalita Chatrung, Amarit Tansawet, Pakkapol Sukhvibul, Suphakarn Techapongsatorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical site infections (SSIs) remain a major concern after abdominal surgery, with incidences of 15–22%. Intraoperative wound irrigation (IOWI) before closure is routinely performed to reduce bacterial contamination, but the benefit of antiseptic irrigation, such as aqueous chlorhexidine, remains uncertain.MethodsThis double-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine, Vajira Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. A total of 408 adults undergoing abdominal surgery with clean-contaminated and contaminated wounds were randomized (1:1) to receive IOWI with either aqueous chlorhexidine or normal saline before skin closure. Randomization was computer-generated with block design, and outcomes were assessed at 30 days postoperatively.ResultsAmong 408 patients (204 per group), SSIs occurred in 21 patients (10.3%) in the aqueous chlorhexidine group and 28 (13.7%) in the saline group (overall 12.0%). The difference was not statistically significant (risk difference − 3.4%; 95% CI − 9.7–2.9%; P = 0.27). No significant differences were found in wound seroma, evisceration, hospital stay, or pain scores.ConclusionsAqueous chlorhexidine irrigation did not demonstrate statistically significant superiority over normal saline for preventing SSI in this population. As this trial was neither designed nor powered as an equivalence or non-inferiority study, the absence of a statistically significant difference should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between the two irrigation strategies.Trial registrationThai Clinical Trials Registry Number (TCTR20211027010).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13017-026-00705-y.Keywords: Intraoperative wound irrigation, Aqueous chlorhexidine, Normal saline, Surgical site infection

Background

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are infections affecting the incision or deep tissue that occur within 30 days postoperatively (or within 1 year following device implantation) [1]. SSIs are categorized as superficial, deep incisional, or organ/body space infections. In the USA, the incidence of SSIs is approximately 2.8% [2], while that involving abdominal incisions ranges from 15.7 to 22.2% [3–5]. SSIs influence patient outcomes, resulting in prolonged hospitalization, higher readmission rates and healthcare costs, physical limitations, diminished health-related quality of life, and increased reoperation risks, amounting to an annual economic burden of $1.6 billion in the USA [6–8].

Risk factors for SSIs are classified as patient-related and procedure-related [1]. To lessen SSI-associated morbidity and mortality, preventive care methods integrating various interventions have been employed [9–11]. These include smoking cessation, weight-based antibiotic prophylaxis, hair removal, skin preparation with alcohol-based solutions, wound protectors, antimicrobial-coated sutures, perioperative glucose control, maintaining normothermia, ensuring oxygen saturation, and changing contaminated gloves and instruments before wound closure aim to minimize SSI risks and enhance patient outcomes [9, 10, 12].

Intraoperative wound irrigation (IOWI) is a preventive method against SSIs, with studies exploring normal saline solution (NSS) and povidone-iodine (PVP-I) compared with no irrigation. Although international guidelines recommend PVP-I for IOWI [13, 14], the Surgical Infectious Society of Thailand (SIST) recommends warm NSS [12]. Chlorhexidine-based antiseptics, which are available in alcohol and water solutions, may also potentially prevent SSIs [15]. Reduced SSI rates using chlorhexidine IOWI in clean wound settings, such as orthopedics joint replacement [16], cesarean section [17], and breast surgery [18], have been reported without adverse effects on wound healing.

Due to chlorhexidine’s favorable efficacy and safety profile, it may be preferred over normal saline solution (NSS) for intraoperative wound irrigation (IOWI). However, its role in laparotomy incisions remains inadequately explored. This trial aimed to compare the effectiveness of subcutaneous IOWI using 0.05% aqueous chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) versus NSS in reducing surgical site infections (SSIs) in abdominal surgeries involving clean-contaminated and contaminated wounds. We hypothesized that CHG irrigation would reduce the incidence of SSIs without increasing postoperative complications. This study complies with the TITAN 2025 guideline for transparency and reporting of artificial intelligence in healthcare research [19].

Methods

A double-blind, randomised controlled trial with a parallel design was conducted from November 2021 to December 2024 at the Faculty of Medicine Vajira hospital, Navamindradhiraj University. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive intraoperative wound irrigation (IOWI) with either 0.05% aqueous chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) or 0.09% normal saline solution (NSS) before skin closure. Ethical approval was granted Vajira Institutional Board Review (COA 205/2564) and Trials registration at Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR2021102701). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines [20].

Participants

Patients aged ≥ 18 years undergoing abdominal surgery involving clean-contaminated or contaminated wounds were eligible. Exclusion criteria included dirty or infected wounds (Class IV), second-look or planned reoperations, and surgeries requiring prosthetic material. Patients classified as ASA I–IV were included. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. Emergency surgery was defined as any unplanned, urgent operative intervention required during the index admission due to an acute surgical condition that could not be safely deferred. This included, but was not limited to, conditions such as acute appendicitis, acute cholecystitis requiring urgent operation, bowel obstruction with strangulation, bowel perforation, and bowel ischaemia. Elective surgery was defined as any planned operative procedure scheduled in advance in a clinically stable patient.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed using permuted blocks of 4–6, generated by an independent statistician using STATA version 16.0. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, stored independently by the research coordinator and opened intraoperatively by the scrub nurse only after fascial closure and confirmation of patient eligibility. This trial was conducted as a double-blind study, in which (1) patients were blinded to treatment allocation throughout the study period, and (2) postoperative outcome assessors were independently trained and maintained blinding throughout the 30-day follow-up period.

To minimise the risk of intraoperative unblinding, the following safeguards were implemented. First, the allocated solution was prepared by the scrub nurse in an identically labelled, unmarked syringe of the same volume (200 mL) prior to administration. Second, a standardised irrigation protocol was applied in all cases, using the same syringe size, pressure, and duration of approximately one minute, so that the manner of irrigation did not differ between groups. Third, after fascial closure, the operating surgeon stepped away from the operative field, and wound irrigation was performed by the surgical resident, who received only the prepared unmarked syringe without knowledge of its contents.

We acknowledge that scrub nurses were necessarily aware of the allocated solution when preparing the syringes, and that complete blinding of all intraoperative personnel cannot be claimed. This represents a potential source of performance bias and is acknowledged as a limitation of this study.

Outcome assessors were trained in postoperative wound assessment according to CDC criteria and remained blinded to group assignment throughout the follow-up period.

Intervention

After fascial closure and hemostasis, the wound cavity was irrigated with 200 mL of either 0.05% aqueous chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) or 0.09% normal saline using a 20 mL syringe with gentle pressure over 1 min. The cavity was gently dried with sterile gauze before subcutaneous and skin closure. The incisions were then closed in layers and covered with a standard occlusive dressing.

Perioperative care

Perioperative management was standardised across both groups. All patients received care according to the hospital’s SSI prevention bundle, which included preoperative antiseptic showering, administration of prophylactic intravenous antibiotics (e.g., cefazolin or suitable alternatives) within 60 min before incision, alcohol-based skin preparation, the use of sterile gowns, gloves, and drapes, and standard postoperative wound dressing. Pain was managed using intravenous or oral analgesics depending on clinical status and the resumption of diet. Daily wound care was provided until suture removal. A complete description of the SSI bundle is available in Supplementary Table 1.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the incidence of surgical site infection (SSI) within 30 days, diagnosed according to CDC criteria, and classified into superficial incisional, deep incisional, or organ/space infections. Diagnosis was based on clinical signs (erythema, purulent drainage, wound dehiscence, systemic symptoms) and confirmed by blinded assessors.

Secondary outcomes included the occurrence of wound seroma, wound evisceration, postoperative pain scores assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) ranging from 0 to 10 on postoperative day 1 and day 7, and the total length of hospital stay measured in days.

Data collection

All clinical data were collected prospectively by trained research assistants and cross-verified by a second blinded reviewer. During hospitalisation, wound status and postoperative complications were assessed daily. After discharge, a dedicated research coordinator contacted each patient to schedule and confirm postoperative follow-up. Follow-up was conducted through in-person outpatient clinic visits for patients who were able to attend, and via video call (telehealth assessment) for patients who were unable to visit the hospital in person. All follow-up assessments were completed within 30 days postoperatively. At each follow-up contact, patients were specifically asked whether they had sought medical attention at any other institution after discharge. Surgical site infections diagnosed and managed outside our hospital were recorded by the research coordinator and included in the primary outcome analysis.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on an estimated SSI incidence of 15% in the control group, derived from published data on contaminated abdominal incisions in comparable surgical populations [3–5]. A clinically meaningful absolute risk reduction of approximately 6% (corresponding to a relative reduction of around 30–40%) was assumed. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, a total of 400 patients (200 per group) was required. We acknowledge that SSI rates vary widely depending on wound class, procedure type, and institution; population-specific data from our own setting were not available at the time of trial design, and this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis using STATA version 16.0. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test based on data distribution. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Post hoc analyses were conducted using logistic regression to evaluate associations between SSI and covariates (e.g., ASA class, wound classification, and surgical site). Subgroup analyses were also performed to explore differential effects within clinically relevant strata. The multivariable logistic regression analysis was post hoc and exploratory in nature. Given the limited number of SSI events relative to the number of covariates, the risk of model overfitting, and the potential for collinearity among predictors, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating only and interpreted with caution.

Role of the funding source

This study received no external funding. The authors conducted and reported the study independently.

Results

Overall, 408 patients were randomly allocated to the IOWI with CHG in water (n = 204) and NSS (n = 204) groups (Fig. 1). There were no missing data for the primary outcome, and all 408 randomised patients were included in the intention-to-treat analysis. The demographics and operational characteristics of the participants are shown in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. This study contains no protocol violations. Table 3 shows the postoperative outcomes between the CHG and NSS groups. Overall, there were 49 (12.0%) cases of SSIs, comprising 33 (8.1%) superficial and 16 (3.9%) deep infections. No organ-space SSIs were observed. In the CHG group, superficial and deep SSIs occurred in 14 (6.9%) and 7 (3.4%) patients, respectively, compared with 19 (9.3%) and 9 (4.4%) in the NSS group. No significant differences were found between the groups (P = 0.27, 0.35, and 0.61 for total, superficial, and deep SSIs, respectively).

Discussion

This double-blind randomized controlled trial assessed the efficacy of intraoperative wound irrigation (IOWI) with 0.05% aqueous chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) compared to normal saline solution (NSS) in reducing surgical site infections (SSIs) following abdominal surgeries involving clean-contaminated and contaminated wounds. Although the CHG group demonstrated a lower SSI rate (10.3%) than the NSS group (13.7%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.27). No significant differences were also observed in other outcomes, including wound seroma, wound evisceration, postoperative pain scores, or length of hospital stay.

SSIs are a prevalent healthcare-associated complication that impact patient outcomes, prolong hospital stays, increase healthcare costs, and may necessitate reoperation or readmission [13, 21, 22]. Thus, preventing SSIs through preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative measures is critical and is often implemented as part of a structured prevention bundle [14, 23]. Despite adherence to such protocols, residual risk remains, especially in high-risk abdominal procedures.

IOWI is one adjunctive strategy aimed at reducing microbial contamination prior to wound closure [24–26]. While NSS is widely used due to its isotonicity and safety, it lacks inherent antimicrobial activity. Povidone-iodine (PVP-I), though effective against a broad spectrum of pathogens, may impair wound healing by affecting fibroblast viability. CHG, in contrast, offers broad bactericidal activity, residual antimicrobial effect, and lower cytotoxicity when used at appropriate dilutions [27].

Previous meta-analyses have shown conflicting results regarding the efficacy of IOWI in SSI prevention, partly due to heterogeneity in study populations and irrigation protocols [28]. CHG has demonstrated benefit in reducing SSI in clean surgical procedures such as orthopedics and gynecology, but data in contaminated abdominal surgery are limited. Arslan et al. reported lower SSI rates in pilonidal surgery using CHG irrigation [29], and other studies have confirmed CHG’s ability to reduce biofilm formation and microbial burden [30–32].

In our study, the overall SSI incidence was 12%, which is slightly higher than rates typically reported for clean-contaminated wounds (3–10%), likely reflecting the inclusion of contaminated and high-risk abdominal cases, frequent open procedures, and emergency surgeries. Our findings align with these observations. Although the difference in SSI incidence did not reach statistical significance, the absolute risk reduction of approximately 3% and the consistent trend favoring CHG suggest potential clinical relevance, particularly in high-risk populations. Even modest reductions in SSI can translate into meaningful benefits in patient outcomes, antibiotic stewardship, and healthcare costs.

Importantly, no significant adverse events were observed. CHG at a 0.05% concentration falls within the safe range described in the literature [33, 34]. Mangold et al. demonstrated that this dilution does not impair wound healing when used intraoperatively [35]. Furthermore, the brief contact time during irrigation minimizes the risk of cytotoxicity. In our study, rates of seroma and evisceration were comparable between groups, supporting the safety of CHG.

Our study has limitations. The single-center design may restrict generalizability. Additionally, the absence of microbiological culture limits pathogen-specific interpretation. Furthermore, although several safeguards were implemented to minimise intraoperative unblinding, complete concealment of the allocated solution from all theatre personnel cannot be fully guaranteed, as the two irrigation solutions may differ in appearance and odour. This represents a potential source of performance bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Randomisation was not stratified by procedure type, urgency of surgery, or wound classification. Given the substantial clinical heterogeneity across these variables, the absence of stratification may have contributed to residual imbalance between groups and could have diluted any detectable treatment effect.

The multivariable logistic regression analysis was post hoc and exploratory in nature. Given the limited number of SSI events relative to the number of covariates, the risk of model overfitting, and the potential for collinearity among predictors, findings from Table 4 should be considered hypothesis-generating only and interpreted with caution.

The study was powered to detect a moderate absolute risk reduction of 6% and was not designed to detect smaller but potentially clinically meaningful differences. The observed between-group difference of 3.4% falls below this threshold, and a Type II error cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the non-significant result should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or non-inferiority between the two irrigation strategies.

The study included a clinically heterogeneous population encompassing a wide range of abdominal procedures, wound classifications, urgency levels, and operative approaches. This heterogeneity may have diluted the detectable treatment effect and limits the generalisability of findings to more homogeneous surgical populations.

Nevertheless, the study offers high internal validity, given its rigorous blinding, standardized protocols, and complete 30-day follow-up. Current WHO and CDC guidelines offer cautious endorsements of antiseptic irrigation, largely due to inconsistent evidence [13, 14]. Our trial contributes controlled data supporting the feasibility and safety of aqueous CHG in abdominal surgery.

Finally, IOWI should be viewed as part of a multimodal infection prevention strategy. When combined with systemic antibiotics, proper skin antisepsis, and meticulous technique, antiseptic irrigation may offer additive benefit. However, further multicenter randomized studies are warranted to confirm efficacy, evaluate microbial outcomes, and perform cost-effectiveness analyses across different surgical contexts.

Conclusion

In this double-blind randomized trial, intraoperative wound irrigation with 0.05% aqueous chlorhexidine gluconate did not significantly reduce surgical site infections compared with normal saline among patients undergoing abdominal surgery with clean-contaminated and contaminated wounds. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, a modest reduction in SSI incidence (approximately 3%) was observed in the CHG group. The consistent direction of benefit, combined with the absence of adverse effects, suggests that aqueous chlorhexidine is a safe and feasible adjunct to standard SSI prevention protocols. However, this study was not designed or powered to determine equivalence or non-inferiority, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Given its low cost, broad antimicrobial spectrum, and favorable safety profile, CHG irrigation may still be considered in high-risk surgical settings where the risk of contamination is substantial. Further multicenter randomized trials with larger sample sizes and microbial outcome assessment are warranted to establish definitive efficacy and to inform future guideline recommendations.
