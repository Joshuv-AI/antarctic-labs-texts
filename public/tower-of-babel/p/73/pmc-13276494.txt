scParadise: tunable, highly accurate multi-level cell type annotation, unknown cell type identification, and modality imputation
Authors: Elizaveta Chechekhina, Liya Shcherbakova, Maksim Vigovskiy, Amir Tukhvatulin, Denis Logunov, Dmitry Tychinin, Dmitry Svetlichnyy, Konstantin Kulebyakin, Vsevolod Tkachuk, Vadim Chechekhin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn single-cell biology, the main limitation has shifted from data generation to converting sparse, heterogeneous single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets into accurate cell types, interpretable multi-omic states, and reproducible conclusions. To address these challenges, we present scParadise, which transforms scRNA-seq data to a new scientific knowledge. scParadise comprises three integrated tools: scAdam, a multi-level cell type annotation tool with unknown cell type identification; scEve, a cross-tissue modality imputation tool; and scNoah, a standardized benchmarking tool. Using scParadise, we corrected annotation mistakes in the Tabula Muris Senis atlas, showing that cells labeled as granulocytes are exclusively neutrophils and that presumably annotated macrophages actually represent a range of different cell types. Moreover, we identify three previously unknown natural killer T (NKT) cell subsets by imputing protein expression across tissues, which include CD56dim CD3+, CD56dim CD3+ CD4+, and CD56dim CD3+ CD8+ cell subsets in human visceral adipose tissue, which we verify by flow cytometry. These new subsets engage in obesity-related tumor necrosis factor-centric crosstalk with myeloid and adipose progenitors, thereby illuminating a new paradigm for immune–stromal interactions that contribute to chronic inflammation and impaired adipogenesis.

Introduction

Modern biomedical research increasingly relies on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. This method is focused on studying the architecture of tissue, its cellular composition, intercellular interactions, and the functional interactions of cells [1]. A crucial step in scRNA-seq analysis is cell type annotation. Manual annotation is a time-consuming process that requires a high level of knowledge about the cellular composition of the tissue. This has led to the rapid development of automatic cell type annotation methods [2]. However, the predictive power of existing automatic annotation methods hardly ever exceeds 90%. Moreover, rare cell types (< 1% of the population) are particularly poorly annotated by most automatic annotation methods.

One of the largest technical challenges in scRNA-seq analysis is high dropout rates—random failures of transcript detection that are actually expressed—leading to an excess of false-zero counts that overwhelm true biological signals. As a consequence, this leads to the failure to detect certain cell type marker genes, which are critically important for accurate cell type annotation. Current single-cell technologies increasingly aim to obtain multimodal molecular profiles from single cells, with technologies such as CITE-seq (cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing) simultaneously detecting the transcriptome and surface proteome, thus offering an extremely effective complementary approach. Such multimodal data help solve the problems of clustering and cell type annotation [3]. Moreover, this approach achieves more accurate quality control and characterization of the functional state of cells. However, experiments involving multimodal data collection are very expensive and labor-intensive. Consequently, methods for predicting and imputing modalities are of significant interest to researchers.

The development of automatic cell annotation methods has led to the lack of a systematic approach in validating the results of model performance. Additionally, model testing is often carried out on a single test dataset, which does not reflect the full variability of scRNA-seq data [4–7]. Consequently, there is an increasing need to unify approaches to testing new methods and comparing them with existing ones. In most cases, assessing the quality of a method using one or two metrics (most often accuracy and F1-score) is not sufficient to fully reflect the quality of the model due to the specificity of scRNA-seq data. This requires a tool that allows for a more detailed quality assessment at the level of individual cell subpopulations. In addition, the development of modality prediction methods also requires unification in quality control.

We introduce scParadise, a new Python framework for fast, tunable, high-performance automatic cell type annotation and modality prediction. scParadise includes three sets of tools: scAdam, multi-level multi-class cell type annotation with unknown cell type identification; scEve, cross-tissue modality imputation; and scNoah, benchmarking cell type annotation and modality imputation. Additionally, we present pipelines for training and using scParadise models in Python and R.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA-seq data analysis

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cell CITE-seq dataset

The matrices of peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) 3′ datasets were downloaded from GSE164378 [8]. The dataset included 161 764 cells from 24 samples from eight donors. We used the provided cell annotations but excluded erythrocytes and doublets from the dataset. We used the scanpy and muon packages for downstream analysis [9, 10]. The gene matrix was normalized using a shifted logarithm transformation. We used centered log ratio (CLR) transformation (muon.prot.pp.clr) to normalize cell surface protein counts. As the isotypes do not contain any biological information, we removed them from the data. We used all protein labels and 2000 highly variable genes [scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes(adata, n_top_genes = 2000, layer = ‘counts’, batch_key = ‘sample’, flavor = ‘seurat_v3’)] for data integration. We used weighted nearest neighbors (WNNs) with default parameters for multimodal data integration [8]. For WNNs, we used 20 principal components from each modality (RNA and protein) after batch correction using scANVI with default parameters [11]. All stages of dataset integration and final annotation are available in the script PBMC_3p_dataset in Figshare (see Code availability).

Human pancreas dataset

The processed anndata file of the pancreas dataset was downloaded from CELLxGENE [12]. We used the provided cell annotations. We split data into a single training dataset and 10 test datasets. Raw matrices of training and test datasets were normalized separately using a shifted logarithm transformation.

Human oropharyngeal cancer dataset

The processed anndata file of the oropharyngeal cancer dataset was downloaded from CELLxGENE [13]. We used the provided cell annotations. We split data into a single training dataset and eight test datasets. Raw matrices of training and test datasets were normalized separately using a shifted logarithm transformation.

Mouse aging brain dataset

The mouse aging brain dataset was obtained from UCSC Cell Browser (https://cells.ucsc.edu/?ds=aging-brain) [14]. No additional pre-processing was applied other than splitting into a single training dataset and six test datasets.

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cell dataset

The human PBMC dataset of healthy donors was downloaded from 10x Genomics (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/5k-human-pbmcs-stained-with-totalseq-B-human-TBNK-cocktail-NextGEM). Doublet detection was assessed using the Scrublet package [15]. We also removed cells with a gene count > 6000 or < 1000, protein count < 8, number of gene UMI (Unique Molecular Identifier) counts > 20 000, number of protein UMI counts > 4000 or < 200, and a mitochondrial gene percentage > 10. The gene matrix was normalized using a shifted logarithm transformation. We used CLR transformation (muon.prot.pp.clr) to normalize observed cell surface protein counts. For principal component analysis (PCA) calculation, we used the 1200 highly variable genes, identified using the function scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes(adata, n_top_genes = 1200). We used 15 principal components and set the number of neighbors to 12 to compute the nearest neighbors distance matrix for RNA data.

We used all observed proteins for PCA calculation. We concatenated 15 RNA-based and 9 observed protein-based principal components for the nearest neighbors distance matrix and UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) computation. Cell surface protein imputation was assessed using the human PBMC-based scEve model. We used our CITE-seq PBMC-based scEve model to impute surface protein abundance. Also, we used the PBMC-based scAdam model for automatic cell type annotation of cells. We concatenated 15 RNA-based and 15 imputed protein-based principal components for the nearest neighbors distance matrix and UMAP computation.

The Leiden algorithm with a resolution of 0.5 was used to cluster cells, as in the case of RNA data, as well as for multimodal data (with observed or imputed proteins). Processing steps and surface protein imputation using scEve in the human PBMC dataset are available in the script scEve_PBMC_clustering.ipynb (Code availability).

Tabula Muris Senis spleen dataset

The processed Tabula Muris Senis spleen dataset was downloaded from CELLxGENE [16]. Raw counts data were normalized using a shifted logarithm transformation. Surface protein imputation for spleen cells was performed using a tuned scEve model trained on a mouse spleen and lymph node dataset. Batch-aware highly variable gene selection (2000 genes) was performed with the “seurat_v3” algorithm on raw counts [17]. All imputed proteins were used for PCA. Batch correction was performed separately for RNA and imputed protein data using Harmony [18]. Nearest neighbors were computed with default parameters on Harmony-corrected PCA embeddings. Multimodal analysis was carried out using WNN in muon v0.1.7 with default parameters [10]. Cell subtype annotation was performed using both imputed surface protein abundance and gene expression.

Human subcutaneous adipose tissue cell atlas

FASTQ files of human subcutaneous adipose tissue scRNA-seq datasets were obtained from the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) database under accession number HRA000901 [19]. We used only datasets from healthy patients (HRI106993, HRI106992, HRI106991, and HRI106990). The FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome from 10x Genomics (version 2020-A) using CellRanger version 7.0.0. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX v1.6.2 [20]. Doublets were identified using scDblFinder v1.12.0 [21]. Cells with > 12% mitochondrial reads, or > 7000, or < 200 genes were removed, and counts were normalized using the shifted logarithm transformation. Cell types were annotated using established marker genes (Supplementary Fig. S16A). Genes for data integration, visualization, and scAdam model training were selected using the standard feature-selection protocol described in the section ‘Feature selection for performing methods’. Batch correction was performed using Harmony [18]. Nearest neighbors were identified using 50 dimensions and 12 neighbors, and UMAP embeddings were generated for visualization with min_dist = 0.75.

Human visceral adipose tissue cell atlas

FASTQ files of human visceral adipose tissue scRNA-seq datasets were obtained from GSE214982 [22]. The FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome from 10x Genomics (version 2020-A) using CellRanger version 7.0.0. Ambient RNA correction was performed using SoupX v1.6.2 [20]. Doublet detection was performed using scDblFinder v1.12.0 [21]. Cells with > 20% mitochondrial reads, > 1% hemoglobin genes, or > 6000 features were removed, and counts were normalized using the shifted logarithm transformation. Batch-aware highly variable gene selection (2000 genes) was performed using the “seurat_v3” algorithm on raw count data [17]. Batch correction was performed using Harmony [18]. Nearest neighbors were identified using 50 dimensions, and UMAP embeddings were generated for visualization with min_dist = 0.75 and spread = 2. Cell types were identified by the subcutaneous adipose tissue-based scAdam model and checked using cell-type-specific marker genes (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Fig. S16B–E).

Data split

To evaluate automated annotation methods, training and test datasets were derived from the same published atlases but strictly split by independent, non-overlapping biological samples/donors to reflect real-world scenarios. To ensure all cell types were represented, we merged several independent samples/donors into one test dataset. Data normalization and gene selection were performed exclusively on the training datasets to prevent any data leakage.

Specifically, datasets were split into two training and multiple independent test datasets: human PBMC (eight test sets), pancreas (10 test sets), and oropharyngeal cancer (eight test sets). For the mouse aging brain, we created one training and six test datasets. The primary training sets were used to train scAdam and competing methods (CellTypist, scGPT, Azimuth, TOSICA, Seurat, scANVI, and Symphony), which were then evaluated on the identical test datasets. For the PBMC data, the first training set was also used for scAdam hyperparameter tuning, while the second was used for warm-start training. For modality imputation, the PBMC training datasets were concatenated to compare Azimuth, Seurat, and scEve.

Finally, to simulate a realistic external application, we evaluated scParadise on completely independent datasets. First, a PBMC-trained scAdam model was applied to an external PBMC dataset from 10x Genomics. Second, an scAdam model trained on subcutaneous adipose tissue was used to annotate a visceral adipose tissue atlas generated by an independent research group. These examples provide a rigorous test of model generalizability on new, investigator-generated data.

Feature selection for performing methods

For all datasets, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type at each annotation level to implement the methods. DEGs were computed using only the first training dataset for each tissue. Specifically, we employed the sc.tl.rank_genes_groups function from the Scanpy package [9]. Subsequently, marker genes were filtered automatically: genes expressed in < 40% of cells within a given cell type or with log fold changes < 1.0 were removed. We then selected the top 50 marker genes based on a combined ranking of log fold change and the ratio of the fraction of cells expressing the gene in the target cell type versus other cells, which identified the most cell-type-specific markers. To ensure that individual genes contributed independently during scAdam training, the final marker list was randomly shuffled (via automatic shuffling in scparadise.scadam.train and scparadise.sceve.train). All steps for DEG computation and filtering are provided in the scAdam training and testing scripts (see Code availability).

Application of methods

The training datasets were pruned based on the selected genes. For the scAdam model training and optimization, the training dataset was balanced for the average cell count across all cell types using the most detailed annotation level. Undersampling of major cell types was performed using random cell selection, while oversampling of rare cell types was conducted using the RandomOverSampler function from the imblearn package with shrinkage set to 1. The training dataset for the scAdam and scEve models was divided into a training dataset (90% of the total) and a validation dataset (10% of the total). The validation dataset was used for performance evaluation, preventing overfitting through early stopping. Competing tools (CellTypist v1.6.3, scGPT v0.2.1, Azimuth v0.5.0, TOSICA v1.0.0, Seurat v5.1.0, scVI v1.3.3, and Symphony v0.2.1) were run with the authors’ default parameters [4–7, 25–27]. When possible (CellTypist, TOSICA, and Symphony), we applied the same gene expression normalization; raw counts were used for scANVI and scGPT. The same gene set was used across methods, except for Azimuth and Seurat due to method–specific requirements. For Azimuth, the 3000 most variable genes were selected with SelectIntegrationFeatures, data were normalized with SCTransform (‘glmgampoi’), and a reference was built from either the primary training set (scAdam comparison) or all training data (scEve comparison) using reciprocal PCA (RPCA) integration. For Seurat, counts were log-normalized (NormalizeData), 2000 highly variable genes were chosen (FindVariableFeatures), and references were generated from the same training subsets with canonical correlation analysis (CCA) integration.

The methods utilized the same training datasets and annotations. The same testing datasets were used to analyze performance of the methods. All steps for applying the methods and evaluating their performance are available on Figshare (see Code availability).

scParadise architecture

Cell type balancing

scRNA-seq data are often imbalanced in the number of cells across different cell types. Minor cell types may include < 1% of the total dataset, whereas major cell types can account for up to 50% of the cells in a dataset. Balancing is performed at the most detailed annotation level (typically the cell state). Cell type labels at other annotation levels are preserved using the cell type hierarchy. To mitigate cell type imbalance, scParadise uses undersampling of major (over-represented) cell types and oversampling of minor (under-represented) cell types. Major cell types are downsampled by randomly selecting cells to bring each cell type closer to the dataset’s mean cell count per cell type. However, to preserve variability, undersampling is not allowed to reduce a major cell type to < 50% of its original number of cells. Minor cell types are oversampled via PCA-based synthetic cell generation. Principal components (20 by default) are learned from the cell-type-specific feature expression matrix. Synthetic cells are generated by convex interpolation in principal component latent space between randomly chosen pairs of real cells from the minor cell type. The interpolation coefficient is sampled from the range 0.3–0.7, ensuring that the synthetic cells lie strictly between the original cells. By default, the size of a minor cell type can be increased by up to 7-fold, which helps avoid generating an excessively large number of synthetic cells. In addition, PCA can be computed only when a minor cell type contains at least five cells.

After balancing, the dataset is split into training and validation sets in an 80%/20% ratio. The split is performed using the most detailed cell type annotation level. The training set is used to fit the model, while the validation enables early stopping to prevent overfitting. Cell type balancing was not used for training scEve models.

scAdam architecture and training

The scAdam model processes normalized gene expression matrices using a gene embedding layer that divides the genes into chunks (default: 16 chunks) and generates a sequence of gene embeddings (default: 256 nodes). The sequence of embeddings is then fed into a transformer encoder, which is composed of blocks (default: 5 blocks). Each block consists of multi-head self-attention with eight heads, a position-wise feedforward network with a hidden size of 512, LayerNorm, and dropout set at 0.3.

The hierarchical scAdam classifier consists of level-specific multilayer perceptron (MLP) heads. For each level, the input is a concatenation of the cell embedding and softmax probabilities from the previous levels. First is single-score classification, where the input is classified as unknown if any of the scores exceed their respective thresholds. The third is combined scoring, where the scores are normalized by summation and compared against a single threshold.

Hyperparameter optimization and warm-start fine-tuning

Hyperparameter optimization is performed using Optuna for 100 trials (by default) with pruning via the Hyperband algorithm. To increase efficiency, 5-fold stratified cross-validation is used with stratification by the most detailed annotation level (e.g. cell state). The performance of each trial is evaluated by aggregating metrics across all annotation levels according to a user-defined weighting scheme. In case of interruption, the optimization algorithm resumes from the saved state corresponding to the best performance achieved during the previous optimization period. Thus, training continues by loading the best-performing model.

Warm-start fine-tuning aligns gene sets between the source and target datasets (filling missing genes with zeros), optionally freezes the transformer block, and applies a reduced learning rate to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

scEve architecture and training

The scEve model is built on a lightweight transformer-based architecture, similar to scAdam. The key difference is that this model has a regression head in place of the hierarchical classifier. Moreover, scEve demonstrates computational efficiency and applicability to various datasets.

Input features from the first modality (e.g. RNA) are fed into the model and normalized per feature. The features are then embedded into chunks and passed to the scEve transformer encoder. By default, the model has four blocks and four chunks per cell with a dropout probability of 0.1. Subsequently, the compact embedding representation of the cell is projected onto the entire feature space of the target (second) modality (e.g. proteins) through a two-layer MLP regressor. Unlike the input features, target features undergo only clipping of negative values. Thus, the model is capable of learning directly on the native scale of the target features. During prediction, features are automatically aligned with the training set. If a feature is absent from the dataset, its value is filled with zeros for all cells.

Benchmarking modality imputation and automatic cell type annotation tasks in scRNA-seq using scNoah

To create a set of tools (scNoah) for analyzing cell type annotation performance, we employed classical metrics for classification analysis, such as accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score. Additionally, we utilized metrics specific to imbalanced data (geometric mean and index balanced accuracy) due to the cell type imbalance present in scRNA-seq data [28, 29]. All classification metrics were performed using the scikit-learn and imblearn packages (scparadise.scnoah.report_classif_full) [30, 31]. We also used a normalized confusion matrix to determine per cell type accuracy (scparadise.scnoah.conf_matrix). Additionally, we added visualization of correct and incorrect annotations on cell embeddings (scparadise.scnoah.pred_status) (Fig. 3G–I, Fig. 6A).

To assess the quality of cell surface protein abundance imputation, we used error metrics such as root mean squared error (RMSE), median absolute error (Median AE), and mean absolute error (Mean AE). We also used the explained variance score (EVS) and the coefficient of determination (R2 score) to evaluate the quality of the regression. These metrics can be applied both to the entire dataset to obtain average prediction values (scparadise.scnoah.report_reg) and to each cell separately (scparadise.scnoah.regres_status) for visualization on cell embeddings. To determine the accuracy of the distribution of a specific imputed cell surface protein, we used the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) (scparadise.scnoah.pearson_coef).

Interpretations, formulas, and examples of the metrics used in scNoah are provided in the Theory section of the scParadise documentation.

Adipose tissue harvest and cell dissociation

Isolation of the stromal–vascular fraction (SVF) cells was performed from visceral adipose tissue (greater omentum) samples obtained from three female donors undergoing laparoscopic gynecological surgeries. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Lomonosov Moscow State University (IRB00010587; protocol #4, June 4, 2018; and protocol #9, October 29, 2018). Written informed consent was obtained from all donors prior to sample collection. Immediately after collection, the adipose tissue specimens were placed in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; PanEco, Russia) and transported to the laboratory within 2 h. Upon arrival, the samples were finely minced and subjected to enzymatic digestion in HBSS containing collagenase type I (1 mg ml−1; Worthington Biochemical Corporation, USA). The digestion was carried out for 50–60 min at 37°C with constant agitation. Following digestion, the cell suspensions were centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min at 4°C. The floating adipocyte layer was carefully removed, and the pelleted cells were resuspended in cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; PanEco, Russia) supplemented with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; PanEco, Russia) for subsequent processing. Cell suspensions were filtered using 70 μm cell strainers (Biologix, China).

Flow cytometry of human visceral adipose tissue immune cells

Cell suspensions obtained after isolation of the SVF were used for flow cytometry analysis to characterize immune cell populations. The cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies anti-CD3 FITC (BD Biosciences, USA), anti-CD4 PC7 (Beckman Coulter, USA), anti-CD8 APC (Beckman Coulter, USA), anti-CD56 PE (Exbio, Czech Republic), and anti-CD45 APC-AF750 (Beckman Coulter, USA). Staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Aliquots of 1 × 106 cells were washed and resuspended in a staining buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% BSA; PanEco, Russia). Antibodies and isotype controls were added at optimal concentrations and incubated in the dark for 15 min at 4°C. After incubation, cells were washed once with staining buffer and resuspended for acquisition. Data were acquired on a flow cytometer Navios (Beckman Coulter, USA), and at least 10 000 events were collected per sample. Compensation controls and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were used to set gating thresholds. Analysis was performed using Kaluza 2.1 software (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Flow cytometry of mouse spleen cells

The mouse study was conducted in compliance with international and local regulations and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Research Center for Epidemiology and Microbiology Named after Honorary Academician N.F. Gamaleya of the Ministry of Health of the Russian Federation (protocol No. 100, June 24, 2025). Spleens from four 4- to 5-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were collected and meshed through a 70 μm nylon filter (SPL, South Korea). A total of 0.5 × 106 splenocytes in each sample were incubated with Mouse Fc Block (BD Biosciences, USA) and Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend, USA) for 15 min at +4°C. After washing with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in PBS, splenocytes were stained with antibodies to distinguish neutrophils (anti-CD45 BV786, anti-CD11b AF700, anti-CD3 FITC, anti-CD19 FITC, anti-NK1.1 FITC, anti-Ly6C PE, anti-Ly6G PE-Cy7), B cells (anti-CD45 BV786, anti-CD19 BV605, anti-CD21/35 APC, anti-B220 FITC, anti-IgD PerCP-Cy5.5), and macrophages (anti-CD45 BV786, anti-CD3 FITC, anti-CD19 FITC, anti-Ly6G FITC, anti-CD11b AF700, anti-CD172a PE-CF594, anti-F4/80 BV711) in Brilliant Buffer (all BD Biosciences, USA). Corresponding FMO controls were used to define gates. Results

Multi-level cell type annotation and unknown cell type identification using scAdam

scAdam is an automatic multi-level multi-class cell type annotation tool. Each “level” corresponds to varying degrees of annotation granularity and includes multiple classes (types, subtypes, and/or specialized cell states). The annotation levels are organized according to a hierarchical principle. For example, we used the multi-level scAdam model on the PBMC dataset: l1 level, major cell types (T cells, monocytes, etc.); l2 level, cell subtypes (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, CD14+ monocytes, CD16+ monocytes, etc.); and l3 level, specialized cell states (CD4+ naïve T cells, CD4+ central memory T cells, CD4+ effector memory T cells, etc.). Multi-level training enables the scAdam model to leverage shared features across various annotation levels, improving predictions across all levels. Training an scAdam model requires prior cell type annotation. However, applying scAdam models to new data does not require any preliminary cell type annotation and/or clustering. scAdam annotates cells using only a normalized gene expression data matrix.

To demonstrate the performance of scAdam models, we selected publicly available, previously annotated human CITE-seq PBMC, pancreas, oropharyngeal cancer, and mouse aging brain datasets [8, 12–14]. The scAdam model training process consists of three key steps: feature (gene) selection; automated dataset balancing; and training (Fig. 1A). Gene selection is the first step in preparing the model for training. We provide two options for selecting genes: either all highly variable genes or marker genes identified using different tools (Scanpy and Seurat). Manual correction of the selected genes is also possible but optional, using marker genes from various databases or by filtering for the top highly variable genes. These approaches allowed us to select the most significant genes at each cell annotation level for training the models.

The second step involved balancing cell types using the most detailed level of annotation. scRNA-seq data are often imbalanced in the number of cells across different cell types. To address this issue, we employed oversampling (upsampling) and undersampling (downsampling) methods. Oversampling is a common solution to the problem of class (“cell type” in this case) imbalance [28, 29]. This method involves adding cells to the minority class, using either synthetic or original copies. We used a PCA-based method to generate new synthetic copies of rare cell types. Synthetic cells are created via convex interpolation in latent principal component space between randomly selected real cell pairs, ensuring that new samples lie strictly between originals with the interpolation coefficient ∈ [0.3, 0.7]. This has the effect of dispersing the synthetic cells in feature space, reducing the likelihood of model overfitting from exact copies. Downsampling addresses class imbalance by decreasing representation of the majority classes to match the size of the rare cell types. This approach prevents the model from overweighting the majority class during training, which can inflate apparent accuracy while decreasing performance on rare cell types in real-world data. To achieve this, we randomly subsampled over-represented cell types to the dataset-wide mean count per class, while retaining at least 50% of the original cells in each to preserve biological variability. By balancing class distribution, downsampling forces models to learn discriminative features for classes, improving generalization [28]. More detailed information about feature (gene) selection and dataset balancing is available in the Materials and methods.

Dataset balancing allows for a marked improvement in the representation of rare cell populations while maintaining the profiles of abundant cell types. For example, in the human PBMC dataset, we used the third (most detailed) annotation level (celltype_l3). We undersampled the most represented cell types (CD14 Mono, NK, CD4 T Naive, etc.) using random cell selection. We also oversampled the rare (least represented) cell types (ASDCs, AXL+ SIGLEC6+ dendritic cells; ILC, innate lymphoid cell; HSPCs, hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, etc.). At the same time, at less detailed levels of annotation, the generated synthetic cells retained their origin cell types according to the hierarchy.

To compare balanced and imbalanced scAdam model training, we used human PBMC, pancreas, oropharyngeal cancer, and mouse aging brain datasets (Fig. 1B). For training the models, we selected part of complete datasets. In order to evaluate the stability of the model’s performance across different scRNA-seq samples, the remaining data were used as separate test datasets. A more detailed explanation of the division of each dataset into training and test datasets is provided in the Materials and methods. The “accuracy paradox” renders accuracy a poor metric for evaluating the performance of a model in annotation due to cell type imbalance in scRNA-seq data. Therefore, we employed six distinct metrics to compare the performance of the methods. Overall performance was established in two steps: (i) for each of the six metrics, we averaged the metric values across all test datasets, and (ii) then we averaged these six metric-wise means to obtain a single overall score (total mean). All metrics are included in the scNoah benchmarking module functions.

This approach allowed us to increase the total average balanced accuracy (+2.35%), F1-score (+1.22%), geometric mean (+1.51%), and index balanced accuracy (+2.44%) between test datasets across all four tissues and six annotation levels. Dataset balancing also led to a slight decrease in the total average accuracy (−0.18%) and precision (−0.46%). The overall score (total mean) increased by +1.15% (Supplementary Fig. S1). These results demonstrate that the balanced scAdam model enhances predictive performance across diverse single-cell datasets, highlighting its potential for robust cell type annotation in computational biology applications.

Figure 1C and Supplementary Figs S2 and S3 show representative ground-truth versus predicted cell types by the balanced scAdam model, along with predicted probabilities for all datasets (higher values indicate higher probabilities of accurate annotation).

To evaluate the performance of the balanced scAdam model trained using default parameters, we compared it with other automatic cell type annotation tools: CellTypist, scGPT, TOSICA, Azimuth, Symphony, scANVI, and Seurat [4–8, 25, 27]. We used the same training and test datasets for all methods. scAdam outperformed all other methods in balanced accuracy, F1-score, geometric mean, index balanced accuracy, and overall score. Additionally, scAdam outperformed all other methods except scANVI in accuracy and precision (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Figs S4 and S5; Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, we compared the performance of the methods at the individual cell type level. All methods failed to identify certain cell types in multiple test datasets. Balanced scAdam failed to identify a single cell type in only three cases among 32 test datasets and six annotation tasks (Supplementary Table S1). Thus, scAdam not only outperformed other methods in most metrics but also achieved more reproducible results in cell annotation.

scAdam not only outperformed other automated cell type annotation tools, but also uniquely supports unknown cell type identification, allowing it to flag cells that do not match any trained label instead of forcing an incorrect assignment. Unknown cell type identification enables the model to discover novel cell types and cellular states across diverse tissues, diseases, and physiological conditions. We demonstrated this capability using a PBMC dataset. We trained scAdam on B-cell-depleted PBMC data and subsequently applied the trained model to predict cell types in unseen data. In contrast to other methods, the B-cell-depleted scAdam PBMC model correctly refrained from assigning a known label to the previously unseen B-cell population (Fig. 1E). By comparison, other automated annotation approaches misclassified B cells as various alternative cell types (Supplementary Fig. S6). scAdam identifies unknown cell types by combining three conceptually distinct methods. The gradient-based score quantifies model confidence by measuring the sensitivity of predictions to perturbations in feature space. The entropy-based score measures confidence using the Shannon entropy of the predicted class probability distribution. Finally, the distance-based uncertainty score quantifies confidence by measuring the distance between a cell’s embedding and the prototype representation of its predicted class (Fig. 1F).

Taken together, scAdam is not only a method for automatic, highly accurate multi-level cell type annotation but also a framework for detecting previously uncharacterized cellular states in real-world scRNA-seq data.

Cell surface protein abundance imputation using scEve

The second component of the scParadise toolkit is scEve (Fig. 2A). scEve imputes cell surface protein abundance based on the expression of selected genes. We compared four different methods for selecting genes to train the scEve model. The best method was highly variable gene selection using the Seurat v3 algorithm (Supplementary Fig. S7). To demonstrate scEve, we used a human CITE-seq PBMC dataset that had previously been utilized for scAdam. As shown in Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S8A, the distribution of imputed cell surface proteins was similar to that observed in the human PBMC dataset. To evaluate scEve’s imputation performance, we benchmarked it against well-established methods Azimuth and Seurat [8, 25]. In comparing methods, we used common regression metrics, including RMSE, Mean AE, and Median AE. In these metrics, lower values indicate better model performance. Additionally, we used the coefficient of determination (R2 score), EVS, and average PCC and Spearman correlation coefficient (SCC) across all proteins to further assess method performance. In these metrics, higher values indicate better model performance.

The default scEve model outperformed Azimuth and Seurat in all error metrics (RMSE, Median AE, and Mean AE), R2 score, EVS, PCC, and SCC (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S8B). Hyperparameter tuning of the scEve model (scEve tuned) will be discussed later in the section ‘Integrated optimization strategies for training of scAdam and scEve models’.

To demonstrate the capabilities of scEve in scRNA-seq analysis of the same tissues, we used the PBMC dataset available from the 10x Genomics website (Data availability section). We trained the scEve model on the whole CITE-seq PBMC dataset. After that, we applied a model to the PBMC dataset from 10x Genomics. Correct imputation was proven by PCC calculation for observed and imputed protein pairs (Supplementary Fig. S9A).

Surface protein abundance facilitates better identification and characterization of different cell types and functional states. A classic example of the clustering and annotation problem involved transcriptionally similar NK and T cells, as well as identification of T-cell subtypes. As shown in Fig. 2D, clustering calculated using only gene expression data did not allow a clear separation of T and NK cells based on marker genes (T and NK scores represent average expression of cell-type-specific marker genes). As a result, cluster 8 included both NK and T cells.

In contrast, integrating observed cell surface protein data with gene expression improved both clustering and cell type annotation. Figure 2E demonstrates the UMAP embeddings and clusters derived from observed multimodal data (gene expression + observed surface proteins). By leveraging surface protein-derived T and NK scores, clusters 4 and 12 were confidently annotated as NK cells, while clusters 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, and 9 represented T cells. Imputed surface proteins achieved comparable separation accuracy. Clusters 5 and 12, defined using imputed multimodal data (gene expression + imputed surface proteins), contained 100% of the cells originally assigned to clusters 4 and 12 in the observed multimodal analysis, with only a marginal increase of two additional cells (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. S9B–D).

Additionally, both observed and imputed surface protein data enabled equally precise separation of various T-cell subtypes, a task that is often challenging using gene expression data alone. To demonstrate this, we annotated cell types in this PBMC dataset using the scAdam model trained on whole CITE-seq PBMC data (Supplementary Fig. S10A–C). To validate the cell type annotations, we analyzed the expression of marker genes as well as observed and imputed surface proteins within the resulting cell types (Supplementary Fig. S10D, E). Using gene expression data alone does not yield clusters that clearly separate T-cell subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S10A). However, integrating observed as well as imputed surface proteins yielded a robust separation between CD8+ (and other) T-cell subtypes and CD4+ T-cell subtypes. On the observed multimodal UMAP, clusters 3, 7, 8, and 9 aligned with CD8+ and other T-cell subtypes, and clusters 1 and 2 with CD4+ subtypes; on the imputed multimodal UMAP, clusters 1, 11, 13, and 8 aligned with CD8+ and other subtypes, and clusters 0, 4, 6, and 10 with CD4+ subtypes. Thus, both observed and imputed surface proteins facilitated comparably robust discrimination of different cell types and subtypes (Supplementary Figs S9A and S11).

In summary, our findings demonstrate that integrating imputed cell surface protein and gene expression data substantially improves the clustering and accuracy in identifying cell types and subtypes. These results highlight the combined power of scEve and scAdam in improving cell type identification, offering a valuable advancement for single-cell analysis.

Integrated optimization strategies for training of scAdam and scEve models

Most of the existing automated cell type annotation tools (e.g. CellTypist, TOSICA, Symphony, Azimuth, Seurat, and Scanvi) lack the built-in possibility of optimization tuning of their computational parameters. However, scRNA-seq datasets vary substantially across technological platforms, tissue types, and biological contexts, and thus need personalized adaptation of model training conditions. To address this, we integrated hyperparameter optimization into the training pipelines of scAdam and scEve (Fig. 3A, left section). Using the Optuna framework, the system automatically tunes 20 hyperparameters to best suit the specific tissue, technology, or experimental design [32].

Further, scAdam also implements a warm-start training, enabling users to fine-tune pre-trained models on new datasets (Fig. 3A, right section). This feature significantly improves the generalizability of the model across different datasets, technological conditions, and sequencing platforms. Also, warm-start training is most helpful for iterative training on large, complex datasets that exceed local computational resources (especially RAM limitations).

To evaluate these optimization strategies, we used the same human PBMC dataset. We trained a default scAdam model on the same primary training dataset and applied a warm-start training using an additional (secondary) training dataset. We then performed hyperparameter tuning over 100 trials (independent training runs) using the same primary training dataset to identify the best set of hyperparameters and retrained scAdam with the tuned configuration. For evaluation, we used the same test datasets previously employed to benchmark scAdam against other automated annotation methods and applied the same metrics to compare the optimized models with the default scAdam.

Hyperparameter tuning led to a very small decrease in accuracy (−0.22%), precision (−2.36%), and F1-score (−0.36%). However, it improved performance on balanced accuracy (+1.61%), geometric mean (+0.92%), index balanced accuracy (+1.65%), and overall score (+0.21%). Warm-start training produced an increase in all metrics and overall score (Fig. 3B–E; Supplementary Fig. S12; Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, both optimization methods improved the identification of transcriptionally similar CD8+ T-cell subtypes at the celltype_l3 level (Fig. 3F–I). Collectively, these results demonstrate the utility of both strategies for refining scAdam’s performance.

We performed hyperparameter tuning over 100 trials (individual training runs) to identify the best set of hyperparameters for the same training PBMC dataset used for scEve default. Hyperparameter tuning of scEve training resulted in a notable improvement in scEve’s performance over all metrics except EVS when compared with the default model, Azimuth, and Seurat reference mapping (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S8B).

Thus, the scAdam and scEve models not only can be trained on new data but also can be improved using integrated optimization strategies. This is one of the critical advantages over methods such as Azimuth, Seurat, CellTypist, scGPT, TOSICA, scANVI, and Symphony, which lack built-in optimization.

scEve enables more accurate clustering, cell type annotation, and visualization of Tabula Muris Senis data

One of the largest projects aimed at analyzing the cellular composition of various organs is Tabula Muris Senis [16]. Our scEve-based reanalysis suggests that the existing cell annotations in the Tabula Muris Senis spleen dataset could be further refined. We applied the scEve model, optimized and trained on a multimodal dataset of mouse lymph nodes and spleen from 10x Genomics, to the Tabula Muris Senis spleen data. Using the resulting multimodal (observed RNA expression + imputed protein) data, we were able to significantly improve clustering, annotation, and visualization of cell types (Fig. 4A). With the help of imputed proteins, we could more accurately annotate myeloid cell subtypes. We found that cells originally labeled as “granulocytes” actually represent exclusively CD11b+ Ly6C+ Ly6G+ neutrophils (Fig. 4B, C). Mouse neutrophils are Ly6G+ cells, distinguishing them from basophils and eosinophils. Notably, the Ly6g gene expression is not detected by scRNA-seq, which prevented the Tabula Muris Senis authors from correctly annotating these cells. We used flow cytometry to validate these findings. These experiments corroborated the results from our surface protein imputation, supporting the identification of neutrophils using a panel consisting of CD11b, Ly6C, and Ly6G (Fig. 4D). In contrast, basophils are represented in the dataset as a small cluster of cells expressing canonical protein (imputed FcεRIα and CD49b) and gene (Mcpt8, Prss34) markers [33]. In the original annotation, they were assigned to a group of incorrectly annotated proerythroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S13A). Additionally, we discovered that cells labeled as “macrophages” in the original annotation actually comprise a mixture of macrophages, monocyte–dendritic cell progenitors (MDPs), classical dendritic cells (cDCs), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). We analyzed the representation of imputed proteins from a classical panel of surface markers for mouse spleen macrophages, i.e. CD11b, CD172a, F4/80. We found that only a subset of the original “macrophages” express these markers, which we also confirmed using flow cytometry (Fig. 4B, C, E) [34]. Dendritic cells (DCs) were annotated using canonical protein and gene markers Ly6G– SiglecH+ Bst2+ for pDCs and Ly6G– Xcr1+ Clec9a+ for cDCs [35]. MDPs were annotated using imputed CD115 and expression of marker genes (Cx3cr1, Kit, Flt3) (Supplementary Figs S14 and S15) [36].

Furthermore, the original annotation of “megakaryocyte–erythroid progenitors” was incorrect, as these cells express markers of more differentiated erythroid progenitor cells (imputed CD71, TER119, and Gata1, Tfrc genes) [37, 38]. The original B-cell annotation was largely correct, as confirmed by imputed proteins (CD19, CD21/CD35, IgD) and flow cytometry analysis (Supplementary Fig. S13). Cross-tissue cell type annotation and modality imputation using scParadise reveals novel NKT cell subtypes in human visceral adipose tissue

When analyzing scRNA-seq data from poorly characterized tissues, researchers often cannot use automatic cell type annotation or modality imputation because no suitable reference dataset is available. However, scParadise addresses this limitation by enabling both tasks not only within the training tissue, but also in cross-tissue settings. For instance, we used the scAdam model trained on human subcutaneous adipose tissue data from Liu et al. to predict cell types in a visceral adipose tissue atlas from Garritson et al. (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S16A) [19, 22]. The scAdam model was able to detect in visceral adipose tissue all cell types that were identified in subcutaneous adipose tissue, as confirmed by marker gene expression analysis (Fig. 5A, B; Supplementary Table S2). At the same time, the scAdam model labeled cells in clusters 2 and 11 as “Unknown”. These clusters contained mesothelial and mast cells, which were absent in the subcutaneous adipose tissue scRNA-seq atlas (Supplementary Fig. S16B–E).

Next, we extracted NK cells from the annotated visceral adipose tissue dataset and used a PBMC-based scEve model to impute proteins (Fig. 5A, C; Supplementary Fig. S17A, B). Using the imputed proteins, we annotated six subtypes of NK cells in visceral adipose tissue. We identified previously described subtypes of adipose NK cells based on CD56 and CD16 expression [40]. Additionally, we described novel, previously unreported subtypes of NKT cells using scEve imputed proteins: CD56dim CD3+ NKT, CD56dim CD3+ CD4+ NKT, CD56dim CD3+ CD8+ NKT (Fig. 5A, B). These cell types cannot be detected using RNA data alone. RNA expression allows identification only of the largest group of CD3+ CD8+ NKT cells without NCAM1 (CD56) expression, the major NK cell marker. Assignment of these cells to NKT cells is possible only based on other NK cell marker genes (NKG7, GNLY), but not on NCAM1 (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. S17B). Using flow cytometry, we confirmed the presence of these three subtypes of NKT cells in visceral adipose tissue. Furthermore, we also detected CD56+ CD3– CD8+ NK cells, which are also identified through protein imputation and appear as single cells in clusters of CD56+ CD16+/– NK cells (Fig. 5C, E; Supplementary Fig. S17C). However, due to their low abundance and distribution, they do not form a separate cluster. Increasing the number of cells may allow these NK cell subtypes to be identified as distinct clusters.

We also analyzed the potential role of the newly discovered NKT-cell subtypes in obesity. Using cell–cell communication analysis directed by differential gene expression, we found that in obese patients, there is deregulation of interactions between NKT cells and adipose progenitor cells (APCs) and myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. S18). In obese patients, NKT cells appear to enhance the pro-inflammatory activity of macrophages and other myeloid cells, primarily through tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-dependent signaling pathways (myeloid cell receptor genes involved are TNFRSF1A/B, NOTCH1, and FAS). Additionally, TNF produced by these cells probably promotes apoptosis (FAS) and inhibits adipogenic differentiation of APCs, thereby contributing to chronic inflammation in adipose tissue during obesity [41].

Cross-tissue cell type annotation and modality imputation using scParadise models accurately infer NK cell phenotyping that RNA data alone cannot resolve. Using the scAdam model trained on subcutaneous adipose tissue enabled rapid, accurate cell annotation without false assignment of known labels to unknown cell types. Applying a PBMC-trained scEve model to visceral adipose tissue made it possible to identify both previously described CD56/CD16 NK phenotypes and three new, previously unknown NKT subtypes (CD56dim CD3+, CD56dim CD3+ CD4+, and CD56dim CD3+ CD8+), confirmed by flow cytometry. These imputed-protein-defined subtypes exposed obesity-associated TNF-centric crosstalk between NKT cells and myeloid/APC compartments, consistent with heightened inflammation, apoptosis signaling, and impaired adipogenesis. Thus, scEve’s cross-tissue imputation both refines NK cell heterogeneity and uncovers mechanistic immune–stromal interactions relevant to obesity.

Cell type classification and protein abundance imputation benchmarking using scNoah

Automated cell type annotation and modality imputation methods for scRNA-seq are frequently evaluated inconsistently, typically relying on one test dataset and a limited set of metrics [4–7]. These practices fail to capture the full complexity of real-world data, resulting in substantial errors in the evaluation of method performance. Standardized and comprehensive benchmarking is therefore critical for effective comparison and further advancement in the field. To address this, we developed scNoah, a standardized benchmarking framework for evaluating automatic cell type annotation and modality imputation methods. First, scNoah allowed visualization of correct and incorrect annotations (Fig. 6A; Fig. 3G–I). Incorrect annotations made by automatic cell type annotation methods were mainly associated with transcriptionally similar cells, cell transition states, and rare cell types. For instance, B-cell subtypes at the celltype_l3 annotation level of the PBMC dataset exemplify transcriptionally similar cells. Lambda and Kappa B-cell subtypes differed primarily in the expression of immunoglobulin light chain genes: IGLC1, IGLC2, and IGLC3 for B lambda cells and IGKC for B kappa cells. The remaining genes were expressed at approximately the same level. An example of annotation errors associated with cell transition states was CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes in the celltype_l2 (or celltype_l3) annotation level (Fig. 3G–I). CD14+ monocytes gave rise to CD16+ monocytes via an intermediate state called CD14+ CD16+ intermediate monocytes [42, 43]. Absolutely accurate annotation of such transition states is impossible, since changes in the cell transcriptome during differentiation occur gradually. Small cell subpopulations often posed a challenge for automatic cell type annotation methods due to the focus on obtaining high accuracy. For example, most methods fail to handle ASDC annotation in PBMC celltype_l2 and celltype_l3 annotation levels. However, we solved this problem within scAdam models (Supplementary Table S1). Second, scNoah allowed rapid application of key performance metrics for cell type annotation prediction, such as total accuracy, balanced accuracy, and per-cell type precision, sensitivity, F1-score, geometric mean, and index balanced accuracy (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Table S1). In addition, scNoah allowed visualization of prediction performance using a confusion matrix (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Fig. S19A, B).

To analyze the quality of cell surface protein abundance imputation, scNoah implemented the correlation (PCC and SCC) and regression metrics (RMSE, Mean AE, Median AE, R2 score, and EVS) (Fig. 6D). In addition, all metrics could be visualized using cell embeddings, which allowed the detection of cells with low imputation quality. Using this method, we demonstrated that all metrics gave uniform imputation results among all cell types, except for a small proportion of platelets (Fig. 6E; Supplementary Fig. S19C).

scNoah also includes functions to determine the gene importance in the scAdam model’s prediction of a specific cell type. Important genes are those which carry the majority of weight in cell type determination by the scAdam model. Figure 6F demonstrated the eight most important genes for predicting CD16+ monocytes. Among these eight genes for identifying CD16+ monocytes, CD16 (FCGR3A) ranked second. Additionally, CDKN1C, RHOC, APOBEC3A, and MS4A7 were also recognized as markers of these cells. Conversely, MS4A6A and AC020656.1 could be considered negative markers that distinguished CD14+ monocytes from CD16+ monocytes (Fig. 6G; Supplementary Fig. S19D).

In summary, scNoah provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating cell type annotation and cell surface protein abundance imputation at the single-cell level.

Discussion

Here, we present scParadise, an open-source Python package containing three integrated tools for scRNA-seq analysis: scAdam for cross-tissue, multi-level cell type annotation with unknown cell type identification; scEve for cross-tissue modality imputation; and scNoah for benchmarking cell type annotation and modality imputation methods. By using these tools, scParadise addresses a main problem in modern biology: the gap between rapidly growing data volumes and the limited ability to convert these data into novel biological knowledge.

A central goal of scRNA-seq analysis is to resolve cellular identity, heterogeneity, and functional state. However, this process is often limited by three recurring problems: first, inaccurate cell type annotation due to cell type imbalance, donor-to-donor variability, and cross-technology, tissue, and experimental design differences; second, the lack of biologically decisive data, which can be caused by sparsity and high dropout rates; and last, evaluation variability, which can hinder reproducibility and cross-study comparability. Advances in finding accurate and efficient solutions to these challenges will reduce the time required to perform tedious, repetitive cell type annotation and experimental design reconciliation, which will then be used to address key questions about cell states, function, tissue organization, and disease.

scAdam directly targets the cell type annotation problem using cross-tissue multi-level hierarchical cell type annotation prediction that handles real-world data imbalance and improves robustness across datasets. In our benchmarking across human PBMC, pancreas, oropharyngeal cancer, and mouse aging brain datasets, scAdam outperforms widely used alternative methods and achieves more reproducible cell type annotation. Unlike other automated annotation methods, scAdam also supports unknown cell type identification, reducing a common and costly error: false assignment of known labels to unknown cell types. Such errors can prevent discovery of a new cell type or state that is a key driver of the investigated process (e.g. disease progression, cell differentiation, or tissue regeneration). Conversely, other methods may assign a known cell type to a cell, even though this annotation is incorrect (“falsely confident” annotation), leading to incorrect conclusions in subsequent analysis. The capability of scAdam for unknown cell type identification allowed us to accurately label cell types in the visceral adipose tissue dataset using cross-tissue cell type annotation with a subcutaneous adipose tissue-based scAdam model. It also prevented false assignment of known labels to mesothelial and mast cells that were unknown to the model.

scEve increases the information that can be derived from standard unimodal scRNA-seq data. scEve can accurately impute new modality in cases when direct multimodal profiling is expensive or unavailable. In our PBMC example, scEve improves the distinguishability of transcriptionally similar cell populations (T and NK cells) by adding protein-level information that refines clustering and phenotyping. scEve not only outperforms other modality imputation methods but also enables new discoveries that would be difficult to achieve with existing approaches. In the Tabula Muris Senis spleen dataset, scEve-imputed modality helped correct annotation errors that could not be resolved using RNA expression data alone. We validated these corrections using flow cytometry. In addition, scEve enabled the identification of previously unreported NKT-cell subtypes in human visceral adipose tissue via cross-tissue protein imputation, validated by flow cytometry. Together, these examples demonstrate that modality imputation is not a trivial feature but rather a tool for discovery that can uncover new scientific knowledge within cellular atlases, such as new cell types, immune phenotypes, and disease-associated interaction programs.

scNoah addresses another key limitation in the scRNA-seq analysis: the lack of standardized, fine-grained, and imbalance-sensitive evaluation that remains applicable over a wide range of different test data. By providing cell-wise and cell-type-wise metrics, confusion matrix analyses, and gene importance, scNoah makes the evaluation of methods transparent, thereby allowing us to understand where methods are failing, e.g. in rare cell types, transition states, and functionally similar cell types. This approach is opposed to using a single metric for evaluating the methods' overall performance. Such evaluation is critical in the field of biology since annotation errors are not uniform over the different cell types or states. Annotation errors tend to be concentrated in areas of particular interest for research, e.g. rare cell types, transition states, and transcriptionally similar cell types.

Overall, scParadise is not only a set of models with high performance but also an infrastructural tool for single-cell biology. scParadise reduces barriers between new data generation and inference, facilitates reproducibility across studies, and enables exploration of uncharacterized cellular states rather than systematically misannotating them. In this regard, the impact of scParadise and similar tools extends beyond accelerating single-cell analysis. The absence of such tools will bias biological findings toward cell features that are easiest to annotate, compromise cross-study reproducibility, and delay the translation of atlases into mechanistic understanding and experimentally tractable targets.

In future work, we will continue to improve our model hub, update existing tools with large datasets and sophisticated optimization strategies, develop new tools for addressing auxiliary problems in scRNA-seq, and continue moving towards a comprehensive open tool for single-cell analysis.

Limitations

Despite its high performance in modality imputation, scEve cannot identify unseen (novel for the model) features. scEve can predict only the modalities present in the training data. This makes scEve, like similar methods, heavily dependent on the quality of the training data. Additionally, if the training dataset contains major annotation errors or modality misassignments, these inaccuracies will be propagated to any dataset on which the scParadise models are applied. Despite modest resource consumption for model inference, training new models—and especially performing hyperparameter tuning—demands considerable computational resources. Therefore, we recommend carrying out model training and optimization with GPU acceleration, which is natively supported by scParadise.
