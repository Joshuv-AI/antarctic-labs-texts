Introduction

Post-publication interventions are implemented when a published article is considered inappropriate for inclusion in the scientific record. Importantly, two distinct types of interventions exist: (i) retraction, which removes the article from the literature due to serious issues, and (ii) correction, which addresses specific errors while retaining the article in the scientific record [1–2].

With the increasing volume of publications across various fields over recent years, both scientific misconduct and unintentional errors have become more prevalent. These errors may involve typographical mistakes, inaccuracies in author information, figures, references, or other sections of the article. In such situations, authors and editors must adhere to ethical scientific standards and promptly engage in the correction process. The presence of inaccurate information undermines the reliability and validity of scientific data [3–4].

Incorrect information in health-related research can particularly have direct consequences on patient treatment and health outcomes. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the publication rate in related fields surged significantly, leading to challenges such as the rapid dissemination of misinformation, disruptions in academic communication, and decreased data reliability. The scientific community must actively address these issues by ensuring that research findings are founded on sound evidence [3, 5–6].

The aim of this study is to identify common errors in articles published in the field of rheumatology and present these findings to readers. By highlighting these issues, we seek to enhance the credibility of scientific publications, prevent the dissemination of inaccurate information, and reduce the likelihood of similar mistakes in future research articles. Ultimately, our goal is to minimize errors and mitigate the associated loss of time and effort in future scientific endeavors.

Material-method

Articles published and subsequently corrected in the field of rheumatology from 2000 to 2024, indexed in the Web of Science (WoS), were evaluated. All articles categorized under “rheumatology” in the WoS database were extracted and saved for further analysis. These articles were filtered by document types labeled as ‘correction’ and ‘correction, addition’. The parties responsible for publishing these corrections—whether authors, editors, or publishers—were identified. The number of corrections per article was recorded, and the specific sections of the articles where corrections occurred were examined, viz., title, author information, abstract, keywords, key points, introduction, materials and methods, results, discussion, tables and figures, references, funding, ethics committee approval, conflict of interest, acknowledgements, supplementary files, digital object identifier (DOI), and copyright.

The severity of corrections was assessed and categorized as either major or minor corrections.

Minor corrections included articles with a small number of errors that did not affect the interpretation or conclusions. Examples of minor corrections are spelling errors, inaccuracies in author information, insignificant errors in table or figure headings, and numerical inaccuracies (2).

Major corrections were defined as those involving multiple errors that altered data interpretation and study outcomes.

A total of 2,220 articles and 78 original journal were initially evaluated, of which 305 were excluded due to irrelevance to the topic. Consequently, 1,915 articles were screened, 199 articles were identified labelled as corrections in WoS but could not be verified on journal websites or erratum sections due to broken links, paywalls, or archival inconsistencies, resulting in 1,716 articles ultimately being investigated (2). Results

A total of 1,825 errors were identified across the 1,716 corrected articles, with 36 articles requiring major corrections (Table 1). The most frequently observed errors involved author information, specifically author names and institutional affiliations, which accounted for 644 errors—the highest among all categories. The next most common errors occurred in tables and figures, with 537 corrections, followed by the results section, which required 164 corrections.

Discussion

Authors, editors, and publishers collectively share the responsibility of identifying errors that may affect data interpretation and the reliability of scientific publications. Minor spelling errors should ideally be reported in a numbered page of the journal issue in which the article appears. For online-only journals, errors should be made visible in subsequent issues. In PubMed, corrections addressing grammatical or typographical errors are linked to the original article as errata, ensuring citation continuity. While some journals may not formally publish minor spelling errors, corrections involving author names, financial disclosures, or errors causing potential confusion are generally issued as formal notices [7].

This study provides a detailed analysis of 1,716 corrected articles, highlighting both the frequency and nature of errors in the rheumatology literature. A total of 1,825 errors were identified, with 36 articles undergoing major revisions, including 19 retractions. On average, there were 1.06 corrections per article, of which 1,680 were minor and 36 (2%) were major. We analyzed the top five biomedical journals (based on 2024 impact factors) and found that major corrections were extremely rare in these journals (only 2 out of 36). Compared with Hauptman et al., who reported 2.4 errors per article with 24% major errors [8], the lower rate observed here may reflect increased editorial vigilance, advancements in plagiarism detection, and improved access to publications in articles published after 2000.

The most frequent errors involved author information, including names and institutional affiliations (644 errors). Accurate author information is fundamental for ensuring appropriate attribution, accountability, and reliable indexing within bibliographic databases. Errors in author details may compromise the traceability of scientific contributions and negatively affect citation tracking and institutional recognition. Previous studies evaluating post-publication corrections have similarly reported that inaccuracies in author metadata represent one of the most common causes of corrigenda and errata in biomedical journals [9, 10]. These findings suggest that weaknesses may exist not only in manuscript submission systems but also in editorial verification processes prior to publication.

Several factors may contribute to these types of errors. Multicenter studies, long study durations, and international collaborations may increase the likelihood of inconsistencies in author names, institutional affiliations, and institutional naming conventions [11]. Furthermore, institutional changes occurring during long-term studies may also lead to discrepancies between the originally submitted author information and the final published version. These findings highlight the importance of implementing stricter verification procedures during both manuscript submission and editorial production stages.

Errors in tables and figures were the second most common reason for corrections. Many of these errors involved numerical inaccuracies, such as misplaced decimal points, incorrect percentage values, or inconsistencies between tabulated data and the textual description of results. Alignment problems within tables or figures were also frequently observed. These issues may arise from manuscripts not fully adhering to journal formatting requirements or from insufficient editorial oversight during the typesetting and production stages [12, 13]. Because tables and figures are often used by readers to rapidly interpret study findings, even minor numerical inaccuracies may substantially influence the interpretation of results.

Similarly, errors identified in tables and figures (537 cases) and in the results section (164 cases) are particularly concerning because they directly affect the presentation and interpretation of scientific data. Inaccuracies in these sections may distort study findings and potentially influence clinical decision-making, guideline development, and future research directions [14]. Therefore, careful verification of numerical data and graphical presentation during peer review and editorial production is essential to maintain the accuracy and integrity of the scientific record.

When assessing errors in the results section, attention was given to whether corrections changed the article’s outcome. Some journals explicitly indicate this, facilitating evaluation. Common errors included incorrect numbers or decimal placement arising during data transfer or statistical analysis [15–16]. Such errors often result from carelessness, typically involving misplaced points or commas. Additionally, corrections related to drug efficacy values at specific time points in some studies suggested potential reporting bias, which can lead to overestimating efficacy and underestimating safety risks [17]. The high frequency of corrections in these sections suggests weaknesses in both the peer review and typesetting processes. Tables and figures often convey core findings, and errors therein may mislead readers, compromise data interpretation, and ultimately erode the article’s scientific integrity. Similarly, inaccuracies in the results section can significantly distort the study’s conclusions and its perceived contribution to the field. Financial disclosures also exhibited frequent errors. Of the 89 errors, many involved omitted information about institutions supporting the study [18]. Some authors in multicenter studies initially failed to disclose funding sources or company affiliations, which were added later [19]. Such inaccuracies in financial disclosures raise concerns regarding potential conflicts of interest [20]. Transparency regarding financial backing is a key aspect of research ethics; thus, any inconsistency in this domain could be perceived as a threat to the credibility of the research.

References require careful attention when drafting manuscripts. Among the 66 errors identified in this section, some manuscripts failed to cite necessary articles, while others referenced studies with questionable scientific reliability [21]. Institutional information or author affiliations within references were also occasionally inaccurate [22]. Likewise, the 66 corrections made to references point to a lack of rigor in manuscript preparation and editorial oversight, potentially affecting the verifiability and interconnectivity of scientific knowledge.

Incorrect DOI numbers were identified 16 times in our analysis, and none of these cases resulted in retraction, sometimes repeated within the same journal. Since DOI assignment and verification are handled during the production stage, these errors are primarily the responsibility of editors and publishers. Although they do not typically lead to retraction, misassigned DOIs can hinder article discoverability, disrupt citation tracking, and impair accurate linking of scientific records within digital databases [23, 24]. Contributing factors may include editorial workflow issues, simultaneous publication acceptance, or rapid production timelines. To reduce such errors, practical measures include automated DOI validation during typesetting or production, routine cross-checking against databases such as CrossRef, and strengthened editorial quality control at the final proofing stage [23].In contrast, other significant errors—such as inaccurate references (66 instances) and inconsistencies in the introduction section (32 instances)—are predominantly attributable to authors, reflecting lapses in careful manuscript preparation, literature verification, or citation formatting. Accurate referencing and a well-structured introduction are essential for proper attribution, supporting the validity of statements, and providing a clear rationale for the study. Grouping errors according to responsibility—author versus editor/publisher—highlights areas where targeted interventions, including author diligence and editorial verification, may most effectively reduce post-publication corrections.

Although fewer in number, certain errors raise significant ethical concerns. For example, the identification of seven plagiarism cases, five of which resulted in retraction, signals a serious breach of academic integrity. The presence of plagiarism in peer-reviewed literature not only undermines trust in the scientific process but may also artificially inflate the apparent productivity of individuals and institutions, thereby distorting academic evaluation metrics and potentially influencing funding opportunities. These findings highlight the critical importance of robust ethical screening during both the peer-review and editorial production stages. The routine use of plagiarism detection software prior to publication represents an essential safeguard against academic misconduct. In addition to plagiarism screening, editorial policies should emphasize verification of potentially high-risk sections of manuscripts, particularly the results, tables, and figures, where data manipulation or reporting inconsistencies may occur. Where applicable, the verification of raw data or supporting datasets may further strengthen the reliability and transparency of published research.Moreover, clear disclosure and careful editorial evaluation of conflicts of interest are essential components of research integrity. Strengthening ethical oversight through systematic screening procedures, transparent reporting standards, and enhanced editorial quality control may help reduce the occurrence of serious ethical violations in scientific publications. Additional areas such as conflict of interest disclosures (11 corrections), article titles (40 corrections), supplementary files (8 omissions), and abstracts (24 errors) further emphasize the multidimensional nature of the editorial and publication lapses. Errors in sections such as acknowledgements, keywords, abbreviations, and consent statements, though fewer in number, suggest a broader issue of editorial neglect or insufficient author compliance with submission guidelines. Most corrections (1,527) were made by authors, 154 by publishers, 14 by editors, and smaller numbers by consensus between parties, underscoring authors’ primary role in correcting errors while highlighting the need for greater editorial and publisher vigilance [25]. This suggests that many errors originate from the manuscript submission stage, possibly due to inadequate author diligence or a lack of familiarity with editorial standards. However, the fact that 154 corrections were attributed to publishers, and 14 to editors, reflects shared accountability across the publication ecosystem. Collaborative corrections, whether between editors and authors or between publishers and authors, though relatively rare, illustrate the potential benefits of joint error resolution. Unfortunately, not all journals disclosed the exact timing of the correction notice. For those that did, we observed a wide range, with most corrections (approx. 60%) issued within the first 3 months post-publication.

The most cited article was Bennett et al.’s 2009 validation of the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire Revised (FIQR), with 599 citations [26]. Other highly cited works included Smolen et al. (297 citations) and Yazıcı et al. (186 citations) [27–28]. These data are consistent with prior literature suggesting that post-publication corrections or retractions generally exert a limited impact on citation metrics, indicating that citation frequency is not necessarily reduced following corrections [29]. However, this observation underscores an important consideration: even widely cited and impactful articles are not immune to post-publication errors. While citation counts may remain high, uncorrected or insufficiently signaled errors can mislead readers and propagate inaccuracies in subsequent research. Therefore, ensuring that corrections are clearly and prominently indicated is crucial for maintaining scientific integrity. Practical strategies may include linking correction notices directly to the original article, displaying prominent alerts or flags in digital databases such as PubMed or CrossRef, and integrating metadata notifications to inform readers of updates. These measures would not only enhance transparency but also help readers and researchers accurately interpret findings, even in highly cited literature. By emphasizing both the visibility of corrections and the rigor of editorial oversight, journals can better balance scholarly impact with reliability, ensuring that high-impact articles do not inadvertently disseminate errors.

Overall, while scientific publishing quality continues to improve, further strengthening of quality control measures is necessary to reduce errors and maintain public trust. Editorial teams, reviewers, and publishers must continue developing robust systems to prevent and correct errors while fostering a culture of ethical research. The findings of this study reveal a pervasive issue with publication quality and editorial control in scientific journals. The high prevalence of errors in fundamental sections, such as author information, results, and figures, necessitates a re-evaluation of editorial workflows, peer review processes, and submission guidelines. Furthermore, the concentration of corrective responsibility on authors points to a need for enhanced training and accountability mechanisms. Given the ethical implications of issues such as plagiarism and conflict of interest omissions, greater emphasis must also be placed on ethical screening and compliance verification. Looking forward, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into manuscript preparation, peer review, and production workflows offers promising opportunities to further reduce post-publication errors. AI-based tools can assist authors in verifying references, ensuring consistency in author metadata, detecting numerical or alignment errors, and enhancing plagiarism checks. Similarly, AI can help editors and publishers by automating DOI validation, metadata verification, and formatting compliance checks. Incorporating AI solutions has the potential to improve efficiency, accuracy, and reliability in scientific publishing, reinforcing trust in scholarly communication. Ultimately, ensuring the accuracy, reliability, and integrity of scholarly communication requires coordinated efforts across authors, editors, and publishers. This study provides a compelling call for systemic improvements and sustained vigilance in the pursuit of publishing excellence.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the widespread occurrence of post-publication corrections across various sections of scientific articles, with author information, tables, figures, and results being the most frequently affected areas. The predominance of author-initiated corrections underscores the need for greater diligence during manuscript preparation, while the involvement of publishers and editors points to gaps in the editorial process. Although less frequent, ethically significant errors such as plagiarism and inaccurate disclosures remain critical concerns. These findings emphasize the importance of strengthening editorial oversight, improving author training, and fostering a transparent correction culture to maintain the reliability and credibility of scientific literature.
