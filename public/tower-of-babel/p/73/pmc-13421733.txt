Potential risk of capsular tension ring implantation combined with phacoemulsification in high myopia patients by increasing central macular thickness and intraocular lens tilt: a retrospective comparative study with 1-week, 1-month, and 3-month outcomes
Authors: Dong-Jun Chen, Zhuo-Wen Zhao, Jing-Shang Zhang, Meng Li, Hui-Ying Zhao, Xiu-Hua Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study investigated the effects of phacoemulsification combined with capsular tension ring (CTR) implantation on the central macular thickness (CMT) and intraocular lens (IOL) tilt in highly myopic eyes with cataract at 3 months postoperatively.MethodsA retrospective study analyzed 169 highly myopic eyes from 91 cataract patients, divided into two groups: the CTR group (47 patients, 86 eyes) underwent phacoemulsification with CTR implantation, and the control group (44 patients, 83 eyes) underwent phacoemulsification alone. Patients were further stratified by axial length (AL) into two subgroups: 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm and AL ≥ 30 mm. Postoperative outcomes, including refractive prediction error (RPE), CMT changes, IOL tilt, and decentration, were assessed at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months. Visual acuity was quantified using logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR).ResultsIn accordance with the principle that a higher LogMAR value indicates poorer vision, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved significantly in both groups compared with preoperative levels at 1 week (0.14 ± 0.23 vs. 0.29 ± 0.44), 1 month (0.14 ± 0.30 vs. 0.25 ± 0.38), and 3 months (0.11 ± 0.21 vs. 0.24 ± 0.39) postoperatively (p < 0.05). Notably, BCVA in the subgroup with AL of 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm was significantly better than that in the subgroup with AL ≥ 30 mm (p < 0.05). Additionally, no significant difference in RPE was observed between the two groups at any of the three time points (all p > 0.05). However, although IOL position (tilt and decentration) did not differ significantly between groups at 1 or 3 months overall, the CTR subgroup with 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm exhibited greater IOL tilt (5.275 ± 3.708°) than the control subgroup (3.846 ± 2.262°) at 3 months (p = 0.021). Similarly, while no overall intergroup difference in CMT was detected across time points, the CTR subgroup with 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm showed a significantly greater mean CMT change (8.831 ± 27.656 μm) compared with the control subgroup (−4.270 ± 32.486 μm) at 1 week (p = 0.046).ConclusionCTR implantation during phacoemulsification poses a potential risk in high myopia patients, particularly those with axial lengths between 26 mm and 30 mm, by increasing both CMT and IOL tilt.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-05050-x.Keywords: High myopia, Central macular thickness (CMT), Capsular tension ring (CTR), Cataract, Intraocular lens (IOL), Phacoemulsification, Refractive prediction error (RPE)

Introduction

The incidence of high myopia, defined as an axial length of ≥26 mm and a refractive error of ≤−6.00 diopters, has been increasing. Projections indicate that by the year 2050, approximately 5 billion individuals globally will be affected by myopia, with 1 billion of these cases classified as high myopia [1]. The prevalence of cataracts is significantly elevated among individuals with high myopia [2]. Eyes with high myopia exhibit distinct anatomical challenges, including a thinner sclera, elongated axial length, and compromised zonular support, which complicate cataract surgery and increase the risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications- such as posterior capsule rupture, intraocular lens (IOL) dislocation, and retinal detachment [3, 4]. These complexities underscore the need for specialized surgical techniques to achieve optimal outcomes in this patient population.

Phacoemulsification is widely recognized as the gold standard for cataract extraction, owing to its minimally invasive nature, shortened recovery time, and high success rates. In patients with high myopia, the procedure can be further optimized through implantation of a capsular tension ring (CTR), which enhances surgical safety [5]. CTRs, composed of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), are designed to stabilize the capsular bag, thereby redistributing capsular forces and minimizing the risk of IOL tilt or decentration [6]. Empirical studies suggest that CTR implantation may improve visual outcomes, enhance IOL stability, and reduce complications in eyes with compromised zonular support [7–9]. However, there remains a significant paucity of robust, prospective clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of CTR implantation, particularly in highly myopic patients undergoing cataract surgery. This evidence gap highlights the need for evidence-based guidelines tailored to this high-risk population [6, 9–11].

Furthermore, whether CTR implantation in highly myopic eyes contributes to refractive prediction error (RPE) remains controversial [9]. Phacoemulsification cataract surgery carries a risk of postoperative macular edema, with reported incidence varying by detection method: clinical ophthalmoscopy yields rates of 0.1–2%, whereas optical coherence tomography (OCT) or fluorescein angiography reveals incidences of 9–19% [12]. To date, few studies have investigated the impact of CTR implantation during phacoemulsification on postoperative central macular thickness (CMT) or its potential role in mitigating macular edema in highly myopic patients—thus limiting our understanding of its effect on visual outcomes.

This study aims to address existing research gaps by evaluating the clinical outcomes associated with the combined use of phacoemulsification and CTR implantation in patients with high myopia and cataracts. Through a systematic analysis of visual acuity, CMT, IOL stability, and complication rates, this research endeavors to offer valuable insights into the safety and efficacy of CTR application within this specific patient population. Our results showed that CTR implantation with phacoemulsification is safe and effective in patients with AL > 30 mm; however, it increases the potential risk in patients with AL of 26–30 mm for causing an increase in IOL tilt at 1 month and CMT at 1 week. This study is expected to contribute to the development of evidence-based surgical strategies, thereby improving cataract management in highly myopic patients and enhancing their postoperative quality of life.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a single-center retrospective investigation carried out at Beijing Tongren Hospital between February 2023 and July 2023. Approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee at Beijing Tongren Hospital (Reference number: TRECKY2020-069). All study procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee at Beijing Tongren Hospital, and no private patient information was disclosed.

Patient recruitment

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Participants must be over 18 years of age; (2) Preoperative refractive error must be less than −6.0 diopters (D); or (3) Axial length must exceed 26 mm; and (4) Emery–Little lens nuclear hardness must be graded between II and IV.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Presence of lens dislocation or subluxation; (2) History of prior intraocular surgery; (3) History of ocular trauma; (4) Active ocular inflammation; (5) Corneal pathologies impacting corneal curvature measurements; (6) Eyelid disorders, conjunctival diseases, corneal pathologies, glaucoma, or uveitis; (7) Presence of other ocular or systemic diseases that may interfere with examinations; (8) Poor compliance or loss to follow-up; (9) Contraindications for cataract surgery. All enrolled patients underwent preoperative anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) using the CASIA 2 device (TOMEY Corporation, Japan) to rule out lens subluxation, dislocation, or other structural abnormalities of the anterior segment. Preoperative ocular biometric measurements were acquired using the IOLMaster, and the intraocular lens (IOL) power was calculated using the Barrett Universal II formula.

The indications for CTR implantation included: (1) Lens subluxation; (2) High myopia with axial elongation and capsular bag laxity; (3) Glaucoma, specifically primary angleclosure glaucoma with zonular laxity; (4) Hereditary disorders with zonular abnormalities (including Marfan syndrome, Marchesani syndrome, retinitis pigmentosa, etc.); (5) Pseudoexfoliation syndrome.

CTR and IOL parameters

The CTR used in this study (ACPi-11, Weilang Medical Co., Ltd., Poland) has a diameter of 11 mm, and the implanted intraocular lens (P-PCF60/A, Henan Universe IOL Research and Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China) has a diameter of 12.5 mm. The length of the CTR varies according to the axial length. Due to its larger diameter, the IOL haptics are relatively flexible and may become entangled with or interfere with the CTR ring or its terminal eyelets within the capsular bag, potentially leading to IOL tilt. In addition, contraction of the capsular bag at three months postoperatively may also contribute to IOL tilt during this period. The positional relationship between the IOL haptics and the CTR ring or terminal eyelets is difficult to verify in vivo; therefore, this hypothesis requires further investigation.

Surgical procedures

All surgical procedures were performed by the same experienced ophthalmologist (XHW) in accordance with established protocols. In the Control group, with the exception of the omission of CTR implantation, all other surgical steps were identical to those executed in the CTR group, as detailed below.

A 0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution was administered to the operative eye two to three times to achieve surface anesthesia. Standard disinfection and draping procedures were conducted, and an eyelid speculum was employed to maintain eyelid retraction. A 3.0-mm clear corneal main incision was created at the 11 o’clock position, and a side incision was made at the 3 o’clock position using a 15° stab knife. Viscoelastic material was introduced into the anterior chamber via the main incision, followed by performance of a continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis using capsulorhexis forceps. Hydrodissection and hydrodelineation of the lens were performed using a curved cannula syringe. The lens nucleus and residual cortex were emulsified and aspirated via phacoemulsification. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of viscoelastic was injected into the lens capsular bag, and a capsular tension ring (CTR, ACPi-11, Bausch & Lomb, Poland) was implanted (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, a hydrophilic acrylic foldable posterior chamber IOL (P-PCF60/A, Henan Universe IOL Research & Development Co., Ltd., China) was implanted into the lens capsular bag (Fig. 1B). The position of the intraocular lens was adjusted, and the viscoelastic substance was aspirated from both the anterior chamber and the lens capsular bag. The clear corneal incisions were allowed to self-seal. Postoperatively, a suitable quantity of tobramycin and dexamethasone ophthalmic ointment was administered to the conjunctival sac of the operated eye, which was subsequently covered with sterile gauze.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes assessed included postoperative changes in RPE and CMT at intervals of 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months, in addition to IOL tilt and decentration evaluated at 1 month and 3 months. Secondary outcomes comprised postoperative BCVA, mean central corneal curvature, and AL, measured at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months. Postoperative refraction examinations were conducted for all patients at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months by the same experienced optometrist. The RPE was determined at each postoperative visit by calculating the difference between the preoperative predicted spherical equivalent (SE) and the actual postoperative SE.

CMT was assessed at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months following surgery using posterior segment OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Germany), conducted by the same skilled technician. The change in CMT was determined by subtracting the preoperative values from the postoperative measurements. Additionally, all subjects underwent anterior segment OCT scanning with the CASIA2 system (TOMEY Corporation, Japan) at 1 and 3 months post-surgery to evaluate IOL tilt and decentration. The system captures 128 cross-sectional images at 1.4° intervals to provide comprehensive anterior segment imaging. For IOL assessments, the scan mode was tailored to IOL-specific parameters, enabling the CASIA2 system to produce anterior segment tomograms from eight distinct angles. The integrated SS-2000 analysis software automatically delineated IOL contours, established reference lines for tilt and decentration assessments, and conducted three-dimensional (3D) analyses to ascertain precise IOL tilt and decentration metrics (Fig. 2).

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) protocol

The fundus of the study eye was scanned using Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg). Before the scan, the pupils of the subjects were dilated, and they maintained fixation. Cross-sectional images of the central retina and macular area were collected. At least three repeated scans were performed on each eye to ensure image quality. The images were independently evaluated by two experienced readers, and the main analyses included retinal thickness, lesion extent, and structural abnormalities.

CASIA2 protocol for IOL tilt and decentration

CASIA2 is a new type of non-contact anterior segment swept-source optical coherence tomography (AS-SS-OCT) device. This device uses a scanning laser with a wavelength of 1310 nm and performs high-frequency axial scanning of the fine structures of the anterior segment of the eye. The scanning width is 16 mm, the scanning depth is 13 mm, the axial image resolution is 1 μm, and the lateral image resolution is 30 μm. It rapidly acquires three-dimensional stereoscopic imaging from the anterior surface of the cornea to the posterior surface of the lens.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Measurement data following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation ( ± s), with comparisons made using the independent-samples t-test. Data exhibiting a skewed distribution were reported as median (P25, P75), and comparisons were executed via the Wilcoxon test. Categorical data were represented as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons performed using the chi-square test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and preoperative characteristics in the two groups

A total of 169 eyes from 91 patients were included in the study: 86 eyes from 47 patients in the CTR group and 83 eyes from 44 patients in the Control group, as detailed in Table 1. Among these, right-eye data were available for 86 patients (43 in the CTR group and 43 in the Control group; Supplementary Table 13), and left-eye data were available for 83 patients (39 in the CTR group and 44 in the Control group; Supplementary Table 16). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups concerning gender distribution, preoperative intraocular pressure (IOP), corneal endothelial cell density, average central corneal curvature, anterior chamber depth (ACD), AL, refractive error (spherical equivalent, SE), CMT, and lens nuclear hardness (all p > 0.05). However, patients in the CTR group were significantly younger than those in the Control group (mean age: 57.47 ± 11.08 years vs. 60.71 ± 7.89 years; p = 0.029). Furthermore, the BCVA was significantly better in the CTR group than in the Control group across all analyses: overall (0.57 ± 0.36 vs. 0.88 ± 0.65, p < 0.001), right eyes (0.64 ± 0.40 vs. 0.93 ± 0.69, p < 0.001), and left eyes (0.50 ± 0.29 vs. 0.82 ± 0.60, p < 0.001).

Changes in RPE, IOL tilt, IOL decentration and CMT in the two groups

In the analysis of primary outcomes, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding changes in RPE, IOL tilt, IOL decentration, and CMT changes at any postoperative visits (Table 2; all p > 0.05). Supplementary Table 1 presents a subgroup analysis of the primary outcomes, stratified by axial length (AL), specifically within the subgroups 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm and AL ≥ 30 mm. Within these subgroups, no significant differences were found in RPE and IOL decentration between the two groups at any postoperative visit (all p > 0.05). However, in the 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm subgroup, the IOL tilt was significantly greater in the CTR group than in the Control group at the 3-month postoperative visit (5.275 ± 3.708 vs. 3.846 ± 2.262, p = 0.021); no significant differences were observed at other visits within the same subgroups (all p > 0.05). Additionally, CMT changes were significantly higher in the CTR group than in the Control group at the 1-week postoperative visit in the 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm subgroup (8.831 ± 27.656 vs. 4.270 ± 3 2.486, p = 0.046); no significant differences were observed at other visits within the same subgroups (all p > 0.05). Analysis of the right -eye data revealed no significant differences between the two groups in RPE, IOL tilt, IOL decentration, or CMT changes at any postoperative visits (Supplementary Table 14; all p > 0.05). Within the subgroups 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm and AL ≥ 30 mm, no significant differences were found in RPE or IOL decentration between the two groups at any postoperative visit (Supplementary Table 5; all p > 0.05). However, in the 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm subgroup, the IOL tilt was significantly greater in the CTR group than in the Control group at the 3-month postoperative visit (−0.032 ± 0.366 vs. 0.377 ± 0.706; p = 0.017); no significant differences were observed at other visits within the same subgroups (all p > 0.05). In the analysis of left-eye data, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in RPE, IOL tilt or CMT changes at any postoperative visit (Supplementary Table 17; all p > 0.05). The IOL decentration was significantly greater in the CTR group than in the Control group at 1 month and 3-month postoperative visit (0.139 ± 0.360 vs. −0.021 ± 0.281, p = 0.033). Supplementary Table 9 presents that subgroup analyses based on axial length (26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm and AL ≥ 30 mm) revealed no significant differences between the two groups in RPE or IOL tilt at any of the postoperative visits (all p > 0.05). However, in the 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm subgroup, the IOL decentration was significantly greater in the CTR group than in the Control group at the 1-month postoperative visit (0.152 ± 0.307 vs. −0.074 ± 0.208, p = 0.005); no significant differences were observed at other visits within the same subgroups (all p > 0.05). Additionally, CMT changes were significantly higher in the CTR group than in the Control group at the 1-week postoperative visit in the 26 mm ≤ AL <30 mm subgroup (10.933 ± 24.400 vs. −8.053 ± 30.918, p = 0.021); no significant differences were observed at other visits within the same subgroups (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we found that although CTR implantation combined with phacoemulsification is safe and effective in patients with AL > 30 mm, it increases the potential risk of IOL tilt at 1 month and increased CMT at 1 week in patients with AL between 26 and 30 mm. The management of cataracts in highly myopic eyes poses distinct surgical challenges attributable to specific anatomical variations, such as deeper anterior chambers, thinner posterior capsules, and frequent zonular abnormalities [13–15]. Our study offers significant insights into the outcomes of phacoemulsification with CTR implantation in these complex cases, with particular emphasis on the role of CTR implantation. Our findings indicate that CTR implantation is safe and does not significantly influence RPE or CMT changes in the majority of cases. To a certain extent, our results are consistent with previous evidence in the literature [9, 16]; however, CTR implantation combined with phacoemulsification presents potential risks in patients with AL ranging from 26 to 30 mm, as it may lead to increased CMT at one week postoperatively and increased IOL tilt at three months.

The heightened variability in RPE among eyes with AL ≥ 30 mm highlights the well-established challenges associated with IOL value calculation in cases of extreme high myopia. This variability is likely attributable to several factors, including the diminished precision of standard formulas in eyes with elongated axial lengths, challenges in estimating effective lens position, and potential postoperative shifts in IOL position due to capsular bag dynamics in myopic eyes [17]. These findings underscore the necessity for developing optimized calculation formulas specifically tailored for high myopia. The present study contributes to the ongoing discourse on the utility of CTR in high myopia by introducing additional complexity. Although no significant differences were observed in overall IOL stability metrics, the increased IOL tilt noted in the subgroup with axial lengths between 26 mm and 30 mm at three months postoperatively suggests potential interactions between the device and ocular structures. This observation may be attributed to several factors, including the relative crowding of the capsular bag in moderately elongated eyes, mechanical interactions between the CTR and IOL haptics, and progressive capsular contraction during postoperative remodeling [18]. Consequently, in this particular cohort, these factors are especially pertinent when considering the implantation of premium IOLs, where even slight decentration or tilt can substantially affect optical quality [19–21].

Although our study found that the CTR implantation carries a potential risk in patients with AL between 26 and 30 mm, namely, increased CMT and IOL tilt, this risk is closely associated with the interaction among CTR design, IOL haptics, and capsular bag anatomy. In terms of structural compatibility, the diameter, elastic modulus, and fixation method of the CTR directly determine its annular supportive effect on the capsular bag. A high degree of matching between the CTR diameter and the actual anatomical diameter of the capsular bag can reduce capsular contraction and irregular deformation, thereby providing a stable intra-capsular space for the IOL. In contrast, the material (e.g., polymethyl methacrylate, polyimide) and design type (e.g., C-loop, L-loop, plate haptic) of IOL haptics affect their contact mode with the capsular bag wall and the CTR. The elasticity and distribution of haptics support points regulate IOL centration within the capsular bag, exerting either synergistic or antagonistic effects with the CTR’s annular support. Meanwhile, the basic anatomical characteristics of the capsular bag (e.g., capsular bag diameter, size of the anterior capsulorhexis, zonular tension) constitute the foundation for these interactions. In eyes with insufficient zonular function or asymmetric capsular bags, the compensatory supportive effect of the CTR and the adaptability of IOL haptics will change significantly, predisposing to uneven stress on the IOL and subsequent tilt.

The transient increase in CMT observed in the subgroup with axial lengths between 26 mm and less than 30 mm during the early postoperative period represents a noteworthy finding. We propose that the anterior displacement of the capsular bag complex, induced by the CTR, may modify vitreous dynamics, potentially generating subtle tractional forces on the macula (see Supplementary Figure 2). This phenomenon appeared to be self-limiting and was not observed in eyes with longer axial lengths, possibly due to the larger capsular dimensions and vitreous cavity volume associated with extreme myopia. Although this finding did not correlate with visual outcomes in our study, it merits consideration in cases with preexisting macular pathology.

The observed enhancement in visual acuity across all study cohorts substantiates the effectiveness of contemporary cataract surgery techniques in patients with high myopia. Nonetheless, the markedly inferior visual recovery in eyes with AL ≥ 30 mm is consistent with existing literature [22], and likely represents the cumulative impact of progressive retinal and choroidal degeneration associated with extreme axial elongation [23–26]. Our results suggest that CTR implantation in high myopia cataract surgery was generally safe but requires careful case selection. For eyes with AL between 26 and 30 mm, surgeons should weigh the potential benefits of zonular support against the possible risk of IOL malposition, particularly when implanting premium IOLs. Several considerations may optimize surgical outcomes, including preoperative assessment of capsular dimensions, selection of appropriately sized devices, consideration of alternative zonular support options in borderline cases, and enhanced postoperative monitoring for IOL position changes.

Despite the potential benefits of CTR implantation in enhancing capsular bag stability and improving IOL position, several considerations warrant a balanced discussion. First, the implantation of a CTR adds an additional step to the surgical procedure, which may increase surgical complexity and operative time, particularly for less experienced surgeons. Second, while rare, potential complications associated with CTR use should not be overlooked. These include the risk of anterior capsular phimosis, posterior capsular opacification, or even the “CTR sandwich”, wherein the IOL haptic becomes trapped between the CTR and the capsule, potentially leading to IOL tilt or decentration. Third, the effect of CTR may vary significantly due to individual patient factors, such as the degree of zonular weakness, axial length, and the specific design and material of both the IOL and the CTR. While our findings support the overall efficacy of CTR in highly myopic eyes, they also underscore that anatomical and functional outcomes are multifactorial. The decision to implant a CTR should be individualized, weighing the anticipated benefits against the potential for increased surgical complexity and device-related interactions. We acknowledge that the three-month follow-up period is relatively short for assessing longterm IOL stability and capsular bag dynamics. Whether the observed small differences in IOL tilt and CMT in the AL 26–30 mm subgroup persist beyond three months—or lead to lateonset complications such as capsule contraction syndrome or progressive IOL decentration, remains unknown. Longer prospective studies with followup of at least 12–24 months are needed to address these questions.

This study is subject to several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the single-center design may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Second, the sample size within AL subgroups was moderate, and the follow-up duration was relatively short, potentially affecting the assessment of long-term stability. Third, the study did not include a quantitative assessment of zonular integrity. Fourth, we recognize that the significant difference in preoperative age between groups represents a potential source of confounding, and this has been acknowledged as a study limitation. Nevertheless, the findings remained robust after statistical adjustment. Despite these limitations, our findings offer valuable insights for surgeons handling these complex cases. Future research utilizing advanced imaging technologies, such as intraoperative OCT [27], could provide a deeper understanding of the mechanical interactions between CTR and IOL during surgery. Moreover, the development of tailored CTR designs specifically for highly myopic eyes may reduce device-related complications while preserving the advantages of capsular stabilization.

Conclusion

Although the implantation of a CTR during phacoemulsification has been shown to be a safe and effective procedure for patients with high myopia, it carries a potential risk in patients with AL between 26 and 30 mm, namely, increased CMT and IOL tilt.
