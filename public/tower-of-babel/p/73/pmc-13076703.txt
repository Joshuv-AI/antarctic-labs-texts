Identification and validation of prognostic genes associated with M2 macrophage and heme metabolism in lung adenocarcinoma through bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing analysis
Authors: Jing Li, Xuehong Bai, Jingying Long, Jing wang, Chuxin Zhang, Hongliang Wang, Ruiya Hei, Jia Ma, Lijun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch has demonstrated the pivotal role of M2 macrophages and heme metabolism in cancer development. This study aimed to identify and validate prognostic genes linked to M2 macrophages and heme metabolism-related genes (HMRGs) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), offering valuable insights for the advancement of targeted therapeutic strategies.MethodsLUAD-related datasets were sourced from public databases. Initially, prognostic genes were ascertained through immune infiltration, correlation analysis, and regression analyses. A risk model was subsequently developed and evaluated. Finally, somatic mutation analysis, immunotherapy assessment, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were conducted.ResultsIn this study, EPB41, ACP5, PPOX, RBM38, and TRIM58 were identified as prognostic genes for LUAD. A risk model was developed to stratify patients into high-risk groups (HRG) and low-risk groups (LRG), with HRG patients demonstrating poorer survival outcomes. Somatic mutation analysis revealed that TP53 (49%) and TTN (44%) were the most frequently mutated genes in both HRG and LRG samples. Furthermore, B cells, M2 macrophages, natural killer cells (NK), CD8 T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) exhibited significant higher infiltration in HRG samples, whereas CD4 T cells exhibited notably lower infiltration in HRG samples. Additionally, ACP5 was found to have strong positive correlations with all immune checkpoints. Finally, scRNA-seq identified 14 annotated cell types, with ACP5 showing distinct expression in M2 macrophages.ConclusionThis study identified five prognostic genes for LUAD, offering valuable insights that could contribute to the development of prognostic markers and targeted therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04742-6.

Introduction

Lung cancer ranks as the primary cause of cancer-related fatalities globally, with the highest incidence and mortality rates compared to other types of cancers [1]. Among its subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent, representing approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases [2]. Despite remarkable progress in diagnosing and treating lung diseases, the prognosis for LUAD patients is still unfavorable, characterized by persistently high mortality [3, 4]. This is especially true for patients with advanced-stage lung cancer, who face a dismal 5-year overall survival rate of less than 20% [5]. In light of these challenges, identifying novel prognostic genes for LUAD is crucial, as they have the potential to notably improve both treatment strategies and patient outcomes.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is crucial for tumor invasion and metastasis [6]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are the most prevalent inflammatory cells in the TME, are significantly involved in the development and progression of various types of tumors [7, 8]. Macrophages are capable of differentiating into M1 or M2 phenotypes based on exposure to different cytokines, with each phenotype having unique functional characteristics [6, 9, 10]. Notably, exosomes derived from M2 macrophages are internalized by LUAD cells, promoting cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis [6]. Additionally, accumulating evidence highlights the critical role of heme metabolism within the TME, with heme accumulation enhancing the proliferative and invasive capacities of tumor cells [11, 12]. This process is strongly associated with macrophages. For instance, a recent study showed that active heme metabolism suppresses macrophage phagocytosis through the TLR4/Type I IFN signaling/CD36 pathway in uterine endometrial cancer [13]. Furthermore, research indicates that the absence of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1, encoded by Hmox1) in macrophages impedes tumor growth [14]. Despite these findings, the interaction between M2 macrophages and heme metabolism in LUAD remains largely unexplored. In light of this, our study aimed to further investigate the roles of M2 macrophages and heme metabolism in LUAD, thereby providing a foundation for potential therapeutic and prognostic strategies.

This study utilized data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, http://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) databases and performed a series of bioinformatics analyses to ascertain prognostic genes associated with M2 macrophages and heme metabolism in LUAD. Relied on these obtained prognostic genes, we constructed and assessed a prognostic model for LUAD, categorizing patients into high-risk (HRG) and low-risk (LRG) groups. Furthermore, we conducted additional analyses, including the development of a nomogram, clinical feature analysis, immune infiltration analysis, somatic mutation analysis, and drug sensitivity analysis, to explore the differences between HRG and LRG LUAD patients. Notably, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis was executed to further investigate the expression of prognostic genes in the cell levels. Overall, these findings offered valuable theoretical support for the treatment and prognosis of LUAD.

Materials and methods

Data collection

In this study, transcriptomic, clinical, and survival data for LUAD were mined from the TCGA and GEO databases. Specifically, the TCGA-LUAD dataset included 517 LUAD and 59 control tissue samples (494 LUAD patients with the complete survival information), while the GSE31210 dataset (GPL570 platform) contained transcriptomic data from 246 tissue samples (226 LUAD samples and 20 control samples). Additionally, a scRNA-seq dataset, GSE131907 (GPL16791 platform), was retrieved from the GEO database, which included 58 tissue samples (11 LUAD samples and 11 control samples).

The 200 heme metabolism related gene (HMRGs) were gained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb) (Supplementary Table S1).

Assessment the M2 macrophage infiltration in LUAD

In TCGA-LUAD dataset, CIBERSORT algorithm from “IOBR” package (v 0.99.9) [15] was applied to fully calculate the infiltration score of M2 macrophage. The samples, with P > 0.05, were removed. Based on the median of infiltration score, LUAD patients were clearly divided into high- and low-infiltration groups. Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier (K–M) analysis was executed employing the “survival” package (v 3.8–3) [16] to assess the survival differences between two infiltration groups (P < 0.05).

Following this, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was executed to further explore functional enrichment of two infiltration groups. Differential expression analysis was first conducted employing “limma” package (v 3.56.2) [17] to identify DEGs (high-infiltration vs low-infiltration), ranking them based on their log2FC values, from highest to lowest. The HALLMARK gene set from MSigDB (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb) and used as the background gene sets for subsequent analysis. GSEA was then carried out to assess the enrichment of the ranked genes within this background set (P < 0.05). Besides, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was carried out to further investigate the biological functional differences between two infiltration groups. The Gene Ontology (GO) gene set, downloaded from MSigDB, served as the background. The enrichment scores of signaling pathways for each sample were assessed applying “GSVA” package (v 1.46.0). The “limma” (v 3.56.2) was subsequently applied to compare functional pathway enrichment diversity between HRG and LRG samples (P < 0.05).

Identification and function exploration of important HMRGs associated with M2 macrophage

In the TCGA-LUAD and GSE131907 datasets, Spearman analysis was utilized to determine the correlation coefficients between the expression levels of HMRGs and the abundance of M2 macrophage infiltration (|cor|> 0.10 and P < 0.05). The genes with the significant correlation with M2 macrophage were separately identified in the TCGA-LUAD and GSE131907 datasets. Thereafter, the two sets of genes were overlapped to identify important HMRGs associated with M2 macrophage. The correlation heatmap of these important genes in TCGA-LUAD and GSE131907 datasets were plotted.

In addition, GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed to explore the biological roles and pathways associated with these important HMRGs, employing the “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.10.0) [18] (P < 0.05).

Construction and validation of risk model

In TCGA-LUAD dataset, univariate Cox regression analysis (hazard ratio (HR) ≠ 1, P < 0.05) was performed on important HMRGs to identify survival-related genes employing the “survival” (v 3.8–3). The HR for each gene associated with survival was computed, where an HR greater than 1 signifies a risk factor, while an HR less than 1 indicates a protective factor. Simultaneously, the LASSO method was also applied to the important HMRGs to ascertain feature genes employing the “glmnet” package (v 4.1–8) [19]. This method incorporated tenfold cross-validation and coefficients ≠ 0 to determine the optimal lambda value (lambda.min). Subsequently, prognostic genes were ascertained by overlapping the survival-related genes and feature genes. Then, multivariable Cox regression analysis was executed on prognostic genes.

A risk model was developed relied on the ascertained prognostic genes, with the risk score calculated employing the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$risk score=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(coefi*Xi)$$\end{document}

Here, “coef” represented the coefficients of the prognostic genes obtained from the multivariable Cox regression analysis, and “X” indicated their respective expression levels.

Thereafter, to better evaluate the performance of the risk model, 494 LUAD patients from the TCGA-LUAD dataset were stratified into a HRG and a LRG relied on the median of the risk score. Risk curves were clearly generated to visualize the distribution of risk scores, and scatter plots were created to illustrate the survival status of LUAD patients in both HRG and LRG. Heatmap was plotted to explore the expression levels of prognostic genes in HRG and LRG. K-M survival curves were then plotted employing the “survminer” package (v 0.5.0) [16] to assess survival differences between the HRG and LRG (P < 0.05). Additionally, 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted employing “survivalROC” (v 1.0.3.1) [20], and the area under the curve (AUC) was fully calculated to further evaluate the predictive accuracy of the risk model (AUC > 0.60).

To validate the robustness of the risk model, the same analyses were performed on the GSE31210 dataset via the identical methods and steps.

Construction and evaluation of a nomogram

In the TCGA-LUAD dataset, the risk score was merged with clinical parameters [age, gender, stage, T, N, M stages] for LUAD patients. Univariate (P < 0.05) and multivariate (P < 0.05) Cox regression analyses were then carried out to better determine the independent prognostic factors for LUAD patients. Subsequently, a nomogram was developed with “rms” package (v 6.7-0) [21] following these analyses, aiming to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for LUAD patients. To assess the predictive accuracy of the model, calibration curves were generated by “rms” package (v 6.7–0), decision curves were clearly plotted with “rmda” package (v 1.26) [22], and ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were fully plotted employing the “timeROC” package (v 0.4) [23]. Overall, these analyses offered a thorough assessment of the nomogram’s precision and clinical applicability in forecasting LUAD survival probabilities, relied on the ascertained independent prognostic factors.

Somatic mutation and drug sensitivity analysis

To further investigate the somatic mutation differences between HRG and LRG patients in TCGA-LUAD dataset, mutation frequencies were fully analyzed employing the “maftools” package (v 2.18.0) [24]. For each group, the 20 most frequently mutated genes were determined, and the results were further illustrated employing waterfall plots for clear visualization.

To evaluate drug sensitivity in HRG and LRG patients from the TCGA-LUAD dataset, IC50 values for common drugs were computed employing “oncopredict” package (v 1.21) [25]. Higher IC50 values typically indicated lower drug sensitivity. Wilcoxon test was then employed to compare IC50 differences between HRG and LRG patients for each drug (P < 0.05).

Immune infiltration and immune therapy analyses

Besides, to investigate immune cell infiltration in HRG and LRG, an immune infiltration analysis was performed. The QUANTISEQ algorithm [26] was employed to calculate infiltration scores for immune cell types across both HRG and LRG samples. Wilcoxon test was applied to compare the infiltration scores between HRG and LRG, pinpointing immune cell types with obvious differences (P < 0.05). Subsequently, correlation analysis was conducted employing the 6 algorithms (CIBERSORT, QUANTISEQ, EPIC, MCP-COUNTER, TIMER, XCELL) to explore the relationships between the infiltrated immune cell types and risk score (|cor|> 0.30, P < 0.05). Additionally, Spearman was executed to explore the correlation between immune checkpoints (ICs) and risk score, prognostic genes.

The scRNA-seq analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset GSE131907 was analyzed employing the “Seurat” package (v 5.2.1) [27]. Initially, quality control (QC) was executed to filter out low-quality cells and genes. Specifically, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes and genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were eliminated. Cells were retained if they met the following criteria: 200 < nFeature_RNA (genes per cell) < 6,000, nCount_RNA (total RNA count per cell) < 5,000, and percent.mt (proportion of mitochondrial gene expression) < 30%. After QC, the data were normalized employing the NormalizeData function. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were ascertained employing the FindVariableFeatures function. Next, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the HVGs with the RunPCA function. The number of principal components (PCs) to retain was determined employing the JackStrawPlot function (P < 0.05). Unsupervised clustering analysis was carried out employing the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. To avoid over-clustering, we chose resolution = 0.30 as the best resolution parameter that yields stable, biologically meaningful clusters (Supplementary Figure F). The RROGUE algorithm was used to identify the optimal resolution of 0.3 for cell clustering [28] (Supplementary Figure G). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for cluster visualization. Then, cell types were annotated relied on the TISCH database (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/home/).

Following this, the expression distributions of prognostic genes in annotated immune cells were displayed in UMAP plots.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

To explore the expression levels of these prognostic genes in clinical samples, IHC analysis was conducted according to the standard immunoperoxidase staining procedure. Specifically, a total of 15 tumor tissue samples (5 Lung squamous cell carcinomas (LUSC), 5 LUAD, and 5 small cell lung cancer) were acquired from the clinic in the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. All participants were given informed consent. The study had the approval of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University ethics committee (approval number: 2020–154). Initially, paraffin-embedded samples from 15 tumor patients were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded alcohol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked employing 3% hydrogen peroxide. The samples were then sealed with 10% universal goat serum to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2). An appropriate amount of biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody was applied to detect the primary antibodies. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used for color development, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Finally, the samples were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Whole-film scan analysis was conducted to evaluate the staining results.

The quantitative analysis of immunohistochemical staining results was conducted using image analysis software (ImageJ v1.8.0). Three non-overlapping bright-field views were randomly selected for each sample, and the Average Optical Density (AOD) was measured to represent the expression level of the target protein. The AOD was calculated using the formula (optional: AOD (%Area) = IntDen / Area). The final AOD value for each sample was determined as the average of the measurements from these fields of view. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Differences in AOD among groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out employing R (v 4.3.0). For further comparisons between two groups, Wilcoxon test was applied, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for comparisons involving more than two groups (P < 0.05).

Discussion

LUAD is a common subtype of NSCLC [29], and its treatment has significantly progressed with the advent of immunotherapy. The use of ICs inhibitors has obviously enhanced the prognosis situation for patients with advanced NSCLC [30]. Macrophages, a vital component of the immune system, can infiltrate the tumor microenvironment, contributing to tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression [31]. Among these, M2-type macrophages are particularly associated with a tumor-promoting phenotype, facilitating immune escape and drug resistance [32–34]. The polarization of M2 macrophages in the tumor microenvironment is often linked to tumor progression and immune evasion [35, 36]. Moreover, heme metabolism has been recognized as a key regulator in M2 macrophage polarization and function [37]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the roles of M2 macrophages and heme metabolism in LUAD remain unclear. To address this gap, our study applied bioinformatics methods to investigate genes associated with M2 macrophages and heme metabolism in LUAD, aiming to identify potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Through comprehensive analysis, we identified five prognostic genes for LUAD: EPB41, ACP5, PPOX, RBM38, and TRIM58. Using these genes, we developed a risk model to classify LUAD patients into HRG and LRG groups. Notably, patients in the HRG group exhibited poorer survival outcomes, underscoring the potential of these genes as prognostic indicators and therapeutic targets for LUAD.

The erythrocyte membrane protein band 4.1 (EPB41) is a key member of the cytoskeletal protein 4.1 gene family, which plays a critical role in various cellular processes, comprising cell adhesion, migration, division, and intercellular signaling [38, 39]. Emerging evidence highlights its significant involvement in cancer development, particularly in NSCLC [4, 40], hepatocellular carcinoma [41], and meningioma [42]. In NSCLC, EPB41 has been shown to suppress proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [4]. Notably, low expression of EPB41 correlates with poor prognosis in NSCLC patients. Our findings align with these observations, as we found EPB41 expression to be significantly downregulated in HRG patients, who also exhibited significantly lower OS. The underlying mechanism involves the disruption of the EPB41-ALDOC (aldolase) complex in NSCLC cells. When EPB41 expression is inhibited, ALDOC is released from the complex, leading to an increase in ALDOC levels. This, in turn, enhances the transcriptional activity of the β-Catenin/TCF/LEF transcription factor complex, which promotes the expression of multiple oncogenes [4, 43]. This cascade of events contributes to the aggressive behavior of NSCLC cells and underscores the potential of EPB41 as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target in this malignancy.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5 (ACP5) plays a critical oncogenic role in LUAD by regulating multiple key biological processes that facilitate tumor progression and metastasis. Several recent studies have underscored the importance of ACP5 in LUAD, demonstrating its potential as a crucial mediator in the disease's development and progression [44–46]. One of the primary mechanisms by which ACP5 exerts its effects is through the regulation of p53, a well-known tumor suppressor[47, 48]. ACP5 enhances the phosphorylation of p53 at Ser392, which in turn promotes its ubiquitination and degradation [49]. The decrease in p53 levels subsequently triggers an upregulation of SMAD3, a crucial regulator in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process. EMT is a fundamental mechanism that facilitates the invasiveness and metastatic potential of cancer cells [49, 50]. EMT promotion by ACP5-mediated SMAD3 upregulation is a likely explanation for the heightened metastatic capacity observed in LUAD cells, highlighting ACP5's role in driving tumor invasiveness. Furthermore, ACP5's impact extends beyond cell signaling. It has been shown to modulate the tumor microenvironment by driving the polarization of M2 macrophages [51, 52], which are associated with immune suppression and tumor promotion. This is accomplished through the upregulation of HO-1, a molecule that contributes to creating a favorable environment for tumor growth [53]. In our study, the presence of ACP5 in M2 macrophages, as confirmed by single-cell analysis, further underscores its significant role in fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports tumor progression. Cancer growth and progression are closely associated with immune suppression. Cancer cells possess the ability to activate various immune checkpoint pathways that exert immunosuppressive functions[54]. These immune checkpoints use distinct ligands to attenuate T-cell activity through multiple mechanisms [55]. In our cohort, ACP5 transcript levels were positively correlated with every queried checkpoint molecule, suggesting that ACP5 may act as a master coordinator of the immunosuppressive landscape. Additionally, ACP5 was identified as a protective factor in univariate Cox analysis. This paradox reflects the context-dependent behavior of well-characterized immune regulators. For instance, the functions of intrinsic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling can vary across different cancer types, influenced by its interaction with other activated signaling pathways [56]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and colorectal cancer, the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway suppresses tumorigenesis, whereas in melanoma, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), thyroid cancer (TC), glioblastoma (GBM), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), it promotes tumorigenesis [57]. Meanwhile, univariate Cox regression may not account for other influencing factors, such as the specific types of immune cells or the complexity of the tumor microenvironment. In the future, we aim to perform multivariate Cox regression analysis or combine other models for further validation. Overall, these findings suggest that ACP5 could be a promising therapeutic target in LUAD, with the potential to disrupt key processes involved in tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion.

Protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPOX) is a key enzyme in the synthesis of heme and chlorophyll, both essential for various biological processes [37]. In heme biosynthesis, PPOX catalyzes the oxidation of protoporphyrinogen IX (Proto) to protoporphyrin IX (PPIX), which is a critical step before the formation of heme [58]. As heme contains iron, PPOX indirectly influences iron metabolism through its role in heme synthesis [11, 59]. Beyond its metabolic functions, PPOX has been linked to cancer biology. Elevated PPOX expression in certain cancer cells has been associated with enhanced cell proliferation and growth. For example, studies have shown that inhibiting PPOX can significantly reduce the growth of colon cancer cells [59]. Despite these insights, the role of PPOX in LUAD remains underexplored. Our research reveals that PPOX is highly expressed in LUAD patients within the LRG. In contrast, low PPOX expression correlates with poorer prognosis in LUAD patients. These results indicate that PPOX could potentially act as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in LUAD, highlighting the need for further research into its mechanisms and clinical significance.

RNA Binding Motif Protein 38 (RBM38), a member of the RNA recognition motif (RRM) family, is an RNA-binding protein (RBP) [60]. It is involved in a range of biological processes, for instance cell proliferation, cell cycle regulation, cell differentiation, and DNA damage response [49, 61–63]. Studies have revealed that RBM38 often forms negative feedback loops with tumor suppressors (TS), like p53 family (i.e., p53, p63, p73), exerting pleiotropic effects in diverse pathological conditions [64]. Due to its abnormal expression in many malignant tumors, RBM38 is associated with high invasiveness, metastatic ability, and poor prognosis [65, 66]. For example, one study revealed that RBM38 can be a valuable tool for assessing the prognosis and immunotherapy potential in melanoma patients. This is attributed to RBM38's role in promoting the proliferation, invasion, and migration of melanoma cells, as well as its association with alterations in the immune microenvironment [66]. Besides, RBM38 not only acts as an important biological target but may also serve as a potential therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of NSCLC chemotherapy. It influences the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to cisplatin by regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and DNA damage [20]. Our study identified RBM38 as a prognostic gene for LUAD associated with M2 macrophages and heme metabolism, indicating its potential as a target for both treatment and prognosis in LUAD.

Tripartite-motif protein 58 (TRIM58) is a member of the TRIM protein family, which possesses E3 ubiquitin ligase activities [47]. In NSCLC, low expression of TRIM58 has been identified as a marker of poor prognosis in patients [67]. Shang et al. found that TRIM58 suppresses NSCLC by interacting with ZEB1 and promoting its degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (UPP) [68]. Our study further supports the role of TRIM58 as a prognostic gene for LUAD, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for this disease.

Moreover, our IHC analysis of clinical samples revealed significantly lower expression of ACP5, EPB41, and PPOX in NSCLC, LUAD, and lung squamous cell carcinoma tissues. This finding suggests that these genes may play key roles in cancer progression, particularly in LUAD. Additional research is required to investigate their underlying mechanisms and assess their potential as therapeutic targets.

Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis revealed that M2 macrophages are significantly more abundant in high-risk LUAD patients. As a subpopulation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), M2 macrophages are known for their anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive functions [69–72], which can promote tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. In our study, the high infiltration of M2 macrophages in high-risk LUAD patients suggests that M2 macrophages can promote tumor progression within the tumor microenvironment.

This study successfully identified five prognostic genes and validated their protein expression in clinical samples through IHC analysis. Additionally, we successfully developed a highly efficient risk model based on these five genes, which effectively differentiates HRG and LRG LUAD patients, with HRG patients exhibiting poorer prognosis. The study also conducted immune infiltration assessment, somatic mutation analysis, and clinical characteristics analysis, providing new insights into the treatment and prognosis of LUAD. Furthermore, through single-cell analysis, we explored the potential mechanisms of LUAD at the cellular level, offering a multi-dimensional reference for further research on the disease. However, this study has certain limitations: 1) a relatively small sample size, which may affect the generalizability of the findings, and 2) the lack of additional experimental validation, such as animal models, gene knockdown, and co-culture assays, due to resource constraints. To address these gaps, we plan to conduct future studies involving qPCR and Western blotting to assess the expression of prognostic genes, particularly ACP5, in LUAD cells co-cultured with M2 macrophages. In the future, we will continue to focus on LUAD research and further optimize and validate our findings.
