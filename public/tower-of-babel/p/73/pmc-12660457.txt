Patterns and Characteristics of Gabapentin Use Among Medicare Beneficiaries
Authors: GYeon Oh, Daniela C. Moga, Patricia R. Freeman, Erin L. Abner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGabapentin is increasingly prescribed to older adults, yet prescribing patterns and characteristics of gabapentin initiators remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of gabapentin initiators using a random sample of age-eligible fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries (2012–2021) enrolled in Parts A, B, and D. Gabapentin initiators were identified from pharmacy claims. We required 180 days of continuous enrollment (washout period) prior to initiation (index date) for inclusion. We analyzed demographics, healthcare utilization within 3 months of initiation, chronic conditions, medication history during washout period, and patterns of gabapentin use. Subgroup analyses compared initiators by duration of continuous gabapentin use (≤ 90 days, 91–180 days, and > 180 days).ResultsThe prevalence of gabapentin prescriptions increased over time, from 6.7% (2013) to 10.2% (2021). Among 247,612 gabapentin initiators (mean age 76.1 years, 61.5% female, 89.2% white), chronic pain (32.6%) was the most commonly documented condition, while epilepsy and postherpetic neuralgia, the approved indications for gabapentin, were documented in fewer than 0.5% of initiators. Among initiators, 38.9% had prior opioid use, and 13.2% were co-prescribed gabapentin and opioids at initiation. About 30% had a history of antidepressant use, predominantly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (17.2%). Subgroup analyses showed similar demographics and prescription patterns across subgroups. However, gabapentin initiators with > 180 days continuous use had more neuropathic pain and chronic condition diagnoses documented, fewer opioid co-prescriptions at index, and lower hospitalization rates.ConclusionsGabapentin was frequently prescribed, apparently off-label, in older adults with a high burden of chronic pain and comorbidities; initiators often had co-prescriptions of gabapentin with opioids. Future research is needed to investigate factors associated with extended gabapentin use (> 180 days) and its appropriateness in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40266-025-01255-9.

Introduction

Gabapentin was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat partial seizures and postherpetic neuralgia [1]; however, gabapentin is now widely prescribed for numerous off-label conditions [2]. In fact, gabapentin was the 7th top prescribed medicine in 2019 [3], despite limited evidence supporting its off-label efficacy and safety. Off-label conditions include neuropathic pain, migraines, substance use disorder, psychiatric symptoms (including anxiety and depression), and refractory chronic cough [2, 4–10]. In a commercially insured adult population, gabapentin prescriptions nearly doubled from 2009 to 2016 [11]. Similarly, a study using Danish healthcare registries found that the incidence of new gabapentin users quadrupled from 2010 to 2023 [12]. This increasing trend also was observed in American older adults, where participant-reported gabapentin use almost tripled from 2006 to 2019 among adults aged 65 years and older [13].

Despite its widespread off-label use, gabapentin’s efficacy and safety in older adults remain areas of concern. Placebo-controlled trials indicate that its efficacy in managing painful conditions is inconsistent or minimal [14, 15]. Notably, these trials included younger adults, leaving an additional gap in evidence specific to older adults. Emerging evidence also highlights gabapentin’s potential for misuse and dependence [16, 17], with motivations including misuse for euphoric effects, polydrug use, self-medication, and substitution for other drugs [18, 19]. Furthermore, gabapentin use has been linked to an increased risk of falls and fractures, which is particularly concerning in older adult populations [20–22]. We recently reported that gabapentin initiation among older adults with normal cognition was associated with cognitive and functional status decline, while those with prevalent cognitive impairment experienced a higher risk of falls [22, 23].

Concerns about the safety and the misuse potential of gabapentin resulted in reclassifying gabapentin as a Schedule V controlled substance medication in Kentucky (July 2017), followed by Alabama, Michigan, North Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, West Virginia, and Virginia, and its listing as a “drug of concern,” reportable to the state prescription drug monitoring program in 13 other states as of April 2023 [24]. In 2019, the FDA issued a warning about the potential risk of breathing difficulties associated with concurrent use of gabapentin and opioids or other central nervous system (CNS) depressants [25]. That same year, the American Geriatrics Society updated the Beers Criteria, adding gabapentin to the list of medications for which to avoid concurrent treatment with opioids [26].

Despite the potential risks, little is known about how gabapentin is used among older adults, including prescribing trends, patterns of use, and characteristics of gabapentin users. This study aimed to address these gaps by characterizing gabapentin initiators and examining usage patterns among older adults.

Methods

Data Source

We used a 5% random national sample of Medicare beneficiaries with Part D enrollment at any time from 2012 to 2021. For this study, data from annual Master Beneficiary Summary Files, Part D event files, chronic conditions data warehouse (CCW), outpatient claims, and Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MedPAR) files were incorporated. Beneficiaries were included if they were enrolled in Medicare Parts A, B, and D owing to age. We excluded the months when beneficiaries had health maintenance organization (HMO) enrollment or were dually eligible for Medicare and Medicaid (Fig. 1). Cohort Selection

Beneficiaries with gabapentin prescription were identified if they received at least one gabapentin prescription during their continuous enrollment period. Gabapentin prescriptions were identified using national drug codes (NDC) linked with Medi-span Generic Product Identifiers (GPI), excluding Gralise® Starter. Since the maximum recommended therapeutic dose of gabapentin is 3600 mg/day [27], prescriptions with an average daily dose (dose × quantity/days of supply) exceeding 10,000 mg/day (137 prescriptions) were excluded as likely data entry errors. To mitigate bias from prevalent users, this study included only beneficiaries initiating gabapentin, defined as having at least 6 months of continuous eligibility prior to the first date of gabapentin prescription (i.e., washout period) (Fig. 1). The first date of gabapentin prescription was defined as the index date.

Prevalence/Incidence of Gabapentin Use by Year

The prevalence of gabapentin use was measured annually from 2013 to 2021. Prevalence was calculated by dividing the number of beneficiaries with at least one gabapentin prescription during the year by the total number of eligible beneficiaries during that specific year. To calculate the incidence rate, the number of new gabapentin users was divided by the total number of eligible beneficiaries in the given year. The year 2012 was excluded owing to the 6-month washout period required to determine initiation.

Characterizing Gabapentin Use

The average number of days of supply from the first gabapentin prescription were calculated; if multiple prescriptions were present at the index date, the prescription with the longer supply was used. Similarly, the average daily dose from the first prescription was calculated. For beneficiaries with more than one prescription at the index, the average daily doses were summed across all prescriptions. To examine maintained gabapentin dosing, the average daily dose from the second prescription was calculated for beneficiaries with additional prescriptions following the index date.

Demographic information, including age, sex, and race, for gabapentin initiators was obtained from the Master Beneficiary Summary Files. To assess medical history during the 3 months prior to and including the index date—which may provide context for gabapentin prescription, given that data on prescribing reasons were not available—we identified diagnoses for the following conditions: acute pain, back pain, chronic pain, neuropathic pain, epilepsy, postherpetic neuralgia, migraine, depression, alcohol use disorder, and substance use disorder using MedPAR and outpatient files. These conditions were defined using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Table S1) located in the first and second positions of the diagnosis fields.

Similarly, emergency department (ED) utilization was identified if beneficiaries had any ED charge amount recorded in MedPAR files or Revenue Center Codes 0450–0459 or 0981 in outpatient files during 30 days prior to and including the index [28, 29]. Hospitalizations were identified if a record existed in MedPAR during 30 days prior to and including the index, and provider indicator codes were used to classify stays as short-term, long-term, or skilled nursing facility (SNF) stays. Surgical procedures were identified as major therapeutic procedures using ICD-9-Clinical Modification (CM) or ICD-10-Procedure Coding System (PCS) codes from MedPAR files [30, 31] or by applying the surgery flag algorithm using Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System/Current Procedural Terminology (HCPCS/CPT) codes from outpatient files [32] within the same time frame.

Chronic conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes, depression, osteoporosis, hip fracture, osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, ischemic heart disease, stroke, and cancer, were examined using CCW files. For the current study, chronic conditions were considered present if their first recorded occurrence date was prior to the index date.

Medication information on CNS depressant use during the 6-month washout period, including opioids, benzodiazepines (BDZ), antidepressants, and antipsychotics, was identified using Part D event files. Antidepressants were further specified by drug class (e.g., serotonin and norepinephrine reupdate inhibitors (SNRIs); selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs); and tricyclic antidepressants (TCA)). Owing to the established risk of concurrent use of opioids and gabapentin, opioid prescriptions at the index date were also examined.

Continuous Use of Gabapentin

Gabapentin prescription patterns were evaluated using Part D event files. Continuous gabapentin prescriptions were defined as those with a gap of 30 days or fewer between fills. We calculated the number of prescriptions, the mean duration of continuous use (from the index date to the first gap exceeding 30 days), and the average daily dose for 90-day, 180-day, and 365-day follow-up periods. In addition, we determined the proportion of beneficiaries with 100% coverage of gabapentin (allowing for a 30-day gap) over 90 days, 180 days, and 365 days.

Subgroup Analyses

For subgroup analyses, gabapentin initiators were categorized on the basis of the duration of continuous gabapentin use: ≤ 90 days, > 90 days and ≤ 180 days, and > 180 days (allowing for a 30-day gap). For each subgroup, we measured demographics, recent medical history, chronic conditions, and healthcare utilization. Furthermore, since the FDA-recommended maximum daily dose of gabapentin is 3600 mg/day, we identified beneficiaries with an initial daily dose exceeding 3600 mg/day (up to 10,000 mg/day), to further describe their demographics, recent medical history, and chronic conditions.

Results

The study included 1,887,445 eligible Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) beneficiaries aged 65 years and older. Among the 1,887,445 eligible Medicare beneficiaries, 358,255 (19.0%) were identified as gabapentin users, and 247,612 (13.1%) were identified as gabapentin initiators (Fig. 1). The prevalence of gabapentin prescriptions increased from 6.7% of beneficiaries in 2013 to 10.2% in 2021. More than one-third of the beneficiaries had opioid prescription during the 6-months prior to and including index date, and 30% had antidepressant prescriptions, mostly SSRIs. Approximately 13% of gabapentin initiators had both opioid and gabapentin prescriptions starting on the same date. ED visits and hospitalizations during the baseline period occurred in 13.1% and 10.3% of beneficiaries, respectively, with the majority of hospitalizations being short stays (9.4%). In addition, 8% of gabapentin initiators underwent a surgical procedure within 30 days prior to and including the index date (Table 1).

Since the growth in gabapentin prescriptions is hypothesized to be mostly for off-label use, we examined the recent medical history of beneficiaries to provide context for gabapentin prescribing. Epilepsy (0.2%) and postherpetic neuralgia (0.4%), the approved indications for gabapentin, had low prevalence among gabapentin initiators. However, 39.5% of beneficiaries had a diagnosis for at least one pain condition documented, and 32.6% had a diagnosis of chronic pain. Chronic conditions documented prior to initiation included hypertension (82.3%), osteoarthritis/rheumatoid arthritis (69.2%), ischemic heart disease (43.3%), diabetes (34.3%) and depression (31.6%) (Table 1).

To assess continuous use, we analyzed gabapentin prescriptions during 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year follow-up. During the first 90 days, beneficiaries had an average of 1.9 (SD 1.2) gabapentin prescriptions with median daily dose of 330 mg (IQR 300–827.6). The average number of continuous days, allowing a 30-day gap, was 54.4 (SD 30.1), and about 46% had full coverage of gabapentin prescriptions during this period. During 180 days of follow-up, the average number of prescriptions was 2.6 (SD 2.0), with a median daily dose of 352.9 mg/day (IQR 300–855.0). About 25% of gabapentin initiators had full coverage during this period. During 1-year follow-up, the average number of prescriptions was 3.5 (SD 3.4), and the median daily dose was 395.6 mg (IQR 300–866), with 15% maintaining continuous coverage for the full 1-year (Table 2). The patterns of continuous use of gabapentin

Gabapentin initiators were then stratified by continuous use: 54.8% (N = 135,569) had ≤ 90 days of continuous use (group 1); 20.3% (N = 50,313) had > 90 days to ≤ 180 days (group 2); and 24.9% (N = 61,730) had > 180 days (group 3). Demographics were similar across the groups. The median days of supply (group 1: 30 [28, 30]; group 2: 30 [30, 88]; and group 3: 30 [30, 30] and median average daily dose (group 1: 300 [300, 766]; group 2: 300 [300, 600]; and group 3: 300 [200, 600]) at index were similar across groups. The average daily dose for the second gabapentin prescription was slightly lower in group 3 (median [IQR]: 300 [300, 797.1]) compared with groups 1 and 2 (both: 400 [300, 900]). The prevalence of chronic pain at baseline was higher in group 1 (34.1%) compared with group 3 (30.2%), while neuropathic pain was more common in group 3 (6.1%) compared with group 1 (4.7%). Group 3 also had a higher prevalence of diabetes (37.8% versus 32.1%) and depression (34.9% versus 29.8%) compared with group 1. Opioid co-prescription at index was more common in group 1 (15.9%) than in group 3 (9.4%), and ED visits (14.3% versus 11.6%), hospitalizations (12.5% versus 7.7%), and surgical procedures (10.8% versus 4.1%) were higher in group 1 (Table 3). The prevalence of several chronic conditions prior to the index date was higher in group 3 compared with group 1 (Alzheimer’s disease: 3.6% versus 2.4%; diabetes: 37.8% versus 32.1%; depression: 34.9% versus 29.8%; hypertension: 84.6% versus 80.9%) (Table 3). Gabapentin initiators exceeding the maximum daily dose of 3600 mg (≤ 10,000 mg) at index (i.e., high-dose users) were identified (N = 107). These high-dose users had shorter days of supply compared with those within the recommended dose range, and pain was their only documented recent medical history. The prevalence of opioid prescription during washout period was higher in the group with high-dose users (45.8%) compared with others (38.8%). However, the high-dose users (4.7%) had fewer opioid co-prescriptions dispensed at the index date (4.7% versus 13.2%) but were more likely to have ≤ 90 days of continuous use (77.6% versus 54.7%) (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

This study characterized gabapentin users and examined patterns of gabapentin use among Medicare FFS beneficiaries. Gabapentin use in this population increased from 2013 to 2021, apparently driven largely by prescribing to treat off-label conditions, such as chronic pain. Our findings highlight that gabapentin initiators had a high burden of chronic conditions and often had a history of opioid or antidepressant use. These patterns raise important concerns about the appropriateness and safety of gabapentin use in this population.

The prevalence of gabapentin use among Medicare FFS beneficiaries aged 65 years and older increased from 2013 to 2021. This trend aligns with prior studies in other populations and older adults in the USA. For example, data from Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data (adults aged > 17 years) showed an increase in gabapentinoid prescriptions from 1.2% in 2002 to 4.7% in 2021, which was predominately driven by gabapentin use [33, 34]. Similarly, analyses of IBM MarketScan claims (adults aged 18–64 years) reported an increase in gabapentin prescriptions from 1.2% in 2002 to 3.9% in 2015 and from 1.3% in 2009 to 2.7% in 2016, respectively [11]. Among older adults enrolled in cohort studies of brain aging, gabapentin use consistently increased from 2006 to 2019 across age, sex, and cognitive status groups [13]. A study from Denmark also observed that the most notable increase in gabapentin use was among the older adult population [12].

In this study, the FDA-approved indications of epilepsy and postherpetic neuralgia were reported in less than 1% of gabapentin initiators during the 3 months prior to the index date. In contrast, nearly 40% of these beneficiaries had a diagnosis of pain, with 32.6% having a documented diagnosis of chronic pain. However, previous studies have highlighted that postherpetic neuralgia is known to be underestimated in claims data owing to the absence of a unique ICD code [35]. To improve identification of beneficiaries with postherpetic neuralgia, some studies have incorporated criteria that include herpes zoster diagnoses [35, 36]. Yet, the prevalence of herpes zoster was also very low (2.3%) among gabapentin initiators in our cohort. These findings suggest that gabapentin prescriptions are mostly off-label among older adults. This is consistent with previous studies that report up to 95% of the gabapentin prescriptions are for off-label use [2]. One study of nursing home residents with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) found that between 2015 and 2019, the percentage using gabapentin increased among residents with reported pain, while the percentage of opioids declined [37]. Despite limited or absent evidence supporting gabapentin’s efficacy for many off-label conditions, a case study reported that informal prescribing practices, including reliance on colleagues or professional meetings, may contribute to the steady growth in gabapentin’s off-label use [38].

Compared with the general older adult population, gabapentin initiators in our study exhibited a higher prevalence of chronic conditions. For instance, 82.3% had hypertension, 34.3% had diabetes, 31.6% had depression, and 20.8% had congestive heart failure. These rates surpass those reported in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) for adults aged 60 years and older (hypertension, 74.5%; diabetes, 20.5%; depression, 8.0%; and congestive heart failure, 9.1% for those aged 80+ years) [39–42]. The high prevalence of diabetes among initiators may be attributed to gabapentin use for pain related to diabetic neuropathy [43]. In addition, since this study included beneficiaries with Part D coverage, the study sample may include beneficiaries with a higher burden of chronic conditions. However, the prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease was low among gabapentin initiators (2.7%) compared with the general older adult population (10.9%) [44]. Similarly, we previously reported that gabapentin use was less common in older adults with dementia (approximately 5%) than in those with cognitive impairment or normal cognition in 2019 [13]. Further research is needed to determine whether these findings reflect actual prescribing patterns or potential selection bias. Nonetheless, our results suggest that gabapentin initiators tend to have more comorbidities than the general population of older adults, with the exception of conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease.

Almost two in five beneficiaries initiating gabapentin had a history of opioid use during the 6-month washout period, and 13.2% were co-prescribed gabapentin and opioids on the index date. Given gabapentin’s frequent use as an alternative to opioids [2, 45], future studies should investigate whether beneficiaries are transitioning from opioids to gabapentin or using both concurrently. In addition, approximately 30% of gabapentin initiators had a history of antidepressant use during the washout period. While some antidepressants are prescribed for pain management, the majority were SSRIs rather than SNRIs, such as duloxetine, which is an FDA-approved medication for neuropathic pain treatment. These patterns suggest that gabapentin was prescribed to older adults with psychotropic conditions, often in the absence of strong effectiveness. Recent research has raised concerns that gabapentin may be replacing opioids and antipsychotics in response to deprescribing initiatives in older adults [46]. One study found that as antipsychotic prescribing declined in nursing homes, gabapentin and other antiepileptics increased substantially—despite limited evidence of their efficacy for behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia and growing evidence of harm [37]. This highlights the importance of critically evaluating the rationale for gabapentin initiation and considering the potential harms of substituting one class of risky medications for another without robust supporting evidence.

The subgroup analysis based on continuous gabapentin use (≤ 90 days, 91–180 days, > 180 days) revealed minimal differences across groups in terms of demographics and prescription patterns. However, those with longer continuous use were more likely to have neuropathic pain, chronic conditions, and antidepressant use, and less likely to have opioid prescriptions at index, ED visits, hospitalizations, or surgical procedures. These findings align with prior research, which reported that prolonged gabapentin use was associated with a higher Charlson Comorbidity Index, suggesting greater overall disease burden in long-term gabapentin users [47]. The absence of major differences across subgroups suggests that factors influencing prolonged gabapentin use are complex and require further investigation. This complexity highlights the need for future studies to elucidate the factors associated with long-term gabapentin use among older adults.

This study has several limitations. First, although we assessed for recent medical history during 3 months prior to initiating gabapentin, identifying the specific reasons for gabapentin use in this population remains challenging using claims data. Second, the ICD codes used to capture the recent medical care and chronic conditions may result in misclassification. Third, the small number of high-dose gabapentin users in the subgroup analysis limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions. Fourth, to reduce bias from prevalent users, we applied a 6-month washout period to identify new gabapentin users. While this does not guarantee that participants were never prior users, the definition is appropriate for describing utilization patterns, as the study was not a comparative analysis. Lastly, this study included only FFS beneficiaries and excluded those with Medicare Managed Care and those with dual eligibility for Medicare and Medicaid, which limits the generalizability of the findings.

In conclusion, this study highlights the increasing prevalence of gabapentin use among older adults, primarily among those without an apparent approved indication. Future studies are needed to investigate factors associated with continuous use of gabapentin and evaluate the appropriateness and safety of gabapentin in this population.
