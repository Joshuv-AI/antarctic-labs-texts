INTRODUCTION

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is a prevalent vestibular condition that predominantly impacts older adults, yet it can also manifest in individuals across various age groups. The posterior canal variant of BPPV stands out as the most common form, representing 88–90% of all diagnosed cases. The condition is caused by the displacement of otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) into the posterior semicircular canal, leading to inappropriate stimulation of the canal and resulting in vertigo. While BPPV is typically self-limiting, it can significantly impact an individual’s quality of life due to unpredictable and disruptive symptoms.[1]

BPPV is diagnosed clinically, most commonly through the Dix-Hallpike maneuver, which induces vertigo and nystagmus, characteristic of posterior canal involvement. The nystagmus observed is typically vertical and delayed in onset, providing strong evidence of posterior canal BPPV. This maneuver is an essential diagnostic tool and helps differentiate BPPV from other vestibular disorders.[2]

The primary treatment for posterior canal BPPV involves repositioning maneuvers, with the Epley maneuver being the most commonly used. This maneuver consists of a series of head movements designed to relocate the displaced otoconia back into the utricle, where they are reabsorbed by the endolymphatic sac. Success rates of the Epley maneuver range from 78% to 95%, making it a highly effective non-invasive treatment. In addition to repositioning, Betahistine, a histamine analog, is often prescribed to alleviate symptoms like nausea and dizziness, although its impact on the success of the maneuver remains debated.[34]

This research seeks to assess the frequency of posterior canal BPPV among all vertigo cases in a tertiary care hospital in India and assess the treatment outcomes of the Epley maneuver combined with Betahistine. The study will provide insights into the management of BPPV in an Indian clinical context.[5678]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective study was carried out in the ENT department of a tertiary care hospital in India from January 2023 to December 2023. The aim was to evaluate the proportion of posterior canal BPPV among all vertigo cases and to assess the treatment outcomes of Epley’s maneuver in conjunction with Betahistine. The research involved 60 patients diagnosed with posterior canal BPPV, as verified by the Dix-Hallpike maneuver. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged 18 to 80 years who experienced at least one episode of vertigo. Exclusion criteria included those with central nervous system (CNS) neoplasms, prior ear surgery, or other vestibular disorders. All patients underwent Epley’s maneuver and received Betahistine for symptom management. Data were collected from patient records and clinical examinations. Treatment success was evaluated by assessing symptom resolution at 1-week and 1-month follow-ups. Descriptive statistics and the chi-square test were employed for the statistical analysis of treatment outcomes, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The present study found a high proportion of BPPV cases in the 41–60 age group, consistent with other studies linking age-related vestibular changes to increased prevalence of BPPV.[5] Most cases were idiopathic, as commonly reported in the literature.[6]

The Epley maneuver showed a success rate of 78.33%, aligning with other studies that report success rates between 70% and 95%.[7] The addition of Betahistine helped alleviate symptoms but did not significantly impact the success rate compared to the maneuver alone.[8] Gender distribution was relatively balanced in this study, though female predominance is often reported in larger cohort studies.[9] Trauma-induced BPPV was also observed, consistent with existing research on head injury-related vertigo.[10]

These findings highlight the effectiveness of the Epley maneuver in treating BPPV, but future research should optimize combined treatments and explore long-term outcomes, including the role of vestibular rehabilitation and surgical options for resistant cases.[111213]

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, posterior canal BPPV is a prevalent vestibular disorder, especially among middle-aged and elderly individuals. The study demonstrates that Epley’s maneuver, combined with Betahistine, remains an effective treatment option, with a high success rate. Although most cases were idiopathic, trauma-related BPPV was also observed. The gender distribution was balanced, and treatment outcomes were consistent with existing literature. Future research should prioritize the optimization of combined treatments and the investigation of long-term therapeutic options, such as vestibular rehabilitation, for patients with refractory BPPV.
