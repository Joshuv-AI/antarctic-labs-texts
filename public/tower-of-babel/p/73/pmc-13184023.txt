Psychometric characteristics of the Persian version of the Dissociative Experiences Scale (DES-II) in patients with multiple sclerosis (PWMS)
Authors: Farhad Radfar, Shahriar Shahidi, Hojjatollah Farahani, Sharareh Eskandrieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with multiple sclerosis (PWMS) may experience psychological challenges, including dissociative symptoms. Accurate assessment of these symptoms requires psychometrically sound instruments. This study aimed to evaluate the reliability and validity of the Persian version of the DES-II for use in PWMS in Iran.MethodsIn this cross-sectional, registry-based study, 257 PWMS were recruited from the nationwide MS Registry of Iran (NMSRI). Data were collected over a four-month period in 2023. The back-translated Persian version of the DES-II was administered. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, Guttman’s lambda, and composite reliability. Validity was evaluated through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), average variance extracted (AVE), maximum shared variance (MSV), and average shared variance (ASV), using SPSS 24 and AMOS.ResultsCFA supported the three-factor structure, with most factor loadings exceeding 0.50. Model fit indices indicated an acceptable fit. Internal consistency was strong across factors (all reliability coefficients > 0.70). However, AVE, MSV, and ASV values suggested limitations in convergent and discriminant validity within this sample.ConclusionsThe Persian version of the DES-II supported satisfactory internal consistency and acceptable factorial validity in PWMS. Nevertheless, concerns regarding certain aspects of construct validity, along with non-random sampling and gender imbalance, warrant cautious interpretation and further validation in diverse samples.Keywords: Dissociative experiences, Multiple sclerosis (MS), Persian version, Psychometric evaluation, Reliability, Validity, Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), Internal consistency

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder marked by inflammatory processes that lead to axonal demyelination in the central nervous system, ultimately resulting in progressive neurological impairment and disability [1–3]. Epidemiological evidence indicates a growing global prevalence, with the number of individuals diagnosed with MS rising from 2.3 million in 2013 to 2.8 million in 2020 [4–5]. MS imposes substantial physical, emotional, social, and economic burdens on patients and their caregivers [6–7]. Beyond physical impairments, individuals living with MS frequently experience heightened psychological distress, including elevated levels of anxiety and depression, disrupted sleep, cognitive difficulties, and diminished psychological well-being [8–11].

Given the intricate relationship between neurological and psychological symptoms in multiple sclerosis, recent studies have highlighted the relevance of dissociative symptoms in autoimmune diseases, including MS, showing that dissociation can complicate the clinical presentation and mimic other neurological conditions, thus challenging differential diagnosis [12–14]. Dissociation is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon in psychopathology, commonly understood as a disruption or discontinuity in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, motor control, or behavior [15].

Dissociative symptoms are often thought to arise in response to overwhelming emotional experiences, unresolved psychological conflicts, or perceived inescapable threats [16]. These symptoms can range from common experiences of intense absorption to more severe states that significantly interfere with daily functioning and well-being [17]. The etiology of dissociation has been examined across multiple domains, including psychological disorders, neurological dysfunction, environmental adversity, and sociocultural influences such as cultural dissonance [18–23]. Neurological disorders have further highlighted the central role of impaired interoception and dissociation in shaping symptom presentation, including motor and sensory disturbances [24]. Studies suggest that dissociation alters the brain’s capacity to accurately process internal bodily signals, leading to disturbances in pain perception, self-awareness, and bodily agency. Notably, PWMS frequently experience psychiatric and somatic comorbidities, which can exacerbate symptoms, complicate differential diagnosis, and impact disease management [25]. These comorbidities may interact with dissociative experiences and altered pain perception, amplifying somatic complaints such as chronic pain and sensory anomalies [26–29]. According to StatPearls [30], the global prevalence of dissociative disorders is estimated to be between 1% and 5%. Importantly, dissociative experiences are not confined to individuals with psychiatric diagnoses; rather, they appear along a continuum and are observed, to varying degrees, in the general population [31–36].

Recent advances in the field have led to the development of increasingly sophisticated instruments to assess dissociative experiences in both non-clinical populations and individuals with psychiatric diagnoses [37–38]. As interest grows in understanding dissociative symptoms among individuals with neurological and somatic conditions, the need for reliable assessment tools becomes more pressing. For example, Makarenko et al. [13] reported notable dissociative symptoms among patients with autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and MS. Similarly, Bruce et al. [12] observed a positive association between MS and dissociative symptoms, suggesting that such experiences may be more prevalent in these populations than previously assumed. In contrast, a study by Yildirim et al. [39] found no significant dissociative symptoms in patients with MS compared to healthy controls, highlighting the heterogeneity of dissociative presentations and the need for further empirical investigation. Collectively, these findings point to the importance of using robust and psychometrically sound instruments to assess dissociative symptoms, particularly in neurologically affected populations such as patients with MS.

One widely used tool for this purpose is the DES, a 28-item self-report questionnaire originally developed in 1986 to evaluate dissociative experiences in both clinical and non-clinical contexts. It utilizes an eleven-point visual analog scale (0% to 100%) to measure the frequency and intensity of dissociative symptoms [40]. A revised version, the DES-II, retains the original items but incorporates a modified response format aimed at improving psychometric performance and scoring consistency [41].

The DES-II has been culturally adapted and validated in numerous countries, including Germany [42], Spain [43], Finland [44], France [45], Israel [46], Italy [47–48], Puerto Rico [49–50], Spain [51], Portugal [52], Greece [53], Turkey [54], and Sweden [55]. These studies generally show strong internal consistency and satisfactory psychometric properties across diverse contexts. However, despite encouraging findings, there is no uniform consensus on its factor structure across populations, underscoring the importance of continued evaluation in culturally distinct samples.

To date, only a limited number of standardized Persian translations of the DES have been developed, primarily in studies focusing on populations with schizophrenia, mood disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [56–57]. Both studies supported the reliability and validity of the scale in their respective samples. Kianpoor was the first to translate and utilize the DES-II in Persian; however, his study did not include a formal factor analysis of the instrument. Kargar-Barzi later employed this version in a clinical sample but treated the scale as a unidimensional measure, analyzing all 28 items under a single factor. This approach diverges from the original conceptualization of the DES-II, which posits a three-factor structure comprising amnestic experiences (items 3–10, 24–26), absorption/imaginative involvement (items 1–2 and 14–23), and depersonalization/derealization (items 7, 11–13, 19, 27–28). As such, the construct validity in these studies may be limited by methodological simplifications that do not fully reflect the multidimensional nature of the instrument. In addition, screening for mental health conditions—particularly dissociative symptoms—is not routinely conducted among PWMS in Iran, underscoring the potential utility of validated assessment tools such as the DES. This gap is especially significant given that dissociative disorders are clinically rarely identified and thus underdiagnosed; for instance, it has been reported that patients with DID have typically been in the mental healthcare service for an average of 6.8 years before being accurately diagnosed [58–60]. Recent advances in neurobiological research have further emphasized the relevance of understanding the brain mechanisms underlying dissociation and the need for more accurate diagnostic instruments [30, 61]. This study seeks to address a notable gap in the literature by evaluating the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the DES-II in Persian-speaking patients with multiple sclerosis. Although the DES-II has been widely validated in both general and psychiatric populations [31, 40, 62–65], its utility in the context of autoimmune diseases, including MS, remains underexplored. By systematically examining its validity and reliability in this clinical group, the present study aims to improve the scale’s applicability for research and screening purposes, and to support the availability of a psychometrically evaluated instrument for assessing dissociative experiences in this clinical population.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

This study employed a non-experimental, cross-sectional psychometric design [66]. Data were collected over a four-month period (March–June 2023) from 257 patients with clinically confirmed multiple sclerosis using a non-random convenience sampling method. Each participant completed all study measures at a single time point. Participants were recruited through the MS Specialty Center at Sina Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, and via the National MS Registry of Iran (NMSRI) database [67]. Data collection was carried out electronically through official and verified online platforms and communication channels affiliated with the Sina MS Research Center, including Telegram and WhatsApp.

Participants

A total of 257 individuals with a clinically confirmed diagnosis of multiple sclerosis (MS) participated in this study. Participants were recruited through the National MS Registry of Iran (NMSRI) and the MS Specialty Center at Sina Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Recruitment through the national registry facilitated access to a geographically diverse clinical population. Eligibility criteria included age between 18 and 68 years, a confirmed MS diagnosis of at least one-year, clinical stability at the time of assessment, and sufficient cognitive capacity to comprehend and complete the questionnaires. Participants were required to be literate in Persian, able to provide informed consent, and capable of independently completing the online survey. Exclusion criteria included self-reported or documented acute psychiatric instability (e.g., active psychosis or manic episode), uncontrolled substance intoxication or withdrawal, major neurological events within the preceding three months (e.g., stroke or severe traumatic brain injury), active central nervous system infection, or significant cognitive impairment interfering with questionnaire completion. Participants with incomplete key data or who withdrew consent were excluded from the final analyses.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.NI.REC.1401.015) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [68]. All participants provided informed written consent. The study adhered to ethical codes of conduct outlined by the American Psychological Association (APA) [69], including assurances of privacy, voluntary participation, and confidentiality.

Measures

Somatoform Dissociation Questionnaire (SDQ-20)

The SDQ-20 is a 20-item self-report instrument designed to assess somatoform dissociation, including both negative symptoms (e.g., analgesia, anesthesia, motor inhibition) and positive symptoms (e.g., pain, sensory alterations). Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Previous research has reported high internal consistency and construct validity for the SDQ-20, with Cronbach’s α values up to 0.96 [74, 75]. For the purposes of the present study, the SDQ-20 was translated into Persian using a forward–backward translation procedure and reviewed by clinical psychologists to ensure conceptual clarity. The instrument was used to examine convergent validity. Although formal psychometric validation of the Persian version was not conducted within this study, its use was authorized by Dr. Ellert R. S. Nijenhuis. The translated version has been archived in an academic repository for non-commercial research use (10.5281/zenodo.17154695).

Dissociative Experiences Scale-II (DES-II)

The DES-II is a 28-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess both normative and pathological dissociative experiences. Respondents indicate the frequency of experiences on a visual analog scale from 0% (never) to 100% (Always). Incomplete responses led to exclusion from analysis. Based on prior literature [41], items were categorized into three subcomponents: amnestic experiences (items 3–6, 8–10, 24–26), the absorption/imaginative involvement factor (comprising items 1–2 and 14–23), and depersonalization/derealization (items 7, 11–13, 19, 27–28). This classification enabled detailed analysis of dissociative domains. Previous studies across Europe, Asia, Latin America, and the United States have reported good reliability and validity for this tripartite structure [70–73].

Procedure

The DES-II was translated from English into Persian using a forward–backward translation procedure to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence. Three independent experts reviewed the translated versions, and revisions were made iteratively to improve clarity and cultural relevance. The final questionnaire was designed and hosted on a specialized online survey platform. The survey link was distributed through professional and patient-oriented Telegram and WhatsApp channels, as well as related online communities. Participants were informed that their responses would remain completely anonymous, and all data were collected electronically through secure online access.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 24; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS (version 24). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were calculated for all demographic, clinical, and psychometric variables. Internal consistency reliability of the Persian DES-II was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and Guttman’s λ₆ coefficients. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using maximum likelihood estimation was conducted to examine the three-factor structure of the Persian DES-II. Model fit was evaluated using multiple goodness-of-fit indices, including the chi-square statistic (χ²), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with 90% confidence intervals, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), comparative fit index (CFI), relative fit index (RFI), normed fit index (NFI), incremental fit index (IFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), and adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI). Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed using composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), maximum shared variance (MSV), and average shared variance (ASV). Associations between DES-II scores and clinical variables were examined using Spearman’s correlation for EDSS and Pearson’s correlation for MS duration. Group differences across demographic and clinical categories were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests (gender and age group) and the Kruskal–Wallis test (marital status, education level, economic status, and MS subtypes). Statistical significance was set at p < .05 (two-tailed).

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 257 patients with clinically confirmed multiple sclerosis were included in the final analyses. The mean age of participants was 39.46 years (SD = 7.99; range = 19–68). The mean disease duration was 9.97 years (SD = 6.32), and the mean Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score was 2.38 (SD = 1.92), indicating overall mild to moderate neurological disability in the sample. The mean DES-II total score (range 0–100), calculated according to the standard scoring procedure, was 26.9 (SD = 15.2). The mean SDQ-20 total score (range 20–100) was 28.7 (SD = 7.9). Between-group comparisons revealed no significant differences in DES-II scores by gender, t(254) = 0.25, p = .804, or age group (< 40 vs. ≥40 years), t(255) = 0.25, p = .804. Correlation analyses showed no significant associations between DES-II scores and disease duration (r = .01, p = .919) or neurological disability as measured by EDSS (ρ = −0.05, p = .426). Reliability

Internal consistency

Internal consistency of the Persian DES-II was evaluated in the full sample (N = 257). Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.91 and Guttman’s λ₆ was 0.86, indicating excellent reliability. At the subscale level, Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.82 to 0.83 and λ₆ from 0.73 to 0.79, all exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. These findings support the reliability of both the total score and the three subscales (Table 2).

All standardized factor loadings for the items of the Persian version of the DES-II were statistically significant (p < .001). Factor loadings ranged from 0.47 to 0.76, with the majority exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50, indicating adequate item-level contributions to the latent constructs. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted on the full sample of 257 patients with MS. The initial model suggested moderate fit, and theoretically justified modifications resulted in improved model fit indices, supporting the proposed three-factor structure of the Persian DES-II (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first effort to translate and psychometrically evaluate the version of the DES-II in a clinical sample of Persian-speaking people with multiple sclerosis (PWMS). This work addresses an important gap in the cross-cultural adaptation of dissociation measures for neurological populations, where the interplay between psychological and physical symptoms can complicate assessment. The findings demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, acceptable model fit, and meaningful associations with somatoform dissociation. Together, these results provide initial evidence supporting the reliability and construct validity of the Persian DES-II in this clinical sample. From a broader perspective, the findings suggest that dissociation measures may also be applicable in neurological populations such as MS, offering a culturally relevant and psychometrically acceptable tool for identifying dissociative experiences in Persian-speaking individuals. These findings may provide a foundation for future investigations into the clinical relevance of dissociation in MS and its implications for psychological assessment and research in similar settings.

The DES-II has been validated across multiple cultural and linguistic contexts, with reported reliability coefficients typically ranging between 0.85 and 0.95 [40–45, 48, 52–55]. In the present study (Table 2), Cronbach’s α of 0.91 falls within this range, suggesting that the Persian DES-II demonstrates a level of internal consistency comparable to previous validations. These findings indicate that the instrument maintains satisfactory internal coherence in a clinical sample of people with multiple sclerosis, a population characterized by neurocognitive and affective variability. Furthermore, replication of the original three-factor structure—amnesia, depersonalization/derealization, and absorption—provides additional evidence for the relative stability of its conceptual dimensions across cultures and clinical contexts. These findings are consistent with previous cross-cultural validations [49, 54–55, 65, 70–73], lending further support to the structural consistency of the DES-II beyond Western settings.

The Persian DES-II demonstrated satisfactory reliability and a coherent internal structure across all subscales (Table 2), suggesting that the translated measure preserved key psychometric characteristics of the original instrument. Item-level analyses (Table 3) showed acceptable factor loadings and item–total associations, consistent with prior IRT-based validations across diverse cultural settings [65, 75]. The three-factor model (Table 4; Fig. 1) of the Persian version showed improved fit following theoretically guided and statistically justified modifications, providing additional support for its conceptual alignment with the original structure while reflecting minor linguistic and contextual influences. These adjustments are consistent with recent recommendations for culturally responsive model evaluation in psychometric research [76–77]. Nonetheless, such refinements highlight the importance of future measurement invariance testing across demographic and clinical subgroups to support the stability of the Persian DES-II [78].

Moderate but significant positive correlations with the SDQ-20 (Table 5) provided additional evidence consistent with the convergent validity of the Persian DES-II, in line with previous findings linking dissociative and somatoform manifestations [74, 79]. The magnitude of these associations was broadly comparable to ranges reported in prior cross-cultural validations, including studies by Mazzotti et al. [48] and Ikeda and Urano [65], which similarly identified moderate interrelations between dissociative and somatoform dimensions alongside stable three-factor structures. Within the subscales, relatively stronger associations for amnesia and depersonalization/derealization compared with absorption may indicate a closer relationship between these dimensions and bodily or stress-related experiences. Such patterns may be particularly relevant in MS, a condition in which somatic symptoms frequently coexist with psychological distress [19, 24].

At the same time, relatively strong inter-correlations among subscales and AVE values below the conventional 0.50 threshold indicated only partial discriminant validity (Table 6). This pattern mirrors findings from Turkish [54], Greek [53], and Japanese [65] validations, lending support to the view that dissociation may be conceptualized as a multidimensional yet interrelated construct rather than a set of entirely independent factors.

Notably, absorption emerged as the most frequently endorsed yet least convergent subdimension, echoing prior cross-cultural evidence that absorption represents the most context-sensitive component of the DES-II [48]. Previous studies have linked absorption to imaginative involvement, rumination, and self-focused attention [17, 80], cognitive tendencies that have been associated with sustained engagement with stress-related material in theoretical models such as the perseverative cognition hypothesis [80]. In the MS context, such mechanisms may plausibly interact with stress-related processes; however, these interpretations remain speculative and require further empirical and multimodal investigation.

Finally, the modest discriminant validity observed may also reflect cultural–linguistic influences in how dissociative experiences are perceived and articulated among Persian-speaking individuals. Comparable findings in Arabic and Japanese samples—where dissociative detachment has been described using moral, relational, or somatic idioms [21, 81]—suggest that cultural context may shape the expression and interpretation of such experiences. In Persian-speaking settings, dissociative phenomena may likewise be framed in embodied or spiritual terms; however, this possibility remains hypothetical and requires direct empirical examination. These cross-cultural considerations highlight the importance of continued culturally sensitive validation efforts, ideally integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches to further clarify universal and culture-specific aspects of dissociation in health and illness.

Group comparisons revealed no significant differences in DES-II scores across demographic or clinical variables, including gender, age, education, disease duration, and disability level (Table 1). Although women comprised the majority of the sample, gender effects on dissociation were nonsignificant, consistent with previous MS research reporting mixed or null sex differences [10–11, 54]. Overall, dissociative symptoms appeared broadly distributed across subgroups, suggesting potential utility of the total score as a general screening indicator; however, this interpretation should be approached with caution. These findings should be interpreted carefully given the limited sample size and unequal gender distribution. Future studies with larger and more gender-balanced samples are needed to further evaluate these preliminary findings and to test measurement invariance more robustly [82]. Transparent reporting of such null findings aligns with the SAGER recommendations for sex- and gender-equitable research [83].

Psychometric research in PWMS often involves methodological and ethical challenges. Fatigue, cognitive slowing, and heightened sensitivity to trauma-related content may reduce participants’ tolerance for lengthy or emotionally demanding assessments [19, 84, 85]. These factors limited the feasibility of incorporating structured diagnostic interviews and ROC analyses in the present study. In line with trauma-informed research principles [86], future investigations would benefit from multimethod designs that integrate cognitive, clinical, and biological indicators while carefully safeguarding participant safety and emotional well-being.

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations. The use of a non-random sample may introduce selection bias, although recruitment was conducted through a neurologically confirmed national registry. The substantial female predominance (~ 84%) may limit generalizability; however, this distribution reflects national [87] and global MS trends [88–89]. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not permit evaluation of temporal stability. Subgroup analyses were likely underpowered due to smaller male and progressive MS subsamples. Reliance on self-report measures may introduce response bias, particularly in the context of fatigue and attentional fluctuation common in MS. Although CFA supported the proposed structure, future research may further benefit from applying more flexible modeling approaches, such as bifactor–ESEM frameworks [90], to better account for cross-loadings and shared variance among dissociative dimensions. Additional validation using longitudinal and gender-balanced samples would be valuable to examine measurement invariance and strengthen construct validity across diverse populations.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first psychometric evaluation of the Persian version of the DES-II in a clinical neurological sample. The findings demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and acceptable factorial validity, providing support for the three-factor structure of amnesia, depersonalization/derealization, and absorption. Reliability estimates were comparable to those reported in previous international studies. While the results suggest that the Persian DES-II may have potential utility as a screening instrument for dissociative experiences in research and clinical contexts, several methodological limitations—including non-random sampling, gender imbalance, cross-sectional design, and reliance on self-report—limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies employing larger, longitudinal, and multimethod designs are needed to further evaluate measurement stability, construct validity, and clinical relevance across diverse populations. Such efforts may contribute to a more refined understanding of dissociative phenomena within the context of chronic medical conditions.
