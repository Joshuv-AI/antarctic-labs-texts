Meeting report for the Second International Conference on Unconventional Animal Models of Alzheimer's Disease and Aging (UAMAA 2026)
Authors: Jane Alshami, Ginny Wu, B. Maximiliano Garduño, Afonso C. Silva, Stacey J. Sukoff Rizzo, Patricia Cogram, Xiangmin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Second International Conference on Unconventional Animal Models of Alzheimer's Disease and Aging (UAMAA 2026) was held February 9 through 11, 2026, in Irvine, California. Building upon the inaugural meeting in Santiago, Chile (2023), this conference expanded an international effort to develop, characterize, and rigorously evaluate non‐traditional animal models with enhanced translational relevance for Alzheimer's disease (AD) and aging research. The meeting convened leading investigators across genomics, systems neuroscience, comparative biology, and pathology to discuss naturally occurring and engineered models including degu (Octodon degus), dog (Canis lupus familiaris), marmoset (Callithrix jacchus), rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), naked mole‐rat (Heterocephalus glaber), and emerging large animal species including elephants and whales. A central theme was the need for cross‐species integration of multimodal and multi‐scale approaches to interrogate cellular vulnerability, gene regulatory programs, and age‐associated neuropathology and circuit alterations across species. UAMAA 2026 emphasized cross‐species comparison, human brain atlas integration, and translational alignment with National Institutes of Health priorities. The conference highlighted growing momentum toward a diversified model ecosystem aimed at accelerating mechanistic insight and therapeutic discovery in AD and aging research. A special journal issue associated with the conference has been announced to extend these themes into peer‐reviewed scientific discourse.

INTRODUCTION

The field of Alzheimer's disease (AD) continues to grapple with the translational limitations of conventional transgenic mouse models. While these systems have provided essential mechanistic insights into amyloid processing, tau pathology, and neuroinflammation, their predictive validity for therapeutic efficacy remains limited. 1 , 2 Recognizing this gap, recent initiatives including those supported by the National Institute on Aging (NIA), have encouraged the development of unconventional mammalian, non‐murine, and naturally aging models that may better recapitulate human neuropathology, gene regulatory programs, and cognitive decline. Since 2023, the International Conference on Unconventional Animal Models of Alzheimer's Disease and Aging (UAMAA) has served as a dedicated forum to advance the field by evolving translational direction.

The Second International UAMAA Conference was held February 9 through 11, 2026, in Irvine, California, USA. Organized by Afonso Silva, Stacey J. Sukoff Rizzo, Patricia Cogram, Bing Ren, and Xiangmin Xu (Figure 1A–E), the meeting expanded upon the inaugural UAMAA conference (2023) 3 by consolidating an international effort to develop, characterize, and rigorously evaluate non‐traditional animal models with improved translational relevance for AD and aging research. Hosted by the University of California (UC) Irvine Center for Neural Circuit Mapping (CNCM), 4 , 5 , 6 in collaboration with the Marmosets as Research Models of Alzheimer's Disease (MARMO‐AD) consortium, 7 the Model Organism Development and Evaluation for Late‐onset Alzheimer's Disease (MODEL‐AD) consortium, 8 and the University of Chile, UAMAA 2026 convened ~150 investigators from North America, South America, Europe, and Asia. The scientific program integrated advances in animal model development, single‐cell and spatial transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, circuit mapping, and behavioral neuroscience (Figure 2). A central objective was to foster direct dialogue among investigators working on unconventional animal models alongside researchers focused on mouse and human systems, thereby promoting cross‐species alignment and translational convergence.

The conference opened with welcoming remarks by Todd Holmes (Professor of Physiology and Biophysics, UC Irvine) (Figure 1F) and Aileen Anderson (Vice Chancellor for Research, UC Irvine) (Figure 1G), who set the tone for the meeting by underscoring UC Irvine's institutional commitment to translational neuroscience and cross‐species approaches to neurodegeneration. Their remarks framed the central question that would guide the next 3 days: How can unconventional animal models deepen our understanding of AD and aging biology?

Through the six thematic sessions—spanning genetics, comparative models, technological innovation, circuit neuroscience, neuropathology, and aging biology—the meeting explored disease mechanisms across species. Detailed information is available in the full program book on the UC Irvine CNCM website: https://cncm.medschool.uci.edu/uamaa‐2026/.

GENETICS AND GENOMICS RESEARCH

Chaired by Xiangmin Xu (UC Irvine), the genetics and genomics session established the foundation for the conference by emphasizing the importance of comparative genomics and gene regulatory architecture across species. Bing Ren (New York Genome Center/Columbia University), a co‐organizer of the meeting, presented recent advances in understanding aging‐associated gene regulatory programs in the human hippocampus. Using multimodal single‐cell genomics across the adult lifespan, their work reveals major cellular and epigenomic shifts, including the loss of resident microglia and synapse‐supporting astrocytes, accompanied by the emergence of pro‐inflammatory monocyte‐like cells and widespread genome reorganization. 9 Extending these findings to AD, their unpublished study in the 5xFAD mouse model identifies hyperactivation of the cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS‐STING) pathway in microglia as a key link between aging, amyloid pathology, and cognitive decline; STING inhibition restores regulatory programs, reduces amyloid burden, and improves memory. Trygve Bakken (Allen Institute for Brain Science) presented a new whole brain consensus cell‐type atlas for human, macaque, and marmosets and discussed cell‐type specialization and vulnerability within human cortical populations, 10 providing critical context for evaluating how unconventional animal models align with human cell‐type diversity. Gregory Carter (The Jackson Laboratory) addressed multi‐omic approaches using marmoset models of normal aging and engineered with AD mutations to bridge the rodent to human translational gap for AD. Nathan Zemke (UC San Diego) presented data on multi‐omic profiling of the aging dog brain, revealing cell‐type and region‐specific changes that align with the hallmarks of human neurodegeneration. Maximiliano Garduño (UC Irvine) provided insights into genetic variation, aging, and AD‐associated risk factors highlighting the natural occurrence of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques in degus as an improved model to traditional mouse models. 11 , 12 Christopher Glass (UC San Diego) concluded the session with perspectives on transcriptional regulation and neuroimmune interactions relevant to neurodegeneration with a specific focus on microglial responses in disease. 13 Broadly, this session set a unifying tone for the meeting by demonstrating that understanding conserved and divergent gene regulatory mechanisms across species, at the level of genetics, genomics, cell types, circuits, and immune interactions, is essential for advancing research in brain aging and neurodegenerative disease.

NEW MODELS AND TECHNOLOGIES

Across two sessions chaired by Patricia Cogram and Bing Ren, these presentations expanded the genetic framework of AD by highlighting naturally occurring models and emerging technologies for deeper characterization of aging and disease‐related phenotypes. Patricia Cogram (University of Chile) presented advances in the degu as a natural model of aging and AD‐like pathology, emphasizing integrated genetic and behavioral analyses. 11 , 14 Vera Gorbunova (University of Rochester) discussed longevity mechanisms across unconventional species, including naked mole‐rats, bowhead whales, and degus, with a focus on DNA repair and genome stability pathways underlying resilience to aging. 15 , 16 Ewan St. John Smith (University of Cambridge) explored sensory and neural adaptations in the naked mole‐rat, linking comparative neurobiology to mechanisms of brain aging. 17 Donna Wilcock (University of Kentucky) highlighted the importance of vascular contributions to AD using the hyperhomocysteinemia mouse model. 18 George Perry (University of Texas at San Antonio) addressed neuropathological and oxidative stress signatures across species. 19 , 20 Adrian Oblak (Indiana University) presented translational neuropathology, biomarkers, and multi‐omics analysis designed as cross‐species comparisons to late‐onset AD (LOAD) risk genes in humans using mouse models engineered with the same LOAD risk genes developed by the NIA‐funded MODEL‐AD consortium. 8 , 21

Mathew Blurton‐Jones (UC Irvine) presented work integrating stem cell–based approaches to study microglia and AD pathology using a novel chimeric mouse model. 22 Jing Liu (Chinese Academy of Sciences) shared recent cross‐species data of emerging induced pluripotent stem cell models in guinea pigs and giant pandas, 23 , 24 highlighting how novel approaches in pluripotency engineering can be used to study regenerative medicine and personalized cell therapies. Amantha Thathiah (University of Pittsburgh) highlighted work from the MARMO‐AD consortium on how direct reprogramming of fibroblasts to induced neurons serves as a surrogate for brain tissue in living species, 25 allowing preservation of age related multi‐omic signatures to elucidate mechanisms that precede disease pathology and cognitive decline. Christine Charvet (Auburn University) discussed evolutionary scaling and comparative cortical organization highlighting a rich dataset including > 30,000 observations from > 2000 individuals spanning species such as cats, chimpanzees, mice, and humans, thus offering critical perspective on structural and developmental differences among species. 26 Elizabeth Head (UC Irvine), a leading expert in dog models of aging and AD, presented data on naturally occurring amyloid pathology and cognitive decline in aging dogs, reinforcing the translational value of this species as an intermediate model bridging rodent studies and human disease. 27 William Van Nostrand (University of Rhode Island) shared insights on gene editing approaches in rats emphasizing their value in studying vascular contributions to AD. 28 Kiho Lee (University of Missouri) presented on genetically engineered swine models and shared details on the National Institutes of Health–funded National Swine Resource and Research Center (NSRRC), which has established multiple AD pig models. 29 Together, these sessions underscored that integrating diverse species models with cutting‐edge cellular, genetic, and multi‐omic technologies is critical for uncovering mechanisms of resilience and vulnerability in aging, and for advancing more translatable models of AD.

NEUROSCIENCE, BEHAVIORAL, BIOMARKER, AND INTERVENTION DEVELOPMENT

A session chaired by Stacey J. Sukoff Rizzo focused on systems neuroscience, translational behavioral measures, biomarkers, and intervention strategies. Kim Green (UC Irvine) presented new work from the UC Irvine MODEL‐AD Center highlighting humanized TREM2 mouse models that have revealed key insights into microglial biology and neuroimmune interactions in aging and AD. 30 Bogdan Bintu (UC San Diego) discussed advanced imaging and chromatin organization technologies including the multimodal MERFISH‐based spatial transcriptomics approaches applicable to large‐brain systems. 31 Xiangmin Xu (UC Irvine) presented efforts at the UCI CNCM to establish Octodon degus as a powerful model of human brain aging and LOAD using multimodal and multiscale approaches. 11 , 12 , 32 , 33 The findings support Octodon degus as a promising and biologically relevant model for uncovering molecular, cellular, and circuit mechanisms of spontaneous LOAD, and identifying novel therapeutic targets. Stacey J. Sukoff Rizzo (University of Pittsburgh) presented on longitudinal cognitive, biomarker, and neuroimaging studies in marmosets genetically engineered with the PSEN1 C410Y risk mutation and their recapitulation of AD trajectories in patients as well as highlighting how studies in marmosets and great apes across the lifespan are revealing primate‐specific mechanisms of cognitive decline and AD susceptibility and resilience. 25 Bernard Schreurs (West Virginia University) demonstrated how high‐fat and high‐sugar diets differentially alter cognition and hippocampal metabolism in rabbit models, linking systemic metabolic dysfunction to learning impairments. 34 Frank Longo (Stanford University) concluded the session by describing the development of small‐molecule modulators of the p75 neurotrophin receptor that promote synaptic resilience in preclinical models and have advanced into clinical testing for AD. 35 Together, this session highlighted the historic limitations of traditional model systems and demonstrated how integrating advanced imaging, translational behavioral paradigms, cross‐species biomarker development, and targeted therapeutic strategies will enable a more mechanistic and clinically relevant understanding of AD, bridging the gap from molecular insight to intervention.

PATHOLOGICAL MODELS

The session chaired by Kim Green focused explicitly on neuropathological characterization across species. John Morrison (UC Davis) presented rhesus macaque tau‐based seeding models that map the temporal progression of synaptic loss, neuroinflammation, and functional decline. 36 Jeffrey Kordower (Arizona State University) discussed tau and multi‐protein co‐pathologies in Parkinson's disease and introduced a primate model expressing alpha‐synuclein, tau, and amyloid to better capture disease complexity. 37 Emilio Kropff (Institute Leloir, Argentina) characterized hippocampal spatial coding and network dynamics in degus, establishing a systems‐level framework for studying age‐related dysfunction. 38 Marina Emborg (University of Wisconsin) described longitudinal behavioral, imaging, and biomarker findings in rhesus macaques carrying the MAPT R406W mutation that model early frontotemporal dementia. 39 Kuo‐Fen Lee (Salk Institute) highlighted approaches in marmosets 40 for humanizing the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene to genetically recapitulate the sequence of the APOE ε4 risk isoform. Taken together, this session highlighted the importance of using complementary model systems that can recapitulate the progressive nature of neurodegenerative disorders to enable improved alignment with human disease progression.

GERIATRICS AND AGING PROCESSES

The geriatrics and aging processes session broadened the discussion to systemic aging biology and resilience. Session chair Afonso Silva (University of Pittsburgh) presented a tau‐seeded marmoset model that accelerates AD phenotypes based on longitudinal characterization using translationally relevant imaging and biomarker analyses. 7 , 41 Dirk Keene (University of Washington) underscored the importance of modernized human neuropathology as the reference standard for experimental models. 42 Roberta Marongiu (Weill Cornell Medicine) demonstrated that endocrine aging accelerates AD pathology, identifying menopause as a biological risk factor and using an ovarian failure model to capture this important biological feature in mice. 43 Natalia Chermont (UC San Diego) introduced space‐induced neural aging in human brain organoids 44 to model neurodegeneration in compressed timeframes. Carol Shively (Wake Forest University) described longitudinal studies in vervet monkeys linking early amyloid pathology, metabolic dysfunction, and functional decline. 45 , 46 Hong‐Wei Dong (UC Los Angeles) presented an artificial intelligence–driven whole‐brain imaging platform for large‐scale mapping of AD pathology and connectomics studies 47 in AD mouse models. Marcelo P. Coba (University of Southern California) examined APOE‐dependent vascular dysfunction in mice and degus, advancing the neurovascular hypothesis of AD. 48 By placing AD within the broader context of aging biology, the session reinforced that neurodegeneration needs to be studied along with organismal aging processes.

A COMPARATIVE FRAMEWORK FOR TRANSLATIONAL MODELING IN AD

Collectively, the breadth of species represented across all six thematic sessions reflects the AD field's continued growth and acknowledgement of the necessity for research using animal models to understand the mechanisms that drive pathogenesis and progression of AD to identify approaches for treatment and prevention. Figure 3 summarizes the distribution of animal models featured across talks and poster presentations and compares key AD‐relevant neuropathological and functional features in selected species discussed at UAMAA 2026. The comparison highlights important cross‐species differences in the emergence and coupling of these features, emphasizing the limitations of traditional mouse models and the potential of unconventional species to better recapitulate aspects of human disease pathogenesis. These observations support a central perspective emerging from UAMAA 2026: Leveraging diverse, complementary model systems within a comparative framework is essential for improving translational alignment with human AD. By capturing naturally occurring, age‐dependent, and multifactorial disease processes, these systems may enhance prediction of therapeutic efficacy relative to conventional transgenic approaches. Many also exhibit behavioral, neuropathological, and biomarker features that more closely parallel human disease, including spontaneous pathology and cognitive decline, and can serve as intermediate platforms bridging mouse studies and clinical cohorts when paired with multimodal and longitudinal designs.

Within this framework, we propose a practical set of criteria for evaluating the translational relevance of unconventional models. 7 , 35 , 49 , 50 These may include: (1) neuropathological resemblance defined by the presence, progression, and spatial distribution of key AD features such as amyloid, tau, neuroinflammation, and neurovascular dysfunction; (2) cognitive and behavioral validity, encompassing longitudinal assessment of domains relevant to human behavior in the context of diagnostic disease criteria; (3) physiological aging trajectories, reflecting natural, age‐dependent progression rather than accelerated or artificial phenotypes; (4) alignment with human biomarkers, including imaging, fluid (cerebrospinal fluid/plasma), and molecular signatures; (5) mechanistic concordance, particularly conservation of gene regulatory programs, cell‐type vulnerability, and circuit‐level dysfunction; (6) evolutionary conserved and divergent mechanisms that may indicate unique human vulnerability to disease. Taken together, these criteria provide a practical framework for systematic cross‐species evaluations toward improving the predictive power of preclinical AD research.

At the same time, several key scientific debates and open questions define priorities for the field. A central issue concerns the extent to which natural aging models recapitulate the full spectrum of human AD, particularly the coordinated progression of amyloid, tau, and neuroinflammatory processes. While many unconventional species exhibit subsets of these hallmarks, it remains unclear whether they capture the complete pathological cascade or instead reflect distinct, species‐specific trajectories. In parallel, the lack of standardized, cross‐species benchmarks for assessing cognitive decline, neuropathology, and molecular signatures limits direct comparison and translational interpretation. Addressing these gaps will require integrated multimodal approaches, longitudinal study designs, and shared protocols for harmonizing phenotypic and molecular readouts across species. Significant practical challenges also remain, particularly for large‐animal and non‐human primate models, which entail substantial cost, logistical complexity, and limited scalability. Variability across species, cohorts, and research sites further complicates standardization, reproducibility, and data integration, underscoring the need for coordinated multi‐site efforts and harmonized phenotyping strategies.

As a community emphasizing the need for improved model systems for studying AD, UAMAA conference attendees were excited to couple the conference platform with a special issue on “Transforming the relevance of preclinical research in AD and related dementias by development and use of novel animal models and cellular systems” with support from the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia: Translational Research & Clinical Interventions. This special issue aligns closely with the scientific themes and translational priorities of UAMAA, providing a formal publication venue for original research and reviews that extend the meeting's conceptual framework, disseminate cutting‐edge findings within the community but also to contribute lasting scientific resources that inform AD research, new and emerging model development, and paradigm‐shifting therapeutic strategy approaches.

The UAMAA 2026 conference expanded a global research network of unconventional models of AD and aging. As the field advances toward multimodal and multi‐scale approaches, discussions underscored the urgent need for harmonized/coordinated data sharing, standardized cross‐species phenotyping, integrated multi‐omic frameworks, and closer integration with human brain atlas and clinical datasets. Looking ahead, UAMAA will continue to serve as a vital forum for the translational frontier of AD and aging research. The organizers will announce details of the next UAMAA conference, scheduled for 2028, to be hosted by The University of Pittsburgh.
