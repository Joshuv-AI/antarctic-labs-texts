Recycle and reuse: an ethological perspective for citizen science data on Lucanus cervus (Coleoptera: Lucanidae)
Authors: Silvia Gisondi, Fiorenza Augusta Spotti, Alice Lenzi, Donato Antonio Grasso, Alessandro Campanaro, Cristina Castracani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCitizen science (CS) has been proven to be a powerful approach for biodiversity monitoring, offering large-scale and long-term data collection opportunities. This study explores the possibility of reusing images collected under a CS initiative aimed at mapping the distribution of the European stag beetle (Lucanus cervus [L.]) in Italy for ethological purposes. Volunteers contributed 2,090 photographic records and, among them, more than one-third were valid for ethological purposes. These records were analyzed to investigate specific behaviors, exploited substrate and seasonal preferences, considering specimen number and sex. Single males represent the majority of the sample, and the most represented couple is male–female. Regarding the ethological traits, the most recorded one is standing/walking for single specimens (both males and females) and reproductive behavior for couples. In addition, a sex-related seasonal variation has been observed with single individuals recorded mainly in July and couples in June. Moreover, substrate preferences indicated males’ affinity for natural wood and females’ inclination toward bare ground. However, the lack of detailed environmental cues limited additional behavioral insights. This being given, the present study underscores the possibility of leveraging volunteer-collected data from an existing CS initiative for ethological purposes, even if some limitations were encountered. For this reason, good practices are provided in order to ensure a broader applicability of ecological and behavioral research in current or future CS projects.

Introduction

In the last decade, public participation in scientific research, commonly known as citizen science (CS), has gained momentum in several fields of science (Bonney et al. 2009, ECSA 2015, Kullenberg and Kasperowski 2016, Haklay et al. 2021), in particular with successful applications in environmental research and biodiversity monitoring (Theobald et al. 2015, Chandler et al. 2017, Fraisl et al. 2022, Austen et al. 2024). Moreover, it has also been proven that the CS approach provides an added value to projects and initiatives in respect to the volunteers’ personal growth (Vohland et al. 2021), as well as to society in general (Bonney et al. 2016, Wehn et al. 2021). Generating new knowledge with the help of volunteers, CS offers a valuable complement to traditional science, ideal for long-term, large-scale data collection (Losey et al. 2012, Dennis et al. 2017, Soroye et al. 2018, Callaghan et al. 2020).

It is this exact feature that possibly triggered the development of several CS projects in the entomological field. Several insect orders have been targeted, ranging from Hymenoptera to Coleoptera, Lepidoptera, and much more (e.g. Lucky et al. 2014, Campanaro et al. 2017, Carpaneto et al. 2017, Castracani et al. 2020, Bardiani et al. 2022, van Swaay et al. 2022, Whitehorn et al. 2022, Giannetti et al. 2023, Lakeman-Fraser et al. 2023, Lenzi et al. 2023). One of the key aspects to consider when discussing CS is data quality (ie the verification of records collected by volunteers) and the strategies needed to ensure it (Crall et al. 2011, Brown and Williams 2019, Balázs et al. 2021). In particular, in projects focused on biodiversity monitoring, it is essential to adopt measures that guarantee the reliability of the data collected by volunteers. In the case of protected species monitoring, one of the most common approaches is to involve experts in the validation process to enhance data reliability and suitability for scientific analysis (Kosmala et al. 2016, Aceves-Bueno et al. 2017).

In this context, the CS project LIFE MIPP (“Monitoring of insects with public participation”—LIFE11 NAT/IT/000252; Mason et al. 2015, Campanaro et al. 2017, Carpaneto et al. 2017) could be considered a case study. Born in 2012 with EU funding, the project changed its name to InNat in 2017 and continued up to 2024 under national funding. These two initiatives can be referred to as one (InNat hereafter) since they share the same objectives and feed the same database, and the target species only increased from 2012 on. In fact, 40 were the targets that could be counted at the end of the project: these included all protected species (and habitats), ranging from Coleoptera to Lepidoptera, Odonata, and Orthoptera (Campanaro et al. 2024, Lenzi et al. 2025), all recorded at the Italian national scale. The most recorded one was Lucanus cervus (L.) (Coleoptera: Lucanidae), a very eye-catching species of lucanid beetle, whose charisma is mainly dependent on the well-developed mandibles exhibited by males, which also gave the species the vernacular name “stag beetle.”

Lucanus cervus (L.) is the largest saproxylic beetle in Europe, and it inhabits mature deciduous forests, especially the lowland and medium-altitude oak woodlands, and requires rotten deadwood at ground level (Bardiani et al. 2017, Kuźmiński et al. 2020). Due to its ecological function and habitat preferences, this species is considered an umbrella species, as it plays a rather relevant role for the identification and conservation of suitable habitats for other saproxylic beetles (Thomaes et al. 2008a, 2008b). Moreover, the species is protected under the European legislation (i.e. included in Annex II of the Habitats Directive, 92/43/EEC) and considered “Near threatened” by the European IUCN Red List (Cálix et al. 2018).

Widely spread throughout Europe (Franciscolo 1997, Bartolozzi and Sprecher-Uebersax 2006, Harvey et al. 2011, Bardiani et al. 2017, Méndez and Thomaes 2021), adults of L. cervus are characterized by a strong sexual dimorphism: males are usually bigger in size and exhibit large mandibles, longer than their heads (although subject to great intraspecific variability, cfr. Harvey and Gange 2006, Hardersen et al. 2011, Romiti et al. 2015), while females are usually smaller and own shorter mandibles, no longer than the head. In central and southern regions of Italy (including Sicily), L. cervus is replaced by the congeneric Lucanus tetraodon Thunberg (Miksic 1959a, 1959b, 1961, Franciscolo 1997, Lapiana and Sparacio 2006, Santoro et al. 2009), even though these two species are syntopic in a number of areas in Central Italy (Bartolozzi 1986, Bartolozzi and Maggini 2007, Cortellessa et al. 2014, Solano et al. 2016), and many specimens exhibit intermediate morphological characters between the two species and are considered hybrids (Santoro et al. 2009).

Given its status of protected species, the ecology of L. cervus has been widely studied by many authors in order to define effective monitoring methods: e.g. aerial flight interception traps (Harvey et al. 2011), collection of adults remains along transects (Campanaro et al. 2011, Harvey et al. 2011), regular transects performed at dusk (Campanaro et al. 2016, Bardiani et al. 2017, Thomaes et al. 2021). Moreover, considering the distinct and recognizable morphological characteristics of the species and its undeniable charisma, CS approaches have been also explored for surveying the presence of L. cervus (Percy et al. 2000, Smith 2003, 2011, Moretti and Sprecher-Uebersax 2004, Méndez et al. 2017, Thomaes et al. 2021, Bardiani et al. 2022, Giannetti et al. 2023).

From a behavioral perspective, L. cervus remains comparatively understudied. While its phenology, abundance, and habitat preferences have been investigated across Europe—both through traditional fieldwork and CS (e.g. Méndez et al. 2017, Thomaes et al. 2021, Giannetti et al. 2023)—little is known about its ethological traits in natural conditions. Specific behaviors such as reproduction, aggression (e.g. fighting or threatening postures), feeding, and dispersal have been described through direct observation (e.g. Kawano 1997, 2000, Giannetti et al. 2024) or telemetry (Tini et al. 2017, 2018, Thomaes et al. 2018). Most of these studies focus on reproductive strategies, which have also been analyzed in relation to the morphological traits that characterize male individuals (Tatsuta et al. 2001, Romiti et al. 2015, 2017, Giannetti et al. 2024), but large-scale behavioral datasets are still missing.

Further research is needed to explore key behavioral aspects such as daily activity rhythms, context-dependent behavioral plasticity, intraspecific variability in mating strategies, antipredator behavior, mechanisms of animal communication (e.g. chemical or vibrational cues), and orientation or navigation in the environment. This context highlights the potential of using CS not only to monitor species presence or abundance but also to explore behavioral ecology at a broader scale. Additionally, although CS has proven to be a powerful tool for the study and monitoring of a wide range of insect species (e.g. van Swaay et al. 2008, Lucky et al. 2014, Appenfeller et al. 2020, Castracani et al. 2020, Potts et al. 2020, Thomaes et al. 2021, Lenzi et al. 2023, Gisondi et al. 2025b), to our best knowledge, few CS initiatives explicitly include behavioral data for invertebrates. Notable exceptions are limited to species such as Asilidae flies (Barahona-Segovia and Pañinao-Monsálvez 2020) or hornets (e.g. Vespa crabro, Pusceddu et al. 2022), where citizens’ observations provided insights into prey capture or territorial behaviors. However, CS would represent a valuable tool for the behavioral studies, allowing for a faster and wider data collection, as demonstrated by already active ethological CS projects targeting noninsect species (e.g. Hecht and Rice 2015, Goodenough et al. 2017, Frigerio et al. 2018, Fukimoto et al. 2023). At the same time, these studies are not exempt from potential biases, particularly in the case of unstructured CS projects. In this approach, participants are free to choose when, where, and how to record observations, often following non-standardized or flexible protocols (Kelling et al. 2019). This lack of methodological rigor can lead to uneven sampling effort and taxonomic or ecological biases. Such limitations are especially relevant in the case of L. cervus, a species with distinct biological traits that may influence detectability. For instance, males are more easily detected due to their larger body size and greater mobility, whereas females are generally smaller, more elusive, and less conspicuous. These differences can lead to a sex-based bias in observations, potentially affecting the representativeness and reliability of the collected data.

The present paper aims at analyzing data collected by InNat volunteers, originally intended for monitoring the species’ presence and distribution, exploring the possibility of reusing them for ethological purposes (secondary data purpose, cfr. Pernat et al. 2024). In fact, many stag beetle behaviors can be detected and classified on photographic evidence. These records were analyzed to investigate expressed behaviors as well as substrate and seasonal preferences. As a secondary point, the same records were also used to extract data on volunteer participation. Finally, the present paper also discusses best practices for the development of new CS-based behavioral studies as well as recommendations for ongoing CS projects to recalibrate their objectives to include ethological purposes.

Materials and Methods

Dataset Building

The original InNat dataset comprised 2,090 photographic records of L. cervus from Italian national territory. These data were collected by 418 volunteers over a very large time span, with the oldest record reported from 1994 and the newest one from 2022. Each record consisted of a photograph, eight descriptive parameters, and a “note” field that was not mandatory for the volunteers (see Table 1 for list and definitions). Once each field was filled in, records proceeded to expert taxonomical ID validation for the final data check and acceptance (please refer to the Acknowledgment section for further details).

The selection of records suitable for ethological analysis was conducted by applying three filtering criteria. First, we deleted images where only parts of the insect were visible, not the whole specimen (filter: “Parts”). Next, we excluded images with clearly or probably dead insects (e.g. antennas touching the substrate, legs gathered near the body, or notes explicitly indicating this condition; filter: “Dead”). Finally, we removed images where insects were clearly being manipulated by observers (i.e. the observer’s hand was visible; filter: “Manipulation”).

All images of the eligible records (i.e. portraying alive and non-manipulated specimens of L. cervus) were processed to extract a series of parameters needed for behavioral analyses: no. of specimens, sex, substrate, and behavior (see Table 2 for list and definitions).

Analysis of Volunteer Participation

According to the level of personal engagement (number of submitted records), the volunteers (contributors) were organized into four categories: “Visitor Contributors,” who contributed only a single record; “Occasional Contributors,” who provided between two and nine records; “Regular Contributors,” who submitted between 10 and 89 records; and “Super Contributors,” who contributed more than 90 records. Ranges for each category were set according to the distribution of the data. For each group, the number of contributors and the number of records were calculated.

Results

After a preliminary filtering process, a total of 821 valid records were classified as “Eligible” and thus retained, while the rest were discarded through the following procedure: 376 records were excluded according to the filter “Parts,” then 179 records were excluded due to the filter “Dead,” and finally 714 records were excluded due to filter “Manipulation” (Fig. 1). The same operation conducted separately for records with one specimen (N = 2001) and with two specimens (N = 76) showed that the percentage of excluded data was lower in the case of two specimens (14.47%) compared to one single specimen (62.57%) (Fig. 1).

Individuals and Substrates

The analysis of the distribution of the specimens according to the substrate shows that, generally, the preferred substrate is Natural wood with a total of 290 records, followed by Artificial substrate (191 records), Bare ground (149 records), Stone (84 records), Grass (46 records), and Tree branches (34 records). For every substrate, single male specimen (M) is the most represented category followed by single female specimen (F), but for Natural wood and Tree branches, the differences are more evident (Natural wood: M = 67.2%, F = 15.5%; Tree branches: M = 97.1%, F = 2.9%) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S2).

The statistical analysis using crosstabs shows a significant relationship between the sex of the specimens and the substrate where the specimens were recorded (χ2(15) = 110.60, P < 0.001). The analysis of standardized residuals showed that males were found on tree branches more than expected, females were found more on bare ground and stone and less on natural wood and tree branches, MF couples were found more on natural wood and less on artificial substrate, and, finally, the other couples (NN) were found more in natural wood (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S2).

Individuals and Behaviors (Single Specimen)

Among the one-specimen records (N = 743), three behaviors were identified: standing/walking (675 records), flying (54 records), and feeding (14 records). For both single male (M) and female (F) specimens, the most expressed behavior is standing/walking (M: 88.0% and F: 97.0%), followed by flying (M: 9.4% and F: 2.6%) and feeding (M: 2.6% and F: 0.4%) (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S3).

The statistical analysis using crosstabs shows a significant relationship between the sex of the specimen and the behavior (χ2 (2) = 15.84, P < 0.001). The analysis of standardized residuals shows that single female specimens tend to fly less than expected (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S3).

Considering only the most recorded behavior (standing/walking), the analysis of the distribution of the two sexes shows that the preferred substrate is Natural wood (216 total records) as well, followed by Artificial substrate (173 records), Bare ground (132 records), Stone (77 records), Grass (42 records), and Tree branches (25 records), although there are some differences between sexes (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table S4).

The statistical analysis using crosstabs shows a significant relationship between the sex of specimens and the substrate (χ2(5) = 49.13, P < 0.001). The analysis of standardized residuals shows that single male specimens (M) are found more frequently than expected in Natural wood, whereas single female specimens (F) are found more frequently in Bare ground and Stone and less frequently in Natural wood and Tree branches (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S4).

Individuals and Behaviors (Couples)

Among the two-specimen records (N = 64), four behaviors have been identified: reproductive behavior (38 records), standing/walking (20 records), aggressive behavior (five records), and feeding (two records) (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Table S5).

The statistical analysis using crosstabs shows a significant relationship between the couple type (MF or NN) and the behavior (χ2(3) = 40.28, P < 0.001). The standardized residuals analysis shows that MF couples perform the reproductive behavior more frequently than expected, whereas NN couples show aggressive and walking/standing behaviors more frequently than expected (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S5).

Among the 418 InNat volunteers, all four categories of contributors (visitors, occasional contributors, regular contributors, and super contributors) were represented. The most represented category is visitors (59.9%), followed by the occasional contributors (33.7%), regular contributors (6.0%), and super contributors (0.7%). Despite the paucity of super contributors, they account for 34.40% of the total analyzed records (Ntot = 2090), and together with the regular contributors, they reach up to 66.4% of total analyzed records. Discussion

The aim of the present paper was to analyze data collected by InNat volunteers, originally intended for monitoring the species’ presence and distribution, in order to explore the possibility of reusing them for ethological purposes. Lucanus cervus was taken as an example due to its ethological relevance and thanks to the high number of records collected over the years by InNat volunteers. Of all L. cervus records, the ones deemed valid for ethological purposes represent more than one-third of the total (39.0%), with photographs of single specimens having the highest share. This result is impressive given that these photographs were aimed differently, ie study of the distribution of this species over Italian national territory (Gisondi et al. 2025b, Lenzi et al. 2025), and no specific guidelines have been given to the volunteers on how to take the pictures. Moreover, the overall highest share of single specimen photographs suggests that this species tends to lead a solitary life, as already reported in Tini et al. (2017), up until mating.

It must be highlighted that, in the case of single specimen photographs, most of the records would have been useful for ethological analyses, though many (35.0%) had to be discarded due to the presence of human disturbance or manipulation. The same does not apply in the case of images portraying two specimens, whose discard rate was much lower (only ca. 7.0%). This, as well, suggests a difference in the attitude of volunteers who appear more respectful in the case of beetle interactions than in the case of single specimens, though always considering the sound difference in the numbers of single-specimen versus two-specimen images (ca. 26:1). A possible explanation could be that, in the case of couples, volunteers are less intrusive because they develop interest in the observed behavior. This given, notwithstanding the highest number of single-specimen photographs, the most ethologically informative ones are images portraying 2 specimens.

Considering the sex ratio, it soundly leans toward males: males are more than double in respect to females (i.e. 511 and 238, respectively). This finding is not unexpected due to the much more elusive habits of females, which make them less easily visible and findable (Tini et al. 2017, 2018), as well as the preference and fascination for the large-jawed males who are more attractive to the public.

Concerning seasonal variations, the analyses conducted for the entire dataset follow what is already known about the stag beetle phenology in Italy (Campanaro et al. 2011, Bardiani et al. 2017) and Europe (Thomaes et al. 2021), having July as the month with the highest number of records, followed by June, August, and May. If males and females are considered separately, single male and single female specimens were increasingly recorded from May to July, then increasingly dropped in August, near the end of the stag beetle phenological season; on the other hand, the highest number of male–female couple records can be found in June, then records slightly decreased in July until being almost absent in August. These results are in line with previous research (Harvey et al. 2011), highlighting the habits of the species: single specimens are abundant in the first part of the season, and couples tend to be established quite early; after copulation, females oviposit (July), and specimens start decreasing (August).

Regarding the substrate analyses, males are found preferentially on natural wood and artificial substrate, while females mainly on artificial substrate and bare ground and secondarily natural wood. These results are in line with previous research as well (Tini et al. 2017, 2018), demonstrating that male stag beetles tend to lead more spatially dispersed lives in respect to females, which are more terricolous. However, during the breeding season, males tend to use tree branches as fighting arenas where females are also lured to for mating purposes (Tini et al. 2017, 2018). Females, on the other hand, except for the breeding season in which they tend to attend tree branches as well, exhibit more terricolous habits, which relate to the need to bury themselves or their eggs under the ground for protection (Harvey et al. 2011, Bardiani et al. 2017, Tini et al. 2017). In the case of the substrate category, natural wood, it was not possible to discriminate whether the trunks or the branches were dead or alive, on the ground or standing: such information would have been interesting in order to relate it with the life stages of L. cervus, since dead and rotting wood constitutes the most important part of the diet for its larvae. The results of the analysis focused on the substrate employed during the standing/walking behavior (ie the most recorded one) do not deviate from the above-mentioned results.

The most common substrate on which couples were recorded has been natural wood: given the ecology of the species, it is not unexpected to observe male–female and male–male couples on this substrate, especially during the breeding season, with different purposes, either mating or fighting, respectively. On the other hand, in the case of female–female couples, the few evaluated records do not allow for further discussion.

In the case of the sex vs. behavior analyses, especially for single-specimen photographs, it is hard to grasp the exhibited behavior. In fact, the behavioral categories are only three, with the “standing/walking” category having the highest share. This result highlights the difficulty in deciphering the observed behaviors due to the lack of many environmental and specific cues, such as the presence of other sexes, specimens, or animals nearby and the state of the specimen (e.g. just landed, about to soar, exploring the space with the antennae). The lack of a “scenario” description by the volunteers results in substantial data loss, which could be avoided just by requesting volunteers for a short and simple description. As an example, the presence of exposed wings was registered as flight even though it does not guarantee a totally correct interpretation of borderline cases of flight behavior (ie cases where the specimens are not seen hovering in the air) precisely due to the lack of specific description. The same applies for the “standing/walking” category, where, with the available data, it is not possible to discern whether the animal was standing still or moving, and thus, without any further descriptive comment from the volunteers, an important piece of information, useful for defining the activity cycle of the insect, is lacking. The only clearly detectable behaviors are feeding and flight, and these were almost exclusively performed by males, with the crosstabs analysis suggesting that females tend to fly less than expected: this could be due to the fact that males do disperse more than females, and thus they need energy (Tini et al. 2018).

Furthermore, in the case of two-specimen photographs, four behaviors are observed, with the reproductive one having the highest share, followed by standing/walking, aggression, and feeding. Given the high number of male–female couples, it is not surprising to state that the most common observed behavior is reproduction, even though it is not possible to discriminate between an effective copula and a mate-guarding event (i.e. no additional information is available regarding whether the genitalia of the two individuals are actually in contact or what happened before and/or after the observed behavior). This lack in the behavior interpretation, as already mentioned above for single-specimen behaviors, could be easily solved by requiring a brief specific description of the scenario in which the behavior is observed: one of the major factors enabling the discrimination of a reproductive copula from a mate-guarding copula is, for example, the presence of other males in the area. Some volunteers did state the presence of other stag beetles nearby, but they did not state the number, the sex, and the distance from the couples, all of these being essential elements for a correct interpretation of a behavior.

Concerning volunteers’ participation, it was possible to detect the presence of four categories of contributors. Among them, the key categories for the creation of the original InNat database were the “super contributors,” representing a minority of volunteers, and the “regular contributors”. These two categories together accounted for two-thirds of total records. However, data show that, despite their efficiency, these volunteers tend to collect records always in the same site, thus creating an overrepresentation of data in certain areas. This skewed pattern of contribution—where a small number of highly active users generates most of the data—is common in CS projects and has been previously documented, for instance, in biodiversity monitoring (Martellos et al. 2021, Gisondi et al. 2025a), astronomy-related human computation initiatives (Ponciano and Brasileiro 2014), and participatory platforms more broadly (Aristeidou et al. 2017). Thus, if taken alone, these records would not be representative of the real distribution of the species. A balance between the two categories, “super” and “regular,” and the other two, “visitors” and “occasional,” contributors would be desirable since it would guarantee a more evenly distributed sampling. Nevertheless, having a high number of records from the same location could be interesting for other purposes, such as targeting areas for ethological studies. Moreover, above-average contributors could be crucial during the beta phases of any ethological update of already ongoing CS projects: in fact, they could test the new features as expert citizens, reporting any possible difficulty or issue to the scientific team of the CS project. This would result in a powerful exchange of ideas between scientists and citizens, leading to the co-creation and development of a new ethologically valuable CS feature in an already existing CS project.

In conclusion, using the above-mentioned CS initiative as a proxy, we here suggest a series of good practices (Table 4): if implemented in ongoing or future CS projects, these good practices will make data collected by volunteers suitable and valuable for both distributional studies and ethological analyses. First of all, any disturbance must be avoided: the specimen must not be too closely observed in order to record a behavior that is not influenced by the presence and disturbance of volunteers. Once the performed behavior reaches the end, the sex (and the species, if needed) of the specimen needs to be registered: this can be done by taking close-up (or zoomed) pictures of the specimen or by reporting the sex (and species) in the note section of the record. Then, the attention needs to be focused on the nearby environment: are there any other specimens nearby? How distant are they from the observed specimen(s)? Moreover, a short description of the substrate must be given. All this information will help researchers to correctly interpret doubtful behaviors (eg copula and mate-guarding) and to discriminate between similar substrates (e.g. dead or rotting wood vs. alive wood). The last suggestion concerns the size of the observed specimens: this information is usually hard to report due to the stochasticity of such records, but it would be a good practice to always give some clues on the dimensions of the recorded specimens. In order not to disturb the individuals, a comparison can be made (and reported) with a commonly known object or, in the case of couples, a comparison between the two specimens can be recorded (i.e. one is clearly bigger than the other; one is half the size of the other). This information could be used as a reference to get an indication of the specimen(s) size (although having a measuring instrument, e.g. a ruler, would be recommended if the measurement does not harm, disturb, or request manipulation of the observed specimens). Due to the unprecedented spread of internet-based solutions and apps for smartphones, it would not be too time-consuming for volunteers to fill in all the requested information.
