Lysozyme-induced 3D assembly of quercetin-zinc rod-like nanocolloids and its functional impact on antioxidant, gut microbiota, and lipid metabolism
Authors: Shuang Tian, Haiyan Tai, Yuxin Cheng, Keshan Wang, Lu Yuan, Xiaotan Jiang, Xingwang Liu, Tingting Zhu, Yuanyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quercetin exhibits antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid metabolism-regulating activities, but poor aqueous stability limits its functional application. Here, quercetin–Zn2+–lysozyme nanorod colloids (QZLs) were constructed as a ternary assembly system to balance quercetin structural stability with gastrointestinal functional accessibility and enhance its microbiota-modulating potential during colonic fermentation. Quercetin coordinated with Zn2+ to form QZ nanorods, followed by lysozyme-mediated hierarchical three-dimensional assembly through electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic interactions, and possible Zn2+–amino acid residue coordination. QZLs improved quercetin storage stability in aqueous systems, retaining more than 95% ABTS radical-scavenging capacity after 20 days. In vitro colonic fermentation showed that QZLs reshaped gut microbiota, enriched beneficial taxa including Mediterraneibacter and Bifidobacterium, and promoted SCFAs production. QZL-20 (lysozyme concentration of 20 μg/mL) reduced lipid droplet accumulation and TG levels and downregulated PLIN2 expression. This lipid-lowering effect may be associated with stabilized quercetin delivery and increased SCFAs. These findings indicate that Zn2+-mediated coordination and lysozyme-assisted assembly enhance quercetin stability, microbiota modulation, and lipid-lowering potential.

Introduction

Functional foods possess the potential to promote host health, among which polyphenolic compounds, characterized by excellent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory activities, represent an important class of functional bioactive constituents (Ed Nignpense et al., 2021). Polyphenolic compounds have shown great potential in the prevention of metabolic diseases, particularly in the prominent effects on the amelioration of intestinal inflammatory diseases (Gowd et al., 2019). The intestine is recognized as a critical site for the action of functional bioactive components. Polyphenols not only act directly on the intestinal epithelium and mucosal barrier, but also indirectly modulate systemic homeostasis by regulating SCFAs production and inflammatory responses (Luo et al., 2025). Quercetin is one of the most extensively studied representative molecules among dietary polyphenols and exhibits pronounced biological activities, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, lipid metabolism regulation, and improvement of insulin resistance. These biological effects are closely associated with the modulation of signaling pathways such as Nrf2/ARE, NF-κB, and MAPK, which are widely involved in oxidative stress control, inflammatory regulation, and epithelial barrier protection (Khan et al., 2016). However, although these molecular mechanisms have been relatively well documented, the efficient delivery of sufficient quercetin to relevant gastrointestinal and metabolic sites remains an unresolved challenge. Owing to its strong hydrophobicity, poor aqueous solubility, insufficient chemical stability in gastrointestinal environments, and low oral bioavailability, quercetin often fails to maintain an effective local concentration at the target sites required to trigger biological responses (Williamson, 2025). Therefore, the key limitation in quercetin application lies not only in whether it possesses intrinsic bioactivity, but also in how to stabilize and deliver bioaccessible quercetin at sufficient levels to the intestinal and related metabolic microenvironments. Accordingly, the construction of food-grade delivery systems to improve the aqueous stability, gastrointestinal bioaccessibility, and delivery of polyphenols to the colonic fermentation environment has become an important direction in functional food research.

Proteins, owing to their excellent biocompatibility, amphiphilic structures, and modifiable functional groups, possess inherent advantages in improving the solubility of polyphenols and enhancing their digestion, absorption, and in vivo bioaccessibility. They are regarded as important food-grade carriers for the construction of polyphenol delivery systems (Zhang et al., 2025). Via hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic forces, proteins interact with polyphenols to form noncovalent complexes. Under certain conditions, protein residues may undergo covalent cross-linking with oxidized polyphenol products, thereby improving the water solubility, dispersibility and thermal or light stability of polyphenols. These interactions contribute to the protection, delivery, and sustained release of active compounds (Li et al., 2024). Lysozyme, a cationic antimicrobial protein derived from egg white and other sources, has been widely applied in food preservation and pharmaceutical formulations (Yang et al., 2025). On the one hand, the construction of a polyphenol-protein complex by conjugating quercetin with lysozyme enhances the physicochemical stability and delivery efficiency of quercetin. On the other hand, it synergistically modulates the gut microbial niche and SCFAs metabolism by leveraging the ability of lysozyme to specifically lyse the cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria and the cationic surface properties (Liu et al., 2025). Notably, after complex assembly with quercetin and Zn2+, the lytic activity of lysozyme may no longer behave as a fully free, nonspecific action, but may instead be modulated by nanostructural encapsulation, interfacial adsorption, and steric shielding effects (Wang et al., 2025). This may help reduce excessive nonspecific disturbance to beneficial Gram-positive bacteria while retaining the functional potential of lysozyme as a food-grade cationic protein, a multibinding-site carrier, and a microbiota-modulating component.

The construction of polyphenol–metal–protein ternary complexes using metal ions as coordination bridges has emerged as a promising strategy for the design of functional factor carriers. Metal ions can coordinate with the ortho-dihydroxyl groups of polyphenols and specific residues of proteins, thereby synergistically strengthening polyphenol–protein crosslinking and driving molecular self-assembly into structurally ordered and stable nanoparticles (Wang et al., 2025). Among metal ions, Zn2+ is not only a chemical coordination linker, but also an essential nutritional trace element closely associated with intestinal mucosal integrity, immune homeostasis, and intestinal barrier function. For quercetin, the catechol-type 3′,4′-ortho-dihydroxyl group on the B ring represents an important metal-coordination site, while other oxygen-containing sites, such as the C-ring C=O(4)/OH(3) and A-ring OH(5), may also participate in coordination. These coordination sites can facilitate the formation of quercetin–metal complexes and promote the construction of ordered polyphenol–metal structures, thereby enhancing the stability and bioactivity of polyphenol delivery systems. Therefore, constructing a quercetin–Zn2+–lysozyme ternary assembly system provides a rational food-grade strategy for improving the functional performance of quercetin.

In summary, this study established a ternary nanocolloidal system by incorporating lysozyme into the quercetin–Zn2+ coordination-driven self-assembly system. In this system, quercetin first coordinates with Zn2+ to form quercetin–Zn2+ rod-like nanocolloids, while lysozyme further provides molecular protection and spatial support through its cationic surface, hydrophobic domains, and multiple binding sites, thereby promoting the formation of hierarchical three-dimensional quercetin–Zn2+–lysozyme nanocolloids. This strategy was designed to improve the aqueous stability of quercetin and effectively preserve its functional bioactivity. The microstructure, molecular interactions, and antioxidant activity of QZ and QZLs nanocolloids were further investigated. This study employed an in vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion–colonic fermentation model to assess whether QZLs could maintain a high level of structural integrity during gastrointestinal digestion, thereby reaching the colonic fermentation environment with the capacity to further modulate gut microbiota composition and SCFAs production. Furthermore, a hepatocellular steatosis model was employed to examine the inhibitory effects of QZLs on lipid accumulation and PLIN2-associated lipid droplet regulation. This study provides a feasible strategy for improving the processing stability and functional efficacy of quercetin, and offers a theoretical basis for its application in functional foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Lysozyme was purchased from Anhui Rongda Food Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). Quercetin, porcine bile salts, L-cysteine, yeast extract, pepsin, and peptone were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The internal standard for SCFAs analysis, 2-ethylbutyric acid, and external standards (acetic, propionic, butyric, isobutyric, valeric, and isovaleric acids) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of QZ and QZLs nanocolloids

ZnCl₂ solution (1 mL, 10 mg/mL), quercetin in absolute ethanol (4 mL, 10 mg/mL), and sodium citrate solution (5 mL, 20 mg/mL) were sequentially introduced into 100 mL of deionized water. Absolute ethanol was used only to pre-dissolve quercetin because of its poor aqueous solubility. After addition to 100 mL of deionized water, the final ethanol concentration in the assembly system was approximately 4% (v/v). After thorough mixing, the dispersion was magnetically stirred at 300 rpm for 40 min at room temperature. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, and the precipitate was collected as quercetin–Zn2+ nanorods (QZ). For the preparation of quercetin–Zn2+–lysozyme complexes (QZLs), lysozyme was additionally added to the above reaction mixture to final concentrations of 2, 10, 20, or 100 μg/mL, and all subsequent procedures were kept identical. The resulting precipitate was resuspended in 15 mL of deionized water. This centrifugation–resuspension procedure minimized residual ethanol in the final QZ/QZL dispersions used for subsequent physicochemical characterization, in vitro digestion–fermentation, and cellular assays. The QZLs with different concentrations of lysozyme were named as QZL-2, QZL-10, QZL-20, and QZL-100, respectively.

Characterization of nanocolloids

The microstructure of the nanocolloids was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Apreo 2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental analysis. The particle size distribution and ζ-potential of QZ and QZLs were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). Samples were equilibrated at 25 °C for 2 min prior to measurement, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate. UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded at the same mass concentration using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, China). Molecular interactions were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Spectrum 100, PerkinElmer, UK) over 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1, using KBr pellets as the background. Crystalline structure was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) over a 2θ range of 5–80°. Surface functional groups and chemical composition were further evaluated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, China).

Determination of antioxidant activity

ABTS radical-scavenging activity: The ABTS stock solution was prepared by mixing 7.4 mM ABTS with 2.6 mM potassium persulfate at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and allowing the mixture to react for 12–16 h in the dark at room temperature. Prior to analysis, the stock was diluted with distilled water to an absorbance of approximately 0.70 at 734 nm to obtain the ABTS working solution. The ABTS working solution (2 mL) was mixed with 1 mL of sample solution at the same dilution (quercetin, QZ, or QZLs), incubated in the dark for 60 min, and the absorbance at 734 nm was recorded (A₁). Distilled water was used in place of the sample as the blank control (A₀). The ABTS scavenging rate was calculated as follows:ABTS radical scavenging rate%=A0−A1A0×100

DPPH radical-scavenging activity: A 0.8 mM DPPH solution was prepared in absolute ethanol, and its absorbance at 517 nm was adjusted to 0.80 to serve as the DPPH working solution. The DPPH working solution (2 mL) was mixed with 1 mL of sample solution at the same dilution (quercetin, QZ, or QZLs), incubated in the dark for 60 min, and the absorbance at 517 nm was recorded (A₁). Distilled water was used in place of the sample as the blank control (A₀). The DPPH scavenging rate was calculated as follows:DPPH radical scavenging rate%=A0−A1A0×100

In vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation

Quercetin, QZ, or QZLs solutions (20 mL, 0.5 mg/mL) were adjusted to pH 2.0, supplemented with pepsin (560 μL, 72 mg/mL; 10,786 U), and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm in the dark for 1 h to simulate gastric digestion. The pH was then adjusted to 6.5, followed by the addition of simulated intestinal fluid (12 mL) containing bile salts (2 mg/mL) and pancreatin (10 mg/mL; trypsin/amylase/lipase activities: 180/315/180 U, respectively). The pH was further adjusted to 7.4, and the digestion was continued under the same conditions for 2 h to obtain the gastrointestinal digesta for each group. During the 2-h intestinal digestion phase, the pH was monitored at regular intervals. If any obvious deviation occurred, NaOH or HCl was used to maintain the pH at 7.4 ± 0.1, thereby minimizing the potential influence of pH fluctuations on the stability of metal–polyphenol coordination.

Four healthy adult volunteers who had not used antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics within the previous 6 months were recruited as fecal sample donors. All participants provided written informed consent before sample collection and agreed that their anonymized samples and related information would be used only for scientific research. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou University (Approval No. HMEE-GZU-2026-T054). Fresh fecal samples were collected and homogenized with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 mol/L) at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) to prepare a fecal slurry. In vitro colonic fermentation was conducted using an anaerobic basal medium (per 1 L) containing peptone (2 g), yeast extract (2 g), Tween 80 (2 mL), NaCl (0.1 g), K₂HPO₄ (0.04 g), KH₂PO₄ (0.04 g), NaHCO₃ (2 g), MgSO₄·7H₂O (0.01 g), CaCl₂·2H₂O (0.01 g), L-cysteine (0.5 g), cholic acid (0.5 g), and resazurin solution (4 mL, 0.025 g/mL). The medium was brought to volume with distilled water and autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, filter-sterilized hemin chloride (10 mL, 5 mg/mL) and vitamin K₁ (1 mL, 1%, w/v) were aseptically added, followed by a filter-sterilized reducing solution (50 mL; Na₂S·9H₂O, 0.3125 g; L-cysteine, 0.3125 g; 1 mol/L NaOH, 2 mL; made up to 50 mL). The final pH of the medium was adjusted to 7.0.

The in vitro colonic fermentation system consisted of simulated gastrointestinal digesta (12 mL, 0.3 mg/mL), basal medium (28.5 mL), and fecal slurry (4.5 mL). The final fermentation system contained 10% (v/v) fecal slurry, corresponding to approximately 1% (w/v) fecal content. After thorough mixing, the cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions (5% CO₂, 5% H₂, and 90% N₂) at 37 °C for 24 h. The dose of QZLs was calculated by human-equivalent dose conversion based on the quercetin dose used in the animal study (15 mg/kg), corresponding to 1.2162 mg/kg for a 60-kg adult. After fermentation, the broths were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to separate the supernatant and pellet, which were stored at −80 °C. The pellet was used for microbial community profiling, whereas the supernatant was used for SCFAs quantification.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total microbial DNA was extracted from the pellets of in vitro colonic fermentation samples and subjected to 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. The hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified using the extracted DNA as template, and the amplicons were purified and used for library construction. Library concentrations were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Q33230, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC (v0.10.1), followed by removal of adapters and low-quality reads. Sequences were then denoised and error-corrected, merged for paired-end assembly, and screened to remove chimeras, yielding an ASV feature table.

Determination of SCFAs

Fermentation supernatant (1.20 mL) was mixed with 50% (v/v) sulfuric acid (240 μL) and the internal standard 2-ethylbutyric acid (120 μL, 7.90 mmol/L), vortexed for 10 min, and acidified at 4 °C for 1 h. Ethyl acetate (1.20 mL) was then added, followed by vortex extraction for 8 min and standing at 4 °C for 10 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 13000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the upper organic phase was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane, and stored at −80 °C for analysis within 24 h. SCFAs were quantified using gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC–FID) equipped with a DB-WAX column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), a split ratio of 1:10, and an FID temperature of 250 °C. The oven temperature program was: 105 °C for 3 min, ramped to 170 °C at 10 °C/min, then to 240 °C at 70 °C/min and held for 2 min.

Cell culture

Hepa 1–6 and HepG2 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL each) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, with medium renewal every 2 days. A steatosis model was established by supplementing the culture medium with oleic acid (OA, final concentration 0.4 mM) and palmitic acid (PA, final concentration 0.2 mM). OA and PA were first complexed with BSA to prepare OA/PA–BSA stock solutions and then diluted to the final working concentrations. Control cells received an equivalent amount of BSA vehicle to exclude the potential influence of BSA on lipid deposition outcomes. Cells were then treated according to the experimental design with QZ or different QZLs (QZL-2, QZL-10, QZL-20, and QZL-100) and incubated for 24 h.

BODIPY staining

To evaluate the effects of different QZL formulations (final concentration, 80 μM) on intracellular lipid droplet formation in hepatocytes, BODIPY fluorescent staining was performed. In all cellular experiments, the treatment concentrations of QZ and QZLs were normalized to quercetin equivalents. After treatment, cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 1 h and washed three times with PBS. Cells were then incubated with BODIPY dye (10 μM) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by three PBS washes. Coverslips were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and lipid droplet fluorescence was imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Oil red O staining

To assess intracellular lipid deposition, treated hepatocytes were subjected to Oil Red O staining. The concentrations of QZ and QZLs used in the Oil Red O staining assay were also normalized to quercetin equivalents, consistent with the BODIPY staining assay. Cells were rinsed with PBS and fixed with 10% formalin at room temperature for 20 min, followed by a rapid rinse with 60% isopropanol for 20 s. Oil Red O working solution was then added and cells were stained for 20 min at room temperature. After staining, cells were sequentially washed with 60% isopropanol and ddH₂O, and images were acquired by microscopy for comparison with the parallel BODIPY staining results.

TG and TC assays

To determine the optimal working concentration of QZL-20, HepG2 cells were treated with QZL-20 at 40, 80, or 120 μM quercetin equivalents. After treatment, cells were harvested and lysed, and intracellular total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG) were quantified using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Following color development, TC and TG were determined spectrophotometrically.

Western blot analysis

The expression of lipid metabolism–related proteins in hepatocytes was analyzed by Western blotting. After treatment, total proteins were extracted using lysis buffer containing PMSF and quantified using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PLIN2 (80362–2-RR, 1:3000) and GAPDH (60004–1-Ig, 1:10000; Proteintech). GAPDH was detected using parallel membranes loaded with the same protein samples and was used as the internal loading control for normalization of PLIN2 expression. After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (LF101, 1:10000; Epizyme) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a Tanon 5200 imaging system and quantified by densitometry with ImageJ.

Data analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graphical plotting were conducted using SPSS (IBM, USA), Origin 2021 (OriginLab, USA), and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences among groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's post hoc multiple-comparison test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Microstructural morphology analysis

The SEM images (Fig. 1B) demonstrate that lysozyme (Lys) exerts a pronounced regulatory effect on the self-assembled morphology of quercetin. In the absence of Lys, QZ exhibited well-defined, well-dispersed, square-like nanorods with lengths of approximately 2–6 μm. This morphology can be attributed to the intrinsic propensity of quercetin, a polyhydroxylated flavonoid, to undergo highly directional assembly driven by π–π stacking, thereby yielding anisotropic aggregates. Moreover, coordination of Zn2+ with quercetin—primarily involving the C-ring C=O(4)/OH(3) sites and the A-ring OH(5) group—can promote supramolecular organization, ultimately driving the formation of ordered nanorods (Andelescu et al., 2018; Nandisha et al., 2024). At a low Lys level, QZL-2 exhibited a morphology comparable to that of QZ. With increasing Lys content, QZL-10 showed pronounced localized aggregation, characterized by clustered nanorod assemblies. Upon further increasing the Lys dosage, QZL-20 gradually evolved from a 2D, planar arrangement of nanorods into a 3D nanoarchitectured assembly. In QZL-100, more abundant yet smaller 3D aggregates were observed; the boundaries of individual nanorods became indistinct and the rods appeared to interconnect, giving rise to porous clusters with an increased number of pores as the Lys content increased. These observations collectively indicate that Lys promotes the evolution of quercetin assemblies from lower-dimensional ordered structures to higher-dimensional, structurally complex 3D aggregates. Notably, the moderately porous three-dimensional structure of QZLs may enhance specific surface area and facilitate the inward diffusion of digestive enzymes, fermentation media, and microbial enzymes, thereby improving the accessibility, release, and functional performance of encapsulated quercetin during gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation. In contrast, despite its stronger structural coverage and higher antioxidant storage stability, QZL-100 may suffer from excessive lysozyme-induced surface over-coating and local structural densification, which reduce effective porosity, limit active-site exposure and release-channel accessibility, and consequently compromise its functional performance in colonic fermentation and cellular models. Mechanistically, lysozyme can act as a multivalent linking hub, associating with multiple QZ building blocks via electrostatic attraction, coordination interactions, and hydrophobic effects, while providing steric support. This, in turn, reshapes the metal-coordination–dominated self-assembly pathway of quercetin and the resulting mesoscale architecture. Zeta potential and particle size analysis

The dynamic light scattering results are presented in Fig. 2A. QZ exhibited a hydrodynamic size distribution in the range of 200–300 nm. Mittal et al. reported that metal moieties can act as capping agents to promote the homogeneous dispersion of quercetin rods and are a key factor in preventing their aggregation (Mittal et al., 2014). With increasing Lys content, the size of the nanocolloidal aggregates showed a progressive increase, in agreement with the SEM observations. At higher Lys concentrations, adsorption of Lys onto the nanorod surfaces likely promotes a "bridging" effect, whereby a single Lys molecule can simultaneously associate with multiple quercetin nanorods, thereby markedly enlarging the apparent particle size. In addition, the increased Lys dosage can induce charge neutralization, which reduces the electrostatic repulsion between nanoclusters and consequently strengthens interparticle aggregation.Fig. 2Particle size, surface charge, and structural characterization of QZLs nanocolloids. Particle size, surface charge, and structural characterization of QZLs nanocolloids. (A) Dynamic light scattering size distribution of QZLs. (B) Zeta potential of QZLs. (C) UV–vis absorption spectra. (D) Fourier transform infrared spectra. (E) X-ray diffraction patterns.

As shown in Fig. 2B, QZ exhibited a ζ-potential of −18.14 mV, which can be attributed to the deprotonated phenolic hydroxyl groups of quercetin. After the incorporation of Lys at different dosages, all QZLs remained negatively charged in aqueous dispersions, while their net surface charge displayed a non-monotonic trend, decreasing initially and then increasing. Specifically, the ζ-potentials of QZL-2, QZL-10, and QZL-20 were all below −15 mV, with QZL-10 exhibiting the most negative value (−18.96 ± 0.99 mV). The reduced net charge of QZL-2 may arise from the rapid adsorption of a low amount of positively charged lysozyme onto the quercetin nanorod surface, partially neutralizing the negative charges. With a moderate increase in lysozyme concentration, additional lysozyme molecules may undergo surface rearrangement and assembly on the nanorods, leading to an enhanced net negative charge. By contrast, at an excessive lysozyme dosage, QZL-100 showed the least negative ζ-potential (−7.98 ± 0.41 mV). This non-monotonic variation in ζ-potential is consistent with the overcharging or charge reversal phenomena commonly observed in protein–polyelectrolyte systems and protein–charged colloid systems. During the progressive adsorption of positively charged lysozyme onto the negatively charged QZ nanorod surface, surface charge neutralization, interfacial rearrangement, multilayer coverage, and local charge reversal may occur sequentially. Therefore, the ζ-potential changes observed in QZLs likely reflect a dynamic balance among electrostatic adsorption, lysozyme redistribution, multilayer surface coverage, and renewed charge compensation at high lysozyme dosages.

UV–vis absorption spectroscopy analysis

Fig. 2C shows the UV–vis absorption spectra of the nanocolloids. Free quercetin exhibited two characteristic absorption bands in the 250–400 nm region: an absorption band at 250 nm, associated with π → π*, n → π*, and n → σ* transitions and attributed to the A-ring absorption of the benzoyl system; and an absorption band at 372 nm, related to a π → π* transition and assigned to the B-ring absorption of the cinnamoyl system. QZ nanorods also exhibited two absorption peaks; however, both the 250 and 372 nm bands showed evident peak broadening. This broadening can be attributed to coordination between quercetin and Zn2+ via phenoxy oxygen atoms, which markedly alters the electron density distribution of the quercetin molecule. In addition, self-assembly driven by π–π stacking among multiple quercetin molecules leads to overlap of their electron clouds. When a wider range of adjacent wavelengths is absorbed, the corresponding excitation-associated bands become broader; consequently, broadened absorption peaks are observed in the UV–vis spectra (Rananaware et al., 2022). With increasing lysozyme concentration, the UV absorption band of QZL-100 exhibited a red shift, suggesting the formation of hydrogen-bonding interactions between the quercetin chromophore and Lys, thereby altering its electronic transition behavior. These results indicate that lysozyme was successfully incorporated into the 3D assembled nanorod architecture, which is expected to restrict the free rotation and molecular mobility of quercetin, reduce its exposure to dissolved oxygen, and consequently provide protective effects.

The peaks at 1650 and 1531 cm−1 were assigned to the stretching vibration of the conjugated peptide-bond CO group (amide I) and to the coupled vibrations of secondary N—H bending and peptide-bond C—N stretching (amide II), respectively (An et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2018). Pronounced changes in band shape were observed in the spectrum of QZ. Specifically, the absorption band at 1353 cm−1 shifted to 1382 cm−1, and the band at 1245 cm−1 exhibited a blue shift to 1263 cm−1. These shifts are likely attributable to deprotonation of quercetin phenolic hydroxyl groups (–OH) upon coordination with Zn2+, leading to the formation of phenoxide anions and subsequent formation of C–O–Zn coordination bonds. Meanwhile, the band intensity at 1093 cm−1 decreased markedly, which may result from Zn2+-mediated rearrangement of molecular packing. Notably, changes in the aryl ketone CO absorption band at 1668 cm−1 provide further evidence for metal–carbonyl coordination. This indicates that Zn2+ does not merely serve as a counterion for charge compensation, but rather acts as a structural ligand involved in the coordination-driven assembly of quercetin nanorods. Together with the shifts in the C—O absorption bands, the variation in the CO band supports the participation of both phenolic oxygen and carbonyl oxygen sites in Zn2+-mediated quercetin assembly. With the incorporation of lysozyme, the band intensity at 3315 cm−1 in QZLs gradually increased, indicating the successful loading of lysozyme onto the quercetin nanoaggregates. In addition, relative to lysozyme alone, this band in QZL-100 shifted from 3302 to 3315 cm−1, suggesting the presence of hydrogen-bonding interactions between quercetin and Lys (Li et al., 2023).

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns (Fig. 2E) showed that QZLs exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 10.8°, 12.3°, 15.9°, 24.4°, and 27.4°, indicating a partially crystalline nature. Notably, these diffraction signals were consistent with the crystalline phase features of zinc–quercetin complexes reported previously (Xu et al., 2020). No characteristic broad diffraction peak attributable to lysozyme near 2θ ≈ 22° was detected in any QZLs sample, and the diffraction peak positions remained essentially unchanged among samples with different lysozyme contents. These results suggest that lysozyme was adsorbed onto the QZLs surface in an amorphous state and did not alter the intrinsic crystal structure of QZLs. Compared with the characteristic sharp crystalline diffraction peaks of free quercetin, QZ and QZLs no longer exhibited its complete native diffraction profile, but instead mainly displayed the characteristic peaks of the Zn2+–quercetin coordination complex, indicating that, after assembly, quercetin was no longer predominantly present in its original crystalline state, but more likely existed in the nanocolloidal system in the form of coordination complexes, molecularly dispersed species, or partially amorphous phases. However, as the lysozyme content increased, the intensities of the characteristic peaks around 10.8°, 15.9°, and 24.4° gradually decreased. This attenuation is likely due to Lys-induced aggregation and crosslinking of QZLs nanorods, which impedes long-range ordered packing and consequently reduces the overall crystallinity of the material.

XPS analysis

XPS was further employed to elucidate the changes in surface functional groups of the nanocolloids, and the results are presented in Fig. 3A. In the XPS survey spectra, lysozyme exhibited distinct characteristic peaks of C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s, whereas quercetin and QZ showed only the C 1s and O 1s signals. For the QZLs, the N 1s signal progressively increased with increasing Lys concentration, and a pronounced C–N-related feature was observed for QZL-100. High-resolution deconvolution of the N 1s region yielded two components at 399.81 and 401.57 eV, assigned to C—N and –NH/–NH₂ species, respectively. The O 1s spectra could be resolved into peaks at 531.53 and 532.71 eV, corresponding to peptide-related oxygen functionalities in proteins (e.g., O–C=O, O=C–N, and C–OH). Collectively, these results provide further evidence for the successful incorporation of lysozyme into the 3D nanoarchitectured colloids (Lu et al., 2018). Notably, the characteristic peaks in the C 1s spectrum of quercetin could be deconvoluted into three components at 284.8, 286.0, and 288.5 eV, corresponding to C—C, C—O, and CO species, respectively. Likewise, the O 1s spectrum could be resolved into two contributions at 533.0 and 531.5 eV, assigned to C—O and CO functionalities, respectively (Kim et al., 2022). In QZ, a pronounced increase in the C—O component was observed, which may be attributed to changes in C—O bond polarity upon Zn2+ coordination and the formation of C–O–Zn linkages. Upon incorporation of lysozyme, the relative contribution of the CO component increased markedly in both the C 1s and O 1s spectra for all QZLs (Fig. 3B and Fig. S1). This increase is mainly ascribed to the enrichment of carbonyl-containing groups at the particle surface after co-assembly of quercetin–Zn2+ nanorods with lysozyme, including abundant amide carbonyls and side-chain carboxyl groups from the adsorbed protein layer. Collectively, these results confirm that lysozyme interacts with quercetin–Zn2+ nanorods to form new aggregates and mediates the transformation of the nanocolloids into a 3D nanoarchitectured morphology.Fig. 3X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization of quercetin, QZ, and different QZLs nanocolloids. Antioxidant activity

As a food-derived bioactive compound, quercetin typically exerts its functions in aqueous environments during dietary intake; therefore, it is critical to evaluate whether the lysozyme-mediated 3D self-assembly strategy can improve its long-term stability. In this study, the ABTS+• and DPPH• radical-scavenging activities of free quercetin, QZ nanorods, and QZLs were determined after storage in water for different durations, and the results are shown in Fig. 4. For the ABTS+• assay, all samples exhibited scavenging efficiencies above 90% on day 1, with free quercetin displaying the highest antioxidant activity (Fig. 4A). This may be attributed to the multilevel assembly and surface group coverage in QZ and QZLs, which partially shields the reactive phenolic groups and reduces their apparent activity relative to free quercetin. However, after 10 and 20 days of storage, the ABTS+• scavenging activities of free quercetin and QZ both declined compared with day 1, and the decrease was more pronounced for QZ than for free quercetin. Quercetin in aqueous media is susceptible to oxidative attack by dissolved oxygen, leading to oxidation of phenolic hydroxyl groups and a concomitant loss of activity (Holzhausen et al., 2025). Notably, all QZLs exhibited an increased ABTS+• scavenging capacity on day 10. This enhancement may be attributed to hydrophobic interactions provided by lysozyme, which create a relatively stable interfacial microenvironment for quercetin over short-term storage and temporarily improve the overall electron-donating capability. Thereafter, the scavenging activity of all QZLs decreased by day 20; however, the activity retention remained above 90% for all formulations. These results indicated that QZLs altered the community structure, reducing microbial richness and diversity, suggesting that QZLs supplementation favored the emergence of dominant taxa, thereby reshaping the original microbiota. The PCA and NMDS analyses (β-diversity) showed the distinctly different clustering patterns between the CK and QZLs-treated groups, suggesting a divergent gut microbiota composition after QZLs supplementation (Fig. 5C, D). Microbial composition, which concretely influenced the results of the α-diversity and β-diversity, was further analyzed at the levels of both phylum and genus to provide support for the divergence among QZL-treated samples. At the phylum level (Fig. 5E), Bacillota was the most abundant across all samples, followed by Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota and Bacteroidota—core phyla commonly found in the healthy adult gut (Tian et al., 2023). QZLs supplementation increased the relative abundances of Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota while decreasing that of Bacteroidota. The change in Pseudomonadota might be related to the high peptide/amino acid environment created by lysozyme in QZLs, which could promote the growth of certain metabolically flexible Pseudomonadota bacteria capable of efficiently utilizing simple nitrogen sources (such as amino acids and ammonia). The Actinomycetota contained important probiotics, such as Bifidobacterium, and the increased abundance was typically associated with prebiotic effects and the anti-inflammatory environment. The lytic action of lysozyme promoted the release of large amounts of cell wall fragments (peptidoglycan), nucleotides, and short peptides. These substances were high-quality nitrogen sources and growth factors that could be effectively utilized by Actinomycetota bacteria like Bifidobacterium. In addition, quercetin in QZLs might also support the proliferation of these beneficial bacteria by modulating the redox state or providing additional carbon skeletons. The Bacteroidota phylum primarily degraded complex polysaccharides (such as dietary fiber and host mucin). The decreased relative abundance might be related to the supplementation of nitrogen sources by lysozyme in QZLs, shifting the focus of microbial competition from scavenging carbohydrates to utilizing nitrogen sources.

Genus-level alterations were illustrated in Fig. 5F. Escherichia-Shigella dominated both the CK and QZLs-treated groups. Moreover, QZLs-treated microbiota exhibited elevated Escherichia-Shigella abundance. This might be related to two factors. On the one hand, although the genus of Escherichia-Shigella included the opportunistic pathogen (Shigella), most species were harmless commensal Escherichia coli that contributed to the microbial community stability. Specific strains could utilize quercetin in QZLs as a carbon source, cleaving the C-ring to yield 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid and other phenolic acids that promoted their own growth (Yang et al., 2024). On the other hand, lysozyme in QZLs preferentially lysed Gram-positive bacteria. The released DNA, peptidoglycan fragments and amino acids from Gram-positive bacteria could serve as nutrients for Gram-negative bacteria. As a typical Gram-negative bacterium, the growth of Escherichia-Shigella could be facilitated by the abundant nutrients. Moreover, the growth of competing Gram-positive bacteria was suppressed by lysozyme. Then, Blautia, Dorea, Collinsella and Phascolarctobacterium were the next most abundant genera. All participated in fermentative metabolism and SCFAs production, these genera would be crucial in modulating the balance of gut homeostasis. As the main acetate-producing bacteria, Blautia and Dorea contributed to the carbohydrate metabolism by producing acetic acid, which was the most abundant SCFAs in the gut (Fusco et al., 2023). Although the abundances of Blautia and Dorea did not differ significantly after QZL supplementation, suggesting that the acid–base physicochemical environment of feces (mainly shaped by SCFAs) was not altered by QZLs supplementation. It demonstrated the harmlessness of QZLs to the intestinal physicochemical environment. In the CK group, the relative abundance of Collinsella was 2.71%. In contrast, Collinsella was significantly (p < 0.05) enriched by QZLs, which was increased to 5.27% (QZ), 5.79% (QZL-2), 3.24% (QZL-10), 5.87% (QZL-20) and 9.69% (QZL-100), respectively. Collinsella could metabolize ingested carbohydrates into beneficial propionate and butyrate, meaning that QZLs might exert the beneficial effects in the gut by enriching propionic and butyric acids (Ali et al., 2025). Different from Blautia and Dorea which degrade complex carbohydrates (cellulose, xylan) into simple sugars, Collinsella preferentially utilized low-molecular-weight carbohydrates (Qin et al., 2019). The QZLs-driven increase in Collinsella might therefore reflect lysozyme- and quercetin-mediated modulation of complex-carbohydrate degraders. In addition, Bifidobacterium, a well-established beneficial genus that promotes SCFAs production as well as enhanced microbial community stability (Arboleya et al., 2016), was also enriched by supplying QZLs. The QZL-20 group reached 1.37-fold the level of CK group in the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium. Holdemanella, an important butyrate producer that nourished colonocytes and exerted anti-inflammatory and barrier-strengthening effects (Gasaly et al., 2021), was likewise increased by QZLs as 2.17% in CK group, 2.94% in QZ group, 2.81% in QZL-2 group, 3.19% in QZL-10 group, 3.47% in QZL-20 group and 3.35% in QZL-100 group. In particular, the QZL-20 group exhibited the highest Holdemanella abundance (1.60-fold vs CK), indicating the optimal microbiota-modulating efficacy at this dose. As shown in the community heatmap (Fig. 5G), the QZLs treatment elevated the relative abundance of Streptococcus and Agathobacter, which participated in the metabolism of lactate-to-butyrate (Louis et al., 2022). It signified a transition toward a healthier, more stable and strongly anti-inflammatory intestinal microenvironment. Moreover, the Parabacteroides, Segatella, Morganella, Enterocloster and Klebsiella were obviously inhibited by QZLs treatment. Regarded as the opportunistic pro-inflammatory bacterium (Federici et al., 2022), the simultaneous decline of these bacteria indicated that QZLs fostered the cooperative anti-inflammatory effect in gut.

Beyond taxonomy, the fecal metabolite environment influenced the stability of microbiota. Detected taxa were classified into transient, intermediate and persistent types (Fig. 5H). A higher proportion of persistent and lower proportion of transient taxa signify reduced environmental sensitivity and greater community stability. In the CK group, transient and persistent taxa accounted for 3.96% and 71.95%, respectively. All QZL treatments decreased the transient fraction and increased the persistent fraction, indicating enhanced resilience. QZL-2 and QZL-20 performed best, reducing transient taxa to 1.45% and 1.40%, respectively. This indicated that after the QZLs treatment, the gut microbiota was reshaped and shifted toward a more stable state by altering the physicochemical environment of the feces.

Holdemanella was identified as the primary taxon driving these differences through Kruskal-Wallis H tests, significantly enhanced by QZLs treatment (Fig. 6A, p < 0.05). Similar to Holdemanella, Fusicatenibacter and Roseburia were also important butyrate-producing bacteria (Li et al., 2021), which were decreased by QZLs. This may be because both Fusicatenibacter and Roseburia were Gram-positive bacteria, which were highly sensitive to lysozyme lysis, leading to a decrease in abundance. In addition, the aggressive rise of Holdemanella might partly encroach upon the ecological niche of Fusicatenibacter and Roseburia. The decrease in Roseburia alongside the increase in Bifidobacterium and Streptococcus may be explained by the prebiotic-like effect of lysozyme-mediated lysis. After lysozyme lyses sensitive bacteria, the released peptidoglycan, DNA, and simple nutrient substrates may provide substrate support for surviving beneficial Gram-positive bacteria and the lactate–butyrate metabolic axis, including the growth or metabolic activity of Bifidobacterium and Streptococcus, thereby promoting microbial cross-feeding and SCFAs production. Therefore, lysozyme in QZLs may not only exert antibacterial pressure on sensitive bacteria, but also reshape the nutrient substrate environment of the fermentation system and indirectly promote functional taxa involved in lactate and butyrate metabolism. Moreover, Morganella, an opportunistic pathogen belonging to the Gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae family, served as a marker of dysbiosis and inflammation. It indicated that QZLs effectively suppressed this potential pathogen (Fig. 6). The decline was likely the combined result of the antibacterial activity from quercetin and the action from lysozyme. In accordance with Morganella, Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 was often positively correlated with high-fat diets, obesity, and inflammation, significantly declining in QZL-treated samples. In particular, the reduction of Erysipelotrichaceae_UCG-003 was generally regarded as a hallmark of a healthier gut microbiota shift, indicating the potential of QZLs to maintain intestinal homeostasis (Kaakoush, 2015). Among the QZLs treatments, QZL-20 showed the strongest effect, which might be attributed to the structure of the QZL-20 nanoparticles. By comparing CK and QZL-20 microbiota, the QZL-20-induced biomarkers were calculated. It suggested the fragility of the gut microbial structure in the CK group, in accordance with the high proportion of transient taxa in Fig. 5F, G. Similarly, Fig. 6C revealed the significant (p < 0.05) positive correlations among Klebsiella, Segatella and Enterocloster. It indicated that the high relative abundance of Segatella in the CK group (Fig. 6C) might induce the increase of Enterocloster and Klebsiella. In contrast, QZL-20-enriched biomarkers included Escherichia-Shigella, Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Agathobacter, Holdemanella, Erysipelotrichales and Mediterraneibacter, which may be mainly associated with the metabolism of butyric acid. The biomarker of Escherichia-Shigella in the QZL-20 group might be due to the lysozyme in QZL-20. By inhibiting Gram-positive bacteria, it provided abundant nutrients and weakened competitive pressure for the Escherichia-Shigella (the typical Gram-negative bacterium). In Fig. 6C, Escherichia-Shigella showed a significant positive relationship with Streptococcus, Mediterraneibacter, and Holdemanella. This may be because Escherichia-Shigella rapidly consumed the simple nitrogen sources released by lysozyme and produced metabolic byproducts that could be utilized by Streptococcus, Mediterraneibacter, and Holdemanella, contributing to the stability of the microbial community. In addition, as a facultative anaerobe, Escherichia-Shigella maintained a low-oxygen environment, which was conducive to the growth of anaerobic bacteria such as Holdemanella and Mediterraneibacter. Importantly, the lysozyme released from QZL-20 could promote the release of simple carbohydrates, providing ideal fermentation substrates for Streptococcus and Lactobacillales to efficiently produce lactate (Kadowaki et al., 2023). Then, the elevated lactate could effectively feed the Agathobacter and Holdemanella, which converted lactate to butyrate via the acrylate pathway. Triggered by Holdemanella, Erysipelotrichales was regarded as a positive signal of a healthier gut microbiota shift, modulating systemic metabolic activity. Mediterraneibacter was a highly efficient mucus-degrading bacterium. During the breakdown process of mucin, intermediates, such as succinate and acetate were released, which could be harnessed by other microbial taxa. The succinate could be converted into propionate and acetate may also participate in butyrate synthesis (Belzer et al., 2017). This study found that QZL treatment specifically and synergistically enriched the beneficial genera Mediterraneibacter and Holdemanella within the family Lachnospiraceae, and their abundances showed a significant positive correlation (Fig. 6C). The enrichment of Mediterraneibacter may indicate that this genus is more likely to thrive in a healthy and renewable mucus niche. Therefore, the moderate enrichment of Mediterraneibacter after QZL treatment may reflect a more stable mucus-associated microbial microenvironment. Together with the increased production of propionate and butyrate, these results suggest that QZLs may indirectly support mucosal barrier homeostasis by improving microbiota-derived SCFAs production and stabilizing mucus-associated microbial interactions. In addition, QZLs treatment promoted the synergistic growth of the butyrate-producing Mediterraneibacter and the probiotic Bifidobacterium, while simultaneously inhibiting Segatella (Fig. 6C), which was potentially associated with inflammation. A significant negative correlation was calculated, constituting a classic ecological model of competitive exclusion and functional replacement. It suggested that QZLs treatment enriched the butyrate-producer Mediterraneibacter by initially promoting the proliferation of Bifidobacterium and acidifying the environment. Then, the microenvironment (low pH, high butyrate) created by Mediterraneibacter and Bifidobacterium, not only directly delivered health benefits but also inhibited the colonization of Segatella. Thus, QZL-20 reshaped the community by (i) bolstering beneficial commensals and (ii) reinforcing the lactate-butyrate axis, thereby reducing environmental sensitivity and enhancing stability.

Furthermore, PICRUSt2-based functional prediction (KEGG level-2, Enzyme and MetaCyc pathways) was performed to assess the metabolic consequences of these compositional changes (Fig. 6). In addition, Phascolarctobacterium, Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Agathobacter and Holdemanella also contributed to the metabolism of carbohydrates, transferring into bioactive metabolism such as propionic and butyric acids. This contraction freed up space for beneficial microbes and aligned with an overall attenuation of intestinal inflammation. Moreover, the enriched Agathobacter and Holdemanella by QZLs contributed to the down-regulation of PWY-7663 pathway. PWY-7222 and PWY-7220 (hyaluronic acid synthesis) were enriched, paralleling the increased Escherichia-Shigella abundance and supporting community structural integrity. Pathways for carbohydrate, secondary bile acid and bile acid metabolism (PWY-5667, PWY0–1319 and PWY-7111, respectively) were all elevated in QZLs-treated groups, corroborating the observed enrichment of corresponding taxa and underscoring the metabolic benefits of QZLs, particularly at the QZL-20 dose. By lysing vast numbers of bacteria, lysozyme released intracellular contents into the lumen, flooding the ecosystem with readily utilizable feedstocks. PWY-5667 pathway converted glucose into pyruvate, which acted as a key junction for numerous downstream fermentation routes. As a primary fermenter, Agathobacter could directly utilize pyruvate generated by PWY-5667 and efficiently channel it via the acetyl-CoA pathway into butyrate. The up-regulation of PWY-5667 furnished it with a richer substrate supply, supporting both expansion and elevated butyrate output. Additionally, the proliferation of Holdemanella prevented lactate accumulation, avoided excessive drops in intestinal pH, and transformed a potential waste product into a high-value metabolite, forming a complementary metabolic division of labor with Agathobacter.

The impact of QZLs on the content of SCFAs

SCFAs are key functional metabolites of the gut microbiota. Changes in their concentrations not only modulated microbial composition but also fed back on the physicochemical environment of feces, thereby steering further structural rearrangements. This was consistent with the stable abundances of Blautia and Dorea in all samples (Fig. 7F, G), which were two genera strongly linked to acetate production. In comparison with the CK group, propionic acid levels were significantly (P < 0.05) elevated to 1.27-, 1.17-, 1.21-, 1.15- and 1.21-fold respective in the QZ, QZL-2, QZL-10, QZL-20 and QZL-100 groups. Besides, butyric acid levels were significantly (p < 0.05) increased to 1.20-, 1.09-, 1.23-, 1.16- and 1.05-fold respective in the QZ, QZL-2, QZL-10, QZL-20 and QZL-100 groups. This correlation was aligned with the QZLs-induced enriched abundance of Collinsella, Agathobacter and Holdemanella (Fig. 7F, G), which participated in the metabolic cascades leading to these SCFAs. Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 7H) further identified a significant positive relationship between Mediterraneibacter and both propionate and butyrate (p < 0.05), even though Mediterraneibacter does not directly synthesize these acids. By adeptly degrading complex glycans, especially mucin, it released lactate and succinate, intermediates that could be channeled into propionate and butyrate production by other taxa. Thus, Mediterraneibacter appeared to foster SCFAs formation indirectly, promoting gut health. Although the association did not reach statistical significance, Bifidobacterium also trended positively with propionate and butyrate, mirroring the modest enrichment depicted in Fig. 7H. By boosting propionate and butyrate, particularly in the QZL-20 group, QZLs not only restructured the microbial community but also enhanced the tolerance to the fecal physicochemical milieu. Collectively, it indicated that QZLs, and QZL-20 in particular, possessed a robust capacity to reshape the host gut microbiota, primarily by increasing the abundance of Collinsella, Streptococcus, Bifidobacterium, Agathobacter and Holdemanella, all of which were instrumental in propionate and butyrate generation. The increased concentrations of propionic and butyric acids, together with microbe–microbe interactions, stabilized the intestinal environment, with QZL-20 conferring the most pronounced benefit. RDA analysis at the genus level for the antioxidant capacity of QZLs revealed that the CK group exhibited low antioxidant capacity, whereas QZLs demonstrated high antioxidant capacity (Fig. 7I). This was associated with the enrichment of beneficial bacterial groups by QZLs, such as Streptococcus, Lactobacillales, Agathobacter, Holdemanella, Mediterraneibacter and Bifidobacterium. Pearson correlation analysis indicated that Bifidobacterium, Holdemanella, Mediterraneibacter, and Anaerostipes were positively correlated with the antioxidant capacity manifested by QZLs (Fig. 7J). On the one hand, the increased abundance of these beneficial bacteria occupied the ecological niches of genera negatively correlated with antioxidant capacity, such as Klebsiella, Enterocloster, and Segatella, forming an overall beneficial gut microecology. On the other hand, the beneficial bacteria associated with SCFAs formation, particularly butyrate producers (Agathobacter, Holdemanella and Mediterraneibacter), enhanced the anti-inflammatory effect within the intestinal system through the accumulation of SCFAs (in particular butyrate). As a result, it reflected the suppressed level of oxidative stress, indicating the positive function of QZLs in gut.

Establishment of a cellular steatosis model and screening of lipid-lowering compounds

After co-incubation with oleic acid/palmitic acid (OA/PA) for 24 h, both BODIPY and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 8A-E) revealed extensive intracellular lipid droplet accumulation in the model group compared with the control, indicating a markedly increased lipid burden. Based on this steatosis model, a preliminary screening was conducted for different QZLs nanocolloids. The results showed that QZL-20 markedly reduced intracellular lipid droplet accumulation in both HepG2 and Hepa 1–6 cells, as evidenced by a pronounced decrease in lipid droplet fluorescence/staining intensity in BODIPY and Oil Red O assays (Fig. 8A-E). This lipid-lowering phenotype was further confirmed under low-serum conditions (2% FBS), where QZL-20 maintained an effect comparable to that observed under conventional 10% FBS culture (Fig. S2), indicating good robustness of both the model and the QZL-20 response. Quantitative analyses further demonstrated that QZL-20 exhibited the strongest lipid-lowering efficacy among all candidates (Fig. 8B and D), and it was therefore selected for subsequent investigations.

Notably, the superior performance of QZL-20 may arise from a more appropriate balance between structural stabilization/protection and bioaccessibility. Formulations with low lysozyme levels (QZL-2 and QZL-10) may provide insufficient protection, thereby failing to suppress oxidative deactivation of quercetin during incubation or to withstand competitive interactions with medium components, ultimately resulting in reduced effective activity. In contrast, excessive lysozyme coating in QZL-100 may generate steric hindrance and an over-shielding effect. This dense protein layer may impede the access of digestive enzymes, fermentation components, or cellular interfaces to quercetin-containing domains, thereby limiting quercetin release and reducing its bioavailability. Therefore, QZL-20 may achieve a more favorable balance between structural protection and functional release, resulting in superior microbiota-modulating and lipid-lowering effects.

QZL-20 attenuates lipid accumulation in a dose-dependent manner

To determine the optimal working concentration of QZL-20 in the cellular steatosis model, OA/PA-induced steatotic HepG2 cells were co-treated with QZL-20 at different doses (40, 80, and 120 μM). BODIPY staining showed a progressive attenuation of lipid droplet fluorescence with increasing QZL-20 concentration, indicating a clear dose-dependent lipid-lowering effect (Fig. 8F-G). Biochemically, the changes in TG levels were highly consistent with the fluorescence phenotype (Fig. 8H). OA/PA induction markedly elevated intracellular TG; 40 μM QZL-20 only showed a decreasing trend without statistical significance, whereas 80 μM QZL-20 partially reduced TG. In contrast, 120 μM QZL-20 significantly lowered TG to a level close to that of the control group. Together with the concordant fluorescence intensity and TG/TC quantification results, these findings indicate that 120 μM QZL-20 achieves near-maximal lipid-lowering efficacy without overt cytotoxicity and was therefore selected for subsequent mechanistic investigations.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the lipid-lowering effect of QZL-20, Western blotting was performed to assess changes in the expression of key proteins involved in lipid metabolism. As shown in Fig. 8I, QZL-20 downregulated PLIN2 expression in steatotic hepatocytes. As a lipid droplet-associated structural protein, PLIN2 coats the lipid droplet surface, promotes lipid droplet stability and neutral lipid storage, and restricts the access of lipases such as ATGL to the lipid core. Therefore, QZL-20-induced PLIN2 downregulation may weaken the protective coating of lipid droplets, enhance lipase accessibility to stored lipids, and thereby facilitate lipid mobilization or lipolysis.

Conclusion

This study successfully constructed a 3D-like hierarchical nanocolloid via lysozyme-bridged coordination assembly of quercetin–Zn2+ nanorods. This architecture embeds quercetin within a lysozyme-connected nanoarchitectured matrix, which significantly impedes oxidative degradation and preserves antioxidant activity during storage (retaining >95% ABTS·⁺ scavenging capacity after 20 days for QZL-20). Beyond chemical stabilization, QZLs also improved the functional availability of quercetin in the colonic fermentation environment. In vitro colonic fermentation revealed that the QZLs reshaped gut microbiota through the combined effects of lysozyme-mediated bacterial lysis and quercetin-associated microbial metabolism. This process may create a positive feedback loop in which bacterial lysis releases peptidoglycan fragments, DNA, nitrogen sources, and other fermentable components, providing metabolic substrates for beneficial taxa such as Mediterraneibacter and Bifidobacterium. This process, in turn, may promote SCFAs production and reduce the potentially inflammation-associated genus Segatella. The enhanced production of SCFAs, particularly butyrate, further suggests that QZLs may reinforce a beneficial microbiota-derived metabolic environment, thereby contributing to intestinal homeostasis-related functions and downstream lipid metabolism regulation. Furthermore, QZL-20 dose-dependently attenuated lipid accumulation in steatotic hepatocytes, and this effect may be closely associated with the downregulation of the lipid droplet-associated protein PLIN2. PLIN2 downregulation may weaken the capacity of lipid droplets to store neutral lipids and promote lipid mobilization, thereby alleviating hepatocellular lipid deposition. Considering the in vitro colonic fermentation results, the lipid-lowering potential of QZL-20 may not represent an isolated cellular effect, but may instead be jointly associated with improved quercetin delivery stability, enhanced functional accessibility, and increased production of microbiota-derived SCFAs, particularly butyrate. Overall, this ternary self-assembly strategy provides a multifunctional platform that enhances quercetin's stability, bioactivity, and gut-microbiota-modulating potential, offering a novel approach for developing quercetin-based functional interventions.
