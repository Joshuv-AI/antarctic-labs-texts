Development and internal validation of a deep learning algorithm for intraoperative arterial pressure-based stroke volume index estimation in children: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Hyun-Lim Yang, Young-Eun Jang, Chul-Woo Jung, Hee-Soo Kim, Eun-Hee Kim, Sang-Hwan Ji, Min-Soo Kim, Hyung-Chul Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveDespite the need for continuous, accurate, and minimally invasive cardiac output measurement in pediatric patients, no recommended methods currently exist. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning (DL)-based algorithm that utilizes a minimally invasive arterial pressure waveform to measure pediatric stroke volume index (SVI) accurately.MethodsA total of 70 pediatric operations (on 67 patients) were included, with a median age of 6 years. We derived stroke volume (SV) from the Doppler-based stroke distance and aortic cross-sectional area, and then adjusted it to the SVI using body surface area. The arterial pressure waveform and patient demographics were used to predict SVI. The model was validated using error, Bland-Altman, four-quadrant plot, cycle-to-cycle variability, and sensitivity analyses. A saliency map was used to visualize the model’s comprehension of the waveform.ResultsThe DL model demonstrated a mean absolute error of 4.1 ± 2.8 mL/m2. The limit of agreement (LOA) ranged from − 11.23 ± 0.01 mL/m2 to 8.7 ± 0.1 mL/m2, which was ± 28.6% of bias. In the sensitivity and saliency map analyses, the model effectively extracted features from the arterial pressure waveform.ConclusionsThe deep learning model showed promising accuracy (LOA < 30%) in estimating SVI in children. However, its ability to track rapid hemodynamic changes is limited in some cases. Further improvement will be useful for optimizing systemic oxygen delivery and enhancing patient outcomes in pediatric care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03428-x.

Introduction

Cardiac output (CO) is monitored to optimize systemic oxygen delivery in critically ill and high-risk surgical patients. However, no generally accepted method exists for continuous and minimally invasive measurement of pediatric CO [1, 2]. The thermodilution method using a pulmonary artery catheter—considered the gold standard for monitoring CO in adult patients—is not recommended for pediatric patients due to its invasiveness and the risk of complications such as bleeding, cardiac perforation, thromboembolism, and infection [3]. Arterial pressure-based cardiac output (APCO) estimation methods, such as the FloTrac™ (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, US) algorithm, are widely used in adult patients who have arterial catheters without additional invasiveness. The arterial pressure waveform is a composite signal generated as the left ventricle ejects its stroke volume (SV) into the compliant artery, where the rise in pressure is governed by the Windkessel equation (SV ≈ C × pulse pressure), linking ventricular volume ejection, total arterial compliance (C), and pulse pressure [4]. Simultaneous aortic flow-probe studies confirm that beat-to-beat changes in pulse pressure correlate linearly with SV when compliance is stable [5]. Commercial pulse‑contour devices have harnessed these relations by mapping contour features—upstroke slope, dicrotic‑notch timing, and diastolic decay constant—to SV with clinically acceptable accuracy across operating‑room and intensive‑care cohorts [6]. However, the currently available APCO algorithms cannot be used for children because the device parameters are not applicable [7]. In pediatric patients, although the Doppler flowmeter can accurately measure stroke distance (SD) [8], commercial Doppler flowmeter-based CO measurement devices such as the CardioQ™ esophageal Doppler monitor (Deltex, Chichester, Sussex, UK) have large errors (mean percentage error; 54.3%) because of their inaccurate nomogram-based estimation of the aortic cross-sectional area (CSA) [9]. The transthoracic electrical bioimpedance method also has a large error (mean percentage error; 36.4%) [10]. In both studies, clinically useful reliability was defined as a mean percentage error ≤ 30% [11].

A convolutional neural network-based deep learning (DL) APCO algorithm [12, 13] exhibited better performance than the FloTrac™ algorithm for adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery and liver transplantation. The DL-based APCO algorithm could learn the nonlinear relationships between the arterial pressure waveform and stroke volume (SV) using the data without any manual intervention or prior knowledge [14]. Moreover, the DL-based model can be improved with more data [15]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have applied DL techniques in pediatric APCO. Furthermore, recent explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods have enabled DL models to visualize the influence of input data on predicted results using saliency maps [16]. This enables medical algorithms to provide accurate predictions and explain how certain predictions are made.

This study aimed to develop and validate a DL-based APCO algorithm that used a minimally invasive arterial pressure waveform to accurately measure pediatric stroke volume index (SVI). We hypothesized that the algorithm can estimate SVI in pediatric patients with a clinically acceptable limit of agreement (< ± 30%) [11] as compared with the SVI calculated from the Doppler-based stroke distance and the aortic CSA measured by 2D-echocardiography.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (Approval No. H-2011-078-1172 and E-2508-127-1668). The need for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective study design and the use of anonymized registry data. According to the IRB requirement, authors did not have access to information that could identify individual participants during or after data collection. This article was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STOROBE) guidelines. The study pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Data were collected using the Vital Recorder Program [17]. Data from pediatric patients who underwent surgery at Seoul National University Children’s Hospital between November 2019 and July 2020 were used. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 19 years, (2) continuous arterial pressure monitoring during anesthesia, (3) use of Doppler flowmeter-based CO monitors (CardioQ™), and (4) intraoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) screening performed using the S8-3 or S12-4 probe as appropriate connected to an iE33 ultrasound machine (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA).

From our study period, 92 operations were eligible. Seventeen operations were excluded from the analysis because they lacked a record of the aortic cross-sectional area. Also, five more operations were excluded because there were no records of their arterial pressure waveform (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2Flow diagram for inclusion and exclusion of the study

Flow diagram for inclusion and exclusion of the study

The database we used to collect data captures and stores all the intraoperative continuous vital signs and bio-signals. The need for CO monitoring was determined by the anesthesiologist. The arterial pressure waveform was recorded at 500 Hz from the analog output port of the TRAM™ module (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) on a Solar 8000™ patient monitor (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The SD was recorded from CardioQ™ at 2-second intervals. For measuring SD, the CardioQ™ Doppler probe was placed in the patient’s esophagus and advanced to the lower esophagus, and blood flow was insonated in the descending thoracic aorta. The aortic root diameter was determined from the anesthesia notes. Intraoperative TTE screening was performed by a single experienced pediatric cardiac anesthesiologist (Y.E.J.) and recorded in the anesthesia records. To ensure consistency, all aortic root diameter measurements throughout the study period were performed by this single operator, thereby eliminating inter-observer variability. The aortic root diameter for cardiac output was measured as the distance between the hinge point of the right coronary cusp and the edge of the sinus at the side of the commissures between the left coronary cusp and the noncoronary cusp at the systolic phase. Additionally, patient demographic information, including age, sex, weight, and height, was collected from the electronic health records.

SV values outside the range of 5 mL to 200 mL were considered as noise and were removed from the dataset. This range was selected conservatively to exclude only noticeable artifacts, such as erroneous Doppler readings or data recording glitches. All SD values obtained from CardioQ™ were converted into SVI values using Eqs. 1–3, and termed SVICardioQ.

The arterial pressure waveforms were resampled from 500 Hz to 100 Hz. Subsequently, we extracted the 20-second segments of arterial pressure waveforms recorded immediately before each SVI value was recorded. This segment length (20 s) was empirically selected by referencing existing studies to ensure each segment contains multiple beats and at least one complete breathing cycle [12, 19], thereby providing the model with appropriate context while filtering out high-frequency noise. The set of SVI values and the corresponding arterial pressure waveform segment, each with a 20-second duration, were referred to as the data samples. To obtain sufficient data for model training, data samples from each operation record were oversampled at the 2-second intervals, which was when the SVI values were recorded. Data samples with arterial blood pressure outside the range of 15 mmHg to 250 mmHg were excluded. To remove artifacts, such as blood sampling or transient arrhythmias, a beat detection algorithm [20] was applied, and arterial blood pressure waveform segments with heart rate (HR) < 30 per min, HR > 180 per min, pulse pressure (PP) < 20 mmHg, PP > 200 mmHg, or frequent (> 50%) ventricular premature beats were removed. To analyze the signals within the given sample more robustly, each sample underwent bandpass filtering with a bandwidth of 5–20 Hz for accurate systolic and diastolic peak detection, and it also passed through bandpass filtering with a bandwidth of 0.25–33 Hz (assuming 30 bpm and 200 bpm, respectively) for accurate heart rate detection.

Because inputs were generated via a sliding 20-second window with a 2-second stride, and SVI measurements are temporally correlated, sample-wise splitting would cause substantial temporal or subject leakage and inflate performance. Therefore, the data were randomly partitioned at the patient level into a training set (80% of patients) and a testing set (20%), with no patient overlap between them, reflecting the intended use case of inference on unseen patients.

Model building

A DL model was developed that takes a pair comprising a 20-second segment of the arterial pressure waveform and the patient’s age, sex, weight, and height as inputs. This approach provides the model with granular information – for example, age is not reflected in BSA but can influence cardiovascular dynamics [21]. The SVI value was the model’s output. The structure of our model was inspired by the encoder part of the Transformer architecture, which consists of convolutional, multi-head attention, fully connected, pooling, and normalization layers [22]. First, the input waveform was abstracted in the convolutional layers (kernel size of nine and 32 filters) and max pooling layers (pooling rate of two). It was then passed to a fully connected layer (32 output units) to reduce the feature dimensions. Subsequently, four self-attention blocks, comprising sequences of multi-head attention layers (with two heads of dimension 32), a normalization layer, two fully connected layers (with output units of 128 and 32, respectively), another normalization layer, and a global average pooling layer, were employed. Finally, the abstracted features were combined with the patients’ demographic data (age, sex, weight, and height) and analyzed using two fully connected layers (output units of 128 and 1) to produce an SVI value. The overall composition and arrangement of the DL model architecture are shown in Fig. 3-A. The gray box with multi-head attention in Fig. 3-A indicates a self-attention block. The detailed components of the multi-head attention layer are illustrated in Fig. 3-B, and the scaled dot-product attention layer within the multi-head attention layer is shown in Fig. 3-C. Layer-specific hyperparameters are marked in Fig. 3 with brackets; a dropout rate of 0.1 was applied to all dropout connections. The details of hyperparameter selection are illustrated in Appendix G.

Model training

We used the Adam optimizer [23] to update the model’s parameters with the root mean squared error between the predicted SVI and measured SVI values as the loss function. The error of the model was calculated using 20% of the training cases at every 20 steps to validate the model’s performance during training. The learning rate started at 0.001 and was reduced by a factor of 0.5 every five epochs if the loss did not decrease. Early stopping was applied to pause the training when the loss did not improve after five epochs, and the learning rate was updated. The details of model training are illustrated in Appendix G. The training was conducted using our own program, written in Python 3.8 and utilizing TensorFlow 2.5.0, on an NVIDIA DGX-station GPU server equipped with a 20-core Intel Xeon CPU and an NVIDIA Tesla V100-32GB GPU.

Statistical analysis and model validation

Patient characteristics were compared between the training and testing datasets using the Kruskal–Wallis test or two-sample t-tests after the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Pearson chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables such as sex. The absolute standardized difference between the training and testing datasets was also calculated [24], except for categorical variables. Model performance was evaluated using the mean error (ME), mean absolute error (MAE), mean percentage error (MPE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) in the testing dataset [25]. ME and MAE were chosen to represent an intuitive error with the same unit (mL/m²). At the same time, MPE and MAPE were selected because they were the ratios of the two aforementioned metrics, depicting errors as percentages (%). These metrics can be used to compare the accuracy of the model with that of other models with different data distributions (e.g., an arterial pressure-based cardiac output model for adults). Bland–Altman analysis [26] was performed to test the agreement between our model and the SVICardioQ. Bias was determined as the mean difference between the SVI value of the DL model (SVIDL) and SVICardioQ, and the upper and lower limits were ± 1.96 standard deviation of bias, which indicated 95% of the limit of agreement. As previously recommended [27–29], a limit of agreement of ± 30% was considered acceptable in this study [11]. Since random measurement error is compounded when two predictions are combined, and assuming two measurements at 20% equals a total error of ± 28.3%, the overall limit of agreement is commonly rounded to ± 30% [11]. It has been widely used as a pragmatic guideline for many previous studies [30, 31]. Sample size calculation for the Bland-Altman analysis [32] was also performed with a type I error (α) of 0.05 and type II error (β) of 0.20 [33]. We assume the mean SVI is 40 mL/m2 and the standard deviation is 5 mL/m2. As we considered 30% of the limit of agreement to be clinically acceptable, 12 mL/m2 was our maximum allowed difference. Therefore, the minimum required number of SVI measurements was 164, calculated using a sample size with α = 0.05 and β = 0.20. Assuming 12 or more independent SVI changes occurred during surgery in a single patient, at least 14 cases were required in the testing data. To ensure the integrity of the validation process, we examined errors and the limits of agreement for the entire test dataset, as well as for each individual in the test dataset.

To assess the trending ability, a four-quadrant plot was created using the differences between randomly sampled 300 pairs of averaged SVI values over 20 s at 10-minute intervals. Subgroup analyses were performed according to age and sex. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis (Appendix A) was conducted to assess the impact of variations in the input variables on model performance. The correlation coefficients were reported using both Spearman’s (ρ) and Pearson’s (r) correlation methods.

To visualize the model’s comprehension of pediatric bio-signals, the Integrated Gradients method [34], was used to explain the effect of the input arterial pressure waveform on the output SVI. Briefly, the method can determine the attributions of the input waveforms and generate saliency maps by highlighting the waveform data points important for prediction. The attributions from the sampled input arterial pressure waveforms were visualized and analyzed qualitatively. Saliency maps from the sensitivity analysis were also visualized to evaluate the model’s comprehension of the bio-signals in the presence of noise.

To evaluate the robustness of the model, cycle-to-cycle variability (CtCV) was evaluated on the test data. Since our model generates output from 20 s of raw arterial blood pressure waveforms, we randomly selected 15 segments from the stable period (a 5-minute interval with an average blood pressure difference of less than 20 mmHg) for each patient and calculated the standard deviation of errors and the square root of the mean of the sum of the squares of the differences (RMSSD) [35]. Detailed calculations are illustrated in Appendix F.

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, median (interquartile range), or absolute number (percent). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Python SciPy 1.5.3, and model validations were conducted using TensorFlow 2.5.0.

Results

The patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. 70 operations from 67 patients were analyzed. There were no significant differences in age, weight, and height between the two datasets (all p > 0.05). Tidal volume per kilogram, ASA physical status, surgical type, intraoperative patient position, or arterial line placement were also not significantly different (all p > 0.05). All patients, except two, were not considered for lung-protective ventilation (< 6 mL/kg) [36]. The two patients had 5.23 cmH2O and 5.65 cmH2O in the training dataset. Training data were extracted from arterial lines located in the radial artery for most patients (approximately 93%), except for four patients: one from the leg, one from the posterior tibial tendon, and two from the dorsalis pedis. Testing data were also extracted from the radial artery for most patients, except for one patient from the posterior tibial tendon. 465,057 samples were extracted from records. 248,362 samples (53.40%) were removed by the SV range filter, and the ABP range filter removed 53,596 samples (11.52%). Note that the high rate of samples removed by the SV range filter is due to the inclusion of cases in which the cardiac output monitor remained connected but monitoring was not performed during the surgical procedure. 13,338 samples (2.87%) were removed by other filters, such as HR, PP, and frequent ventricular premature beats. Following pre-processing, the total number of data samples was 110,372 for the training dataset (from 56 operations on 54 patients) and 39,389 for the testing dataset (from 14 operations on 13 patients).

Table 2 lists the mean SV and SVI, along with the errors and biases of the DL model for the test data. The MAE was 4.1 ± 2.8 mL/m2, and the MAPE was 12.9 ± 10.4% in the overall group (Table 2). The Spearman ρ value was 0.77 (p < 0.001), and Pearson’s r value was 0.77 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4-A). The median Spearman correlation across individual cases was 0.48 (IQR: 0.31, Q1: 0.39, Q3: 0.70) and Pearson’s correlation was 0.38 (IQR: 0.32, Q1: 0.31, Q3: 0.63), illustrating variability in performance (see Appendix C). Individual plots of the time courses of the measured and predicted SVIs and the Bland-Altman plot in all 14 pediatric operations are available in Appendix C. The mean of the per-patient MAE of SVI was 4.3 ± 1.7 mL/m², and three (anonymized CaseID 5, 11, and 13) among 14 testing patients demonstrated non-satisfactory performance, which yielded errors corresponding to approximately one-quarter of the testing data. Discussion

This study demonstrates that the DL-based APCO algorithm can estimate the SVI with a clinically acceptable limit of agreement ( < ± 30%) as compared with the SVI calculated from the Doppler-based SD (measured by CardioQ™) and the aortic CSA measured by 2D-echocardiography in pediatric patients. However, it has not yet been proven reliable for clinical trend monitoring without further validation and possibly enhancements. Our DL model achieved a MAE of 4.1 ± 2.8 mL/m2 and a MAPE of 12.9 ± 10.4%, a Spearman rho value of 0.77, and ± 1.96 standard deviation of -11.23 mL/m2 (=-32.2%) and 8.7 mL/m2 (= 24.9%), which indicated a 95% limit of agreement of ± 28.6%.

Current APCO algorithms, such as FloTrac™, have poor performance for pediatric patients, with up to an 84% error [7]. However, this is unsurprising because the features and parameters used in existing APCO algorithms are derived from animal or adult patient data, which are unsuitable for children. Furthermore, the pediatric minimally invasive CO monitoring method (e.g., conventional esophageal Doppler flowmeter) has an error of up to 54.3%, and the noninvasive method (e.g., bioimpedance) has an error of up to 45–55%. Of note, only a few adult studies of arterial pressure-based CO monitors often report ~ 20–30% errors [38, 39]. In contrast, those in our DL model were trained to be optimal for children. Our model showed a 13% MAPE error, which is encouragingly low, albeit achieved on an internal dataset. The convolutional layers in the first part of our model extract optimal features from the arterial waveform, and the self-attention blocks in the following layer abstract the positional relationships among the extracted features. The final fully connected layers regress these relationships to produce SVI values, which play a role similar to that of the nomogram-based parameters in existing APCO algorithms. These characteristics of the DL techniques, along with clinical operation records obtained from a children’s hospital, enabled our model to estimate the pediatric SVI at a clinically acceptable level.

The gold standard cardiac output measurement method is the thermodilution-based method; however, this is rarely used due to its invasiveness. Other alternative methods for measuring cardiac output in pediatric patients include TTE and TEE (transesophageal echocardiography). However, TTE and TEE require manual measurement by experienced physicians and provide data only at specific time points, making it difficult to obtain continuous data during the dynamic phases of surgery. Commercial Doppler flowmeter-based CO measurement devices such as the CardioQ™ esophageal Doppler monitor (Deltex, Chichester, Sussex, UK) are used to continuously monitor the cardiac output in pediatric patients [8, 40, 41]. However, inaccuracies of aortic CSA estimation cause large errors in children [9]. Inaccurate probe positioning may also increase measurement error [42]. To mitigate those instabilities, we used the Doppler flowmeter-based SD and independently measured 2D-echocardiography-based aortic CSA [9] by an experienced pediatric cardiac anesthesiologist to correct the SV using Eq. 2, SV (mL) = SD (cm) × Aortic CSA (cm2). Note that for our testing datasets, the discrepancy between the SV value directly measured by the CardioQ™ monitor and our corrected SV value was 9.282 mL/m² of MAE and 42.60% of MAPE, due to an inaccurate estimation of aortic CSA. The thermodilution-based gold standard CO measurement method makes it challenging to obtain a large amount of SV data in children due to its invasiveness and high risk of serious complications. Doppler-based CO estimation methods have been demonstrated to correlate highly with invasive Fick-based [8], thermodilution [43], and cardiac MRI [44] methods, provided that the measurements are accurate. Additionally, numerous studies have concluded that Doppler-based methods are an acceptable approach for CO monitoring in children [8, 45, 46]. Accordingly, we devised a model based on Doppler-based measurements that can obtain relatively accurate CO while providing a substantial amount of data.

Although the Doppler flowmeter-based CO measurement method can continuously measure SV in children, it is challenging to apply in routine clinical practice because it requires an echocardiography and a Doppler flowmeter device from a specialist. In contrast, the trained DL-based APCO model requires no specialists or devices beyond the arterial line and transducer, which are typically already in place for high-risk patients. Therefore, the proposed DL-based APCO model is readily applicable to the care of high-risk pediatric patients. In summary, our deep learning model showed encouraging performance in estimating pediatric SVI from arterial pressure, but it has limitations that must be addressed before it can be considered for clinical use.

Model performance was validated through a sensitivity analysis, wherein patients’ characteristics or input waveforms were replaced with averaged values or white noise. With the average general characteristics, the MAE of the model increased by 59.45%, whereas the noisy waveform increased by 105.41%. This result can be interpreted as indicating that the raw arterial pressure waveform is more important than the accuracy of the patients’ general characteristics in predicting SVI using the DL model in pediatric patients.

We also visualized the explanations of how our model analyzed the waveform using the Integrated Gradients method [34]. The saliency maps from sample segments in the testing data (Fig. 5A and B) indicate that the model focuses on the systolic upstroke of the arterial waveforms and considers the vast area of fluctuations in the entire segment to analyze the input waveform. Interestingly, the values around the dicrotic notch (Fig. 5A-C) were negatively correlated with the predictive values. A dicrotic notch is known as strongly associated with vascular resistance; if the resistance increases, the SVI decreases. Although the effect cannot be directly quantified in our saliency map, it seems that the model learned the appropriate features from the arterial blood pressure waveforms to make accurate predictions.

These results also indicate that our model primarily analyzes the beat intervals of the arterial waveform to extract the relevant features from the arterial pressure waveform, and therefore functions correctly. The model successfully detected the systolic upstroke of the arterial waveforms, regardless of the patient’s characteristics being inaccurate (Fig. 5-C). Furthermore, for pediatric arterial waves with high noise (Fig. 5-D), the model accurately analyzed the arterial waves, even when severe noise was introduced into the waveform. Note that the beat at the end of the segment had a greater influence on the prediction because most beats had small variations in a single segment (Fig. 5A to B). Therefore, we expect that shorter segments (e.g., 10 s) can be used for future model improvements.

Additionally, we observed that our model predicted fairly constant values and failed to secure a trending ability for some data (e.g., the orange or light green dots in Fig. 4 and Cases 3, 7, and 11 in Appendix C). The detailed procedures and results of the additional analysis of our model’s behaviors are presented in Appendix D. In summary, the two mean beats derived from the two samples with fairly constant predictions were highly similar. However, there was an apparent difference in the mean beat between the two samples, with varying predictions. This indicated that there was no significant alteration in the waveform of the patient’s arterial blood pressure during the surgery.

However, this study was conducted retrospectively; therefore, the quality control for the arterial blood pressure waveform may not have been sufficient. For instance, we found that some arterial blood pressure waveforms exhibited cases of arterial line damping, characterized by the absence of the dicrotic notch and a blunted systolic upstroke. However, because these cases occur in the real world, retaining them from the training and testing datasets will increase the credibility of this study. Furthermore, although the aggregate error metrics were within clinically acceptable ranges, the model, in its current form, may smooth out or delay the recognition of rapid hemodynamic changes in some patients. We therefore stress the need for further improvements and evaluations focused on trend detection.

Our study had a few limitations. This was a single-center retrospective study with no external validation, which may have introduced biases and the potential risk of overfitting. The retrospective study design inherently limits the ability to distinguish the effects of pharmacological interventions on stroke volume during surgery. However, these effects might already be modelled indirectly from the raw arterial blood pressure waveform. A prospective study is required to evaluate the model’s performance during the pharmacological intervention. All the included patients were mechanically ventilated. The first and third quartiles of patient age used for model training were 3.5 years and 10 years, respectively, which did not sufficiently cover the age range of pediatric patients in the remaining intervals; therefore, the model’s reliability may be limited in this age range. The measured SVI values were mostly within the normal range and had a small range of changes. Therefore, most of the points were concentrated in the 10% exclusion zone, and the trending ability results might not be reliable. Given the retrospective nature of the study, it was not possible to fully control for all variables. As a result, the findings may not reflect the impact of all possible interventions. Also, there may be sampling bias due to convenience sampling. However, due to the extremely limited data available on cardiac output in pediatric patients, we used a retrospective study and tried to capture as many cases as possible between the time the device was introduced and the time the IRB research protocol was written. Further research with additional data is required to validate the model’s performance using prospective data collection. Given that there were no data from other cardiac monitoring devices in pediatric patients, it was not possible to make additional performance comparisons with those devices. Additionally, due to the limited number of patient data, a 5-fold cross-validation to ensure demographic homogeneity between training and test data was not feasible. Future work requires obtaining additional data to perform cross-validation and validate model performance more rigorously. Another limitation is that the SD values measured by CardioQ™ may have some errors, primarily due to the difficulty in maintaining the Doppler probe in position towards the descending aorta. Furthermore, the majority of patients included in the study were limited to those with orthopedic or neurosurgical conditions. This patient group met the conditions of requiring arterial catheterization during surgery and allowing for the stable measurement of cardiac output by positioning the CardioQ™ in the esophagus. Finally, there may be room for further improvement in the model’s structural design or data preprocessing method. For example, incorporating a recurrent or sequence-to-sequence model architecture could allow the network to learn from longer temporal patterns and might better capture sudden changes in SVI. Also, a robust outlier detection, like IQR filtering per patient or media absolute deviation criterion, could be an advantageous refinement in model performance if more training data can be obtained. This introduces additional complexity and falls outside the scope of our initial study, but it is a promising direction for future work.

Additionally, the aortic diameter measured by 2D-echocardiography may also be subject to errors, especially in small children. We acknowledge that combining the Doppler-based stroke distance from CardioQ™ with a 2D-echocardiographic aortic CSA measurement created a composite (hybrid) reference standard rather than a single-device ground truth, and that label noise in this reference standard may propagate into the model’s predictions. However, our design feature mitigates these concerns. All aortic root diameter measurements in this study were performed by a single experienced pediatric cardiac anesthesiologist using a standardized protocol, thereby eliminating inter-observer variability. Published data indicate that the remaining intra-observer variability for 2D-ecocardiographic aortic root measurement is very low, with a coefficient of variation of approximately 0.5% in a large multinational adult study [47] and consistently below 5% even in preterm infants [48], the most technically challenging population for echocardiographic imaging. These values are an order of magnitude smaller than 54.3% mean percentage error attributable to the CardioQ™ nomogram-based CSA estimation [9]. Since the use of pulmonary artery catheters in children is decreasing, we believe that this composite Doppler-echocardiographic approach remains one of the most validated and practical methods for obtaining large volumes of CO measurement data in pediatric patients. Additionally, all subgroup analyses in this study—including stratification by age group and sex—are exploratory and underpowered due to the limited sample size of the test set (n = 14 cases total). Any observed differences in performance between subgroups should be considered hypothesis-generating and will require confirmation in a larger, multi-center study. Furthermore, our model may predict nearly constant values in a very few patients due to the inherent limitations of the APCO methods. If the arterial blood pressure waveform remains the same with each heartbeat, regardless of changes in cardiac output, an accurate prediction is impossible. For instance, a change in position or the use of medication during surgery may alter cardiac output, yet the waveform of arterial blood pressure may remain unchanged. Thus, although many factors may affect intraoperative cardiac output, there is a limit to the information that may not be adequately reflected in the arterial blood pressure waveform. Finally, there are limitations in modeling patient-specific information due to a lack of data and regression to the mean, where the model predicts nearly constant values per operation. A prospective validation is warranted to confirm the accuracy of the DL-based prediction APCO model and to determine its clinical utility in children.

In conclusion, the DL-based APCO algorithm exhibits clinically acceptable performance in children. However, its ability to track rapid hemodynamic changes is limited in some cases. This model represents a promising new approach to monitoring cardiac output in pediatric care. Additionally, we expect that further improvement can provide hemodynamic status information to the anesthesiologists with reduced invasiveness, which may lead to the optimization of systemic oxygen delivery and improve patient outcomes.
