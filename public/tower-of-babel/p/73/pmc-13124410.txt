Characteristics and prognostic factors of patients with community-acquired pneumonia combined with pulmonary embolism: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Lanchun Sun, Huaxiu Lin, Huibin Lin, Biman Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a frequently missed complication in elderly patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), partly due to overlapping clinical features. This study aimed to identify early indicators for PE detection in this population. A prospective cohort of 180 elderly CAP patients (≥60 years) admitted between March 2023 and July 2024 was enrolled. Patients with elevated D‑dimer (>0.50 mg/L) underwent computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA). Of 140 CTPA-evaluated patients, 80 had CAP+PE and 60 had CAP alone. Demographics, comorbidities, symptoms, and laboratory parameters (D‑dimer, fibrinogen, CRP, PCT) were compared, and diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves. The CAP+PE group was older (71.35 ± 5.04 vs. 69.03 ± 4.57 years; P <.05) and had higher rates of hypertension (65% vs. 40%), coronary artery disease (48% vs. 25%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (55% vs. 30%), cancer (35% vs. 15%), diabetes (50% vs. 28%), and long-term bedridden status (38% vs. 18%) (all P <.05). Syncope (28% vs. 8%), chest pain (45% vs. 20%), and dyspnea (75% vs. 55%) were more prevalent in CAP+PE (P <.05). D‑dimer (median [IQR]: 4.2 [2.8–6.1] mg/L vs. 1.9 [1.1–2.7] mg/L) and fibrinogen (4.1 ± 1.2 g/L vs. 3.3 ± 0.9 g/L) were significantly elevated in CAP+PE (P <.05). ROC analysis showed D‑dimer (cutoff 3.13 mg/L) had moderate diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.73; sensitivity 81%, specificity 63%), while fibrinogen (cutoff 2.79 g/L) had lower accuracy (AUC 0.68). Elevated D‑dimer (>3.13 mg/L) serves as a practical screening tool for PE in elderly CAP patients, particularly in resource-limited settings lacking CTPA; high-risk comorbidities and atypical symptoms (syncope/chest pain) further support early PE evaluation.

1. Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is an inflammation of the lung parenchyma caused by infectious pathogens, including those with a definite incubation period leading to symptoms within an average incubation period after hospital admission.[1] Common symptoms include productive cough, coughing, fever, or worsening of existing symptoms with changes in sputum characteristics, with or without chest pain.[2] CAP has a high incidence and potential mortality rate, posing a significant threat to human health. Patients often have markedly weakened immune systems and frequently have comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, which increase their risk of concurrent infections or other diseases, significantly adding to the societal economic burden.[3] Pulmonary embolism (PE) refers to a disease or clinical syndrome caused by various types of emboli obstructing the pulmonary vasculature. Over 90% of PEs are pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), where thrombi obstruct the pulmonary vessels, primarily characterized by respiratory and pulmonary circulatory impairments. PE symptoms are diverse and nonspecific, commonly including chest pain, hemoptysis, syncope, and dyspnea or shortness of breath.[4]

As age increases, the incidence of PE tends to rise. Previous research has indicated that the incidence rate of PE in individuals over 60 years old is approximately 0.2%, with a mortality rate exceeding 70% among elderly patients with PE.[5] Infections elevate the risk of concurrent PE in elderly populations. Most epidemiological data on PE derive from studies on venous thromboembolism (VTE), which in some European countries is a leading cause of morbidity, hospitalization, and mortality, with an annual incidence rate of about 1 to 2 per 1000. Infections are the primary cause of CAP.[6] Numerous studies, both domestic and international, have confirmed that Streptococcus pneumoniae is the main pathogen in CAP. Current guidelines on the management of CAP issued by various thoracic societies, including the Chinese Medical Association of Respiratory Diseases, American and British Thoracic Societies, the South African Thoracic Society in collaboration with the Infectious Diseases Society of South Africa, and the Japanese Respiratory Society, all highlight Streptococcus pneumoniae as the most significant pathogen.[7] Recently, the proportion of atypical pathogens such as Mycoplasma in CAP infections has been increasing, with viruses also playing a crucial role. Factors such as smoking, advanced age, excessive alcohol consumption, and comorbidities substantially increase the incidence of CAP. Smoking not only impairs the ciliary clearance ability of the airway mucosa but also damages the respiratory epithelium, thereby elevating the risk of CAP.[8] Alcohol consumption can suppress immune function, leading to pneumonia, and significantly increases the risk of aspiration pneumonia. Chronic underlying diseases such as diabetes also heighten the risk of CAP.

PE and VTE share common risk factors, which include any factors that can lead to blood stasis, endothelial damage, and a hypercoagulable state. These are divided into primary and secondary factors. Primary factors related to PE are associated with genetic variations that cause recurrent thrombosis in patients. The main primary risk factors include decreased antithrombin, elevated homocysteine levels, Factor V mutation, reduced quantity or activity of plasminogen, and deficiencies in Protein S and Protein C. Secondary factors such as prolonged bed rest, fractures, postsurgical conditions, concurrent malignancies, post-venous catheterization, obesity, advanced age, hypertension, pulmonary heart disease, and elevated blood lipids can also trigger PE.[9] These factors can exist independently or coexist with multiple factors.

In recent years, with the advancement of technology, various diagnostic techniques have emerged, significantly increasing the detection rate of PE. Consequently, the prevention and treatment of PE have become key areas of research. The clinical symptoms of PE are not specific and can vary widely, which, along with its lower incidence compared to other pulmonary diseases, makes it prone to misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis.[10] When CAP coincides with PE, the clinical symptoms are very similar to those of CAP alone, making identification challenging.[11] This paper aims to explore the differences in age, gender, underlying diseases, clinical symptoms, and laboratory tests between 80 patients with CAP combined with PE and those with only CAP. The goal is to identify valuable indicators for the early screening of CAP combined with PE, aiding clinicians in early recognition of both conditions.

2. Patients and methods

This study selected 180 patients admitted to our hospital’s respiratory medicine ward for CAP from March 2023 to July 2024. Upon admission, patients underwent routine D-dimer testing, and those 140 patients who exhibited clinical symptoms suggestive of PE and had significantly elevated D-dimer levels underwent computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) examinations. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhanjiang Central People’s Hospital (Approval Number: ZJCH-EC-2023-045), and all patients provided informed consent.

Inclusion criteria

CAP Group: 1. Patients meet the diagnostic criteria for CAP. 2. Significantly elevated D-dimer levels (>0.50 mg/L). 3. CTPA shows no clear signs of PE.

CAP with PE Group: 1. Meet the diagnostic criteria for CAP. 2. Significantly elevated D-dimer levels (>0.50 mg/L). 3. CTPA indicates signs of PE.

Exclusion criteria

previously diagnosed with PE or other thrombotic conditions prior to admission. 2. Unable to undergo CTPA due to physical conditions or other reasons. 3. Incomplete data.

2.1. Clinical and laboratory assessments

Among 180 patients hospitalized for CAP, 140 underwent CTPA due to elevated D-dimer levels. Based on the CTPA results, patients were categorized into groups of CAP with PE and CAP alone. A comparative analysis was conducted on age, gender, clinical symptoms, and laboratory findings between the 2 groups. All enrolled patients had approximately 4 mL of venous blood collected the morning after admission for routine blood tests, D-dimer, CRP, PCT, and fibrinogen measurements. About 2 mL of arterial blood was drawn from the radial artery immediately for testing, with collections done either before oxygen administration or 30 minutes after cessation. Within 24 hours of admission, all patients routinely underwent echocardiography to assess atrial and ventricular dimensions and arterial pressures. All patients had a CTPA performed post-admission. Prior to the examination, the condition of the patient’s upper limb veins was assessed. A high-pressure injector was used to initially inject 20 mL of saline at a rate of 5 mL/s; if no extravasation occurred, contrast agent Iodixanol was administered. Utilizing a Philips 64-slice spiral CT scanner, the scan covered from the lung apex to the costophrenic angle with a pitch of 0.625:1, tube voltage of 180 kV, and tube current of 350 mA. The scan was automatically triggered at the superior vena cava level with a threshold of 150 HU and a delay of 2 seconds before scanning. Post-scan, images were uploaded to the workstation for independent assessment by 2 senior technicians.

2.2. Statistical analysis

The data will be analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (Chicago) statistical software. Continuous data will be presented as (x ± s), while non-normally distributed metric data were described using quartiles M (P25, P75). Categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages. For metric data that followed a normal distribution, independent sample t-tests were used for comparisons between groups. For metric data that did not follow a normal distribution, rank sum tests were employed. Chi-square tests were used for comparisons between groups for categorical data. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) to determine the optimal cutoff values, sensitivity, and specificity. Generally, an AUC of 0.5 to 0.7 indicates low diagnostic value; an AUC of 0.7 to 0.9 indicates moderate diagnostic value; and an AUC >0.9 indicates high diagnostic value.

3. Result

3.1. Comparison of clinical characteristics between patients

A total of 140 patients met the criteria and were included in this study. Among them, 80 patients were in the CAP combined with PE group, consisting of 41 males and 39 females, with an average age of 71.35 ± 5.04 years. The CAP group included 60 patients, with 27 males and 33 females, and an average age of 69.03 ± 4.57 years. There was no significant difference in gender between the 2 groups (P >.05). The average age of patients in the CAP combined with PE group was higher than that in the CAP group. The differences in common symptoms such as fever, cough, expectoration, and hemoptysis between the 2 groups of patients were not significant (P >.05). 3.6. Diagnostic value of D-dimer and fibrinogen

When the D-dimer level is 3.13 mg/L, its diagnostic sensitivity for CAP combined with PE is 0.81, and specificity is 0.63. The area under the ROC curve for D-dimer is 0.730, indicating a moderate diagnostic value for CAP combined with PE. At a fibrinogen level of 2.79 g/L, the sensitivity is 0.55, and specificity is 0.78, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.676, suggesting a lower diagnostic value for CAP combined with PE (Fig. 1 and Table 6).

4. Discussion

Despite advancements in the management of CAP, its significant incidence and mortality persist, imposing substantial societal burdens.[12,13] Concurrently, PE, a common cardiovascular condition characterized by diagnostic challenges and high mortality, can complicate CAP. Pathogens in CAP can induce vascular endothelial damage and a prothrombotic state through direct invasion or inflammatory responses, increasing susceptibility to PE.[14–20] Given the overlapping clinical features between CAP and PE and the relative scarcity of research on their co-occurrence, timely diagnosis of CAP combined with PE (CAP + PE) in the elderly is crucial yet difficult.[21–24]

4.1. Key findings

This study identified key differences between elderly patients with CAP + PE and those with CAP alone. Firstly, patients in the CAP + PE group were significantly older than those in the CAP group (P <.05). This aligns with the known age-related increase in thrombotic risk due to physiological changes such as increased procoagulant factors, blood viscosity, and endothelial vulnerability.[25–28]

Secondly, comorbidities significantly associated with thrombotic risk were more prevalent in the CAP + PE group. Patients had higher rates of hypertension, coronary heart disease, COPD, cancer, diabetes, and prolonged bed rest (all P <.05). These conditions contribute to thrombosis via mechanisms including endothelial damage (hypertension, diabetes), hypercoagulability (cancer, diabetes, COPD), and stasis (bed rest, heart failure).[29–34]

Thirdly, while symptoms like fever, cough, and hemoptysis were similar between groups, chest pain, dyspnea, and syncope occurred significantly more frequently in the CAP + PE group (P <.05). The pathophysiology of PE (e.g., increased right ventricular strain, ventilation/perfusion mismatch, potential arrhythmias/hypotension) explains the heightened severity of dyspnea and increased incidence of syncope.[35–37] Consequently, patients with CAP + PE also exhibited a significantly higher rate of respiratory failure (PO₂<60 mmHg) compared to those with CAP alone (P <.05).

4.2. Regarding laboratory and functional markers

CRP, PCT, and WBC are 3 indicators that can reflect the level of inflammation in the body, particularly effective in indicating bacterial infections.[38–40] In patients with PE, serum CRP levels can also rise. The reasons for CRP elevation in PE are believed to include: Patients with PE may experience systemic hypotension, leading to insufficient blood supply to the liver and kidneys, affecting their ability to clear CRP, thus elevating its levels; Vascular endothelial damage can cause thrombosis, which also promotes CRP production, increasing its levels; Macrophages present in the thrombi of PE patients, once activated, secrete cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-1, which promote CRP production. In this study, patients with CAP combined with PE had slightly higher serum CRP levels than those with CAP alone, but the difference was not statistically significant (P >.05). PCT has high value in detecting bacterial infections and can distinguish between noninfectious and infectious inflammations.[41] This study found no significant statistical difference in serum PCT levels between patients with CAP combined with PE and those with CAP alone (P >.05). White blood cell count is also a relatively sensitive indicator of infection; in CAP patients, white blood cells can vary with the severity of the infection, while the combined presence of PE has a relatively minor effect on blood white cell counts. This research observed no significant statistical difference in white blood cell counts between patients with CAP combined with PE and those with CAP alone (P >.05).

Fibrinogen, a glycosylated protein molecule produced by liver cells, is maintained at a relatively low level under normal conditions.[42] During inflammatory responses, levels of fibrinogen can significantly increase. Fibrinogen also participates in the body’s clotting process, affecting platelet aggregation, altering blood viscosity, damaging vascular endothelium, and potentially leading to thrombotic diseases.[43] In patients with CAP, elevated fibrinogen levels are observed, and such elevations are associated with an increased risk of thrombotic diseases.[44,45] This study demonstrates that fibrinogen levels in the CAP combined with PE group are higher than in the CAP group, with statistically significant differences (P <.05). D-dimer, a product formed from the degradation of cross-linked fibrin in thrombi, indicates activation of the fibrinolytic system.[46] Infection can damage vascular endothelial cells, leading to localized activation of the coagulation system and thrombus formation. During shock caused by infection, blood stasis within vessels can lead to microthrombi formation and further activate the fibrinolytic system, causing an increase in D-dimer levels.[47,48] When PE is present, the degradation of fibrin in thrombi by the fibrinolytic system further increases D-dimer levels. This paper finds that D-dimer levels in patients with CAP combined with PE are higher than in those with CAP alone, and the difference is statistically significant (P <.05). In cases of PE, pulmonary vessels are obstructed, leading to a gradual increase in pulmonary artery pressure and an increase in the right ventricular afterload, subsequently causing remodeling of the right ventricular structure.[49] Acute PE, especially with large emboli obstructing the main pulmonary artery, can lead to a sudden increase in right ventricular afterload, causing right ventricular dysfunction.[50,51] This study found a significant increase in the number of patients with right ventricular dysfunction in the CAP combined with PE group compared to the CAP group alone, with statistically significant differences (P <.05). This study aims to identify valuable indicators that can help clinicians promptly recognize elderly patients with CAP combined with PE. Additionally, this study acknowledges its limitations, including a small sample size and potential bias.

4.3. Significance and limitations

The significance of this study lies in identifying simple indicators for the early detection of elderly CAP combined with PE in settings where CTPA is not widely available. Given the limited availability of advanced imaging modalities such as CTPA in primary care settings, our findings highlight the potential utility of D-dimer as a simple and accessible screening tool for early detection of PE in elderly CAP patients. This approach could help reduce misdiagnosis rates and improve clinical outcomes, particularly in resource-constrained environments. We acknowledge the study’s limitations, including its retrospective design, relatively small sample size, and potential for selection bias. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings and potentially develop risk stratification tools.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies advanced age, comorbidities, and elevated D-dimer levels as key risk factors for PE in elderly CAP patients. D-dimer testing, with its moderate diagnostic value, offers a practical approach for early screening of PE in settings where CTPA is not readily available.
