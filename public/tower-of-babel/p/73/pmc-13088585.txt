Risk prediction model for malnutrition in older adults: a systematic review
Authors: Ruijuan Liu, Lu Li, Yansheng Peng, Yuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal population aging is a major public health trend. The incidence of malnutrition among older adults is also continuously rising. The occurrence of malnutrition among older adults will affect their quality of life and disease prognosis. Although various risk prediction models have been developed to identify the risk of malnutrition in older adults, a comprehensive systematic review of these models is currently lacking.ObjectiveThe aim is to systematically evaluate the risk prediction models for malnutrition in older adults that have been published both domestically and internationally, assess their predictive performance, verification status, and methodological quality, thereby providing a basis for selecting appropriate models in clinical practice.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in Chinese and English databases. These included PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP Database, and SinoMed. Relevant literature on predictive models for malnutrition risk in older adults was included. The search period covered the establishment of the databases to July 1, 2025. Only Chinese and English publications were considered. Two researchers independently screened the literature according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. They evaluated the quality of the included literature and extracted the data. The PROBAST risk of bias and applicability tool was used to assess the risk of bias and applicability of the included research models. Data extraction included the first author, publication year, country, study subjects, study type, predictive factors, model construction methods, and predictive performance.ResultA total of 27 articles were included, comprising 27 models. The sample size ranged from 115 to 3387 cases. Regarding model construction methods, Logistic regression models and Machine learning approaches were used. For model presentation, nomograms and regression equations were primarily used. The number of final included predictive factors ranged from 3 to 17, with common factors including age, BMI, hemoglobin level, serum albumin level, depression status, and daily activity ability (ADL). In terms of performance, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) ranged from 0.687 to 0.984. Validation was conducted internally in 16 studies, while 8 studies conducted external validation. Overall, the applicability of the included models was good; however, all studies demonstrated a high risk of bias.ConclusionThe included models have relatively good predictive performance. However, the overall study has a high risk of bias. In the future, it may be considered to adopt visual model presentation methods to build models with low bias risk, superior predictive performance, and strong clinical applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07235-x.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) report indicates that the global population aged 60 and above is projected to increase from 110 million in 2023 to 1.4 billion in 2030 [1]. As global aging accelerates, healthcare and society will face significant challenges. These challenges involve maintaining the health and quality of life of older adults. The United Nations’ “Decade of Action on Nutrition” (2016–2025) has listed malnutrition as the top priority concern [2].

Malnutrition (MN) is a common geriatric syndrome. It refers to an energy or nutrient deficiency caused by insufficient intake or utilization disorders. It is an important risk factor for clinical adverse outcomes [3]. A study in China found that the incidence of malnutrition or the risk of malnutrition among elderly hospitalized patients was 30% to 50% [4]. Additionally, a meta-analysis showed that the prevalence of malnutrition among community-dwelling elderly in China was 2.4% to 52.5% [5]. Another study indicated that about one quarter of older adults aged 65 and above in Europe are at high risk of malnutrition [6]. A researcher in China used GBD data to study changes in the burden of malnutrition among older adults worldwide from 1990 to 2021. The results showed that although malnutrition was more common in less developed regions, its incidence also increased in regions with higher socioeconomic development [7].

The above research indicates that malnutrition among older adults is widespread globally, and its incidence is not optimistic. However, in clinical practice, the nutritional deficiencies of the older adult population are often overlooked. It has a high rate of missed diagnosis, as it is usually diagnosed only when patients exhibit obvious clinical symptoms [8]. Therefore, the issue of malnutrition in older adults urgently requires the attention of medical professionals.

Malnutrition in older adults is a widespread and concerning health issue, characterized by not only reducing the quality of life of older adults, causing adverse clinical outcomes, prolonging their hospital stays, and increasing economic burdens [9–10], but also significantly increasing the infection rate, readmission rate, mortality rate, and disability rate of elderly patients [11–13]. Malnutrition can lead to a series of health problems, including muscle atrophy, cognitive decline, and the occurrence of cardiovascular adverse events [14–16]. Therefore, early screening and precise identification of high-risk groups for malnutrition among elderly patients are crucial for their rehabilitation and prognosis.

Risk prediction models are mathematical formulas used to assess the existence of specific conditions or the probability of future events, capable of effectively identifying disease risk factors and quantifying the risk degree of each factor [17–18]. In recent years, although there are various models for predicting the risk of malnutrition in older adults, there is currently no systematic review that conducts a comprehensive assessment of their quality and applicability. This study aims to comprehensively and systematically evaluate the quality and applicability of nutrition risk prediction models for older adults, providing scientific guidance for clinical medical professionals in choosing reliable prediction models, and proposing constructive suggestions for the development and improvement of future models, with the aim of more effectively guiding the nutrition management of older adults.

Materials and methods

Research design and registration

This review followed the recommended guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) [19]. This systematic review has been registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251123757). This study was carried out strictly in accordance with the registration protocol without any modifications.

Literature search strategy

A total of 9 databases were searched, including Chinese databases such as CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP Database, and CBM, and English databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and CINAHL. The search was conducted using a combination of subject terms and free words. Chinese search terms included: “elderly”, “old age”, “nutritional disorders”, “nutritional risk”, “malnutrition”, “forecasting model”, “risk prediction”, “risk assessment”, “risk factor”, “predictive factor”, “predictive factor”, “nomogram”. English search terms included: “aged”, “elderly”, “nutritional disorders”, “nutritional risk”, “Malnutrition”, “forecasting”, “predictive model”, “risk prediction model”, “prognostic model”, “nomograms”. The search period was from the establishment of the databases to July 1, 2025. Taking PubMed as an example, the search strategy is shown in Fig. 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for literature

Inclusion Criteria: ①Population: Elderly individuals aged 60 years or above who meet the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition; ②Intervention/Model: Studies on the development and/or validation of models for predicting the risk of malnutrition in older adults; ③Comparative model: No comparator model; ④Outcome: The primary outcome is malnutrition, and the specific operational definition adopted is MNA-SF, NRS-2002, MNA, PG-SGA, NSI, GLIM, albumin level, prealbumin level, ESPEN, and the status of biomarkers indicating micronutrient deficiency;⑤Study Design: Cross-sectional studies, retrospective studies, and prospective studies.

Exclusion Criteria:①Language: Publications in languages other than Chinese or English; ②Availability and Completeness: Studies for which the full text or data are incomplete; ③Model Development: Models developed based on systematic reviews or meta-analyses; ④Number of Predictive Variables: Models with fewer than two predictive variables (these are considered simple screening tools, not multivariable prediction models).

Literature screening and data extraction

Firstly, two researchers (Liu R.J. and Li L.) who have received training in evidence-based medicine developed a search strategy for conducting literature retrieval. We discussed expanding the retrieval strategy with the librarian. Subsequently, two reviewers (Liu R.J. and Li L.) imported the retrieved literature into EndNote 20 software for independent screening. During the literature screening, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Firstly, the titles of each study will be reviewed. Studies that are obviously irrelevant will be excluded. Then, further reviews of the abstracts and full texts will be conducted, and literature that does not meet the standards will be excluded. When crucial information in the study is not clearly stated, the authors contacted the original research authors via email to obtain the relevant information. In cases of disagreement during the literature screening process, the two reviewers (Liu R.J. and Li L.) resolved these differences through discussion. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer (Li Y.) will serve as an arbitrator, and a final decision will be made. This study adopted the strict evaluation and data checklist (CHARMS, checklists for critical appraisal and data extraction for systematic reviews of prediction modelling studies) [20]. The final extracted data include: the first author, publication year, country, study type, sample size, incidence rate, handling of missing data, model construction method, variable selection, model performance (discrimination and calibration), validation method, model presentation, and the final included predictive factors.

Methodological quality evaluation

Two researchers who have undergone evidence-based training independently evaluated and cross-checked the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [21]. The risk of bias assessment covers four areas: study subjects, predictors, results, and statistical analysis. It includes 20 questions, each answered with “Yes”, “No”, or “Unclear”. If all questions in a particular area are answered as “Yes”, that area is considered to have “low risk of bias”; if one or more questions are answered as “No”, that area is considered to have “high risk of bias”; if any question is answered as “Unclear”, that area is considered to have “unclear risk of bias”. If all four areas have low risk of bias, the overall study belongs to “low risk of bias”; if any one area has high risk of bias, the overall risk of bias is high; if the risk of bias is unclear in at least one domain and low in all others, the overall rating is “Unclear”. The applicability evaluation only assesses the first three areas, and the applicability of each area and the overall applicability are classified as “High Risk”, “Low Risk”, or “Unclear”. The evaluation process is similar to the risk of bias assessment.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted of the construction process of the risk prediction model, its performance and validation, bias risk, and evaluation of applicability.

Ethical considerations

This study does not require ethical approval, given the use of previously published secondary data and the nature of the systematic review.

Results

Process and results of literature screening

A total of 3,219 relevant articles were initially screened. After a series of screening procedures, 27 studies [22–48] were finally included. The process and results of literature screening are shown in Fig. 2.

Basic characteristics of included studies

A total of 27 studies were included in this research. They were published from 2016 to 2025. Among them, 2 studies were conducted in Europe, and 25 in Asian countries. Most studies were cross-sectional. Only 6 studies [22, 24, 30, 40, 41, 47] reported methods for handling missing values, including direct deletion, mean interpolation, multiple imputation, and random forest imputation. The diagnostic criteria included MNA-SF, NRS-2002, MNA, PG-SGA, NSI, GLIM, albumin level, prealbumin level, ESPEN, and the status of biomarkers indicating micronutrient deficiency. Regarding the model type, 11 articles [25–27, 31, 34, 36, 37, 39, 42, 43, 47] were only model development, 8 articles [32, 33, 35, 38, 40, 45, 46, 48] were model development plus internal validation, and 8 articles [22–24, 28–30, 41, 44] were model development plus internal and external validation. Construction methods of the included models and prediction efficacy

The total sample size ranged from 115 to 3,387 participants in this study. The prevalence of malnutrition risk among older adults ranges from 21.60% to 73.73%. Regarding the construction of the prediction models, 19 models used Logistic Regression (LR), 6 models used LASSO-Logistic Regression, and, in addition, models were built using machine learning methods, such as Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) and XGBoost. The internal validation methods for the model include Bootstrap, Cross-validation, and Random splitting. The external validation methods include Space validation and Temporal validation. All included models used the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) to evaluate discrimination. 23 articles [22–29, 31, 33–34, 36–43, 45–48] reported the AUC during modeling, ranging from 0.687 to 0.984, 9 articles [29, 30, 32, 35, 38, 40, 44, 46, 48] reported the AUC in internal validation, ranging from 0.788 to 0.965, and 7 articles [22–24, 28–30, 41] reported the AUC in external validation, ranging from 0.753 to 0.928. Regarding the calibration methods, 18 articles [22, 24, 25, 27–33, 35, 38, 40–42, 44, 45, 48] used calibration curves, 16 articles [22–25, 27–29, 33–35, 39, 41, 43, 46–48] used the H-L test, 2 articles [30, 47] used the Brier score, and 12 articles [22, 24, 25, 29–31, 38, 40–42, 44, 48] used decision curve analysis. 16 articles [22, 23, 25, 26, 29, 30, 32, 34, 35, 36, 37, 39, 40, 42, 45, 47] reported the specificity and sensitivity of the model, with the specificity of all being greater than 0.651 and the sensitivity of all being greater than 0.637. However, the report on the model’s accuracy indicators is simply lacking. Detailed results are shown in Table 2.

Model report of the malnutrition risk prediction model

The final presentation formats of the models also varied. For predictive model development studies, the number of events per variable (EPV) for each independent variable was ≥ 20, and for validation studies, the sample size was ≥ 100 [49]. 11 studies [24, 27, 31, 33, 35, 39, 40, 43–46] had an insufficient sample size. Only 14.81% (4/27) [22, 24, 40, 41] of the studies reasonably handled missing data, and 29.63% (8/27) [23, 26, 34, 36, 37, 39, 43, 46] of the studies did not report or only used the H-L goodness-of-fit test for calibration. Finally, 40.74% (11/27) [25–27, 31, 34, 36, 37, 39, 42, 43, 47] of the studies did not conduct model validation. These issues further highlight the potential limitations of these studies.

The applicability evaluation of the included studies is divided into three fields: study subjects, predictors, and results. The included models are applicable across all three aspects, and the overall applicability evaluation is relatively high.

Discussion

Analysis and discussion of predictive factors for malnutrition in older adults

Due to differences in research types and the variables included, the predictive factors adopted in each study differ. However, this study found commonalities among the predictive factors across different studies. The review found that models frequently incorporated factors such as age, BMI, hemoglobin level, serum albumin level, depression status, and ADL, which align with their established biological or clinical links to malnutrition, as shown in studies [50–54].

Age is a predictive factor for malnutrition risk in elderly patients [23, 24, 26–28, 33–37, 39–41, 44, 47], consistent with the research results of American scholar Truijen [47]. BMI [26, 30, 32, 37, 39, 40, 42, 44, 45] is also a key factor in predicting malnutrition risk in elderly patients, consistent with the results of the Korean scholar Kim [50]. Biochemical markers such as hemoglobin [25, 32, 39, 41, 42, 46, 47] and serum albumin [30, 32, 37, 39, 40, 44, 45] are key predictors of malnutrition risk in elderly patients. Possible reasons are that low serum albumin levels are closely related to malnutrition, as serum albumin is an important indicator reflecting protein-energy malnutrition [51]. Depression status [22, 25, 30–32, 43, 46] is also a key factor in predicting malnutrition risk in elderly patients, consistent with the findings of Japanese scholar Kunugi H [52]. The possible reason is that depression not only directly affects the eating behavior of older adults, but also interacts with nutritional metabolism through the neuro-endocrine-immune mechanism, significantly increasing the risk of malnutrition. ADL [22, 23, 30, 40, 45] is also an important indicator for predicting malnutrition risk in elderly patients, which is consistent with the research results of Chinese scholar Song Y [53]. Japanese scholar Nagai K’s research has found that a decline in functional ability (primarily a decrease in daily activity ability) is significantly associated with a deterioration in nutritional status [54].

In addition to the six key factors discussed above, multiple comorbidities, multiple medications, eating habits, number of teeth, BI score, and other factors are also important for predicting the risk of malnutrition in elderly patients.

Model performance and overall characteristics

The models included in this systematic review demonstrated good discriminative ability, with pooled area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.687 to 0.984. Certain models (such as the malnutrition prediction model for elderly lung cancer patients receiving chemotherapy developed by Wei et al. [32]. The malnutrition prediction model for long-term bedridden elderly patients was developed by Zhang et al. [38], and the prediction model for elderly hospitalized cancer patients was developed by Duan et al. [40]) all exhibited AUC values > 0.940, indicating excellent performance and suggesting high potential for application in specific elderly subpopulations. However, the malnutrition prediction model for Alzheimer’s disease patients developed by Xiao et al. [34] showed poor performance, with an AUC of 0.687. However, the distribution of included studies revealed significant imbalances. There was a notable geographical concentration: 24 studies originated from China, 1 from Poland [43] developing a malnutrition prediction model for older adults (AUC = 0.840), 1 from Singapore [46] developing and validating a malnutrition prediction model for elderly cancer patients (AUC = 0.799, 0.788), and 1 from the UK [47] developing a malnutrition prediction model for free-living older adults (AUC = 0.788). Although these models demonstrated good predictive performance in assessing the risk of malnutrition among older adults across different countries, the excessive representativeness of the Chinese study (24/27) is a key limitation that may limit the generalizability of the review’s conclusions. Secondly, the majority of studies focus on predicting malnutrition risk in elderly patients with chronic diseases and cancer. This likely stems from the fact that these patient groups face higher malnutrition risks due to both the diseases themselves and treatment side effects. However, this focus also introduces limitations in research samples, potentially overlooking malnutrition conditions and prediction needs among non-chronic disease patients or healthy elderly populations, thereby restricting the applicability and accuracy of prediction models in broader elderly groups. Regarding modeling approaches, logistic regression (LR) remains the mainstream choice, primarily because of its simple model structure and easily interpretable results, which can be directly converted into nomograms or risk scores. Only a few studies have attempted machine learning algorithms (such as random forest or XGBoost).

Methodological quality and risk of bias

In the studies, some models lacked external validation. External validation assesses the predictive performance of the model on new datasets to ensure its robustness and applicability [55]. It is worth noting that in this study, the depression assessment tools used were GDS-15 and SDS to evaluate the depression status of older adults. Although these tools are widely used, they may introduce subjective biases. Moreover, given the model’s cross-regional cultural differences, it is recommended to select indicators that are universal, accurate, objective, and cross-culturally validated as predictors.

In this study, some scholars used machine learning methods for model development [40]. However, in the studies, model development primarily used logistic regression for variable selection, which has limitations for handling nonlinear relationships among variables. It is notable that only 6 studies [22, 24, 30, 40, 41, 47] reported methods for handling missing data. For the unreported and direct deletion of missing data [30, 47], this may introduce bias and ultimately affect the model performance. Therefore, it is recommended to develop a standardized guideline covering aspects such as the selection of prediction factors, model development, validation design, and handling missing data.

Clinical implications and translational challenges

An ideal prediction model can enhance the quality of life for older adults and improve disease prognosis by identifying those at high risk of malnutrition, enabling precise medical interventions, and thereby optimizing the allocation of healthcare resources. Based on this risk stratification, healthcare professionals can develop more targeted preventive interventions, such as customized nutritional supplementation plans, regular health monitoring, and lifestyle guidance, to effectively delay disease progression and reduce the incidence of malnutrition-related complications. However, significant barriers remain in translating existing models into clinical practice. Firstly, these models carry a high risk of bias and lack sufficient validation, undermining their foundational reliability and leading clinicians to approach prediction tools that may fail in specific scenarios with caution. Secondly, most models are presented only as static nomograms or regression equations, which fail to support dynamic risk calculation and thus limit their clinical application. This observation is highly consistent with the evaluation of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) prediction models by Frondelius et al. [56], who found that most models exhibited low technical readiness and lacked workflow integration.

Implications for future research and practice

Based on this systematic review, future development of malnutrition risk prediction models for older adults should emphasize methodological rigor and clinical utility. It is recommended to adopt cross-validation both internally and externally, or to use multiple independent external test sets to better evaluate generalization performance. Furthermore, it is recommended to standardize the handling of missing data by prioritizing multiple imputation over direct deletion, and to strictly adhere to reporting standards such as CHARMS, ensuring comprehensive documentation of candidate variables, algorithm parameters, and calibration plots to minimize bias during model development. The model construction should systematically incorporate multidimensional predictors encompassing physiological nutrition, functional activity, psychological cognition, disease burden, social environment, and dietary behavior. Concurrently, validation and optimization through multicenter prospective study designs are advised to establish precise prediction tools that not only holistically reflect the essence of risk but also adapt to evolving clinical practices.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides strong evidence for predicting malnutrition risk in older adults. The quality of the prediction model was scientifically evaluated through the PROBAST tool, ensuring the rigor of the research method. However, this study also has some limitations. Firstly, including only Chinese and English literature in the systematic review may introduce publication bias. Excluding grey literature and studies in non-Chinese/non-English languages may lead to the aforementioned regional bias problem. Secondly, due to the high heterogeneity across studies, a descriptive analysis was conducted of the predictive factors. However, no in-depth quantitative analysis was conducted, resulting in relatively limited assessment outcomes. The included risk prediction models performed well in terms of applicability, but they are at high risk of bias, and most have not undergone external validation, which limits their extrapolation ability.

Conclusion

This paper systematically reviews models for predicting malnutrition risk in older adults. The research results indicate that age, BMI, hemoglobin level, serum albumin level, depression status, and ADL are important risk indicators for predicting malnutrition in older adults. Although the overall predictive performance of the models in this study is relatively ideal, there is a high risk of bias, and most of the models have not undergone external validation. Future researchers should prioritize rigorous research design, internal validation, external validation, and transparent reporting. Utilizing machine learning to conduct in-depth mining of multi-center, large-scale clinical data is recommended, particularly to strengthen the external validation. Assessing and managing the nutritional status of the older adult population is of great significance. This will alleviate the economic burden of medical care and promote healthy aging.
