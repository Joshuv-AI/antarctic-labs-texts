To Migrate or not to Migrate? Exploring the Genomic Basis of Partial Migratory Behavior in Bats
Authors: Diego M Peralta, Juan P Jaramillo-Correa, Helena S Hernández-Rosales, Juan I Túnez, Jaime Gasca-Pineda, Rodrigo A Medellín, Luis E Eguiarte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigration is a widespread phenomenon in animals that involves the synchronized movement of numerous individuals across habitats. While migratory traits appear to be environmentally triggered, evidence also points to a still poorly understood genetically regulated mechanism. The study of both the genomic architecture of migration and the degree of similarity across migrating taxa is a recurrent topic in evolutionary biology. Here, we investigated the genomic basis of migration in a flying mammal, the lesser long-nosed bat (Leptonycteris yerbabuenae), a nectar-feeding bat with a partially migratory behavior. Each year, the migrant group of females travels north from central Mexico to give birth in the Sonoran Desert, while the resident females remain and give birth in central Mexico. Using RAD-seq, we detected a demographic decline in this species during the Last Glacial Maximum and found that resident and migratory females form a single genetic cluster. Nevertheless, we identified 10 divergent genomic regions enriched with highly differentiated SNPs (FST values three or more orders of magnitude above the mean). Seven of such regions bear signatures of balancing selection and contain genes that have been identified in other migrating animals; such genes are qualitatively enriched for nervous system-related functions, potentially linked to circadian cycle, orientation, and navigation. Given that the migratory behavior is supposed to have originated recently in L. yerbabuenae (i.e., 10 to 20 Ka), we hypothesize that strong diversifying selection is operating in specific regions of the genome, while the rest is homogenized by the effect of males, which mate indistinctively with females from both groups.

Understanding the genomic basis of migration, a widespread biological phenomenon, is of significant importance to evolutionary biology. A particular challenge lies in systems of partial migration, where individuals from a single population coexist and exhibit different behaviors (migratory and resident). The genetic mechanisms that allow for the persistence of such behavioral polymorphisms are, however, still poorly understood. This study addresses this fundamental question by investigating a unique system: a bat species with partial migratory behavior in a panmictic population. Our findings suggest that balancing selection maintains this behavioral polymorphism by acting on specific genomic regions. This provides new insights into the evolution of migration and how complex behavioral differences are maintained in nature.

Introduction

Migration is a widespread phenomenon in the animal kingdom that occurs in flying, terrestrial, and aquatic organisms. It entails the physiological preparation and synchronization of the long-distance and directional movement of a large number of individuals on a cyclic, usually annual, basis (Dingle and Drake 2007). Theoretically, animals migrate searching for more favorable conditions that allow them to access high-quality resources and evade predators and diseases (Dingle and Drake 2007; O’Connor et al. 2018). To accomplish this, migrants possess a suite of morphological, sensory, physiological, and behavioral adaptations that are activated seasonally, likely in response to specific environmental cues, and which are believed to be genetically encoded (Merlin and Liedvogel 2019).

Our understanding of migration has accumulated over more than a century. This includes the study of migratory routes (Chapman et al. 2014), the environmental cues that trigger migration (Gwinner 1996; Vardanis et al. 2011), the impact of environmental changes on the migratory behavior (Berthold 1999; Newton 2007), and the orientation mechanisms used during migration (Mouritsen 2018). However, our knowledge of the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon is still limited (Freedman and Kronforst 2023; Gu et al. 2024).

Over the past decade, the high-throughput sequencing revolution has allowed the sequencing of reference genomes and the identification of multiple regions that bear the signature of selection in numerous migratory species, birds, fish, insects and mammals (Miller et al. 2011; Zhan et al. 2011; Bazzi et al. 2016; Markert et al. 2016; Lemopoulos et al. 2019; Merlin and Liedvogel 2019; Merlin et al. 2020; Bonar et al. 2022; Cavedon et al. 2022; Clare et al. 2023; Freedman and Kronforst 2023; Le Clercq et al. 2023; Gu et al. 2024; Trulio et al. 2024). These studies have provided initial insights into the genomic architecture and evolution of migration (Merlin and Liedvogel 2019; Fudickar et al. 2021), leading to concepts like the “migratory threshold” (Pulido 2011). Such a concept proposes that, in partially migratory populations, the migratory behavior follows a dichotomous model (migrant-resident) with a polygenic basis, in which a migratory individual needs to inherit enough migratory-associated variants to surpass the nonmigration threshold.

When compared to other taxa, our overall understanding of migration in bats is much more modest (Fleming 2019). While recent years have seen a significant expansion in this field, driven by the use of tools such as stable isotopes, high-frequency radio transmitters (VHF), species distribution models, and studies of phenology, morphology, and immune responses associated with migration (Campbell et al. 2020; Zamora-Mejías et al. 2020; Haest et al. 2021; Wieringa et al. 2021; Rogers et al. 2022), the genomic basis of migration remains virtually unexplored in bats. This knowledge gap is particularly striking given that bats play critical ecological roles, including plant pollination, seed dispersal, and pest control, and that they are important reservoirs of zoonotic pathogens with major public health implications (Richter and Cumming 2006; Fleming 2019; Shapiro et al. 2021). These factors underscore the urgent need to deepen our understanding of bat migration, especially in light of the global bat population decline largely driven by human activities (Medellín et al. 2014; Nassar et al. 2020).

The lesser long-nosed bat (Leptonycteris yerbabuenae, Fig. 1) is a partially migratory species within the Phyllostomidae family (Simmons and Cirranello 2024) that weighs between 23 and 26 g. Its diet primarily consists of nectar and fruits, which makes it a key pollinator and disperser of columnar cacti (such as Stenocereus spp.) and agaves (Agave spp.), with which it shares close co-evolutionary relationships (Trejo-Salazar et al. 2023). These plants have significant ecological, economic, and cultural values (Medellín 2019). For instance, they include the blue and espadín agaves (A. tequilana and A. angustifolia), which are at the base of the tequila and mezcal industries, for which bats play an indirect and critical role (Sperr et al. 2011; Trejo-Salazar et al. 2016).

The distribution of L. yerbabuenae spans from Arizona and parts of New Mexico in the United States, extensive areas of Mexico, primarily along the Pacific coast, and south into Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador (Cole and Wilson 2006; Medellín 2019) (Fig. 2). In terms of reproduction and migration, L. yerbabuenae displays two distinct groups of females with contrasting migratory patterns and asynchronous reproduction. The first group mates between October and December in southwest Mexico, before migrating north during the boreal spring to maternity sites in the Sonoran Desert. In such sites, they give birth and nurture their offspring before returning south in the boreal autumn (Cole and Wilson 2006; Burke et al. 2019). The second group mates between June and July, giving birth during the boreal winter (Galindo et al. 2006); these females remain at latitudes below 21°N throughout the year (Rojas-Martínez 2001; Galindo et al. 2006; Morales-Garza et al. 2007). All adult males are residents, mainly in central Mexico, and mate with females from both groups (Medellín et al. 2018).

Demographic studies suggest that L. yerbabuenae developed a migratory behavior after the Last Glacial Maximum, as a likely result of the northward expansion of agaves (Trejo-Salazar et al. 2021). Several studies have failed to genetically distinguish the migratory and resident female groups (Menchaca et al. 2020; Trejo-Salazar et al. 2021), and a single panmictic population is believed to persist via male-driven gene flow. However, the identification of differentially expressed genes in migratory and resident female brains (Menchaca Rodriguez 2019) might suggest that this behavioral divergence has a genetic basis, potentially involving alleles with adaptive value and related to the migratory/resident behavior (Winker 2010; Saino et al. 2017; Fudickar et al. 2021).

In this study, we genomically characterized this partially migratory species. We first explored the genetic variation of both female groups and compared their demographic history. Then, we searched for SNPs with abnormally high differentiation between resident and migrant females and looked for signs of selection and its mode in the genomic regions that were particularly rich in such variants. Finally, we annotated these candidate regions, examining whether they contained genes involved in specific functions or pathways that could be related to migratory behavior (i.e., nervous or muscular systems, early reproduction, metabolism, cognition, etc.). Looking for the genomic basis of adaptation in partially migratory species may help us to understand how the genomic variants associated with such behavior appear and are maintained within species; it may also provide candidate regions to explore in other migratory taxa and look for convergent evolution.

Results

SNP Discovery

The genotyping of 50 L. yerbabuenae individuals yielded a total of 793,146,896 paired-end reads, with an average of 6,468,749 reads per individual. After quality filtering, 98.76% of the reads mapped to the reference genome (mPhyDis1.pri.v3, Vertebrate Genomes Project, Rhie et al. 2021). Recovered RAD loci had an average read depth of 11X. After filtering, 17,732 SNPs were retained for outlier detection tests, 7,172 of which remained after linkage disequilibrium filtering for demographic analyses. All markers were genotyped for the 32 individuals that passed filtering thresholds (19 migratory and 13 residents).

Population and Demography Analysis

Diversity estimates for the whole sample set yielded values of Ho = 0.292, He = 0.332, π = 0.338, and FIS = 0.13. Only slight differences were observed between the resident and migratory groups (Table 1), but the migratory group exhibited five times more private alleles than the resident group.

Selection of Candidate Genome Regions

Using a basic and iterative approach (see Discussion), we looked for candidate genomic regions that harbor alleles associated with the migratory/resident phenotypes. First, given that we are dealing with a panmictic population (see above) where two behavioral forms coexist with seemingly equal fitness, natural selection should be favoring opposing alleles in specific genomic regions (Charlesworth 2006). Thus, we estimated Fst between the resident and migratory groups in sliding windows of 4 kb with steps of 1 kb and preselected those with Fst values above 0.2; this value represents the 0.975 tail of the distribution of Fst values for all surveyed SNPs. Second, considering that Fst is strongly influenced by within-population diversity (Charlesworth 1998), we also calculated Absolute Nucleotide Divergence (Dxy) between the resident and migratory groups. Dxy is a measure of divergence that is not affected by differences in the internal variability of the compared populations (Cruickshank and Hahn 2014). Subsequently, after intersecting both measures, we retained windows with Fst values exceeding 0.2 and Dxy values exceeding 0.5 (97.5% upper CI) (Fig. S3). This approach identified 67 SNPs distributed across 50 windows on eleven chromosomes (Fig. 5). However, to minimize false positives, we then filtered the data to retain only those windows containing at least two SNPs. This yielded a final set of 29 SNPs within 10 windows.

Third, given that both resident and migratory behaviors continue to exist over time, subsequent to the recent emergence of the latter, we might be facing a case where balancing selection is maintaining migration and resident alleles at intermediate frequency. These regions are expected to show positive Tajima's D values, therefore, we inferred and compared this statistic between the candidate regions and neutral Tajima's D baseline values. We observed that five of the ten previously retained genomic regions had exceptionally high Tajima's D values, differing by at least |1.0| from the neutral baseline. The other four regions also showed high values, but to a lesser extent (a difference of at least |0.5|). Only one region had D values similar to the neutral baseline; however, its neutral comparison window was more than 10,000 bp away from the candidate region, rendering the comparison inconsistent with the others (Table S1). Finally, we followed a similar approach to our Tajima's D analysis, comparing π values between the candidate regions and the neutral baseline. We found that nine of the ten candidate regions showed a π value at least twice that of the neutral baseline. As with Tajima's D, the one candidate region that did not exhibit this difference was the one compared to a neutral window located more than 10,000 bp away.

Annotation

We were able to annotate nine of the SNPs located within the ten Fst candidate regions using SNPEff 5.2 and the P. discolor reference genome. Six of these outliers were located within introns and one more was upstream (<5000 bp) of known genes; one more variant was located in an intergenic region (i.e., at distances exceeding 5000 bp from known genes), while the remaining ones were within an intron of an unknown gene (Table S1).

Expression patterns (taken from Merkin et al. 2012) indicated that four of the annotated candidate genes are preferentially expressed in the mammal brain (bovine model), which also had higher Tajima's D and π compared to the neutral baseline. These genes are involved in learning, memory, and neural development. One more gene was over-expressed in the kidney (ENPP6) and is related to lipid metabolism; this window also had higher Tajima's D and π compared to the neutral baseline. The other three genes did not have a predominant tissue of expression, but were mostly associated with metabolic and immune processes, including anticancer mechanisms (Table 2; Fig. S6).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to genomically characterize the migratory behavior of a flying mammal, the bat Leptonycteris yerbabuenae. This species exhibits a remarkable migratory pattern among mammals (Medellín 2019), involving two poorly genetically differentiated groups that have contrasting migratory habits, one that migrates over 3000 km round-trip and a resident group that does not migrate. This makes it an excellent model for investigating the origin and ongoing evolution of migration (Merlin and Liedvogel 2019).

Surprisingly, migratory bats have received little attention from the scientific community when compared to other, perhaps more charismatic, migratory animals (Merlin and Liedvogel 2019). L. yerbabuenae is not genetically structured and has conserved a high genetic diversity despite having experienced a strong population decline during the LGM. The genetic differences between the migrant and resident females are indeed minimal and restricted to particular genomic regions. Five of these ten genomic regions also exhibited strong signals of balancing selection, while another four showed similar, albeit weaker, signals when comparing candidate regions with adjacent neutral zones. Several of these regions further harbored genes associated with the nervous system. These findings provide crucial insights for future research on the evolution of migration.

Genomic Diversity and Demographic History of a Migratory Bat

Leptonycteris yerbabuenae has higher genetic variation (Ho = 0.292) and is more inbred (FIS = 0.13) than most previously studied bats with similar genomic methods (i.e., RAD-seq). For example, Ho values ranged between 0.088 and 0.275 for species as varied as Lasionycteris noctivagans, Lasiurus cinereus, and Lasiurus borealis in North America (Sovic et al. 2016), Miniopterus schreibersii in Europe (Dufresnes et al. 2023), or Pteronotus gymnonotus in South America (Ito et al. 2022). On the other hand, FIS values in L. yerbabuenae were higher than those reported for P. gymnonotus (FIS = 0.039; Ito et al. 2022) or M. schreibersii (FIS = 0.053; Dufresnes et al. 2023), but lower than those of Lasiurus cinereus (FIS = 0.163; Cornman et al. 2021). This last species is apparently facing conservation issues related to wind turbines in the Midwest of the United States, which have increased its mortality rate in the last few decades (Frick et al. 2017). The elevated FIS value observed in L. yerbabuenae could plausibly be linked to recent demographic shifts, which would be consistent with our results of the species' demographic history (see below). However, it is also possible that this value could be an artifact of the experimental protocol (but see below) or, as a central hypothesis of our study, it could reflect the effect of differential selection (Pearman et al. 2022). In contrast, the nucleotide diversity found in this study reveals high levels of diversity, which lies between those reported for the highly diverse P. gymnonotus (π = 0.84; Ito et al. 2022) and the endangered, isolated, and genomically depauperate Lasiurus semotus from Hawaii (π = 0.0014; Pinzari et al. 2020). The nucleotide diversity observed for L. yerbabuenae further translates into a high Ne, and supports previous results from mitochondrial and nuclear DNA data (Ramirez 2011; Arteaga et al. 2018; Menchaca et al. 2020; Trejo-Salazar et al. 2021).

The abovementioned studies on L. yerbabuenae and our analyses show that the migratory and resident groups of L. yerbabuenae have similar amounts of genetic diversity, although the migratory group has a higher number of private alleles. To ensure the results were not artifacts of missing data, we conducted a cross-validation using SNPs mapped to the Desmodus rotundus genome (Supplementary material). The results were consistent with those presented here, further confirming the observed trends (Table S2, Supplementary material). In any case, the elevated number of private alleles in the migratory group provides evidence for a recent divergence, which appeared with the onset of migratory behavior.

The migration of L. yerbabuenae likely emerged in response to the decline of certain Agave species in central Mexico (e.g., A. kerchovei and A. marmorata, see Aguirre-Planter et al. 2020; Ruiz-Mondragón et al. 2024, respectively) after the Last Glacial Maximum (∼21kya). This may have prompted a group of L. yerbabuenae to move north to find novel food sources (Trejo-Salazar et al. 2023). Indeed, comparisons of ecological niche models between several Agave species and the migratory groups of two Leptonycteris species (including L. yerbabuenae) have shown a coinciding northward range expansion by the end of the LGM. Our demographic analysis of the migratory and resident groups (Fig. S5) revealed that they had similar historical demographies, with an initial period of stability followed by a gradual decline beginning around 30,000 years ago during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM). This decline coincides with the reduction in its food sources, and at the same time, with the emergence of the migratory behavior, which may have arisen as an adaptive response (Trejo-Salazar et al. 2021). Subsequently, the species experienced a sharp reduction in Ne near the onset of the Holocene (Fig. 4). The causes of this abrupt decline remain unknown, highlighting the need for future studies to investigate the underlying factors.

Our study further shows that the migratory and resident groups of L. yerbabuenae are genetically similar, which confirms the previously suggested idea that this species is composed of a single panmictic population (Menchaca et al. 2020; Trejo-Salazar et al. 2021). The mean observed FST value (0.0031) was indeed lower than those reported for bats that are assumed to lack population genetic structure (Pinzari et al. 2020; Ito et al. 2022; Dufresnes et al. 2023). This has two main evolutionary implications: i) the genetic connectivity of the species is likely maintained by males, which all seem to be resident and indistinctively mate with females from both groups (Medellín 2019); ii) the divergence between the migrating and resident groups is likely recent, which invokes the action of strong selective forces to maintain such behavioral difference in the presence of male-driven homogenizing gene flow.

Evolutionary Basis of Migration in L. Yerbabuenae

We explored the putative genomic bases of the migratory behavior in L. yerbabuenae, assuming that its emergence was driven by selective pressures that favored spatial-temporal movement patterns (Alerstam et al. 2003; Hansson and Åkesson 2014). For such a behavior to evolve and persist, it must thus confer advantages or be equally adaptive to the resident behavior, regarding the survival or reproduction of individuals (Lundberg 1988; Griswold et al. 2010; Zuniga et al. 2017).

Partial migration is a widespread phenomenon that shows extreme intra-population variation, involving both migratory and resident individuals (Hansson and Åkesson 2014). Some authors believe that partial migration represents an incipient stage in the evolution of migration (Chapman et al. 2011) and that it provides an opportunity to study parallel adaptive scenarios that include opposing selective pressures (Buchan et al. 2020). Such pressures are believed to follow a disruptive selection model, which favors the genetic variants that determine two phenotypic extremes (migratory or resident) based on a “migratory threshold” (Pulido 2011).

We used a basic, iterative, and complementary approach to look for candidate genomic regions with signs of selection, as expected for a panmictic population within two groups of individuals with divergent phenotypes, one of which emerged recently. We identified 10 genomic regions potentially involved in determining the “migratory threshold” in L. yerbabuenae; five displayed very high Tajima's D values, indicative of strong balancing selection, with another four also showing high Tajima's D values, consistent with balancing selection but to a lesser extent when compared to adjacent neutral zones. Moreover, these nine regions exhibited nucleotide diversity (π) values that were double those of adjacent neutral regions, a genomic signature expected under balancing selection (Charlesworth 2006).

This finding is particularly compelling as both migratory and nonmigratory females coexist within a single population, mating with a common group of males. Such a scenario, where distinct migratory and resident life histories are maintained within a single population despite gene flow, has been observed in species like brown trout (Moran et al. 2024), where partial migration also has a polygenic basis. In those cases, balancing selection emerges as a plausible mechanism for maintaining the genetic variation underpinning these coexisting behavioral phenotypes, particularly in contexts where both strategies can coexist and remain viable, as proposed by Moran et al (2024). These authors further highlight how environmental differences (e.g., in food availability or habitat structure) can drive divergent selection on migratory strategies, ultimately shaping the genomic architecture of complex life-history traits like facultative migration.

The combination of classical statistics, such as FST, Dxy, Tajima's D and π, has been widely employed since the seminal review by Luikart et al. (2003), with remarkable examples including the detection of artificial selection in dogs or drug resistance in pathogenic fungi (Akey et al. 2010; Rhodes et al. 2022). While these approaches may encounter difficulties in more sophisticated population scenarios or when sampled populations have independent histories (Lotterhos and Whitlock 2014; François et al. 2015), this is not the case for the virtually panmictic population of L. yerbabuenae, similar to many other systems where classical statistics are robustly applied (Delmore et al. 2018; Zhou et al. 2022; Clare et al. 2023; Kwon et al. 2024). We acknowledge that modern, more sophisticated approaches (e.g., Bayesian analyses, latent mixed models, or redundancy analyses) often provide more robust results and correct for false positives (de Villemereuil and Gaggiotti 2015; Lotterhos and Whitlock 2015; Caye et al. 2019; Capblancq and Forester 2021), and that the use of whole-genome scans with a larger sample would allow for better results than those using a reduced-representation sequencing approach (Lowry et al. 2017; but see: Catchen et al. 2017; McKinney et al. 2017). We therefore selected this strategy given the limitations of our experimental design, while also leveraging its strengths.

Our study focuses on a single panmictic population with a sole and simple demographic history, but which harbors individuals with two contrasting behaviors (migrants and residents) that have evolved in sympatry, characteristics that enhance the reliability of the classical tests employed, as highlighted by Narum and Hess (2011) or Schneider et al. (2021), among other authors (Lotterhos and Whitlock 2014; Charlesworth and Jensen 2021). Furthermore, we believe that the co-existence of the two contrasting migratory behaviors over time (Burke et al. 2019) within a panmictic population can only be explained by the action of strong balancing selection pressures in specific regions of the genome. This underlying selective pressure should increase our chances of finding such regions, despite the limitations of our study. Nonetheless, we recognize the inherent complexities and potential pitfalls in interpreting genome scan results. Observed signals, for instance, can be influenced by neutral demographic histories that can mimic selection, by the inherent randomness of evolutionary processes, or by our specific methodological and sampling choices that impact detection power and introduce biases, rather than solely reflecting direct adaptive pressures (Buerkle et al. 2011; Tiffin and Ross-Ibarra 2014; Lotterhos and Whitlock 2015; Hoban et al. 2016).

The annotation of the retained candidate regions provides further support for our findings. They contain or are closely located to genes that have been previously associated with migration in other animals. For instance, they include the gene PLXNA4, which also appears with different expression levels in the brains of migratory and resident L. yerbabuenae females (see Menchaca Rodriguez 2019). This gene is also involved in neuronal development in the cerebral cortex (Okada and Tomooka 2012) and with Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases in humans (Schulte et al. 2013; Kang et al. 2016). The gene NPAS3, which is also preferentially expressed in the mammal brain (Brunskill et al. 1999; Luoma and Berry 2018), has been associated in humans with recognition, memory, and learning nurturing instincts (Pickard et al. 2006), and disorders such as autism (Pickard et al. 2006; Li et al. 2022b). Most notably, the NPAS3 gene was recently identified as being under selection in migratory and resident individuals of the common yellowthroat (Geothlypis trichas) (Zamudio-Beltrán et al. 2025). This gene also belongs to the Core CLOCK gene family (Wu et al. 2016), that have been frequently described as clock-regulating genes in migratory birds and fish (O’Malley et al. 2013; Bourret and Garant 2015; Bazzi et al. 2016; Romano et al. 2018; Bossu et al. 2022; Le Clercq et al. 2023). Finally, the ENPP6 gene, associated with lipid metabolism and showing differential expression in the liver (Morita et al. 2016), is also noteworthy. Members of this gene family (ENPP) have been found to be overexpressed in migratory compared to resident juncos (Fudickar et al. 2016).

Additional worth mentioning genes include NEURIM1 (educational achievement in humans; Lee et al. 2018), and TRMT10B (response to stress and disease, possible immune function; Vilardo et al. 2020; Pozzi 2024). Altogether, these genes may suggest that migration in L. yerbabuenae has a genomic basis that is similar to that of other migrant taxa. Alternatively, as Moran et al. (2024) suggest, it is possible that the genes we identified were not directly related to the propensity to migrate itself, but rather to the performance of migration and the lifestyle associated with maintaining this behavior, such as navigation abilities, energy homeostasis, temperature tolerance, etc.

Last, although these regions do not overlap with the coding sequences of these genes, they may lie within regulatory regions, which could modulate gene expression. This opens the question of whether the modulation of gene expression also plays a role in the evolution of migration.

Potential Genomic Drivers of migration

There is increasing evidence that the genes influencing migration are often linked to nervous system development and function, such as those regulating direction, timing, and the decision to migrate (Lundberg et al. 2017; Contina et al. 2018; Clare et al. 2023). According to our results and those from Menchaca Rodriguez (2019), this can now be expanded to bats.

There are several ways in which the nervous system may play a role in the migratory behavior of bats and other migrant animals, including environmental exploration (i.e., echolocation; Schabacker et al. 2021), navigation (e.g., by using Earth's magnetic field, or locating the setting sun; Holland et al. 2006; Lindecke et al. 2019; Lindecke et al. 2021), and regulating the timing of migration (Hansson and Åkesson 2014; Fudickar et al 2021). This has led to the hypothesis that selection is favoring polymorphisms that activate or overexpress the responsible genes for such functions.

As for the immune system, migratory animals are capable of suppressing immunity to preserve energy (Eikenaar et al. 2018). Genetic mechanisms regulating immune responses have been inferred in migratory fish and birds; although the processes and potential interactions with other physiological responses are still unknown (O’Malley et al. 2010; Frias-Soler et al. 2020; Koch and Narum 2020; Hegemann et al. 2022; Narum et al. 2024). In bats, which are reputed reservoirs of zoonotic pathogens, the role of immunity during migration remains poorly understood (Merlin and Liedvogel 2019; Zamora-Mejías et al. 2020; Fudickar et al. 2021; Gu et al. 2024). However, recent studies have shown that these animals also exhibit reduced immunological activity and increased humoral responses during migration (Voigt et al. 2020; Rogers et al. 2022).

Migration is additionally supported by various physiological and morphological adaptations in bats and other animals; these include fuel storage strategies, the rate of ATP production relative to oxygen consumption, specialized wing shapes and body forms, and the ability to transition between contrasting environments, among others (Altizer and Davis 2010; Voigt et al. 2012; Chapman et al. 2015; Kipanyula and Maina 2016; Liechti and McGuire 2017; Gutiérrez et al. 2019; Menchaca Rodriguez 2019; Brown et al. 2023; McGuire et al. 2023). Recent studies have started exploring the genomic basis for some of these factors, particularly in birds and fish, but also in monarch butterflies and deer (Zhan et al. 2014; Fudickar et al. 2016; Franchini et al. 2017; Cavedon et al. 2019; Horton et al. 2019; Cavedon et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022a; Contina et al. 2023). Some of the regions pinpointed in our study have been associated with physiological factors, but this is still a largely unexplored avenue, especially because the migratory strategy of bats demands a higher energy consumption and metabolic rate than that of other migratory animals.

Long-term Maintenance of a Partial Migratory Behavior?

Independent of the genomic regions and pathways implicated, it appears, from this and other studies, that migration has a polygenic nature, likely based on modifying gene expression before and during migration, or on heritable alleles that determine phenotypes favoring the migratory syndrome (Liedvogel and Lundberg 2014; Merlin and Liedvogel 2019). In species with a partial migratory behavior, and consistent with our findings, a polygenic basis for migration would imply the existence of polymorphisms whose additive effect would determine the migratory or resident state of an individual (Pulido 2011).

From an evolutionary point of view, it could be hypothesized that the long-term maintenance of a migrant and a resident group of females that reproduce with the same group of males in a panmictic population could only be explained by balancing or disruptive selective forces acting on such genes (Buchan et al. 2020). In the context of a “migratory threshold,” males would directly affect the likelihood of surpassing this threshold through their homogenizing genetic effect, thereby shaping the behavior of the next generation. For example, a female from a migratory mother might inherit alleles that do not exceed the threshold, making her resident, while the reverse could occur for a female from a resident mother (Pulido 2011). This concept may explain transitions between migratory and resident states across generations, and the maintenance of panmixia in L. yerbabuenae (Merlin and Liedvogel 2019; Trejo-Salazar et al. 2021). Building on this theoretical framework, our study identified specific genomic regions that could potentially be used to test the “migratory threshold” hypothesis. For instance, we could monitor for allele frequency changes over time and determine whether migrant or resident strategies are favored and influenced by climate change and other anthropogenic factors. This should be facilitated by the ever-improving next-generation sequencing technologies and statistical analyses within a quantitative genetics framework.

Long-term studies are also necessary in other migratory taxa to look for shared evolutionary mechanisms among species. For instance, future investigations could explore the genomic landscape of L. nivalis, the sister species of L. yerbabuenae, which also has a partial migratory behavior (Bogan et al. 2017). This will allow us to test for phylogenetic conservatism in the genetic mechanisms governing migration, deepening our understanding of this phenomenon and its evolutionary dynamics.

Conclusion

This genomic study has identified genomic regions that differentiate migrant and resident individuals within a panmictic bat population. Furthermore, this population underwent a bottleneck event, with its timing near the emergence of migratory behavior. Most of the identified genomic regions exhibit signs of balancing selection and are enriched in genes related to the functioning of the nervous system and immune response. These findings highlight the critical role of bats as models for understanding the origins and evolution of migratory behavior. Specifically, regarding L. yerbabuenae and its role as a pollinator of economically and ecologically important plants in Mexico, this study underscores the urgent need to develop effective conservation strategies to safeguard the migration routes of this species, a need that has become even more pressing in the context of ongoing environmental changes.

Methods

Sampling

Sampling of L. yerbabuenae individuals took place when the migratory and resident groups were fully segregated in two distinct sites during the summer of 2022 (Fig. 2). First, we sampled 25 migratory females in June at the maternity cave at “El Pinacate and the Gran Desierto de Altar Biosphere Reserve”, Sonora, northern Mexico (hereafter referred to as Pinacate). Then, in August, we sampled 25 resident females at the maternity cave known as “El Salitre”, located in the state of Morelos, central Mexico. Animals were captured using mist nets, adhering to the Animal Care and Use protocols established by the American Society of Mammalogists (Sikes and Gannon 2016) and with appropriate scientific permits from Secretaría de Medio Ambiente y Recursos Naturales (SGPA/DGVS/08072/21). Tissue samples were obtained using a 5 mm2 biopsy from a wing punch in areas devoid of capillaries and nerve terminals, and were preserved in 70% alcohol at −20 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNAEasy Kit. DNA concentration (averaging 2 ng/μl) was quantified with the Qubit dsDNA broad-range kit, and its quality and integrity were determined after electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels. DNA standardization and genomic library preparation were performed at the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Center using the RAD-seq method (Baird et al. 2008; Davey and Blaxter 2010) with the ApeKI enzyme. Each sample was assigned a unique barcode; after sample pooling, DNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 2 × 150.

SNP Calling and Filtering

Raw sequences were analyzed with FastQC to assess quality, demultiplexing, and filtering; assembly was carried out with Ipyrad (v.0.9.87) using the genome of another phyllostomid bat, Phyllostomus discolor, as reference (mPhyDis1.pri.v3, Vertebrate Genomes Project, Rhie et al. 2021) and recommended parameters for paired-end GBS data (https://ipyrad.readthedocs.io/, accessed on April 6, 2023). While some bias might have occurred, in particular because of a different chromosome number between the reference species and L. yerbabuenae (n = 16 for P. discolor, and n = 32 for L. yerbabuenae), the use of this reference genome provided an ideal compromise between annotation quality and phylogenetic distance to our focus species. This increased our power to retain and annotate highly conserved regions of the genome that have undergone strong selective pressure, compared to a de novo analysis without a reference genome or the use of lower-quality genomes available for related species (Rhie et al. 2021; Formenti et al. 2022).

We excluded individuals with missing data exceeding 70% using VCFtools (−missing-indv). The final dataset for filtering and SNP calling was thus reduced to 32 individuals. Using vcftools and plink, we then established two SNP sets: one for examining overall genomic variability, genetic structure, and demographic history, and another for outlier detection tests. The initial filtering criteria were applied to both sets. Loci were retained only if they had a minimum average depth of six (−minDP 6 in VCFtools), and two alleles per RAD locus (−max-alleles 2 –min-alleles 2 in VCFtools). A minimum allele count of three (−mac 3 in PLINK) ensured that alleles were only considered if present in at least two individuals, while still preserving rare alleles, which is crucial for identifying candidate SNPs in regions potentially linked to natural selection. We finally eliminated sites with over 30% missing data using PLINK (−geno 0.7).

Additionally, and exclusively for the SNPs set for demographic and structure analyses, we filtered out SNPs that were not in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (−hwe 0.0001 in PLINK) and pruned linked variants using Plink v1.9 (−indep-pairwise 30 3 0.15). To do so, we applied a sliding window method and r2 values selected based on PopLDdecay results (Zhang et al. 2019) (Fig. S1). All demographic analyses were conducted using this data subset, except for the fineRADstructure, which used all available SNPs (see below).

Population and Demographic Analyses

Private alleles, observed and expected heterozygosities (Ho and He, respectively), nucleotide diversity (π), and inbreeding coefficient (FIS) were calculated using the populations module of STACKS (Catchen et al. 2013) for all individuals, and for the resident and migrant individuals separately.

Population structure was assessed using four different approaches. First, we used ADMIXTURE v.1.3 (Alexander et al. 2009) to cluster individuals into different groups or populations by estimating each individual's co-ancestry. Next, we determined the genetic differentiation by estimating the pairwise fixation index (Fts) according to Weir and Cockerham (1984), with the stamppFst function in the R package StAMPP (Pembleton et al. 2013). Third, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) using the R package adegenet (Jombart and Ahmed 2011) with the glPCA function. Fourth, we used the fineRADstructure package (Malinsky et al. 2018), which constructs a co-ancestry matrix of haplotypes through a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) clustering algorithm and quantifies nearest neighbor relationships between all individual pairs in the analysis. This approach measures recent co-ancestry by focusing on the most recent coalescent events among the haplotypes at each locus. High values, indicating a high probability of shared co-ancestry, are clustered along the diagonal, and these individuals are clustered into populations in the accompanying tree. The first 100,000 iterations were discarded as burn-in and were followed by 100,000 MCMC steps that were sampled every 1,000 steps for constructing the tree with 1,000 hill-climbing iterations. This analysis used SNPs (n = 17,720) that were not filtered for linkage disequilibrium, given that fineRADstructure utilizes LD signatures to enhance the establishment of relationships between individuals. Results were visualized and plotted using R scripts fineRADstructurePlot.R and FinestructureLibrary.R (available at https://github.com/millanek/fineRADstructure).

The demographic history was determined for the whole sample and for the residents and migrants groups separately using Stairway Plot 2 (Liu and Fu 2020), which implements a flexible multiepoch model to infer changes in population size over time using site frequency spectrum (SFS). We used the average genome mutation rate for Phyllostomidae bats, 2.0 × 10−9 per base pair per year (Paulat et al. 2023) and assumed a generation time of 4 years, based on the average for medium-sized bats (Mundinger et al. 2022). As input into the stairway plot analysis, a folded SFS was generated with δaδi v.1.7.0 (Gutenkunst et al. 2010). Finally, the demographic plot was constructed following the recommendations of Liu and Fu (2020) and Nadachowska-Brzyska et al. (2022).

Selection of Candidate Genome Regions

We first estimated FST between the resident and migratory female groups with Weir and Cockerham's estimator (1984) implemented in VCFtools (−weir-fst-pop), using a sliding window approach (−fst-window-size) with a size of 4k. Subsequently, we calculated the mean and 95% and 99% confidence intervals (CIs) of the distribution of FST values. We defined a threshold of FST > 0.2, corresponding to a midpoint between the 95% and 99% CIs, to retain the initial set of candidate loci (Fig. S2). We also calculated Absolute Nucleotide Divergence (Dxy), which quantifies absolute differentiation without accounting for within-population diversity. This was done using pixy v0.95.090 (Korunes and Samuk 2021) over 4-kbp nonoverlapping windows to ensure our candidate windows were not an artifact of the FST calculation. Subsequently, we retained as candidates those windows exceeding the threshold of FST > 0.2 and Dxy > 0.5 (97.5% upper CI) (Fig. S3). At this point, we decided to filter those regions containing fewer than two SNPs to avoid including single SNP windows that might stochastically exhibit high differentiation.

We then calculated Tajima's D, using VCFtools (−TajimaD 4000), for the candidate regions by combining individuals from both the resident and migratory groups. Additionally, we calculated Tajima's D for our set of neutral SNPs to establish a baseline for this measure. Neutral Tajima's D values were obtained from nearby windows, within 4 kbp, whenever possible. Candidate regions were considered to have a different D from the baseline when they differed by at least |1.00| in Tajima's D. Positive values suggest an excess of medium frequence alleles, which may be indicative of balancing selection or population decline, while negative values suggest an excess of rare alleles, which in turn indicate positive selection or population expansion (Tajima 1989; Vitti et al. 2013). Last, we calculated nucleotide diversity (π), also using VCFtools (−pi-windowed 4000) and following a similar approach. Here, a region was considered to have a high value if it doubled the baseline π value. Candidate regions were expected to exhibit both positive Tajima's D values and higher π values, consistent with the hypothesis that balancing selection maintains migration and resident alleles at intermediate frequency.

We mapped and highlighted these windows in a Manhattan plot per chromosome using ggplot (Wickham 2006), where candidate regions should be observed as overlapping “skyscrapers” (Wang et al. 2022).

Annotation

We used SNPEff 5.2 for gene annotation (Cingolani 2012). This tool enables the use of a reference genome against which candidate SNPs are mapped, along with annotation information (the associated transcriptome); this allows pinpointing the position and effect of each variant. The output is a VCF file that indicates whether a marker is located within a gene (such as in an exon, intron, or regulatory region), close to a gene (upstream or downstream), or in an intergenic region (specifying which genes are the closest on each side). Additionally, it identifies if the SNP results in a synonymous or nonsynonymous mutation when it is located within a coding region.

We then used the web resource https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa/home to explore the differential expression profile of the annotated candidates, using the strand-specific RNA-seq data of nine cow tissues (Merkin et al. 2012). This allowed us to qualitatively compare whether the expression pattern of our candidate genes resulted from chance or exhibited a pattern potentially linked to a particular biological system. Bos taurus was chosen because it was the phylogenetically closest species to L. yerbabuenae among the available options. We finally examined the function of the annotated candidate genes through a nonsystematic literature search, using gene or gene-family names. This search focused on studies published between 2,010 and 2025 that aim to explore gene/family functions and to determine whether such genes/families had been identified in previous studies related to animal migration.
