Low GHG emissions and high sediment sequestration define the carbon function of cascade reservoirs in the Wujiang River Basin, Southwest China
Authors: Xiaoling Wu, Xiaohua Xiang, Hong Yang, Zhiwei Wang, Chao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA comprehensive assessment of hydropower’s climate impact requires considering both greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and carbon burial in sediment. This study examines the Wujiang River in southwestern China, where seven cascade reservoirs were categorized into upper (Group 1), middle (Group 2), and lower (Group 3) reaches according to their geographic locations and proximity to key sediment source areas. The G-res Tool was conducted to simulate greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from these reservoirs, which ranged from 2,231 to 19,774 t CO2e yr−1 after impoundment. The primary influencing factors include reservoir age, surface area, and water retention time. Due to the steep mountainous terrain, deep valleys, and pre-impoundment clearing, the GHG emissions from these groups of reservoirs are lower than those of other reservoirs at similar latitudes worldwide. As the cascade reservoirs were gradually constructed, sediment accumulated behind the dams, leading to the long-term storage of terrigenous carbon. Notably, the upstream Group 1 reservoirs exhibited an exceptionally high terrigenous carbon storage rate during their early impoundment phase (7,468 gC m−2 yr−1), largely due to significant sediment input from the upstream Yachihe Basin, a major sediment source. In contrast, the downstream reservoir (Group 3) exhibited relatively smaller total terrigenous carbon storage rates (319 gC m−2 yr−1), corresponding to reduced sediment retention. Despite variations in sediment retention among the groups, the total terrigenous carbon storage of all groups of reservoirs (ranging from 43,020 to 999,403 tC yr−1) exceeded their post-impoundment GHG emissions. By integrating carbon emissions and sedimentary carbon sequestration across a cascade system, this study provides a system-scale carbon balance assessment of hydropower reservoirs. Our results indicate that the cascade reservoir systems, like those on the Wujiang River, function as a net terrigenous carbon storage, mainly due to the large sediment sequestration. Introduction

Globally, more than one million dams are currently in operation, playing a pivotal role in water resource management and energy production [47]. Due to the increasing demand for renewable energy, the total surface area of reservoirs worldwide is projected to reach 380,000 km2 by 2040 [68]. While hydroelectric development maximizes the utilization of renewable energy, it also poses some environmental challenges, including greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, sediment accumulation, disruption of fish migration, and other ecological impacts [30, 94]. Estimates of the global reservoir GHG footprint vary widely, ranging from 0.5 to 2.3 Pg CO2e yr−1 [1, 8]. Although GHG emissions are a major source of carbon release from reservoirs, long-term sediment storage also contributes to their terrigenous carbon storage. In soil erosion regions, approximately 70–90% of eroded soil is transferred and deposited in downstream reservoirs [71]. While sediment retention by dams reduces reservoir capacity and impairs water storage, it also represents a potentially significant sink of carbon, contributing to long-term carbon burial in reservoirs [81]. Globally, lake and reservoir sediments store up to 820 Pg of carbon, with an annual accumulation rate of 0.2–0.6 Pg C yr−1 [6]. Given that sedimentary carbon is a key component of the global carbon budget, quantifying the balance between gaseous carbon emissions and organic carbon burial could lead to a reassessment of reservoirs’ role in the global carbon cycle [44].

Reservoir GHG emissions exhibit pronounced seasonal and spatial variations, influenced by multiple factors including biological processes (e.g., phytoplankton activity), physical characteristics (e.g., thermal stratification and hydraulic retention time), and anthropogenic activities [34]. The burial of organic carbon in reservoirs is influenced by watershed land use [28], the reservoir characteristics (e.g., dam height, age, and water depth) [13, 98], local temperature [16], hydraulic conditions [45], and reservoir-related environments [40]. Among these factors, terrigenous organic carbon input from the watershed plays an important role in determining the amount of organic carbon available for burial [51, 103]. When carbon input is sufficient, favorable hydrological conditions — particularly prolonged water residence time — can enhance the deposition and long-term preservation of organic carbon in reservoir sediments [6].

China is the world’s largest producer and consumer of hydropower, with over 220 medium- and large- scale hydroelectric power plants (> 50 MW) and over 40,000 small hydroelectric power plants [19]. Southwest China, which holds more than two–thirds of the nation’s hydropower resources, has become the focal point for cascade dam construction [19]. The Wujiang River, a major tributary of the upper Yangtze River, plays a pivotal role in China’s West–East Power Transmission project. The Wujiang River contributes approximately 5% of the Yangtze River’s annual runoff while historically accounting for 12–16% of its sediment load, making it one of the major sediment sources in the upper Yangtze basin. However, since the construction of the Dongfeng Reservoir in the upper reach, sediment transport from the upper Wujiang River has declined by 37.7% [90]. With the sequential completion of cascade hydroelectric stations along the river, the accumulation of carbon trapped in the reservoir area has continued to rise, exhibiting spatial variation across the upper, middle, and lower reaches. This pronounced spatial heterogeneity provides an ideal opportunity to investigate how cascade regulation influences carbon dynamics along the river continuum, offering insights that cannot be obtained from single-reservoir studies [82].

The Wujiang cascade reservoirs release GHGs and act as a carbon source to the atmosphere, while simultaneously receiving substantial carbon inputs from the catchment area, leading to storage of terrigenous carbon in sediments. The balance between these processes — carbon emissions versus terrigenous carbon storage — has become a critical factor in determining whether the Wujiang cascade reservoirs function as a carbon source or a carbon sink [78]. However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have explicitly evaluated the carbon balance of cascade reservoir systems by jointly considering GHG emissions and terrigenous carbon storage in the sediment. Existing studies have largely treated reservoirs as isolated units and emphasized emission processes [26], thereby overlooking the potential role of sediment-associated carbon sequestration in determining net carbon outcomes.

To address this knowledge gap, this study conceptualizes reservoir systems as coupled carbon source–storage systems and advances integrated carbon accounting in human-regulated aquatic environments [41]. The main research aims are: (1) to estimate carbon emissions in the form of carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane (CH4) from seven cascade reservoirs in the upper (Group 1), middle (Group 2), and lower (Group 3) reaches of the Wujiang River Basin; (2) to quantify the storage of terrigenous carbon for each reservoir based on sediment retention, deposition characteristics, and water impoundment plans; and (3) to assess the carbon budget of each reservoir group. These findings are expected to improve the accuracy of carbon budget assessments for hydropower systems and provide a scientific basis for carbon management and climate mitigation strategies in reservoir-regulated river basins.

Material and methods

Study site description

The Wujiang River, one of the major tributaries of the Yangtze River, flows through Chongqing City and Guizhou Province. The Wujiang River Basin (WRB) spans an area of approximately 87,920 km2 [92]. Notably, 75.6% of the WRB is characterized by extensive karst landforms formed within carbonate rocks, with elevations ranging from 81 to 3,052 m above sea level (Fig. 1). The basin is situated within a subtropical monsoon humid climate zone, with an average annual air temperature of 12.3 ℃. Air temperatures extremes range from 35.4 °C in summer to − 10.1°C in winter.

Distribution of seven hydroelectric power plants and four hydrological stations in Wujiang River Basin. HJD is Hongjiadu, PD is Puding, YZD is Yinzidu, DF is Dongfeng, WJD is Wujiangdu, GPT is Goupitan, and SL is Silin

Annual precipitation ranges from 1,100 and 1,300 mm, exhibiting significant seasonal variation driven by the influence of the southwest monsoon. Heavy rainfall and associated flooding during the wet season (May to October) account for approximately 75% of the total annual precipitation. This distinct hydrological regime significantly influences the basin’s hydrological and ecological processes, making it an important region for studying climate interactions and water resource management.

Development of hydroelectric power plants

The Wujiang River has an annual runoff of approximately 49.5 billion m3 with a total elevation drop of 2,124 m, making it a critical resource for China’s large-scale West-to-East Power Transmission Project [73].

The river supports seven cascade hydroelectric reservoirs, including Puding, Yinzidu, Hongjiadu, Dongfeng, Wujiangdu, Goupitan and Silin reservoirs (Fig. 1).

Based on geographic location and hydrological divisions, these reservoirs are classified into three groups (Fig. 2). The upper reach, Group 1, comprises the Puding, Yinzidu, Hongjiadu, and Dongfeng reservoirs, located upstream of the Yachihe hydrological station. The Wujiangdu reservoir represents the middle reach (Group 2), defined by the Wujiangdu hydrological station, while the Goupitan and Silin reservoirs from the lower reach (Group 3), with the Sinan hydrological station making the downstream boundary. This study focuses on the different characteristics of these three reservoir groups in terms of GHGs emissions and sediment sequestration. The cascade hydroelectric power plants were implemented from the 1980s to the 2010s. Watershed surface conditions and sediment transport

In the WRB, the predominant soil types include yellow soil, yellow–brown soil, lime soil, and purple soil [111]. Due to its steep mountainous terrain, fragmented topography, intensive weathering, erosion, slope farming, and other anthropogenic activities, the WRB contributes substantial sediment loads to the Yangtze River. The upper reaches of the Wujiang River exhibit forest coverage of less than 12% and sloping farmland reclamation rates of 64.2%–85.9% before 1980s [108]. Additionally, the extensively distributed purple sandstone shale, which lies beneath the yellow soil, is highly susceptible to weathering, making it the primary source of sediment to the reservoirs. Under intense rainfall and severe erosion, the erosion modulus in tributaries, such as the Sancha River and Liuchong River, reaches 694.71 t km−2 yr−1 and 811.53 t km−2 yr−1, respectively [109]. Since the 1970s, sediment transport in the WRB has increased significantly, with mean annual sediment loads at the Hongjiadu and Wujiadu monitoring sites rising by 59.6% and 50.1%, respectively, compared to pre- 1970 levels [108].

The construction of the Wujiadu hydroelectric power plant in 1970s marked the beginning of cascade hydroelectric power development along the Wujiang River mainstream. Reservoir impoundments have resulted in reduced flow velocities and gradual sediment deposition within the reservoir zones, leading to a progressive decline in sediment transport along the river’s main course.

As the outlet of the WRB, the Wulong hydrological station transported an average of 3.23 × 107 t yr−1 of sediment to the Yangtze River between 1956 and 1984 (Hydrological Data of Changjiang River Basin, 1956–1984). However, with the progressive construction of cascade reservoirs along the Wujiang River, sediment transport at the Wulong hydrological station declined markedly. Between 1985 to 2020, the mean annual sediment transport decreased to 1.15 × 107 t yr−1, less than half of the previous load. Following the completion of major hydroelectric power plants on the mainstream of the Wujiang River, sediment transport from the Wujiang River to the Yangtze River has remained relatively stable [66].

Data sources

Data, including reservoir — specific parameters, hydrological and meteorological information, are required by the G-res Tool to estimate CO2 and CH4 emissions [57, 59]. The reservoir — specific parameters, including surface area, construction year, storage capacity, and installed capacity, were obtained from relevant literature [20, 42, 46, 80, 83, 91, 102, 110]. The catchment area and land cover data (provided by Geospatial Data Cloud site, Computer Network Information Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences. (http://www.gscloud.cn)) for each reservoir were extracted using the spatial analyst tool in ArcGIS 10.2 (Esri, Redlands, CA USA), integrated with spatial datasets. Hydrological and meteorological data, including mean annual air temperature and wind speed at 10 m above ground level, were derived from monthly records spanning the period from 1980 to 2018 (China Meteorological Data Service Center, http://data.cma.cn/

). Runoff and sediment transport data originated from the Hydrological Yearbook [22], with the longest available dataset extending from 1955 to 2020, except for occasional data gaps. Global horizontal irradiance (GHR) data for the reservoirs were sourced from the NASA Surface Meteorology and Solar Energy (SSE) database [52]. Local soil properties were retrieved from the Soil Sub Center of the National Earth System Science Data Center, part of the National Science and Technology Infrastructure of China (http://soil.geodata.cn).

Data analysis

Overall, carbon emissions from seven reservoirs in the WRB were estimated using the G-res Tool, while terrigenous carbon storage within these reservoirs was calculated based on variations in sediment discharge.

CO2 and CH4 emissions

Conducted by the UNESCO Chair in Global Environmental Change and the International Hydropower Association, the G-res Tool provides a reliable approach for estimating and mapping GHG emissions from reservoirs [57, 59]. Following the recommendations of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [24], the tool calculates the “net GHG emission” associated with reservoir creation, offering a comprehensive framework for evaluating the environmental impacts of hydroelectric power. This tool compiles CO2 and CH4 emission measurement data from 223 reservoirs across multiple countries and regions worldwide, establishing an empirical methodology for estimating reservoir greenhouse gas emissions [58].

G-res Tool can calculate the flux of CO2 and CH4 emitted through surface diffusion, as well as CH4 bubbling and degassing emissions, to estimate reservoir GHG emissions. The calculation of post-impoundment CO2 diffusive emissions is based on reservoir age, mean annual temperature [23], modelled phosphorus concentration [79], reservoir area [54], and pre-inundation reservoir surface soil carbon content [3, 43]. In contrast, CH4 diffusive emissions are estimated using reservoir age, mean annual temperature, and percent of littoral area. The G-res Tool’s results indicate that as reservoir age — the dominant factor — increases, both CO2 and CH4 diffusive emissions exhibit a declining trend. Notably, CO2 emissions decrease more steeply compared to CH4 and stabilize more rapidly to a new equilibrium. A logarithmic equation of cumulative GHR [88], along with the percentage of the littoral zone, was found to be the most representative predictor of CH4 bubbling across the entire reservoir. Therefore, in G-res Tool, CH4 bubbling emissions are estimated by incorporating both littoral zone area and cumulative GHR. For degassing emissions, variations in water discharge pathways have differential impacts on CH4 emissions from the reservoirs. The G-res Tool identifies key factors affecting degassing emissions by comparing CH4 concentration differences between the upstream and downstream sections of the reservoir. A function of water residence time (WRT) and post-impoundment annual CH4 diffusive emission was as a proxy of CH4 production [57–59]. Notably, over a 100-year timescale, as defined by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), CH4 exhibits a global warming potential (GWP) 34 times greater than that of CO2 [10, 57, 59]. Therefore, the CH4 emission flux estimated using the G-res Tool was converted into CO2-equivalent emissions (CO2e) by applying the GWP factor.

The G-res Tool calculated CO2 and CH4 emissions from individual reservoirs, facilitating a comprehensive assessment of emissions throughout a reservoir’s lifetime.

Within the watershed, eroded soil from slopes is carried by water flow into the river channels and eventually reaches the reservoir area. As the dam intercepts the river and the flow velocity decreases, sediment carried by the river is deposited within the reservoir, a process that is particularly pronounced during the initial impoundment period [25, 31]. The rapid and substantial deposition of terrigenous carbon results in its large burial within reservoir sediments, enhancing its preservation potential. Therefore, the contribution of this external carbon source should be incorporated into the assessment of the reservoir’s carbon balance [50].

In the WRB, the organic carbon in reservoir sediments primarily originates from terrigenous sources [100]. The soil organic carbon content in this region ranges from 0.3% to 4.6% [111]. Owing to its high preservation potential and resistance to decomposition, coupled with sediment retention ratios ranging from 38.46% to 64.67% (mean: 49.5%) [60], the retention of terrigenous organic carbon is substantially affected. For each gram of sediment transported and retained in the reservoir, approximately 0.0015–0.023 g of terrigenous organic carbon is also preserved. For the subsequent calculations, an average carbon–to–sediment ratio of 1.2% was adopted [62].

Results

CO2 and CH4 emissions from three groups of cascade reservoirs

The annual mean CO2 emission fluxes of three reservoir groups in the WRB were of 75 g CO2 m−2 yr−1 (Group 1), 96 g CO2 m−2 yr−1 (Group 2) and 108 g CO2 m−2 yr−1 (Group 3), respectively (Fig. 3). Across individual reservoirs, the annual mean CO2 flux ranged from 63 to 124 g CO2 m−2 yr−1.

Within each group, the figure presents CH4 fluxes associated with three emission pathways — diffusion, bubbling and degassing — as well as the diffusive flux of CO2

The annual mean CH4 emission fluxes of the three reservoir groups were estimated at 63 g CO2e m−2 yr−1 (Group 1), 76 g CO2e m−2 yr−1 (Group 2), and 87 g CO2e m−2 yr−1 (Group 3), respectively. Diffusive fluxes constituted the dominant pathway of CH4 emissions from reservoirs (61.39%), whereas bubbling fluxes represented small proportion (Fig. 3).

The annual total GHG emission fluxes were 22 g C m−2 yr−1 (Group 1), 28 g C m−2 yr−1 (Group 2), and 31 (Group 3) g C m−2 yr−1, respectively. Among them, the Group 3 — comprising Goupitan and Silin Reservoirs located in the downstream region — exhibited the highest carbon emission fluxes, ranging from 21 to 36 g C m−2 yr−1. In contrast, the Group 1 — consisting of Puding, Yinzidu, Hongjiadu, and Dongfeng Reservoirs located in the upstream — had the lowest carbon fluxes, ranging from 19 to 24 g C m−2 yr−1.

In all three reservoir groups, annual mean CO2 emissions accounted for more than half of the total GHG emissions, slightly exceeding the contribution from CH4 emissions.

Sediment trapped in reservoirs

At Yachihe Hydrological Station, a significant reduction in sediment transport was observed starting from1994 (Fig. 4a). By comparing the observed and predicted values using the double accumulation curve method, it was estimated that the sediment transport decreased by 78.19 × 106 t between 1995 and 2000, representing an approximate 97.0% compared to the pre- 1994 period. Thereafter, sediment transport continued to decline, and between 2004 and 2018, nearly clear water was discharged downstream from Yachihe Station. A similar trend was observed at Wujiangdu Hydrological Station, where the mean annual sediment transport decreased by 15.38 × 106 t yr−1 between 1980 and 2017, representing a 98.2% reduction compared to pre-1980 levels. Located in the middle reaches of the Wujiang River, Sinan Hydrological Station primarily receives water and sediment inputs from upstream reservoirs and intermediate catchment areas. Following the large-scale development of hydroelectric power plants in the middle and lower reaches of the Wujiang River after 2000, sediment interception intensified further. Between 2007 and 2014, the estimated sediment reduction at Sinan Station was 15.99 × 106 t, representing an 69.9% reduction compared to pre-development levels.

Upstream of the Yachihe hydrological station, Group 1 — comprising four reservoirs — played a dominant role in reducing sediment transport, effectively intercepting upstream sediment input. The Wujiangdu hydrological station, situated downstream, marks the boundary between Group 2 and Group 3. The observed sediment reduction at Wujiangdu hydrological station was largely attributed to the sediment trapping effect of the Wujiangdu Reservoir, the sole component of Group 2. Similarly, the sediment decrease observed at the Sinan hydrological station resulted from both reduced upstream sediment supply and the sediment retention capacity of the reservoirs in Group 3. Based on the construction timeline of the reservoirs (Fig. 2), sediment reduction at the Yachihe hydrological station between 1995 and 2000 was primarily due to sediment trapping by the Puding and Dongfeng Reservoirs in Group 1, with an estimated reduction of approximately 78.19 × 106 t. After 2004, nearly all upstream sediment from the Yachihe basin was intercepted by Group 1 reservoirs, amounting to around 113.36 × 106 t till 2014.

The Wujiangdu Reservoir, as the sole member of Group 2, intercepted sediment inflow from the Yachihe basin between 1980 and 1985, with an estimated retention of 82.35 × 106 t. In addition, following the completion of the cascade reservoirs in Group 1, both Groups 1 and 2 jointly trapped most of the sediment delivered from the upstream basin after 1995.

Group 3, comprising the Silin and Goupitan Reservoirs, began construction after 2003 and retained approximately 15.99 × 106 t of sediment by 2014.

By this time, all reservoirs in Group 1 had been in impoundment for nearly a decade, and GHG emissions from these reservoirs had also become relatively stable.

Discussion

Contribution of CH4 and CO2 to reservoir GHG emissions

Following inundation, the decomposition of submerged plant material within the first three years results in substantial emissions of CH4 and CO2 [19, 96]. In this study, all three reservoir groups have been inundated for over a decade, reaching a stable state of GHG emissions. The GHG emissions from the three reservoir groups ranged from 22 to 31 g C m−2 yr−1, which are much lower than the global average GHG exchange rate of reservoirs (more details are in Appendix A).

The factors influencing reservoir GHG emissions are multifaceted, encompassing climatic conditions, underlying surface characteristics, and land cover. Among these, CH4, a key GHG emitted from reservoirs, is affected by various environmental parameters, including temperature [36], wind speed [17] and water depth [107]. The production of CH4 is primarily driven by the anaerobic microbial decomposition of organic matter, with temperature playing a critical role, as methanogenic bacteria are highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations. The optimal temperature for CH4 production is approximately 25°C. The three reservoir groups, located in a subtropical region, experience an annual average water temperature ranging from 13.5°C to 21.2 °C, which is below the optimal temperature for methanogenesis [18]. This suboptimal thermal condition may constrain CH4 production rates in these reservoirs. Consequently, the CH₄ emission flux from the three reservoir groups ranged between 4.27 and 8.14 mg m−2 d−1, markedly lower than those of tropical reservoirs [69]. It has been noted that CH4 production in tropical sediments is at least 2.8 to 2.9 times higher than that in northern sediments, primarily due to temperature effects [48].

Additionally, wind speed is another critical climatic factor influencing GHG emissions through diffusion. Specifically, wind enhances gas exchange by promoting the release of dissolved gases from the water column, particularly when wind speeds exceed 3.7 m s−1 [2]. However, the mean annual wind speed across the three reservoir groups remained relatively stable (1.6 m s−1, ranging from 1.13 to 2.4 m s−1), well below this threshold. This relatively low wind speed may explain the limited variability in GHG diffusion emissions in this study.

The average water depth of these cascade reservoirs is approximately 45 m, increasing the likelihood of CH4 being either dissolved or oxidized before reaching the atmosphere. This process likely accounts for the relatively stable and low CH4 emission fluxes observed in the WRB.

Because all water intakes are located below the thermocline, CH4 degassing emissions are influenced by multiple factors, including intake depth, water residence time, annual runoff, reservoir surface area, and catchment area (Prairie et al., 2022). As shown in Table 3, the highest CH4 degassing emissions were observed in Group 3 reservoirs, which are characterized by a shorter impoundment history and a larger reservoir area.

These findings highlight that, in addition to climatic conditions [17], pre-impoundment surface conditions in reservoir inundation zones can significantly influence post-impoundment GHG emissions [97]. For instance, the implementation of extensive pre-impoundment clearing activities in the three reservoir groups contributed to the reduced post-impoundment rise in GHG emissions, which were only 1.2–2.7 times higher than pre-impoundment levels. These activities included the removal of vegetation (e.g., trees and shrubs), demolition of buildings and structures, clearance of solid waste, and disinfection and sterilization [37]. Consequently, the annual mean GHG emissions in the three groups of reservoirs (2,231–19,774 t CO2e yr−1) were substantially lower than those in tropical and subtropical reservoirs with fertile soils and dense vegetation (1.05 × 104–1.5 × 107 t CO2e yr−1) [64, 65, 72] as well as temperate reservoirs (2.4 × 107–2.33 × 108 t CO2e yr.−1) [1, 9].

Compared with the field observation of CO2 fluxes reported by Wang et al. [85], mean values simulated by the G-res Tool (22, 28, 31) fall within the observed range (− 113.47–556.82), (− 130.27–1071.00), (− 123.81–2665.19). Assuming a uniform distribution of observations, the standardized deviation (Z ≈ − 1.03), (Z ≈ − 1.27), and (Z ≈ − 1.54) remain well within the acceptable statistical range (|Z|< 2), indicating that the model results are broadly consistent with the observations within the uncertainty bounds. Among the three reservoir groups, substantial heterogeneity in GHG emissions was observed, likely driven by variations in reservoir characteristics, particularly age and surface area. Given that key environmental parameters influencing GHG production — such as air temperature, soil type, land cover, and wind speed — are relatively consistent across the reservoirs, these specific differences likely play a dominant role in shaping GHG emission patterns [29]. Notably, the youngest reservoir, Goupitan, was impounded in 2011 and has the largest surface area (96.46 km2) exhibiting the largest annual GHG emission nearly eight times that of the Dongfeng reservoir, which has a much smaller surface area (19.1 km2). As the only multi-year regulated reservoir among the seven, the Hongjiadu reservoir has the longest water retention period of approximately 369 days. Its annual GHG emissions are estimated to reach 1,785 t C yr−1, making it the second-highest emitter among the seven reservoirs, though only half of the emissions from the Goupitan reservoir.

Beyond air temperature, water depth, land cover, impoundment area and reservoir age, other factors such as water quality, carbon input, and the unique geomorphological characteristics of the region, including deep canyons and steep slopes, also exert significant influence on CO2 and CH4 emissions. Hydroelectric power plants are typically constructed within deep valleys, leveraging the natural elevation drop to maximize the potential energy of impounded water [105]. The average slope of the Wujiang River is approximately 1.02‰, with the steepest section in the Group 1 Yachihe segment reaching 2‰. Such steep topographic features not only enhance hydroelectric power generation efficiency but may also affect carbon flux dynamics within the reservoir system, influencing the retention and transformation of carbon within these aquatic environments [67].

Sediment retention and terrigenous carbon storage in cascade reservoirs

The sequential construction of cascade reservoirs along the Wujiang River has made sediment retention the primary factor contributing to the decline in sediment transport within the basin [4]. A significant portion of sediment is trapped and stored in deep canyon reservoirs, where it accumulates over time [31]. Between 1995 and 2000, only two reservoirs — Dongfeng (completed in 1995) and Puding (completed in 1995) — were operational in Group 1. In the absence of significant slope activity during this period, sediment interception by these two reservoirs was identified as the primary driver of the observed decline in sediment transport at the Yachihe hydrometric station. A sediment transport prediction, based on the double accumulation curve of runoff and sediment discharge at the Yachihe hydrological station, estimated that total sediment retention from 1995 to 2000 amounted to 78.19 × 106 t, with a mean annual interception rate of 15.64 × 106 t yr−1. This estimate closely aligns with measured sediment accumulation in the two reservoirs, which averaged 15.5 × 106 t yr−1 [66].

Furthermore, in Group 2, only the Wujiangdu Hydroelectric Station — the first hydroelectric power plant constructed in the WRB — exhibits distinct sediment deposition characteristics. Due to low flow velocity, limited sediment transport capacity, and prolonged water retention, sediment accumulates in the perennial backwater area and gradually migrates toward the dam as reservoir operations continue [14].

The variation in sediment transport recorded at the Wujiangdu Hydrological Station, located downstream of the dam, directly reflects the sediment retention by Wujiangdu reservoir and its upstream reservoirs. Calculations indicate that Wujiangdu retained an average of 16.47 × 106 t yr−1 from 1980 to 1985. Following the construction of Dongfeng and Puding reservoirs in Group 1, the combined sediment retention of reservoirs from Groups 1 and 2 (Dongfeng, Puding, and Wujiangdu) reached 15.72 × 106 t yr−1 between 1995 and 2000. This estimate is consistent with the reported values [66], highlighting the uneven distribution of sediment among reservoirs. Notably, sediment retention varies significantly depending on the construction timeline and each reservoir’s position within the upstream–midstream–downstream system [61].

Since a portion of soil carbon is transported and deposited alongside sediment, carbon balance assessments in reservoirs must account for not only GHG emissions but also the influx and long-term sequestration of terrigenous carbon [98]. As shown in Table 2, the upper reaches of the Yachihe River serve as a major sediment source within the WRB. As the first reservoir in the basin, Wujiangdu exhibits an exceptionally high terrigenous organic carbon deposition rate of 4,135 g C m−2 yr−1, significantly exceeding those of most of reservoirs worldwide (more details are in Appendix A). This pattern persisted until the Group 1 reservoirs were constructed in the primary sediment-producing area. However, with enhanced sediment retention by upstream reservoirs and the implementation of soil and water conservation measures in key sediment-producing areas [75, 93], organic carbon deposition rates in reservoirs Group 3 decreased significantly to 203 g C m−2 yr−1 (Fig. 5), approaching the lower end of the typical range for Asian reservoirs [51].

Carbon balance in reservoirs: greenhouse gas emissions versus sediment terrigenous carbon storage

Unlike terrestrial ecosystems, aquatic ecosystems play a distinct dual role, acting both as sources of carbon emissions to the atmospheric and as long-term terrigenous carbon storage through sediment accumulation. Reservoirs could receive substantial inputs of carbon from their catchment areas and sequester a portion of this carbon in their sediments (e.g., [5, 13, 97]). This process is considered as a partial offset to carbon emissions from reservoirs [11, 56]. Based on the calculations of GHG emissions from these three groups of reservoirs in the Wujiang River Basin, the carbon flux released in the form of CH4 and CO2 ranged from 22 g C m−2 yr−1 to 31 g C m−2 yr−1 (Table 4 and Fig. 6).

The carbon balance of reservoirs in the current research and published studies

The overall carbon balance of reservoirs, whether they act as carbon sources or storages, can be assessed by evaluating both GHG emissions and terrigenous carbon storage in reservoir sediments [54]. Comparative analysis reveals that GHG emissions from Group 2 are 28 g C m−2 yr−1, which is significantly lower than the early stage of terrigenous carbon flux sequestered through sediment deposition (4,135 g C m−2 yr−1). A similar pattern was observed in the Three Gorges Reservoir, where GHG emissions varied between 119 and 539 g C m−2 yr−1 [33, 35, 37, 38], whereas terrigenous organic carbon deposition was as high as 5,899 g C m−2 yr−1 [27, 32]. Although the terrigenous carbon deposition flux in Group 3 (203 g C m−2 yr−1) was lower than that in upstream reservoirs, it still exceeded the annual average carbon emission flux of itself (31 g C m−2 yr−1). As the Group 3 reservoir is situated downstream of the Yachihe Basin — the primary sediment source in the upper reaches of the Wujiang River — the sediment trapping effect of the upstream reservoir groups is more pronounced than that of the Group 3 reservoirs. Although the estimated terrigenous carbon storage capacities of the Dongfeng, Puding, and Wujiangdu reservoirs in the first five years after impoundment far exceeded the reported average for freshwater reservoirs (400 g C m−2 yr−1, [7]), they still fell within the range of high accumulation rates reported for many reservoirs (148–17,000 g C m−2 yr−1) [13, 76]. As shown in Table 4, carbon buried flux exceeded carbon emission flux in nearly all reservoirs, albeit to varying extents, except for those located in tropical regions of Brazil. Given that many countries, including China, have committed to achieving net-zero carbon emissions by 2050 or 2060 (e.g., [95]), it is important to accurately assess the carbon function of reservoirs. These findings indicate that, when considering the cascade reservoirs on the Wujiang River as an integrated system, the terrigenous carbon storage exerts a dominant influence on the overall carbon balance of the reservoirs, outweighing carbon emissions.

Limitations and future research

Similar to most studies, there are some limitations in the current study. First, comprehensive spatiotemporal sampling data were still unavailable for all seven reservoirs across the three groups, so GHG emissions were calculated using G-res Tool. Although the results met the statistical criterion of |Z|< 2 when compared with measured data reported in the literature [85], suggesting that these calculated values to multiple cascaded reservoirs might still introduce uncertainty in the emission estimates. Second, the proportions of organic carbon deposition in sediments were derived from literature rather than direct sampling. The use of a fixed organic carbon content may introduce uncertainties into the estimation of reservoir carbon storage, thereby compromising the representativeness of the study results. Furthermore, the historical sediment transport data were obtained from hydrological yearbooks. However, due to discontinuous measurement periods, sediment transport records are unavailable for certain years. These data gaps introduce uncertainties into the calculation of cumulative sediment retention. Finally, assessing the reservoir carbon balance requires long-term monitoring, particularly considering the transition from a non-equilibrium to a stable state in GHG emissions and sediment trapping. Future research should focus on quantifying reservoir carbon dynamics during this transitional phase to enhance the understanding of long-term carbon budgets in cascade reservoir systems.

Conclusions

The comparative analysis of GHG fluxes and sedimentary carbon burial across three groups of cascade reservoirs in the upper, middle, and lower reaches of the WRB reveals that, under comparable climatic and geographical conditions, reservoir-specific characteristics lead to divergent GHG emissions. The largest and youngest reservoir (Goupitan) exhibits the highest flux (205 g CO2e m−2 yr−1), while Hongjiadu — the only multi-year regulated reservoir (water retention approximately 369 days) ranks the second with an average annual emission of 1,784 t C yr−1 over its operational lifetime. The sediment-trapping effect intensifies with sequential cascade construction, especially downstream of key sediment-producing areas such as the Yachihe Basin, where terrigenous carbon storage rates across the three groups range from 319 to 7,468 g C m−2 yr−1 (total terrigenous carbon storage: 43,020–999,403 t C yr−1), exhibiting significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity and a progressive decrease from upstream to downstream and from older to younger reservoirs. Importantly, by integrating GHG emissions and sedimentary carbon sequestration across a cascade system, this study provides a system-scale assessment of reservoir carbon dynamics beyond the conventional single-reservoir perspective. Terrigenous carbon storage exceeds GHG emissions, indicating that the system functions as a net carbon storage. These results underscore the importance of sediment processes and coupled source–storage interactions in regulating carbon balance, and improve carbon budget estimation in hydropower systems. More broadly, sediment-rich cascade reservoirs may represent an underappreciated pathway for regional carbon sequestration.
