Phenological growth phases of dwarf apple trees: coding and description based on the BBCH scale
Authors: Yinkai Zhang, Qifeng Wu, Dongxue Lv, Lang Xin, Muhammad Masood Nabi, Lei Zhang, Zhenxi Cao, Xingpeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe BBCH scale (Biologische Bundesanstalt, Bundessortenamt und Chemieindustrie) is a fundamental tool for standardizing plant phenological observations. As apple production shifts towards high-density dwarf orchard systems to enhance yield and facilitate mechanization, a significant gap exists in a unified phenological description framework for dwarfing rootstock apple trees, particularly under desert climate conditions. This study aims to systematically characterize the phenological growth stages of dwarf-rootstock apple using an extended BBCH scale to support precision orchard management.ResultIn our study, phenological monitoring was carried out systematically through the utilization of optical observation equipment and manual observations throughout the entire growth cycles. The extended BBCH scale with a three-digit coding system was applied, where the first digit indicates the principal growth stage (0–9), the second digit represents a mesostage (1 for spring, 2 for autumn growth), and the third digit describes secondary stages (0–9). We described and illustrated eight main growth stages: bud development (stage 0), leaf development (stage 1), shoot development (stage 3), inflorescence emergence (stage 5), flowering (stage 6), fruit development (stage 7), fruit ripening period (stage 8), and senescence and beginning of dormancy (stage 9). A total of 43 secondary stages were defined. Crucially, mesostages were introduced to differentiate the distinct spring and autumn vegetative growth flushes characteristic of the bimodal growth pattern observed under desert conditions.ConclusionsThe developed three-digit BBCH scale offers a standardized and refined framework for monitoring apple phenology in high-density dwarf orchards. It serves as a vital tool for optimizing the timing of key agronomic practices like irrigation, fertilization, pruning, and pest control, thereby supporting the intelligent implementation of precision agriculture. This framework effectively standardizes phenological observations across diverse environments, laying a foundation for improved yield, fruit quality, and sustainable orchard management.Keywords: Apple (Malus domestica Borkh); BBCH, Phenology, Phenological stage

Introduction

Apple (Malus domestica Borkh) belongs to the genus Malus within the subfamily Maloideae of the Rosaceae family and ranks as the third most-produced fruit worldwide, playing a pivotal role in global fruit production and the agricultural economy [1]. The genus Malus comprises approximately 35 species distributed mainly across Asia, Europe, and North America [2]; to date, more than 3,000 cultivars of domesticated apple have been developed and are cultivated worldwide. Chinese apple germplasm traces largely to Malus sieversii and shares ancestry with domesticated apples; however, divergent ecological and geographic conditions have driven distinct morphological traits and phenotypic variation [3]. Since the introduction of cultivated apple varieties into China in 1871, they have progressively replaced some traditional Chinese apple cultivars over more than a century of cultivation and selection. According to FAO (www.fao.org), global apple production in 2023 is estimated to reach 97.34 million tonnes, of which China accounts for 49.6 mil tons (approximately 51%), making it the largest apple producing country worldwide.

Apple trees possess the ability to adapt to a wide range of climatic conditions, thriving best in regions between 35° and 50° latitude north and south [3]. A mature apple tree can reach a height of 10–15 m [4], with a typically rounded canopy, a relatively short trunk, and grayish-brown bark that develops irregular longitudinal fissures or flaky peeling as the tree ages. Leaves are 4.5–10 cm long and are commonly elliptic, ovate, or broadly elliptic, with rounded, blunt teeth along the leaf margins [5, 6]. Flowers typically have five white petals with a pinkish flush, and each inflorescence usually bears 5–8 flowers [7]. The fruit is generally oblate, with a diameter typically > 2 cm (some varieties reaching over 7 cm). Both the bottom and top are often depressed, and fruit color and size vary considerably depending on the variety [8]. Apple buds are classified into leaf and floral buds; floral buds are mixed buds. Fruiting branches derived from mixed buds are commonly categorized as short fruit branch (< 5 cm), medium fruit branch (5–15 cm), and long fruit branch (> 15 cm) [9]. Due to its low self-pollination rate, it typically requires cross-pollination, meaning most varieties need different apple varieties pollinator trees to bear fruit properly [10]. With population growth, reduced arable land, and increasing market demand, production goals have shifted from pursuing high yields per tree to boosting yields per unit area. Consequently, high-density dwarf apple tree cultivation systems have gained significant interest in both academic and industrial sectors [11].

High-density dwarf cultivation systems is a key trend in modern fruit tree production and a basis for labor-saving cultivation. These apple trees are significantly smaller, typically 1/2 − 1/3 the size of trees on vigorous rootstocks. Fully-grown trees usually have a canopy diameter of 1.0–1.2 m, and planting density in dwarf orchards is generally 2–3 times higher than in arborizing orchards. This approach not only maximizes land use but also increases row spacing, facilitating mechanized management and enabling cultivar renewal [12]. The use of dwarfing rootstocks is the primary method for achieving dwarf apple cultivation, as these rootstocks effectively control tree crowns and are suitable for high-density planting. In 1917, the East Malling Research Station in the UK introduced the M series rootstocks, with M9 and M26 being widely adopted [13]. The Netherlands has further developed the M9T337 virus-free dwarfing rootstock, overcoming the low disease resistance of the M9 series [14]. Currently, dwarf apple orchards account for 60%-80% of orchards in Europe and the United States, achieving near-complete mechanization in cultivation. Measuring apple yields by unit area has become a reality [15]. However, the widely used phenological quantification standards for apple trees are primarily based on observations of arborized planting orchards [16]. These phenological phase classifications and growth cycle parameters fail to accurately reflect the altered growth rhythm, significantly earlier fruiting, and changes in high-density dwarf cultivation systems. As a result, they no longer fully meet the precision cultivation management needs of modern high-density dwarf cultivation orchards [17]. Therefore, this study aims to develop a phenological phase quantification and evaluation system tailored for high-density dwarf cultivation systems in apple orchards (BBCH scale), with the goal of providing a scientific basis for precision agriculture management and optimizing production practices.

The Biologische Bundesantalt, Bundessortenamt, and Chemische Industrie (BBCH)-scale originated in Germany in 1990 [18], with the aim of addressing the issue of multiple phenological classification systems, such as the Baggiolini scale [19] and the French Fleckinger scale [20], coexisting without standardized protocols. The core of the BBCH scale lies in its use of a decimal coding system, dividing the entire plant growth cycle into 10 principal stages (0–9), each further subdivided into 10 secondary growth stages (0–9) [21]. This structure enabled phenological descriptions to be standardized, refined, and quantified for the first time, significantly facilitating communication and computational processing in agriculture, horticulture, and research fields. With the evolution of the times, the application of the BBCH scale, initially limited to a few crops, has shown a rapid expansion trend. Currently, it has rapidly expanded beyond its initial coverage of perennial fruit trees, including stone fruits like apricot [22], mango [23], lychee [24] and longan [25]; berries like pomegranate [26], kiwifruit [27], mangosteen [28] and pome fruits like pear [29]. At the same time, the scale has evolved to accommodate different crop development forms, expanding from a two-digit to a three-digit coding system.

Today, combined with artificial intelligence, the expansion of the BBCH scale is advancing precision agriculture to a higher level by optimizing yield prediction, resource management, and other processes [30]. It not only provides a unified language for global plant growth monitoring, but also empowers farmers to develop more scientific cultivation strategies through data-driven decision support systems. This enhances resource utilization efficiency, reduces environmental impact, and promotes sustainable agricultural development while ensuring food security [31]. In light of this, this study adopts the expanded BBCH scale to define the phenological growth stages of dwarf rootstock apples, aiming to provide a valuable contribution to research on the cultivation of high-density dwarf cultivation systems for pomaceous fruit trees.

Methods

Experimental area

The orchard is located in Alaer City, Xinjiang, China (40°36′42″ N, 81°03′01″ E), at an elevation of 1,024 m (Fig. 1). Its geographic context at the national, regional, and local scales is shown in Fig. 1a, b, and c, respectively. According to the Köppen climate [32] classification, the region falls under the cold desert climate (BWk). During the experimental period, the annual mean temperature was 11℃, with a mean daily maximum temperature of 20℃, a mean daily minimum temperature of 4℃, a daily average relative humidity of 58% and an annual average precipitation of 42 mm. Meteorological data were sourced from a HOBO U30-NRC (Onset Computer Corporation, USA, Technical Support | Onset’s HOBO Data Loggers) automatic weather station located 30 m from the orchard experimental area.

Orchard area location map

Plant material

The apple variety selected for this study is ‘Yanfu No. 8’, with a tree age of six years. The trees were planted using the high-density dwarf cultivation system with M9T337 rootstock. The planting density was set at 3.5 m × 1 m, and the irrigation method was underground drip irrigation with fertigation. The fertilization amount was based on local orchard fertilization practices, with adaptive adjustments made to the nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium ratio according to the different growth stages of the trees.

During the various growth stages of the apple trees, optical observation equipment was pre-installed to monitor the development status of the trees. The equipment recorded video footage of the target trees at fixed preset observation points between 10:00–12:00 and 18:00–20:00 daily. During the flowering and budding periods, the equipment was activated daily for recording; at other times, recordings were made 1–3 times per week. Concurrently, the growth conditions of representative parts of the trees were identified and described, and representative photographs were taken. All photographs were taken using a Hikvision DS-2DE4423IW intelligent spherical camera (Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China, https://www.hikvision.com) and measurement data were collected using a Deli DL91150 caliper (Ningbo Deli Tools Co., Ltd, Ningbo, China, https://www.deli-tools.com).

The BBCH scale

Based on the physiological characteristics of apples, a three-digit extended BBCH coding system was selected. The first digit represents the main growth stage, the second digit denotes the mesostage between main and secondary growth, and the third digit indicates the secondary growth phase. The main growth stage coding encompasses 10 stages from 0 to 9. Given that fruit trees are perennial crops, eight stages were selected, as follows: starting with bud development (stage 0), followed by leaf development (stage 1), Shoot development (stage 3), inflorescence development (stage 5), flowering (stage 6), development of fruit (stage 7), maturity of fruit and seed (stage 8), and senescence and dormancy (stage 9). Tillering (stage 2) and development of harvestable vegetative plant parts (stage 4) were not recorded, as they do not align with the growth process of perennial fruit trees (Fig. 2).

The principal growth stages (PGS) of dwarf rootstock ‘Yanfu No. 8’ apple trees in Alaer City, Xinjiang, with monthly average temperatures per BBCH scale. The blue shaded area shows the monthly temperature range during 2024-2025

The growth of ‘Yanfu No. 8’ apples typically spans from April in spring to November in autumn, with two peak periods of vegetative growth [33]. To differentiate between various stages of the vegetative growth period, the academic community commonly adds several mesostages (1-n) between the main and secondary growth stages to describe the different growth phases within the growth period. In this study, only stages 1 and 2 were selected as mesostage codes. Mesostages 1 and 2 are specifically used to encode the growth processes of leaves and shoots, distinguishing the spring shoot growth phase (1) from the autumn shoot growth phase (2). The secondary growth stages coding further subdivides the main growth stages into 10 secondary growth phases, represented by digits 0–9 in the third position, to characterize the growth percentage of phases such as buds, leaves, shoots, inflorescence, flowering, fruit development, fruit maturity and senescence. For example, the value of 5 in main stage 3 (shoot development phase) corresponds to the growth of spring shoots reaching 50% of their final length, with the code 315. Similarly, the code 709 indicates stage 7 (fruit development phase) with a value of 9, meaning the fruit has reached approximately 90% of its final size.

Principal growth stage 0: bud development

Most apple cultivars produce late-budding buds, which do not sprout until the following year after they are formed. The development of vegetative buds typically occurs in two rounds. When the average day-night temperature in spring reaches around 10 °C, the first round of leaf buds ends dormancy and begins to swell. From mid-July to late August, some apical or bud-clip buds will sprout again on the basis of the spring shoots.

Principal growth stage 1: leaf development

About 80% of the leaves on mature apple trees are formed within a few days at the end of flowering, and these leaves develop from the leaf primordia of the previous year. As the buds begin to swell and grow, the newly formed leaf primordia subsequently develop into leaves, accounting for about 20%. These are post-formed leaves that differentiate as the new shoots continue to extend. The period from leaf unfolding to cessation of growth typically lasts 10–35 days.

Discussion

The BBCH scale is widely used to standardize observations of plant growth and development in agricultural and horticultural research and in studies of climate-driven phenological change. Here, we systematically characterize, for the first time, the phenological growth stages of ‘Yanfu No. 8’ apple grown in a high-density dwarf orchard using the extended BBCH scale (three-digit code). The advantages of the extended BBCH scale are summarized in Table 1. This work addresses the lack of a standardized phenological description for this cultivar and closely related apple germplasm.

Vegetative growth: Under desert climate conditions in Xinjiang (annual mean temperature of 11℃), apple vegetative growth exhibits a pronounced bimodal pattern, consisting of two growth periods: spring shoot growth and autumn shoot growth. Vegetative growth generally begins in April and progresses through bud development (stage 0), leaf development (stage 1), and shoot development (stage 3). During the spring shoot growth period (mesostage 1), buds begin to swell when the average daily temperature reaches approximately 10℃ (12℃ for the ‘Yanfu No. 8’ cultivar) [35]. Leaf buds require 10–35 days to progress from dormancy (010) to full leaf expansion (119), while shoot growth from initial development (311) to 90% of final length (319) typically takes about 8–10 weeks [36]. The autumn shoot growth period (mesostage 2) occurs from late July to early September, during which terminal buds or pruning-cut buds re-sprout, forming a second growth flush (320–329). This bimodal growth pattern is closely associated with the accumulation and allocation of photosynthetic assimilates. Approximately 80% of the total leaf area of mature trees is contributed by spring shoots, primarily derived from leaf primordia formed in the previous year, whereas autumn shoot leaves mainly support late-stage fruit development [37]. Accurate characterization of vegetative growth facilitates the optimization of pruning timing; for example, the end of spring shoot growth (319) represents a critical window for summer pruning to regulate canopy density [38].

Flowering development: Apple flower buds are mixed buds, predominantly terminal buds. Flowering development (stages 5 and 6) is closely associated with temperature [39]. Inflorescence development from dormancy (500) to petal appearance (509) requires approximately 3–4 weeks. The flowering phase (600–609) lasts 7–10 days, with each inflorescence producing 5–8 flowers. The central flower opens first, followed sequentially by the lateral flowers. The optimal pollination period occurs 3–5 days after individual flower opening [40]. Owing to the low self-pollination efficiency of apple, cross-pollination is required. In this study, the flowering process was refined based on the BBCH scale into stages such as 30% flower opening (603) and 50% flower opening (605), providing precise temporal windows for artificial pollination or honeybee introduction. For example, pollination conducted at stage 605 (full bloom) can significantly enhance fruit set [35].

Fruit development: Apple fruit is a false fruit developed from the ovary and the receptacle. Its growth follows a single “S”-shaped curve and can be divided into a cell division phase and a cell enlargement phase [41, 42]. The duration from petal fall (701) to full maturity (807) varies substantially among apple cultivars. Late-maturing cultivars typically require 170–180 days (e.g., ‘Yanfu No. 8’), whereas early-maturing cultivars such as ‘American No. 8’ and ‘Gala’ require approximately 95–110 days and 110–125 days, respectively [43]. Maturity indicators include the development of the characteristic red peel color and crisp, sweet flesh. The BBCH scale encodes fruit size as a percentage of the final size (e.g., 705 represents 50%), which facilitates the identification of critical periods for fertilization and irrigation. For example, during the fruit enlargement phase (707–709), an increased proportion of potassium fertilizer is recommended to improve fruit quality [44]. In addition, maturity-stage codes (e.g., 805, indicating advanced coloration) can be used to guide harvest timing, thereby avoiding premature harvesting that may result in insufficient fruit flavor.

Agronomic management applications: The BBCH scale provides a scientific basis for refined management of apple orchards under high-density dwarf cultivation systems. Based on orchard practices in Xinjiang in this study (M9T337 rootstock, subsurface drip irrigation, and fertigation), management measures can be further optimized. During leaf development (110–129), powdery mildew should be monitored and fungicides applied promptly when needed. Summer pruning should be conducted after the end of spring shoot growth (319) to limit excessive autumn shoot elongation. During dormancy (stage 9), winter pruning should be performed from leaf fall to spring bud break to maintain a canopy architecture with high light-use efficiency. The N-P-K supply should be adjusted according to developmental stage: nitrogen should be prioritized during bud development (010–019) to promote bud break, phosphorus increased during flowering (600–609) to support pollination, and potassium emphasized during fruit enlargement (707–709) [45]. Drip irrigation frequency can be adjusted according to the leaf development phase (110–129) to reduce water stress. Artificial pollination or the release of pollinating insects can be implemented at full bloom (605). Flower and fruit thinning should be initiated after stage 601 (10% flowering), prioritizing the retention of central flowers and central fruit to improve fruit uniformity. Fruit bagging can be performed before stage 705 (fruit reaches 50% of final size), which helps balance disease suppression, coloration improvement, and insect control. The timing of bag removal should be determined based on cultivar coloration characteristics and local climatic conditions. For example, for ‘Yanfu No. 8’, bag removal 15–20 days before harvest (805–807) is recommended in cool regions or areas with large diurnal temperature ranges, whereas in maritime-climate production regions, bag removal 25–30 days before harvest is suggested [46].

Limitations: Although the BBCH scale provides a standardized framework for describing apple phenology, several limitations persist. In particular, during early vegetative stages of bud development (010–013) and early fruit development (701–705), code assignment often relies on visual estimation of developmental progress. These estimates are sensitive to environmental variability (e.g., temperature fluctuations), which can increase observer uncertainty and reduce staging consistency. Furthermore, the scale does not capture developmental dynamics of belowground organs (e.g., root growth). In addition, the use of calendar days and simple temperature thresholds is a limitation of the current study, and that the integration of metrics like chilling accumulation and Growing Degree Days (GDD) for a more physiologically robust and transferable model is a key objective for future research. Moreover, the scale does not explicitly account for climate-driven shifts in phenology; for instance, warm winters may disrupt dormancy progression, requiring dynamic interpretation supported by local meteorological data.

The present BBCH scale was established based on systematic observations of the specific apple cultivar ‘Yanfu 8’. It is fully acknowledged that validation across a wider range of cultivars (including other Fuji strains, ‘Gala’ and ‘Honeycrisp’) and under different cultivation regimes is essential for a standardized tool to achieve broad applicability. Therefore, this study is intended to serve as a foundational and open framework for such future work. Future work could integrate remote sensing and sensor monitoring to improve the timeliness and objectivity of BBCH scale-based phenological assessments.

Conclusion

Using the extended BBCH scale, this study systematically characterized the phenological phases of apples cultivated under high-density dwarf cultivation systems, laying a foundation for standardized and intelligent orchard management. The application of this scale can support the optimization of irrigation and fertilization, pollination management, and pest and disease control, ultimately improving yield per unit area and fruit quality. It may also reduce indiscriminate pesticide and fertilizer inputs, delivering both economic and ecological benefits. In the future, integrating the BBCH scale with artificial intelligence and remote sensing could enable automated monitoring of apple phenology and provide a standardized reference framework for orchard management and research across the global apple industry.
