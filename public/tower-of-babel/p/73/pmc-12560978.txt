Gastrointestinal Hemorrhage in Patients Receiving Antithrombotic Therapy: Clinical Patterns and Prognosis from Two Tertiary Care Hospitals
Authors: Hamdan S. AlGhamdi, Mohammed A. Koshan, Nasser F. AlOtaibi, Fahad M. AlOtaibi, Faisal F. AlSubaie, Hussain A. Ekhuraidah, Rayan H. Al Zahrani, Abed A. AlLehibi, Najd S. AlGazlan, Abdulrahman A. Aljumah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To evaluate clinical patterns and prognosis of gastrointestinal hemorrhage (GIH) in patients on antithrombotic therapy (AT).Materials and Methods:This retrospective study included patients hospitalized with GIH between January 2010 and January 2020 at two tertiary care centers in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Demographics, clinical/laboratory/endoscopic findings, and outcomes were analyzed.Results:The study included 759 GIH patients: 354 AT users and 405 non-users. AT users were older (69.2 ± 13.9 vs 59.7 ± 17.1 years; P < 0.0001) with more cardiovascular comorbidities: hypertension (72.0% vs 38.5%), diabetes (63.3% vs 31.4%), ischemic heart disease (28.0% vs 4.7%), and stroke (15.0% vs 3.0%) (for all, P < 0.0001). Endoscopy showed AT users had higher erosive gastritis (19.2% vs 10.4%; P = 0.001) and colonic ulcers (9.9% vs 5.7%; P = 0.029), while non-users had more esophageal varices (24.4% vs 12.4%; P < 0.0001) and hemorrhoids (25.9% vs 16.4%; P = 0.001). AT users required more transfusions (1.43 ± 2.5 vs. 0.95 ± 1.8 units; P = 0.005), had lower hemoglobin (98.9 ± 29.1 vs. 105.7 ± 30.9 g/L; P = 0.002), higher creatinine (137.89 ± 138.5 vs. 111.3 ± 136.3 µmol/L; P = 0.011), more ICU admissions (28.5% vs. 19.0%; P = 0.002), and higher mortality (15.8% vs. 8.9%; P = 0.042). Independent predictors of mortality were AT (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.067, 95% CI: 1.102–3.879; P = 0.024), alongside liver cirrhosis (aOR: 2.573, 95% CI: 1.26–5.252; P = 0.009), malignancy (aOR: 2.836, 95% CI: 1.481–5.431; P = 0.002), transfusions (aOR: 1.246, 95% CI: 1.141–1.361; P < 0.001), and serum urea (aOR: 1.037; 95% CI: 1.017–1.057; P < 0.001).Conclusion:Patients on antithrombotic therapy with gastrointestinal hemorrhage present with distinct risk profiles, severe anemia/renal impairment, higher resource utilization, and significantly increased mortality. These high-risk patients require careful management to improve their clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with atrial fibrillation or atherosclerosis in the coronary or peripheral arteries are at a greater risk of cardiovascular events. To mitigate this risk, antithrombotic therapy (AT) is recommended, as it has been shown to significantly reduce the likelihood of major adverse cardiovascular events and major adverse limb events.[1] However, AT can pose a risk of severe bleeding, particularly in the form of intracerebral or gastrointestinal hemorrhage (GIH), despite its potential to reduce the risk of life-threatening events.[2] Individuals receiving AT who develop GIH may face severe, potentially life-threatening complications, such as hemorrhagic shock, acute coronary syndromes, or ischemic strokes resulting from emergency AT reversal or therapy interruption, multiorgan failure secondary to prolonged shock, and complications from emergency interventions (e.g., endoscopy-related perforation or transfusion reactions).[3]

The management of acute GIH often necessitates the discontinuation of AT. However, given the patients’ existing medical conditions, discontinuing these medications could significantly increase the risk of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular consequences, which could lead to severe disability or death.[4] AT increases the relative risk of upper GIH (UGIH) by up to 10% in patients, and the yearly risk ranges from 1.5% to 4.5%.[5] Additionally, the risk of GIH increases dramatically when these medications are administered in combination or continued during GIH attacks. For example, warfarin combined with a low-dose aspirin (ASA) regimen is associated with a greater incidence of GIH than either regimen alone.[6]

After GI bleeding (GIB) ceases, AT may be restarted within 3–7 days.[7] According to the current literature, discontinuing anticoagulants in individuals taking AT significantly increases the risk of thromboembolic events and even death.[8]

There is a scarcity of locally published data regarding the impact of AT therapy on GIB. One early study by Alam et al.[9] investigated 564 patients admitted with acute UGIB between May 1996 and April 1998. The authors reported that esophageal varices and duodenal ulcers were the predominant causes of UGIB, with an overall mortality rate of 15.8%. A subsequent study by Alruzug et al.[10] examined the distribution of variceal and non-variceal UGIB as well as temporal trends in endoscopic findings over a 13-year period and found that the most frequent endoscopic diagnosis was gastroduodenal erosions, followed by duodenal ulcers, reflux esophagitis, esophageal varices, and gastric ulcers. Importantly, no significant temporal changes in the distribution of endoscopic findings were observed. In another retrospective study, Almadi et al.[11] analyzed 259 patients with UGIB in Riyadh and reported that non-variceal sources accounted for 80.1% of cases, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 4.4%. Similarly, Fallatah et al.[12] evaluated 125 cirrhotic patients and demonstrated a high long-term mortality rate of 65%. Notably, none of these studies specifically examined the association between GIB, antithrombotic therapy, and mortality. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the clinical and endoscopic characteristics of Saudi patients hospitalized with GIH while receiving AT therapy in tertiary centers, and to evaluate the impact of AT on mortality outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design, settings, and patients

This retrospective study included all adult Saudi patients (aged ≥18 years) hospitalized with GIH between January 2010 and January 2020 at two tertiary care centers (King Abdulaziz Medical City, National Guard Health Affairs, and King Fahad Medical City, Ministry of Health) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Patients were included if they presented with symptoms of overt GIH, such as hematemesis (bright red or coffee grounds), melena, or hematochezia (fresh red rectal bleeding or maroon-colored stool), and underwent upper endoscopy or colonoscopy. Patients who were aged <18 years, had missing endoscopy reports, or missing AT information were excluded. Patients with obscure overt GIH were excluded from the study. Patients who presented with overt GIH and had normal endoscopic findings were also excluded.

Data collection

We collected data on demographics, vital signs, medical history, clinical presentation, comorbidities, AT use (such as antiplatelets [low-dose ASA or clopidogrel] or anticoagulants [enoxaparin, warfarin, or apixaban]), baseline laboratory investigations (blood glucose, lipid profile, complete blood count, blood chemistry tests, and coagulation tests), endoscopic findings (esophagogastroduodenoscopy [EGD] and colonoscopy), and mortality. The data were gathered into a case record form, transferred to an Excel spreadsheet, and then to statistical software for the final analysis.

Definitions and endpoints

AT is defined as the use of pharmacological agents to prevent or treat thrombotic events, including antiplatelet agents (low-dose ASA or clopidogrel) and anticoagulants (heparins, warfarin, or direct-acting anticoagulants such as apixaban). UGIH was defined as bleeding with symptoms of hematemesis, melena, or hematochezia, with EGD confirming the source of bleeding from the upper gastrointestinal tract (esophagus, stomach, or duodenum).[13] Lower GIH (LGIH) was defined as bleeding presenting as hematochezia with a confirmed lower gastrointestinal tract source of bleeding from the rectum or colon on colonoscopy.[14]

Bleeding etiology was defined by the presence of direct stigmata of recent hemorrhage, such as active bleeding, adherent clot, oozing, visible vessels, or variceal nipple sign. Concurrent upper/lower GIB required endoscopic evidence of active bleeding or high-risk stigmata in both the upper and lower GI tracts, supported by the corresponding clinical symptoms.

The primary endpoint of the study was the confirmation of endoscopic diagnosis of GIH associated with recent AT use. The use of AT was considered recent if it occurred within 7 days prior to hospitalization. The secondary endpoint was in-hospital mortality due to GIH in patients with and without AT.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software for Windows version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro‒Wilk test and by visual inspection. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between groups using the independent sample t-test. Variables with a non-normal distribution are presented as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between the study variables and mortality. The multivariate analysis was adjusted for 22 potential confounders, including demographics, comorbidities, laboratory values, ICU admission, and blood transfusion. All reported P values were two-tailed, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review boards of the included hospitals. The need for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, as updated in 2013.

RESULTS

Patient demographic data

The medical records of 832 patients who were diagnosed with GIH at discharge were screened; however, 73 patients were excluded (50 patients were aged <18 years, 16 had missing endoscopy reports, and 7 had missing AT information). Accordingly, 759 patients were included, of whom 354 (46.6%) were AT users and 405 (53.4%) were non-users. The patients’ demographic data are summarized in Table 1. Compared with non-AT users, patients receiving AT were older, with a mean age of 69.2 ± 13.9 (P < 0.0001), presenting more often with melena (175, 49.4%; P < 0.0001), had cardiovascular risk factors for hypertension (255, 72.0%; P < 0.0001), diabetes (22, 63.3%; P < 0.0001), ischemic heart disease (99, 28.0%; P < 0.0001), and stroke (53, 15.0%; P < 0. 0001). Non-AT users had a history of smoking (22, 5.4%; P = 0.007), alcohol use (9, 2.2%; P = 0.023), and liver cirrhosis (61, 15.1%; P = 0.017).

Mortality in the study cohort

The overall in-hospital mortality of patients who presented with GIH was 12.1% [Table 4]. Compared with non-AT users, AT users required more blood transfusions, with a mean of 1.43 ± 2.5 units (P = 0.005). A greater proportion of AT users (28.5%) than non-AT users (P = 0.002) required ICU admission. In-hospital mortality is shown in Figure 3. Mortality was higher among AT users (15.8%) than among non-AT users (8.9%) (P = 0.042). A trend toward differences in UGIH mortality between AT and non-AT users was observed (9.9% vs. 5.4%, respectively; P = 0.058). Although the mortality rate in all patients who presented with LGIH was 3.6%, which was lower than that among patients with UGIH (7.5%), there was a statistically significant difference in LGIH mortality between AT and non-AT users (5.1% vs. 2.2%, respectively; P = 0.008). The overall mortality in patients with concurrent UGIH and LGIH was 1.1%. UGIH accounted for 62.0% of the overall GIH mortality, whereas LGIH accounted for 29.3% (P < 0.0001). The significant risk factors for GIH mortality were AT users (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.067, 95% CI: 1.102–3.879; P = 0.024), liver cirrhosis (aOR: 2.573, 95% CI: 1.26–5.252; P = 0.009), malignancy (aOR: 2.836, 95% CI: 1.481–5.431; P = 0.002), transfusions (aOR: 1.246, 95% CI: 1.141–1.361; P < 0.001), and urea (aOR: 1.037; 95% CI: 1.017–1.057; P < 0.001) were associated with increased mortality risk.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we assessed the risk and outcomes of GIH in patients with AT and found that significant risk factors in these patients were older age, hypertension, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, and stroke. Usually, these patients are on primary or secondary antithrombotic cardiovascular preventive measures, which increase their risk of GIH. Patients with GIH tend to have an increasing number of non-gastrointestinal comorbidities.

Previous studies have explored whether comorbidities increase the risk of GIH. Siebenhüner et al.[15] retrospectively analyzed 1101 patients with GIH and reported that the prevalence of GIH was greater among patients with a multimorbidity profile. Crooks et al.[16] conducted a matched case‒control study in England in which patients aged ≥15 years with nonvariceal GIH were matched to five controls by age, sex, year, and practice. The authors reported that comorbidities were strongly associated with gastrointestinal bleeding; the adjusted odds ratio for a single comorbidity was 1.43, and for multiple or severe comorbidities, it was 2.26. Li et al.[17] analyzed the risk factors for various bleeding events in a large prospective population-based study of long-term antiplatelet treatment for the secondary prevention of vascular disease. The authors demonstrated that the incidence of UGIH and the risk of fatal bleeding increased significantly in patients aged >75 years.

In our study, we observed that the frequencies of upper and lower GIH were similar in patients who received AT (47.2% and 47.7%, respectively). Lanas et al.[18] studied 978 consecutive patients hospitalized with GIH who were taking antithrombotics or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and reported a similar rate of UGIH and LGIH in patients taking low-dose ASA, non-ASA antiplatelet agents, and anticoagulants, whereas a higher rate of UGIH was noted only in those taking NSAIDs.

In our study, there was no significant relationship between the class of antithrombotic medication and type of GIH. This remains a matter of debate in the current literature, as some studies have reported a correlation between the medication class and GIH type, whereas others have reported no such association. In the ROCKET-AF trial, patients taking rivaroxaban had rates of both upper and lower GIH similar to those of patients taking warfarin; 48% of rivaroxaban users and 47% of warfarin users had upper GIH, whereas 22% of rivaroxaban users and 24% of warfarin users had lower GIH. Additionally, 30% of rivaroxaban users and 29% of warfarin users experienced rectal bleeding.[19] Lanas et al.[18] reported that non-ASA antiplatelet medications have a similar risk of both upper and lower GIH as low-dose ASA alone. Although low-dose ASA can directly harm the gastrointestinal mucosa, the magnitude of GIH associated with non-ASA antiplatelet medications is similar because these medications also have an antiplatelet effect on preexisting lesions, such as angiodysplasia and diverticula.

In our study, hypertension was the most common comorbidity (54.0%). In a study conducted in a large population with GIH, the most common non-GI comorbidity was hypertension (48.2%).[20] GIH events are increased by anticoagulants in combination with antiplatelet medications.[21] One study suggested that anticoagulants, low-dose ASA, NSAIDs, and other non-ASA (APA) acetylsalicylic acid medications can lead to upper and lower GIH, with anticoagulants appearing to be the main cause of GIH.[22] The addition of a single antiplatelet agent to an oral anticoagulant is estimated to increase the risk of bleeding by 20%–60%, whereas the addition of a dual antiplatelet agent further increases the risk by 2–3 fold.[2324]

In our study, erosive gastritis was the most common cause of UGIH in patients on AT, whereas hemorrhoids were the most common cause of LGIH. A previous study revealed that peptic ulcers were the main cause of UGIH, while hemorrhoids were the predominant cause of LGIH.[25] In a recent study of 434 patients with GIH who were taking antithrombotics, Bozic et al.[26] reported that the most frequent causes of UGIH were gastric and duodenal ulcers (51.2%), while the most common causes of LGIH were hemorrhoids (7.1%) and diverticula (6.7%).

In our study, the overall in-hospital mortality of patients with acute GIH was 12.1%. According to a recent study, antithrombotic medication remains a major risk factor for mortality.[27] In a large population study obtained from the Stockholm study by Komen et al.,[28] in 4291 patients with severe GIH, the mortality rate was 16.2%, regardless of AT type. In our study, there was a nonsignificant trend toward differences in UGIH mortality between AT and non-AT users (4.6% vs. 2.9%, respectively; P = 0.0584). In a multicenter Japanese study by Matsuhashi et al.,[29] of 2205 patients with UGIH, the mortality rate in AT users was significantly greater than that in non-AT users (6.0% vs. 3.7%; P < 0.05).

In our study, the difference in mortality between AT and non-AT LGIH patients (2.4% vs. 1.2%) was significant (P = 0.008). According to other studies, AT use is associated with an increased risk of mortality in patients with LGIH.[30]

In the present study, AT use, liver cirrhosis, malignancy, elevated serum urea level, and blood transfusion were predictors of in-hospital mortality. Although we adjusted for numerous confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may have persisted. However, the comprehensive nature of our adjustment model strengthens the validity of these findings.

Our findings are in concordance with those of other studies showing that the presence of medical comorbidities and blood transfusion are significant predictors of increased mortality in patients with GIH.[3132] In our study, UGIH accounted for 62.0% of the total GIH mortality. A study from Canada by Rahme et al.[33] revealed that the mortality of UGIH was 80% in all patients with GIH.

Limitations

Our study has limitations, mainly related to its retrospective and chart review design. Despite the participation of the two centers, the study population was smaller than those in other studies. In addition, we also did not examine the impact of withholding AT or the timing of AT resumption on mortality. Furthermore, our study did not report the indications for AT or assess the endoscopic intervention rates, technical success, or rebleeding events due to inconsistent documentation across participating centers. Our analysis did not classify bleeding events by severity using standardized criteria, which could have provided additional prognostic information. Future prospective studies may benefit from incorporating these assessments.

Nonetheless, our multicenter study provides the first comprehensive characterization of GIH patterns and outcomes in patients from Saudi Arabia who received antithrombotic therapy. Our findings establish local mortality benchmarks among antithrombotic therapy users, distinct endoscopic profiles, and local-specific risk factors that were previously unreported in the regional literature.

CONCLUSION

The study highlights significant risks associated with gastrointestinal hemorrhage in patients receiving antithrombotic therapy. Patients on antithrombotic therapy who presented with gastrointestinal hemorrhage were generally older and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, which collectively contributed to the heightened risk of gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Our findings indicate that the frequency of upper and lower gastrointestinal hemorrhage was similar in patients with antithrombotic therapy, with no significant association between antithrombotic therapy class and gastrointestinal hemorrhage type. However, antithrombotic therapy users experienced more severe outcomes, including higher rates of ICU admission, blood transfusions, renal impairment, and in-hospital mortality than non-antithrombotic therapy users.
