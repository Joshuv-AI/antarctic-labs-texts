Tobacco Smoking Cessation and Cancer Stage
Authors: Koji Matsuo, Matthew W. Lee, Emmeline L. Friedman, Hadi Erfani, Rohan D. Luhar, Jane V. O’Bryan, Alexia J. Ouzounian, Jennifer A. Yao, Lynda D. Roman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study uses data from the National Cancer Database to examine the association between tobacco smoking cessation and cancer stage among US adults.

Introduction

Tobacco smoking is a well-studied risk factor for the development of numerous malignant neoplasm types, including leukemia and bladder, colon, rectal, esophageal, kidney, laryngeal, liver, lung, mouth, pancreatic, stomach, uterine, and cervical cancer.1 While many oncologic studies have focused on cancer incidence, survival, and treatment implications among ongoing tobacco users, tobacco smoking cessation is another important area of investigation.2 Quitting tobacco smoking lowers the risk of developing the aforementioned malignant neoplasms,3 and tobacco smoking cessation both improves prognosis and decreases mortality risk.2 Yet whether cancer severity differs for former smokers has not been completely evaluated. This study examined the association between tobacco smoking status (current or former) and cancer stage.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted from February to May 2026 using National Cancer Database (NCDB) data (eMethods in Supplement 1).4 Although the NCDB is not population based, it captures approximately 70% of new incident cancer cases annually from more than 1500 US Commission on Cancer–accredited facilities.5 The University of Southern California Institutional Review Board deemed the study non–human participant research due to use of publicly available, deidentified data and waived the need for informed consent. We followed the STROBE reporting guideline.

Adults (aged 18-90 years) who received a new invasive malignant neoplasm diagnosis in 2023 and had cancer stage and tobacco smoking status available were included. A total of 11 cancer categories were included, spanning 56 sites and types. The study year was selected due to the introduction of tobacco smoking status in the NCDB (variable: tobacco use),6 including never, current, or former smoker status. Cancer types were identified per National Cancer Institute coding schemas using the World Health Organization International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition,7 and were grouped according to the American Cancer Society schema (eTable in Supplement 1).8 Cancer stage followed the NCDB schema (variable: analytic stage group), categorized as stages I to II or III to IV (eFigure in Supplement 1).

All patients had histopathologic diagnosis confirmation. Patients with secondary malignant neoplasms were excluded. The association between tobacco smoking status and cancer stage was assessed with a multivariable generalized linear model with Poisson distribution, adjusted for clinical demographic characteristics.

Statistical significance was based on 95% CIs that did not cross 1.9 Analyses were performed using SPSS, version 30.0 (IBM SPSS), and R, version 3.5.3 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Among 729 906 patients (median age, 65 [IQR, 56-73] years; 397 181 [54.4%] female, 332 725 [45.6%] male), 118 313 were current smokers (16.2%), 236 317 were former smokers (32.4%), and 375 276 were never smokers (51.4%). The proportional rate of stage III to IV cancer was 48.7% (95% CI, 48.4%-49.0%), 39.5% (39.3%-39.7%), and 31.6% (31.4%-31.7%) among current, former, and never smokers, respectively. Current smokers were 15% more likely to have stage III to IV disease compared with never smokers (adjusted rate ratio [ARR], 1.15 [95% CI, 1.14-1.16]) (Table 1). This association was attenuated for former smokers, who were only 5% more likely to have stage III to IV disease compared with never smokers (ARR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.04-1.06]). A similar association was seen in all clinical demographic subgroups examined, including by facility cancer program type (Table 2).

When assessed for cancer type in exploratory analysis (Table 1), current smokers with malignant neoplasms of the pharynx (55.5% vs 25.7%; ARR, 1.99 [95% CI, 1.82-2.18]), larynx (67.2% vs 38.2%; ARR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.44-1.88]), or tongue (59.8% vs 36.8%; ARR, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.42-1.67]) or cutaneous melanoma (31.3% vs 18.4%; ARR, 1.52 [95% CI, 1.42-1.64]) were more than 50% as likely to have stage III to IV disease compared with never smokers. Discussion

In patients of Commission on Cancer–affiliated facilities, tobacco smoking status was associated with cancer stage at diagnosis. Specifically, current smokers were more likely to have advanced disease compared with never smokers; however, this association was attenuated for former smokers. While limited to hypothesis-generating interpretation, the association between tobacco smoking status and cancer stage appeared more prominent for malignant neoplasms known to be associated with tobacco smoking.2 Because cancer stage is an important prognostic factor, these data add useful information when counseling patients on tobacco smoking cessation.

Study limitations include unmeasured confounding factors such as duration of tobacco smoking cessation, alcohol use status, dietary information, unknown accuracy of smoking status (current or former), and access to health care services. At the time of analysis, survival was not available for patients in 2023 but is an important outcome measure for this study objective.

The findings of this study call for action to decrease tobacco smoking–related cancer incidence and mortality. Because smoking cessation can be an individual choice jointly determined with many other choices, further investigation to account for such confounding and possible random effects for multilevel heterogeneity is warranted. Moving forward, providing data to support the positive outcomes of quitting tobacco smoking among patients with cancer can encourage tobacco smoking cessation.
