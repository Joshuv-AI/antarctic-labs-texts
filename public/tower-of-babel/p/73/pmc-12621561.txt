Effects of flour types and fermentation times on low-protein steamed buns
Authors: Cuiping Shi, Ye Zi, Zhenfeng Liu, Wei Cai, Jian Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Low-protein foods have attracted much attention in the medical field. Herein, low-protein steamed buns (LPSBs) were prepared, and their properties were evaluated. The flour types, yeast/flour ratios, fermentation temperatures, and fermentation times were optimized to obtain LPSBs with smooth surfaces and internal porous structures. The LPSBs had a low protein content (0.50 g/100 g) and 80 volatile organic compounds (VOCs). The LPSBs made with different types of flour had distinct characteristic VOCs, allowing for their differentiation. In addition, 10 yeast-metabolism-derived VOCs were identified in the LPSBs. The post-storage textural properties were affected by the flour types, fermentation times, storage temperatures, and storage times. The flours and fermentation times affected the in vitro starch digestion rates. Finally, the LPSBs had lower consumer's acceptability than the steamed bun made with medium-gluten flour. The obtained results are beneficial for understanding the effects of flour types and fermentation times on the properties of LPSBs.

Introduction

Low-protein foods are important for the nutritional treatment of diseases that require a limited amount of protein or the absence of certain amino acids. These conditions include abnormal amino acid and organic acid metabolism, urea cycle disorders, and chronic kidney disease. The goal of a low-protein diet is to reduce the production of harmful amino acids (Handoom et al., 2018), organic acids (Garcia-Arenas et al., 2023), and ammonia (Francini-Pesenti et al., 2020), and achieve a low protein/creatinine ratio (Zhang et al., 2023). The first three diseases are rare inherited disorders with low incidence rates (e.g., phenylketonuria has an incidence rate of 1/10701) (Song et al., 2022) and require a lifelong amino acid-restricted diet. On the other hand, chronic kidney disease is highly prevalent, with 697–843 million people affected globally in 2017 (Selby & Taal, 2024). A low-protein diet (0.6 g protein/kg/day vs. 0.8 g protein/kg/day for healthy adults) was suggested for the nutritional treatment of chronic kidney disease (Mafra et al., 2025). Therefore, low-protein foods can be considered lifelong “drugs” for these individuals.

Low-protein foods have attracted much attention in the medical field. As examples, low-protein pastas have a protein content of 2.94–5.76 g/100 g dry sample (Yaseen & Shouk, 2011), low-protein breads have a protein content of 1.40–1.43 g/100 g bread (Scortegagna et al., 2020), and low-protein biscuits have a protein content of 1.27 g/100 g biscuit (Azaripour & Abbasi, 2020). Proteins serve dual roles as nutrients and structural building blocks in foods (Foegeding, 2015); thus, low-protein foods should ensure a sufficient protein content to maintain an adequate food structure. The relationship between the process factors and properties of low-protein foods should be explored, aiming to develop products with protein contents, such as <1.0 g/100 g low-protein foods.

Steamed bun (mantou, steamed bread) is a yeast-fermented wheat product. As a traditional Chinese food, steamed buns are one of the most popular staples in the Chinese diet because of their pleasant mouthfeel and nutritional value (Hu et al., 2015). Steamed buns account for around 70 % of wheat consumption in northern China (Sha et al., 2007). However, they have a high protein content (7.17 %–8.83 %) (Shao et al., 2023). Therefore, it is necessary to develop low-protein steamed buns (LPSBs) for individuals with diseases that require a limited protein intake.

This study aimed to explore the influence of flour types and fermentation times on the preparation and properties of LPSBs. First, the effects of flour types, yeast/flour ratios, and fermentation temperatures on the preparation and textural properties of the LPSBs were studied. Second, the effects of fermentation times on the preparation, textural properties, and physicochemical properties of the LPSBs were investigated. Third, the nutritional components of the LPSBs were determined to confirm they were low-protein foods. Fourth, the influence of flour types and fermentation times on the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the LPSBs was analyzed using headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS). Fifth, the effects of flours and freezing times on the textural properties of the LPSBs were studied. Sixth, the impacts of fermentation times, storage temperatures, and storage times on the textural properties of the LPSBs were investigated. Finally, the effects of flour types and fermentation times on the in vitro digestion behaviors of the buns were assessed.

Materials and methods

Raw materials

Aishushu™ low-protein flour for steamed bun (ALPFSB), with a protein content of 0.47 g/100 g, was bought from Shanghai Zhunshen Food Dongtai Co., Ltd. (Dongtai, Jiangsu, China). Zhongen™ low-protein flour for steamed bun (ZLPFSB), with a protein content of 0.32 g/100 g, was bought from Cangzhou Enji Biological Products Co., Ltd. (Hebei Province, China). Fengzheng™ medium-gluten flour (FMGF), with a protein content of 11.5 g/100 g, was bought from Weifang Fengzheng Flour Co., Ltd. (Weifang, Shandong, China).

Yutang™ white granulated sugar (first grade) was bought from Dafeng Yinmore Sugar Industry Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, Jiangsu, China). Yeast with a protein content of 35–60 g/100 g was bought from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Yichang, Hubei, China). Drinking water was procured from China Resources Yibao Beverage (Yixing) Co., Ltd. (Yixing, Jiangsu, China). α-Amylase from porcine pancreas (9 U/mg) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). WANVI® glucoamylase solution (2000–3300 U/mL) was procured from Shanghai Linghan Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). A Biosharp® glucose content assay kit was bought from Labgic Technology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, Anhui, China). Sodium acetate acetic acid buffer solution was obtained from Shanghai Macklin (China). Anhydrous ethanol (AR, ≥99.7 %) was purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd. (China).

Preparation of steamed buns

The steamed buns were prepared using a simple fermentation and steaming method (Fig. 1A). Briefly, sugar (0.1 g/g flour) and yeast (0.01 g/g flour) were mixed in 20 mL of preheated (35 °C) drinking water. Then, the mixture was added to 40 g of low-protein flour in a glass bowl and kneaded to form loose dough, and then smooth dough. The glass bowl was sealed with a plastic wrap and incubated at 35 °C in an oven (PT3520W-G, Midea Group Co., Ltd., Foshan, China) for 45 min. Then, the fermented dough was split into three hemispherical dough portions and steamed in an electric steamer (MZ-ZGC322301, Midea Group Co., Ltd.) with cold water for 25 min. The obtained steamed buns were cooled at room temperature for 5 min, cut from top to bottom into two halves, and photographed using a digital camera.Fig. 1Preparation process of the low-protein steamed buns (LPSBs). Three types of flour (ALPFSB, ZLPFSB, and FMGF) were used to prepare the steamed buns. To use as little yeast as possible, five yeast/flour mass ratios (0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04 g/g flour) were tested. Furthermore, five fermentation temperatures (25, 30, 35, 40, and 45 °C) were assessed, aiming to prepare LPSBs without cracks on the surface. Six fermentation times (0, 25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 min) were also tested.

Textural analysis

The steamed buns were cut into two halves from top to bottom, and then cut from the inside to the outside into pieces with a thickness of 13 ± 1 mm. Three steamed buns were used to prepare three parallel pieces to determine their textural properties (hardness, chewiness, cohesiveness, resilience, and springiness) using a TA.GEL instrument (Shanghai BosinTech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) (Guo et al., 2022). A TA/36R probe (column shape and 36 mm in diameter) was used to determine the overall texture profile. The pre-test, test, and post-test speeds were 3.00, 1.00, and 1.00 mm/s, respectively. The compression strain objective was 20 % of the original piece height. The trigger force was set at 0.049 N. The dwell time was 5.00 s.

Colorimetric properties

A CR-400 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) was used to determine the colorimetric properties of the steamed bun pieces (Erdem & Kaya, 2021). Three colorimetric parameters were obtained: lightness (L*), redness-greenness (a*), and yellowness-blueness (b*). The whiteness values of the steamed bun pieces were calculated using Eq. 1 (Zhang et al., 2018):(1)Whiteness=100−100−L∗2+a∗2+b∗2

Weight increase percentages

An MTS5000D electronic balance (Meilen, Shenzhen Meifu Electronics, Guangdong, China) was used to determine the weights of the shaped doughs (m1) and the obtained LPSBs (m2). Then, the weight increase percentages of the doughs during steaming were calculated using Eq. 2:(2)W%=m2−m1m1×100

Moisture content

A drying method was applied to determine the moisture contents of the LPSBs according to the Chinese National Standard GB 5009.3–2016 (Tao et al., 2022). Briefly, the LPSBs were broken into crumbs with a size of ≤2 mm. Then, the LPSB crumbs (5 g) were put in constant-weight bottles to obtain a thickness of ≤5 mm. After heating at 103 ± 1 °C in an oven to a constant weight (mass difference ≤ 2 mg), the moisture contents of the steamed buns were calculated using Eq. 3:(3)Moisture content%=m4−m5m4−m3×100where m3 is the weight of the bottle (g), m4 represents the total weight (g) of the bottle with the samples before heating, and m5 stands for the total weight (g) of the bottle with the samples after heating.

Water activity

A handheld AwTester Basic water activity (aw) measuring device (Wisdom Shanghai Instrument, Shanghai, China) was used to determine the aw values of the LPSB samples (Zhang et al., 2024). The LPSB crumbs were put in the sample cups, filling less than 2/3 of the cup volume. Then, the aw values were measured following the user manual.

pH of LPSBs

The pHs of the LPSBs were measured according to the Chinese standard GB/T 21118–2007 (Chinese steamed bread made of wheat flour). Briefly, the LPSBs were broken into crumbs and ground for 3 min. Then, 50.0 g of the ground sample was mixed with 150 mL of ultrapure water, previously boiled and cooled. The sample was crushed to a uniform paste. Finally, the pH of the LPSB paste was measured using a FE28 table acidity meter (Mettler Toledo, Shanghai, China).

Nutritional component determination

The nutritional components of the LPSBs were determined by the Shanghai

WEIPU Testing Technology Group Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and the Instrumental Analysis Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Shanghai, China). The procedures were qualified by both the China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment and the China Accredited Laboratory. The protein, fat, moisture, ash, and sodium contents were measured following the Kjeldahl method in GB 5009.5–2016, the acid hydrolysis method in GB 5009.6–2016, the direct drying method in GB 5009.3–2016, the total ashes method in GB 5009.4–2016, and the inductively coupled plasma emission spectroscopy method in GB 5009.91–2017. Finally, the energy and carbohydrate content were calculated following the procedure in GB/Z 21922–2008.

Headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS)

The steamed bun crumbs (2.0 g) were put in 20 mL headspace bottles and heated at 80 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the sample was examined using HS-GC-IMS (FlavourSpec® Sensitive Analyzer, G.A.S. Gesellschaft für analytische Sensorsysteme mbH, Dortmund, Germany) (Chen et al., 2021). The run time was 30 min. A DB-WAX column (30 m × 0.32 mm i.d., 0.25 μm; Stabilwax-MS, Restek, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was used. The carrier/drift gas was N2. The IMS temperature was set at 45 °C. The sample injection time was 0.5 min. The sample was incubated at 80 °C for 30 min. The injection syringe was kept at 85 °C.

The volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were determined using the VOCal (Version: 0.4.03) software with four plugins: the Reporter plugin for topographic plots and their difference assessment, the Gallery Plot plugin for the fingerprint spectrum comparison, the FSA plugin (fingerprint similarity analysis), and the PCA plugin (principal component analysis). The qualitative analysis of VOCs was performed using the VOCal with the built-in NIST 2020 and IMS databases.

In vitro digestion behaviors

The total starch contents of the steamed buns were measured following published enzymatic hydrolysis methods (Guo et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2018), with minor modifications. Briefly, the α-amylase from porcine pancreas (43 mg) was mixed with 10 mL of sodium acetate–acetic acid buffer (0.2 mol/L, pH 5.2) at 300 rpm for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged (7104 ×g, 10 min; H1650, Hunan Xiangyi Laboratory Instrument, Changsha city, Hunan Province, China). The supernatant was mixed with 0.1 mL of a glucoamylase solution, and the obtained enzyme mixture was used for the subsequent studies.

The freshly prepared steamed buns were broken into crumbs (W6 = 200 mg) and boiled in 5 mL of water for 30 min. Then, 15 mL of sodium acetate acetic acid buffer was added, and the total volume (20.0 mL) was recorded as V. The mixture was shaken (100 rpm) at 37 °C in a DKZ-1 water bath (Shanghai Yiheng, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. After adding 0.1 mL of the preheated (37 °C, 10 min) enzyme mixture, the sample was shaken at 37 °C for 180 min. During the process (20, 40, 60, 90, 120, and 180 min), 0.2 mL aliquots were diluted with 0.8 mL of anhydrous ethanol with a dilution factor (D) of 5. The diluted solution was then centrifuged (7104 ×g, 5 min; H1650). The supernatant was examined using a d-glucose (GOPOD) assay kit (Biosharp, Labgic Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with a standard glucose solution (CStandard = 1 mg/mL). Finally, the glucose concentration (C, mg/mL) in 20.0 mL of the digestion solution was calculated using Eq. 4:(4)C=CStandard×ASample−AWaterAStandard−AWater×Dwhere ASample, AWater, and AStandard are the absorbances of the supernatant, ultrapure water, and standard glucose solution, respectively, taken at 520 nm using a microplate reader device (BioTek Synergy H1, USA).

The steamed bun crumbs were dried at 103 ± 1 °C for 18 h to remove the moisture content per the Chinese National Standard GB 5009.3–2016 (Tao et al., 2022). The starch contents were calculated using Eq. 5:(5)Starch content%=W9−W7W8×100where W7 is the weight of the glass bottle, W8 represents the weight of the steamed bun crumbs, and W9 stands for the dried weight of the samples (the steamed bun pieces + the glass bottle) after drying at 103 ± 1 °C.

Finally, the starch hydrolysis percentages were calculated using Eq. 6:(6)Starch Hydrolysis%=Hydrolyzed starch amountStarchamount in LPSBs=0.9×C×VW6×Starch content×100where 0.9 is the conversion factor from the glucose molecular mass to starch monomer units of anhydro-glucose (Jiang et al., 2021).

Sensory properties

A descriptive sensory analysis was used for the sensory evaluation of the LPSBs (Liu et al., 2012). The sensory evaluation did not involve medical research. Therefore, it did not require formal ethical approval by the Ethics Review Board of the Xinhua Hospital (Streule et al., 2024). The authors confirmed the utilization of the suitable protocol to safeguard the rights and privacy of all participants during the execution of the research (Pu et al., 2025). All the eparticipants were asked to sign informed consent forms to give their consent to take part in the sensory study and use their information. Briefly, 10 panel members (Ages: 20–35 years; Male/Female: 2/8; Healthy) were recruited to understand the experiment. According to the Chinese National Standard “GB/T 35991-2018 Sensory Evaluation Standard for Wheat Steamed Buns”, LPSBs were evaluated by nine attributes, namely, specific volume (20 scores), width-to-height ratio (5 scores), elasticity (10 scores), surface color (10 scores), surface structure (10 scores), section structure (20 scores), toughness (10 scores), viscosity (10 scores), taste (10 scores). The nine attribute scores were summarized to obtain the total scores.

The evaluation was performed according to the criteria in the Chinese National Standard GB/T 35991–2018. As for “specific volume”, the evaluation criteria were: i) specific volume ≥ 2.8 (20 scores); ii) specific volume ≤ 1.8 (5 scores); iii) specific volume of 2.8–1.8 (A deduction of 1.5 points for every 0.1 decrease). The LPSBs were sealed with plastic wraps, and their specific volumes were measured using a water displacement method. As for “width-to-height ratio”, the evaluation criteria were: i) width-to-height ratio ≤ 1.4 (5 scores); ii) width-to-height ratio ≥ 1.6 (0 scores); iii) specific volume of 1.4–1.6 (A deduction of 1 point for every 0.05 decrease). As for “elasticity”, the evaluation criteria were: i) good elasticity when pressed with fingers (8–10 scores), ii) moderate elasticity when pressed with fingers (6–7 scores), and iii) weak elasticity when pressed with fingers (4–5 scores). As for “surface color”, the evaluation criteria were: i) good glossiness (8–10 scores), ii) slight darkness (6–7 scores), and iii) gloomy color (4–5 scores). As for “surface structure”, the evaluation criteria were: i) smooth surface (8–10 scores), and ii) shrinkage, collapse, bubbles, or hot spots (4–7 scores). As for “section structure”, the evaluation criteria were: i) fine and uniform pores (18–20 score), ii) few larger pores (13–17 score), a deduction of 1 point if skin separation from the inside, iii) almost uniform pores with few bubbles or rough structure (10–12 scores), and iv) uneven pores or rough structures (5–9 scores). As for “toughness”, the evaluation criteria were: i) strong biting force (8–10 scores), ii) general biting force (6–7 scores), and iii) poor biting force, debris was produced during cutting, or dry and hard feeling when they were chewed (4–5 scores). As for “viscosity”, the evaluation criteria were: i) refreshing and non-sticky to the teeth (8–10 scores), ii) non-refreshing (6–7 scores), and iii) sticky to the teeth (4–5 scores). As for “taste”, the evaluation criteria were: i) moderate sweetness, refreshing taste, and non-sticky to teeth (20–16 score), ii) high or low sweetness, slightly sticky to teeth (15–11 score), and iii) excessively high or low sweetness, seriously sticky to teeth (10–0 score).

Statistical analysis

Three parallel samples were used for each experiment to obtain the mean ± standard deviation. The data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's test for significant comparison (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Effects of flour types on the preparation and textural properties of the steamed buns

The steamed buns were prepared using a simple fermentation and steaming method (Fig. 1). Under the optimal manufacturing conditions, all three flours (ALPFSB, ZLPFSB, and FMGF) produced steamed buns with good appearance (intact and smooth) and an internal porous structure (Fig. S1). This result indicates that medium-gluten flour was suitable for making steamed buns (Tian et al., 2022). Furthermore, the two low-protein flours (ALPFSB and ZLPFSB) were adequate raw materials to prepare LPSBs.

The textural properties of the doughs and steamed buns depended on the type of flour used. As shown in Table 1, ALPFSB resulted in closer textural properties (hardness and chewiness) to those of FMGF than ZLPFSBF. All the steamed buns exhibited similar cohesiveness, resilience, and springiness. Moreover, the textural properties were comparable to those of steamed breads made with purple corn flour (Guo et al., 2022). Thus, ALPFSB appeared to be suitable for preparing steamed buns with similar textural properties to those of steamed buns made with medium-gluten flour. Therefore, ALPFSB was used to prepare LPSBs for subsequent analyses. Nonetheless, ZLPFSB may be adequate for preparing steamed buns under other conditions (e.g., using different yeasts). Thus, further optimization is required for manufacturing LPSBs using the ZLPFSB flour.

Effects of yeast/flour ratios on the preparation and textural properties of the steamed buns

Five yeast/flour ratios (0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04) were evaluated to prepare LPSBs. All the doughs had good appearances (Fig. S2). The LPSBs with a yeast/flour ratio of 0.00 showed obvious cracks on the surface, whereas LPSBs with yeast/flour ratios of 0.01–0.04 showed no cracks on the surface. In addition, the LPSBs with yeast/flour ratios of 0.01–0.04 exhibited internal porous structures, whereas the bun lacking yeast was dense, with no internal pores. The porous structures were due to the production of carbon dioxide during the fermentation (Luo et al., 2018). Therefore, yeast addition was crucial for preparing the LPSBs, as is the case with conventional steamed buns (Zheng et al., 2024). Moreover, only a yeast/flour ratio of 0.01 resulted in a smooth surface (Fig. S2), indicating that this was the optimal yeast/flour ratio to prepare LPSBs. Therefore, this ratio was selected for other studies.

The textural properties of the doughs and LPSBs also depended on the yeast/flour ratios (Table 1). The textural properties of the doughs showed no obvious laws on the yeast/flour ratios. The yeast/flour ratio of 0.01 resulted in the LPSBs with the lowest hardness and chewiness. This result was consistent with their smooth surface (Fig. S2). Therefore, the 0.01 yeast/flour ratio was optimal for preparing the LPSBs.

Effects of fermentation temperatures on the preparation and textural properties of the steamed buns

Five fermentation temperatures (25, 30, 35, 40, and 45 °C) were assessed. As shown in Fig. S3, all the doughs had good appearances. The LPSBs fermented at 30, 35, and 40 °C exhibited surfaces with no cracks; however, other samples showed obvious cracks on the surface. Too low a temperature (25 °C, lower than the optimal fermentation temperature) may result in a lower metabolic activity of the yeast and a lower production of carbon dioxide, which induces cracks on the surface during steaming. On the other hand, too high a temperature (50 °C, above the optimal temperature) may result in inhibition of the metabolic activity of the yeast and a lower production of carbon dioxide, which also induces cracks on the surface during steaming. Fig. S3 shows that all the LPSBs had adequate porous structures. The optimal temperature for yeast fermentation was reported to be 25–32 °C (Huang et al., 2021). Therefore, low-protein flour might require a higher fermentation temperature (30–40 °C). Here, 35 °C was selected as the fermentation temperature for other studies.

The textural properties of the doughs and LPSBs also depended on the fermentation temperatures (Table 1). The textural properties of the doughs showed no obvious laws on the fermentation temperatures. All the LPSBs showed similar cohesiveness and resilience, suggesting that the different fermentation temperatures had similar effects on the cohesiveness and resilience of the LPSBs. In addition, fermentation temperatures of 25–35 °C resulted in LPSBs with similar textural parameters. However, the steamed buns fermented at 40–45 °C had lower hardness, chewiness, and springiness than other samples. The best fermentation temperature of yeast has been reported to be 25–32 °C (Huang et al., 2021). High temperatures might promote the activity of yeast (Pang et al., 2022), increasing the amounts of carbon dioxide produced during the fermentation (Luo et al., 2018), and, in turn, resulting in LPSBs with lower hardness, chewiness, and springiness. Nonetheless, considering that different people have different preferences, we cannot say that a given fermentation temperature is optimal for making the LPSBs. However, based on the surface appearances (Fig. S3), 30–40 °C was considered a suitable fermentation temperature for preparing the LPSBs.

Effects of fermentation times on the preparation, textural properties, and physicochemical properties of the steamed buns

Six fermentation times (0, 25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 min) were assessed. These fermentation times were comparable to those (20, 40, and 60 min) used for preparing steamed buns in a previous work (Xi et al., 2021). All the doughs were crack-free (Fig. 2A). The LPSBs fermented for 25–65 min showed good surface appearances, without cracks; however, the unfermented dough showed an obvious crack on the surface. Fig. 2A shows that the LPSBs fermented for 35–65 min had adequate porous structures. Moreover, fermentation times of 35–65 min resulted in LPSBs with similar internal structures to the steamed buns made using medium-gluten flour (Fig. 2A: 45 min FMGF). Therefore, fermentation for 35–65 min was considered appropriate for preparing LPSBs. The textural properties of the doughs and steamed buns depended on the fermentation times (Table 1). Fermentation for 45–65 min resulted in similar textural properties (hardness, chewiness, cohesiveness, resilience, and springiness), and the same was true for samples fermented for 25–35 min. This result suggests that long fermentation times (≥45 min) are required to prepare LPSBs with a soft texture. Previous work reported that long fermentation times are crucial to obtain fermented foods with optimal structures (Sciammaro et al., 2018). In addition, long fermentation times are key to maintaining an optimal balance between acidity and volatile compounds (Quintana-Fuentes et al., 2025). Long fermentation times also increase the amounts of carbon dioxide produced during fermentation (Luo et al., 2018) and, thus, result in LPSBs with lower hardness, chewiness, and springiness. Here, 45 min was considered the optimal fermentation time for preparing LPSBs, and was selected for other studies.

The steamed buns manufactured using ALPFSB and FMGF showed different colors; thus, colorimetric analysis was performed. The colorimetric parameters of the LPSB and control samples were measured (Figs. 2B–E). All the LPSBs showed similar whiteness (Fig. 2B), L* (Fig. 2C), a* (Fig. 2D), and b* (Fig. 2E) values. Therefore, the fermentation times had no obvious effects on the colorimetric parameters. However, the LPSBs showed different colorimetric parameters (Figs. 2B–E) from those of the steamed buns made using medium-gluten flour, consistent with their diverging appearances (Fig. 2A). Therefore, the color of the raw materials affected the color of the steamed buns (Mamat et al., 2023).

As shown in Fig. 2F and G, the LPSBs fermented for 0–35 min had lower weight increases (from 0.81 % ± 0.02 % to 0.83 % ± 0.02 %) and moisture contents (ranged between 39.75 % ± 0.71 % and 41.54 % ± 0.19 %) than the LPSBs fermented for 45–65 min (weight increases from 1.18 % ± 0.18 % to 1.31 % ± 0.07 %; moisture contents from 43.31 % ± 1.40 % to 44.25 % ± 1.22 %). This result suggests that the LPSBs with long fermentation times (≥45 min) retained more moisture than those with short fermentation times (≤35 min). This was consistent with the observation that long fermentation times (≥45 min) produced a softer texture (Table 1).

As shown in Fig. 2H and I, the LPSBs fermented for 0–65 min had similar aw values (from 0.922 ± 0.001 to 0.946 ± 0.009) and pHs (from 6.36 ± 0.01 to 6.58 ± 0.01). In summary, the soft texture (Table 1), enhanced weight increase (Fig. 2F), and high moisture content (Fig. 2G) did not affect the aw and pH of the LPSBs with long fermentation times (≥45 min). Therefore, slight increases in the weight increase percentages (Fig. 2F) and moisture contents (Fig. 2G) could not induce obvious effects on the water activity and pH of LPSBs.

Nutritional component analysis

The optimized LPSB was prepared using ALPFSB at a yeast/flour ratio of 0.01 g/g flour, and was fermented at 35 °C for 45 min. Then, its nutritional components were analyzed by two units with the qualified procedures. The protein, carbohydrate, fat, sodium, moisture, and ash contents were 0.50–0.51 g/100 g, 59.7–61.5 g/100 g, 0.4–0.5 g/100 g, 37.6–43.5 mg/100 g, 37.2–40.0 g/100 g, and 0.27–0.35 g/100 g, respectively. The energy content was 1021–1073 KJ/100 g (244–256 Kcal/100 g), lower than that of wheat–red kidney bean biscuits (1595–1628 KJ/100 g) (Roy et al., 2020). This result might be due to the differences in the moisture content (37.2–40.0 g/100 g vs. 2.06–2.61 g/100 g). Furthermore, the protein contents of the steamed buns (0.50–0.51 g/100 g) were lower than those of many low-protein foods, including low-protein breads (1.40–1.43 g/100 g) (Scortegagna et al., 2020).

Previous work has suggested that the amount of yeast, fermentation time, and steaming time affect the aroma of steamed buns (Xi et al., 2021). Therefore, the influence of the preparation factors on the aroma of the LPSBs was evaluated. The effects of the different flour types on the VOCs of the steamed buns were assessed using HS-GC-IMS (Chen et al., 2021). The representative topographic plots (Fig. S4A) and topographic subtraction plots (Fig. S4B) of the steamed buns showed 80 areas (spots, indicated by arrows with numbers) that were recognized as VOCs. Among these 80 VOCs, 67 were identified and classified into eight types: 16 esters, 14 alcohols, 12 aldehydes, 10 ketones, three alkanes, three amines, two acids, and seven others (Table S1).

Effects of fermentation times on the VOCs

The effects of the fermentation times (0, 25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 min) on the VOCs of the LPSBs were evaluated using HS-GC-IMS (Chen et al., 2021). The representative topographic plots (Fig. S5A) and topographic subtraction plots (Fig. S5B) showed 80 areas (spots), which were recognized as VOCs. The recognized 80 VOCs, identified 67 VOCs, and 8 types of identified VOCs were the same to those in the LPSBs with different flours (Figs. S4 and Tables S1).

The Gallery Plot plugin was used for the fingerprint comparisons of the LPSBs with different fermentation times (Fig. 4A). All the samples showed different VOC fingerprint plots. The LPSBs fermented for 25–65 min exhibited a significant increase in nine VOCs (benzene, butyl-, butanoic acid butyl ester, 2-heptanone, acetic acid-D, cyclohexane, 2-methyl butanal, n-pentanal, unknown area 33, and 2-octanone), compared to the unfermented LPSB (0 min). These nine VOCs were mainly produced by the yeast fermentation and could be used as characteristic VOCs for identifying the LPSBs fermented with yeast. In addition, the amount of one VOC (ethyl propanoate) increased and then decreased with increasing fermentation time. Moreover, the highest amount of ethyl propanoate (164.11 ± 23.94 a.u.) was reached at 45 min of fermentation (Table S2). Thus, this compound could be used as the characteristic VOC for the LPSB fermented for 45 min. However, other fermentation times did not result in obvious characteristic VOCs. Effects of flour types and freezing times on the textural properties of the steamed buns

The effects of the flour types (FGMF, ZLPFSB, and ALPFSB) and freezing times (0–168 h) on the textural properties of the steamed buns were studied (Fig. 5). All three flours could be used to prepare steamed buns with adequate appearances and internal porous structures (Fig. S1); thus, samples prepared with these three flours were frozen to analyze the effects of the flour types. The steamed buns were prepared as described in the first paragraph of Section 2.2. The steamed bun made with FMGF had hardness between 4.0 ± 0.8 and 18.3 ± 0.3 N (Fig. 5A), chewiness between 3.3 ± 0.8 and 13.6 ± 0.3 N (Fig. 5B), cohesiveness between 0.77 ± 0.01 and 0.88 ± 0.01 (Fig. 5C), resilience between 0.36 ± 0.03 and 0.54 ± 0.01 (Fig. 5D), and springiness between 0.81 ± 0.01 and 0.95 ± 0.05 (Fig. 5E). Effects of flour types and fermentation times on the in vitro digestion behaviors

The digestion behaviors of the steamed buns made with different flours (FMGF, ZLPFSB, and ALPFSB) and fermented for varying durations (0 and 45 min) were analyzed using a simulated intestinal model (Guo et al., 2022). All the LPSB crumbs were still present (images not shown) after 3 h of shaking in the digestion fluid. Therefore, as expected, the shaking and enzyme (α-amylase + glucoamylase) did not substantially change the morphologies of the LPSB crumbs.

Fig. 7 displays the starch digestion curves of the steamed buns. The steamed buns made with FMGF and ZLPFSB and fermented for 45 min showed similar and low starch hydrolysis rates (7.5 %–8.1 %) after 3 h in the digestion fluid. The LPSB made with ALPFSB and fermented for 45 min showed the highest starch hydrolysis rate (43.1 %) after 3 h in the digestion fluid. The LPSB prepared with ALPFSB and unfermented showed the second-highest starch hydrolysis rate (40.2 %) after 3 h in the digestion fluid. The starch hydrolysis rates were lower than those (75 %–90 %) of steamed buns made with superfine purple corn flour (Guo et al., 2022). Therefore, the flour was the key factor affecting the starch hydrolysis behavior. Effects of flour types on the sensory properties

The consumer's acceptability is very important for the development of novel foods. The effects of flour types on the sensory properties of the steamed buns were studied (Table 1). The sum sensory values were dependent on the flour types: ZLPFSB (67.0 ± 5.6) < ALPFSB (72.8 ± 5.3) < FMGF (84.7 ± 6.4). Therefore, the LPSBs had lower consumer's acceptability than the steamed bun made with FMGF. The low-protein contents might significantly decrease the sensory properties of the steamed buns. The decrease was mainly due to three attributes: specific volume, internal structure, and taste. Therefore, proteins serve three roles as nutrients, structural building blocks, and flavoring agents in foods. Further studies should improve three attributes to increase the consumer's acceptability.

Conclusions

Low-protein steamed buns (LPSBs) were prepared, and the effects of the preparation factors (flour types, yeast/flour ratios, fermentation temperatures, and fermentation times) on the properties of the LPSBs were analyzed. The results indicated that the LPSBs had a low protein content (0.50 g/100 g). All four preparation factors affected the textural properties of the steamed buns. Both ALPFSB and ZLPFSB could be used to prepare LPSBs with good appearances, without cracks. The optimal preparation factors for the LPSBs made using ALPFSB flour were a yeast/flour ratio of 0.01 g/g flour, fermentation temperatures of 30–40 °C, and fermentation times of 35–65 min. Fermentation times of 45–65 min resulted in higher weight increases and moisture contents than fermentation times of 0–35 min. The flour types and fermentation times affected the volatile organic compound (VOC) composition of the steamed buns. After storage, the textural properties of the steamed buns were partially affected by the flour types, fermentation times, storage temperatures, and storage times. In addition, the flour types and fermentation times affected the starch digestion rates in vitro. Finally, the LPSBs had lower consumer's acceptability than the steamed bun made with FMGF due to three attributes: specific volume, internal structure, and taste. The obtained results provided useful information to understand the effects of flour types and fermentation times on the properties of the LPSBs. This study also proposes a useful method to prepare LPSBs for the potential nutritional treatment of abnormal amino acid and organic acid metabolism, urea cycle disorders, and chronic kidney disease.

Nonetheless, further research is needed to address the limitations of this work. First, the key VOCs in the LPSBs should be further studied using other techniques to determine the effects of different preparation factors on the aroma of the LPSBs. Second, the nutritional behaviors of the LPSBs are crucial for their clinical application. The in vivo behavior of a food generally differs from its in vitro behavior. Therefore, the in vivo behaviors of the LPSBs should be evaluated to determine whether they can satisfy patients' requirements. Third, the response surface methodology should be employed to analyze and optimize the preparation of the LPSBs. Fourth, it would be interesting to develop LPSBs using other raw materials (e.g., other low-protein flours and yeasts) available in the market. Finally, it is important to improve three attributes (specific volume, internal structure, and taste) of the LPSBs to increase their consumer's acceptability.
