Bacteroides-associated NAD⁺ depletion correlates with exacerbated radiation-induced colorectal injury and impaired mucosal proliferative capacity
Authors: Jiayuan Huang, Qiyuan Qin, Xiangyu Li, Keming Jiang, Jun Xu, Yudan Mao, Wanying Kang, Rongsui Gao, Yikan Cheng, Wenjing Zhao, Jia Ke, Xiangyu Mou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation proctitis (RP) is a frequent complication of pelvic radiotherapy that compromises treatment delivery and patient quality of life, yet the factors shaping injury severity remain incompletely defined. We prospectively profiled pretreatment fecal microbiomes and metabolomes from 55 patients and stratified them by outcome into mild versus severe RP. Baseline microbial composition showed Bacteroidales enriched in severe RP and Firmicutes enriched in mild cases. Multi-omics integration highlighted nicotinate/nicotinamide pathways; severe RP was characterized by concomitant reductions in both fecal and tissue NAD⁺ levels, along with an enrichment of microbial nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism genes, primarily contributed by Bacteroides ovatus, B. xylanisolvens, and B. fragilis. In mice, fecal microbiota transplantation from severe-RP donors exacerbated radiation-induced colorectal injury and decreased colorectal NAD⁺, supporting a causal role for the microbiota. Gavage with Bacteroides similarly worsened pathology and lowered NAD⁺, whereas nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) supplementation attenuated the injury. Mechanistically, Bacteroides gavage reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, decreased the Lgr5⁺ stem-cell proportion and proliferative indices, associated with Wnt pathway modulation. NMN reversed these effects in parallel with NAD⁺ restoration. Together, these results identify a microbiota‒metabolite association wherein Bacteroidales enrichment is associated with NAD⁺ depletion, reduced mucosal proliferative capacity, and exacerbated radiation-induced colorectal injury. The work deepens insight into RP pathogenesis and suggests a potential basis for microbiome- and metabolite-targeted approaches to attenuate severe RP.Keywords: Radiation proctitis, gut microbiome, Bacteroides, NAD⁺ metabolism, epithelial proliferative capacity

Introduction

Radiotherapy is an indispensable therapeutic modality for pelvic and abdominal malignancies, including cervical, prostate, and rectal cancers. However, collateral radiation-induced injury to healthy tissues remains largely unavoidable. While such injuries commonly involve the rectum, colon, and small intestine, radiation proctitis (RP) is recognized as the most prevalent and refractory manifestation.1,2 RP is characterized by rectal bleeding, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and tenesmus.3 Radiation-induced colorectal toxicity affects approximately 75%–90% of patients receiving abdominal irradiation, with 10%–20% progress to chronic radiation proctitis.4 Such complications compromise treatment continuity, impair patient quality of life, and ultimately jeopardize therapeutic outcomes.5

The pathogenesis of RP involves a complex cascade initiated by both direct and indirect effects of ionizing radiation. Directly, high-energy ionizing radiation induces DNA double-strand breaks in rapidly proliferating cells, leading to intestinal crypt loss and impaired epithelial regeneration.6 Indirectly, radiation triggers excessive generation of reactive oxygen species, which drives acute oxidative stress, endothelial damage, microvascular injury, tissue hypoxia/ischemia, and further impairment of epithelial repair.7 Consequently, these radiation-induced processes disrupt the intestinal barrier, including tight-junction and mucus layer integrity, promote bacterial translocation, and activate immune responses, resulting in amplified inflammation and progressive fibrosis.2,8,9 Mechanistically, microbial products and damage-associated signals can engage innate immune sensing (e.g., TLR/NF-κB-related pathways), sustaining proinflammatory cytokine production and leukocyte recruitment, thereby promoting persistent inflammation and profibrotic remodeling.10,11 However, the key factors that determine the severity and progression of radiation-induced colorectal injury remain insufficiently defined.

The gut microbiota plays crucial roles in regulating immune homeostasis, epithelial barrier integrity, and inflammatory responses, thereby influencing the development of multiple diseases the outcomes of various therapeutics.12,13 Gut microbiota has also been linked to inter-individual variability in radiation response. Clinical and translational studies have shown that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and probiotic supplementation can alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms in patients receiving irradiation.14,15 Specific taxa, including Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, have been implicated in modulating radiation-induced intestinal injury through the production of metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (e.g. propionic acid) and tryptophan derivatives (e.g. indole-3-carboxaldehyde), as well as by regulating host signaling pathways including GPR43- and AhR-mediated immune responses in mice.16–19 Moreover, Bacteroides has been repeatedly associated with radiotherapy-related gastrointestinal toxicity. A systematic review of pelvic radiotherapy cohorts reported that shifts in Bacteroides abundance are linked with gastrointestinal toxicities, including diarrhea and radiation proctitis.20 Mechanistically, the genus Bacteroides has been reported to activate inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and exacerbated enteritis during radiotherapy.10 Together, these findings highlight specific gut microbial taxa as mechanistically relevant modulators of radiation-induced injury. However, the mechanisms by which specific microbiota–metabolite–host interactions govern the severity of radiation proctitis remain incompletely understood. Elucidating these interactions is essential for the development of microbiota-based strategies for attenuating radiotherapy-induced colorectal toxicity.

In this study, we integrated metagenomic and metabolomic profiling of clinical cohorts with murine irradiation models to systematically explore the roles of the gut microbiota, focusing on Bacteroides, NAD⁺ metabolism, and to characterize their associations with colorectal epithelial proliferative capacity in RP pathogenesis. By integrating patient-derived multiomics data with animal experimental validation, we characterized a microbiota-metabolite-host axis associated with proliferative capacity and injury severity. These findings offer important translational insights into RP pathogenesis and provide a rationale for developing microbiota- and metabolite-based strategies to prevent and manage radiotherapy-induced colorectal toxicity.

Results

Microbial taxonomic composition differs between patients with mild and severe RP

To investigate the relationship between the gut microbiota, microbial metabolites, and the development of radiation proctitis (RP), we prospectively enrolled 55 patients (including 39 colorectal cancer patients and 16 cervical cancer patients) scheduled for radiotherapy and collected pretreatment fecal samples for shotgun metagenomics with parallel metabolomics. Based on follow-up outcomes assessed at 4–6 weeks post-radiotherapy using the clinical criteria,21 patients were stratified into a mild group (no or mild RP, scores 0–1; n = 30) and a severe group (moderate-to-severe RP, scores 2–4; n = 25). Representative H&E staining of rectal biopsies further verified the histopathological distinctions between the two groups (Figure S1). No significant differences in age or sex were observed between the groups (Tables S1 and S2).

The microbial community structure at baseline differed by RP severity: PCoA of Bray–Curtis distances revealed a clear separation between the two groups (PERMANOVA R² = 0.034, P = 0.046; Figure 1A, Table S3). In contrast, α-diversity metrics, including Shannon and Simpson indices, showed no significant differences between groups (Figure S2), indicating that RP severity was associated with compositional rather than richness-related shifts in the microbiota. Taxonomic profiling revealed phylum-level differences between groups: the mild group showed higher Firmicutes (52% vs. 41%) and lower Bacteroidetes (39% vs. 51%) relative to the severe group (Figure 1B,C), indicating a Firmicutes-dominant configuration in mild cases and a relative Bacteroidetes expansion in severe cases. Consistently, LEfSe analysis identified the Bacteroidetes phylum, class (Bacteroidia), and order (Bacteroidales) as significantly enriched in the severe group, whereas several Firmicutes lineages (including Streptococcaceae and Actinomycetaceae) were enriched in the mild group (Figure 1D,E, Table S4). At the species level, no Bacteroidetes species showed significant differences between groups by LEfSe, whereas several non-Bacteroidetes species were differentially abundant, including Anaerobutyricum hallii, Sutterella wadsworthensis, and Anaerostipes caccae (Figure 1F, Table S4). In summary, the taxonomic composition of gut microbiota differed between the mild and severe groups, including an enrichment of Bacteroidales in patients who developed severe RP.

Metagenomic profiling identifies key functional pathways associated with RP severity

To characterize microbial gene and functional differences between mild and severe RP, we mapped the metagenomic reads to the Enzyme Commission (EC) database using HUMAnN 3.0, yielding quantitative gene abundance profiles (Table S5). Functional categorization indicated that these genes were predominantly involved in key metabolic pathways such as nucleotide metabolism, amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (Figure S4). Differential abundance was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05, fold change >1.5 or <0.67), identifying 98 of 2335 annotated genes as significantly different, with 17 enriched in the severe group and 81 in the mild group (Figure 1G, Table S6). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these genes revealed significant differences in starch and sucrose metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and cofactor biosynthesis pathways (Figure 1H), indicating distinct microbial metabolic capacities related to carbohydrate utilization and NAD⁺-associated metabolism between the two groups.

Integration of metabolomics and metagenomics reveals Bacteroides-mediated NAD⁺ metabolic shifts associated with RP severity

Untargeted fecal metabolomic profiling was performed to characterize global metabolic alterations associated with RP severity. A clear separation between mild and severe RP patients was achieved by the PLS-DA model based on their metabolite profiles (Figure 2A). Among the 650 annotated metabolites, 42 were upregulated and 32 were downregulated in the severe group compared to the mild group, as determined by Wilcoxon testing (Tables S7 and S8). A heatmap of the top 30 differential metabolites is shown in Figure 2B. KEGG enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites revealed multiple amino acid metabolism pathways, including arginine biosynthesis and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (Figure 2C). More importantly, the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism pathway was also significantly overrepresented, consistent with the metagenomic findings (Figure 2C). Given that the metagenomic and metabolomic profiles reflected the luminal environment, we further investigated whether these alterations extended to the host tissue. Consistent with the microbial findings, detection of NAD⁺ levels in colonic tissue samples revealed significantly reduced NAD⁺ and elevated NADH concentrations in the severe group (Figure S4), indicating a disruption in host tissue redox homeostasis.

To integrate microbial and metabolic features, we performed Spearman correlation analysis incorporating the four differential bacterial orders identified above, two differential nicotinamide pathway-related genes, EC1.4.1.21, encoding aspartate dehydrogenase and EC1.6.1.1, encoding NAD(P)+ transhydrogenase, two differential metabolites (NAD⁺ and quinolinic acid), and RP severity scores. The resulting correlation matrix revealed that RP severity was positively correlated with Bacteroidales abundance but negatively correlated with NAD⁺ levels. In addition, Bacteroidales abundance was inversely correlated with NAD⁺ levels. Notably, NAD⁺ abundance negatively correlated with both EC1.4.1.21 and EC1.6.1.1 gene abundances, which in turn were positively correlated with Bacteroidales abundance (Figure 2D). Moreover, quinolinic acid, a precursor of NAD⁺, was positively associated with RP severity and Bacteroidales abundance, but negatively correlated with NAD⁺ levels (Figure 2D). EC 1.4.1.21, encoding aspartate dehydrogenase, catalyzes the conversion of aspartate to oxaloacetate while utilizing NAD⁺ as a cofactor, thereby contributing to NAD⁺ consumption.22 EC 1.6.1.1, encoding NAD(P)⁺ transhydrogenase, mediates hydride transfer between NAD(H) and NADP(H), regulating the cellular redox balance and influencing NAD⁺ availability.23 We next analyzed the taxonomic contributors within the gut microbiota for these two NAD⁺-associated genes. The results showed that members of the Bacteroidales order were the predominant sources, with Bacteroides ovatus, Bacteroides xylanisolvens, and Bacteroides fragilis together accounting for a substantial proportion of these gene abundances (Figures 2E,F and S5). Consistent with their functional contributions, quantitative metagenomic analysis revealed that B. ovatus and B. xylanisolvens were markedly enriched in the severe group (0.83% ± 1.26% and 0.41% ± 0.69%, respectively) compared to the mild group (0.31% ± 0.49% and 0.14% ± 0.45%). In contrast, B. fragilis was less abundant in the severe group (0.58% ± 1.22%) than in the mild group (1.35% ± 5.92%). Further, to validate these metagenomic observations, we performed species-specific qPCR, which confirmed the significant enrichment of B. ovatus in the severe group (Figure S6). Together, these findings indicate that the enriched Bacteroides species in the severe group may be associated with NAD⁺ availability through the presence of nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism genes, suggesting a potential link to RP severity.

FMT from severe RP donors exacerbates radiation-induced colorectal injury in mice

To experimentally assess the impact of the gut microbiota on radiation-induced colorectal toxicity, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) into antibiotic-treated, microbiota-depleted mice using stool suspensions from patients with mild RP or severe RP, with FMT administered before and after a single 12 Gy abdominal irradiation (Figure 3A). To verify the successful colonization of key donor taxa, we analyzed the relative abundance of B. ovatus in the recipient mice. Consistent with the human donor profiles, the severe-FMT group exhibited a significantly higher abundance of B. ovatus compared to the mild-FMT group (Figure S7). Mice receiving FMT from severe-RP donors exhibited greater body weight loss and higher disease activity index (DAI) scores during the 10-d observation period compared with the mild-FMT group (Figure 3B,C).

To assess colorectal injury and NAD⁺ levels, colons were excised at the experimental endpoint. Mouse colorectal NAD⁺ levels, as measured using the WST-8 method, were significantly lower in the severe-FMT group, whereas NADH levels showed no significant difference between groups (Figure 3D). Colon length was significantly shorter in mice receiving severe-RP FMT compared with those receiving mild-RP FMT (Figure 3E,F). Histological examination by H&E staining revealed more severe mucosal injury, including epithelial disruption and inflammatory cell infiltration, which was reflected by significantly higher histological injury scores in the severe-FMT group (Figure 3G,H). Colonic tissue homogenates were analyzed by ELISA, which showed that levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the severe-FMT group compared with the mild-FMT group (Figure 3I,J). Together, these findings demonstrate that severe-RP-derived microbiota exacerbate radiation-induced colorectal injury and inflammation, supporting a functional role of the gut microbial composition in radiation-induced colorectal injury severity.

Bacteroides exacerbates radiation-induced colorectal injury via NAD⁺ reduction, reversed by NMN supplementation

To further establish a causal link among Bacteroides, NAD⁺ metabolism, and radiation-induced colorectal injury, the mice were allocated into five groups: a sham-irradiated group (PBS gavage without irradiation), a control group (PBS gavage plus irradiation), a Bacteroides ovatus group, an NMN group, and a combined B. ovatus+NMN group. All groups except the sham group were subjected to a single abdominal irradiation (Figure 4A). We specifically selected B. ovatus for this intervention because it exhibited significant differential abundance between the mild and severe groups, served as the primary contributor to key genes involved in the nicotinate and nicotinamide pathways altered in patients, and demonstrated a strong correlation with both clinical severity and decreased NAD⁺ levels. Following irradiation, the mice that received B. ovatus showed significantly greater body weight loss and persistently elevated DAI scores compared with controls, reflecting more severe colorectal injury. NMN supplementation alleviated weight loss and DAI scores in both irradiated control and Bacteroides-treated mice, indicating a protective effect against radiation-induced toxicity (Figure 4B,C).

We compared colorectal NAD⁺ and NADH levels across the five experimental groups. The results showed that irradiation significantly reduced colorectal NAD⁺ levels compared with sham mice. Among irradiated groups, NAD⁺ concentrations were further reduced in Bacteroides-treated mice, NMN supplementation restored NAD⁺ levels in both irradiated groups, regardless of Bacteroides treatment. By contrast, NADH concentrations showed no significant differences among the five groups (Figure 4D).

Further, we measured colon length and performed H&E staining of colonic sections to evaluate colorectal injury. The results showed that marked shortening in all irradiated mice compared to sham controls, with the greatest reduction in the Bacteroides group. NMN treatment attenuated this effect, resulting in significantly longer colons in the Bacteroides+NMN group compared to the Bacteroides group (Figure 4E,F). H&E staining further demonstrated aggravated mucosal damage in irradiated mice, with the most severe epithelial disruption and crypt loss in the Bacteroides group. Compared with the Bacteroides group, the Bacteroides+NMN group exhibited improved mucosal architecture and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, accompanied by significantly lower histological injury scores (Figure 4G,H).

To further evaluate epithelial barrier integrity, we analyzed the expression of the tight junction protein Occludin in colonic tissue at the experimental endpoint by Western blot, as an indicator of epithelial junctional structure and function (Figure 4I,J). The results showed that Bacteroides treatment led to a marked decrease in Occludin expression in irradiated mice, which was restored by NMN supplementation (Figure 4I,J). Consistent with these findings, colonic levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were highest in the Bacteroides group and significantly reduced by NMN supplementation (Figure 4K,L).

Together, these results demonstrate that Bacteroides exacerbates radiation-induced colorectal injury, in association with reduced NAD⁺ level, and that NMN supplementation restores NAD⁺ levels, preserves barrier integrity, attenuates inflammation and histopathological damage.

Microbiota-NAD⁺-mitochondria axis regulates post-irradiation ISC function and epithelial proliferative capacity

To investigate the mechanistic basis by which Bacteroides and NAD⁺ influence post-irradiation recovery, we focused on mitochondrial activity and colorectal stem cell (ISC) function, as NAD⁺ serves as a critical metabolic cofactor supporting mitochondrial bioenergetics and ISC-driven epithelial repair. Flow cytometry was used to assess the proportion of ISCs using CD44 and EpCAM markers (CD44+ EpCAM–).24,25 Additionally, mitochondrial function of the marked cells was assessed by evaluating the mitochondrial membrane potential using TMRE staining, which offers insights into the mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic activity of the cells.26 Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that Bacteroides supplementation led to a reduction in the proportion of ISCs in irradiated mice, which can be restored by NMN supplementation (Figure 5A,B). Mitochondrial function, assessed by TMRE staining, was impaired in irradiated groups compared to the sham group, with Bacteroides supplementation exacerbating the reduction in mitochondrial activity. However, NMN supplementation effectively restored mitochondrial membrane potential, suggesting a protective role of NAD⁺ in alleviating radiation-induced mitochondrial dysfunction (Figure 5C).

We next evaluated Lgr5 protein levels as a marker of ISC abundance and activity. Western blot analysis revealed that irradiation markedly reduced Lgr5 expression compared to the sham group, and Bacteroides gavage further exacerbated this reduction. NMN supplementation significantly restored Lgr5 expression levels, both in irradiated mice receiving Bacteroides gavage (Figure 5D,E). These findings are consistent with the observed decrease in the ISC population by flow cytometry and support a model in which Bacteroides-driven NAD⁺ depletion compromises ISC maintenance.

We also assessed cellular proliferation through Ki67 expression using immunofluorescence. The results showed that irradiated mice did not exhibit a decrease in proliferation compared to sham mice, likely due to radiation-induced recovery. However, Bacteroides gavage reduced Ki67 expression, while NMN supplementation enhanced Ki67 levels. These findings suggest that Bacteroides impairs proliferation, whereas NMN supplementation promotes proliferation and aids in postirradiation recovery (Figure 5F,G).

To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we examined β-catenin expression, a key regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway that plays a crucial role in stem cell maintenance and tissue regeneration.27 The results showed that β-catenin expression was significantly reduced following irradiation. Moreover, Bacteroides gavage further decreased β-catenin levels, while NMN supplementation increased its expression, and notably promoted its nuclear translocation, as evidenced by the colocalization with DAPI (Figure 5H,I). Altered β-catenin expression and nuclear localization may reflect modulation of Wnt signaling; however, confirmation of canonical pathway activation would require evaluation of downstream targets (Axin2 and Ascl2),28 which remains to be addressed in future studies. Importantly, to validate the clinical relevance of these mechanistic findings, we assessed the expression of β-catenin and the tight junction protein Occludin in human rectal biopsies. Consistent with the murine model, we observed significantly reduced protein levels of both β-catenin and Occludin in the severe RP group compared to the mild group (Figure S8), suggesting that reduced β-catenin expression and decreased epithelial integrity may represent shared pathological features in both clinical and experimental RP.

Finally, 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples collected at the study endpoint revealed significant perturbations in gut microbiota composition in all irradiated groups, as evidenced by distinct clustering in PCoA analysis (Figure 5J). Radiation alone caused a notable shift in microbial community structure, which was further exacerbated by Bacteroides gavage, particularly increasing the abundance of Bacteroidales. In contrast, NMN supplementation modulated these alterations. As shown in the order-level stacked bar plot (Figure 5K), while NMN treatment did not fully restore the microbiota to the Sham-level composition, it partially reduced the extreme expansion of Bacteroidales and increased the abundance of other orders such as Lachnospirales compared to the B. ovatus-supplemented radiation group. These findings indicate that radiation-induced dysbiosis is influenced by Bacteroides supplementation and can be alleviated or partially counteracted by NMN supplementation.

Taken together, these findings suggest that Bacteroides is associated with impaired colorectal stem cell marker expression and mitochondrial dysfunction, potentially linked to NAD⁺ depletion, while NMN supplementation re-establishes cellular energy balance and facilitates postirradiation mucosal repair.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively characterized the gut microbiota-metabolite-host axis in radiation proctitis. Baseline gut microbial composition differed significantly by RP severity, with Bacteroidales enriched in patients who developed severe RP and Firmicutes predominating in those with mild disease. Multiomics integration revealed functional differences concentrated in nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism pathways, accompanied by reduced fecal and tissue NAD⁺ levels in severe cases. These associations were further supported by murine experiments demonstrating that FMT or Bacteroides gavage exacerbated radiation-induced colorectal injury and further depleted NAD⁺, whereas NMN supplementation restored NAD⁺ levels, enhanced mucosal proliferative capacity, and reduced inflammation (Figure 6). Collectively, these findings suggest an association among Bacteroides enrichment, NAD⁺ depletion, and reduced epithelial proliferative capacity as a potential contributor to RP severity.

Schematic overview illustrating the association between Bacteroides-driven NAD⁺ depletion, reduced epithelial proliferative capacity, and exacerbated radiation-induced rectal injury.

Previous studies have shown that the gut microbiota play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of radiation-induced colorectal injury.16–19 Much of this work has centered on probiotic-associated taxa, notably Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, and on metabolite-driven mechanisms whereby short-chain fatty acids (e.g. propionate, 3-hydroxybutyrate16,17) and tryptophan derivatives (e.g. indole-3-carboxaldehyde19) strengthen epithelial barrier function and reduce mucosal inflammation. Most prior studies emphasize immune-centered mechanisms to explain host responses to microbiota changes, particularly via GPR43,16,17 AhR,19 and TLR2 pathways18; with comparatively little attention to postirradiation epithelial repair. Consistent with prior reports, we observed microbiota-dependent modulation of postirradiation inflammation; we also found that microbiota-linked alterations in nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism were associated with changes in epithelial proliferative capacity.

Bacteroides are Gram-negative, obligately anaerobic members of Bacteroidota (class Bacteroidia, order Bacteroidales, family Bacteroidaceae) that rank among the most abundant taxa in the adult human gut microbiota.29 Their core metabolic capabilities include polysaccharide utilization loci (PUL)-mediated glycan degradation, anaerobic SCFA production (e.g. acetate, propionate), and peptide and amino-acid metabolism, among other pathways.30,31 Bacteroides exerts context-dependent effects on the host, modulated by niche, immune status, and strain-to-strain variation, spanning protective to pathogenic outcomes.32–34 For instance, in colorectal cancer, enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) secretes B. fragilis toxin, a metalloprotease that triggers a pro-carcinogenic and inflammatory cascade, fostering colon tumorigenesis.35 Moreover, in inflammatory bowel disease, Bacteroides vulgatus protesase positively correlates with disease severity, and protease inhibition prevents B. vulgatus-induced colonic epithelial damage.36 Furthermore, multiple commensal Bacteroides species can induce colitis in host-genotype-specific manner, underscoring strain-, host-, and context-dependent pathobiont behavior.37 Notably, Bacteroides ovatus has been reported to ameliorate dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced colitis,38 whereas in our irradiation model B. ovatus was associated with exacerbated radiation-induced colorectal injury. The divergence likely comes from injury modalities between models: DSS causes chemical barrier injury, whereas radiation induces DNA damage-driven injury and epithelial repair. Accordingly, in this study, we examined whether microbial nicotinamide pathways modulate NAD⁺ homeostasis and, in turn, influence epithelial proliferative capacity after irradiation.

Radiation-induced colorectal injury is initiated by DNA double-strand breaks and bursts of reactive oxygen species, leading to crypt stem-cell loss, epithelial barrier disruption, and mucosal inflammation.39 Repair proceeds via rapid epithelial restitution to re-establish barrier integrity, followed by Wnt/β-catenin-dependent crypt regeneration driven by Lgr5⁺ colorectal stem cells.39–41 Unresolved acute inflammation and inadequate epithelial repair foster a pro-fibrotic microenvironment that triggers myofibroblast activation and excessive collagen deposition, ultimately resulting in progressive fibrotic remodeling and luminal stricture formation.39–41 Because epithelial restitution and crypt regeneration are highly energy demanding, NAD⁺ availability is a key influencer of mitochondrial function and downstream Lgr5⁺ stem-cell proliferation and repair.42–44 Gut microbial communities may constrain host NAD⁺ availability not only through direct consumption but also by competing for precursors (e.g., NMN) and modulating the NAD⁺/NADH redox balance via diverse metabolic processes.45,46 In our cohort, severe RP showed lower fecal NAD⁺, which correlated with Bacteroidales abundance and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolic gene levels. In mice, severe-donor FMT or Bacteroides gavage lowered colorectal NAD⁺ and worsened radiation-induced injury. In parallel, reductions in mitochondrial membrane potential and Lgr5⁺ stem-cell marker expression were observed, consistent with reduced epithelial proliferative capacity. Collectively, the clinical and experimental data suggest an association between Bacteroides-enriched communities, reduced NAD⁺ availability, and compromised epithelial proliferative capacity following irradiation, which may collectively contribute to amplified tissue injury.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the clinical cohort sample size (n = 55) was relatively small, which may have limited the strength of evidence of the observed associations. Second, although we identified an association between Bacteroides enrichment and reduced NAD⁺ levels, the mechanistic depth remains limited: we did not quantify upstream precursors or downstream metabolites to delineate metabolic flux, nor evaluate host NAD⁺ biosynthesis and degradation pathways such as NAMPT, NAPRT, or CD38, which may modulate systemic NAD⁺ availability.47 Moreover, the proliferative markers reflect changes in epithelial proliferative capacity but do not constitute direct functional evidence of epithelial regeneration, which would require organoid-based or clonogenic assays in future studies. Third, our current approach cannot distinguish host- from microbe-derived NAD⁺ pools, nor definitively establish whether microbial influence occurs through direct consumption and transformation or indirect modulation of host pathways.

Future studies should incorporate strain-level functional comparisons, including targeted genetic or pharmacologic perturbation of NAD⁺-related pathways, to establish causality more rigorously. Furthermore, exploring the temporal host–microbiota crosstalk, including how microbial shifts affect the sequential progression from acute mucosal inflammation toward chronic tissue remodeling, remains a critical area for future investigation. Expanded, longitudinal clinical cohorts and stable isotope tracing approaches could further dissect host–microbe metabolic interactions and clarify whether targeted NAD⁺ restoration or microbiota modulation can be translated into therapeutic benefit for patients with radiation proctitis.

In summary, this study integrates metagenomics, metabolomics, and in vivo validation to reveal that Bacteroides-enriched microbiota is associated with aggravated radiation proctitis, accompanied by NAD⁺ depletion and reduced epithelial proliferative capacity. These findings establish a clinically grounded correlative framework warranting further mechanistic and functional validation, and suggest potential microbiota- and metabolite-based strategies to alleviate radiation-induced colorectal toxicity.

Methods

Patient enrollment and clinical sample collection

This single-center, prospective observational study was conducted at the Departments of Gastrointestinal Surgery and Radiation Oncology, The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China) between December 2021 and November 2022. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: 2021ZSLYEC-262), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed colorectal cancer or cervical cancer; (2) recommendation by the treating physician to receive long-course neoadjuvant radiotherapy (radiation dose: 45–50 Gy) prior to surgery; and (3) willingness to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) gastrointestinal bleeding within 4 weeks prior to enrollment; (2) use of antibiotics or probiotics within 4 weeks prior to enrollment; and (3) history of colorectal tumor resection or bowel resection.

Fecal samples were collected in the hospital 3 d before the initiation of radiotherapy (baseline). Fresh stool was placed in sterile tubes, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. The disease severity of radiation proctitis was assessed at 4–6 weeks postradiotherapy, and patients were stratified into a mild group (no or mild RP, scores 0–1; n = 30) and a severe group (moderate-to-severe RP, scores 2–4; n = 25) according to the Chinese Expert Consensus on Multidisciplinary Diagnosis and Treatment of Radiation Rectal Injury (2021 Edition),21 and the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) and the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) radiation scoring criteria (Table 1),48 integrating clinical symptoms, endoscopic findings, imaging, and histopathology.

Metagenomic sequencing

Fecal DNA was extracted from each sample using the Fecal Microbial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Library preparation and sequencing were performed by Novogene (Beijing, China) on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, USA), generating paired-end 150 bp (PE150) reads with an average output of approximately 10 Gb per sample.

Raw sequencing reads were processed through a Linux-based metagenomic bioinformatics pipeline. KneadData (v0.12.0) was used for quality control and removal of host-derived sequences, integrating Trimmomatic (v0.33) for adapter and low-quality read trimming and Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) for alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38).49 Only high-quality, nonhost reads were retained for downstream analyzes. For taxonomic profiling, reads were classified using Kraken2 (v2.1.2), and Bracken (v2.5) was employed to refine abundance estimates and convert count data into relative abundances.50 For functional profiling, the clean reads were processed with HUMAnN 3.0 for translated searches against the UniRef90 database to annotate microbial gene families and metabolic pathways.51 Gene family abundances were normalized to counts per million (CPM). Downstream statistical and ecological analyzes (e.g. diversity metrics and differential abundance analyzes) were carried out using R packages (e.g., phyloseq, vegan and ggplot2) and online platforms (e.g., MicrobiomeAnalyst52 and Wekemo Bioincloud).53

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Approximately 500 mg of frozen fecal sample was extracted with precooled methanol:water (4:1, v/v) solution to obtain metabolite-containing supernatants for LC‒MS analysis. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed by BGI (Shenzhen, China) using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with a high-resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC-HRMS) platform (Waters ACQUITY UPLC system and Thermo Fisher Q Exactive Orbitrap MS). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column, and metabolites were detected in both positive and negative ion modes. Metabolite features were extracted and aligned using Compound Discoverer software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Annotation was based on accurate mass and MS/MS fragmentation pattern matching against public databases including HMDB and METLIN. MetaboAnalyst 6.054 was used for multivariate analysis, differential metabolite identification, and KEGG pathway enrichment to pinpoint significant metabolic changes.

Animals and establishment of radiation-induced colorectal injury model

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from TopBiotech Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (approval ID: SYSU-IACUC-MED-2022-B090) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

A radiation-induced colorectal injury model was established using a RadSource RS2000 Pro 225 irradiator (Rad Source Technologies, USA) with a single 12 Gy dose of irradiation, a widely used murine model for inducing and assessing radiation-associated colonic injury, as previously described.19 A lead shield was used to ensure the radiation was localized to the abdominal area, and our subsequent analyzes focused on the colorectal segment to align with the symptomatic focus of our clinical cohort. Control mice were sham-irradiated.

To evaluate the severity of colorectal inflammation, body weight, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding were monitored daily. Each parameter was scored on a 0–4 scale (0 = normal, 4 = most severe) according to established criteria, and the disease activity index (DAI) was calculated as the mean of the three subscores (range 0–4), as previously described.55,56 Mice were euthanized on day 10 postirradiation for the collection of colon tissues and fecal samples, which were then preserved for further analyzes.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Fecal samples were collected from a representative subset of patients (three patients per group) diagnosed with mild RP (mild RP donors) and severe RP (severe RP donors). For each group, fecal samples from the three donors were pooled, diluted in sterile saline (40 mg/mL), homogenized, and filtered through a 0.25-mm stainless steel sieve. The resulting suspensions were supplemented with 10% sterile glycerol, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until use. Prior to FMT, recipient mice were treated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail consisting of ampicillin (1 g/L), vancomycin (0.5 g/L), neomycin sulfate (1 g/L), and metronidazole (1 g/L) administered in the drinking water for 7 d to deplete the gut microbiota. The mice then received 200 μL of thawed fecal suspension by oral gavage three times per week for two consecutive weeks.

Bacterial culture

Bacteroides ovatus ATCC 8483 was cultured under strict anaerobic conditions in an anaerobic workstation (Shellab Bactron EZ-2, USA). Cultivation was performed using prereduced DSMZ Medium 110 (HuizaoBio, China) supplemented with sterile-filtered hemin (chlorinated heme) and vitamin K1. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) with a spectrophotometer (INESA, 752 N, Shanghai, China). For oral administration to mice with an intact microbiota (no antibiotic pretreatment), the cultured bacteria were suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a concentration of 5 × 10⁸ colony-forming units (CFU)/ml and administered via gavage in a volume of 0.2 ml per mouse, delivering a total dose of 1 × 10⁸ CFU.

Quantification of NAD⁺/NADH

Colonic tissues were obtained from surgically resected patient specimens or harvested from mice, rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and homogenized in the extraction buffer supplied with the NAD⁺/NADH Quantification Kit (WST-8 method; Cat# S0175, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The supernatants were collected and assayed for NAD⁺ and NADH levels following the manufacturer's instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, Agilent, USA).

Validation of bacterial abundance by quantitative PCR (qPCR)

gDNA was isolated from fecal samples using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (DP328, Tiangen Biotech, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The relative abundance of the species Bacteroides ovatus was quantified by qPCR. The specific primer sequences used were as follows: B. ovatus (F: 5′-AAGTCGAGGGGCAGCATTTT-3′, R: 5′-CACAACTGACTTAACAATCC-3′); and universal 16S (F: 5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′, R: 5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′).57 qPCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeason Biotechnology, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). The relative abundance of the target bacteria was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the universal 16S rRNA levels to compare differences between groups.

Quantification of inflammatory cytokines

Colonic tissues harvested from the mice were rinsed with ice-cold PBS and homogenized using a cryogenic tissue grinder (JXFSTPRP-CL, Shanghai Jingxin, China). After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected and diluted tenfold (1:10), and the concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α were quantified with ELISA kits (Basic IL-6 Mouse ELISA Kit, Cat# ECM007; Basic TNF-α Mouse ELISA Kit, Cat# ECM008; Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Colonic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Tissue slices were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard protocols. After dehydration and mounting, histological morphology was observed and imaged using an Olympus slide scanner (Olympus Slideview VS200). H&E stained sections were evaluated for inflammation severity, crypt architecture, and mucosal damage, and scored semiquantitatively on a 0–4 scale according to published criteria.58

Western blotting

Colonic tissues, obtained from surgically resected patient specimens or harvested from mice, were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (50 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE (12.5%) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Occludin (Abcam), Lgr5 (Abcam, #ab75850) or β-catenin (Abcam, #ab6302), with GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology) as the loading control. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software, with band intensities normalized to GAPDH levels.

Immunofluorescence staining

Colonic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (Thermo Fisher, #RM9106) and β-catenin (Invitrogen) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 60 min at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime). Whole-slide images were acquired using an Olympus slide scanner (Olympus Slideview VS200), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using OlyVIA 4.1.1 (Olypmpus) and ImageJ software.

Flow cytometric analysis

For flow cytometric analysis, colonic cells were isolated at the experimental endpoint, and single-cell suspensions were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD44 (BV421, BD Biosciences) and EpCAM (CD326, Invitrogen) to identify colorectal stem cell populations (CD44⁺ EpCAM⁻),24,59 following the manufacturers' instructions. To assess mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were stained with TMRE (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. After staining, cells were washed and analyzed using a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo software (v10).

16S sequencing

To investigate radiotherapy-induced alterations in the gut microbiota of mice, fecal samples were collected prior to euthanasia under sterile conditions and immediately stored at –80 °C. Microbial DNA was extracted, and the V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were PCR-amplified. Sequencing was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China) on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. The raw reads were quality-filtered, annotated, and analyzed using QIIME2 for taxonomic classification and differential comparison.60

Statistical analyzes

For the animal experiments, the data are presented as mean ± SD and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc multiple comparison test for experiments with more than two groups. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
