Economic and demographic influences on health expenditures: robust approaches for income and aging effects
Authors: Canser Boz, Fatma Sevinç Kurnaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth expenditure is influenced by complex interactions between economic, demographic, social factors, with significant variations across countries. This study aims to investigate the determinants of health expenditures employing robust regression methods offering a more flexible and reliable approach to dealing with outliers and high data variation.MethodsThis study employs robust regression methods, Weighted Least Squares (WLS) and MM-estimator regression, to examine the determinants of health expenditures. The analyses were conducted using data from 179 countries for the year 2021 with the R Studio.ResultsThe findings indicate that income and ageing are significant determinants of health expenditures, and sixteen outliers were identified. In contrast, education level, public health expenditure, disease patterns showed no significant effect.ConclusionThis study fills gap in the literature by using robust regression methods to account for outliers and provides new insights into the role of economic and demographic factors in health expenditures.Keywords: Health economics, Health expenditure, MM estimation, Robust regression, Aging

Introduction

Health expenditure indicators serve as crucial tools for tracking resource flows, shaping health policy development, and enhancing the transparency and accountability of health systems [47]. Globally, countries exhibit notable differences in their health expenditure allocations. In wealthier nations, the average per capita health spending (PPP) surpasses USD 6,000, while in low-resource countries, it remains as low as USD 100 per capita in 2022 [46]. Among OECD countries, In 2021, the United States had the highest per capita health expenditure, totaling 12,555 U.S. dollars. This amount was significantly higher than Switzerland's, which ranked second. Germany, Norway, and the Netherlands also placed among the top five countries in per capita health expenditure, each spending approximately 8,000 U.S. dollars. Additionally, while the average per capita health expenditure among OECD member countries is over 5,000 U.S. dollars, it stands at around 200 U.S. dollars in Sub-Saharan Africa. In the United States, which leads globally in per capita health expenditure, annual spending amounts to 12,555 U.S. dollars. In contrast, according to World Bank data, South Sudan has the lowest per capita health expenditure globally, amounting to only 33 U.S. dollars. Health expenditure varies widely in relation to economic development. While some countries devote more than 12% of their GDP to health, others allocate less than 3%. Additionally, the United States allocated the largest proportion of its gross domestic product to health care, dedicating nearly 16.6 percent of its GDP to health services [41]. In Brunei Darussalam, this ratio is calculated at only 2.2 percent. While the average among OECD member countries exceeds 13 percent, in Africa and many other less developed and developing countries, the rate is around 5 percent [46].

Health expenditures play a critical role in protecting, improving, and managing the health of individuals and societies [48]. Economic growth, an aging population, technological advancements, and healthcare policies are among the key factors that determine the level and distribution of these expenditures. These expenditures not only affect the healthcare sector but also have significant implications for economic development, social welfare, and financial sustainability [33]. Therefore, analyzing the determinants of health expenditures is essential for optimizing resource allocation and enhancing the efficiency of healthcare systems. There are significant global disparities in the levels of health expenditures. These differences have consistently drawn the attention of health economists, and the reasons behind and potential impacts of these variations have been empirically examined in the literature. The topic remains a current and actively debated area of discussion. The subsequent section of the study presents the findings of empirical research conducted on the topic.

Related literature

Various studies have stated that the determinants of health expenditures using different methods. Some of these studies involve models based on micro-level data, while others rely on econometric models constructed with the aid of macroeconomic data [22, 34]. In existing studies, Income, represented by per capita GDP, is recognized as a key determinant in explaining variations in both the level and growth rate of total health expenditures across countries. The literature includes numerous studies that examine the relationship between individual or national income levels and various health expenditures. For instance, the researchers found that income level is one of the most significant determinants of health spending [1, 18, 51]). In his study, Hu and Wang [19] found that an increase in income level have a positive effect on health expenditures [19]. Baltagi and Moscone [3] analyzed the relationship between health spending and income over the long term for 20 OECD countries from 1971 to 2004. Their analysis revealed that most health expenditures and their determinants are non-stationary, and they discovered a long-term relationship between health expenditures and income levels [3].

Another factor examined in the literature for its impact on health expenditures is the level of education. Education influences the demand for healthcare services and, consequently, health expenditures. People with higher levels of education are more inclined to use healthcare services compared to those with lower education levels, as they tend to have better health awareness. This increased usage subsequently raises health expenditures [22]. On the other hand, there are arguments suggesting that people with higher education levels may experience better health outcomes due to improved health literacy, greater adherence to hygiene practices, and preventive measures. Consequently, this could lead to reduced healthcare demand and, therefore, lower health expenditures [4, 28].

According to Xu et al. [22], another determinant of health expenditures is the characteristic structure of the healthcare system. The characteristic structure of the healthcare system consists of factors such as service delivery, service financing, external support, and payment mechanisms [22]. For example, in countries where there is a gatekeeping feature and primary healthcare services are heavily utilized, health expenditures tend to decrease. The level of health expenditure also varies depending on whether the healthcare system is financed through taxes, social insurance premiums, or out-of-pocket payments. For example, a study conducted in OECD countries found that countries with a social health insurance mechanism have higher per capita health expenditures [45]

Another important determinant of health expenditure is the structure of society. With modern societies globally facing rapidly aging populations, population aging is one of the significant changes in societal structure that has emerged in the twenty-first century [20]. Among these determinants, aging has emerged as one of the most studied factors for many years [26]. Health policymakers in numerous countries have voiced concerns about the increasing financial strain that aging populations may impose on healthcare costs. Generally, younger populations tend to have lower health expenditures compared to older populations due to a reduced incidence of diseases, decreased need for healthcare, and lower utilization of health services [7, 10]. Xu and colleagues found that in lower-middle-income countries, the government health expenditures is positively associated with proportion of the population aged 60 and above [22]. Similarly, a study revealed a statistically significant and positive relationship between the percentage of the population aged 65 and older and health expenditures [21]. Additionally, Çalışkan examined the link between the population aged 65 and over and health expenditures—total, public, and private—per capita in OECD countries from 1984 to 2005. The study found positive and significant relationships between the proportion of the elderly population and various health expenditures [9]. As stated in a report released by the “Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development” (OECD), the cost of health expenditures per capita for the elderly in developed countries has been increasing at a higher rate in recent years compared to the costs associated with individuals under 65 years of age [39].

Another factor that is known to have an impact but has not been extensively empirically studied is the disease profile of the population or the prevalence of diseases within the community. Recent studies suggest that the rising incidence of noncommunicable diseases has contributed to higher impacts on both individuals and societies [5, 40] For instance, systematic review revealed that non-communicable diseases contribute significantly to both increased health expenditures and decreased economic output [44] According to another study by Liu et al., the cost of coronary heart disease in England in 1999 was estimated to be 1.73 billion pounds. Schmid calculated the direct medical costs associated with treating cardiovascular diseases in Germany, highlighting the substantial financial burden of these conditions on the healthcare system [38].

In the study conducted by Akca and colleagues, GDP per capita, the proportion of the population covered by public and private insurance, out-of-pocket health expenditure as a share of total expenditure, the age dependency ratio, life expectancy at birth, the number of hospitals per million people, the number of physicians per 1,000 individuals, perceived health status, and pharmaceutical sales were considered as independent variables. Among these, GDP per capita, life expectancy at birth, the age dependency ratio, the number of hospitals, and the percentage of the population with poor perceived health status were identified as key factors in estimating health expenditures in OECD countries [1].

To summarize in general, studies on the determinants of health expenditures reveal that these expenditures are influenced by various factors such as income level, aging, education level, health system characteristics, population disease pattern, technological advancements etc. Numerous studies have demonstrated that per capita income significantly affects health expenditures. Aging, with the increasing proportion of elderly individuals in the population, is another critical factor driving higher health expenditures. Education level influences health expenditures by shaping both the demand for healthcare services and health awareness. The structural characteristics of health systems, including service delivery, financing methods, and payment mechanisms, also play a crucial role in determining expenditures. The population’s disease profile, particularly the rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases, is another key factor contributing to increased health spending. Furthermore, factors such as health insurance systems, out-of-pocket expenditures, age dependency ratio, fertility rate, literacy rate, life expectancy, the number of doctors and hospitals, and the crude birth rate are also among the determinants of health expenditures. The literature includes studies examining these factors at both micro and macro levels using various methods [16, 50].

Although health expenditure data has been widely studied in the literature, a common feature across many studies is the presence of high variation and outliers in this data. The dynamics of health expenditures are shaped by the complex interactions of economic, demographic, and social factors, making high variation an inevitable characteristic. Additionally, disparities between countries, regions, or individuals frequently result in the presence of extreme values (outliers). These outliers often stem from sudden shifts in healthcare policies, unexpected economic shocks, or extraordinary circumstances. However, previous studies have often overlooked the challenge of addressing these outliers through robust statistical techniques, which can result in biased or unreliable estimates. This study aims to fill this gap in the literature by employing robust regression methods to model the determinants of health expenditures, offering a more flexible and reliable approach to dealing with outliers and high data variation. By doing so, this research provides more accurate and generalizable insights into the factors influencing health expenditures across countries. So, the research question is as follows:

Why should robust regression methods be applied to address outliers and high variation in cross-country health expenditure data, and what are the key determinants identified through these methods?

Materials and methods

Aim and scope

This study presents a robust regression approach aimed at identifying the determinants of health expenditure levels across countries. The data is from the year 2021. A total of 179 countries with accessible data are included in the scope of the study. The proposed process provides a comprehensive perspective on the determinants of health expenditures in the presence of outliers. The data were analyzed using the R software [31].

Data cleaning & transformation

Health Expenditure: Current health expenditure per capita, Purchasing Power Parities, PPP (current international $): Refers to per capita health spending, adjusted for purchasing power parity, and expressed in international dollars.

Education

Educational attainment at least completed upper secondary, population 25 +, total (%) (cumulative) represents the percentage of individuals aged 25 and older who have attained or completed upper secondary education.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP per capita, PPP (constant 2021 international $): This refers to GDP per capita adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP). PPP GDP is calculated by converting the gross domestic product into international dollars using PPP rates, where an international dollar has the same purchasing power over GDP as the U.S. dollar within the United States. GDP at purchaser’s prices represents the total value added by all resident producers in a country, including product taxes and subtracting any subsidies not included in product values. This calculation does not account for depreciation of assets or depletion of natural resources.

Percentage of the total population above 65 (AGE)

Population ages 65 and above as a percentage of the total population refers to the proportion of individuals aged 65 and older within the entire population. The population count includes all residents, regardless of legal status or citizenship.

Disease pattern

Epidemiologists categorize the disease burden into three main types: non-communicable diseases (NCDs), communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases, and injuries. The total disease burden is measured in Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) per year. DALYs quantify the overall burden of disease by accounting for both years of life lost due to premature death and years lived with a disability. One DALY represents one lost year of healthy life. It's important to highlight that this chart reflects the relative proportion of DALYs from each category. In the study we used the share of NCDs in total DALY as proxy for disease pattern in population. we use R studio for all the analysis.

Share of public health expenditure in total health expenditure (PBL)

It refers to the characteristic features of the health system. We use the share of public health expenditure in total health expenditure, which is a ratio that shows how much of the total health expenditure of a country or region is financed by the public sector. This ratio represents the proportion of health expenditures made by the public sector in relation to the total health expenditure.

We obtained the health expenditure, GDP, aging and public health expenditure data from World Bank Data, and disease pattern data from Global Health Data Exchange, both of which are reliable secondary data sources widely recognized for their accuracy and credibility.

The selection of explanatory variables in this study is based on variables commonly addressed in the literature and follows the framework outlined in Xu and his colleagues research [22]. These variables are frequently examined as key determinants of health expenditures, particularly focusing on economic and demographic factors. The choice of variables, such as GDP, aging, disease patterns, and public health expenditure, is well-supported by previous research, which emphasizes their role in shaping health expenditure dynamics. Therefore, the selected variables are deemed to effectively capture the key factors influencing health expenditures, contributing to a more robust understanding of the topic.

Estimation method

The first phase of the modeling process involves utilizing the information from the per capita health expenditure variable (in USD-PPP)"he"to identify independent variables for its determinants based on the literature. In the second phase, it was identified that the dependent variable distribution has high values, a wide variation, and outliers. To address this, the MM estimator, which is a combination of two basic estimation methods, M-estimation and Minimum Variance (MV) was used. The final robust regression estimation results were then obtained. In the study, missing values in the variable (n = 65) were handled using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method applied in the R software [43]. MICE is an advanced technique for handling missing data that generates multiple imputed datasets through an iterative process. This method predicts each missing value by modeling it with information from other observed variables in the dataset. The process consists of a set of regression models designed to estimate missing values by leveraging the connections between the observed variables. The procedure is repeated multiple times to generate various imputed datasets, which are analyzed and combined to provide unbiased estimates. In our study, the MICE algorithm was applied to predict the missing data, generating multiple possible values for each missing entry. These multiple imputations were combined to account for the uncertainty introduced by missing data. Generally, the number of iterations (often referred to as m or maxit) is between 5 and 50 iterations with common choices. We used 20 iterations to generate the imputations, and the results from these multiple imputations were combined to produce the final estimates, ensuring that the uncertainty of the imputation process was properly incorporated into the analysis. These multiple imputations reduce bias and uncertainty that might arise from single imputation techniques, leading to more robust statistical results.

Due to the presence of outliers in the model, the classical regression estimation method, (Ordinary Least Square) OLS coefficients, would be biased, leading to incorrect predictions. Therefore, robust regression results were obtained during the modeling phase. Robust regression is a method used to obtain reliable results when The conditions of classical linear regression are not met (especially in the presence of outliers or heteroscedasticity). This method reduces the model's sensitivity to outliers and provides more reliable predictions. Classical linear regression (OLS) is sensitive to outliers in the data and may perform poorly when assumptions such as normality and homoscedasticity are violated. Robust regression addresses these issues, particularly reducing the impact of outliers.

In the study, the robust regression technique MM-Estimation was used [29, 37]. It is an extension of the M-estimation method and is more resistant to strong outliers. It provides both high outlier protection and good accuracy [24]. MM estimation can produce reliable estimates even in datasets containing up to 50% outliers. This threshold is higher than that of many other robust regression methods. MM estimation also demonstrates efficiency close to that of the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method when the model assumptions hold. This ensures strong statistical performance, particularly in cases where the data distribution is approximately normal [42]. An alternative method, S-estimation, has a high breakdown point but lower statistical efficiency. Another alternative, Quantile Regression, is particularly useful when the dependent variable has an asymmetric distribution. However, compared to MM estimation, it provides less precise parameter estimates and primarily focuses on the distribution of variables rather than identifying outliers [8]. Given the presence of outliers in the dataset used in this study and the necessity of maintaining statistical efficiency, MM estimation is the most appropriate method. Instead of merely ignoring outliers, MM estimation minimizes their impact while simultaneously providing reliable estimates, making it the preferred approach. To assess the consistency of the MM estimation results, we used Weighted Least Squares (WLS), alternative robust approaches to compare results. WLS is employed to obtain more reliable estimates in the presence of heteroscedastic data. M-Estimator helps reduce the influence of outliers, providing more robust estimates. WLS (Weighted Least Squares) regression is employed as a method to provide more reliable estimates in the presence of heteroskedasticity, where the variance of the errors is not constant across observations. In this study, weights are assigned to each observation inversely proportional to its variance. To assess heteroskedasticity, the Breusch-Pagan Heteroskedasticity Test was conducted, and the results indicated the presence of heteroskedasticity in the data. Consequently, the application of WLS was deemed an appropriate choice to correct for this heteroskedastic structure.

In the weight calculation process, no specific proxy for the variance of each observation was used. Instead, WLS was directly employed to account for heteroskedasticity by weighting the observations accordingly. This approach aims to address the heteroskedastic structure in the data and enhance the reliability of the results.

In this method, outliers are not directly removed but instead, weights are assigned to the observations, reducing the influence of extreme values on the model parameters. As a result, the MM method prevents outliers from distorting the model's predictions, ensuring more reliable and robust results. This approach enhances the accuracy of the model by mitigating the negative effects of outliers on the overall trends.

The Table 3 presents regression results using Weighted Least Squares (WLS) and MM Estimator, showing how variables like GDP and age influence the dependent variable, with statistical significance indicated by p-values, and model fit assessed by R-squared and residual standard error; these metrics help evaluate the strength and reliability of the model's predictions.

The MM robust regression analysis highlights several important findings regarding the associations between economic and demographic factors and the dependent variable. The positive and statistically significant relationship between GDP and health expenditures (β = 0.0603, p < 0.01) suggests that higher economic capacity is linked to greater investments in healthcare infrastructure, services, and technologies. This association is consistent with existing literature, which frequently finds that wealthier countries tend to allocate more resources to healthcare, potentially improving access and quality. Similarly, the age variable demonstrates a significant and substantial positive relationship (β = 69.648, p < 0.01), indicating that countries with aging populations are generally associated with higher healthcare expenditures. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that older populations typically require more medical care, leading to increased healthcare spending. However, as this study is based on cross-sectional observational data, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than direct causal effects. This result is consistent with the growing demand for healthcare services in older populations, who are more likely to require medical attention due to the prevalence of chronic diseases and age-related conditions. It underscores the importance of demographic changes as a key driver of healthcare spending and the need for policies addressing the healthcare demands of aging societies. In contrast, education (β = 1.675), public health expenditure (β = 0.901), and the dependency ratio (β = − 4.9886) were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that their direct impact on the dependent variable may be less pronounced or context dependent. For instance, the effect of public expenditure could vary depending on how efficiently resources are allocated within the healthcare system or the type of healthcare policies in place. Similarly, the non-significant relationship for education may reflect indirect pathways through which education influences health outcomes, such as improved health literacy or preventive behaviors.

The results from the Weighted Least Squares (WLS) and MM-Estimator methods exhibit strong similarities. Both models show that GDP and age have significant positive impacts on the dependent variable, with GDP (β = 0.0535, p < 0.01) and age (β = 92.93, p < 0.01) demonstrating strong relationships with health expenditure. This reinforces the idea that economic growth and population aging are key factors influencing healthcare expenditures. Additionally, the adjusted R-squared value of 0.8154 in both models indicates that the models explain a large portion of the variance in healthcare expenditure. However, both models also found that education, public expenditure, and dependency ratio were not statistically significant, with p-values above 0.05, suggesting that their effects may be more complex or dependent on other contextual factors. This aligns with the WLS and MM-Estimator models, where similar variables did not show significant effects. The Wald test-statistic for the MM-Estimator (Statistic: 688.4, p < 0.01) also emphasizes the robustness of the findings in both models. Residual analysis indicates that both models generally align well with the observed data, though the presence of extreme residuals in the MM-Estimator (with a minimum of − 3325.1 and a maximum of 2757.0) suggests that certain country-specific factors may not be fully captured. These extreme residuals may reflect unmeasured variables or unique country-level factors influencing healthcare expenditures, such as healthcare system efficiency or specific policy approaches. Overall, the findings from both WLS and MM-Estimator provide valuable insights into the economic and demographic determinants of healthcare expenditure. They underscore the importance of economic capacity and population aging, while highlighting the complexity of other factors, such as education and public spending, in shaping healthcare systems globally. Additionally, when predictions were made using OLS, the t-statistics indicating the significance of variables such as AGE and GDP were smaller compared to the MM estimator. For instance, the GDP test statistic in the MM model was 26.222, while in the classical OLS it was 15.5. This demonstrates that the OLS model, due to its sensitivity to outliers, resulted in less reliable estimates. By providing these comparisons and diagnostic tests, we have shown that robust regression methods, such as MM, are more appropriate for this dataset.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a significant skewness and high variation in key variables, such as health expenditures and GDP per capita, which are consistent with similar studies in the literature [36, 50]). This variation highlights the complexity of cross-country comparisons, where health expenditure data often exhibit large fluctuations due to differing economic conditions, health policies, and demographic profiles [30]. As observed in previous studies, such as that by Boyd et al. [6], the presence of outliers in economic and health data is a common challenge that can lead to biased results if not properly addressed. The diagnostic tests conducted in this study—heteroskedasticity, normality, and multicollinearity—show that traditional Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression assumptions are violated. Specifically, these findings underscore the importance of using robust regression methods, as classical OLS regression is prone to inefficiencies and bias when these assumptions are not met. The use of robust regression techniques, such as the MM-estimator, has been advocated in similar contexts to mitigate these issues and produce more reliable and stable estimates. These results further confirm the necessity of robust methods when dealing with highly variable and non-normal data [2, 23] Moreover, the identification of outliers in the dataset using the MM algorithm supports the argument that such observations can significantly affect the results of regression models, as noted by Hadi [15] and more recently by Raza et al. [35]. By handling outliers appropriately, this study provides a more accurate representation of the relationships between health expenditures and key determinants, compared to traditional methods such as OLS.

In terms of the determinants of health expenditures, our results align with existing literature, particularly regarding the significant role of income (GDP per capita) and aging populations. Furuoka et al. [14] reported GDP and aging population as the two major variables of health expenditure in Asian countries. Numerous studies (e.g., [1], ; [19]) have consistently shown that higher income levels are positively associated with increased health expenditures, as wealthier countries can afford greater investments in healthcare.

Furthermore, the findings of this study are consistent with the research conducted by Farag et al. [11], who found that income is a crucial determinant of health expenditure in both high-income and middle-income countries. However, they noted that the relationship is not always linear, as countries at different income levels may have varying healthcare needs, policy priorities, and institutional frameworks that influence how income translates into health expenditure. This aligns with the results of this study, where income remains a consistent determinant across the robust models used, underscoring the importance of income in shaping healthcare spending across countries. Moreover, the relationship between GDP per capita and health expenditure may also be influenced by other factors, such as the structure of the healthcare system and government spending priorities. For example, countries with a stronger public health system might show a less pronounced increase in health expenditures with rising income, as public spending can absorb a significant portion of the cost. Conversely, in countries with more privatized healthcare systems, a rise in income might directly translate into higher out-of-pocket healthcare expenses, thus increasing overall health expenditure. For instance, Halıcı et al. [17] investigated the influence of health expenditure on economic growth. They used twenty-five high-income and nineteen low-income countries’ data and found that there is a positive relationship between government spending on health and economic growth, which also means that public health expenditures have a role of determination of economic growth along with health expenditure. It can be stated that if both private and public expenditures are raised, it also leads to an increase in the positive effect of health expenditures on economic growth because productivity of the labours depend upon their health status.

In this study, GDP per capita showed a significant and positive effect on health expenditures, confirming the results of these prior studies. This consistency across multiple robust regression models not only reinforces the validity of the findings but also highlights the robustness of income as a key determinant in health expenditure models. The strong relationship observed in this study suggests that as countries experience economic growth, they are more likely to increase investments in healthcare, whether through public or private channels. However, it is important to note that while income plays a significant role, it is not the sole determinant of health expenditure. Future research could examine how income interacts with these other variables to provide a more nuanced understanding of the factors driving health expenditures across countries. Additionally, the reliance on cross-sectional data for 2021 means that temporal variations and long-term trends are not considered, which could provide a more comprehensive understanding of health expenditure dynamics using robust methods.

Aging, or the increasing proportion of elderly individuals in a population, has long been identified as one of the most significant determinants of health expenditures. As populations age, the demand for healthcare services typically increases due to the higher incidence of chronic diseases, disabilities, and general healthcare needs associated with older age [7, 22]. For instance, Yang and colleagues examined the health care use of 25.994 elderly for six years. Their results confirmed strongly that monthly health care expenditures for elderly people do increase substantially with age [49]. In this study, the results consistently show that aging is a significant determinant of health expenditures, which aligns with theoretical expectations and existing literature. The relationship between aging and health expenditures can be understood through the concept of age-related health costs. As populations grow older, healthcare systems are required to allocate more resources to address the specific needs of elderly individuals, including increased hospital admissions, long-term care, and specialized medical treatments [7, 21]. In terms of statistical findings, our study supports these conclusions. In this study, the consistent identification of aging as a significant determinant across all robust regression models highlights the importance of demographic changes in shaping health expenditure patterns. The robust nature of this finding, observed across both MM-estimator and WLS models, underscores the reliability of the results and supports the growing body of evidence that links aging populations to higher healthcare spending.

The consistent finding that aging is a key determinant of health expenditures has important policy implications. Policymakers must consider the financial implications of aging populations and adopt strategies to ensure the sustainability of healthcare systems. This may involve reforms such as increasing healthcare efficiency, promoting preventive care to reduce chronic conditions, and expanding long-term care services to meet the needs of elderly individuals. Additionally, future research could examine the effectiveness of specific healthcare system models in mitigating the impact of aging on health expenditures. For example, comparing countries with different healthcare delivery systems (e.g., public vs. private or universal vs. targeted care) could provide valuable insights into which approaches are most effective in addressing the healthcare needs of aging populations while controlling costs.

However, the findings regarding education, public health, and disease patterns, which failed to show statistically significant effects on health expenditures in this study. One potential explanation for the non-significant results for education and public health is that, while these factors are important for improving population health, their direct impact on health expenditure may be more indirect and nuanced. Education and public health investments interventions typically have long-term effects on outcomes, which may not immediately translate into higher health spending. For instance, an educated population may be better at managing health conditions through prevention and healthier lifestyles, thus reducing the need for costly healthcare interventions in the short term [32, 33]. Similarly, public health programs that focus on prevention and health promotion can lower the incidence of diseases over time, potentially reducing overall healthcare spending. Therefore, the immediate effect of these variables on health expenditures might be less pronounced compared to more direct determinants like income and aging.

Additionally, existing literature has reported both significant and non-significant findings regarding the impact of education and public health on health expenditures. Some studies have found that these variables play a crucial role in reducing healthcare costs, particularly in the long term, by improving population health outcomes and reducing preventable diseases (e.g., [50]) However, some studies have struggled to demonstrate a direct relationship, suggesting that the effects of these variables may be mediated by other factors or may not be immediately observable in cross-sectional data (e.g., [12, 22, 45]. This study's non-significant results align with these latter findings, where the influence of education and public health on health expenditures is either too indirect or too delayed to manifest in short-term models. In light of these considerations, it is possible that the effects of non-significant variables on health expenditures are not adequately captured within the scope of this study’s model. Future research could explore these relationships using longitudinal data or more sophisticated modeling techniques to account for the longer-term and more complex effects of these factors on healthcare spending.

Conclusion

This study underscores the importance of robust statistical approaches in addressing the challenges posed by high variation and outliers in health expenditure data. Our findings revealed that the dataset includes a high degree of variability and several influential outlier observations. Furthermore, none of the assumptions required for classical regression models—such as normality, homoscedasticity, were met in the health expenditure data. As a result, we adopted robust regression techniques (WLS and MM-estimator) as a more appropriate methodological approach. Importantly, in line with theoretical expectations, income level (GDP per capita) and aging were consistently identified as statistically significant determinants of health expenditures across all robust models. This consistency confirms the robustness and validity of our methodological approach in addressing the research question.

By employing the MM-estimation technique, the analysis reveals that GDP per capita, and the elderly population ratio are significant and strong determinants of health expenditures, highlighting the critical influence of economic performance and demographic structure on healthcare spending. In contrast, variables such as education level, public health expenditure level, and disease patterns are not statistically significant in this model, suggesting that they have limited explanatory power in determining health expenditures in the context of this study. This lack of significance may be attributed to several factors. First, there could be measurement limitations or data constraints, such as inaccuracies in reporting or variations in how these variables are quantified across countries. Second, the relationship between these variables and health expenditures may be more complex or indirect than initially assumed, meaning they might not have a direct or linear impact on health expenditures. Additionally, other unobserved factors, such as governance quality, healthcare infrastructure, or cultural differences, might play a significant role in shaping health expenditures, but were not included in the model. Future research could explore these potential influences and refine the analysis by incorporating more detailed data or alternative methodologies.

The research findings have important implications for policymakers. First, promoting economic growth should remain a priority, as higher GDP is associated with increased health expenditures, which may improve access to and quality of healthcare services. However, it is important to note that this relationship is not necessarily causal, and higher GDP could also result from other factors such as a healthier workforce or other economic dynamics. Second, the significant role of the elderly population ratio highlights the need for targeted policies to address the healthcare needs of aging populations. This could include expanding geriatric care services, investing more in preventive healthcare for older adults, and developing sustainable financing mechanisms to manage rising healthcare costs associated with demographic shifts.

These findings offer crucial insights for future research, particularly in exploring how healthcare accessibility, insurance coverage, and other institutional factors influence health expenditures. Additionally, the study advocates for the broader application of robust regression techniques in health economics to generate more reliable results and support evidence-based policy decisions that can be more adaptable to diverse healthcare systems.

We acknowledge several limitations in our data and methods. One potential limitation of this study is the issue of endogeneity and omitted variable bias. While GDP and aging are key factors influencing health expenditures, other economic and institutional variables, such as inflation, governance, and healthcare infrastructure, could also play a significant role. Also, the use of secondary data may have limitations related to the timeliness of information. To address these issues, we carefully selected reliable data sources, such as the World Bank Data and Global Health Data Exchange, ensuring they are widely recognized for their accuracy. Due to data limitations and the scope of this study, these variables were not included in the model. Future research could address this limitation by incorporating additional variables to better account for endogeneity and omitted variable bias, which would further strengthen the analysis and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of health expenditures. In this study, outlier observations were identified and down-weighted using a robust regression method. However, the residual analysis indicates that some countries deviate significantly from the model’s predictions. This may be due to country-specific factors that were not included in the model. Therefore, a limitation of this study is that the countries with large residuals were not explicitly identified, and the potential reasons for these deviations were not thoroughly examined. Future research could address this issue by analyzing these countries in detail and evaluating additional variables that could be incorporated into the model.

Finally, this study utilizes data from a single year (2021), which provides a snapshot of health expenditures across countries. However, this cross-sectional approach may be influenced by the unique circumstances of that specific year, particularly the ongoing impact of the COVID- 19 pandemic on health expenditure. The paper does not address potential pandemic-related anomalies or outliers in the data, which could have skewed the results. Additionally, the use of a single year's data limits the ability to infer trends or causal relationships over time. Future research should consider using panel data from multiple years to better capture temporal trends and mitigate the influence of year-specific anomalies, including those related to exceptional events like the COVID- 19 pandemic.
