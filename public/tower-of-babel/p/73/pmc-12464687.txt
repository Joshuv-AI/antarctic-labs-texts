MilM from mildiomycin biosynthesis is an oxygen-, pyridoxal phosphate-dependent arginine hydroxylase
Authors: Kairel EK Edwards, Megan E Wolf, Lindsay D Eltis, Katherine S Ryan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMildiomycin, a potent antifungal, is biosynthesized through a complex pathway involving the enzyme MilM. This study reassigns MilM as an oxygen (O2)-, pyridoxal phosphate–dependent hydroxylase that hydroxylates the l-arginine–derived side chain of mildiomycin at the C4 position. This activity is conserved with the homologs MppP from enduracididine biosynthesis and RohP from azomycin biosynthesis. We found MilM effectively hydroxylates ʟ-arginine via both a two- and four-electron oxidation pathway in a O2-dependent manner. We also show the hydroxyl group originates from H2O, and the reaction mechanism transitions through conjugated quinonoid intermediates. The O2-, pyridoxal phosphate–dependent transformation of l-arginine catalyzed by MilM ultimately yields the 5-guanidino-2,4-dihydroxyvalerate side chain of mildiomycin. Recharacterization of this essential step in mildiomycin helps elucidate the full pathway of mildiomycin production.Keywords: pyridoxal phosphate, oxidase, mildiomycin, arginine, biosynthetic pathway, enzyme mechanism

The biosynthesis of natural products frequently involves complex enzymatic transformations, and the cofactor pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP) plays a critical role in a large number of such transformations (1). Approximately 4% of all known enzymatic activities are PLP-dependent, particularly those involving amino acid metabolism (2). PLP is required to catalyze various reactions such as transaminations, decarboxylations, racemizations, β-substitutions/eliminations, and γ-substitutions/eliminations. As such, PLP-dependent enzymes are particularly indispensable in natural product biosynthesis (3). In the resting state, PLP covalently binds to the enzyme via a Schiff-base linkage with the ε-amino group of an active site lysine residue to form what is known as the internal aldimine. Upon substrate binding, transimination displaces the lysine residue, forming an external aldimine, which is followed by removal of the proton, carboxylate group, or side chain at α-C, generating a quinonoid intermediate (Fig. S1).

The quinonoid intermediate is a carbanion, and, like all carbanions, is highly reactive with electrophiles, including molecular oxygen (O2) (4). Historically, the interaction of O2 with PLP-dependent enzymes was thought to be limited to paracatalytic side reactions. For example, in addition to its main function as a decarboxylase, the PLP-dependent Escherichia coli glutamate decarboxylase also catalyzes the reaction of O2 and l-glutamic acid to form succinic semialdehyde. Similarly, mammalian dopa decarboxylase, a PLP-dependent enzyme central to neurotransmitter biosynthesis, also displays O2 paracatalytic activity (5, 6). It catalyzes the oxidative deamination of transient quinonoid intermediates to aldehydes such as 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetaldehyde (6). Recent discoveries have shown that certain PLP-dependent enzymes utilize O2 as a primary cosubstrate in their catalytic processes (2, 3). Some of the most recently described examples of PLP enzymes that use O2 as a primary cosubstrate include the lincosamide biosynthetic enzyme CcbF and the monooxygenase-decarboxylase Cap15, both of which harness O2 for their primary activities (Fig. 1A) (7, 8).

Arginine oxidases are a fascinating group of enzymes which are dependent on both PLP and O2. These enzymes, which utilize l-arginine (l-Arg), play key roles in the early steps of assembly of diverse, heterocycle-containing natural products like indolmycin, azomycin, and enduracididine (9, 10, 11). Arginine oxidases are divided into two groups: desaturases, exemplified by Ind4 and Plu4; and hydroxylases, exemplified by MppP and RohP (Fig. 1C) (12). Both groups of enzymes produce 2-ketoarginine from consumption of a single O2 molecule, but generate different products upon consumption of two molecules of O2: the desaturases form didehydroarginine (2-amino-5-guanidinopenta-2,4-dienoate) (10, 12), whereas the hydroxylases produce (S)-4-hydroxy-2-ketoarginine (9, 13). The desaturases and hydroxylases are proposed to share initial mechanistic steps. Spectroscopic studies on Ind4 revealed that the quinonoid directly reacts with O2 early in the reaction cycle (10). Later in the reaction cycles, the enzymes are thought to form a conjugated intermediate, which can be attacked by water to give (S)-4-hydroxy-2-ketoarginine, or released to give didehydroarginine. The presence of the water molecule in the active site and its ability to facilitate nucleophilic attack are thought to depend on enzyme dynamics, which govern the conformation of the active site (12). When the water molecule is preorganized in the active site, it undergoes an enantioselective nucleophilic attack at the C4 position of l-Arg from the pro-S face, leading to the hydroxylated product. Structural studies via X-ray crystallography confirm this stereochemical outcome in both RohP and MppP (9, 13).

The milM gene in the mildiomycin (MIL) biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) encodes a homolog of the arginine oxidases (14). MIL is a potent antimicrobial peptidyl nucleoside antibiotic produced by Streptoverticillium rimofaciens B-98891, effective against powdery mildew, a significant fungal pathogen affecting cereals and ornamental plants (15). The MIL BGC spans approximately 20 kb and contains 17 genes (denoted milA through milQ) (14). Gene deletion experiments have demonstrated that all genes within this cluster, except for milL, are essential for MIL production. Of the 17 genes within the cluster, only four of the encoded enzymes have been experimentally characterized: MilA, MilB, MilC, and MilO. MilO is a LuxR family protein that modulates MIL production by directly regulating the transcription of milA (16). MilA catalyzes the conversion of CMP into 5-hydroxymethyl CMP, while MilB hydrolyzes 5-hydroxymethyl CMP or CMP into hydroxymethylcytosine or cytosine, respectively (17, 18). These intermediates are subsequently coupled with UDP-glucuronic acid by MilC to form 5-hydroxymethyl cytosylglucuronic acid or cytosylglucuronic acid, respectively (14). Despite these insights, the subsequent steps in MIL biosynthesis remain poorly understood. Genetic and bioinformatic analyses have proposed roles for several other proteins in the pathway. MilD and MilG are predicted to function as a transaminase and a cytosylglucuronate decarboxylase, respectively, contributing to the conversion of the C4′ hydroxyl group to an amino group and the formation of the C2′, C3′ double bond of the pyranose ring (16). MilM and MilN are hypothesized to modify and attach the arginine side chain, while MilH and MilI are proposed to activate and load the serine side chain (Fig. 2).

MIL is characterized by its 5-guanidino-2,4-dihydroxyvalerate group, which is derived from l-Arg (14). Initially, it was hypothesized that 4-hydroxyarginine serves as a substrate in MIL biosynthesis. However, feeding experiments using labeled hydroxyarginine in S. rimofaciens cultures revealed no significant incorporation into MIL. Consequently, it was proposed that hydroxylation of the guanidino side chain occurs later in the biosynthetic sequence, after attachment to the pyranoside ring.

MilM was initially annotated as an aminotransferase based on its sequence homology, and its function was hypothesized to involve conversion of l-Arg to its α-ketoacid derivative. However, it has a high degree of sequence similarity to arginine oxidases, such as RohP and MppP, which perform O2-dependent hydroxylation of l-Arg (9, 11). Global pairwise sequence alignments revealed a 60% identity between MilM and RohP, and a 37% identity between MilM and MppP, with fully conserved active site residues (Fig. S3). Phylogenetic studies (Fig. 1B) also revealed strong evolutionary relationships among these enzymes, suggesting that MilM likely performs a similar hydroxylation reaction. Additionally, the MIL BGC lacks alternative candidate hydroxylases, further suggesting that MilM carries out this transformation (14).

Here, we provide biochemical validation for MilM as an arginine oxidase, establishing its role in the O2-dependent hydroxylation of l-Arg. The experimental framework draws heavily from previously characterized arginine hydroxylases (9, 11, 13); nonetheless, the biochemical characterization of MilM remains relevant as it expands the known family of l-Arg oxidases and reveals a new step in MIL biosynthesis, requiring a reassessment of how MIL is assembled.

Results

Activity of MilM on arginine

Prior to activity assays, MilM was reconstituted with PLP and tested for cofactor occupancy. HPLC-based quantification using hydroxylamine derivatization revealed that 23 μM PLP was bound per 25 μM MilM, consistent with near-stoichiometric cofactor incorporation (Fig. S4). This result indicates efficient formation of the internal aldimine and validates that the enzyme was PLP-loaded for all subsequent assays.

To probe MilM’s enzymatic function, we tested the activity of purified MilM on l-Arg in aerobic conditions. Following overnight incubation at room temperature (RT), l-Arg was completely consumed in the presence of MilM or RohP, an enzyme which efficiently hydroxylates l-Arg (Fig. 3A) (9). No significant l-Arg consumption was detected in either heat-inactivated MilM or no-enzyme controls (Fig. 3A). This result suggests that MilM, like RohP, reacts directly with l-Arg in aerobic conditions. The products of the MilM-catalyzed reaction had m/z values of 146.0924, 162.0875, 174.0876, and 190.0823, which match the theoretical [M + H]+ of 4-guanidinobutyric acid (C5H12N3O2+), 3-hydroxy-4-guanidinobutyric acid (C5H12N3O3+), 2-ketoarginine (C6H12N3O3+), and 4-hydroxy-2-ketoarginine (C6H12N3O4+), respectively (Fig. 3B). In the presence of catalase, which reacts with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), only ions at 190.0823 and 174.0876 were observed, indicating that 2 and 4 are the primary products of the enzymatic reaction, and that these undergo decarboxylation in the presence of H2O2 to yield 3 and 5, respectively (Fig. 4B).

NMR analysis of the MilM-catalyzed reaction products revealed the presence of 3 and 5 in the reaction mixture (Fig. S5), indicating that the products of the reaction are 2 and 4, which undergo nonenzymatic conversion to 3 and 5, respectively, in the presence of H2O2. The configuration of the hydroxyl group in 2 (and consequently, in 3) remained undetermined. However, we hypothesize that the configuration is likely to be (S), similar to the products of the MppP and RohP reactions, based on the conservation of active site residues between MilM and these enzymes (Fig. S3) (9, 13).

Kinetics and stoichiometry of arginine oxidation by MilM

We hypothesized that MilM requires O2 for activity in the same way as RohP (9). Accordingly, we measured O2 consumption of the MilM-catalyzed reaction with l-Arg. First, we optimized the pH of reaction (Fig. 4A). MilM exhibited the highest rate of O2 consumption in 100 mM MOPS buffer at pH 7.2 with an ionic strength of 0.1 M. Under optimized conditions, we examined the stoichiometry of O2 consumption in the MilM-catalyzed reaction by measuring O2 depletion rates in the presence and absence of catalase. The observed rate of O2 consumption was 15 μM/min. Upon addition of catalase, which converts two molecules of H2O2 into one molecule of O2, the rate of O2 consumption decreased to 7 μM/min (Fig. 4B). This reduction reflects the recycling of a portion of the H2O2 product back into O2 via catalase, thereby offsetting the net O2 consumption. The extent of this reversal is consistent with a 1:1 stoichiometry in which each O2 molecule consumed by MilM is converted to one molecule of H2O2. These results support the conclusion that H2O2 is the primary oxidized product of O2 in the MilM reaction.

To confirm this stoichiometry, we conducted further experiments where we incubated 2.5 μM MilM with 300 μM ʟ-Arg, alongside 0.25 mg/ml horseradish peroxidase (type VI) and 1 mM 2,2′-azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) and quenched the reaction upon consumption of 100 μM O2. Under these conditions, we observed the production of 99 ± 2 μM of H2O2, confirming a stoichiometric conversion of O2 to H2O2. Additionally, we found that 86 ± 6 μM of ʟ-Arg was consumed concomitantly with the consumption of 100 μM O2. These data suggest that the conversion of ʟ-Arg requires more than one equivalent of O2 and thus, the existence of two oxidation reactions, similar to those catalyzed by RohP. In one reaction, ʟ-Arg undergoes a two-electron oxidation with the consumption of one molecule of O2. In the second reaction, arginine undergoes a four-electron oxidation, consuming two molecules of O2. These two reactions yield distinct products, such as 4 for the first reaction and a more oxidized product, such as 2, for the second reaction. Both 2 and 4 are the more abundant ions compared to the carboxylated species, suggesting that the ketoacid acid may be the primary intermediate in the subsequent biosynthetic step. Mass spectrometry analysis further supports distinct oxidation patterns between MilM and RohP (9). In both enzymes, the oxidation pathway requiring less O2 is favored over the hydroxylated pathway. However, this preference is weaker in MilM (1.3:1) than RohP (2:1), indicating that MilM promotes the hydroxylated product formation to a greater extent.

We next evaluated the steady-state kinetic parameters MilM for ʟ-Arg and O2. The Km of MilM for ʟ-Arg was 16.8 ± 0.9 μM and the kcat was 5.6 ± 0.1 min-1 in air-saturated buffer ([O2] ∼300 μM) (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, in the presence of 500 μM ʟ-Arg, MilM exhibited a Km of 15.5 ± 2 μM and a kcat of 5.9 ± 0.1 min-1 for O2 (Fig. 4D). These values are on the same order of magnitude with previously reported kinetic parameters of the arginine oxidases, indicating conserved catalytic behavior (Table S2) (9, 10, 12).

Origin of hydroxyl group

To assess whether MilM follows the same hydroxylation mechanism as its homologs, we conducted H218O-labeling experiments previously used to study RohP and MppP (9, 13). The MilM-catalyzed reaction was carried out in 50% H218O (Fig. 3C). In addition to the peaks identified in the unlabeled reaction (m/z 190.0823 (2), m/z 162.0875 (3), m/z 174.0876 (4), and m/z 146.0924 (5) (Fig. 2C);), additional peaks were detected on the mass spectrum. Each of 2, 3, 4, and 5 exhibited +2 peaks indicative of labeled carbonyl oxygens from the H218O-labeled imine hydrolysis (Fig. 1B). However, only 2 and 3 have accompanying +4 peaks suggesting that two labels are present—one label from hydrolysis of the imine product by H218O, as in 4 and 5, and the second label from the incorporation of H218O into the hydroxyl group. These data are consistent with the stoichiometric 1:1 conversion of O2 to H2O2. Altogether, these results clearly demonstrate that the hydroxyl group in 2 and 3 originate from water and not O2 or H2O2.

O2-dependent formation of quinonoid intermediates in the MilM-catalyzed reaction

To provide insight into the possible mechanism of the MilM-catalyzed reaction, we performed UV-visible spectroscopy analysis under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. The spectrum of the MilM-catalyzed reaction displayed a peak at λmax 417 nm, which corresponds to the internal aldimine formed between MilM and PLP. In air-saturated buffer, following the addition of 250 μM l-Arg, we recorded a marked decline in absorbance at this wavelength (Fig. 5B). Simultaneously, the appearance of a peak at 515 nm was detected, consistent with the generation of a quinonoid intermediate (Q1) (Fig. 5A). An additional peak at 563 nm was observed, initially increasing before declining after 5 min. Notably, this transient peak at 563 nm is consistent with previous reports of peaks at 563 nm for RohP (9) and 567 nm for Ind4 (10), both assigned as conjugated quinonoid species. These results imply that MilM transitions through a more conjugated quinonoid intermediate (Q2) during its catalytic cycle, with a λmax at 563 nm.

In the absence of O2, following addition of 250 μM l-Arg, the λmax at 417 nm, attributed to the internal aldimine, rapidly decreased (Fig. 5C). In contrast, only an increase in absorbance at 515 nm was observed over the 10-min incubation. This observation indicates that the formation of Q2 is dependent on the presence of O2.

Discussion

This study provides experimental and biochemical validation for the proposed function of MilM as an l-Arg hydroxylase, expanding the family of known l-Arg oxidases. We demonstrate that MilM catalyzes the identical reaction as MppP and RohP, that is, the O2-dependent hydroxylation of l-Arg’s C4 position, producing 4-hydroxy-2-ketoarginine. Kinetic and stoichiometric analyses reveal MilM’s catalytic parameters are consistent with those of related arginine oxidases. These results highlight conserved mechanistic features within this enzyme family while affirming MilM’s functional similarity to RohP and MppP.

The conserved mechanistic features of the O2-PLP–dependent enzymes that hydroxylate l-Arg extend to the origin of the hydroxyl group and the nature of the intermediates (Fig. 6A). Thus, the 50% H218O labeling experiments unequivocally establish that water is the source of the hydroxyl group in MilM’s reaction product, consistent with the mechanism of the other O2-dependent arginine hydroxylases (9, 13). UV-visible spectroscopy analysis of MilM revealed that the internal aldimine (λmax = 417 nm) and the quinonoid (Q1, λmax = 515 nm) formed readily under anaerobic conditions. However, the lack of a peak at 563 nm in the absence of O2 indicates that Q2 is not generated without molecular O2. This result aligns with observations reported for Ind4 (10) and reflects the general mechanistic feature of this enzyme subclass.

The activities of the arginine hydroxylase MilM.A, MilM catalyzes a four-electron oxidation and hydroxylation, yielding 4-hydroxy-2-ketoarginine. This intermediate preferentially leads to the formation of mildiomycin. B, the incorporation of 2-ketoarginine, a secondary product of MilM, likely results in the formation of an alternative product, mildiomycin D.

Our findings provide evidence for the probable origin of the 5-guanidino-2,4-dihydroxyvalerate side chain in MIL. Prior labeling experiments using [guanidino-13C]-4-hydroxyarginine demonstrated no significant incorporation into MIL, indicating it is not a direct substrate (14). Our data support this conclusion and identify 4-hydroxy-2-ketoarginine as the likely substrate, as recently hypothesized by Li et al. (16), based on sequence homology with arginine oxidases (10). Notably, while the MIL BGC encodes several enzymes involved in the formation of the pyranoside core and side chain modifications, including MilA, MilB, and MilC, which process the cytosylglucuronic acid moiety, as well as MilD and MilG, which contribute to the formation of the C2′, C3′ double bond, none of these enzymes have hydroxylase activity (14). The absence of any alternative candidate for guanidino-side chain hydroxylation in the MIL BGC strongly suggests that this transformation is exclusively performed by MilM. This assignment is further supported by the proposed function of MilN, which is thought to catalyze the coupling of the modified arginine side chain to the pyranoside core. If another hydroxylase were present, the timing and specificity of the reaction would likely differ, impacting the final structure of MIL. In addition, the biosynthesis of a structural analog of MIL, MIL-D (19), further informs this hypothesis. MIL-D is structurally identical to MIL except that the hydroxyl group at the C4 position of the side chain is replaced with a proton, suggesting that its biosynthesis involves 2-ketoarginine as the precursor. This difference highlights the substrate specificity of MilN, as 2-ketoarginine would likely result in the formation of the product MIL-D instead of MIL (Fig. 6B).

Altogether, our findings collectively establish MilM as a member of the l-Arg oxidase family, with a function consistent with other O2-dependent arginine hydroxylases like MppP and RohP (9, 11). This work further elaborates the biosynthetic pathway of MIL, filling a gap in our understanding of how its 5-guanidino-2,4-dihydroxyvalerate side chain is formed. By clarifying MilM’s role, we refine the enzymatic steps involved in MIL biosynthesis and expand our knowledge of PLP-dependent enzyme functions.

Experimental procedures

General methods

Primers were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies. Recombinant plasmid sequencing was carried out by DNA Sequencing and BioAnalyzer Core Facilities, Centre for Molecular Medicine and Therapeutics, The University of British Columbia. Reagents were purchased from Alfa Aesar, Anatrace, Bio Basic Inc, Gold Biotechnology, Hampton Research, MilliporeSigma, New England BioLabs, Thermo Fisher Scientific, and VWR International. Chelating Sepharose Fast Flow resin and HiLoad Superdex 16/600 Superdex column 200 pg size exclusion column was purchased from GE Healthcare. HPLC was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity Technologies HPLC apparatus equipped with a Luna C18(2), 5 μm, 4.6 mm ID × 250 mm column (Phenomenex). Mass spectrometry analysis was performed with an Agilent 6546 liquid chromatography quadrupole time of flight (LC/Q-TOF) mass spectrometer operated in positive ion mode. All solvents used for HPLC or LC/Q-TOF mass spectrometry were Sigma-Aldrich HPLC grade or EMD Millipore LC-MS grade. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AV-600 spectrometer with a 5 mm CPTCI cryoprobe. 1H chemical shifts are referenced to the residual deuterium oxide (D2O) (δ 4.79 ppm). O2 consumption was monitored using a clark-type polarographic O2 electrode (Hansatech).

Cloning, expression, and purification

The gene encoding MilM from S. rimofaciens ZJU5119 (accession no.: JN999998) was synthesized by Bio Basic Inc. DNA coding regions of MilM was optimized for expression in E. coli and synthesized into the pUC57-Amp vector. This gene was cloned into pET28a downstream of the T7 promoter, replacing the region between the NdeI and EcoRI sites, using Gibson Assembly to give pET28a-MilM. The resulting vector was transformed into chemically competent E. coli DH5α by heat shock followed by selection on lysogeny broth (10 g NaCl, 10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, 10 g agar per 1 L water) plates containing kanamycin (50 μg/ml). Colonies were screened by colony PCR with T7 primers (Table S1). Plasmids from successful colonies were purified using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit from Qiagen and confirmed by sequencing. Successful plasmids were transformed into chemically competent E. coli BL21 (DE3) by heat shock. For protein production of recombinant MilM, transformed E. coli cell cultures were grown in lysogeny broth (37 °C, 200 rpm) containing kanamycin (50 μg/ml), to an absorbance at 600 nm of 0.4 to 0.6, and then cooled down to 16 °C for 30 min. Protein expression was induced with 100 μM IPTG and cultured further for an additional 18 h (16 °C, 130 rpm). Cells were harvested by centrifugation (4 °C, 3000×g, 10 min) then frozen at −20 °C until protein purification. For purification of MilM, the cells were thawed and resuspended in 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) buffer then lysed by sonication (25% amplitude, 3 s on/6 s off cycles, 6 min). The lysate was then centrifuged (4 °C, 33,746×g, 45 min) to remove insoluble materials. The lysate was then loaded onto an immobilized metal affinity chromatography column containing ∼2.5 ml Chelating Sepharose Fast Flow resin charged with Ni2+. The lysate was gravity filtered through the resin, and the resin was washed with 20 ml of 20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) buffer. The column was then washed with 5 ml volumes of 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) containing 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 300, and 500 mM imidazole in a stepwise manner to elute the protein. The MilM-containing fractions were visibly yellow and primarily eluted at 100 to 300 mM imidazole. Protein purity was confirmed by 10% SDS-PAGE, followed by Coomassie Blue staining (Fig. S2A). The fractions containing MilM were then pooled and concentrated to a volume of ∼5 ml using an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal filter (30,000 molecular weight (MW) cut-off, EMD-Millipore). The concentrated fraction was loaded onto a HiLoad Superdex 16/600 Superdex column pre-equilibrated with 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) buffer. The protein was eluted using a flow rate of 1 ml/min. The fractions containing purified MilM were pooled and dialyzed against 20 mM Hepes, 250 μM PLP (pH 7.5) for 16 h. Excess PLP was removed by further dialysis with 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5). Finally, MilM was concentrated to ∼20 mg/ml (∼450 μM) by centrifugation with an ultracentrifugal filter and stored in 10% glycerol at −70 °C. SDS-PAGE was used to assess the purity of resulting protein (Fig. S2B).

Quantification of PLP bound to MilM

Reactions were carried out in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) containing either 25 μM MilM or 25 μM PLP in the presence of 0.1 M hydroxylamine (NH2OH). Reaction mixtures (final volume: 50 μl) were incubated at RT for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged, and 20 μl of the resulting supernatant was subjected to HPLC analysis. Reaction products were analyzed by reversed-phase HPLC on a ZORBAX StableBond C18 column, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm at a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min. The elution was as follows for mobile phase composition of 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) and acetonitrile (v/v): 98:2, 0 to 10 min; linear gradient to 70:30, 10 to 12 min; 70:30, 12 to 24 min. Detection of PLP and PLP-oxime peaks were monitored at 388 nm and 330 nm, respectively. Reactions were carried out in triplicate and the data indicate efficient reconstitution of the PLP cofactor with MilM, with 23 μM PLP bound per 25 μM enzyme, consistent with near-stoichiometric formation of the internal aldimine (Fig. S4).

In vitro biochemical assays and product analysis

HPLC in vitro reactions were conducted using a reaction mixture comprising 30 μM MilM and 1 mM l-Arg dissolved in a 20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) buffer. The reaction proceeded for 16 h at RT within a total volume of 100 μl. HPLC analysis of the reaction was carried out following precolumn derivatization with dansyl-chloride (DNS-Cl). A reaction mixture of 50 μl was treated with 80 mM Li2CO3 (40 μl), CH3CN (70 μl), and 3 mg/ml DNS-Cl dissolved in CH3CN (25 μl). The reaction was conducted for 1 h at RT, and then 40 μl of 2% ethylamine was added to the mixture to react with excess DNS-Cl. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged, and 20 μl of the resulting supernatant was subjected to HPLC analysis. Elution was carried out at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min utilizing a mobile phase comprising a linear gradient of water and acetonitrile ((v/v): 95:5, 0–15 min; 50:50, 15–20 min; 0:100, 20–23 min; 95:5, 23–25 min) with both solvents containing 0.05% (v/v) TFA. DNS-Arg was detected at a wavelength of 330 nm. Initial in vitro assays for electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) analysis contained 10 μM MilM, 1 mM l-Arg in 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) buffer in a 100 μl reaction mixture. Later in vitro assays contained 10 μM MilM, 1 mM l-Arg, and 0.025 mg/ml catalase in 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) buffer in a 250 μl reaction mixture. These reactions were incubated at RT for 4 and 6 h, respectively, and then quenched with equivolume methanol. Precipitated protein was removed by centrifugation, following which 10 μl of the supernatant was subjected to LC/Q-TOF mass spectrometry analysis.

NMR analysis of MilM-catalyzed reaction products

The final reaction mixture (5 ml) contained 35 μM MilM and 10 mM l-Arg in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2). The mixture was incubated in an unsealed 50 ml vial at RT (11 h, 130 rpm). The initial concentration of MilM in the reaction mixture was 10 μM, with subsequent additions of 2 μM occurring hourly until reaching a final concentration of 24 μM. At the 8-h mark, the concentration of MilM added the reaction was increased to 35 μM and then allowed to proceed uninterrupted until the 11-h endpoint. The solvent was then evaporated overnight using a SpeedVac plus vacuum concentrator. The dried solids were resuspended in 3 × 200 μl of D2O and centrifuged to remove any residual undissolved solids prior to NMR analysis. All spectra were acquired utilizing a Bruker Avance 600 MHz spectrophotometer.

Steady-state kinetics for the MilM-catalyzed reaction

Kinetics assays were performed by monitoring O2 consumption using a Clark-type polarographic O2 electrode (Hansatech). Calibration of the electrode was performed daily using air-saturated water and sodium hydrosulfite following the manufacturer’s instructions. The standard reaction was performed in 1 ml of air-saturated 100 mM Mops buffer (I = 0.1 M, pH 7.2) at 25 °C. The reaction chamber was pre-equilibrated with buffer and 500 μM l-Arg mixtures before initiating the reaction with the addition of 1 μM MilM. Reaction rates were measured from the slope at maximum velocity, averaged over at least 1 min, and corrected by subtracting the average background O2 consumption rates prior to reaction initiation. The effect of pH on the rate of the MilM-catalyzed reaction was evaluated using air-saturated 100 mM buffers (I = 0.1 M) of MES (pH 6.7), Mops (pH 7.2), Mops (pH 7.5), TES (pH 8.0), and Ches (pH 8.5) and Ches (pH 9.0). The study elucidated the steady-state kinetic characteristics of MilM with respect to l-Arg under normoxic conditions (less than 3 p.p.m. O2), employing a concentration range of l-Arg (5−503 μM) and 1 μM MilM. Concurrently, the investigation examined MilM's steady-state kinetic properties relative to O2, utilizing 1.7 to 442 μM O2 at a fixed l-Arg concentration of 500 μM. O2 levels were controlled by introducing O2-N2 gas mixtures into the reaction chamber prior to initiation, subsequently standardized to air-saturated buffer levels. Kinetic parameters were derived through least-squares fitting of the Michaelis−Menten equation using LEONORA and graphed using GraphPad Prism 10 (graphpad.com).

Stoichiometry of the MilM-catalyzed reaction

The production of H2O2 was evaluated by comparing the reaction rates catalyzed by 2.5 μM MilM and 300 μM l-Arg with or without 0.25 mg/ml of catalase. Additionally, H2O2 production was quantified colorimetrically through the supplementation of the reaction with 0.25 mg/ml of horseradish peroxidase (Type VI) and 1 mM 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazolineethyl benzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid). Upon consumption of 100 μM O2, the reaction was quenched with one volume of 10% trichloroacetic acid. Experimental values were then juxtaposed against a calibration curve of H2O2. To determine the l-Arg:O2 stoichiometry, reaction mixtures (1 ml) comprising 10 μM MilM and 250 μM l-Arg in 100 mM Mops (I = 0.1 M, pH 7.2) buffer were incubated at 25 °C. Before the addition of MilM, the mixture was equilibrated to ambient O2 levels. Upon the introduction of MilM, O2 concentration was monitored using an Oxygraph until the consumption of 100 μM O2, whereupon the reaction was halted by quenching with one volume of MeOH. Subsequently, the remaining l-Arg was quantified via HPLC after precolumn derivatization with o-phthalaldehyde (OPA)/mercaptopropionic acid (MPA). The quenched solution was incubated with one volume of 0.4 M borate buffer (pH 10.2) and one volume of OPA/MPA solution (1 mg/ml OPA, 0.1% (v/v) MPA in 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 10.2)) for 3 min prior to HPLC analysis. Analysis was conducted on an Agilent 1260 HPLC apparatus employing a Luna C18(2), 5 μm, 4.6 mm ID × 250 mm column (Phenomenex). Elution proceeded at 1.0 ml/min with a mobile phase composed of a linear gradient of water and acetonitrile ((v/v): 98:2, 0–1 min; 80:20, 1–5 min; 50:50, 5–6.5 min; 0:100, 6.5–8 min; 98:2, 8–10 min), both containing 0.05% (v/v) TFA. Derivatized l-Arg was detected at a wavelength of 330 nm. Experimental data were compared against a calibration curve of l-Arg under identical conditions. Mean values ± SD are reported, with n = 7.

Spectroscopic analysis of the MilM-catalyzed reaction

Aerobic reaction mixtures (1 ml) were prepared in a quartz cuvette with a path length of 1 cm and contained 25 μM enzyme and 250 μM l-Arg in 20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.5). All solutions were RT and air saturated with O2. The reaction was initiated upon addition of substrate. For anaerobic experiments, the reactions were initiated in a Labmaster glovebox (Mbraun) under a N2 atmosphere with O2 levels maintained below 4 p.p.m. O2. The buffer (20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, 25 ml) and substrate (l-Arg, 10 mM, 25 ml) were vigorously bubbled with N2 for 25 min, capped in an airtight vial then transferred into the glovebox. Enzyme solutions were purged with N2 for 10 min on ice and subsequently stored in airtight vials then transferred into the glovebox immediately before use. All spectra were recorded at 25 °C using a Agilent Cary 60 spectrophotometer equipped with a thermostatted cuvette holder (Varian Canada) and controlled by Cary WinUV software version 6.00. Anaerobic spectra were recorded using an airtight cuvette (Starna Cells, Inc.) with a threaded top.
