Effects of ultrasound-guided posterior quadratus lumborum block on atelectasis in pediatric patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair and orchiopexy surgeries: A randomized controlled study
Authors: Çağdaş Baytar, Duygu Tatlı Uçarcı, Merve Sena Baytar, Keziban Bollucuoğlu, Hilal Ayoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Insufficient lung expansion due to inadequate pain management and opioid consumption is one of the most important factor for perioperative atelectasis and and the use of ultrasound in the diagnosis of atelectasis has been quite common in recent years. The effectiveness of posterior quadratus lumborum block (QLB) in postoperative analgesia after open surgery is well established. The present study aimed to investigate the effects of posterior QLB on perioperative atelectasis in pediatric surgeries. Sixty patients who underwent elective inguinal hernia repair and orchipexy surgery under general anesthesia were included in the study. The patients were divided into 2 groups: Control (n = 30) and QLB, (n = 30). Posterior quadratus lumborum block was administered to the QLB group. Incidence of atelectasis and modified lung ultrasound (LUS) scores were recorded before induction (T1), at the end of surgery (T2), and before leaving the recovery unit (1 h after extubation) (T3). Pain was evaluated with Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) scores in the postoperative period. The analgesics administered in the recovery unit and ward were recorded. A significant difference was observed in the incidence of atelectasis and total LUS scores between groups at T2 and T3. Both outcomes were higher in the control group. A significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of FLACC scores at 0, 2, and 6 h postoperatively. In all three time periods, the scores in the QLB group were lower. A significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of rescue analgesic usage in the recover room and during ward follow-up. Analgesia requirement was higher in the Control group. Ultrasound-guided posterior QLB in pediatric patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair and orchiopexy surgery decreases the incidence of atelectasis and total LUS scores by reducing pain scores and the amount of opioids required. Introduction

The incidence of atelectasis after general anesthesia induced with a laryngeal mask (LMA) in pediatric patients is remarkably high1. There are several risk factors for postoperative atelectasis. Insufficient lung expansion due to inadequate pain management and opioid consumption is one of the most important factors. Even minimal atelectasis does not resolve for several days and causes serious complications2.

Although chest radiography and computed tomography have been widely used for atelectasis detection, lung ultrasonography has also gained importance in recent years. The advantages of ultrasonography over other techniques include easy accessibility, noninvasiveness, repeatability, and radiation-free nature3,4.

With the widespread use of ultrasonography, many truncal blocks can be easily applied with a very low risk of complications. The posterior quadratus lumborum block (QLB) has been shown to be effective for postoperative analgesia in pediatric patients undergoing open abdominal surgeries5,6.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of posterior QLB on perioperative atelectasis in pediatric patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair and orchiopexy surgeries. The primary outcomes of the study were the incidence of atelectasis and modified lung ultrasound (LUS) score, and the secondary outcomes were postoperative Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) scores, rescue analgesic administration, and block-related complications.

Methods

This prospective randomized controlled study was conducted at Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Hospital between September 2021 and November 2023 and was approved by the Local Ethics Committee (Protocol No: 2021/03–11, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04830280). The study included 66 patients, classified as ASA I–II, who underwent elective unilateral inguinal hernia repair or orchiopexy surgeries and were between the ages of 2 months and 2 years. This study adhered to the CONSORT guidelines.

Patients with acute or chronic pulmonary pathology, those contraindicated for block application, those allergic to local anesthetic agents, and those for whom consent could not be obtained from their parents were excluded. The parents of the children were provided with a detailed explanation of the preoperative procedure, and verbal and written informed consent was obtained. The patients were randomized into the following two groups using the sealed envelope method: Control (n = 30) and QLB, (n = 30). The randomization and sealed envelopes were prepared by a researcher who was not involved in the study. All assessments and data collection on the ward were carried out by the non-study staff, who were blind to which group the patient was in. Additionally, the nurses in the ward did not have any information about the randomization.

LUS evaluation was performed three times: T1 (before induction), T2 (end of the surgery), and T3 (1 h after extubation). All evaluations were performed by the same researcher experienced in lung ultrasonography, using a high-frequency linear probe (ÇB). Each hemithorax was divided into six regions using three longitudinal (parasternal, anterior, and posterior axillary) and two axial (just above the diaphragm and 1 cm above the nipple) lines. In total, 12 regions were sequentially evaluated for the degree of atelectasis using Monastesse’s modified LUS Score7. Each region was scored from 0 to 3 based on B-lines and juxtapleural consolidation as follows: 0–2 B-lines = 0, ≥ 3 B-lines or ≥ 1 small subpleural consolidation separated by normal pleural lines = 1, multiple combined B-lines or multiple small subpleural consolidations separated by irregular or thickened pleural lines = 2, and white lung or > 1 × 2 cm subpleural consolidation = 3. Significant atelectasis was considered if a score of ≥ 2 was recorded in any region. Significant atelectatic regions and total LUS scores were recorded at T1, T2, and T3.

After routine monitoring, an appropriate LMA was placed in all patients after standard general anesthesia induction. During induction, 2–3 mg/kg of propofol and 1 µg/kg of fentanyl were administered. After LMA placement, anesthesia was maintained with 2% sevoflurane and 50% oxygen. Ventilation was provided in the volume control mode (Maquet, Flow-I, Sweden). The tidal volume, and PEEP were set at 8 mL/kg, 5 cm H2O, respectively. The respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain ETCO2 between 35 and 45 mmHg.

All blocks were administered by the same anesthesiologist (ÇB). Patients in the QLB group were placed in the lateral decubitus position. Following proper sterilization, a sterile linear ultrasound probe was placed anteriorly and superiorly to the iliac crest. Once the external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis muscles were identified, the probe was advanced posteriorly. The posterior aspect of the quadratus lumborum muscle (QLM) and middle layer of the thoracolumbar fascia were identified at the posterior border of the external oblique muscle. Using the in-plane technique, a 22 G 50 mm needle was advanced anteriorly and posteriorly. After confirming the needle tip location with 0.5 mL saline, 0.5 mL/kg of 0.25% bupivacaine was injected onto the posterior surface of the QLM (Fig. 1).

Subsequently, the patients were handed over to the surgical team 10 min after the block. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon (DTU), using the same procedure. At the end of surgery, 10 mg/kg of intravenous (IV) paracetamol was given to all patients. Following the removal of the laryngeal mask, the patients were transferred to the recovery unit. The intraoperative hemodynamic parameters, duration of anesthesia, and surgery were recorded.

In the postoperative period, the pain levels of the patients were assessed using the FLACC scale in the recovery unit and at 2 and 6 h in the ward. In the recovery unit, if the score was ≥ 5, pain was managed with IV administration of 0.5 µg/kg fentanyl. In the ward, if the score was ≥ 5, 7 mg/kg oral ibuprofen was given. The analgesics administered in the recovery unit and ward were recorded. All block-related complications were recorded.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined prior to the pilot study based on the incidence of significant atelectasis. In the pilot study, which included 10 patients not enrolled in the main study in each group, the phi coefficient was 0.6, with a Type I error of 0.05, a power of 99%, and an effect size of 0.6. Therefore, the required minimum sample size per group was calculated to be 30, totaling 60 patients. Considering a dropout rate of 10%, 66 patients were included in the study.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and outcomes. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median, depending on the distribution of the data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of distribution for continuous variables. For normally distributed data, independent samples t-tests were used to compare means between groups. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables between groups.

Results

A total of 66 patients were initially enrolled in the study, and six patients were excluded, leaving 60 patients for statistical analysis (Fig. 2). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of demographic and clinical data (Table 1).

A significant difference was observed in the incidence of atelectasis between groups at T3 (QLB group, 16.7%; control group, 70%; p < 0.001). At T2, a significant difference was observed in the incidence of atelectasis between the two groups (QLB group, 16.7%; control group, 76.7%; p < 0.001). However, at T1, there were no significant differences between the groups (QLB group, 10%; control group, 16.7%; p = 0.706). At T3, a significant difference was observed in the total LUS score between the groups (QLB group, median 4.5 [range 0–11]; control group, median 8 [range 1–15]; p < 0.001). At T2, a significant difference was observed in the total LUS score between the groups (QLB group, median 5 [range 0–11]; control group, median 6.5 [range 2–14]; p = 0.029). However, at T1, no significant differences were observed in the total LUS score between the groups (QLB group, median 4 [range 0–8]; control group, median 3.5 [range 0–8]; p = 0.458) (Table 2; Fig. 3 A).

A significant difference in FLACC scores was observed between the groups at 0, 2, and 6 h postoperatively, with the QLB group showing lower scores during all three time periods (Table 3; Fig. 3B). Additionally, in the postoperative period, the number of patients receiving fentanyl to control acute pain in the recovery unit was significantly lower in the QLB group (QLB group, n = 3 (10%); control group, n = 13 (43.3%); p = 0.004). Furthermore, a significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of rescue analgesic usage during ward follow-up (QLB group, n = 2 (6.7%); control group, n = 10 (33.3%); p = 0.01). There were not any block related complications. No clinically apparent postoperative respiratory symptoms were observed in either group during recovery unit or ward follow-up. None of the patients with positive LUS findings had associated clinical respiratory symptoms.

Discussion

In our study, we determined that patients in the QLB group had a lower incidence of significant atelectasis and lower total LUS scores both at the end of surgery and in the early postoperative period. We also found that the patients in the QLB group had lower FLACC scores and required fewer rescue analgesics at all postoperative time points.

Children have smaller and fewer alveoli, limited alveolar surface for gas exchange, and more dead spaces. They are also known to have a lower total lung capacity than adults. Children are more prone to atelectasis due to the underdevelopment of collateral ventilation and anatomically more flexible chest wall compliance8. The incidence of anesthesia-induced atelectasis in children undergoing surgery is quite high9. Atelectasis begins with anesthesia induction and usually occurs in the smaller alveoli. After physiological changes, the residual functional capacity decreases and alveolar collapse deepens10. If inadequate pain management is administered to a patient group already predisposed to atelectasis, atelectasis will undoubtedly become even more pronounced. Due to inadequately managed postoperative pain, the function of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles is restricted, respiration becomes superficial, and atelectasis deepens11. Li et al. investigated the effect of the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block on atelectasis after laparoscopic surgery in pediatric patients. Their study included children aged 1–6 years, and they showed that the TAP block decreased the incidence of atelectasis12. Kılıc et al. showed that local anesthetic infiltration effectively reduced the development of postoperative atelectasis during upper abdominal midline surgeries13. In our study, we investigated the effect of a posterior QLB on atelectasis and determined that both the total LUS scores and incidence of atelectasis were significantly lower in the QLB group.

Opioids cause respiratory depression and the inability to cough strongly depends on the dose. This is another important cause of perioperative atelectasis14. Friedrich et al.15 investigated the effects of fentanyl on respiratory complications and reported that patients who received high-dose fentanyl during the perioperative period developed atelectasis more frequently than those who received low-dose fentanyl. In our study, we used fentanyl to treat acute pain in the recovery unit and found that fentanyl consumption was higher in the control group. We believe that higher fentanyl consumption also affected atelectasis development, with a higher incidence in the control group.

Notably, QLB provides effective analgesia by spreading the local anesthetic along the thoracolumbar fascia into the paravertebral space16. Gözen et al.5 compared the analgesic efficacy of the posterior QLB and caudal block in pediatric patients undergoing lower abdominal surgery. They concluded that the posterior QLB provided more effective and longer analgesia than the caudal block. Ahmad et al.17 reported that the posterior QLB provided more effective analgesia, better hemodynamic stability, and had lower FLACC scores than the caudal epidural block during upper abdominal surgery in pediatric patients. In our study, when comparing QLB to the Control group, both FLACC scores and the need for rescue analgesics to relieve acute pain in the postoperative period were significantly lower in the QLB group.

Our study had some limitations. At our hospital, inguinal hernia repair and orchiopexy are planned and performed as ambulatory surgeries. Patients are usually discharged 6–8 h after surgery. Therefore, one of our limitations was that we only monitored the total LUS and FLACC scores for up to 6 h postoperatively. In addition, the researcher performing the LUS assessments was also the practitioner performing the blocks, which prevented double-blinding during the intraoperative data collection. However, ward assessments were performed by fully blinded staff. Another limitation is the lack of evaluation of block success in the postoperative period owing to the inability of patients to cooperate because of their age group. Finally, as our cohort comprised children aged 2 months to 2 years, the findings may not be generalizable to the broader pediatric population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we determined that ultrasound-guided posterior QLB in pediatric patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair and orchiopexy surgery decreases the incidence of atelectasis and total LUS scores by reducing pain scores and the amount of opioids required. We believe that the posterior QLB is feasible in this patient population who are prone to atelectasis.
