Artificial Intelligence Chest X-Ray Opportunistic Screening Model for Coronary Artery Calcium Deposition: A Multi-Objective Model With Multimodal Data Fusion
Authors: Jiwoong Jeong, Chieh-Ju Chao, Reza Arsanjani, Chadi Ayoub, Steven J Lester, Milagros Pereyra, Ebram F Said, Michael Roarke, Cecilia Tagle-Cornell, Laura M Koepke, Yi-Lin Tsai, Chen Jung-Hsuan, Chun-Chin Chang, Juan M Farina, Hari Trivedi, Bhavik N Patel, Imon Banerjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo create an opportunistic screening model to predict coronary calcium burden and associated cardiovascular risk using only commonly available frontal chest x-rays (CXR) and patient demographics.Patients and MethodsWe proposed a novel multitask learning framework and trained a model using 2121 patients with paired gated computed tomography scans and CXR images internally (Mayo Clinic) from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2022, with coronary artery calcification (CAC) scores (0, 1-99, and 100+) as ground truths. Results from the internal training were validated on multiple external datasets (Emory University Healthcare and Taipei Veterans General Hospital—from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2022) with significant racial and ethnic differences.ResultsClassification performance between 0, 1-99, and 100+ CAC scores performed moderately on both the internal test and external datasets, reaching average f1-scores of 0.71±0.04 for Mayo, 0.65±0.02 for Emory University Healthcare, and 0.70±0.06 for Taipei Veterans General Hospital. For the clinically relevant risk identification, the performance of our model on the internal and 2 external datasets reached area under the receiver operating curves of 0.86±0.02, 0.77±0.03, and 0.82±0.03 for 0 versus 400+, respectively. For 0 versus 100+, we achieved area under the receiver operating curve of 0.83±0.03, 0.71±0.02, and 0.78±0.01, respectively. Prospective evaluation across 3 Mayo Clinic sites is on par with the external validations and reports only minimal temporal drift.ConclusionOpen-source fusion artificial intelligence-CXR model performed better than existing state-of-the-art models for predicting CAC scores only on internal cohort, with robust performance on external datasets. This proposed model may be useful as a robust, first-pass opportunistic screening method for cardiovascular risk from regular CXR.

Coronary artery disease remains an important global health concern, contributing to substantial morbidity and mortality.1 As such, effective risk assessment and early intervention strategies are essential, with early identification of coronary artery calcification (CAC) burden playing an important role.2, 3, 4 Electrocardiogram-gated computational tomography (CT) imaging allows determination of CAC scores,5, 6, 7 which are used for risk stratification and guiding preventive interventions, such as statin and aspirin therapy, especially in asymptomatic individuals.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 More recently, the role of CAC testing has been expanded for the assessment of symptomatic low-risk individuals.13

Despite the advantages of gated CT imaging in assessing CAC scores, there are drawbacks such as the need for electrocardiogram-gating requirements, technical challenges in the setting of arrhythmias, and image processing and analysis by trained specialists,14 which may not be operationally feasible at small health care facilities or in developing countries. Furthermore, CAC testing is not universally covered by insurance plans and requires upfront identification and referral of at-risk individuals for a dedicated scan.15 While there is evidence that CAC is cost-effective in patients with a family history of coronary disease,16 this has not been shown for universal CAC screening using current gated CT protocols in asymptomatic individuals.17 In light of these limitations, there is growing clinical interest in exploring alternative methods to identify patients with high CAC burden who may benefit from early referral and intervention.18 Although there are less than 1 million gated CTs performed annually in the United States, there are over 70 million chest X-rays (CXRs) and it is nearly universally accessible across various health care settings.19 Therefore, although CXRs are not typically used as the primary modality for assessing CAC, we propose that CXRs obtained for other reasons can be used opportunistically to identify patients with high CAC and stratify the risk. While CAC estimation from CXRs would likely be coarse compared to its CT counterpart, it could provide an early detection and risk stratification opportunity for a wider population, particularly for asymptomatic patients.

This study aims to apply deep learning models to CXRs to identify patients with a high CAC burden. However, estimating CAC from noisy and often varying quality CXR images is an extremely challenging problem, primarily due to a lack of large data CXR cohorts where CAC is mentioned (typically this is not mentioned by radiologists) and the overall low prevalence of patients with high CAC. Our primary contributions are as follows: (i) proposing a multitask learning framework—artificial intelligence (AI)-CXR to correlate CAC and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) prediction tasks using the same model backbone and showing improvement in performance for CAC; (ii) creating a pipeline for a novel multichannel CXR image generation to capture prominent anomalies of the heart (lung masked image) and lung (rib suppression); and (iii) showing robust generalizability of the proposed model on one domestic institution and one foreign institution and a retrospective-prospective validation for 24 months.

Materials And Methods

Internal Cohort for Training and Validation

We collected a retrospective internal cohort of 2121 patients who had a coronary CT scan from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2021, and corresponding CXR imaging examination with a frontal view within ±1 year period of the CT examination day at the Mayo Clinic (Table 1). Informed consent waiver and ethical approval were obtained from the Mayo Clinic institutional review board (IRB protocol #22-006839). Two-thirds of the internal dataset was used for training and the one third was split into validation and testing, resulting in 1424/356/341 patients, respectively. The CAC scoring was performed on cardiac chest CT according to Agatston scoring methods.5 The CAC scores were grouped into 3 bins based on accepted clinical cutoffs and literature defining prognosis by CAC category: no CAC-0, 1-99, and 100+.11,17 Additionally, patients with CAC 400+ were also identified to test the models’ performance in differentiating high versus low-risk cases and comparing them to available literature.11,20,21

External and Prospective Cohort

To test the model’s robustness and generalization capabilities, we collected testing data from 2 external health care centers (domestic and foreign)—Emory University Healthcare (EUH) and Taipei Veterans General Hospital (VGHTPE approved by IRB: 2023-09-009CC) in Taipei, Taiwan, with considerably varying patient populations in terms of race and ethnicity. To match the internal data, we applied the same patient selection criteria—coronary CT scan from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2022, and a CXR examination with frontal view within a ±1 year period of the CT (Table 1).

Additionally, to observe the model drift over time, we used a prospective dataset (prospective to the model) from Mayo Clinic with the same criteria as the internal dataset with CXRs collected after the last internal dataset acquisition (December 31, 2022, to December 31, 2024) up to 24 months.

Multichannel Image Formation

Preprocessed CXR images (Supplemental Figure 1) were masked to exclude noncardiac regions—including soft tissues, bones, and lung fields—to enhance model focus on the cardiac silhouette and mediastinum, where radiographic markers of cardiovascular risk are most prominent. Exclusion of these regions minimizes the influence of noninformative or potentially confounding features learned from the training data, reducing the risk of spurious correlations and improving model generalizability.

We used a pretrained lung segmentation model22 to segment the lungs and computed a tight bounding box for cropping the center chest area. We inverted the lung mask to obtain a rough segmentation of the heart and generate a lung masked image. An off-the-shelf bone suppression code22 was used to suppress the ribs in the CXR images to hide the bone density.23 Finally, we combined the original cropped chest-centered image, lung masked image, and rib bone-suppressed image to generate a 3-channel image to highlight the lung and heart regions (Supplemental Figure 1, [available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/]).

Multitask Image Classification Model

Given the complexity of the CAC detection task, we designed a multitask learning (MTL) paradigm by combining the MACE prediction task,24 which includes acute myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization due to cardiac event, and cardiovascular mortality, with the assumption that these events correlate with CAC risk assessment. This parallel task can help extract additional information to support the primary CAC score prediction. On the basis of chart review, we manually curated MACE events within 2 years of the CXR study. We design the MTL paradigm with joint learning, where both tasks are optimized with weighted loss: ltotal=γ1lMACE+γ2lCAC, where γ is the weighting parameter and lMACE, lCAC are the individual branch losses (Figure 1). We trained a ResNeXt10125 backbone with the MTL strategy that uses a shared convolutional neural network (CNN) for both tasks, and the backbone will be updated with the loss from both tasks. This strategy would have a synergistic effect on the backbone training for the challenging task of CAC detection.26

Multitask model. A ResNeXt101 backbone was used to classify MACE event and CAC category in parallel with weighted loss. CAC, coronary artery calcification; MACE, major adverse cardiovascular events

MTL CAC Fusion Model

We provided additional data about patient and acquisition protocol, which is routinely available as part of the CXR (ie, metadata), and combined simple tabular data—patient demographics (age and gender) and X-ray device manufacturer category—with the CXR image using late fusion strategy (decision level fusion).27 The minimal set of variables is intentionally designed to operate using only information readily available in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine header—to ensure broader applicability and ease of deployment across diverse clinical environments.

Additionally, patient demographics and device information may also help reduce the bias in the model and allow generalization. We trained an individual supervised model (random forest) for the tabular data and a meta-learner model that takes input CAC task prediction probability from both the MTL image model and tabular model and creates an aggregated function of the probabilities. With optimal learned weight by the meta-learner, theoretically, better or equal performance can be achieved compared to either of the individual modality models. The codebase for model training and inference is provided in the following link: https://github.com/jeong-jasonji/CXR_MTL_CAC.

Results

Quantitative Performance

We evaluated the MTL CAC fusion model on a hold-out test dataset from Mayo Clinic (n=341) and an independent dataset from EUH (n=386) and VGHTPE (n=499) using class-wise area under the area receiver operating curve (AUROC) (Figure 2) score using a one-vs-all strategy to assess the model’s probabilistic classification accuracy. We first evaluated the performance of several CNN backbone models to choose the best one and saw that ResNeXt101 had the best performance on the internal test, as seen in Supplemental Table 1, (available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). On the internal test set, the model achieved 0.797±0.032 and 0.702±0.021 AUROC for the ≥100 CAC category (clinically significant CAC) and 0 CAC category, respectively. The performance was suboptimal for the intermediate 0-99 CAC category (0.652±0.016 AUROC), with a similar trend observed for the EUH and VGHTPE external datasets. For the overall 3-class CAC classification, the performance remained moderate with an average f1-score—0.705±0.037 for Mayo, 0.654±0.023 for EUH, and 0.696±0.059 for VGHTPE cohorts (Supplemental Table 2, [available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org]). Despite a wide racial and ethnic difference between the centers, the performance remained consistent across the external settings. The MACE prediction was performed as a parallel auxiliary task within the MTL paradigm and only evaluated on the Mayo hold-out test set since the MACE outcome was not available on the external EUH and VGTHPE datasets (Figure 2). The model achieved a 0.832±0.03 AUROC score and 0.954±0.023 precision and 0.828±0.019 recall for identifying the MACE category (Supplemental Table 3, [available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org]). In Table 2, we present a comparative analysis between single-modality models (image-only and EHR-only) and the proposed MTL CAC fusion model, as well as between single-task models (CAC-only and MACE-only) and the proposed MTL image-only model. Experimental results on both internal and external datasets report that the fusion model consistently outperformed the single-modality models, and the multitask CAC estimation model outperformed its single-task counterparts.

Discrimination of High and Low CAC Groups

Although detection of 100+ CAC score from cardiac CT is important for clinical intervention with a statin, opportunistic screening for the detection of higher-risk coronary calcium burden from regular low-cost CXR imaging is also clinically useful to triage patients to further dedicated diagnostic evaluation. Thus, we evaluated the MTL CAC fusion model performance for binary classification tasks to differentiate high CAC candidates (Figure 3) and we experimented with both 0 vs 100+ and 0 vs 400+, respectively. The model reported high performance (0.817±0.038 average AUROC) for differentiating high from low coronary calcium burden using only CXR imaging and achieved fair performance (0.772±0.050 average AUROC) for moderate coronary calcium burden on both internal and external datasets. This suggests that the MTL CAC fusion model can identify the highest risk, 400+ category, as a patient with clinically actionable cardiovascular risk with 86% confidence from the regular CXR imaging on similar populations. The confidence is reduced, 77% and 82%, on different United States and international populations, respectively. Additional ablation experiments are discussed in the supplementary materials. Model bias analysis using disparity metrics suggested that age and gender were the demographic characteristic features in which we saw the most unfair disparities (Supplemental Figure 2, [available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/]). Older patients (60-80 years) were slightly more biased to have false negatives than the reference group (40-60 years) for higher CAC category, and interestingly female patients were less likely to have false negatives than males. Age groups under 40 and above 80 as well as non-White patients, were not included in the bias analysis as there were too few samples to derive significance from. Further discussion and bias plots of our analysis can be found in the supplementary materials and Supplemental Figure 4.

Model Drift Performance Study

We measured potential model drift (decay) by testing it on the prospective data (data after 2022) at different time intervals to understand the effect of changes in the environment or between the variables. We randomly selected a total of 400 CXRs (100 patients for each 6 consecutive month period for balancing temporal effect) from 2022-2024 and showed the 3-class cohort (0 vs 1-99 vs 100+) and 2-class cohort (0 vs 100+) performance AUROC in Figure 4. Although we observed that there is 1.3% minimal performance difference for 3-class cohort and a 4.82% performance difference for 2-class cohort, the overall performance is on par with the external validation performance. Notably, the model reported improved performance on prospectively collected data.

Discussion

The major contribution of this study is the development and external validation of an AI-based model for opportunistic screening of patients with undiagnosed calcified coronary atherosclerosis using routine CXRs, the most commonly utilized imaging study. This approach utilizes a MTL model to extract calcification features from routine CXRs and classify CAC score category, with external validation on datasets containing relevant different population representations (Mayo internal 74% White, EUH 50% African American, and VGTHPE 100% Asian). Additionally, incorporating clinical factors such as age, sex, and frailty score further enhanced the model performance in CAC classification. Although the model performance was moderate for classifying lower amounts of CAC (1-99) with 0.652±0.031 AUROC on the Mayo test and an average 0.579±0.017 AUROC on VGTHPE and EUH, it was not our attempt to provide precise CAC estimates; rather, it focuses on the clinically relevant extremes. The introduction of the intermediate risk-group (CAC 1-99) was mainly to enhance the discriminative ability of the model and bridge the gap between 0 and 100+ CAC. For the patients that require aggressive preventative intervention (CAC 100+ and 400+), according to the contemporary guidelines by Golub et al,21 the model achieved high performance (0.817±0.038 average AUROC) for differentiating CAC 400+ from absent CAC. This is a well-established cutoff to stratify patient populations with higher CVD risk in whom additional diagnostic evaluation or therapeutic intervention would be warranted clinically.17,28,29 Notably, the fusion of CXR imaging and clinical factor consistently outperformed single-modality models, and the MTL approach improved CAC prediction performance compared with single-task models, reporting the benefit of both data integration and task co-learning. When evaluating the prospective data from a 24-month period, we observe a performance improvement, which could be due to enhancement of the image quality compared with the retrospective training dataset. A further discussion of the qualitative interpretation of the model is given in the supplementary materials.

Existing Literature and Comparative Analysis

Previous works have examined the performance and prognostic value of AI models-based on other imaging modalities as an alternative to CT for assessment of CAC scores.18 Kamel et al29 created a deep learning model to predict binary CAC classification (high versus low) from CXR images and reported 0.74 AUC (100+ CAC vs 0 CAC) on frontal CXR and 0.7 (400+ CAC vs 0 CAC) on lateral CXR images; however, only validated on a single institutional data set, so its generalizability and clinical impact remain unclear.30 Interestingly, their model had worse performance in handling cases with more distinct CAC scores. Our AI-CXR model exhibited high discriminatory abilities (0.83-0.86 AUROC) in predicting zero CAC and 400+ CAC groups, and the findings were confirmed on external datasets.

Yuan et al18 reported a video-based AI CNN, which was trained to predict 0 versus high (400+) CAC scores from parasternal echocardiography long-axis views. While reporting good performance (0 CAC: 0.81 and 400+ CAC: 0.74), the study used a relatively clear cutoff and did not address patients with intermediate-risk (CAC score between 0 and 100), which is a group with more challenges in preventive intervention decisions. Our model had similar performance in differentiating cases of CAC 0 and CAC 100+ (Figure 4); we believe the superior performance for high CAC group relates to the fundamental difference of the 2 modalities, in which CXR images the entire heart is visible, whereas one transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) view only provides limited vision of specific cardiovascular structures; additionally, ultrasound is poor for visualization of calcification caused by attenuation. Furthermore, while TTE is a radiation-free modality, its cost-effectiveness, turnaround time, and availability may not be as favorable as CXR in resource-limited practice scenarios. Ultimately, the Yuan et al18 study suggests that deep learning models based on TTEs hold promise for adjunctive coronary artery disease risk stratification and guiding preventive therapies.

Limitations

The study only considers the frontal view of the CXR image (anteroposterior/posterioanterior) due to wider availability and data consistency. In the future, the lateral view can also be assessed as it may provide additional data to boost the model. The model only obtained moderate performance for the 3-class CAC detection task (AUC 0.717±0.060); however, given the opportunistic screening goal of the framework, the binary risk stratification model obtained an impressive 0.817±0.038 average AUROC score on the internal and external validation cohort and should be capable in differential screening. Given training with the MTL paradigm with MACE, the model derived misclassification cases for pleuropulmonary conditions and aortic calcification. The basic metabolic index (BMI) could also have been a confounder as higher BMI can lead to poorer penetration and image quality; however, we did not have BMI information to include in our bias analysis.31 Further research and validation are warranted to optimize the model’s performance and evaluate its real-world clinical utility.

Clinical Perspectives

An AI-Enabled Opportunistic Screening Tool—Broad Impact Our model has dual advantages that make it highly beneficial for cardiovascular risk assessment

First, CXR imaging is widely available and accessible across various health care settings, including primary care clinics, urgent care centers, hospitals, and even remote or resource-constrained areas.19,32 Compared to CT imaging, a CXR-based AI approach could offer several distinct advantages. First, it considerably decreased ionizing radiation exposure (0.1 mSv vs 0.8-10.5 mSv).33,34 This widespread utilization and availability enables the model to facilitate opportunistic screenings, allowing for the identification of individuals at risk of cardiovascular disease without the need for specialized cardiac evaluations. This inclusive approach ensures that even patients who may not be aware of cardiovascular screening can benefit from risk assessment using CXR imaging, particularly asymptomatic and young patients. Without any additional cost, we can also reuse the retrospective CXR images to stratify the wider population based on coronary artery disease risk.

The combination of CXR’s wide availability and low radiation dose, coupled with the power of AI in the CXR-CAC model, provides a simple, cost-effective, and efficient screening tool. It holds particular importance in large-scale screening programs and resource-limited areas where access to specialized cardiac imaging, such as cardiac CT, may be limited. By leveraging existing infrastructure and the widespread availability of CXR imaging, the CXR-CAC model enables targeted interventions, public health initiatives, and timely risk stratification for individuals at risk of cardiovascular disease in underserved areas. This has the potential to improve patient outcomes and contribute to the early detection and prevention of cardiovascular conditions on a population scale. In areas where resources, infrastructure, or expertise for cardiac CT are scarce, this approach has the potential to broaden the availability of CVD screening and risk stratification, thereby enabling timely interventions and preventive measures for individuals in underserved areas. This is paramount, considering that low-income and middle-income countries contribute a massive share to the global burden of cardiovascular conditions and cardiovascular deaths.31

Conclusion

In summary, the AI-CXR model, with its ability to identify patients with high coronary calcium burden from routine chest X-rays, offers a viable and cost-effective screening tool for opportunistic cardiovascular risk assessment. The proposed CXR-CAC model reports the potential to extract calcification features from a wide population variation (internal, external, foreign, and prospective) and classify CAC scores into clinically relevant categories. This model represents a step toward harnessing the capabilities of AI and CXR imaging for cardiovascular risk assessment.
