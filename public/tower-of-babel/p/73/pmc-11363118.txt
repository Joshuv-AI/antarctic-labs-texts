Charged Gold Nanoparticles for Target Identification–Alignment and Automatic Segmentation of CT Image-Guided Adaptive Radiotherapy in Small Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Jianjun Lai, Zhizeng Luo, Jiping Liu, Haili Hu, Hao Jiang, Pengyuan Liu, Li He, Weiyi Cheng, Weiye Ren, Yajun Wu, Ji-Gang Piao, Zhibing Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBecause of the challenges posed by anatomical uncertainties and the low resolution of plain computed tomography (CT) scans, implementing adaptive radiotherapy (ART) for small hepatocellular carcinoma (sHCC) using artificial intelligence (AI) faces obstacles in tumor identification–alignment and automatic segmentation. The current study aims to improve sHCC imaging for ART using a gold nanoparticle (Au NP)-based CT contrast agent to enhance AI-driven automated image processing. The synthesized charged Au NPs demonstrated notable in vitro aggregation, low cytotoxicity, and minimal organ toxicity. Over time, an in situ sHCC mouse model was established for in vivo CT imaging at multiple time points. The enhanced CT images processed using 3D U-Net and 3D Trans U-Net AI models demonstrated high geometric and dosimetric accuracy. Therefore, charged Au NPs enable accurate and automatic sHCC segmentation in CT images using classical AI models, potentially addressing the technical challenges related to tumor identification, alignment, and automatic segmentation in CT-guided online ART.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Computed tomography imaging, Adaptive radiotherapy, Gold nanoparticles, Tumor automatic segmentation

Globally, liver cancer is the sixth most common malignancy, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) making up 85%–90% of primary liver cancers.1 Advances in modern diagnostic techniques have elevated the detection rate of small hepatocellular carcinoma (sHCC) in early stages. With the widespread adoption of precision radiotherapy, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has emerged as a crucial treatment method for sHCC because of its high precision and low toxicity.2,3 Concurrently, computed tomography (CT)-based adaptive radiotherapy (ART) is a significant research area in precision radiotherapy. This therapy continually improves treatment accuracy by addressing anatomical, organ, and tumor changes while undergoing the treatment.4 Therefore, integrating SBRT with ART to develop CT-based online ART–SBRT technology represents a key advancement in achieving precise radiotherapy for sHCC, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes.5,6

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) image-processing technology have enabled rapid and automated segmentation of various tumors, characterized by their distinct anatomical, structural, and CT imaging features. This progress has contributed significantly to the development and use of online ART technology.7−9 However, despite these advancements, accurately segmenting tumors in sHCC remains challenging with the current AI techniques. This is primarily because of the uncertainty of anatomical location and low image resolution of plain-scan CT. The current obstacles and challenges in developing CT image-guided, online ART–SBRT for sHCC involve identifying and aligning the tumor target area and automatic segmentation.9−11

Two primary approaches exist to combat the challenges of identifying and segmenting tumors in radiotherapy for sHCC targets. The first approach uses multimodal imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance (MR), ultrasound, and nuclide imaging for tumor identification.12,13 However, the standard radiotherapy method to determine the radiation dose depends on the CT electron density (ED) dose algorithm. Despite their specific benefits, other imaging modalities must be converted into CT images with ED or combined with CT images for accurate radiotherapy dose calculations. This approach is applicable during the initial radiotherapy planning and offline ART processes.14,15 The second approach uses small-molecule contrast agents for CT imaging to improve the visibility of sHCC. Patients with sHCC require multiple treatments and imaging throughout a single radiotherapy course. However, iodine-based small-molecule contrast agents commonly used in clinical practice have rapid metabolism, short contrast duration, and poor targeting. Multiple high-dose administrations of these agents can induce adverse reactions and allergies in some patients.16

Both methods present challenges when managing the ART–SBRT workflow for sHCC based on CT images. Therefore, innovative techniques are required to improve the contrast of sHCC in CT imaging consistently. Recently, nanotechnology has garnered widespread attention in biomedicine. Nanomaterials depict improved permeability and retention (EPR) effects, enabling tumor-selective high permeability and retention. They can respond to the tumor microenvironment via nanozyme and nanoprobe technologies. These properties allow nanoscale contrast agents to target and persist in tumor sites effectively.17 Therefore, developing a nanoscale CT contrast agent to significantly improve the ability of AI models to identify, align, and automatically segment sHCCs in CT images can help achieve a breakthrough in sHCC ART.18−25

This study attempted to develop a pH-responsive, charged gold nanoparticle (charged Au NP)-targeted CT contrast agent with prolonged imaging capability and high contrast for several days in CT-enhanced imaging of sHCC. This novel agent addresses the image identification and alignment challenges in ART radiotherapy for sHCC (Figure 1B). Additionally, we attempted to integrate a charged Au NP-enhanced CT imaging methodology using classical AI image segmentation models to overcome the challenge of automatic segmentation of target volumes in ART for sHCC (Figure 1C). Our main aim was to overcome the obstacles linked with identification alignment and automatic-segmentation technology in daily CT image-guided online ART in sHCC. In this research, we introduced a novel concept for improving AI model performance using nanotechnology, which differs from classical ART research that depends solely on AI algorithms. Activatable nanoprobes are a promising approach in nanotargeted diagnostics that leverage nanoparticles (NPs) to respond to their surrounding physiological environment and biomarkers to perform specific functions. To develop a nanoscale CT contrast agent tailored for specific targeting to sHCCs, we initiated our synthesis process by fabricating gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) via a sodium citrate reduction method. Then, we coated these Au NPs with ligands carrying different charges to form charged Au NPs (Figure 2A). Specifically, 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid served as the negatively charged ligand, while (11-mercaptoundecyl)-trimethylammonium bromide functioned as the positively charged ligand. By modifying the proportion of these two ligands on the surfaces of the Au NPs, we aimed to induce NP aggregation within the acidic environment typical of liver cancer, thereby enhancing their positioning and imaging capabilities.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has shown that the charged Au NPs are evenly distributed at pH 7.4 throughout the field of view with a particle size of 28 ± 0.618 nm (Figure 2B,C). At pH 6.5 and 5.0, the charged Au NPs demonstrate significant aggregation that increases with time (Figure 2E). The color of the gold nanoparticles changes from the original wine red to gray-black (Figure 2D). However, uncharged Au NPs do not exhibit any aggregation, regardless of pH. These findings ascertain the successful synthesis of pH-responsive charged Au NPs. We also examined the UV absorption spectra of Au NPs and charged Au NPs under different pH conditions to decipher the impact of the pH on NP behavior. Figure 2F indicates that the UV absorption of Au NPs remains consistent across the different pH levels. In contrast, charged Au NPs undergo significant changes in UV absorption under acidic conditions characterized by absorption shifts and reductions (Figure 2G). Under alkaline conditions, the absorption peak at 520 nm shifts toward the red spectrum with a tail under acidic conditions, depicting NP aggregation induced by low pH. Subsequent dynamic light scattering analysis revealed that the median particle size under acidic conditions is approximately 1039 nm with a maximum length of 1406 nm. This exceeds the endothelial gaps in the tumor vasculature, thereby highlighting significant aggregation. The propensity for aggregation of charged Au NPs within sHCC indicates a substantial potential to extend their visualization duration.

We performed CCK-8 cytotoxicity assays to examine the cellular safety of Au NPs and charged Au NPs.26Figure 3A illustrates that the viability remained unchanged after 24 h of exposure to Au NPs and charged Au NPs, which suggests good biosafety for both NPs. This was confirmed after 48 h exposure to different concentrations of Au NPs, and charged Au NPs yielded a >85% survival rate (Figure 3B). The in vivo biosafety of Au NPs and charged Au NPs was assessed in healthy and tumor-bearing mice by determining routine blood and serum biochemistries. Neither the healthy nor the tumor-bearing mice showed abnormalities after injecting the NPs (Figure 3C–E). Additionally, our metabolic distribution study revealed that charged and uncharged Au NPs undergo hepatic metabolism. However, charged Au NPs exhibit slower metabolic clearance because of the aggregation effect affecting metabolism and excretion.27,28 Based on our results, we speculate that the charged Au NPs initially circulate in a nonaggregated state and are excreted through both urine and feces. Because of the acidic response, they aggregate at tumor sites to form clusters and remain at the tumor site, thereby possibly extending the imaging duration. However, the enhanced particle size delays metabolic clearance, hinders urinary excretion, and leads to most charged Au NPs being excreted via feces (SI Figure S1). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues depicted no significant tissue damage irrespective of treatment group, which suggests favorable biocompatibility of normal and charged Au NPs (Figure 3E). The cytotoxicity and organ toxicity studies support the biosafety of the synthesized charged Au NPs as potential CT imaging contrast agents.

An in situ HCC mouse model was developed to assess the impact of Au NPs and charged Au NPs on the enhancement of CT scans of sHCCs. In vivo CT imaging experiments were conducted at various time points postinjection. In the Au NP group, initial scans before injection and 48 h postinjection indicated no tumor opacification in 16 mice. However, 11 mice displayed a significant reduction in tumor volume at 24 h compared to 6 h postinjection (Figure 4A). Figure 4B and SI Table S1 depict the statistical analysis of CT imaging data for 11 mice. The results suggest a decrease in tumor volume from 0.34 ± 0.06 cm3 at 6 h postinjection to 0.20 ± 0.05 cm3 at 24 h postinjection. Moreover, the tumor ED decreased from 1708.36 ± 233.44 e Å–3 to 926.73 ± 198.13 e Å–3. These findings suggest that in the Au NP group, NPs in the tumor were metabolized after 6 h postinjection. This resulted in an effective imaging duration of <24 h (defined as the time during which no significant changes in tumor enhancement volume could be observed). Furthermore, the maximum liver ED was 71.01 ± 11.22 e Å–3 at 24 h postinjection, which declined to 67.45 ± 9.92 e Å–3 by 48 h. The Au NPs did not significantly elevate the liver ED and were rapidly metabolized in normal liver tissue.

While analyzing the charged Au NP group of 14 mice over 6–144 h postinjection, 7 mice completed CT imaging and consistently exhibited high-density lesions across all time points (Figure 4C). Statistical analysis of CT imaging data for the seven mice indicated a gradual increase in the ED over time, which peaked at 144 h (from 1705.86 ± 194.58 to 2450.14 ± 163.01 e Å–3) (Figure 4D and SI Table S2). This increase was associated with the effective aggregation of charged Au NPs in tumors. Additionally, the tumor volume remained stable (average change < 0.03 cm3) from 6 to 120 h postinjection. However, a significant reduction was observed at 144 h relative to the peak volume at 24 h (average decrease = 34.3%). This reduction may be attributed to the accelerated metabolism and degradation of charged Au NPs in the tumor after 120 h. Thus, only the larger-sized charged Au NP particles remained, resulting in a substantial ED increase and a volume decline. Our analysis indicates that the charged Au NPs demonstrated strong aggregation properties in the weakly acidic environment of in situ liver tumors, thereby effectively extending their metabolic duration in the tumor with an effective imaging time of up to 120 h. Furthermore, the liver ED statistics evidenced minimal retention and rapid metabolism of charged Au NPs in normal liver tissue. Pathology confirmed abnormal masses on the liver as hepatocellular carcinoma within the 36 mice used in the experiments with imaging enhancement failure noted in four mice, possibly due to unsuccessful tail vein injection (SI Figure S2).

The above study findings indicate that both Au NPs and charged Au NPs significantly enhanced the contrast of sHCC in liver CT images (SI Figure S3) and demonstrated minimal toxicity within biocompatibility experiments. This suggests that gold nanoparticles are well suited for targeted enhanced CT imaging in HCC because of their strong X-ray absorption and excellent biocompatibility.29,30 Additionally, tumor microenvironment-responsive nanozyme technology facilitates nanoprobe creation that activates under specific tumor conditions, such as acidity and high hydrogen peroxide concentration. This induces tumor-specific catalytic reactions elevating or delaying imaging signals.30−35 Leveraging the unique properties of nanozyme technology, the superb X-ray imaging performance of gold nanoparticles, and the acidic tumor microenvironment, our enzyme-modified charged Au NPs significantly extended the adequate imaging time up to 120 h compared with <24 h for the Au NP group. In a previous study, Li et al.35 employed a similar approach to the one described above. They modified gold nanoparticles using thiol-binding molecules to regulate pH-induced aggregation. However, their approach was effective for only 48 h in subcutaneous tumors. The approach was shorter than the 120 h duration observed here. Current image-based identification and ART alignment for liver cancer depend on liver morphology and location, which reduces the accuracy. Some researchers have used metal markers in liver cancers before radiotherapy to position tumors with CT scans. However, this method enhances costs and involves invasive procedures.36,37 In our study, the charged Au NPs enable sustained contrast-enhanced CT imaging of sHCC for over 120 h using noninvasive methods. Thus, tumor identification and alignment in sHCC ART–SBRT happened within 1 week after a single injection, suggesting significant clinical potential.

We used 62 sets of radiation oncologist-labeled effective imaging CT data from the charged AuNP group to evaluate the performance of deep learning algorithms for automated segmentation of sHCC in charged Au NP-enhanced CT images. The labeled data exhibited high dice similarity coefficient (DSC) values in both intraobserver and interobserver variability tests to achieve scores of 87.12 ± 1.12% and 85.12 ± 1.12%, respectively. The sets were divided into 50 training and 12 testing sets, and the training set was elevated to 200 through data augmentation. Our assessment employed the 3D U-Net and 3D Trans U-Net models (Figure 5A). It demonstrated strong generalizability and effectiveness through 10-fold cross-validation (Figure 5B,C and SI Table S3), thereby achieving similar performance (P < 0.05).38 The study showed high geometric accuracy with the mean DSC at 81.71% for 3D U-Net and 83.50% for 3D Trans U-Net, with the latter showing closer alignment with radiation oncologist segmentations (Figure 5E,G and SI Table S4). In SBRT treatment planning simulations, both models maintained high dosimetric precision. However, the 3D Trans U-Net outperformed with a 5000 cGy dose coverage of 95.5% ± 1.4% and a conformity index (CI) of 0.84 ± 0.04 (Figure 5F,H and SI Table S5). These results suggest that both AI models are effective. Still, the 3D Trans U-Net’s superior geometric and dosimetric accuracy are more suitable for this task. This stems from integrating U-Net’s segmentation strength and the Transformer’s contextual awareness. This study used two classic AI models to automatically segment sHCC in CT images enhanced by charged Au NPs.

The advancement of AI image segmentation technology, particularly deep learning–based techniques, has significantly affected online ART. However, accurately segmenting small and low-contrast target tissues is a challenge for current AI techniques11,39 explicitly involving the automatic segmentation of sHCCs on plain CT images. U-Net-based deep learning frameworks have demonstrated robustness and scalability in medical image segmentation with extensive research focusing on their application in the automated segmentation of liver tumors.39−46 Our study indicated that by using charged Au NP contrast the 3D U-Net and 3D Trans U-Net models achieved high segmentation accuracy on CT images of tumor-bearing mice. Notably, the 3D Trans U-Net model outperformed the enhanced 3D U-Net model with a mean DSC of 83.50%, as described elsewhere.39−46 HCC shows high contrast and resolution on MR images, which often allows for better automatic segmentation.43 In this study, the automatic segmentation results of sHCC in CT images are consistent with the automatic segmentation results of MR images in previous studies.43 This further confirms the effectiveness of the charged gold-nanoparticle-targeted contrast agent method applied to the AI automatic segmentation of sHCC in CT images. We postulate that utilizing the charged Au NP-targeted contrast agent will consistently aid an AI model in precisely and automatically segmenting the small target volume of sHCC on CT images within approximately 1 week in the radiotherapy course. The combination of nanotechnology and AI presents a promising solution for automating tumor segmentation in CT-guided online ART for sHCC.

In this study, we achieved high-contrast imaging and precise automated segmentation of sHCC in CT images of tumor-bearing mice over a continuous period of 5 days. A single injection was sufficient to meet the needs for tumor identification–alignment and automatic segmentation in ART for sHCC within 1 week. This breakthrough addresses the bottleneck in online ART technology for sHCC on the basis of CT images. Our innovative integration of nanotechnology with AI opens new avenues for clinical applications in online ART for sHCC, thereby demonstrating the significant promise of nanoimaging-assisted AI techniques. Moreover, a novel approach was introduced to advance ART technology for other lower-resolution tumors in CT images. Previous investigations have demonstrated the accuracy of nanoreagent distribution and imaging in liver tumors.47−54 However, the study focused on designing a nano-CT-enhanced contrast agent for prolonged imaging in sHCC and establishing its efficacy in automatic segmentation for AI. The study had limitations, such as the unvalidated imaging accuracy of sHCC using the charged AuNP contrast agent and the limited data set for training the deep learning-based segmentation model. In future studies, the goal is to improve relevant research and create an integrated ART nanoagent platform capable of CT-enhanced imaging and radiotherapy sensitization functions for sHCC. This could mark a new era of convergence of nanotechnology and AI for medical imaging and treatment.
