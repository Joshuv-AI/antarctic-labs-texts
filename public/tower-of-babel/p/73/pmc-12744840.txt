Disease-modifying effect of efruxifermin in compensated cirrhosis due to MASH: To miss the forest for the tree(s)
Authors: Mattias Mandorfer, Georg Semmler
Published: 2025

Noureddin M, Rinella ME, Chalasani NP, et al. Efruxifermin in Compensated Liver Cirrhosis Caused by MASH. N Engl J Med. 2025 Jun 26;392(24):2413-2424. doi: 10.1056/NEJMoa2502242.

The first effective treatment for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)-related compensated cirrhosis is still awaited. Just recently, the SYMMETRY phase IIb study on efruxifermin, a bivalent fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) analogue, has been published, investigating fibrosis improvement without worsening of MASH after 36 (primary endpoint) and 96 weeks (secondary endpoint).1 Following an overwhelmingly positive press release in January 2025, the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis including 181 patients (78% with MASH, 22% with cryptogenic cirrhosis, Table 1) showed no significant differences for the primary endpoint (13% [placebo] vs. 18% [28 mg] vs. 19% [50 mg]), but a signal towards an improvement after 96 weeks: 11% vs. 21% vs. 29%. As expected, the per protocol analysis for the same time point (comprising only 134/181 patients) indicated numerically higher response rates (15% vs. 29% vs. 39%), which are also supported by consistent improvements in non-invasive fibrosis biomarkers. After a period of trials and tribulations in MASH-related compensated cirrhosis (Table 1), these signals should not be underestimated. Choosing histological cirrhosis regression as the primary endpoint sets the bar very high. Focusing on this single aspect (i.e. ‘tree’) poses the risk of overlooking the potential ‘forest’ of clinically meaningful benefit that – in case of efruxifermin – will hopefully be revealed by the ongoing phase III clinical trial capturing direct endpoints (Fig. 1).

Focusing on histological cirrhosis regression as a single surrogate (i.e. ‘tree’) poses the risk of overlooking the ‘forest’ of therapeutic efficacy. Notably, prevention of hepatic decompensation (i.e. clinically meaningful benefit) is more directly conferred by the prevention of progression to CSPH or regression of CSPH, as applicable. The ongoing phase III clinical trial on efruxifermin captures direct endpoints, and thus, the magnitude of efruxifermin’s disease-modifying potential in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis-related compensated cirrhosis. CSPH, clinically significant portal hypertension.

From a clinical and patient-centric perspective, compensated cirrhosis/F4 is not a uniform condition. It rather represents a continuum of decompensation (i.e. morbidity) probability ranging from negligible to substantial in individual patients, with portal hypertension being the key surrogate of this risk.3,4 Specifically, clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH, as defined by a minimally invasively assessed hepatic venous pressure gradient ≥10 mmHg) denotes the hemodynamic threshold for hepatic decompensation.3,4 In clinical practice, the probability of CSPH is estimated by non-invasive models,5 such as the ANTICIPATE±NASH or the Non-Invasive CSPH Estimated Risk (NICER) models, with the latter attaining a discriminative ability as high as ∼0.9.6 This enables quantification of an individual’s decompensation risk, which affects the absolute risk reduction/number-needed to treat of disease-modifying treatments. A higher CSPH probability enriches clinical trials with individuals at risk, which translates into a lower number-needed to treat to prevent hepatic decompensation (desirable for phase III trials), but at the same time, a lower probability of cirrhosis regression (problematic for phase II trials relying on this endpoint), as the disease is simply more advanced.

Coming back to Noureddin et al.’s seminal study on efruxifermin,1 we roughly estimated an overall CSPH probability of 34% by imputing the study population’s means. Notably, even with a prototypical aetiological therapy such as HCV cure, CSPH – and therefore cirrhosis – persisted in 75% of patients after a period that aligns with the chosen 36-week timeframe. Moving to histological endpoints, more than half of individuals with HCV-induced compensated advanced chronic liver disease and an estimated CSPH prevalence of 41% before treatment (i.e. comparable to the phase IIb study on efruxifermin) had histological cirrhosis after a median time of ∼3 years.8 Importantly, the rate of cirrhosis regression following removal/suppression of the primary aetiological factor is clearly a function of time (measured in years rather than weeks), as evidenced by studies in people living with HBV infection.9 Accordingly, even for a universally effective treatment, histological cirrhosis regression is quite uncommon during the first 1-2 years, and thus, inclusion of patients with a low probability of CSPH, high(er) sample size, long treatment periods, or combinations thereof are required to increase chances of success in phase II studies when relying on histological cirrhosis regression. A potential workaround would be a shift to more advanced patients for phase II, i.e. those with CSPH, and to replace histology with measurement of hepatic venous pressure gradient, which seems to be more dynamic, potentially allowing for shorter treatment durations (e.g. 1 year), and providing more granular information regarding hepatic decompensation risk reduction. Whether such a shift in disease severity has implications for the efficacy of certain mechanisms of action remains to be investigated. For instance, FGF21 regulates energy homeostasis, and thus, efruxifermin improves metabolic dysfunction.2 While the latter is is a key driver of disease progression, its relevance in established CSPH is less certain.

Important phenotypic differences, including BMI, diabetes, and the presence of cryptogenic cirrhosis, distinguish the MASH-related compensated cirrhosis populations studied in recent trials (Table 1). The influence of these factors on treatment response in general, or on the efficacy of particular mechanisms of action, remains largely unknown and requires further study. All things considered, histological cirrhosis regression represents the top of the hierarchy of potential surrogate endpoints (Fig.1) and is not a conditio sine qua non for diminishing decompensation risk. Thus, solely focusing on histological cirrhosis regression poses the risk of overlooking the ‘forest’ of clinically meaningful benefit. Importantly, preventing progression to CSPH or achieving regression of CSPH are sufficient to preclude hepatic decompensation in the absence of hepatocellular carcinoma7,10 and are expected to occur in a substantially higher proportion of patients than histological cirrhosis regression. While these surrogate endpoints were not captured in the SYMMETRY phase IIb study,1 the trajectories of portal hypertension will be decisive when moving to phase III, as they determine the incidence of hepatic decompensation, a key constituent of the composite endpoint that may lead to approval.

In conclusion, the SYMMETRY phase IIb study on efruxifermin1 provides encouraging results on cirrhosis regression, an outcome that would be highly desirable but is hard to achieve. Moreover, it only partially captures the potential therapeutic benefit of disease-modifying drugs. Thus, the community is eagerly anticipating the phase III results on direct endpoints (SYNCHRONY Outcomes, NCT06528314), which is run in parallel to the MAESTRO-NASH-OUTCOMES study (NCT05500222) investigating the thyroid hormone receptor β agonist resmetirom, the ENLIGHTEN-Cirrhosis study (NCT06419374) on the FGF21 analogue pegozafermin, and the LIVERAGE-Cirrhosis study (NCT06632457) on the incretin analogue survodutide.
