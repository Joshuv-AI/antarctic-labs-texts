Response to “Photon-Counting CT in Cardiovascular Imaging: From Technical Promise to Clinical Translation”
Authors: Prabhakar Shantha Rajiah, James M. Williams, Michael LaVere, Shuai Leng, Phillip M. Young
Published: 2026

We thank Drs. Ge and Zhang [1] for their thoughtful and constructive comments on our review article, “Photon-Counting CT in Cardiovascular Imaging: Clinical Applications” [2]. We appreciate their emphasis on key considerations in translating photon-counting CT (PCCT) from a technical innovation into routine clinical practice. As with any emerging imaging technology, this transition requires validation, standardization, and continued investigation. We welcome the opportunity to further address these important issues.

First, Ge and Zhang [1] highlight the distinction between improvements in imaging performance and the demonstration of patient-centered clinical outcomes. We agree that this distinction is critical when evaluating new imaging technologies. Early investigations of PCCT focused on imaging metrics such as spatial resolution, contrast-to-noise ratio, artifact reduction, and spectral imaging capabilities [34]. These parameters are not merely technical surrogates but also fundamental determinants of diagnostic accuracy and reliability. Emerging evidence suggests that these technical advantages translate into clinically meaningful benefits, particularly with ultra-high-resolution (UHR) imaging. Several studies have demonstrated that reduced blooming artifacts with PCCT improve the assessment of luminal stenosis in heavily calcified plaques and coronary stents, with higher accuracy than conventional CT scans, using invasive coronary angiography as the reference standard [56]. These scenarios have traditionally limited the specificity and positive predictive value of conventional CT coronary angiography. Early data also suggest that improved diagnostic confidence with PCCT may reduce unnecessary downstream testing and enhance the efficiency of clinical decision-making [4]. For example, Sakai et al. [7] reported in a cohort of 7,833 patients that PCCT was associated with fewer referrals to invasive coronary angiography (9.9% vs. 13.1%), lower rates of unnecessary angiography (5.6% vs. 8.4%), and higher revascularization rates among those referred (43.4% vs. 35.5%). Similarly, Vecsey-Nagy et al. [8] demonstrated potential cost savings with PCCT through reductions in follow-up functional testing (18.9%), invasive angiography (6%), and procedural complications (9.4%). We agree that larger multicenter studies evaluating outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and resource utilization are needed to further define the clinical impact of PCCT.

Second, Ge and Zhang [1] emphasize the importance of standardization and reproducibility across acquisition protocols and imaging platforms. PCCT introduces multiple acquisition and reconstruction parameters, including scan mode, detector thresholds, reconstruction kernels, iterative reconstruction strength, matrix size, and spectral post-processing, all of which can influence quantitative imaging metrics [2]. Similar challenges were encountered with earlier technologies, such as dual-energy CT. Recent studies have begun to address these issues. For example, Skoog et al. [9] demonstrated that reconstruction kernel selection and virtual monoenergetic imaging parameters significantly affect segmentation-based coronary plaque measurements, underscoring the need for protocol harmonization. These findings highlight the importance of standardized acquisition protocols and quantitative thresholds, particularly for emerging applications, such as plaque characterization, iodine quantification, myocardial extracellular volume (ECV) measurement, and virtual calcium scoring. As PCCT platforms from multiple vendors become available in the near future, standardization across systems will become increasingly important. Collaborative efforts among academic institutions, professional societies and the industry essential for establishing consensus guidelines and reproducible workflows.

Third, Ge and Zhang [1] raise important considerations regarding the performance of PCCT in real-world patient populations. Cardiovascular CT is frequently performed in patients with obesity, tachyarrhythmia, extensive calcification, prior intervention, and limited breath-holding capacity. UHR imaging modes may increase image noise, particularly in larger patients, whereas smaller detector collimation may increase sensitivity to motion artifacts. These factors underscore the need to tailor acquisition strategies to patients and clinical questions. UHR imaging may be most beneficial for evaluating coronary stents, small vessels, and heavily calcified plaques, whereas multienergy modes are preferable when spectral information, such as iodine maps or virtual monoenergetic reconstructions, is required. In larger patients or when noise is a concern, standard-resolution multi-energy imaging may provide a more favorable balance between image quality and radiation dose.

Fourth, the authors suggested the need for a clearer distinction between established and emerging applications of PCCT. We agree that the current evidence spans a spectrum of maturity. The benefits of UHR imaging for assessing calcified plaques, coronary stents, and small vessels are increasingly supported by studies across diverse patient populations. In contrast, spectral applications, including plaque separation, plaque characterization, myocardial perfusion imaging, late iodine enhancement, and ECV quantification remain investigational [1011121314]. Early studies have demonstrated encouraging correlation between PCCT-derived ECV and MRI [11]; however, further validation in larger cohorts is required before routine clinical implementation. Moreover, the incremental benefits over existing spectral CT techniques remain understudied.

From a practical standpoint, the clinical implementation of PCCT is expanding. Early institutional experience suggests advantages in evaluating heavily calcified coronary arteries, coronary stents, small vessels, and complex vascular anatomies. In these settings, improved spatial resolution and spectral imaging enhance visualization and diagnostic confidence compared with conventional CT. Although these observations are preliminary and institution-specific, they illustrate how technical advances may translate into meaningful improvements in routine clinical practice.

In summary, we appreciate the insightful comments of Ge and Zhang [1] and agree that continued investigation is essential to define the clinical role of PCCT. Ongoing multicenter studies, protocol standardization, and technical refinements are critical to translating these capabilities into improved patient outcomes. We believe that PCCT represents an important advancement in cardiovascular imaging and look forward to continued collaborative efforts to ensure responsible and effective clinical implementation.
