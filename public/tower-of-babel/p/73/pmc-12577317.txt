Background

Ectopic pregnancy remains the leading cause of haemorrhage-related maternal mortality [1], with 2–4% of ectopic pregnancies occurring in the cornual region of the uterus [2]. The development of cornual pregnancy is associated with pelvic inflammatory disease, uterine malformations, previous salpingectomy, and uterine surgery [3]. In recent years, assisted reproductive technology has also emerged a significant risk factor for cornual pregnancy [4].

Cornual pregnancy, responsible for 20% of all maternal deaths from ectopic pregnancy [5], possesses distinct anatomical characteristics. The cornual region features a relatively thin muscle layer, reduced extensibility, and an abundant blood supply at the junction between the uterine artery and ovarian artery branches. Consequently, while rupture typically occurs later than in tubal pregnancies, cornual pregnancies carry a greater risk of life-threatening, catastrophic hemorrhage [6].

Currently, conservative laparoscopic surgery represents the predominant approach for managing traditional cornual pregnancy, primarily comprising: laparoscopic cornual resection; laparoscoic mini-cornual excision; cornual wedge resection; cornuectomy with salpingectomy; hysteroscopic evacuation of pregnancy tissue [7–10]. While these established techniques preserve fertility, ensure complete removal of gestational products, and minimise rebleeding risk, they carry potential iatrogenic complications. The resultant thinning of the uterine wall may predispose to abnormal placentation in subsequent pregnancies. Furthermore, postoperative scarring and myometrial weakness may elevate the risk of uterine rupture. The indications for cervical curettage under ultrasound or laparoscopic monitoring are limited, and the uterine horn muscle layer around the gestational sac is thin, with poor contractility and increased risk of bleeding [11, 12] .

In order to minimize intraoperative bleeding and reduce the risk of damaging uterine integrity, this study used transvaginal suction of pregnancy materials under laparoscopic monitoring, while non penetrating suturing was performed around the pregnancy site to reduce bleeding.

Case presentation

The patient was a 23‑year‑old woman, gravida 2, para 0 (with one missed abortion), who presented with 53 days of amenorrhoea and two weeks of irregular vaginal bleeding. Her menarche occurred at the age of 14, with previously regular menstrual cycles (lasting 4 days, every 30 days). Aside from the irregular vaginal bleeding and mild nausea, she reported no other discomfort.

Prior to admission, transvaginal Doppler ultrasound revealed a hypoechoic area containing a gestational sac (~ 15 mm in length) within the right cornua uteri (26 × 20 mm), with visible primitive heart tube pulsations. The outer margin of the gestational sac was situated approximately 5 mm from the myometrial serosa. No free fluid was detected in the pelvic cavity. The serum human chorionic gonadotropin level was 24,953 IU/L. On gynecological examination, there was no cervical motion tenderness, and only a small amount of vaginal blood was observed. Uterine and adnexal palpation elicited no tenderness. Given the ultrasonographic findings and elevated hCG level, a cornual pregnancy was suspected. Following detailed counseling on treatment options, the patient—who expressed a strong desire for future fertility—opted for uterine aspiration under laparoscopic guidance.

Under general anaesthesia, the patient was placed in the lithotomy position. A Veress needle was inserted at the umbilical level to establish pneumoperitoneum, followed by placement of a 10-mm umbilical trocar for the camera system. Two additional 5-mm secondary ports were inserted in the left upper and lower abdominal quadrants. Systematic laparoscopic exploration of the abdominal and pelvic structures was performed in a clockwise manner. No active bleeding was observed in the pelvic cavity. A 3.5 cm bulging mass was identified in the right uterine cornua, displacing the round ligament (Fig. 1A). Both ovaries and fallopian tubes appeared morphologically normal.

A The initial presentation of an unruptured cornual pregnancy (The black arrow indicates the bulging at the right cornual region). B Transvaginal suction procedure under laparoscopic guidance (the black arrow indicates the steel suction tube within the uterine cavity)

A conservative laparoscopic approach was employed, adhering to the following key surgical principles: (1) purse-string suture applied on the base of the cornual pregnancy mass using 2 − 0 absorbable suture material (Ethicon). Simple interrupted sutures applied at 1 cm apart. The needle insertion depth is 2–4 mm and does not penetrate the uterine cavity (Fig. 2A). (2) transcervical suction evacuation of the cornual pregnancy (5-mm suction cannula. Negative pressure of 100–200 mm Hg) (Fig. 1B) followed with immediate tightening of the purse-string suture. (3) using the same stitching method to invaginate the purse-string suture and reinforce the cornual wall (Fig. 2B).

A Suture diagram of laparoscopic surgical sutures (not tightened). B Laparoscopic appearance of the uterus following transvaginal suction evacuation and two-layer suturing

The estimated intraoperative blood loss was 15 ml, with a total procedure duration of 20 min. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of cornual pregnancy. The patient’s serum β-Hcg level decreased to 1000U/L on postoperative day one, and she was discharged the following day following an uncomplicated recovery. Serum β-hCG levels were tested once a week, zero within two weeks. The patient reported no postoperative complications and expressed satisfaction with the treatment outcome. At the six-month follow-up, the patient underwent diagnostic hysteroscopy in preparation for pregnancy. The examination revealed a normal uterine cavity with no evidence of adhesions at the right cornual region (Fig. 3).

Hysteroscopic view of the right uterine cornu six months postoperatively

Following hysteroscopic assessment, the patient commenced pregnancy planning and achieved natural conception after twelve months. Antenatal surveillance progressed uneventfully. At 37 + 5 weeks’ gestation, she underwent lower segment caesarean section for dysfunctional labour, delivering a male neonate weighing 3360 g with good Apgar scores. Intraoperative findings demonstrated symmetrical myometrial thickness at both cornual regions (Fig. 4A), with satisfactory uterine contractility and minimal blood loss (Fig. 4B). The postoperative recovery was uncomplicated, permitting discharged on the third day.

Discussion

Cornual pregnancy is a rare form of ectopic pregnancy, arising from the implantation of the embryo within the mucosa at the junction of the fallopian tube and the uterine cavity. Although its incidence is low, b rupture carries a significant risk of severe haemorrhage and high maternal mortality. Early diagnosis and prompt intervention have been associated with improved outcomes [13].

Currently, the early diagnosis of cornual pregnancy relies primarily on transvaginal color Doppler ultrasound and elevated serum β-hCG levels [14]. Three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound may provide additional diagnostic support [15], while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has also been reported to facilitate early detection [16]. Nevertheless, distinguishing between cornual and interstitial pregnancies remains a diagnostic challenge [17]. According to expert consensus, the diagnostic criteria for cornual pregnancy should include: (1) An empty uterine cavity, (2) A gestational sac located medial to the round ligament (or displacement/extrusion of the ligament), surrounded by a myometrial layer >5 mm in thickness, and (3) The absence of an interstitial line sign [18]. Owing to the unique vascular anatomy of the cornual region—characterised by rich blood supply and a thin muscular layer—rupture carries a high risk of uncontrolled intraperitoneal haemorrhage. Thus, there is a pressing need for improved early diagnostic and intervention strategies.

The management of cornual pregnancy primarily involves conservative medical therapy, surgical intervention and “wait-and-see” [19]. Methotrexate, a folate antagonist that inhibits DNA synthesis, is the most commonly employed medical treatment for ectopic pregnancies. However, its use is associated with a failure rate of 9–65% [20], which has been correlated with foetal cardiac activity and elevated β-hCG levels [21]. The surgical approaches for cornual pregnancy include: minimally invasive techniques (laparoscopic or ultrasound-guided hysteroscopic procedures), Conventional open procedures (cornual wedge resection and salpingectomy), cornuostomy [22]. While hysteroscopic surgery represents fertility-preserving approach that maintains uterine cavity integrity, it is associated with several limitations, including prolonged operative duration, increased intraoperative blood loss, and potential for significant surgical complications. Laparoscopic cornual wedge resection and cornuostomy currently represent the two most frequently employed surgical procedures. A case series of 17 cornual pregnancies treated by laparoscopic wedge resection demonstrated reduced conversion rates to laparotomy; however, this approach requires longer operative durations and results in greater myometrial disruption compared to our present case [23]. Persistent or recurrent interstitial pregnancy has been documented, with studies reporting elevated uterine rupture rates following cornual wedge resection [23]. Research has additionally investigated combined cornual resection with ipsilateral salpingectomy, which appears to decrease both intraoperative and postoperative rebleeding risks while minimising residual tissue retention [6]. However, salpingectomy may compromise the ovarian blood supply derived from the uterine artery branches within the mesosalpinx, potentially impairing ovarian reserve function. While these laparoscopic procedures are less invasive than conventional laparotomy and enable complete recision of the gestational mass, they nevertheless result in endometrial integrity disruption. Furthermore, the resultant thinning and weakening of the cornual myometrium elevates risks in subsequent pregnancies, including abnormal placentation and uterine rupture [24].

Two fundamental principles must be observed in the surgical management of cornual pregnancy: (1) complete excision of the gestational mass, and (2) effective haemostasis. Alternative approaches have been explored, including ultrasound-guided transvaginal negative pressure aspiration for interstitial pregnancy. In our presented case, laparoscopic intervention was selected due to its superior visualisation compared to Doppler ultrasound. This approach facilitates precise identification of bleeding points and enables immediate intraoperative haemostasis. Furthermore, any uterine perforation can be promptly identified and managed under direct laparoscopic visualisation.

Our case demonstrated favourable operative outcomes, with reduced procedure duration and minimal blood loss. We observed the mass retracted briefly after transvaginal negative pressure suction but soon bulged again. The cornual pregnancy’s anatomical structure carries high post-aspiration bleeding risk. Following gestational sac aspiration, the cyst cavity enlarged, indicating rebleeding. Uterine contraction disorderdue to cornual muscle defects prevented effective haemostasis. Therefore, prior to suction, a purse-string suture was placed at the base of the mass and tightened immediately after suction. Secondary folding sutures were then applied to minimise postoperative bleeding. No adverse complications occurred, and the patient recovered well. In this case, the stretched myometrium at the implantation site was also sutured twice to prevent postoperative recurrence of bleeding. Folding sutures of the muscle layer promote healing and reduce the risk of muscle defect formation.

This case report demonstrates the feasibility of managing cornual pregnancy surgically without myometrium incision, an approach that is particularly for preserving reproductive potential. Compared to current surgical methods such as incision or resection, they can damage the integrity of the uterus and alter the thickness of the muscle layer [7]. The indications for laparoscopic or ultrasound-guided cervical evacuation surgery for ectopic pregnancy are limited [11], as the local muscle layer in the uterine horn is thin, the contraction force is poor, and there is an increased risk of bleeding. Our method of adding local purse string suturing can reduce bleeding at the site of gestational sac attachment.

Our surgical approach also has some shortcomings, as we were unable to accurately assess whether the patency of the uterine horn and fallopian tubes was affected during the operation. After surgery, the patency of the fallopian tubes and the thickness of the bilateral uterine horn muscle layer were not evaluated, and the thickness of the uterine horn muscle layer was not emphasized during pregnancy. Further research will increase relevant evaluations.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrated that laparoscopic surgery can maintain myometrial integrity in the management of cornual pregnancy. However, the procedure presents technical challenges due to the region’s abundant vascular supply and the thin, stretched muscle layer, which complicate suction evacuation. Additionally, the risk of intraoperative uterine perforation necessitates a surgeon with advanced expertise in precise suturing, the ability to manage emergent bleeding effectively, and proficiency in achieving rapid haemostasis.
