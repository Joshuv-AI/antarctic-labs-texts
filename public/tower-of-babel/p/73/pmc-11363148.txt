Advances in Extracellular-Vesicles-Based Diagnostic and Therapeutic Approaches for Ocular Diseases
Authors: Yun Su, Moxin Chen, Wei Xu, Ping Gu, Xianqun Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale membrane vesicles of various sizes that can be secreted by most cells. EVs contain a diverse array of cargo, including RNAs, lipids, proteins, and other molecules with functions of intercellular communication, immune modulation, and regulation of physiological and pathological processes. The biofluids in the eye, including tears, aqueous humor, and vitreous humor, are important sources for EV-based diagnosis of ocular disease. Because the molecular cargos may reflect the biology of their parental cells, EVs in these biofluids, as well as in the blood, have been recognized as promising candidates as biomarkers for early diagnosis of ocular disease. Moreover, EVs have also been used as therapeutics and targeted drug delivery nanocarriers in many ocular disorders because of their low immunogenicity and superior biocompatibility in nature. In this review, we provide an overview of the recent advances in the field of EV-based studies on the diagnosis and therapeutics of ocular disease. We summarized the origins of EVs applied in ocular disease, assessed different methods for EV isolation from ocular biofluid samples, highlighted bioengineering strategies of EVs as drug delivery systems, introduced the latest applications in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular disease, and presented their potential in the current clinical trials. Finally, we briefly discussed the challenges of EV-based studies in ocular disease and some issues of concern for better focusing on clinical translational studies of EVs in the future.Keywords: Extracellular vesicles, ocular disease, diagnosis, therapeutics, isolation, ocular drug delivery, blood−ocular barrier, anterior segment, posterior segment

Introduction

The human eye is a highly specialized organ containing structures, including the cornea, iris, lens, and retina, which receive a large amount of information from the external world. Therefore, the integrity of each structure is essential for light perception and normal visual function. Due to the complexity of the eye, factors such as inflammation, immune imbalance, and carcinogenesis can cause a variety of blindness-causing diseases, such as glaucoma,1 age-related macular degeneration,2 and uveal melanoma,3 which affect more than 20 million people worldwide, leading to a large burden on individuals, families, and society.4 The current diagnostic strategy for ocular disease consists of a series of eye examinations and relies heavily on complete testing equipment, including slit lamp examination, optical coherence tomography (OCT), color fundus photography (CFP), and electroretinogram (ERG), which are time-consuming for both physicians and patients. Besides, the experience of an ophthalmologist greatly affects the accuracy of the diagnosis. Therefore, an inexpensive, less time-consuming, and highly objective diagnostic test for ocular diseases would be of great benefit. In the field of treatment, surgical and pharmaceutical interventions are the major treatment strategies for ocular diseases. Due to the invasive nature of surgery and limited efficacy of single-target drugs, the development of alternative therapies with high safety and efficiency will be a great breakthrough.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are cell-derived nanoparticles that contain such cargos as RNAs, lipids, and proteins,5−7 providing a large number of informative molecules which can be used as biomarkers for diagnosis and functional molecules for treatments.8−10 EVs are widely distributed in all ocular biofluids, such as tear fluid, aqueous humor, and vitreous humor.11−13 Recent studies have demonstrated a significant difference in the carrier composition of EVs between patients with a wide range of ocular diseases and healthy controls, indicating a great potential for EVs to be a rapid and cost-effective diagnostic approach.14−19 The functions of EVs include intercellular communications, immune modulation, and regulation of physiological and pathological processes.20−22 When EVs bind to the recipient cells, they transmit the cargo into these cells, thereby regulating the function of the recipient cells. It has been reported that EVs also play an important role in the immune response and modulation of inflammation and angiogenesis.23,24 For example, EVs can regulate immune system balance via modulating macrophage polarization, thereby facilitating cornea healing, and inhibiting ocular tumor metastasis as well.25−27 Particularly, as EVs are released from cells, their toxicity and immunogenicity are relatively low, which enables EVs to be promising nanocarriers for precision therapy.28 In addition, various modification methods, such as therapeutic payload encapsulation, surface membrane modification, and genetic modification, can also be applied to engineer EVs to enhance their therapeutic efficacy.29−32 Therefore, the multifunctionality, low toxicity, low immunogenicity, and high engineerable nature make EVs an up-and coming star for therapeutics in ocular diseases.33,34

Herein, this review aims to summarize the recent advances in the field of EV-based studies on the diagnosis and therapeutics of ocular disease by discussing: (1) the origins of EVs and different isolation methods for EVs used for the diagnosis of ocular diseases, (2) bioengineering strategy of EVs as drug delivery systems, (3) the latest advances in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases and their clinical potential, and (4) challenges and future perspectives of EV-based studies in ocular diseases. This Perspective will provide insights for future EV-based research, promoting clinical translational studies of EVs in the future.

Origin, Isolation, and Bioengineering of EVs in Ocular Disease

For an EV-based diagnosis or treatment for ocular diseases, EVs need to be first produced in cells and isolated from biofluids or cells. Apart from isolation, EVs that are being bioengineered, if necessary, will enter the ocular system for therapeutic purposes through different routes. Therefore, the biogenesis process, various origins, isolation methods, bioengineering strategies, and administration routes of EVs in ocular disease will be discussed in detail.

Biogenesis of EVs

EVs are generally categorized into ectosomes and exosomes based on size and biogenesis mechanisms. Ectosomes are EVs with diameters of 100–1000 nm, produced by outward curvature of the plasma membrane.35 The production of exosomes (diameter: 30–150 nm) is more complex and comprises three major steps involving the endocytic pathway. First, the inward budding of cytoplasmic membrane forms early endosomes, which further develop into late endosomes containing intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) formed by the curvature of the microsomal membrane. Second, membrane budding and fission induced by post-translational modifications lead to the formation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) packaging with functional molecules including RNAs, lipids, and proteins. Third, vesicles within MVBs fuse with cytoplasmic membrane and ultimately form exosomes, which are then released into extracellular matrix.36,37 Once released, EVs are present in surrounding biofluids and blood as they cross the tissue barriers or travel through the circulatory system, so that they can be detected and isolated for use in the diagnosis and treatment of diseases38 (Figure 1).

Origin of EVs Applied in Ocular Disease

EVs applied in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular disease come from a variety of origins, which can be categorized as the ocular system, circulatory system, and stem cells. EVs derived from ocular biofluids (tears, aqueous humor, and vitreous humor) and serum/plasma are mostly applied to diagnose ocular diseases due to their relatively easy accessibility. On the other hand, EVs originating from cells, including ocular cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and neural progenitor cells, have been used to treat a series of ocular diseases. Origin of EVs Applied in Ocular Diseases

Ocular System

EVs isolated from ocular biofluids, such as tears and aqueous humor, have been widely reported for the diagnosis of ocular diseases.14,39−41 For example, in EVs isolated from the tears of keratoconus patients, a significant decrease in CD63+/CD9+ and CD63+/CD81+/CD9+ was found compared to controls, providing a promising strategy for early diagnosis of keratoconus.39 Additionally, Mueller et al. extracted EVs from aqueous humor, and discovered that glaucomatous patients had smaller EVs and lower levels of STT3B protein compared with healthy controls.14 Other than ocular biofluids-derived EVs, several studies also utilized EVs released from ocular cell lines, such as retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, for the treatment of ocular diseases.42,43 The RPE is the outermost layer of the retina, and EVs derived from RPE cells are enriched in proteins related to the mechanisms of age-related macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa, which may contribute to a higher efficiency of EV-based therapy for retinal diseases.43 However, due to the low yields, methods for isolation and purification of EVs derived from ocular biofluids and ocular cell lines still need to be improved.

Circulatory System

As EVs are able to enter the bloodstream after release by ocular cells, isolation of EVs from serum or plasma has also been reported for the diagnosis of ocular disorders, especially for posterior ocular diseases like optic nerve disorder, age-related macular degeneration (AMD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), and uveal melanoma (UM).15,16,44,45 This has allowed the diagnosis of these diseases without the need for expensive morphologic and functional screening equipment. Studies have shown significant changes in the levels of a range of microRNAs (miRNAs) in patients with posterior ocular diseases, including hsa-miR-122-3p,15 miR-26b-5p,16 miR-19a,44 and hsa-miR-144-5p.45 In addition, two clinical trials using serum for the diagnosis of dry eye diseases (ChiCTR1900027452) and DR (NCT03264976) have also been conducted, which will lead to promising strategies for future diagnosis of ocular diseases.

Stem Cells

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), including bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs), human umbilical cord MSCs (HUMSCs), human placenta-derived MSCs (hPSCs), and adipose-derived MSCs (ADSCs), are mostly applied in the studies to generate EVs for treatment of ocular disorders in preclinical studies and clinical trials (NCT04213248, NCT05738629, IRCT20211102052948N1, NCT03437759, and NCT05413148) because of their abundant source of cells and well-established isolation methods.46−49 The application of EVs of corneal MSCs (cMSCs) isolated from human cadaver corneas has also been reported, though the source is limited, and the standardization of the extraction process remains challengeable.50 Another study used neural progenitor cells (NPCs)-derived EVs to treat retinal degenerations, which showed a protective effect on photoreceptors.51

Isolation Methods of EVs in Ocular Disease

EVs can be found within ocular biofluids, including tear fluid, aqueous humor, and vitreous humor, and are utilized for identifying disease biomarkers, evaluating disease progression, and facilitating the treatment of ocular disease (Figure 2). The common methods to isolate EVs from biofluids mainly include differential ultracentrifugation, polymer-based precipitation, size-exclusion chromatography, and immunoaffinity capture. However, due to the limited volume of ocular biofluids, usually less than 100 μL, the yield and purity of EVs cannot be guaranteed by these isolation methods. Let alone the high price and time-consuming procedures.52 By using ultracentrifugation, for example, EVs are easily damaged mechanically by centrifugal force, and it is also hard to effectively maintain the bioactivity and morphological integrity of EVs.53 Immunoaffinity capture is based on the specific binding between antibodies and exosomal proteins, but the available markers on the surface are usually not entirely specific to EVs, so the yield of EVs is low by this method.54 Therefore, recent developments in isolation methods are emerging to achieve EVs with high yield and purity and normal bioactivity and integrity. Examples of EVs Isolation Methods Applied in Ocular Diseases

Tear fluid is a noninvasively available biofluid on the surface of the eye that protects and lubricates the ocular surface. A series of proteins, glycoproteins, and lipids in the tears function to defend against infection, promote wound healing, and maintain high-quality vision.55 Compared to other biofluids, tear fluid is an ideal source for EVs due to the lower protein and debris content in tears.56 Their easy accessibility for biomarker analysis enables tear EVs to have great value in the diagnosis of pathological changes of the ocular system57−59 and nonocular disease.12,60,61 However, due to their limited volume and practical constraints, the clinical value of tear EVs cannot be fully utilized, especially in EV isolation and detection. Currently, ultracentrifugation is the main technique for tear EVs isolation, but its efficiency is limited by the low achievable purity, yield, integrity, and long processing time.62 To overcome these disadvantages, Chen et al. developed an exosome detection method via an ultrafast-isolation system (EXODUS) by introducing negative pressure oscillations and double-coupled resonators to generate dual frequency transverse waves on the membrane. The oscillation of nanoporous membranes and cartridges significantly suppressed fouling effects to avoid clogging and achieve ultrafast purification of exosomes.63 Based on this negative-pressure-oscillation strategy, Hu et al. proposed an incorporated tear-EVs analysis and isolation system (iTEARS) which could isolate EVs with high yield and purity from a small volume of tears (∼10 μL) within 5 min (Figure 3). This system with on-device detection also enabled quantitative analysis of exosomal signals by targeting antibodies to the membrane proteins, which was a promising platform for the discovery of biomarkers in tear EVs and the establishment of tear-EV-based precision medicine in ocular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer.64

Additionally, given the limited volume of ocular biofluids, it is crucial that these methods can isolate EVs directly from raw samples in a short time without preprocessing so that the yield, concentration, and function of EVs can be maintained to the maximal extent. Takeuchi et al. developed an antibody-conjugated signaling nanocavity platform to improve the detection of intact small EVs (sEVs) in tears. The nanocavities were fabricated by silica nanoparticles as a dynamic mold to recognize the size by using a molecular imprinting approach. These sEVs-sensing chips turned out to be efficient in detecting breast cancer by analyzing sEVs in tears collected from patients.65

Vitreous humor is a cell-free biofluid inside the eye. There are plenty of EVs in the vitreous, mainly derived from the retina. Therefore, EVs in the vitreous humor have a high potential to contain biomarkers for retinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and uveal melanoma.17,18,66 Current EV isolation methods are not appropriate for biological samples less than 100 μL.67 Therefore, to improve the EVs isolation method from vitreous humor, Chen et al. developed a method using the chemically modified cellulose photolabile chips to capture CD63+-EVs from low-volume samples (<30 μL) at a high density due to the large surface-to-volume ratio. EVs were then released following a brief UV exposure due to the existence of a UV-sensitive linker.68

Plasma EVs have also been reported to play a role in the diagnosis of ocular disease. Isolation of high-purity plasma EVs is the foundation for the application of circulating EVs in disease diagnosis and biomarker discovery. The substantial number of dissociative contaminants in the plasma makes it a great challenge to isolate EVs.69 Pan et al. proposed an easy-to-use system to directly collect sEVs from human plasma using TiO2-coated magnetic beads. The TiO2 coating and island-like surface enhanced the ability to capture sEVs reversibly within 20 min due to phospholipid affinity, increased binding sites, and size-exclusion-like effect. This system was then applied to isolate plasma-sEVs from patients with diabetic retinopathy to verify its efficacy as an analytical tool for plasma-based liquid biopsy.70

Other technologies for EV isolation from small-volume samples, such as microfluidic devices, will also be promising for ocular disease diagnosis and treatment. Leung et al. assessed ExoDisc, a commercially available microfluidic tangential flow filtration (mTFF) device that separated sEV from biofluids using a disposable mTFF cartridge with nanoscale pores.71,72 When compared to the differential ultracentrifugation (UC) method, mTFF yielded higher numbers of sEVs separated from polarized RPE cells derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESC-RPEs), as well as higher purity and concentration of particles. They also found improved separation times with the mTFF device and the same therapeutic efficacy with UC in the preservation of photoreceptor in animal models,73 which indicated that the mTFF device held the potential to enable large-scale, good manufacturing practice (GMP), and chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC)-compatible EV production to develop intraocular EV therapeutics. It is worth noting that this device was accompanied by subsequent analysis by in situ enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) or fluorescence labeling, facilitating downstream molecular analysis. This is an important aspect of point-of-care testing in clinical practice and may address current challenges or provide potential strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases.

Strategies for EV Engineering in Ocular Diseases

Therapeutic Payload Encapsulation

Due to their good biocompatibility, EVs are considered as excellent nanocarriers for loading a therapeutic payload.74,75 Combining therapeutic payload with EVs can not only increase the therapeutic efficacy of EVs but also make it easier for the payload to reach the target site.76 Small molecule drugs including PACAP38,77 rapamycin,78 and bevacizumab79 have been reported to be loaded into EVs to treat traumatic optic neuropathy, autoimmune uveitis, and diabetic retinopathy, respectively.

The encapsulation process of a therapeutic payload requires physical or chemical methods, including sonication, electroporation, or chemical-assisted loading. The sonication process is the creation of pores in the EV membrane by ultrasound to allow efficient entry of the therapeutic load into EVs.80 Like sonication, electroporation also forms pores in the EV membrane using high-intensity and short-duration voltages to load the drug, and it can load large molecules.81 However, both sonication and electroporation cause aggregation and cleavage of EVs due to the disruption of the EV membrane, thus limiting the application of these techniques, and the stability of these methods to EVs needs to be further improved in future studies. Apart from sonication and electroporation, chemical-assisted loading is another simple-to-operate exogeneous loading method, which alters the osmotic pressure of the EV membrane by means of a chemical (e.g., triton x-100) to load the cargos.82 However, it has been found that drugs loaded by triton x-100 have the disadvantage of a poorer controlled release of the drug as compared to the sonication method.80 Since each exogeneous loading method has its drawbacks, it is therefore recommended for researchers to adopt an appropriate method based on downstream experiments.83 Additionally, in tumor therapy, combination drug therapy was reported to be achieved by encapsulating more than one drug into EVs, which could also be a candidate strategy for the treatment of ocular disease in future research.80

Surface Membrane Modification

Fusing EV membrane proteins with tissue-specific peptides or ligands is a general approach to increase the affinity of EVs to the target cells,84,85 thereby generating tissue-specific EVs with high therapeutic efficacy. The surface membrane modification can be achieved by extrusion or freeze–thaw cycles with the advantage of simplicity of operation. Extrusion is a technique of mixing drug cargos with EV by means of a lipid extruder, which has the advantages of high efficiency and uniform vesicle size. For example, cyclic RGD (cRGD) peptide was reported to have a high affinity with RPE cells;86 therefore, modifying the surface membrane of EVs with cRGD enabled the targeting of EVs to RPE cells and provided a better therapeutic effect for retinal degenerations.87 Other than cRGD, lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2), lactadhesin, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor have been widely used as fusion proteins to generate bioengineered EVs by extrusion.83 However, it should be noted that bioengineered EVs generated by extrusion have low purity and uneven loading, which reduces their therapeutic efficiency.87 Apart from peptides and proteins, biomaterials like hydrogels can also be applied to control the release and modulate the microenvironment of EVs, and effectively prolong and promote the treatment effects of EVs.88−90 Another surface membrane modification technique, called freeze–thaw cycling, creates temporary pores in the EV membrane by repeated freeze and thaw cycles.91 Freeze–thaw cycling is simple and cost-effective, but the disruption of the membrane decreases EV stability. In addition, it has been reported that EVs can be modified by combining therapeutic payload encapsulation and surface membrane modification, which not only increase the affinity of EVs with target cells but also enables controlled release.87

Genetic Modification

Genetic modification of EVs donor cells is another widely used EV modification technique. Using tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α to treat cells or feeding cells under hypoxia conditions will change the status of donor cells and the expression levels of miRNAs, thereby influencing the miRNA expression levels in cell-derived EVs.92,93 Another commonly used genetic modification approach for donor cells is transfection. Lentiviruses were reported to be applied in donor cells to construct stable-transfected cell lines with overexpression of a candidate gene, and the mRNA level of the gene was found to elevate in cell-derived EVs as well.94 However, by genetic modification of the donor cells, the exact expression level of the target gene in EVs is difficult to control. Therefore, future studies on donor cell modification need to focus on controlling the expression level. Other than genetic modifications of the donor cells, sorting a certain subtype of immune cells through its membrane biomarkers is also a common approach to generate modified EVs expressing different cytokines, which has been widely used in EVs-based treatment for autoimmune ocular diseases like uveitis.95,96

Storage Methods of Bioengineered EVs

In the guidelines on hPSC- and hMSC-derived EVs in China,97 it is recommended that EVs should be sealed, packaged, and labeled for storage conditions after production. The standard temperature for EV storage should be lower than −80 °C, and EVs should be stored to avoid repeated freezing and thawing as well as fluctuations in temperature. However, there has been no published guideline on the storage of bioengineered EVs. Given the diversity of bioengineering strategies, different storage conditions can used to preserve the biological activity of bioengineered EVs.99 It has been recognized that drug-loaded EVs are susceptible to rupture and degradation during storage and transportation;100 therefore, storing EVs encapsulating therapeutic payloads at stable temperatures below −80 °C and for shorter periods of time may improve their efficiency. Of our interest, a recent study reported that EVs encapsulated into biomaterials such as hydrogels remained stable under room temperature for two months, suggesting that such hydrogels may be excellent carriers for bioengineered EVs.101 Besides, another study showed storing genetically bioengineered EVs in PBS supplemented with human albumin and trehalose improved the long-term preservation of EVs,91 which provides researchers with an alternative choice of EV storage diluents.

Administration Routes of EVs in Ocular Disease

When EVs are administrated to the eye, the blood–ocular barrier is a special structure to overcome before EVs can reach the target tissue inside the eye, which is composed of the blood–aqueous barrier (BAB) and the blood–retinal barrier (BRB). The BAB is created by the ciliary epithelium, the vascular endothelial cells of the iris, and the endothelium of Schlemm’s canal.102 The BRB is formed by the vascular endothelial cells of the retina, RPE, Bruch’s membrane, and choriocapillaris.103 Due to the existence of BAB and BRB, immune cells in peripheral blood can hardly enter the ocular environment, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment distinctive from other parts of the body.104 Additionally, the drug metabolisms in the eye are also different from other organs.105 For instance, fat-soluble drugs can more easily cross the blood–ocular barrier compared with water-soluble drugs. Therefore, EVs as particles delineated by a lipid bilayer have an advantage of entering the blood–ocular barrier with or without additional loading therapeutic drugs. Currently, local applications of EVs, including topical delivery, subconjunctival, intravitreal, and subretinal injection, are widely used to treat ocular diseases (Figure 4b).106

Topical Delivery

Topical delivery (eye ointments and drops) is the most accessible approach for ocular drug administration, with the advantage of being noninvasive and convenient. A drug in eye ointments and drops can enter the inner ocular tissue in two ways. First, the drug diffuses to the ocular surface, penetrates the sclera to the choroid, and reaches the RPE. Second, the drug penetrates through the cornea, anterior aqueous, and lens and reaches the vitreous and the retina. The applications of EV-contained eye ointments or eye drops have been widely reported in the treatment of ocular anterior segment diseases, including corneal damage and dry eye disease.47,107 However, due to the short-term tear film stability, the use of EVs in eye drops for treating posterior segment diseases is limited;108,109 therefore, efforts for achieving the controlled release and improving penetrating efficiency of topical delivery are needed in the future.

Subconjunctival Injection

Subconjunctival injection is an approach by injecting drugs into the inferior conjunctival fornix after topical anesthesia, which can overcome the obstacle of the ocular–blood barrier. After subconjunctival injection, a drug penetrates through the sclera to the choroid and reaches the RPE from the vessels of the choroid. Subconjunctival injection of EVs can improve corneal wound healing efficiency,46,89 and has been reported to treat glaucoma and uveitis as well.78,82 It was reported that subconjunctival injection of particles with different diameters and electric charges disperse differently. Particles with a positive charge and large diameter were reported to sustain in the injection site, whereas the neutral or negatively charged and small ones were more dispersed.110 Therefore, the diameter and electric charge of EVs used in this approach should be carefully designed to enhance the effectiveness of the treatment.

Intravitreal Injection

Intravitreal injection is applied by injecting drugs into the vitreous body. The vitreous is a jelly-like substance without blood vessels and nerves, and its nutrition comes from the choroids and aqueous humor. The intravitreal injection delivers the drug into the vitreous humor, and the drug diffuses in the vitreous humor and reaches the retina without systemic toxicity. Intravitreal injection of EVs has been reported to treat many posterior segment ocular diseases, including glaucoma-related optic nerve disorder, traumatic optic neuropathy, and retinal ischemia-reperfusion.92,111,112 However, it is undeniable that intravitreal injection has disadvantages as an invasive operation with possible complications, such as endophthalmitis and vitreous hemorrhage.

Subretinal Injection

During subretinal injection, the drug is injected into the subretinal space between the neural retina and the RPE and diffuses to the RPE and retina. It has been applied for gene delivery in the treatment of many retinal diseases in clinical practice, such as RP and Leber’s congenital amaurosis.113 Other than gene therapy, subretinal injection of stem cells and stem-cells-derived-EVs is also applied to treat outer retinal degenerations.42,114 Although the procedure of subretinal injection is more complex than intravitreal injection, subretinal injection would be more effective in treating retinal degenerations, which mainly affect the outer retinal layers (photoreceptor cells and RPEs).

Other Injection Methods

Other injection methods of EVs in treating ocular disease include anterior chamber injection, retro-orbital injection, and intravenous injection. Anterior chamber injection of EVs was reported to treat retinal ischemia,93 whereas retro-orbital injection and intravenous injection are mainly applied to treat uveitis, which is involved in systemic inflammation.96,115

EV Dosage of Different Injection Methods

The EV dosage applied in ocular diseases in the literature varies widely and is expressed in different units. Some studies use protein content, ranging from 0.02 μg/μL88,89 to 1000 μg/μL.116 Others have used particle numbers ranging from 2 × 105 particles/μL117 to 1.09 × 1011 particles/μL.51 The amount of EVs used varies among the different injection methods, generally being higher for intravenous injections and lower for intravitreal injections, which is related to the limited volume of the vitreous body. The different sources and functions of the cells also affect the EV dosage. In the topical treatment of corneal alkali burns, for example, Hu et al.118 applied 1 μg/μL of EVs derived from human amniotic epithelial cells, whereas Sun et al.89 only used as little as 0.02 μg/μL of adipose-derived MSCs and achieved comparable therapeutic efficacy. In conclusion, there is no generally accepted standard for the EV dosage in the treatment of ocular diseases, which needs further research in the future.

Diagnostic and Therapeutic Potential of EVs in Ocular Disease

It has recently been widely reported that EVs are crucial in ocular disease. Table 4 and Table 5 summarize the applications of EVs in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular disease. Herein, the current progress of the application of EVs in ocular disease will be introduced based on the eye structures, namely the anterior and posterior segment. The anterior segment is the front one-third of the eye, composed of the conjunctiva, cornea, iris, anterior chamber, and lens, which is primarily responsible for directing light to the back of the eye, while the posterior segment is the back two-thirds of the eyes, including the vitreous humor, choroid, and retina, which involves the process of light transduction.

Anterior Segment Ocular Disease

The main conditions of the anterior segment of the eye are corneal diseases (corneal damage and dry eye disease), glaucoma, cataracts, and uveitis. EVs have been applied to treat anterior segment ocular diseases by topical delivery, subconjunctival injection, and intravitreal injection.

Corneal Damage

The cornea is a transparent tissue serving as a refractive media and barrier for inner eye structures.119 Cornea damage can be caused by injury, chemicals, foreign bodies, infections, and exposure to ultraviolet (UV) lights.120−122 It is estimated that over 10 million people suffer from corneal-damage-related blindness worldwide, which lays a heavy burden on the economy and society.123 The cornea is composed of five layers: the epithelium, anterior limiting lamina (Bowman’s membrane), substantia propria (stroma), posterior limiting lamina (Descemet’s membrane), and endothelium. The epithelium and the Descemet’s membrane are regenerable, while the Bowman’s membrane, stroma, and endothelium are not. The healing process of damaged cornea involves survival, proliferation, migration of corneal epithelial cells, neuroinflammation of the corneal microenvironment, and remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM).124−126 Current therapies for mild corneal damage (involving the epithelium) include artificial tears, antibiotics, and anti-inflammatory eye drops.127 Immunosuppressants, laser or nonlaser surgeries, and corneal transplant surgery are applied in cases of moderate to severe corneal damage (involving layers deeper than the epithelium).128 Due to the invasive nature of surgery and the limitations of donor cornea, stem-cell-derived EV therapy has become a focus of research in the treatment of corneal damage due to its low immunogenicity and high biocompatibility.88,129,130

EVs-based therapy has shown excellent therapeutic effects on healing of mechanical corneal wounds. Samaeekia et al. used corneal MSC (cMSC)-derived EVs to heal corneal wounds induced by debriding a 2 mm epithelial wound in a mouse model. After treatment, the cMSC-derived EVs were successfully taken up by the corneal epithelium, and the remaining wound area of the EVs-treated group at 16 h post-treatment was half of that of the control group.50 The impact of the EVs dose on the wound healing effects was also evaluated by using bone-marrow-derived MSCs-EVs, and a higher concentration was correlated with better effects.47 Subsequent research provided possible mechanisms of such EVs-based therapeutic effects, including EVs-delivered miR-21 and regulated PTEN/PI3K/Akt pathway to promote the proliferation and migration of corneal epithelial cells,46 and miR-23-3p in EVs that regulated the expression level of fibrotic genes Col3a1 and Acta2 to reduce fibrosis and inflammation of the cornea.131 In alkali injury animal models, EV-based therapy also showed beneficial effects. Human placenta-derived MSCs, BMSCs, and human amniotic epithelial cells (hAECs)-derived EVs are all able to reduce scar formation and promote corneal transparency after being applied to the injured cornea.118,132,133 It was reported that hAECs-derived EVs exhibited great potential for regulating ECM organization through adhesion signaling pathway, which might be excellent candidates for treating ECM lesion-based diseases.118

Recently, the application of engineered EVs to treat corneal damage has also been reported. For example, loading EVs into hydrogels can achieve sustained release of EVs and enhance therapeutic effects. Tang et al. combined EVs derived from induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-MSCs with thermosensitive chitosan-based hydrogels (CHI-hydrogel) and found that the hydrogels remained in the synthesized corneal stroma at day 7 post application. The EV-imbedded hydrogels regulated collagen biosynthesis and ECM organization via the expression of miR-432-5p, thus promoting corneal healing effectively (Figure 5a–d).88 Sun et al. loaded miR-24-3p into adipose-derived MSCs-EVs to create miR-24-3p-rich EVs for accelerating corneal epithelial migration and then constructed a thermosensitive DEGMA-modified hyaluronic acid hydrogel (THH) to control the release of EVs. As a result, this nanoplatform successfully reduced corneal fibrosis and macrophage activation in a rabbit alkali burn model.89

Taken together, plenty of evidence has shown that EV-based therapy had great benefits for corneal wound healing in mechanically and chemically damaged animal models by promoting corneal epithelial cell proliferation and migration or modulating ECM remodeling. Biomaterials such as hydrogels help to regulate the microenvironment of EVs and promote the therapeutic effects by controlled release. However, therapeutic payload capsulation and genetic modification methods have not been reported in EV-based therapy for corneal wound healing. Therefore, future studies on combining therapeutic molecules into EVs or modifying gene expressions of EV-producing cells can be explored to better enhance EV-based corneal healing treatments.

Dry Eye Disease

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common disease of the ocular surface that affects 8%–10% of the population worldwide,134,135 with a clinical manifestation of a burning or stinging sensation in eyes, photophobia, and blurred vision, significantly reducing the quality of life.136,137 Female sex, contact lens use, cataracts, laser refractive eye surgery, keratoconus, extended screen time, osteoarthritis, atherosclerosis, and connective tissue diseases have been reported as risk factors for DED.138 As digital devices are increasingly used in the global population, extended screen time was also found to be correlated with the occurrence of DED.139,140 According to the affected regions, DED can be divided into nonsystemic DED (only affects the eyes) and systemic DED (involves other parts of the body than the eyes). Systemic DED includes graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)-associated DED, Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and rheumatoid arthritis (RA).141−144 Recent studies have identified inflammation as the core mechanism of DED, and the levels of inflammatory cytokines are commonly upregulated in tear fluid and serum of patients with systemic and nonsystemic DED.145,146 Current therapeutic options for DED include lubricating eye drops, topical cyclosporine, topical steroids, antibiotics, and mucin secretagogues,147−149 which need to be applied frequently and may disturb the homeostasis of the ocular surface. Recent studies showed that the application of EV-based therapy on DED has anti-inflammatory effects, thereby improving the therapeutic effect of DED treatments.107

Recent studies showed that the application of EVs-based therapy on nonsystemic DED had anti-inflammatory effects, thereby improving the therapeutic efficacy of nonsystemic DED treatments.107 In murine nonsystemic DED models induced by 0.2% benzalkonium chloride (BAC) eye drops, ADSCs-derived EVs inhibited corneal epithelial cell apoptosis, down-regulated the levels of inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, interferon (IFN)-γ, and TNF-α, and up-regulated the level of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby significantly increasing the tear volume and alleviating irregular morphology compared with the sham group.48 Another study used HUMSCs-derived EVs to treat nonsystemic DED mice and found that the IRAK1/TAB2/NF-κB associated miRNAs such as miR-125b and miR-6873 were highly expressed in HUMSCs-derived EVs, and the application of the EVs increased the corneal integrity and lowered the inflammatory cytokines IL-4, IL-8, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17, and TNF-α.150 Moreover, studies demonstrated that modifying the surface with EVs improved the healing efficacy of EVs. Ma et al. developed a multifunctional gold nanoparticle (AuNP)-modified MSCs-derived EV via the reduction of ascorbic acid onto the exosomal membrane, which showed greater anti-inflammation and corneal epithelium healing capacity, compared with only MSCs-EVs.107

EV-based therapy was also applied in systemic DED in animal models and individuals. Fujii et al. applied MSCs-derived EVs in acute GVHD mice models, and the pathologic damage was reduced in multiple targeted organs including the colon, liver, intestine, and skin.151 Subsequently, Zhou et al. and Jia et al. used MSCs-derived EVs to treat GVHD-associated DED mice and rat models, which demonstrated great anti-inflammation effects by regulating M1/M2 polarization and decreasing the levels of IFN-γ and CXCL1 mechanically.152,153 Moreover, the application of MSCs-derived EVs was also reported to treat animal models of Sjögren’s syndrome. Subconjunctival injection of MSCs-EVs significantly alleviated the symptoms of dacryoadenitis in rabbit models of Sjögren’s syndrome via regulating the proportion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M1/M2 macrophages.154,155 A recent study reported that the use of EVs from young but not aging iPS-derived MSCs-EVs showed a greater protective effect on treating sialadenitis in a mouse model of Sjögren’s syndrome, which provided a promising therapeutic method for Sjögren’s syndrome in the future.156

The application of EVs, including naïve EVs and engineered EVs, has shown great potential in the suppression of inflammation and promotion of corneal healing in DED of various subtypes. However, the mechanism of the molecules in the EVs remains unclear. Further research conducted on the detailed mechanism of EVs in the improvement of DED is made possible based on the findings above. Also, as a superficial ocular disease, exploring the application of noninvasive EV-based therapy (eye drops) in upcoming studies will facilitate future clinical applications of EVs to DED treatment.

Optic Nerve Disease

The optic nerve is the second pair of cranial nerves and is located in the back of the eye.157 The vision signal is processed by retinal cells and transmitted to the visual cortex of the brain via the optic nerve, making the optic nerve a crucial gateway to visual formation. Optic nerve diseases are the world’s most common irreversible blinding diseases, including glaucoma and optic nerve injury, and are pathologically characterized by retinal ganglion cell (RGC) axon damage, irreversible cell body death, and neuroinflammation.158,159 In the course of glaucoma and optic nerve injury, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and optic nerve damage cause irreversible impairment to visual function, resulting in visual field defects and vision impairment.1 Patients with optic nerve disease have a significantly higher risk of accidents, such as falls, than the general population, and their quality of life is heavily reduced, threatening their health and well-being.160 Current medical therapies for glaucoma include prostaglandins, beta blockers, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors to alleviate the symptoms by prohibiting the production or increasing the outflow of the aqueous humor.161 In clinical practice, corticosteroids have been promptly applied to treat optic nerve injuries, but this proved to be effective in only half of the population.162 Recently, the anti-inflammatory effects of EVs have also played an important role in the diagnosis and treatment of optic nerve disease.

In the diagnosis of glaucoma, EVs can be good biomarkers. A study compared specific proteins of EVs by quantitative labeling (iTRAQ) and reported that STT3B was significantly reduced in EVs of the aqueous humor in primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) patients.14 Another study analyzed EVs in the aqueous humor of pseudoexfoliation (PEX) glaucoma patients and age-matched controls, and discovered CD63, CD9, and CD81 were significantly higher in PEX glaucoma patients, providing potential biomarkers for early glaucoma detection and treatment.40

EV-based treatment demonstrated great therapeutic effects in acute and chronic glaucoma animal models. Seong et al. reported that intravitreal injection of human amniotic membrane MSCs (AMMSCs) and amniotic membrane epithelial stem cells (AMESCs)-derived EVs rich in growth factors (GFs) and neurotrophic factors (NFs) significantly decreased the IOP and preserved retinal structures in an acute glaucoma rat model with high IOP induced by saline.163 EVs also showed beneficial effects in the treatment of chronic glaucoma. In chronic glaucoma rat models induced by injecting microbeads or conjunctival fibroblasts into the anterior chamber, treatment of BMSCs or HUMSCs-derived EVs not only prevented retinal nerve fiber layer loss but also rescued the scotopic threshold response.164 RNA-Seq confirmed a total of 43 miRNAs including miR-30a-3p, miR-223-5p, and miR-17-5p were upregulated in BMSCs-EVs, with the function of neuroprotection via suppression of inflammatory response.165 In another rat model with glaucoma filtration surgery, EVs successfully delivered aptamer S58 and greatly reduced conjunctival scarring and fibrosis compared with naked S58 treatment, revealing that EVs might be good drug carriers for glaucoma treatment, but further studies are needed to improve the performance of EVs as drug carriers.82 In optic nerve crush rat models, EVs derived from HUMSCs, BMSCs, hPSCs, and embryonic stem cells (ES)-MSCs were also reported to promote axon growth and prevent nerve fiber layer thinning by regulating levels of inflammatory factors including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 (Figure 6a–g).49,166−168 Moreover, delivery of functional endogenous neuroprotective peptide PACAP38 with EVs promoted retinal ganglion cell survival and optic nerve axon regeneration.77 The above studies revealed that EVs showed great therapeutic effects in several glaucoma and optic nerve crush models. In future studies, the combination of EVs and anti-inflammatory biomaterials may further improve the efficacy of EVs in treating optic nerve disease.

Another disease that affects the optic nerve is optic neuritis (ON). ON is an autoimmune inflammatory demyelination disease in the central nervous system that is commonly indicative of neurological disorders, such as multiple sclerosis and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).169,170 Timely use of corticosteroids is important to improve the prognosis of ON patients, but the diagnosis of ON can be complex due to the diversity of disease subtypes.170 Of our interest, recent studies have shown the promising potential of serum EVs for ON diagnosis.171,172 Exosomal miRNAs in the serum, such as hsa-miR-122-3p and hsa-miR-200a-5p, were able to distinguish the NMOSD status from patients with multiple sclerosis and healthy controls. In addition, they were also found to have positive correlations with disease severity in NMOSD patients.15 The level of miR-129-2-3p in the astrocyte-derived EVs was also identified as a key miRNA mediating the demyelinating pathogenesis via downstream target gene SMAD3.171 Ma et al. enriched the plasma immune proteins from patients with neuromyelitis optica with therapeutic plasma exchange therapy and identified several exosome proteins probability contributing to the disease with plasma exchange, such as CFHR5, BIN2, and neuroplastin.172 However, an increased sample size of studies is needed to further evaluate the accuracy of EVs for ON diagnosis.

Posterior Capsule Opacification

Posterior capsule opacification (PCO) is the most prevalent cause of vision loss after cataract surgery, with an incident rate as high as 50% in the elderly and up to 100% in children.173 PCO is mainly caused by the hyperproliferation, matrix contraction, matrix deposition, and cell transdifferentiation from the epithelial to the myofibroblast phenotype of residual lens epithelial cells (LECs) adhering to the IOL surface and posterior capsule initiated by surgical injury.174,175 Neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser capsulotomy is commonly used to treat PCO. However, there remains a series of risks and complications, such as IOL injury, macular cystic edema, and retinal detachment.176,177 Therefore, strategies have been investigated to lower the incidence of PCO. Strategies involving the implantation of specially designed intraocular lenses (IOLs) against the proliferation and fibrosis of lens epithelial cells (LECs) have successfully slowed the progression of PCO.178−180 Zhu et al. modified the IOL surface with LECs-derived exosomes and loaded the antiproliferative drug doxorubicin (Dox) into the exosomes through electroporation.81 By electrostatic interaction, Dox@Exos were immobilized on the aminated IOLs surface to form Dox@Exos-IOLs. The exosomes in this study not only played roles as high-efficacy drug delivery vectors but their homologous targeting ability also facilitated improved cellular uptake of Dox@Exos by LECs in experiments in vitro. After implantation into the rabbit model, Dox@Exos-IOLs showed excellent intraocular biocompatibility and efficiently inhibited the occurrence and development of PCO by releasing Dox and killing the residual LECs. This study provided an insightful strategy for PCO prevention through surface modification of IOL functionalized by EVs, which can be investigated for safety and efficacy in future clinical studies.

Uveitis

The uvea is the pigmented layer of the eye and mainly consists of the iris, choroid, and ciliary body. Uveitis is a form of ocular inflammation that affects the uvea, generally caused by autoimmune diseases including SLE, ankylosing spondylitis, and Behçet’s disease, as well as drugs or infections.181 Uveitis can be divided into three categories, anterior uveitis (affects the iris and ciliary body), intermediate uveitis (affects vitreous, retina, and blood vessels behind the lens), and posterior uveitis (affects retina and choroid).182 The global prevalence of uveitis is approximately 1/2000,183 whereas uveitis can be vision-threatening and lead to blindness without timely treatment.184 The first-line treatment for noninfectious uveitis is topical or systematic application of corticosteroids with or without combining immunosuppressive therapy, which requires frequent injection with complications of high IOP. Therefore, finding potential treatment has been a research hotspot in the field of uveitis.185

EVs have immune modulation function; therefore, EVs are considered as promising therapeutics for autoimmune diseases like uveitis. Shigemoto-Kuroda et al. used MSCs-derived EVs to treat mice models of experimental autoimmune uveoretinitis (EAU).115 As a consequence, EVs inhibited antigen-presenting cell activation, as well as suppressed the development of T helper 1 (Th1) and Th17 cells, thus successfully attenuating the immune response in EAU mice.115 Another study generated EVs derived from IL-35- and IL-27-producing regulatory B cells (i35-Bregs and i27-Bregs) to treat mice of EAU and found that Th17 responses were suppressed, greatly alleviating the retinal morphology and function of EAU mice.95,96 Studies on the EV treatment of uveitis have focused on the drug-loading property of EVs as well. Li et al. used lentivirus to create MSCs overexpressing IL-10, and generated EVs from IL-10 overexpressing MSCs (sEV-IL10). Mice treated with sEV-IL10 had a lower proportion of Th1 and Th17 cells in the eyes and presented with less photoreceptor layer damage and milder retinal folds than the vector sEV-treated group and control group.94 Another study loaded rapamycin into MSCs-derived EVs and created a rapamycin-loaded MSCs-EVs delivery system (Rapa-sEVs). In clinical practices, rapamycin is usually given to uveitis patients by intravitreal injection, and the frequent injection causes severe complications like intraocular hemorrhage and retinal detachment. By loading rapamycin into MSCs-EVs, Rapa-sEVs were successfully delivered to the retina by subconjunctival injection, which greatly reduced the risk of complications from intravitreal injection. In addition, Rapa-sEVs reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and alleviated the retinal damage in EAU mice models, which showed great potential for treatment of uveitis.78 Therefore, utilizing the drug-loading property of EVs can offer an alternative treatment strategy in uveitis, which needs to be further investigated in clinical trials.

Clinical Potential

With increasing evidence showing the therapeutic effect of EVs on the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases in the anterior segment, some EV-based therapy has been applied in many clinical trials (Table 6). In a study conducted by Zhou et al., the tear secretion and tear-film breakup time were found to be significantly elevated in 28 GVHD human eyes after being treated with MSCs-derived EVs, and that effect was induced by miR-204-mediated IL-6/IL-6R/Stat3 pathway via immunoregulation of M1/M2 macrophage polarization.152 Based on this finding, they further carried out a phase I/II study (NCT04213248) to evaluate the tolerability and efficacy of an MSC-EV eye drop in the treatment of GVHD-associated dry eye disease in patients with poor response to current treatments. In addition, another phase I/II study (NCT05738629) aims to use iPSCs-derived MSCs EVs eye drops to treat dry eye diseases post refractive surgery and associated with blepharospasm, and this study is not yet recruiting study subjects. Researchers of another phase II study (IRCT20211102052948N1) generated EVs from Warton jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells to treat dry eye disease in primary Sjögren’s syndrome and just completed recruiting study subjects. Diagnostic use of EVs was applied in ocular inflammatory diseases (NCT00331331) and ocular surface diseases (ChiCTR1900027452) as well.

Posterior Segment Ocular Disease

Posterior segment ocular disease is commonly defined as diseases of the retina and choroid and usually includes age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and retinal ischemia. Ocular tumors, such as uveal melanoma and retinoblastoma, most commonly originate from the choroid and retina, respectively. Intravitreal and subretinal injections are common drug delivery methods of EVs to treat posterior segment ocular diseases.

Age-Related Macular Degeneration

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is one of the leading causes of blindness and visual impairment all over the world. It is estimated that the number of AMD patients will increase to 17.8 million by 2050, and this number continues to increase with the aging population.186 AMD is a degenerative disease caused by multifactorial processes initiated by the focal accumulation of drusen, lipid-rich membranous debris containing fragments of RPE cells, lipids, minerals, and components of the complement cascade, which is thought to induce oxidative injury from hypoxia and chronic inflammation, leading to photoreceptor cell death and RPE dysfunction.187 Dry AMD, also known as geographic atrophy (GA), and wet AMD, also known as choroidal neovascularization (CNV), are the two types of advanced stages of AMD. Dry AMD is characterized by the degeneration and cell death of RPE and photoreceptors. There is no effective treatment for dry AMD. Wet AMD manifests with neovascularization of choroidal vessels and the penetration of these blood vessels into the retina. Intravitreal injection of antivascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is the main treatment to suppress CNV.188−190

Emerging studies have proven that EVs play important roles in the diagnosis and treatment of AMD. AH, VH, and serum contain abundant EVs secreted from various cells in the eye. Proteins and miRNAs from EVs extracted from these bioliquids of AMD patients may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for AMD.18,44,191−193 Comparing the protein profiles in the AH of 28 AMD and 25 control eyes, Tsai et al. observed that exosomal proteins in patients with AMD were enriched in the lipoprotein metabolic process. Among them, six were significantly different from the controls, including APOA1, OPTC, CLU, C3, PTGDS, and one unidentified protein. When comparing AMD patients who were receiving anti-VEGF treatment, two proteins, SERPINA1 and AZGP1, were decreased with the duration of the anti-VEGF treatment.194 Calpain-2, an autophagy-associated protein, secreted from RPE-derived EVs was also found to be a possible diagnostic biomarker for AMD.191 Exosomal miRNAs, such as miR-184 (from AH), miR-143-3p and miR-145-5p (from VH), and miR-19a, miR-126, and miR-410 (from serum), may serve as biomarkers reflecting the angiostatic capacity in AMD.8,18,44

EVs are natural nanosized drug carriers with low immunogenicity and long circulation times, which allow them to promote cellular uptake while protecting inclusions from extracellular degradation. Thanks to their size and structure, they can easily bypass blood–retinal barriers and carry cargo or drugs directly into injured cells.195,196 Since stem cells play a crucial role in tissue repair, most investigations on the use of EVs to treat AMD have focused on stem-cell-derived EVs, including BMSCs, human ES-MSCs, HUMSCs, tonsil-derived mesenchymal stem cells (T-MSCs), and stem cells from apical papilla (SACP).197−201 Intravitreal transplantation of BMSCs-derived EVs was found to effectively suppress N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU)-induced retinal degeneration. After only one injection, the therapeutic effects on photoreceptor preservation and retinal maintenance lasted for 1–2 months. The authors further identified that miR-21 mediated the therapeutic effects on MNU-induced photoreceptor apoptosis and retinal dysfunction, indicating exosomal miR-21 as therapeutic for retinal degeneration.197 EVs isolated from hES-MSCs were able to alleviate retinal degeneration by promoting the retrodifferentiation of Müller cells into retinal progenitor cells via HSP90 in EVs. HSP90 functioned by upregulating the expression of Oct4 in Müller cells.198 T-MSCs-derived EVs also showed excellent preventive and therapeutic effects on cell-death-delaying of RPE, probably due to the efficient ability of T-MSC EVs to regulate intracellular oxidative stress suggested by transcriptome sequencing. Protection of the outer nuclear layer was achieved by intravitreal injection of T-MSC EVs in the Pde6b gene knockout rat, an animal model of inherited retinal degeneration. Human palatine tonsil tissues could be easily obtained as medical waste following tonsillectomies, so they can be an important homogeneous source of EVs. Further, T-MSC EVs may be a beneficial alternative for the treatment of retinal degeneration in both the initiation and progression.199 In addition to retinal degeneration, subretinal fibrosis, which is the end stage of wet AMD, was effectively ameliorated by EVs derived from HUMSCs via the suppression of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process of RPE. The authors further revealed that miR-27b in HUMSCs EVs cargo suppressed the EMT process induced by transforming growth factor-beta 2 (TGFβ-2) by inhibition of HOXC6 expression.200 Subretinal injection of SCAP-EVs in RCS rats (an animal model of retinal degeneration) also demonstrated therapeutic effects, including preserved visual function, reduced retinal cell apoptosis, and alleviated thinning of the outer nuclear layer.201

Moreover, retinal cells, such as NPCs and RPE cells, have also been reported as important sources of EVs with therapeutic functions.42,51,202 Bian et al. grafted NPCs-derived EVs in the subretinal space of RCS rats, and directed subretinal injection of EVs delayed the progression of the disease, including delay of photoreceptor degeneration, preservation of visual function, prevention of outer nuclear layer (ONL) thinning, and decreased apoptosis of photoreceptors. These EVs were specifically internationalized by retinal microglia and suppressed their activation by inhibition of inflammatory signaling pathways by targeting TNF-α, IL-1β, and COX-2. These findings suggested that NPC-EVs and their contents may be a promising agent for treating retinal degeneration.51 Wang et al. demonstrated that subretinal delivery of RPE-EVs efficiently enhanced visual function impairments and alleviated structural damage in the retina of MNU-induced mice. RPE-EVs suppressed apoptosis and reduced oxidative damage, indicating that RPE-EVs had therapeutic effects in the treatment of retinal degeneration.42

Several studies modified EVs with some additional functions to enhance their therapeutic effects.13,87,111,203 Liu et al. developed an engineered EVs-based delivery system to improve the treatment of retinal degeneration. They modified MSC-derived EVs with a cyclic RGD (cRGD) peptide on the surface by membrane fusion technology and loaded them with an antagonist of the IL-1 receptor, anakinra (Figure 7a–l). The engineered cRGD-EVs were excellent candidates for preferential combination therapy with drugs and neuroprotective factors due to their increased targeting efficacy against hyperactivated microglia. The combination of anakinra@cRGD-EVs achieved a strong protection effect on photoreceptors in cell and mouse models through targeted immune modulation of these engineered EVs.87 Using cRGD peptides to enhance the active targeting ability of EVs is also applied as a strategy for CNV. Pollalis et al. engineered EVs isolated from whole mouse retina tissues and modified them with ASL, composed of membrane Anchor (BODIPY), Spacer (PEG), and targeting Ligands (cRGD). After intravitreal injection, ASL-EVs demonstrated excellent retinal penetration through the retina and actively targeted the area of CNV including ONL, RPE, and choroid in laser-induced CNV mouse models with 89.5% colocalization with integrin αv. No immediate reactive gliosis was observed with either naïve EVs or ASL-EVs, indicating that ASL-EVs were good candidates as intraocular drug delivery vehicles, enabling an active targeting strategy.203 Other therapeutic peptides have also been modified on EVs to inhibit neovascularization. KV11, an antiangiogenic peptide, was linked to endothelial-cell-derived EVs by an anchoring peptide, CP05. After loading onto EVs, KV11 was more efficiently delivered to retinal blood vessels via retro-orbital injection, compared to intravitreal injection, and its antiangiogenic effect was greatly enhanced by inhibition of VEGF-downstream signaling, when delivered with EVs.111 Tian et al. engineered EVs derived from regulatory T (Treg) cells (rEXS) with anti-VEGF antibodies (aV) using a peptide linker (cL), which could be cleaved by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) expressed in neovascularization lesions. The rEXS-cL-aV was selectively accumulated in the CNV lesions due to the ability of rEXS and released aV and rEXS in response to MMP-mediated cleavage, thus suppressing inflammation and VEGF in both mouse and nonhuman primate models of CNV.13 The design strategy of these engineered EVs may also be applied in the treatment of other autoimmune and inflammatory diseases.

The application of EVs in AMD, both CNV and retinal degeneration, is emerging in the current years. The studies mentioned above have proven the diagnostic effect of EVs in AMD as potential biomarkers in the aqueous and vitreous humor and serum. Moreover, EVs with various sources have been applied in the treatment of AMD, functioning as anti-inflammatory, antiangiogenesis, and antioxidative therapies. EVs also exhibit potential as vectors in drug delivery to the retina, which provides a promising method in the treatment of AMD. However, due to the complex structure of the retina, the ability and mechanism of EVs’ traversal of the BRB, as well as the distribution and elimination of EVs after injected into the eyes as therapeutics remains to be investigated.

Diabetic Retinopathy

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and is one of the leading causes of blindness worldwide in adults between 20 and 74 years of age.204−206 It is estimated by the International Diabetes Federation that there will be 700 million diabetic patients worldwide by 2045.207 Individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus have a lifetime risk of 50–60%, whereas those with type 1 diabetes mellitus have a lifetime risk of up to 90%.208 The development of DR is mainly correlated to complex pathophysiological mechanisms triggered by hyperglycemia and other causal risk factors, including genetic and epigenetic factors.209 Implicated pathways include accumulation of sorbitol, increased production of free radicals, formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGE), inflammatory factors, and increased VEGF levels.33 Anti-VEGF therapies, such as bevacizumab, ranibizumab, and aflibercept, are currently indicated for diabetic macular edema. Although prompt laser therapy is effective in preserving vision in cases of proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and diabetic macular edema (DME), its ability to reverse visual loss is limited. For advanced retinopathy, vitrectomy surgery may sometimes be necessary.210

Due to the ability of exosomes to deliver intrinsic molecular cargos directly to recipient cells, EVs may be considered as potential diagnostic or therapeutic tools for DR. Circulating EVs may be important biomarkers for disease progression in DR with various stages. During the development of DR, increased vascular permeability can facilitate the accumulation of circulating EVs in the eye, which may be critical for the development of the most progressive stages of the disease. Zhang et al. compared serum samples from patients with DR and healthy controls, and noticed that miR-26b-5p, a hyperglycemia-induced miRNA closely related to endothelial dysfunction, in serum-derived EVs of DR patients was up-regulated.16 When comparing the expression profile of serum exosomal miRNAs from patients with diabetes and DR, Yang et al. found that miRNA-3976 had the potential as a biomarker for DR, especially by regulating NFκB-associated pathways in the early stages of DR.211 Jiang et al. studied serum exosomal miRNAs from patients with diabetic macular edema (DME) and diabetes. They revealed that miR-377-3p in serum EVs inhibited VEGF expression to suppress RPE proliferation and might serve as a diagnostic biomarker for DME.212 In the stage of PDR, Li et al. identified 26 circRNAs differently expressed in PDR serum EVs, compared to patients with age-matched senile cataracts, and suggested that they might play important roles in the pro-angiogenic mechanism.213 Other studies have investigated the roles of plasma-derived EVs as indicators of the stage of DR progression. Yu et al. showed that the expression of miR-431-5p in plasma-derived EVs was 2.3 times higher in patients with PDR compared with non-PDR and healthy controls, which indicated miR-431-5p as a potential marker of disease progression to PDR stage.214 Xiao et al. also compared these three groups of the population and found that 90 proteins were significantly changed in DR using iTRAQ-based quantitative proteomics. They confirmed that tumor necrosis factor-α-induced protein 8 (TNFAIP8) was significantly upregulated in plasma-derived small EVs in DR and might be an important mediator of angiogenesis in the disease progression.215 LncRNAs in plasma EVs, such as DLX6-AS1, PRINS, and FAM190A-3, were also identified as potential diagnostic biomarkers for DR. Exosomal DLX6-AS1 expression was identified as a risk factor for DR, whereas PRINS and FAM190A-3 were identified as protective against DR.216

There are also many studies focusing on the diagnostic function of EVs derived from tears, VH, retinal tissue, and urine samples. A study of tear EVs concluded that miR-145-5p, miR-214-3p, miR-218-5p, and miR-9-5p were dysregulated in DR.12 Kot and Kaczmarek highlighted miR-125 and miR-21 in the vitreous EVs as potential targets for further analysis regarding their putative roles in the pathogenesis of PDR.217 Vitreous EVs were also found to contain a lot of proteins that were different with PDR, such as LDHA, FCN 3, Apo B, and Apo M and their expressions had diagnostic values to predict PDR.66 In the urine samples, Mighty et al. investigated proteins specifically in urine EVs derived from diabetic patients with and without DR. They discovered that the junction plakoglobin was a specific protein in EVs released from both ex vivo human retinal tissues and urinary EVs of DR patients. So they suggested junction plakoglobin as an early screening biomarker for DR diagnosis with the potential to prevent blindness caused by DR.218

The therapeutic function of EVs to alleviate retinal damage and dysfunction in DR has been revealed in many studies. EVs from HUMSCs suppressed retinal inflammatory responses and oxidate injury in DR by shuffling miR-17-3p to target STAT1.219 Xu et al. investigated the potential of HUMSCs-sEVs as biomaterials for anti-inflammatory and antiapoptotic effects in DR by transferring miR-18b to mediate the MAP3K1/NF-κB axis.220 Their EVs also alleviated diabetic retinal neurodegeneration and protected retinal structure through inhibition of the p38MAPK phosphorylation.221 Sun et al. also proved that intravitreal injection of HUMSC-EVs effectively alleviated retinal oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammation, and angiogenesis by modulating the HIF-1α/EZH2/PGC-1α and PTEN/AKT/NRF2 signaling pathways through miR-5068 and miR-10228, respectively.222,223 Similarly, several studies have reported that HUMSC-EVs carrying specific miRNAs, such as miR-30c-5p and miR-22-3p, improved retinal function by targeting PLCG1 and NLRP3, respectively.224,225

BMSCs-derived EVs also played a protective role in DR. Li et al. found that BMSCs-derived exosomal miR-486-3p inhibited oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis by regulation of the TLR4/NF-κB axis in DR.226 The suppression of Wnt/b-catenin signaling was also proven to be a possible mechanism to explain the protective effect of BMSCs-derived EVs in DR.227 BMSC-EVs loaded with miR-133b-3p have been demonstrated to suppress angiogenesis and oxidative stress via repression of FBN1 in the KK/Upj-Ay mice model, a spontaneous diabetic nephropathy model of diabetes mellitus.228

EVs secreted by adipose-derived MSCs have also been suggested to delay the inflammatory response and angiogenesis in DR by shuttling miR-192, which targeted and negatively regulated ITGA1, thus ameliorating diabetic retinal damage.229

To further enhance the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-EVs in DR, Reddy et al. loaded the anti-VEGF drug bevacizumab into EVs by exogenous drug loading (Figure 8a–d). They found that MSC-sEVs loaded with bevacizumab significantly reduced the frequency of intravitreal injection required for the treatment of DR. The neuroprotective effect was sustained for two months, while the effect of bevacizumab alone was observed for one month. The prolonged benefits of EV-mediated drug delivery systems were promising for clinical application of retinal diseases since they persevered vitreous clarity in the optical axis, as well as their similar composition with cells.79

In a word, a lot of biomarkers in the EVs have been investigated in the diagnosis and progression of DR from noninvasive or minimally invasive samples, such as tears and aqueous humor. These studies offer promising candidates for EVs in clinical translational studies in DR. In addition, EVs have antiapoptosis and neuroprotective effects in animal DR models that maintain the retinal function and structure, which further facilitates the clinical application of EVs in the future. Similar to their application in AMD, a comprehensive understanding of the origins and temporal dynamics of EVs in the diagnosis and treatment of DR is in great demand.

Other Retinal Diseases

MSC-EVs have also shown beneficial effects on the treatment of other retinal diseases such as retinal detachment and retinal ischemia. Retinal detachment (RD) is characterized by a physical separation of the retinal neurosensory layer from the RPE. RD leads to ischemia and oxygen deficiency in the retina, thus resulting in multiple pathological events.230,231 Treatment with MSC-EVs significantly benefited photoreceptor survival and maintained normal retinal structure when subretinally injected at the time of retinal separation in the rat model of RD. The proteomic analysis further revealed that MSC-EVs contained proteins associated with anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and antiapoptotic functions. The protective role of MSC-EVs made it possible for combating photoreceptor loss in RD, as well as many other retinal diseases with similar pathology.116

The condition of retinal ischemia is one of the common pathways contributing to many retinal and optic nerve diseases. The hypoxic status altered the production of miRNAs in MSC-EVs and enhanced their properties, such as cytoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective functions.232 Among the differently expressed miRNAs, exosomal miRNA-424 was identified as a candidate to improve cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory properties. Based on this finding, Mathew et al. functionally engineered MSC-EVs with overexpression of miRNA-424 and revealed that these EVs provided a multipronged approach to promote the recovery of retinal ischemic injury by reducing inflammation and attenuating oxygen free radicals in the retina.93 EVs derived from young bone marrow stem cell antigen 1-positive (Sca-1+) cells were intravitreally injected into old mice with retinal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury and demonstrated a protective effect on ganglion cells apoptosis and visual function due to exosomal miR-150-5p via the MEKK3/JNK/c-Jun axis, demonstrating neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects against retinal I/R injury.233 Moreover, EVs from CD44 highly expressed endothelial colony-forming cells also presented as promising therapeutic agents for the treatment of retinal ischemia and neurodegeneration.117 Recently, Bao et al. applied EV-loaded degradable poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) microcapsules with micrometric pores for the treatment of retinal I/R injury. EVs were settled in the vitreous humor and released for over one month, thus restoring the retinal thickness to that of nearly heathy retina. These EV-loaded microcapsules also had therapeutic effects in the mice and nonhuman primates with primed mycobacterial uveitis.234

Ocular Tumors

Uveal Melanoma (UM) is the most common primary intraocular malignant tumor in adults with an annual incidence of 6 per million people per year.235

The combination of histopathological, patient-related, and molecular prognostic markers allows for a more precise assessment of the metastatic risk than relying on a single parameter. Although the primary UM of the eye has been treated very effectively with eye-sparing radiation-based techniques or enucleation, current therapies are still unable to prevent or treat metastasis. Despite the successful treatment of the eye tumor, almost half of patients develop metastatic diseases, which usually involve the liver, and long-term survival is rare.236 EVs released by tumor cells have been shown to play a critical role in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and response to treatment through the communication between cancer cells and the tumor stroma.237 Therefore, EVs have been extensively investigated as biomarkers to monitor cancer progression in patients.

Pessuti et al. compared the EVs of different samples from UM patients, including vitreous humor (VH), aqueous humor (AH), and plasma, and found that EVs isolated from AH, VH, and plasma shared consistent proteomic profile, and supported the use of blood as a noninvasive liquid biopsy to monitor UM patients.17 Wröblewska et al. explored the miRNAs and proteins extracted from from circulating EVs for potential biomarkers in UM diagnosis and metastasis. They found that miRNAs, such as hsa-miR-144-5p and hsa-miR-191-5p, are the most promising biomarker candidates to categorize patients into local and advanced UM. Hsa-miR-191-5p, -223-3p, -483-5p, and -203a had the potential to be early markers for the presence of UM.45 They also revealed that metastatic UM had significant enrichment of inflammatory-related proteins, such as interferon γ, IL-2, IL-11, and IL-12 (p40), thus having the potential to be utilized as markers for determining metastasis of UM.238

Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most common intraocular malignant tumor in childhood, affecting 1:15,000 to 1:20,000 live birth.239 Tumorigenesis typically initiates with the occurrence of biallelic mutation in the RB1 tumor suppressor gene, which is then followed by a cascade of genetic and epigenetic alterations. The RB1 gene encodes the retinoblastoma protein and plays a vital role in regulating cellular replication via interactions with E2F transcription factors and chromatin remodeling proteins.240 When retinoblastoma is diagnosed sufficiently early and the lesion is localized, current therapies can be effective in saving lives and preserving eyes and vision, including focal cryotherapy, laser therapy, and radiation therapy; systemic or locally delivered chemotherapy; and surgical removal of the eye for advanced or recurrent tumors.241 Although RB can be diagnosed by an experienced ophthalmologist, predicting the progression of diseases, and accompanying secondary malignancy, is challenging.242 Therefore, ideal markers through less or noninvasive procedures, such as liquid biopsy, would serve as the best option for children to prognosticate RB progression and metastasis.243

Manukonda et al. analyzed serum-derived EVs RNA profiling from RB patients and age-matched controls and revealed the regulatory roles of the cargo in the small EVs, which included miRNAs, lncRNAs, and their target mRNAs, in cell cycle, metabolism, and tumor-associated signaling pathways in RB. This finding suggested that serum EVs were of great importance in the discovery of prognostic biomarkers for RB.244

Aqueous humor is also a reliable liquid biopsy source of molecular tumor information, which can be safely obtained at diagnosis and during RB treatment.245,246 In the study of Peng et al., sEVs in AH from RB eyes were identified. They also observed increased tetraspanin heterogeneity in the AH samples from RB eyes before and after treatment.19 Their team further confirmed a significant enrichment of CD63/81+ sEVs in AH from RB eyes before treatment and those with a greater burden of tumors. Therefore, they proposed that further research into the cargo of CD63/81+ sEVs might be a vital step to discovering potential RB biomarkers.247

In addition, EVs can also be used as cargos to load small drugs, such as doxorubicin (Dox), to induce the apoptosis of RB and enhance the antitumor effect. Farhat et al. isolated EVs from two metastatic breast cancer cell lines (4T1 and SKBR3) that expressed a marker that electively interacted with RB cells. Dox were loaded into the producer cells by passive endogenous loading machinery of cells through simple incubation. They found that Dox-loaded EVs not only lowered the dosage of the drug but also inhibited cancer cell growth by activating the caspase-3/7 pathway. Their findings supported the potential of EVs as ideal methods to locally deliver Dox to treat RB while avoiding systematic effects.248

Although most of the studies of EVs in ocular tumors are focused on their diagnostic potential, the treatment effects of EVs can also be expected. EVs from immune cells, such as T-cells and macrophages, may facilitate the immune modulation and antitumor strategy in ocular tumors.

Clinical Potential

The therapeutic potential of MSC-EVs in retinal diseases is currently under investigation in clinical trials (Table 7). A phase I study investigated EVs derived from HUMSCs to promote the healing of large and refractory macular holes in China (NCT03437759). EVs will be intravitreally injected during vitrectomy, together with internal limiting membrane peeling and tamponade with silicon oil or air. Recently, a phase II/III study using HUMSCs-EVs in the treatment of retinitis pigmentosa is also carried out in Turkey (NCT05413148). In this study, EVs are derived from MSCs obtained from allogenic human Wharton jelly, which is the mesenchymal tissue of the umbilical cord. After injection into the subtenon space, a six-month follow-up visit will be performed to investigate their efficacy on visual functions. Studies on the prognostic role of serum EVs in DR are also ongoing. A team from China collected serum samples from patients with/without DR every 12 months for 5 years (2018–2023) and investigated the significantly altered exosomal miRNAs indicative of the onset and development of DR (NCT03264976).

Clinical Trials of EVs in the Treatment and Diagnosis of Posterior Segment Ocular Disease

EVs in the posterior segment ocular disease are widely used in the studies. Due to the importance and complexity of the posterior segment of the eye, the diagnostic application of EVs from noninvasive or minimal-invasive biofluid become particularly critical to monitor the onset and progression of diseases, such as AMD, DR, and ocular tumors. Apart from the abundant studies on exosomal biomarkers, EVs as therapeutic therapies are also a hot research topic. Different from the anterior segment disease, the routes of administration of EVs are also various, including intravitreal, retro-orbital, and subretinal injections, offering more possibilities to treat diseases from different parts of the eye. Given the less immunogenic, safe, and nontoxic properties, their clinical application is promising as a future direction of development.

Challenges and Future Perspective

EVs have received extensive attention in recent years due to their diagnostic and therapeutic potential in various diseases. There are still many challenges in the fields of production, safety, manufacturing, and quality control before EVs go into the clinic (Figure 9).

Challenges in the diagnostic and therapeutic application of EVs in ocular disease.

Isolation of EVs in Ocular Biofluids

The isolation method for EVs remains a problem. The high yield and purity of EVs are prerequisite factors for downstream application if EVs are to be employed as potential biomarkers or therapeutics for ocular disease. Although emerging methods and technologies have been developed to extract EVs efficiently, the yield and purity of EVs cannot be guaranteed at the same time. The coisolated contaminants together with EVs such as lipoprotein and nonexosomal functional vesicles will not only affect the accuracy of diagnosis but the safety of clinical application will be suspected. Meanwhile, the structural and biological integrity of isolated EVs is also a problem that influences the accuracy of subsequent experiments. In addition, most of these technologies are specifically designed for large-volume samples, such as plasma and culture medium, instead of ocular biofluids, including tear fluid, aqueous humor, and vitreous humor, which limits further investigation of EVs in these samples. Therefore, isolation technology for high-purity EVs with maintained function, especially appropriate for ocular biofluids, is needed based on a clear classification of EV subtypes and characterization of other nonexosomal functional vesicles or components. Meanwhile, methods to amplify the signal of the EV surface or cargos can also be ways to either benefit isolation of EVs or analysis of disease mechanism. This combination of isolation and analysis will facilitate point-of-care testing in clinical practice in that even though the yield of EV in ocular biofluid cannot be guaranteed, subsequent EV-based studies can still be carried on.

Safety Issues in EV-Based Therapy

Although EVs have a lower immunogenic potential than their parental cells, toxicity and side effects are still a main concern with EV-based therapy, which should be taken into consideration before conducting a clinical examination. It has been reported that EVs derived from genetically engineered T cells with chimeric antigen receptors have a low risk of toxicities, such as cytokine release syndrome due to similar therapeutic effects with their parental cells, despite their efficacy in the inhibition of tumor development.249 The different routes of administration, such as intravenous and intravitreal, can also impact the biological distribution and clearance of EVs. When applied systemically, some of the EVs may induce immune regulation while some may induce apoptosis of certain cells in the body.250 Moreover, interfering neural communication, unexpected proliferation in the eye, and potent immunosuppressive properties of MSC-EVs-based therapy have also been considered as potential risks of EVs in their therapeutic application.195,251,252 All these uncertainties arise from an unclear understanding of the high complexity and heterogeneity of EVs, including their size, content, function, and source/origin, which may greatly impact their effects on recipient cells. In addition, the supraphysiological injection dosage of EVs and frequency of administration in different studies also add to the uncertainties of the safety issues. With the advancing knowledge of the heterogeneity of EVs, their cargo, and functions, the demand for a precise and accurate characterization of EVs in the pathogenesis and treatment of ocular diseases will persist and flourish.

Cost-Effectiveness in EV Manufacturing

The manufacturing of EVs is a highly sophisticated process that requires careful optimization and precise control at every stage. Particularly when there is growing anticipation for the clinical application of native/engineered EVs as therapeutics for various ocular diseases, the quality of the final EV product appears to be of great significance. It is influenced by the manufacturing workflow from the choice of biofactory and production conditions to downstream processing, characterization, and storage. Although devices and techniques are emerging, the cost of large-scale manufacturing of EVs is still high. The accessibility and affordability of expensive equipment or techniques may limit the clinical application of EVs, especially in developing countries. To improve the cost-effectiveness of EV manufacturing, researchers and manufacturers are exploring easily accessible and affordable strategies, such as the development of isolation and purification techniques, optimization of production process, innovation of culture systems for large-scale EV production, and investigation of potency assays.6,253,254

Storage of EVs in Quality Control

Storage and preservation of produced EVs are important parts of the quality and amount of the final product. Though the storage of EVs is not expected to be as difficult as cell-based therapy preservation, some limitations undoubtedly exist. To date, it is recommended that storage at −80 °C in phosphate-buffered saline is the most used way to preserve isolated EV samples.255 But this certainly adds to increased costs and limitations in transportation. In addition, both the storage of freshly isolated EVs and the recovery of EVs from previously stored samples appear to affect EV properties,256 while the mechanism of storage-induced changes in EVs stability remains unclear. Also, the process in the recovery and purification of EVs after storage is not consistent. Especially with the emerging application of bioengineered EVs, the impact of the storage method on the characteristics of these EVs still needs investigation. Currently, there is already specific criteria for the identification of the physical and biochemical characteristics of MSC-EVs.257 Further development of assays to measure therapeutic activity of EVs before and after storage and in-depth knowledge of the optimal storage methods for EVs will allow EV-based therapies to fully realize their potential as an emerging class of promising therapeutics.

Conclusions

EVs have received growing interest as a promising tool for cell-free strategies due to their high diagnostic and therapeutic potential in ocular disease. EVs derived from plasma/serum, aqueous humor, and vitreous humor have been used for the diagnosis of a wide range of ocular diseases, like glaucoma, myopia, AMD, diabetic retinopathy, and ocular tumor. The delicate structure of the eyeball (i.e., enclosed space, multilayer structure of cornea/retina) and different routes of administration to treat ocular disease (i.e., topical, intravitreal injection) enable EV-based therapies to maintain high drug concentration in the eye. Even though there are still many knowledge gaps to be filled and considerable challenges in the practical use to be confronted with, EVs still have huge potential to be used in the clinic. To achieve this, feasible technologies/devices designed for EV isolation from a tiny volume of biofluid (i.e., tear fluid, etc.), with both high yield and purity, are still an unmet need. Following this, more tear EV-based noninvasive diagnostic biomarkers for different ocular diseases may emerge. Also, strategies to unravel EV subpopulation derived from a specific type of tissue/cell/disease of interest (tissue derived EVs, single vesicle analysis, etc.) are essential for biological studies. Through the application of interdisciplinary approaches, it is anticipated that there will be an increasing number of research and clinical efforts focusing on EV-based studies in the future, providing innovations for conventional therapeutic and diagnostic practice.

Cell-derived nanoparticles that contain RNAs, lipids, proteins, and other functional molecules, and play an important role in intercellular communications, immune modulation, and angiogenesis regulation

A special structure of the eye, which is composed of a blood–aqueous barrier and blood–retinal barrier, and creates an immunosuppressive microenvironment distinctive from other parts of the body
