Fecal and colonic microbiota differ in composition, function, diagnostic utility and correlation with inflammatory cytokines in ulcerative colitis
Authors: Wenting Chen, Qianhui Zhao, Jinlong Li, Zubiyan Ainiwaer, Chenfei Zhang, Aizizai Palihati, Wenli Fu, Hongru Wu, Xiaoling Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFecal samples have been frequently used to investigate ulcerative colitis (UC)-related gut microbiota study due to their easy availability. However, whether fecal microbiota accurately represents the mucosal microbiome of UC patients remains controversial. This study aims to analyze the gut microbiota in feces and colonic tissue, and to demonstrate from different perspectives whether the gut microbiota in feces can fully characterize the gut microbiota in colonic tissue. We employed 16S rRNA sequencing and standardized microbiome analysis for fecal and colon tissue samples from 12 UC patients and 16 non-IBD controls (NIC). And we also integrates ELISA, functional analysis, and machine learning techniques to discuss the similarities and differences among differentially identified UC-related microorganisms screened from two sample types. As results, colonic tissue exhibited greater microbial diversity than feces. And some taxa were only detected in colonic tissue. In UC, α-diversity was higher in colonic tissue but lower in feces compared to NIC. Only two microbial genera (Ligilactobacillus and Intestinimonas) showed consistent differential abundance across both sample types. At the functional pathway level, both fecal and colonic microbiota were primarily associated with metabolic pathways; however, colonic microbiota were additionally linked to immune-related pathways, such as the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. IL-1β, IL-17 and IL-22 were significantly overexpressed in UC. Most fecal differential microbes were negatively correlated with those three cytokines. Regarding the two shared differentially expressed genera, abundance of Ligilactobacillus in tissues was more strongly correlated with the expression of inflammatory cytokines. The correlation between the abundance of Intestinimonas and the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines showed opposite trends in tissues and feces. The combined SHAP and random forest algorithms shown that the UC classification model based on tissue markers was more stable and exhibited weaker overfitting than the model based on fecal markers. In general, fecal microbiota profiles did not adequately represent the microbial landscape of colonic tissue in patients with UC. For studies investigating disease mechanisms, colonic tissue was the more appropriate sample type. Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease that primarily affects the colon and rectum, characterized by mucosal inflammation and ulceration of the intestinal lining1. The disease imposes a significant burden on patients’ quality of life, manifesting through symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and rectal bleeding, and contributes to increasing healthcare costs and societal burden. Recent studies have identified notable alterations in the gut microbiota of UC patients, including decreased diversity and composition shifts2, suggesting potential mechanistic link between microbial dysbiosis and UC pathogenesis. A more comprehensive understanding of microbial abundance and shifts in UC may facilitate the discovery of novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies3.

Fecal samples are accessible biological specimens and are frequently used in gut microbiome research. However, whether fecal microbial composition accurately represents the mucosa-associated microbiota remains contentious. Several studies have demonstrated inconsistencies between microbial communities in feces and colonic tissues, raising concerns about the validity of using fecal samples as surrogates for intestinal biopsies4. For example, Li et al. collected fecal and colonic tissue samples from the same colorectal cancer (CRC) patient and observed marked differences in microbial composition between the two sample types5. Additionally, Feng et al. reported that while the α-diversity of CRC tissue samples was higher than that of healthy controls, no such difference was found in fecal samples6. Similar observations have been reported in UC studies. Lin et al. compared microbial profiles of the cecum, rectum, and feces in UC and healthy individuals, and found that specific phylum-level alterations (e.g., reduced Proteobacteria, increased Bacteroidetes) were exclusive to the rectal mucosa7. Furthermore, even in patients with benign conditions such as common colon polyps, substantial differences were detected between fecal and mucosal microbial communities8. These findings collectively suggest that gut microbiota exhibit sample-type-specific distributions.

Xinjiang is recognized as a high-incidence region for UC in China9. However, local studies on UC remain at a preliminary stage. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic analysis of fecal and colonic mucosal microbiota in UC and non-IBD controls (NIC) using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and standardized microbiome analysis. We further assessed the consistency of microbial biomarkers across sample types and evaluated their potential for diagnostic model development. Our findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of gut microbial heterogeneity in UC and provide methodological guidance for future biomarker discovery and mechanistic research.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 28 participants who denote paired colonic tissue and fecal sample were enrolled in this study for microbiome analysis, including 12 UC patients and 16 NIC. The UC group included 8 men and 4 women, while the NIC group included 7 men and 9 women; no significant sex difference was observed between groups. Likewise, there were no significant differences in age, body mass index (BMI), or ethnicity. However, UC patients exhibited significantly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and white blood cell (WBC) counts, reflecting systemic inflammation, whereas other clinical parameters showed no significant group differences (Table 1). For subsequent microbiome analyses, samples were further subdivided into UC_F, NIC_F, UC_T, and NIC_T, representing feces (F) and tissue (T) samples from each group.

Alpha and beta diversity analysis

A total of 626 ASVs were identified in fecal samples and 3,293 in tissue samples. All six α-diversity indices (Observed, Chao1, ACE, Simpson, Shannon and Pielou) differed significantly between tissue and fecal samples (Fig. 1A–F). In fecal samples, UC patients displayed significantly reduced α-diversity compared to NIC (P < 0.05), whereas in tissue samples, UC patients exhibited higher α-diversity than NIC, though the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

PCoA analysis revealed significant differences in gut microbiota between fecal and colonic tissue samples (Fig. 1G). While there was no difference in microbiome composition between UC and NIC in fecal samples (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.0433, P = 0242), tissue sampes showed distinct clustering (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.1366, P = 0.005). ANOSIM further confirmed that between-group variation exceeded within-group variation (R = 0.5179, P = 0.001) (Fig. 1H).

Taxonomic analysis

At Phylum level, both fecal and tissue microbiota were dominated by Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota, with no major differences between UC and NIC (Fig. 2A), though the changes between UC and NIC were generally more pronounced in tissues. For example, Proteobacteria decreased in UC feces relative to NIC, while showing an opposite increase in UC tissues. At the genus level (Fig. 2B), several genera, including Enterococcus, exhibited high relative abundance in tissue samples, particularly in UC patients, but were minimally detected in fecal samples.

Bars represent mean relative abundances of the top 10 taxa per group; taxa outside the top 10 were grouped under “Others” for clarity of visualization. Colors correspond to different bacterial taxa.

Differential abundance analysis

Based on LEfse analysis, 23 genera were found to be differentially abundant between UC and NIC groups in fecal samples, while 107 genera showed significant differences in colonic tissue samples. The top 20 genera with highest LDA of fecal and tissue were shown in Fig. 3A,B respectively. Notably, only 2 genera (Ligilactobacillus and Intestinimonas) were commonly altered in both sample types, suggesting a substantial divergence in microbial profiles between feces and tissue.

Functional pathway analysis

Functional pathway prediction revealed distinct profiles between fecal and tissue microbiota. In fecal samples (Fig. 4A), differential pathways between UC and NIC were primarily associated with metabolism, including fructose and mannose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, linoleic acid metabolism, and glycerolipid metabolism.

Left panels display the relative abundance of predicted KEGG pathways. Right panels show the corresponding log₂ fold changes between groups and adjusted P values. Red and blue bars represent enrichment in UC and NIC, respectively.

In contrast, tissue microbiota (Fig. 4B) exhibited broader functional shifts. While several metabolic pathways overlapped with those observed in feces, UC tissues additionally showed enrichment in immune and inflammatory pathways such as NOD-like receptor signaling and antigen processing and presentation, alongside disease-related pathways including bladder cancer, prion disease, and cardiomyopathies. These findings suggest that tissue-resident microbiota may better reflect the host’s immunopathological state in UC.

Correlation between gut microbial and inflammatory cytokines

Intense inflammation was one of the defining characteristics of UC. Among 14 inflammatory cytokines in colonic tissue, IL-1β (Fig. 5A), IL-17 (Fig. 5B) and IL-22 (Fig. 5C) of UC were significantly higher than NIC. The result of other cytokines were shown in Fig. S1 and Supplement Table 1. Most fecal differentially expressed gut microbiota were negatively correlated with those three cytokines (Fig. 5D). Genera Peptostreptococcus and Barnesiella showed the highest positive and negative correlations, respectively. Among the selected tissue-differentiated gut microbiota, genera Herbaspirillum, HT002, and Erysipelothrix showed the highest correlations with IL-1, IL-17, and IL-22, respectively (Fig. 5E). Notably, for the common differentially expressed microbiota Ligilactobacillus, its abundance in colonic tissue correlated with inflammatory cytokines was more strongly than in feces. However, Intestinimonas in tissue shown a positive correlation with cytokines but opposite in fecal.

Scatter plots of IL-1β (A), IL-17 (B) and IL-22 (C) expression in UC and NIC, and heatmaps of correlation analysis with fecal (D) and colonic (E) differential microbiota.

Classification of UC using gut microbial biomarkers

Potential microbial biomarkers for UC diagnosis were initially identified using LEfSe analyses. Genus-level taxa that met the differential abundance thresholds were subsequently refined via AUC base on their relative abundance. The AUC of all differentially genera in feces were over 0.7. Among 107 differentially genera in tissue, 97 genera with an AUC greater than 0.7 were preserved (Supplement Table 2). Base on SHAP value, the top 5 important genera of feces were Alistipes, Barnesiella, Phascolarctobacterium, Peptostreptococcusa and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group. (Fig. 6A). while in tissue were Herbaspirillum, Erysipelothrix, Nocardioides, Myroides and Tissierella. (Fig. 6B).

Feature importance analysis results and model construction results. (A,B) shown The top 10 feature with the highest SHAP value in fecal sample and tissue sample, respectively. (C,D) show the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of the random forest models based on fecal biomarkers and colon tissue biomarkers, respectively. Red represents the ROC of the training set under leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), and blue represents the ROC of the independent validation set.

The randomforest model was trained on the top 5 important genera under consistent model parameters, and performance was assessed using LOOCV. The model based on fecal biomarkers achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.964 in training dataset and 0.750 in validation dataset (Fig. 6C), shown a obvious overfitting. Compared with the model base on fecal biomarker, model of tissue biomarker was relatively more stable and has a weaker overfitting effect, with AUCs of 0.987 and 0.933 on the training set and independent validation set, respectively (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Despite extensive research on UC, a comprehensive understanding of its pathogenesis remains elusive, particularly concerning the role of site-specific gut microbiota. The intestinal microbiome has emerged as a critical factor in UC, and its characterization may unlock novel strategies for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic intervention. However, whether fecal samples, which are widely used due to their accessibility, accurately reflect colonic mucosal microbiota remains unresolved. This study addresses this gap by comparing fecal and tissue-derived microbiota from UC patients and NIC.

Diversity and taxonomic analyses revealed substantial compositional differences between fecal and tissue microbiota. Colonic tissue samples exhibited greater microbial complexity compared to fecal samples. Consistent with previous studies, UC patients showed significantly reduced fecal α-diversity relative to NIC10–13. However, in colonic tissue, α-diversity was higher in UC patients than in controls, corroborating earlier reports14,15. This divergence highlights the site-specific nature of microbial alterations in UC. Although dominant taxa were generally consistent at the phylum level across both sample types, several microbial families and genera were uniquely detected in tissue, suggesting a distinct mucosa-associated microbiome.

These compositional distinctions were further emphasized through differential abundance analyses. Only two genera (Ligilactobacillus and Intestinimonas) were consistently altered across both fecal and tissue samples, underscoring the limited overlap in microbial markers. This observation aligns with prior findings that fecal microbiota only partially represents mucosal communities16,17. Moreover, colonic microbiota, compared to fecal counterparts, displayed a broader range of altered genera, supporting their greater relevance to disease pathophysiology18. We also explored the functional implications of these microbial shifts. Functional pathway predictions revealed that fecal microbiota alterations in UC were predominantly related to metabolic pathways, consistent with previous metagenomic studies19,20. In contrast, tissue microbiota was linked not only to metabolism but also to immune signaling pathways, such as the NOD-like receptor signaling and antigen processing pathways, indicating a more direct interaction with mucosal immunity21,22. Although functional speculation based on 16S sequencing data still needs to be validated by metagenomics and metabolomics, the current results indirectly suggested that the colonic microbiota had a dual function, which may help to understand the pathogenesis and immune dysregulation of ulcerative colitis.

Persistent mucosal inflammation is a hallmark of UC and is closely linked to microbial imbalance23. Disrupted microbial communities can directly interact with the intestinal epithelium and immune cells through pattern recognition receptors like Toll-like receptors and NOD-like receptors24, triggering downstream signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB) and leading to excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators25,26. In this study, we observed high expression of IL-1β, IL-17 and IL-22 in the patient’s colon tissue. Those three cytokines mainly associated with the macrophage-mediated Th17 cell immune pathway and were considered to be the key factor in the pathogenesis of UC27. In fecal, We found that a significant number of differentially expressed microbiota were negatively correlated with inflammatory factors, including beneficial bacteria of the genera Barnesiella, Lachnospira, and Alistipes. Furthermore, uncommon genera of microbiota such as Herbaspirillum and Myroides were also found in the tissue samples. Literature indicates that these microorganisms are typically found in immunocompromised individuals28,29, suggesting that the damaged colonic mucosal barrier in UC patients provides conditions for their survival and reproduction. Finally, it is worth noting that Intestinimonas showed significant intergroup differences in both fecal and tissue samples, but exhibited the opposite trend in the calculation results of inflammatory cytokines. In this study, Intestinimonas was not detected in any UC fecal samples, but it was enriched in tissue samples of UC. We speculate that this difference may be related to spatial competition among microbiota. Intestinimonas, a butyrate-producing gut microbe, struggles to competing for metabolic niches in the intestinal tract with other dominant butyrate-producing bacteria (Bacteroides and Parabacteroides)30,31. When the gut environment is damaged, a large number of inflammatory cytokines are released, leading to a reduction in beneficial bacteria, while Intestinimonas gains more opportunity to colonize the colonic mucosa. This intrinsic compensatory mechanism, unaffected by inflammatory cytokines, holds promise for providing new insights into microbial therapy for UC.

Based on interpretable machine learning models, this study selected different combinations of biomarkers from fecal and tissue cohorts using the same strategy. The random forest model for fecal biomarkers showed significant overfitting (AUC of 0.964 in training vs 0.750 in validation), while the model for tissue-based biomarkers demonstrated better UC discrimination ability (AUC of 0.987 in training vs 0.933 in validation). These results suggest that while tissue samples may better capture pathophysiological alterations. Due to the small sample size in this study, it was impossible to truly evaluate the efficacy of fecal biomarkers and their models. Although the random forest model based on fecal biomarkers exhibited overfitting in this study, fecal samples remain a viable and non-invasive alternative for clinical diagnostics32,33. Nonetheless, this study has limitations. The sample size was relatively small, and findings should be validated in larger, independent cohorts. Additionally, metagenomic and metabolomic integration may provide deeper insights into functional microbial dynamics17,34. Future studies incorporating longitudinal designs and multi-omics data could further elucidate the causal relationships between gut microbiota and UC progression.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the site-specific nature of gut microbiota alterations in UC. Colonic tissue harbors a more diverse and functionally versatile microbiota, which may engage more directly with mucosal immune processes and contribute to disease pathogenesis. Nevertheless, fecal microbiota remains a valuable, non-invasive source for diagnostic biomarker discovery due to its accessibility and translational potential. Together, these findings highlight the critical importance of sampling location in microbiota-based analyses and support a complementary approach that integrates both fecal and tissue-derived microbial profiles in UC research and clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Participants and samples collection

A total of 43 participants were recruited from the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, comprising 17 patients diagnosed with UC and 26 Non-IBD controls (NIC). UC diagnosis was establihed based on clinical presentation, radiological findings, endoscopic sevaluation, and confirmed by histological examination of colonic biopsies35. NIC participants were individuals without IBD but with general gastrointestinal conditions such as polyps or functional disorders. All participants were excluded if they had received antibiotic treatment within three months prior to sample collection.

Fecal and colonic tissue samples were obtained from each participant and immediately stored at − 80 °C until further processing. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (approval number: KY2024120190), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

DNA extraction and 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing

Genomic DNA were extracted from fecal samples using HiPure Stool DNA Mini Kit (Magen, China) and from colonic tissue using the Magnetic bead tissue genome extraction kit (Nanomagbio, China) , following the the manufacturer’s protocols. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR (95℃ for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles at 95℃ for 30 s, 55℃ for 30 s, 75℃ for 15 s, then 72℃ for 5 min) with the forward primer 341F (5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’) and the reverse primer 805R (5’-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3’). PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1.5% agarose gel, purified using magnetic beads, and sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., USA).

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequence reads were processed using QIIME2 (version 2024.10)36. DADA2 plugin was utilized to denoise and grouped the sequences into Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). After the rarefy normalization, taxonomic annotation of the ASVs was performed using the Naïve Bayes classifier in QIIME2, referencing the SILVA database (release 138, SSURef_NR99)37.

Six alpha diversity indices (Observed features, Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson and Pielou) were calculated, and statistical differences between UC and NIC were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Beta diversity was analyzed using Bray–Curtis distance metrics, visualized via principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and statistically evaluated using PERMANOVA nad ANOSIM.

Differential taxonomic abundance between UC and NIC groups at each taxonomic level was identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe; LDA > 2, adjust P < 0.05) , implemented in the R package microeco (v1.14.0).

Functional prediction

Microbial community functions were analyzed using PICRUSt2 (v2.5.2)38, based on ASVs representative sequences. Predicted KEGG Orthologs (KOs)39 were mapped to KEGG pathways. The R package ggpicrust2 (v2.1.2) was employed to identify KEGG pathways were differentially abundant between groups.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Frozen colonic tissues were thawed on icerinsed in ice-cold PBS. After the initial centrifugation, samples were resuspended in 250 uL 1 × PBS and crushed. The lysate was ultrasonically disrupted (200W, 5 min) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was taken and the protein concentration was determined by BCA method. The concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-9, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17, IL-21, IL-22, IL-23 were quantified utilizing commercial ELISA kits (MultiSciences, China; TB Healthcare, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The OD value of each well was determined at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.4.3). Patient characteristics were summarized as absolute and relative frequencies for categorical data and as medians and interquartile range (q1, q3) for numerical data. Group comparisons used Mann–Whitney U, Fisher’s exact, or chi-square tests as appropriate. Correlations between gut microbial abundance and clinical cytokine level were assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation.

Potential microbial biomarkers selecting

Potential microbial biomarkers were initially screened using LEfSe results, and then calculated the AUC base on the relative abundance by using pROC R package. Microbial biomarkers over 0.7 were enrolled to train the Randomforest model by using RandomForestClassifier function in sklearn package of python 3.11. The SHAP value (shap package of python 3.11, v0.51.0) was used to selected the most important feature. Finally, a new Randomforest model was trained based on the top 5 important feature under leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) and was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.
