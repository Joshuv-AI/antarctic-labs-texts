Re‐entry of donors deferred for false‐reactive screening tests in Canada: 10 years post implementation
Authors: Chinchin Wang, Steven J Drews, Mark Bigham, Mindy Goldman, Sheila F O'Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesCanadian Blood Services implemented a re‐entry programme in 2014. Donors deferred because of false‐reactive or indeterminate screening tests can provide a specimen for re‐entry testing 6 months after their initial test, and resume donating if they test negative for all infectious markers. We evaluated the impact of the programme on donor return and retention, and assessed factors associated with reactive results upon re‐entry testing.Materials and MethodsUsing extracted data from our donor database, we followed up donors who tested false‐reactive for hepatitis B virus (HBV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV), from the implementation of the programme on 3 February 2014 until 30 June 2025 (n = 8094). We characterized the donors reaching each step of the programme by sex, age, donation status and infection marker. We employed logistic regression to identify important predictors of obtaining reactive results upon re‐entry testing.ResultsOverall, 17.3% of donors eligible for re‐entry testing successfully returned and donated. Re‐entry testing participation rates were 35.5%, with repeat and older donors more likely to participate. Among re‐entry tested donors, 59.2% tested negative. Most donors continued to donate for multiple years upon re‐entry. Upon return, 13.1% tested false reactive on a subsequent donation. False‐reactives upon re‐entry testing were more likely for first‐time donors and less likely when a different assay was employed than for the initially false‐reactive.ConclusionThe implementation of a donor re‐entry programme has resulted in donor retention and increases in the blood supply. Sending reminder notifications to eligible donors could increase participation.Keywords: blood donors, false‐reactives, infectious disease, re‐entry, testing

Highlights

The donor re‐entry programme at Canadian Blood Services has resulted in the successful return and requalification of 1443 donors over a 10‐year period, who would previously have been indefinitely deferred because of false‐reactive screening results for anti‐human immunodeficiency virus (anti‐HIV), anti‐hepatitis C virus (anti‐HCV) and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).

INTRODUCTION

Ensuring the safety of blood components and reducing the risk of transfusion‐transmitted infections is a critical priority for blood operators. Canadian Blood Services (CBS) collects blood donations in all Canadian provinces except Quebec. Blood donations are universally tested for hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), syphilis and human T‐cell lymphotropic virus (HTLV). Reactive donations are discarded and made to undergo further confirmatory testing prior to notification of donors and provincial and territorial public health authorities [1].

False‐reactive results, where a sample is repeat‐reactive on the initial screening test but negative or indeterminate on the confirmatory test, can occur because of cross‐reactivity with non‐infection‐related antibodies [2, 3]. False‐reactive results have been associated with increased antibody production following vaccinations, infections with other viruses and allergies [2, 3]. False‐reactive results may be assay‐specific and non‐reactive when retested on a different assay [2], and be transient or long‐term, persisting for several months to years [3, 4]. Historically, donors with reactive samples on screening tests were permanently deferred from donating, regardless of confirmatory test results [2]. This precautionary approach was intended to preserve the safety of the blood supply but limits the pool of potential donors by excluding those with false‐reactives who are unlikely to have been infected [3, 5].

CBS implemented a re‐entry programme on 3 February 2014 for donors deferred because of false‐reactive or indeterminate screening tests for HCV antibody (anti‐HCV), HIV‐1/2 antibody (anti‐HIV‐1/2), hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and false‐reactive nucleic acid tests (NAT) for these viruses. In 2023, the programme was expanded to include donors with false‐reactive results for syphilis and HTLV. Individuals are invited to provide a specimen‐only sample for re‐entry testing at least 6 months after their initial false‐reactive status. Donors who test negative for all markers may resume donating, while those with reactive specimens are permanently deferred. Re‐entered donors who obtain a subsequent false‐reactive result for any marker are also permanently deferred. In April 2021, CBS changed serology screening assays, resulting in individuals who received a false‐reactive result prior to the change and provided a specimen for re‐entry after being tested on two different platforms. Donors with reactive results for hepatitis B core antigen antibodies (anti‐HBc) are not currently eligible for re‐entry, as CBS does not have confirmatory testing facility for this marker.

There is a dearth of evidence regarding the effectiveness of various re‐entry strategies, and there is no consensus of protocols among blood operators [3]. Different jurisdictions employ different waiting periods, may not defer donors until two repeat reactive results and may not require a specimen‐only sample before donating. Although re‐entry protocols retain donors and increase donations, the balance with operational efficiency must be considered.

Here we report the evaluation of our donor re‐entry programme for donors with HCV, HIV and HBV false‐reactive results, using over 10 years of data. This report does not include the more recently implemented donor re‐entry for HTLV and syphilis. We describe the impact of the programme on donor return and retention as well as blood supply, characteristics of participating donors, subsequent reactive results and the consequences of the change in testing platforms. Insights into the successes and limitations of our re‐entry protocol may inform strategies for other blood operators, as well as areas for improvement.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Screening for transfusion‐transmissible diseases

All donations are individually screened for anti‐HCV, anti‐HIV‐1/2, HBsAg and anti‐HBc (except source plasma for anti‐HBc) [6]. Samples that are reactive on initial serological screening test are subject to up to two repeat tests. If one of the repeat tests is reactive (‘repeat‐reactive’), the donation is discarded and confirmatory testing is done (not applicable for anti‐HBc).

Donations are also screened by multiplex nucleic acid amplification testing for HCV RNA, HIV RNA and HBV DNA in pools of six units, with single‐unit testing when a reactive sample is identified. Donors with reactive NAT samples are indefinitely deferred from donating. Repeat‐reactive screening serological samples with a non‐reactive corresponding NAT are subjected to confirmatory testing for anti‐HIV‐1/2, HBsAg and anti‐HCV. Samples that are reactive on the initial screening test but non‐reactive on NAT and non‐reactive or indeterminate on confirmatory tests are considered false‐reactives. Reactive NAT results likely to have been subject to contamination may be deemed false‐reactive on occasion after review of donor test results and history.

Testing platform and assays

Before April 2021, serological screening tests for anti‐HIV‐1/2, HBsAg, anti‐HBc and anti‐HCV were conducted using the Abbott PRISM chemiluminescent assay (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, Illinois). All serological screening tests from April 2021 onwards are conducted using the Roche Elecsys electrochemiluminescence assay (Roche Molecular Systems, Branchburg, New Jersey). NAT was conducted using the cobas TaqScreen MPX test on the cobas s201 system (Roche Molecular Systems) until end‐December 2017 and has been conducted using the MPX test on the cobas 6800/8800 system (Roche Molecular Systems) since then. Confirmatory tests include third‐generation HCV serology assays, fourth‐generation HIV serology assays and HBsAg assays with neutralization.

Donor re‐entry programme

The donor re‐entry programme is conducted as part of standard operating procedure. Donors with false‐reactive serological screening or NAT tests for HCV, HIV and HBV are eligible for re‐entry testing 6 months after their initial false‐reactive test. Donors are informed of their test results and date of eligibility for re‐entry testing in a notification letter. Donors must make an appointment by phone for re‐entry testing at a permanent or mobile CBS donation site. Re‐entry testing comprises currently used serology screening assays and single‐unit NAT screening for the false‐reactive and all other markers. Donors who obtain non‐reactive results for all tested markers on their re‐entry test are ‘re‐entered’ and able to resume donating as normal. Donors who obtain a repeat‐reactive or indeterminate screening test on a donation after re‐entry are considered ‘re‐entry‐reactives’ and are indefinitely deferred from donating, with no opportunity to re‐enter again. Donors who successfully re‐enter but subsequently have a reactive or indeterminate screening test on a donation are considered ‘subsequent reactives’ and are also indefinitely deferred.

When the re‐entry programme was initially implemented in 2014 and expanded in 2023, donors who had tested false‐reactive in recent years were retrospectively notified of their eligibility for donor re‐entry. Although not all donors who had false‐reactive results prior to the start of the programme were notified, they were also able to participate by contacting CBS.

Study population

This study focuses on donors who tested false‐reactive for HCV, HIV or HBV and were notified of their eligibility for re‐entry following programme implementation in 2014. Donors who tested false‐reactive prior to the start of the programme but who re‐entered following retrospective notification or through voluntary participation are excluded, as well as donors who tested false‐reactive for HTLV or syphilis.

Data source

Data on test results, donations, deferrals and demographics were extracted from CBS's Epidemiology Donor Database. Donors were followed up from 3 February 2014 to 30 June 2025.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the donors reaching each step of the donor re‐entry programme overall, as well as by sex (female, male), age (17–29 years, 30–49 years, 50+ years), donation status (first‐time, repeat), infection marker (HCV, HIV, HBV, multiple markers), clinic type at the initial false‐reactive (permanent, mobile) and initial testing platform (Abbott, Roche). Descriptive statistics were also used to examine trends by year of eligibility and re‐entry from 2014 to 2024. Multivariate logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios of successfully re‐entering and donating by sex, age, donation status, infection marker and clinic type, as well as obtaining a repeat reactive on the specimen re‐entry test by sex, age, donor status, infection marker, testing platform and the time between the initial false‐reactive and re‐entry testing in months. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Ethics statement

Because this study is an evaluation of an operational programme and only aggregate non‐identified data are presented, study approval was waived by the CBS Research Ethics Board.

RESULTS

Participation in the donor re‐entry programme

In total, 8094 donors tested false‐reactive for HCV, HIV and/or HBV between 3 February 2014 and 30 June 2025. Over this time, 2870 donors (35.5%) provided a specimen for re‐entry testing. Among them, 1699 (59.2%) tested negative, and 1443 of these donors (85.0%) returned to donate (Figure 1). Overall, 17.3% of donors eligible for re‐entry testing successfully re‐entered and donated.

HCV false‐reactives made up most donors who were eligible for re‐entry testing (51.6% of false‐reactives), followed by HIV (39.5% of false‐reactives), HBV (7.4% of false‐reactives) and multiple markers (1.5% of false‐reactives). HCV false‐reactives also accounted for 52.0% of donors who successfully re‐entered and donated (HIV: 36.8%; HBV: 10.8%; multiple markers: 0.4%). However, HBV false‐reactives had the highest percentage of negative re‐entry specimen test results and were 50% more likely to re‐enter and donate than HCV or HIV false‐reactives.

The majority (53.4%) of donors who provided a specimen for re‐entry testing did so within their first 6 months of eligibility, although 14.2% waited between 6 months and 1 year and 32.4% waited for more than 1 year. The percentage of donors providing a specimen within 6 months of eligibility increased after 2020, while the total number of donors eligible for re‐entry testing and re‐entered donors varied from year to year (range 25%–45%) (Figure 2).

Most donations have been from repeat donors, particularly older age groups. However, among first‐time donors, individuals aged 17–29 years made the most donations (Figure 3). Reactives on re‐entry test

Table 2 displays re‐entry reactive rates by donor characteristics, transmissible disease and testing platform. A total of 1171 donors (40.8%) tested reactive on their re‐entry test. Re‐entry reactive individuals tended to be older and were over 3 times as likely to be first‐time donors compared to repeat donors.

The majority (64.8%) of eligible donors obtained their initial false‐reactive screening result on the Abbott testing platform, which was in place until 1 April 2021. False‐reactive rates for donations made over the follow‐up period were higher on the Abbott platform than on the Roche platform (0.55% vs. 0.47%). However, the rate of re‐entry reactive tests exclusively using the Abbott platform was 39.5% compared to 55.9% for donors exclusively tested using the Roche platform. Reactive rates on the re‐entry test were much lower—9.3% for donors tested initially on Abbott with the re‐entry test on Roche. These donors waited an average of 3.8 years between their initial false‐reactive and re‐entry test compared to 10 months among Abbott‐only donors and 6 months among Roche‐only donors. Reactive tests on re‐entry were slightly less likely with increasing elapsed time between the initial false‐reactive and re‐entry testing (Table 3). This time effect was more evident for donors initially tested using the Abbott platform (Figure S2).

Subsequent reactives from re‐entered donors

There were 189 donors (13.1%) who returned to donate following successful re‐entry but obtained a subsequent reactive screening test on a donation attempt and were deferred indefinitely. While 43 donors (22.8%) were deferred on their first attempt, 146 (77.2%) donors amassed a total of 748 donations (636 whole blood; 65 plasma; 47 platelet) before their subsequent reactive screening test. Just over half (51.9%) of subsequent reactive tests occurred within 6 months of re‐entry testing and 72.5% within 1 year.

Among these deferred donors, 177 (93.7%) tested repeat‐reactive for the same transmissible disease as their initial false‐reactive status (111 HCV, 53 HIV, 13 HBV). Subsequent reactive rates were 13.1% for HCV, 9.1% for HIV and 9.1% for HBV. Out of the 12 donors who tested reactive for a different transmissible disease, 7 were for HTLV or syphilis, and the majority (83.3%) obtained their subsequent reactive test over a year after re‐entry testing. No re‐entered donors obtained positive results upon confirmatory testing.

DISCUSSION

Between inception of CBS' donor re‐entry programme in February 2014 and June 2025, over one‐sixth (17.3%) of donors with false‐reactive results for HCV, HIV and HBV have successfully re‐entered and donated. These 1443 returned donors have accumulated 13,733 donations. Participation rates for re‐entry testing were at 35.5%. The majority (59.3%) of re‐entry tests were negative, with negative tests more common among repeat donors, donors with HBV false‐reactive results and those tested on the Abbott platform. Among returned donors, approximately one‐eighth (13.1%) obtained a subsequent reactive test, resulting in indefinite deferral.

Repeat donors were much more likely to participate in the re‐entry programme and return to donate, similar to the findings from Chinese blood centres [7, 8]. Repeat donors typically have greater motivation to donate and develop a self‐identity as a blood donor after repeated donations [9]. Return rates tend to be much lower for first‐time donors after any type of deferral, but particularly those related to infectious test results [10]. Among repeat donors, more donations were gained from those in older age groups, likely because they include more long‐term donors with a strong commitment to blood donation [9, 10]. Among first‐time donors, more donations were gained from those aged 17–29 years. These younger donors represent an important pool of potential long‐term donors who would otherwise be permanently deferred.

Donors with false‐reactive results for HBV were more likely to undergo re‐entry testing and successfully re‐enter and donate than those with false‐reactive results for HCV or HIV, with a 25.2% return rate compared to 17.4% for HCV and 16.1% for HIV. Few donors with false‐reactive results for multiple markers underwent re‐entry testing, and only half of those who were re‐tested obtained a negative test. However, initial false‐reactive results were more likely for HCV and HIV, resulting in a larger number of these donors returning through the re‐entry programme.

The number of donations by re‐entered donors has steadily increased each quarter, with the exception of a drop at the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic in March 2020. The majority of re‐entered donors continued to donate over 3 years post re‐entry, despite some year‐to‐year donor loss. Further, while re‐entered donors accounted for 0.09% of the donor pool, they provided 0.15% of total donations, donating more frequently than the average donor. This suggests that the re‐entry programme has been successful in retaining re‐entered donors.

Participation rates for re‐entry testing represent a key evaluation metric with room for improvement. Over the study period, 35.5% of eligible donors returned, and participation rates within 1 year of donor notification (6 months of eligibility) were between 11.9% and 26.8% depending on the year, with higher rates since 2021 (during and after the COVID‐19 pandemic). There have been no operational changes in programme recruitment, and reasons for the increase in participation are unclear. Participation rates were slightly lower than those reported by a similar programme at Héma‐Québec within 1 year of notification (42.1%) [11], but higher than the overall rate of 18.2% in Chonqing, China [8], and similar to overall rates reported from Dalian, China (32%) [7]. At CBS, donors are only informed once about the re‐entry programme, in the notification letter for their false‐reactive result. Donors may not notice or understand, or may lose interest, become distracted or forget, that they are able to re‐enter over the 6‐month waiting period, or may find providing a specimen‐only sample inconvenient. Qualitative research from the Netherlands found that donors reported confusion upon receiving letters regarding false‐reactive test results and that re‐engaging deferred donors once re‐eligible for donating could improve return rates [12]. Research on reasons for donor participation or non‐participation in the Canadian context would provide further insight into strategies for recruitment. Reminder messages sent shortly before a deferral ends have been shown to improve return rates for various temporary deferrals [13]. This cost‐effective strategy could strengthen donor awareness of and participation in the re‐entry programme and is something CBS is examining implementing.

Other blood operators employ different criteria for donor re‐entry. The waiting period after testing false‐reactive for HIV or HBV is 3 months at Héma‐Québec (6 months for HCV) [11]. Similar to CBS, the Chinese Blood Transfusion Society recommends a waiting period of 6 months before re‐entry testing but an additional 3 months after a successful re‐entry test before returning to donate [7]. In the United States, there are waiting periods of 6 months for HCV and 56 days for HIV and HBV [14, 15, 16, 17]. Imposing a shorter waiting period or not requiring a specimen‐only sample could increase participation rates in the CBS programme but could also result in more re‐entry reactives [2]. Our waiting period of 6 months is conservative and was adequate in allowing over half (59.3%) of donors to resume donating after one false‐reactive result. It is also standardized across infectious markers, which may be simpler for operations and donor messaging.

The most important predictor of obtaining a reactive result on the re‐entry test was the testing platform. The re‐entry reactive rate for donors tested initially using Abbott and subsequently using Roche was just 9.3%, compared to 55.9% for Roche only and 39.5% for Abbott only. This is in line with evidence suggesting a lower rate of false‐reactives when re‐tested using a different platform [2], as well as results from Héma‐Québec (42.9% re‐entry reactive rate for same platform vs. 2.7% for different platform) [11]. Like other studies [8, 11], reactive rates upon re‐entry testing decreased slightly with increasing time between the index false‐reactive and re‐entry test. Our findings suggest that re‐testing on a different assay gives a lower false‐reactive rate and that recruitment of donors with false‐reactives tested using the Abbott platform (pre‐April 2021) could be particularly beneficial to increasing the donor base, now that the Roche platform is in place.

Other predictors of obtaining a reactive test upon re‐entry testing were donor status and type of transmissible disease. Like other studies, first‐time donors were three times as likely to test reactive on their re‐entry test as repeat donors [8, 11, 18]. This could be due to false‐positive results from repeat donors being more likely to be spurious or transient, given that they had non‐reactive results in the past, compared to first‐time donors who may have persistent false‐reactive results for biological reasons (e.g., past exposure or antibody cross‐reactivity) [2, 3]. In our study, re‐entry reactives were less common for HBV markers compared to HCV and HIV, which may be due to non‐specific background in HBsAg assays or recent HBV vaccination. These patterns are very similar to those from Héma‐Québec, Australia and Chongqing (Figure S3) [8, 11, 19]. However, as a larger number of donors obtain false‐reactives for HCV and HIV than for HBV, the latter represents just 10% of donors who successfully re‐entered.

In the United States, a specimen‐only sample is not required for HBsAg false‐reactives [16, 20], and in Australia, donors are indefinitely deferred only after two false‐reactive results for HBV, HIV, HCV and HTLV within a 12‐month period [19]. Allowing donors to return to donate after a 6‐month waiting period and simply testing their donation would increase the number of donations, as 15.0% of donors with negative specimen tests did not return to donate. It might also encourage more donors to participate in re‐entry testing by streamlining the steps required to re‐enter. This approach could be reasonable for donors who obtained their initial false‐reactive on the Abbott platform and would now be tested using Roche, as over 90% of these donors passed their specimen test. However, among donors who obtained their initial false‐reactive on Roche, over half of subsequent donations would need to be discarded, given its higher re‐entry reactive rate. As such, this approach may not be cost efficient, as the specimen‐only approach and may place a greater burden on donors.

Three re‐entry samples were considered positive, although after closer examination of results it is unclear if they were truly positive. The results suggest these could be resolved infections (in the case of HCV) or recent vaccination (in the case of HBV).

Among donors who passed their re‐entry test and returned to donate, 13.1% obtained a subsequent reactive test on a donation attempt. Similar to the study by Héma‐Québec [11], the majority (72.5%) of these tests occurred during the first year after re‐entry and may similarly be due to transient biological cross‐reactivity [2]. Although factors associated with subsequent reactive tests were not explored, no affected donors tested positive on confirmatory testing.

Strengths and limitations

This was a robust evaluation of a large‐scale donor re‐entry programme, with over a decade of data. During this time, we changed serological screening assays, allowing us to assess the impact of different assays on reactive rates upon re‐entry testing. Given the limited available evidence on various re‐entry strategies, our findings may provide valuable insight for blood operators.

As a post‐implementation evaluation of an operational programme, we were unable to assess the impact of employing different waiting periods on participation rates and false‐reactive rates upon re‐entry testing. We were similarly unable to assess the use of different eligibility criteria or recruitment strategies on programme success. Our study was based solely on operational data, and we did not have qualitative data exploring reasons for donor return or non‐return. Our findings are specific to the population served by CBS (all Canadian provinces except Québec) and may not be generalizable to other jurisdictions. Finally, given the lack of published data regarding donor re‐entry strategies, we were limited in our ability to make comparisons with other blood services.

In conclusion, over one‐sixth of donors with false‐reactive results for HCV, HIV and HBV have successfully returned to donate through CBS's donor re‐entry programme over the last decade, resulting in the retention of over 1000 donors who would otherwise be permanently deferred. About one‐third of eligible donors underwent re‐entry testing. This programme has been partially successful, as it has resulted in the retention of many repeat and older donors who are committed to blood donation, underscoring the value of implementing a donor re‐entry programme as a part of standard operations. However, the majority of eligible donors do not participate in the current donor re‐entry programme. Improvements in donor recruitment, such as donor outreach and reminders, could increase the programme's impact on donor participation, retention and, ultimately, the blood supply. Further research into reasons for donor return or non‐return would provide additional insight for donor recruitment.
