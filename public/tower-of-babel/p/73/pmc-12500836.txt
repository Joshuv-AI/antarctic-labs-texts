Pain and opioid use after robotic colectomy with natural orifice vs abdominal wall extraction: a single-institution analysis
Authors: Katherine Baur, Kara K. Brockhaus, Marianne Huebner, Nivya Kolli, Robert K. Cleary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe specimen extraction incision is the largest during minimally invasive colectomy and conceivably the most painful. We recently adopted natural orifice extraction through the rectum or vagina, leaving only port site incisions for abdominal wall pain. Previous studies demonstrating natural orifice extraction advantages are inconclusive. The study aim was to determine if natural orifice specimen extraction is associated with less pain and opioid use than abdominal wall specimen extraction after enhanced recovery robotic anterior (sigmoid) resection for benign disease.MethodsThis is a single-institution retrospective analysis comparing abdominal wall and natural orifice extraction sites after robotic anterior resection for diverticular disease in a prospectively maintained institutional colorectal surgery database from 1/1/2021 to 11/21/2024. The primary outcomes were postoperative opioid use measured by morphine milliequivalents analyzed with generalized linear models and postoperative pain scores analyzed with ordinal logistic regression models.ResultsThere were 251 anterior (sigmoid) resections for diverticular disease, 136 patients with abdominal wall specimen extraction sites, and 115 patients with natural orifice extraction. Patient characteristics were well matched between groups. One-fifth (20.7%) of the study population required no postoperative opioids. There was no significant difference in postoperative opioids, measured by morphine milliequivalents, between natural orifice and abdominal wall extraction site groups (aOR1.21, p = 0.59). Lower preoperative pain (aOR0.73, p = 0.02) and better mental health (aOR1.05, p = 0.05) were associated with no postoperative opioids. Patient-reported preoperative PROMIS-10 pain scores and younger age were associated with higher postoperative numeric pain scores. There was no significant difference in postoperative complications between groups (abdominal wall 8.1% vs natural orifice 13.0%).ConclusionThere was no significant difference between abdominal wall and natural orifice extraction in postoperative pain, postoperative opioids, and complications after robotic anterior resection for diverticular disease. These data may assist targeting postoperative pain solutions and counsel patients considering specimen extraction site options.

Enhanced recovery pathways include minimally invasive surgical approaches and multimodal pain management strategies that emphasize non-opioid pain regimens because opioids are associated with postoperative ileus and increased hospital length of stay [1, 2]. The specimen extraction site is the largest incision and conceivably the most painful after minimally invasive colorectal resection. We previously showed in a robust enhanced recovery pathway (ERP) that there is no significant difference in postoperative pain and opioid use when comparing different off-midline extraction sites during robotic colorectal surgery [3]. Discharge opioid prescriptions were written for 48% of this patient population that consisted of an average of 6–7 tablets with each tablet equivalent to 5-mg oxycodone.

Our ERP team sought to identify options to decrease opioid use for colorectal surgery patients. Recently, we have adopted natural orifice extraction (NO) through the rectum or vagina for non-malignant disease, leaving only port site incisions as a source for abdominal wall pain. Current literature is inconclusive for the advantage of NO, and there is a paucity of literature specific to the robotic surgical approach [4–6].

The primary aim of this study was to compare cumulative opioid dose and maximum postoperative pain scores in patients undergoing natural orifice specimen extraction through the rectum or vagina versus specimen extraction through abdominal wall (AW) sites after robotic anterior (sigmoid) resection for diverticular disease. The primary hypothesis was that NO is associated with less postoperative pain and less opioid use than after AW specimen extraction. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS-10) variables provide valuable insight beyond medical record data and were collected preoperatively [7].

Methods

Data source and study population

The Trinity Health Ann Arbor Institutional Review Board approved this study under the exempt category with waiver of HIPAA Authorization. This is a retrospective analysis of all elective ERP robotic anterior (sigmoid) colectomies for diverticular disease registered in a prospectively maintained institutional REDCap® database between 01/01/2021 and 11/21/2024. All study patients were ≥ 18 years of age with an ASA score of 1–3. Urgent and emergent cases, other colorectal procedures, cases with concomitant procedures, those converted-to-open, and patients with a history of chronic opioid and/or cannabis use were excluded.

Visual analog numeric pain scores and PROMIS-10 Global Health (GH) data are routinely collected in our prospective REDCap® database. PROMIS-10 assesses global health through physical and mental health subdomains and incorporates pain-related questions.

Outcome and predictor variables

The primary outcome was no opioids received after surgery and cumulative opioid doses received after surgery for the total postoperative hospital stay, as measured by morphine milliequivalents (Total MME). Total MME was further divided into post-anesthesia care unit (PACU MME) and patient care unit (Floor MME) subgroups to help understand potential differences in opioid requirements in these units. All opioids, both intravenous and oral, were converted to MME. Postoperative maximum numeric pain scores (NPS) were collected on the day of surgery (POD-0), on postoperative day one (POD-1), and on postoperative day two (POD-2).

The exposure variables were abdominal wall (off-midline, Pfannenstiel, and midline) and natural orifice (rectum and vagina) specimen extraction sites. Covariates included age, ASA classification, and patient-reported PROMIS-10 preoperative physical, mental health, and pain scores.

Preoperative patient variables included age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, ASA classification, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, cardiac complications, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, sleep apnea, tobacco use, chronic kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, venous thromboembolism, other), alvimopan use, and transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block. Diabetes included those who are insulin and non-insulin dependent. Cardiac complications include coronary artery disease, history of myocardial infarction, and cardiac arrhythmias. Preoperative variables also included patient-reported PROMIS-10 preoperative physical, mental health, and pain scores.

Other outcomes variables were discharge opioids, operative time from skin incision to skin closure, time to first flatus (ttf), time to first bowel movement (ttbm), hospital length of stay, postoperative complications, emergency department (ED) visits, and readmissions. Ileus was defined as no oral intake ≥ 4 days after surgery or the need for nasogastric tube insertion after surgery and within 30 days after discharge.

Procedures

Alvimopan, acetaminophen, and celecoxib were routinely administered in the preoperative suite except when contraindicated due to allergy. A standard bupivacaine TAP block was routinely administered in the preoperative suite by the anesthesia team. Acetaminophen, celecoxib, and sometimes gabapentin were continued postoperatively unless there were contraindications. Anterior (sigmoid) resection was done with the robotic approach by four surgeons using standard port placement and operative techniques. All dissection and anastomoses were intracorporeal. Abdominal specimen extraction sites were Pfannenstiel or off-midline by extending the 12 mm right lower quadrant port site incision. The AW specimen extraction choice was dependent on surgeon preference and patient body habitus. The anastomosis in patients with AW specimen extraction was done with a circular stapler after transection of the upper rectum with a robotic stapler and after placing the anvil in the descending colon using intracorporeal techniques. Viability of the descending colon and rectum used for the anastomosis was confirmed using immunofluorescence.

NO extraction was performed according to surgeon preference and the frequency of cases increased during the study period with experience. Most transanal extractions were done after passing a wound protector per anum. If the specimen was not easy to extract in one piece and since all cases were for benign disease, the mesentery and colon were separated and the specimen passed per anum in two or more pieces. The anvil was typically passed per anum prior to extracting the specimen and was positioned in the descending colon using the same intracorporeal methods used during AW extraction cases. Managing the rectum after specimen extraction and prior to the anastomosis varied among surgeons with some stapling the upper rectum and then performing the anastomosis as usual. Other surgeons placed a hand-sewn purse string suture on the upper rectum that was then tightened around the stapler spike and followed by a circular stapled anastomosis.

Some patients received an intravenous opioid bolus by anesthesia in the early postoperative period in the post-anesthesia care unit. This was followed by oxycodone written as a PRN postoperative order when acetaminophen, celecoxib, and gabapentin did not control pain or when there was a contraindication to these non-opioid medications. No patients received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia.

Statistical analysis

To compare patient characteristics between the NO and standard surgery groups, continuous variables were summarized with means and standard deviations or medians and quartiles, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized with frequencies and percentages. Wilcoxon, Pearson tests, or univariable generalized linear models were used as appropriate. The PROMIS Global Health items were used to calculate T-scores for physical health and mental health for further analysis. T-scores were calculated by converting raw patient responses into standardized scores using tools available on the HealthMeasures scoring website (https://www.healthmeasures.net/score-and-interpret/calculate-scores), aligning raw data with population-based normative values, with T-scores scaled to a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, where higher T-scores indicate better overall health and lower pain levels. Patient characteristics were compared for those with missing pain score values and those with complete observations. It is reasonable to assume that these were missing at random due to clinical processes of obtaining such data, except for pain scores on POD-2 when many patients were discharged.

Ordinal logistic regression models were used to examine associations with pain score outcomes, namely maximum pain scores on POD-0, POD-1, and POD-2. Since a proportion of patients had no opioid requirements, we used hurdle models consisting of a zero-inflation model and a conditional model with a Tweedie distribution for opioid usage outcomes (i.e., PACU total MME, floor total MME, and in-hospital total MME) [8]. Explanatory variables were surgical group, age, and preoperative PROMIS-10 scores (pain, physical health, mental health). Other covariates included sex, BMI, and ASA.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 [9] and the package glmmTMB v.1.1.10 [10].

Results

The study population of 251 anterior (sigmoid) resections for diverticular disease included 136 patients with standard AW specimen extraction sites and 115 patients with NO extraction (Fig. 1). AW extraction sites included 69 (50.7%) off-midline right lower quadrant and 67 (49.3%) Pfannenstiel. NO extraction included 85 (73.9%) specimens passed per rectum and 30 (26.1%) per vagina. Maximum postoperative pain scores

However, baseline patient characteristics were the same for those with missing values and those with complete observations, overall and within the NO group. Thus, we proceeded with a complete case analysis. Table 3 shows that NO was associated with less pain than AW on POD-0, but this did not reach significance (OR 0.607, p = 0.054). There was no difference in maximum pain scores between NO and AW groups on POD-1. Preoperative pain was associated with higher maximum NPS on POD-0 (OR 1.198, p = 0.023) and POD-1 (OR 1.204, p = 0.022). Lower preoperative physical health scores were associated with a higher maximum NPS on POD-1 (OR 0.941, p = 0.022). On POD-2, some patients were already discharged, and NPS was missing for a larger percentage of patients (34.6% in AW and 40.9% in NO). Differences in baseline characteristics between patients with missing and complete observations indicate a younger age (mean 59.3 vs 62.2, p = 0.071), a lower percentage of ASA 3 (39.4 vs 51.0%, p = 0.075), and a shorter operative time (214.2 vs 240.8 min, p = 0.003) for those patients with missing NPS data. In the subgroup with available NPS, there was no difference in maximum pain scores between NO and AW groups on POD-2. Older age was associated with a lower NPS on POD-2 (OR 0.968, p = 0.020). Better preoperative mental health scores were also associated with a lower NPS, although it did not reach significance (OR = 0.96, p = 0.065).

Discharge opioids

There was no significant difference between groups in patients who received discharge opioid prescriptions (AW 36.0% vs NO 33.9%, p = 0.726), and 64.9% of the study population did not receive discharge opioid prescriptions. Multivariable analysis revealed that older patients had fewer discharge opioid prescriptions (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–0.99, p = 0.022). Higher maximum numeric pain score during the hospital stay was associated with discharge opioids (OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.17–1.56, p < 0.001).

Other postoperative outcomes

Other postoperative outcomes are described in Table 4. There was no significant difference between groups in time to return of gastrointestinal activity defined as the time to first flatus (AW 1.12 days vs NO 1.23 days, p = 0.266) and the time to first bowel movement (AW 1.11 days vs NO 1.02 days, p = 0.397). Median postoperative LOS was not significantly different between groups (AW 2.00 vs NO 2.00, p = 0.817). Postoperative complications occurred in 13.0% (n = 15) of patients in the NO group and 8.1% (n = 11) of patients in the AW group (p = 0.125). Discussion

This retrospective analysis of patients who underwent robotic anterior (sigmoid) resection for diverticular disease in a robust dedicated enhanced recovery pathway shows that there was no significant difference in postoperative opioids administered, as measured by MME, in patients with NO specimen extraction when compared to AW extraction sites. Higher preoperative pain scores as measured by PROMIS-10 were associated with receiving opioids on the floor, or in total, but not in PACU. Better mental health scores were associated with the absence of opioids, although it did not reach statistical significance.

There was no significant difference in postoperative pain scores between NO and AW groups. Different factors were associated with maximum NPS on different postoperative days. Initially, higher preoperative pain scores as measured by PROMIS-10 were associated with a higher maximum NPS on POD-0 and POD-1 but not on POD-2. Advanced age was associated with lower maximum NPS on POD-1 and POD-2. While better preoperative physical health was associated with less pain on POD-1, better mental health was associated with less pain on POD-2.

These data may be used to counsel patients in preadmission ERP class with implementation of an individualized multimodal non-opioid pain management strategy that may better fit patient needs. Patient perspective obtained with standardized PROMIS-10 scores may provide valuable insights beyond medical record data [7]. Evaluating PROMIS-10 scores in our study was important for comparing the NO and AW extraction techniques because these patient-reported scores provide a standardized, comprehensive measure of pain, physical and mental health.

NO during colectomy was first described with the laparoscopic approach by Morris Franklin in 1993 [11]. Our operative approach was adopted based on the NICE procedure, reported using the robotic approach by Haas in 2019 [5]. A matched pair case–control study by this group showed that the NICE procedure was associated with earlier return of bowel function, shorter hospital length of stay, and lower total opioid use when compared to laparoscopic left-sided colorectal resection with the conventional extracorporeal-assisted technique. There were no differences in complications between groups [5]. Other studies have shown favorable outcomes associated with NO specimen extraction that include shorter return of bowel function, shorter hospital LOS, and less postoperative pain [5, 6, 12–14].

Previous studies have shown less postoperative pain for the NO approach in both laparoscopic and robotic surgery [5, 15]. In contrast to previous studies, our study population was less heterogeneous in that both NO and AW groups were patients who had robotic anterior (sigmoid) resection for diverticular disease in a robust ERP, all with intracorporeal techniques that included intracorporeal anvil placement and intracorporeal anastomosis. We have previously shown outcomes advantages for intracorporeal compared to extracorporeal techniques that include less conversion-to-open, shorter return of bowel function, and shorter hospital length of stay [3, 16]. The largest previous study evaluating the NO technique compared robotic NO to extracorporeal laparoscopic AW [5]. In contrast to this and other analyses, our study groups were both robotic and intracorporeal and differed only in the site of specimen extraction. This may be why our study did not demonstrate NO advantages for time to return of bowel function and shorter hospital LOS as shown in other studies [5, 6, 10–13, 17, 18]. It is possible that the combination of robotic and ERP in both groups mitigated some of the outcomes differences shown in other studies.

Transrectal extraction exposes rectal mucosa and sometimes stool to the peritoneal cavity. A study addressing the potential for increased infections with transrectal extraction during laparoscopic sigmoidectomy found no increase in abscesses or other complications when compared to abdominal wall extraction sites [19]. The complication rate was not different between NO and AW groups in our study, and there were 2 (1.5%) organ space SSI III in the AW group compared to 5 (4.3%) organ space SSI III in the NO group. This NO organ space SSI III rate is slightly higher than the organ space SSI III rate in the NICE study (3.6%) and may require follow-up evaluation. We currently irrigate the rectum after anesthesia induction to decrease the risk of stool spillage in the operative field when transecting the rectum. The readmission rate was significantly higher for the NO group than the AW group, but the readmission rate was only 4.4%, and none were due to postoperative pain.

Our study is limited by retrospective design. However, our ERP robotic study groups differ only in abdominal wall versus NO extraction site in contrast to other studies, thereby mitigating confounders that may be responsible for outcomes differences in other studies. Previous studies have shown less postoperative pain for NO extraction using visual analog pain scores with only one other study measuring morphine milliequivalents [5, 6, 13, 15, 18]. There are limited data assessing these measures in colorectal surgery patients. Intraoperative opioids are not available in our database and are uncommon in our robotic ERP patient population who have received TAP blocks. Nevertheless, there may be a small number of patients who received intraoperative opioids, and this could conceivably impact our results. Of 251 study patients, preoperative PROMIS-10 surveys were available for 218 patients, and NPS were available for 194 patients. However, there were no differences in baseline patient characteristics for those with missing and complete observations at these time points.

While postoperative pain and opioid use were not significantly different in NO and AW groups, it may be that other elements of ERP mitigate pain even more so than specimen extraction site. Our pharmacist-led multimodal pain management strategy has resulted in a 21% opioid-free robotic practice going back to at least 2019 and with associated decrease in discharge opioids. These data suggest that the NO option for specimen extraction is a viable option for patients who want this approach with outcomes comparable to AW specimen extraction. Possible cosmetic and long-term incisional hernia NO advantages without an AW extraction site were not addressed in this study and should be further investigated.

The NO approach has not yet been widely adopted possibly because of perceived technical challenges [5]. Robotic vision and articulated instruments likely allow more patients to be considered for NO as it previously did for minimally invasive intracorporeal techniques [3, 5]. Dividing the upper rectum safely and extracting the specimen safely without injury to the rectum requires education and experienced proctoring. Further studies are needed to confirm these results and explore whether the NO approach is associated with other favorable patient-reported outcomes.

Conclusion

Natural orifice specimen extraction after robotic anterior (sigmoid) resection for diverticular disease is an option with comparable outcomes compared to abdominal wall extraction but with no significant difference in patient-reported postoperative pain and postoperative opioid use. These data may serve to counsel surgeons and patients considering specimen extraction site options.
