The past, present, and future of abdominal aortic aneurysm repair
Authors: Gregory A Magee
Published: 2024

The first open aortic aneurysm repair was performed by Dubost in 1952, and generations of vascular surgeons performed open aortic repairs and perfected the technique. However, the problem of blood loss, the cardiovascular stress of cross clamping the aorta, and open abdominal surgery limited the utility of this operation in patients with advanced age and comorbidities.

Since Juan Parodi performed the first successful endovascular repair of an abdominal aortic aneurysm (EVAR) in 1990, there has been great enthusiasm for the procedure, and in the two decades that followed, EVAR has been widely adopted and has nearly completely replaced open surgical repair as the treatment of choice. However, despite standardization of the procedure and continuous improvement in device design, the long-term success and durability of EVAR is still a concern. In 1998, Torsello reported, in the Journal of Vascular Surgery, the case of a 76-year-old man who underwent successful EVAR in Germany.1 There was no evidence of endoleak at completion of the procedure, nor on computed tomography angiography scan 7 months after repair, but the aneurysm sac had grown 5 mm. Sixteen months after his repair, the patient presented with a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm and underwent successful conversion to open repair. This is believed to be the first reported case of a delayed failure of EVAR resulting in rupture, and the implications of this revelation still reverberate to this day.

In the wake of reports like this, four large randomized clinical trials were conducted to compare the outcomes of EVAR vs open surgical repair—three in Europe (the EVAR-1 trial in the UK, the DREAM trial in the Netherlands, and the ACE trial in France) and one in the United States (the OVER trial). The EVAR-1 trial found EVAR had a lower short-term mortality, but at 8 years, the survival curves crossed over, and beyond that open repair had significantly lower all-cause and aneurysm-related mortality.2 The significantly higher aneurysm-related mortality in the EVAR group was predominantly attributed to secondary aneurysm rupture. The EVAR group also had higher rates of death from cancer, potentially due to increased radiation exposure from the procedure and surveillance imaging. The DREAM trial also found an early survival benefit of EVAR, but this advantage was lost after 1 to 2 years, and after 12 years of follow-up, there was no survival difference.3 The ACE trial found no difference in overall survival but more aneurysm-related deaths in the EVAR group.4 The OVER trial also found that EVAR had a short-term survival advantage, but after 5 years, there was no survival difference.5 Interestingly, this trial also found that EVAR had better long-term survival for patients under 70 years old, but not for patients older than 70, who would be expected to have more benefit from the less invasive procedure. A subsequent large Medicare study demonstrated similar findings, with lower perioperative mortality following EVAR, but by 3 years, the overall survival curves converged, and by 4 years, significantly more ruptures occurred in the EVAR group.6

Although the scientific jury remains out on the long-term benefit of endovascular vs open repair, the court of public opinion clearly remains in favor of EVAR, as its use has increased to greater than 85% in the United States. A common refrain from EVAR enthusiasts is that endovascular technology is constantly evolving and improving. Endograft fabrics have progressed with coatings, weave designs, and multi-layer designs, largely resolving the issue of graft porosity seen in early generation grafts. Device designs have incorporated tissue fixation and improved conformability to hostile necks. Novel ideas such as use of polymers as well as endo-anchors have been developed with the goal of improved proximal seal. Even more imaginative solutions have been tested such as the use of polymer-filled endobags that fill the aneurysm sac, and, although initial results have not been overwhelmingly positive, this concept may progress and be incorporated into other device designs in the future. Another unorthodox solution that gathered much interest several years ago was flow-diverting stents, and although this concept has fallen out of favor, it demonstrates how much interest there is among vascular surgeons for continued innovation in aortic aneurysm therapy. Fenestrated and branched EVAR has enabled much longer proximal seal zones, and now nearly every segment of aorta can be repaired endovascularly, but the challenges of endoleaks, continued aneurysm sac growth, and delayed rupture continue to bedevil endovascular repair.

A growing body of evidence suggests that aneurysm sac growth of greater than 5 mm following EVAR is common and is associated with higher rates of rupture and all-cause mortality. Thus, aneurysm sac enlargement appears to represent a failure of EVAR and preventing it remains a priority. Based on these data, the Society for Vascular Surgeons practice guidelines for the treatment of abdominal aortic aneurysms recommends treatment of endoleaks when there is greater than 5 mm of sac enlargement at any point following EVAR.

Endoleaks are a critical failure mode for EVAR, so treatment and prevention of these has received its share of attention. Type Ia endoleaks are increasingly treated with fenestrated-branched EVAR, and type Ib endoleaks can be treated by embolization of the internal iliac artery with extension to the external iliac artery or iliac-branched grafts. Type III endoleaks can be treated with relining, but type II endoleaks are the most insidious and obstinate. A randomized trial in Japan of preemptive embolization of the inferior mesenteric artery demonstrated higher rates of aneurysm sac shrinkage and lower rates of aneurysm sac growth and secondary interventions.7 Some have additionally advocated for prophylactic embolization of the aneurysm sac at the time of EVAR; however, this has as yet failed to demonstrate significant benefit. Delayed embolization of type II endoleaks can successfully halt aneurysm growth, but it can be a tedious endeavor, and reported results have been modest at best.

What remains as clear today as it did when Torsello published his report in 1998 is that our current methods for EVAR are inadequate, and there is still a need for an improved treatment with both low short-term morbidity and long-term durability. The future of abdominal aortic aneurysm treatment will likely involve medical therapy, preventive therapies, and refined therapeutic procedures. The future is certainly ripe for innovative minds to devise improved treatment options.
