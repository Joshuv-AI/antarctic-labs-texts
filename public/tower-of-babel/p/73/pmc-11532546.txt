METTL16 inhibits differentiation and promotes proliferation and slow myofibers formation in chicken myoblasts
Authors: Yifan Liu, Yanju Shan, Lichuan Pang, Yanfei Tang, Ming Zhang, Yunjie Tu, Gaige Ji, Xiaojun Ju, Hualian Jiang, Binghong Xie, Shiying Shi, Jingting Shu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
N6-methyladenosine (m6A) plays a crucial regulatory role in muscle growth and development. In our previous studies, we identified a m6A methyltransferase, Methyltransferase like 16 (METTL16), which is associated with chicken muscle development and muscle fiber type conversion. To further understand the regulatory role of METTL16 in chicken muscle function, we analyzed its expression in muscle tissues with different myofiber type compositions and in chicken primary myoblasts (CPMs) at various stages. We also manipulated METTL16 expression in CPMs to examine its effects on cell proliferation, differentiation, muscle fiber type formation, and global m6A RNA methylation status. Our results showed that METTL16 expression increased during myoblast proliferation and gradually decreased in the late differentiation stage. Furthermore, METTL16 exhibited specific expression in slow-twitch muscles. Cell Counting Kit-8 assays, 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine staining, RT-qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence analyses showed that METTL16 promotes myoblast proliferation while inhibiting myoblast differentiation. We also observed that METTL16 induces the upregulation of slow-twitch myosin heavy chain (MyHC) and slow-twitch-specific genes in myotubes, while downregulating fast-twitch MyHC and fast-twitch-specific genes. Furthermore, both interference and overexpression of METTL16 led to changes in overall cellular m6A modification levels and Methyltransferase like 3 (METTL3) expression levels. These findings confirm that METTL16 plays a key role in myoblast proliferation, differentiation, and muscle fiber type formation in chickens. Considering the role of myoblasts in chicken muscle growth and meat quality regulation, METTL16 may serve as a key target for molecular selection in chicken meat traits.

INTRODUCTION

With the improvement of living standards, the demand for high-quality meat products is increasing. Enhancing both the yield and quality of chicken meat has become a critical goal for broiler breeders (Emmerson, 2003). In chickens, skeletal muscle accounts for approximately half of the body weight and is essential for animal growth and health (Frontera and Ochala, 2015). Skeletal muscle is composed of a mixture of highly heterogeneous muscle fibers, which exhibit diverse physical, chemical, and metabolic characteristics (Hoppeler, 2016). During the embryonic stage and muscle regeneration, chicken myoblasts proliferate and differentiate to ultimately form mature muscle fibers (Dayton and Hathaway, 1991). The size and composition of muscle fibers can change with growth and external stimuli (Sänger and Stoiber, 2001). Numerous studies have shown that the composition of muscle fiber types can lead to differences in meat quality (Ismail and Joo, 2017; Huo et al., 2022; Park et al., 2022). It is generally believed that meat with a higher proportion of slow-twitch fibers possesses superior quality. With the application of genomic selection and gene editing technology in livestock and poultry breeding (Jin et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023a), understanding the molecular regulatory mechanisms of myoblast proliferation, differentiation, and muscle fiber type formation is of significant importance for accelerating the genetic improvement of chicken meat quality traits.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation is the most common RNA modification and has been widely demonstrated to regulate many biological processes (Yue et al., 2015). m6A modifications are mediated by 3 types of enzymes: "writers," "erasers," and "readers" (Yang et al., 2018). The m6A writers, also known as m6A methyltransferases, are responsible for catalyzing the m6A modification of adenine on mRNA. This function is executed by a methyltransferase complex consisting of Methyltransferase like 3 (METTL3) and Methyltransferase like 14 (METTL14) (Liu et al., 2014). Previous studies have shown that METTL3 and METTL14 are involved in the regulation of muscle development. For instance, METTL3 promotes the proliferation and myogenic differentiation of myoblasts in mice in an m6A-dependent manner (Zhao et al., 2022). METTL3/14 can repress skeletal muscle differentiation by methylating MAP kinase-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 2 (MNK2) mRNA (Xie et al., 2021). In poultry, Liu et al. reported that METTL3 promotes the proliferation and differentiation of quail myoblasts (Liu et al., 2023b).

Methyltransferase like 16 (METTL16), a more recently discovered RNA methyltransferase, was initially thought to be a ribosomal RNA methyltransferase (Satterwhite and Mansfield, 2022). Subsequent research has shown that METTL16 binds to mRNAs, lncRNAs, and snRNAs, and is involved in various cellular processes (Warda et al., 2017; Mendel et al., 2018; Su et al., 2022). Currently, there are no reports on the functions of METTL16 in muscle development. In our previous transcriptomic studies on different types of chicken muscles, we observed significant changes in METTL16 expression across different muscles, suggesting its association with muscle growth and fiber type transition (Liu et al., 2020). To further understand the specific role of METTL16 in regulating muscle function, we examined 3 chicken muscle tissues with distinct myofiber type compositions and manipulated METTL16 expression in chicken primary myoblasts (CPMs). Our findings reveal that METTL16 regulates the proliferation, differentiation, and myofiber formation of chicken myoblasts. This provides a theoretical basis for further analysis of the regulatory mechanisms of m6A methylation in chicken muscle development and for improving poultry production.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics Statement

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Science (Approval ID: S20221023, Yangzhou, China). All the experiments in this study were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations set by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People's Republic of China.

Experimental Animals and Tissues

One hundred one-day-old chickens and 100 fertilized eggs of Rugao-Huang chickens were purchased from Jiangsu Xianglong Chicken Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). At 60-days-old, 10 males with similar body weight (average 1.25 kg) were slaughtered to collect 3 typical skeletal muscle tissues with different myofiber type compositions for expression pattern analysis, including pectoralis major (PM), medial gastrocnemius (MG), and latissimus dorsi (LD). Muscle samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C. Fertilized eggs for cell isolation were incubated in a laboratory incubator (DF120, Yunfeng Incubator, China).

Myosin ATPase Staining

Each muscle sample was sectioned into continuous 10 µm thick slices perpendicular to the direction of myofibers using a CM1805 cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Myosin ATPase staining was performed to determine the myofiber type composition of various skeletal muscles, following the methods outlined in our prior publication (Shan et al., 2023). Fiber type classification followed the criteria established by Brooke and Kaiser (1970), where dark staining indicated glycolytic (IIb) muscle fibers, light staining indicated oxidative (I) muscle fibers, and intermediate staining indicated mixed (IIa) muscle fibers.

Cell Isolate and Culture

The CPMs were isolated from E11 Rugao-Huang chickens following established methods (Shan et al., 2020). Briefly, leg muscles were excised, washed with PBS, and minced in proliferation medium containing DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The suspension was vortexed, filtered through 200- and 400-mesh filters, and cells were collected by centrifugation at 1,000 r/min for 5 min, then resuspended in proliferation medium. Serial plating was used to enrich myoblasts and remove fibroblasts.

Upon reaching 80% confluence, FBS was replaced with 5% horse serum (Procell, Wuhan, China) to induce myogenic differentiation. Cells were harvested at 50% and 100% confluence, as well as at 1-, 3-, 5-, and 7-d post-differentiation for expression analysis, with 3 replicates per time point.

Plasmid Construction, Small Interfering RNA Synthesis and Transfection

The coding DNA sequence (CDS) fragment of METTL16 was amplified from E11 chicken leg muscle. The CDS sequences are presented in Supplemental Table 1. The amplified sequence was then inserted between the EcoRI and XhoI digestion sites of the pcDNA3.1-3xFLAG vector (Promega, Madison, WI) to construct an overexpression plasmid. Additionally, si-METTL16 and si-NC were designed and synthesized by RiboBio Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). The METTL16 siRNA sequence was 5′ -CUCACACGGAAUUCUGUCA- 3′. Transient transfection with plasmid or siRNA was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions.

RNA Isolation, Complementary DNA Synthesis, and Quantitative Real-Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from CPMs and muscle tissues using the TRNzol Universal Total RNA isolation kit (DP424, Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using the HiScript III RTSuperMix RT Reagent Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed for cDNA quantification using ChamQ SYBR Color qRT-PCR Master Mix (Q411, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a real-time PCR system (Mx3000P, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA). Chicken β-actin served as an internal control, and gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Primers used in this study were designed using the Primer3 website (https://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/) and are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Cell Counting Kit-8 and 5-E5-Ethynyl-2′-Deoxyuridine Incorporation Assays

For the following experiments, CPMs were transfected with siR-METTL16, siR-NC, pcDNA3.1, or pcDNA3.1-METTL16 once the cell density reached 30 to 50%.

CCK-8 Assay: CPMs were seeded in 96-well plates, and 10 μL of Cell Count Lite 2.0 reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was added to each well at 24, 36, and 48 h post-transfection. The relative luminescence intensity (RLU) was then measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each experimental group included 6 biological replicates.

EdU Assay: CPMs were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected for 24 h according to the protocol of the Cell-Light EdU Apollo 567 In Vitro Kit (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China). Four replicates were performed for each treatment. Images from 6 randomly selected fields were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Immunofluorescence Staining

CPMs were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated with an anti-Myosin heavy chain (MyHC) antibody (B103; DHSB, Bethesda, MD, 1:50) and DyLight 488-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (A23210; Abbkine, Wuhan, China) for immunofluorescence detection of MyHC. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Fluorescence images from 3 randomly selected fields were captured using a DMi8 fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) to visualize the areas labeled with anti-MyHC. Myotube areas were quantified and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics Inc.).

Western Blots Assay

Total protein was extracted from cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with phenylmethane sulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) protease inhibitor (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Western blotting was performed according to standard procedures. The primary antibodies used included: anti-METTL16 (ab221036; Abcam, Cambridge, UK, 1:1,000), anti-Myogenin (MyoG) (bs-3550R; Bioss, Beijing, China, 1:2000), anti-Myosin heavy chain 1F (MYH1F) (F59, DHSB, Bethesda, MD, 1:200), anti-Myosin heavy chain 7B (MYH7B) (S58, DHSB, 1:200), mouse anti-β-actin (EAB011002; ErwanBio; Suzhou, China, 1:10,000). Secondary antibodies used were Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (bs-0296G; Bioss, Beijing, China, 1:2,500) and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (bs-0295G; Bioss, Beijing, China, 1:5,000).

Quantification of Overall m6A Levels

Total mRNA from CPMs was used to assess m6A RNA methylation levels using the m6A RNA Methylation Assay Kit (ab185912; Abcam, UK). Following the manufacturer's instructions, 200 ng of mRNA from each sample was added to the assay wells, then incubated with a detection antibody, capture antibody, and enhancer solution. The m6A methylation status was quantified by measuring the color intensity at a wavelength of 450 nm using the Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Differences between 2 groups were assessed using an independent sample t-test, while one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Significance was set at P ≤ 0.05 and P ≤ 0.01. In multiple group comparisons, significance levels were indicated by different letters, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

METTL16 Promotes the Proliferation of CPMs

To explore the role of METTL16 in cell proliferation, we transfected CPMs with siR-METTL16, siR-NC, pcDNA3.1-METTL16, and pcDNA3.1 to modulate METTL16 expression. The efficiency of overexpression and interference was confirmed (Figure 2A), allowing for subsequent functional analyses. Using the CCK-8 assay, we found that METTL16 overexpression significantly enhanced CPM viability compared to controls, while METTL16 interference reduced cell viability (Figure 2B). Moreover, the EdU incorporation assay showed that METTL16 markedly promoted cell proliferation relative to the control group (Figures 2C and 2D). METTL16 Inhibits the Differentiation of CPMs

To further investigate the role of METTL16 in CPM differentiation, we analyzed its effect on the expression of key differentiation markers. qRT-PCR results showed that the mRNA levels of differentiation marker genes, including MyoG, Myogenic differentiation 1 (MyoD), and MyHC, were significantly upregulated following METTL16 interference (Figure 3A). In contrast, METTL16 overexpression led to a significant decrease in the mRNA levels of these marker genes. Western blot analysis of MyoG protein expression levels corroborated these findings (Figures 3B and 3C). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining for MyHC revealed that METTL16 interference significantly increased the myotube area compared to the control group, while METTL16 overexpression resulted in reduced myotube formation (Figures 3D and 3E). METTL16 Promotes Slow Skeletal Muscle Fiber Formation

To explore the effects of METTL16 on skeletal muscle fiber types, we measured the expression levels of myofiber type-specific genes using qRT-PCR. Interference with METTL16 resulted in a significant increase in the expression of fast-MyHC MYH1F and a significant decrease in slow-MyHC MYH7B (Figure 4A). Additionally, we observed upregulation of fast-twitch myofiber-associated genes such as SRY-box transcription factor 6 (SOX6), Troponin C Type 2 (TNNC2), and Troponin T type 3 (TNNT3), and downregulation of slow-twitch myofiber-associated genes such as Troponin C type 1 (TNNC1), Troponin I type 1 (TNNI1), and Troponin T type 1 (TNNT1) (Figure 4B). Conversely, METTL16 overexpression led to opposite results (Figure 4C and 4D). DISCUSSION

There is increasing evidence suggesting a connection between m6A methylation and muscle function (Wang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). Methyltransferases are key enzymes involved in the regulation of m6A methylation. Among them, the core members METTL3 and METTL14 have been shown to play crucial roles in the proliferation and differentiation of myoblasts (Liang et al., 2021; Petrosino et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). METTL16, a recently discovered m6A methyltransferase, targets a variety of RNA molecules, including U6 snRNA, lncRNA, and mRNA (Brown et al., 2016; Pendleton et al., 2017; Aoyama et al., 2020;Nance et al., 2020). The role of METTL16 in the cell, as well as the functions and importance of its various domains, is still being explored. METTL16 has been shown to play a key regulatory role in the development of various cancers (Wang et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2022). However, the relationship between METTL16 and muscle function remains unclear.

In this study, we observed that METTL16 expression levels in chicken primary myoblasts initially increased and then decreased during proliferation and differentiation phases. A previous RNA-seq study also reported a similar expression pattern of METTL16 during the proliferation and differentiation stages of chicken myoblast (Li et al., 2018). Our CCK-8 and EdU incorporation assays demonstrated that METTL16 overexpression significantly enhanced the viability and proliferation of chicken CPMs, while METTL16 interference resulted in the opposite effect. Wang et al. (2021) found that METTL16 promotes cell proliferation by upregulating cyclin D1 expression in gastric cancer. Additionally, other studies have shown that METTL3 also promotes myoblast proliferation in mice and quails (Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023b). Therefore, our findings suggest that METTL16 contributes to the proliferation of chicken myoblasts.

Previous studies have shown that myogenic regulatory factors (MRF) like MyoG, MyoD, and MyHC are highly expressed and play crucial roles during myoblast differentiation and myotube formation (Hernández-Hernández et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2024). Therefore, in this study, we selected MyoG, MyoD, and MyHC as markers of cell differentiation in chicken myoblasts. Our study found that overexpression of METTL16 significantly reduced the mRNA levels of MyoG, MyoD, and MyHC. Additionally, the protein expression level of MyoG was also significantly decreased. The MyHC immunofluorescence assay further revealed a significant reduction in myotube formation following METTL16 overexpression, consistent with the observed changes in the expression of differentiation marker genes. Interference with METTL16 confirmed its role in inhibiting the differentiation of CPMs. A previous study showed that METTL3 and METTL14 have a negative effect on myoblast differentiation (Hernández-Hernández et al., 2017). In conclusion, our findings suggest that METTL16 plays a significant role in regulating myogenesis.

It is well established that the composition of different muscle fiber types is closely linked to chicken meat quality traits (Mo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of muscle fiber type formation and conversion is crucial for accelerating the broiler breeding progress. Although m6A methylation modification has been shown to play a role in the regulation of muscle fiber types, the existing literature on this topic is very limited (Tan et al., 2022). Our previous study found that METTL16 mRNA expression was significantly higher in oxidative muscles than in glycolytic muscles in chickens (Liu et al., 2020). In this study, we analyzed the mRNA and protein expression levels of METTL16 in 3 types of muscles. The results indicated that METTL16 was highly expressed in slow-twitch fiber-rich LD and MG muscles, but nearly undetectable in the fast-twitch fiber-dominant PM. This suggests that METTL16 plays a role in slow-twitch fiber formation.

With the development of research on muscle fiber types, many marker genes related to muscle fiber types have been identified. For instance, MYH7B and MYH1F encode myosin heavy chain proteins for chicken type I and type IIB muscle fibers, respectively (Verdiglione and Cassandro, 2013). SOX6 is involved in the growth and maintenance of fast-type muscles in chickens (Liu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). The troponin complex, integral to the Ca2+-regulation of muscle contraction and relaxation, includes troponin genes that exhibit muscle fiber type specificity, such as fast-twitch fiber-related TNNC2 and TNNT3, and slow-twitch fiber-related TNNC1, TNNI1, and TNNT1 (Gillis et al., 2007; Rasmussen and Jin, 2021). Therefore, we used these marker genes to further investigate the effect of METTL16 on muscle fiber types through in vitro experiments. We observed that interference with METTL16 reduces the expression of fast-MyHC and fast-twitch fiber-specific genes, while increasing the expression of slow-MyHC and slow-twitch fiber-specific genes. Conversely, overexpressing METTL16 produced the opposite results. These findings confirm that METTL16 promotes the formation of slow-twitch fibers and inhibits the formation of fast-twitch fibers in CPMs.

Although only a few RNAs can be directly methylated by METTL16, many studies have confirmed that the depletion of METTL16 leads to a genome-wide reduction in m6A levels (Pendleton et al., 2017; Mermoud, 2022). Our study found that interference with METTL16 decreased overall cellular m6A methylation levels, while its overexpression increased them, suggesting that METTL16 may regulate muscle function through m6A methylation. The study also observed that interference or overexpression of METTL16 altered the expression levels of METTL3. Given the significant structural differences and distinct binding mechanisms of METTL3 and METTL16, we speculate that they may share a competitive relationship in regulating cellular m6A methylation (Mansfield, 2024). However, the specific mechanisms require further investigation.

CONCLUSIONS

To conclude, this is the first study on the role of METTL16 in chicken muscle function. These results suggested that METTL16 is expressed in a fiber type-dependent manner in chicken skeletal muscles, plays a crucial role in chicken myoblast proliferation and differentiation, and affects myofiber type formation. This function may be driven by an m6A methylation-dependent mechanism, but the specific mechanism requires further research. Our findings may provide key genetic targets for genetic selection and gene-editing breeding in broilers.
