Female-specific risk factors for lung cancer: insights from the All of Us Research Program
Authors: Muhammad Rafiqul Islam, Syeda Masuma Siddiqua, Humayera Islam, Mohammad Hasan Shahriar, Muhammad Ashique Haider Chowdhury, Saira Tasmin, Sreenivas Konda, Habibul Ahsan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer deaths in females, with slower declines in incidence compared to males. While smoking is the leading risk factor, it doesn’t fully explain the increasing lung cancer burden in females. Reproductive factors, metabolic disorders, family history, and their interactions may also influence risk; however, these remain poorly understood, particularly across diverse groups. To explore this, we conducted a case-control study using All of Us Research Program data to examine these factors and their association with lung cancer risk in adult females.MethodsUsing de-identified data from the All of Us Research Program, we conducted an age-matched case-control study to investigate the relationship between sociodemographic factors, reproductive history, metabolic disorders, and family history and lung cancer risk in adult females. Cases and controls (n = 5,500) were identified using SNOMED-CT codes with 1:1 matching. We used descriptive statistics, as well as chi-square and t-tests for group comparisons, followed by multivariable logistic regression to adjust for confounders and estimate the odds of lung cancer.ResultsLung cancer patients had a mean age of 67.9 years (SD ± 11.9) and BMI of 28.9 (SD ± 7.1). Most were non-Hispanic Whites (63.4%), followed by non-Hispanic Blacks (14.5%) and Hispanics (12.2%). Overweight females had lower odds (adjusted OR = 0.78, p = 0.0003), while smoking increased the risk (adjusted OR = 1.28, p = 0.0023). Postmenopausal bleeding (adjusted OR = 1.59, p < 0.001) and menopause (adjusted OR = 1.47, p < 0.001) increased the risk, especially in smokers (adjusted OR = 2.65, p < 0.001) and Asian menopausal females (adjusted OR = 2.67, p = 0.04). Hyperlipidemia (adjusted OR = 1.57, p < 0.001) and hypothyroidism (adjusted OR = 1.63, p < 0.001) were significant, with smoking amplifying hyperlipidemia risk (adjusted OR = 1.71, p < 0.001). Risk was also higher in smokers with lung cancer family history (adjusted OR = 1.94, p = 0.03), postmenopausal females with such a history (adjusted OR = 2.10, p = 0.03), and females with breast cancer family history combined with hypothyroidism (adjusted OR = 1.64, p = 0.0038) or postmenopausal bleeding (adjusted OR = 1.82, p = 0.017).ConclusionBeyond smoking, sociodemographic, reproductive, metabolic, and family history factors, along with their interactions, also influence lung cancer risk in females. Identifying these can aid early detection and prevention.

Background

Lung cancer has emerged as the leading cause of cancer-related death in females [1]. Regardless of smoking status, on average, about 1 in 18 women will develop lung cancer during their lifetime [2]. The lung cancer incidence and smoking rate have declined in the USA over the last two decades [3, 4]. While smoking remains the primary cause of lung cancer in both sexes, the incidence of lung cancer is decreasing at a slower rate in females (1.1%) compared to males (2.6%) [5]. However, differences in smoking habits do not fully explain this trend. Additionally, recent updates suggest that lung cancer is more common among non-smokers and younger females than males in the USA [6, 7]. This shift is raising the importance of understanding the characteristics and risk factors associated with lung cancer in females. Several studies have suggested that the pathogenesis of lung cancer in females may differ from that in males. For example, females have greater CYP1A1-dependent lung DNA adduct formation and altered metabolism of tobacco-related carcinogens, which is influenced by the mutated glutathione S-transferase M1 (GSTM1) [1, 8, 9]. Additionally, females have reduced smoking-related DNA repair capacity, which may heighten susceptibility to lung cancer [10]. Notably, 31.2% of never-smoking female lung cancer patients reported no identifiable exposure to known major carcinogens, pointing to possible unknown or underrecognized risk factors [11]. A positive Family history of lung cancer is also contributing as a risk factor for lung cancer. Besides these risk factors, Reproductive factors may play a role in lung cancer among female adults due to their influence on hormonal dynamics, immune function, and genetic interactions. Factors such as age at menopause, postmenopausal bleeding, and menstrual cycle patterns can control hormonal fluctuations, which can impact lung tissue biology and repair mechanisms, potentially affecting lung cancer risk [12, 13]. Additionally, pregnancy-related hormonal changes and hormone replacement therapy can modulate hormone levels and alter lung cancer susceptibility [14, 15]. Additionally, metabolic disorders, such as dyslipidemia or diabetes mellitus, can increase the risk of lung cancer in females [16]. Furthermore, interaction between sociodemographic, reproductive and metabolic syndromes can also influence the lung cancer risk among females [17].

Evidence regarding the interplay between sociodemographic factors, reproductive factors, and metabolic disorders in female lung cancer risk remains limited and inconsistent. Most existing studies have focused on generalized risk factors or selected individual genetic variants within predominantly European-descent populations, which limits the generalizability of their findings. More comprehensive and diverse research is needed to develop better prevention and treatment strategies for female with lung cancer. This study aims to fill this gap by employing a case-control design within the All of Us Research Program cohort to investigate the relationship between sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive factors, metabolic disorders, and lung cancer risk among a diverse female adult.

Methods

Data source

To conduct our analysis, we used data from the All of Us Research Program, specifically the Controlled Tier Dataset v8 (CT v8), version C2024Q3R4, released on February 3, 2025. The quality and collection procedures for these data have been described previously [18]. Data were accessed via the All of Us Researcher Workbench, a cloud-based platform that allows authorized researchers to build datasets from cohort data and conduct analysis in Jupyter Notebooks. The cohorts, concept sets, and analysis notebooks used for this project are available to authorized Workbench users in the Researcher Workbench Version 8 Workspace Rafiq Projects (https://workbench.researchallofus.org/workspaces/version8rafiqprojects/ or from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. We used the Workbench Dataset Builder to create datasets of female participants categorized into two groups: those with lung cancer and those without lung cancer, determined through observation of Systemized Nomenclature of Medicine–Clinical Terminology (SNOMED-CT), ICD-09, and ICD-10 codes. Included participants were aged 19–95 with a recorded sex at birth of female and electronic health record data linked to their All of Us record. Participants were excluded if they were pregnant at the time of All of Us enrollment.

The All of Us database is a comprehensive nationwide resource that prioritizes inclusivity and the representation of diverse populations. All participants provided written informed consent at the time of enrollment. The collected data undergoes rigorous de-identification procedures before being made accessible to researchers. As this study involved secondary analyses of de-identified data, it is classified as research not involving human subjects under current regulatory guidelines.

Age and race/ethnicity were derived from participant-provided information at enrollment. Age was recorded in complete years and categorized into four groups (19–35, 36–50, 51–60, and 66–95 years), which were used for matching controls. Self-reported race and ethnicity were recoded as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Asian, other race, and no response. Educational attainment, alcohol use, smoking status, and family history of lung, breast, and ovarian cancer were obtained from the Basics, Lifestyle, and Personal and Family Health History surveys. Education was categorized into four groups: less than high school, high school, some college, and finished college. Smoking status was categorized by lifetime exposure: <100 cigarettes as never smokers, ≥ 100 cigarettes as ever smokers. Body mass index (BMI) at enrollment was calculated and categorized as underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25-29.9), and obese (≥ 30) [19]. Neighborhood deprivation was assessed using the American Community Survey-derived Deprivation Index from the Zip Code Socioeconomic Status Data concept set, binned into quartiles (Q1: 0.0-0.20, Q2: 0.21–0.30, Q3: 0.31–0.40, Q4: 0.41–0.63), where lower scores indicate lower neighborhood deprivation [20]. Reproductive factors and metabolic disorders, including the history of estrogen use, postmenopausal bleeding, menopausal status, history of abortion, fertility, polycystic ovarian syndrome, hyperlipidemia, hypothyroidism, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, were ascertained from electronic medical record data linked to participants’ All of Us records. These variables were categorized as binary and coded as no = 0 and yes = 1.

Study design

We conducted a case-control study to assess sociodemographic, reproductive, and metabolic factors associated with primary lung cancer among female participants of the All of Us Research Program. The total cohort included 633,540 participants, of whom 4,878 had a diagnosis of lung cancer. Focusing on female participants with primary lung cancer yielded 2,750 cases. To ensure adequate statistical power (80%) at a 95% confidence level, we selected a 1:1 age-matched control group of females without lung cancer, resulting in a total study sample of 5,500 participants.

Statistical analysis

We utilized the Python Jupyter notebook (v.3.10.2) for conducting descriptive and inferential statistics on the study participants. Descriptive statistics were generated including mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Bivariate analyses were performed to compare characteristics between lung cancer cases and non-lung cancer controls, utilizing Chi-square tests for categorical variables and unpaired t-tests for continuous variables. Multivariable binary logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between various risk factors and lung cancer occurrence among female participants in the All of Us cohort. Separate models were developed for different categories of variables, with specific adjustment strategies employed for each model type. Sociodemographic models were adjusted for body mass index (BMI), race/ethnicity, area deprivation index, smoking status, and alcohol use. Reproductive factor models were adjusted for estrogen use, postmenopausal bleeding, menopausal status, history of abortion, infertility, and polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS). Metabolic disorder models were adjusted for hypothyroidism, BMI, postmenopausal bleeding, menopausal status, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and PCOS. Family history models were adjusted for family histories of lung cancer, breast cancer, and ovarian cancer. All models compared lung cancer cases to female controls without lung cancer from the All of Us cohort. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Of the 633,540 participants in the All of Us cohort, 4,878 (0.77%) had a lung cancer diagnosis, and of these, 2,750 female participants were included in this analysis. The characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of female lung cancer patients was 67.9 ± 11.9 years, and their mean BMI was 28.9 ± 7.1 kg/m². In the lung cancer group, a normal BMI (28.4%) and underweight status (2.5%) were more common, while obesity was significantly more prevalent in the control group (47.9%) (p < 0.001). Regarding neighborhood deprivation, the largest proportion of lung cancer patients (44.9%) belonged to the moderately deprived group (Q3: 0.31–0.40). Notably, both the least deprived (Q1) and most deprived (Q4) groups had higher proportions of lung cancer patients compared to controls (4.65% vs. 2.36% for Q1 and 10.72% vs. 9.67% for Q4, respectively < 001). Most female lung cancer cases were among non-Hispanic Whites (63.4%), followed by non-Hispanic Blacks (14.5%), Hispanics (12.2%), Asians (2.4%), and other races (1.1%) (Fig. 1). Lung cancer patients were less likely to have completed college education compared to healthy females (42.5% vs. 47.9%). Furthermore, 16% of lung cancer patients reported smoking, whereas only 0.1% reported alcohol use. Regarding metabolic disorders, 63.5% of female lung cancer patients had hyperlipidemia, 36.3% had hypothyroidism, 8.1% had type 2 diabetes mellitus, and 18.6% had hypertension. In terms of reproductive factors, 88.8% of patients were postmenopausal, 11.6% reported postmenopausal bleeding, and 28.7% had used estrogen either as contraception or hormone replacement therapy. Among family history variables, breast cancer was the most prevalent (12.1%), followed by lung cancer (10.7%).

Table 2 presents the results of multivariate logistic regression. Overweight was protective against lung cancer (AOR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70,0.92), while underweight showed a non-significant increased risk (AOR 1.48, 95% CI: 0.98,2.24; p = 0.06). Females in the least deprived group (Q1) had a 63% higher lung cancer risk compared to the most deprived (Q4) (AOR 1.63, 95% CI: 1.37, 2.71), whereas Q2 and Q3 groups had lower risks. Female smokers had a 32% higher risk than non-smokers (AOR 1.32, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.55). Among reproductive factors, postmenopausal status and postmenopausal bleeding were associated with 47% and 59% increases in lung cancer risk (95% CI: 1.25–1.72 and 1.31–1.92, respectively) after adjustment. Other factors, such as a history of estrogen use, abortion, and infertility, showed increased risks, but these associations were not statistically significant (p = 0.60, 0.40, and 0.32, respectively). There was also an interaction between menopausal status, smoking, and race. Postmenopausal smokers had a 2.65 times higher risk of lung cancer (95% CI: 1.75–4.01) compared to non-smokers. Additionally, being Asian was associated with a 2.67 times higher risk (95% CI: 1.01–7.13) compared to women of other racial groups.

Among metabolic disorders, females with hyperlipidemia and hypothyroidism had an increased risk of lung cancer compared to the control group (AOR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.40–1.77, and AOR: 1.63, 95% CI: 1.45–1.85, respectively). In contrast, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and PCOS were not associated with a higher risk of lung cancer. Interestingly, even non-smoking females with hyperlipidemia had a higher risk of lung cancer, with their risk increasing from 57% to 71% compared to females without hyperlipidemia. A family history of lung cancer was also associated with an increased risk (AOR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.98–1.40), although this finding was not statistically significant. However, females who smoked and had a family history of lung cancer had a 94% higher risk (AOR: 1.94; 95% CI: 1.03–3.64) compared to non-smokers. Additionally, Females with postmenopausal bleeding and a family history of breast cancer had a higher risk of lung cancer (AOR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.11–2.99). Similarly, those with both hypothyroidism and a family history of breast cancer also had an increased risk (AOR: 1.64; 95% CI: 1.17–2.30) compared to having either condition alone.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to explore female lung cancer risk factors using the All of Us Research data. Among sociodemographic factors, we observed a notable shift in the mean age of female lung cancer patients in the U.S. While existing studies report an average age of about 70 years, our All of Us cohort analysis found a slightly lower average of 67.9 years [21]. This age difference varied significantly by race, with non-Hispanic White females having the highest average age (69.6 years) and Asian females the lowest (62.8 years). A particularly striking finding was that 7.6% of lung cancer cases occurred in females under 50, with Hispanics showing the highest rate of early-onset cases at 16.4%. This observation aligns with earlier studies that have reported a rise in lung cancer among younger females, supporting the trend of a gradual drop in the average age at diagnosis [22] (Fig. 1).

Although BMI does not have a direct association with increased lung cancer risk in females [23], our analysis found that obesity played a protective role compared to normal BMI, while undernutrition was linked to a higher risk. Interestingly, non-Hispanic Black women had the highest percentage of obesity in this cohort, yet they did not have the lowest number of lung cancer cases (Fig. 2). This observation aligns with previous studies describing the “obesity paradox” in lung cancer, where higher BMI may offer a survival advantage or lower risk, despite traditional assumptions about obesity and health risk [24–27]. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because reverse causality is possible: weight loss may occur during the preclinical phase of lung cancer, making lower BMI a consequence of undiagnosed disease rather than a causal risk factor. Since BMI in this study was measured at the time of survey rather than years before diagnosis, prediagnostic weight loss could partially explain the apparent protective effect of obesity. Further studies with longitudinal BMI measurements are needed to clarify whether the association between BMI and lung cancer risk differs across racial groups and to better disentangle causal relationships. We used the deprivation index, a composite measure calculated for each 3-digit zip code prefix area in the United States used in the All of Us dataset, to understand how neighborhood-level socioeconomic conditions might influence female lung cancer outcomes across diverse geographic regions. This index was constructed through principal components analysis of six key socioeconomic variables from the 2017 American Community Survey: (1) Public Assistance Income/SNAP utilization (2), Educational Attainment (3), Median Household Income (4), Health Insurance Coverage types (5), Poverty Status, and (6) Occupancy Status.

Participants in the lowest deprivation quartile (Q1) had a 63% higher risk of lung cancer compared to those in the highest quartile. This may be due to individuals in low-indexed areas typically having better healthcare access, greater health awareness, and improved insurance coverage, leading to higher detection rates and better access than those in deprived areas who remain undiagnosed [29]. Additionally, lifestyle patterns in higher socioeconomic groups, including differing smoking behaviors and higher hormone replacement therapy usage—both risk factors for lung cancer—may also play a role [30, 31]. In contrast, those in deprivation index quartile 3 experienced a 22% lower risk than the reference quartile 4, suggesting that moderate improvements in neighborhood deprivation may confer some protective benefit. Literature suggests that Healthcare access and awareness are significantly influenced by Social Determinants of Health (SDoH) [32]. However, in this analysis, the relationship between lung cancer risk and deprivation index in females is complex and varies among different socioeconomic subgroups. Further research is needed to clarify this relationship.

Smoking is a well-established risk factor for lung cancer [33]. Our analysis also supports this, showing that smoking increased the risk by 32% in female smokers compared to non-smoking females. In this cohort, non-Hispanic Black females had the highest proportion of smokers (34.1%). In comparison, 84% of all participants were non-smokers (Fig. 4). The relatively modest effect size observed in our study may be attributable to the binary classification of smoking status (< 100 cigarettes as never smokers, ≥ 100 cigarettes as ever smokers), which does not capture intensity, duration, or current versus former smoking, potentially diluting the true effect. Existing literature indicates that non-smokers are more likely to be diagnosed at a younger age and tend to have adenocarcinoma or EGFR-mutated subtypes of lung cancer [34]. These findings have important public health implications, highlighting the need for early detection, genetic testing, and targeted therapies, which are associated with a better prognosis compared to non-mutated lung cancer. Furthermore, evidence suggests that female smokers are more susceptible to tobacco-related carcinogens than male smokers [35, 36] and have a higher risk of developing lung cancer compared to male smokers [37–39]. This disparity points to potential biological, hormonal, or environmental factors that may increase female vulnerability. Further investigation is needed to identify these contributing factors, understand subgroup differences, and better explain the rising incidence of lung cancer among females in USA.

We examined various reproductive factors, including estrogen used for contraceptive or hormone replacement therapy, menopause status, history of postmenopausal bleeding, abortion, infertility, and PCOS. We found that their relationships with lung cancer risk exhibited different patterns. Among them, a history of estrogen use was initially associated with a modest increase (18%) in risk. However, after adjustment, this association was no longer statistically significant, suggesting that other reproductive factors may influence the effect. The literature on estrogen use and lung cancer associations is mixed; some studies report decreased risk, while others report increased risk [40–43]. Further research is needed to identify vulnerable subgroups, especially menopausal females using estrogen as a hormone replacement therapy. Another reproductive factor, menopausal status was associated with increased risk for lung cancer in females. Min et al. also found that menopausal status was associated with increased lung cancer risk [44]. Usually, Menopause is related to older age; this cumulative effect can increase risk. To minimize this impact, we matched our cohort by age group. Still, menopause stood as an independent risk factor for females. Surprisingly, we found that females with a history of postmenopausal bleeding (pmbleeding) had a higher risk of lung cancer compared to those without this history. While pmbleeding itself is not directly linked to an increased risk of lung cancer in females, it is associated with a higher risk compared to females experiencing menopause alone (AOR 1.57 vs. 1.47). This finding requires further evaluation. One possible explanation relates to hormone replacement therapy (HRT). A substantial proportion of women in the United States have historically used HRT, which may contribute to postmenopausal bleeding, particularly due to withdrawal effects associated with hormonal fluctuations. Although the usage rate has decreased—from 26.9% in 1999 to 4.7% currently [45]—it still represents a noteworthy portion of women who rely on HRT. Additionally, PMB may represent a presenting symptom of underlying gynecologic malignancies, particularly endometrial carcinoma, which could introduce confounding if diagnoses temporally overlap with lung cancer. Although our analytic approach accounted for documented lung cancer diagnoses where available, residual confounding remains possible, especially in cases of occult or undiagnosed malignancy at baseline. If PMB reflects preclinical gynecologic pathology rather than an independent exposure, the observed association with lung cancer may be overestimated. Therefore, findings should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies with detailed hormonal histories and clearer temporal sequencing are needed.

Furthermore, when we analyzed the interaction between menopause and smoking, it was linked to a significantly increased risk of lung cancer (AOR: 2.65). This suggests that the combined effect of hormonal decline and tobacco exposure may amplify carcinogenic risk. Zhai et al. found that early natural menopause and smoking had a synergistic effect [46], and even after age matching, we confirmed this synergistic relationship. Additionally, we found that Asian postmenopausal females are more vulnerable (AOR: 2.67) than other racial groups, possibly due to genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, or differences in hormonal metabolism. Fig. 5 shows that among non-Hispanic Black postmenopausal females; the highest proportion was smokers (29.8%). This subgroup of women may be at higher risk for developing lung cancer. Further investigation is needed to better understand these racial disparities. In contrast, we did not find any significant association between abortion, infertility, or PCOS and lung cancer risk.

Metabolic syndrome is more prevalent in females than males and is related to an increased in the risk of lung cancer in females [47, 48]. We investigated the association between Hypertension, type 2 diabetes dyslipidemia, hypothyroidism, PCOS, and lung cancer in this cohort. After multivariate logistic regression, we found that both hyperlipidemia and hypothyroidism significantly increased the risk of lung cancer (57% and 63%, respectively). Literature suggests that dyslipidemia is a risk factor for lung cancer, particularly in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [49, 50]. The exact mechanism by which hyperlipidemia increases the risk of lung cancer in females is not well established. However, hyperlipidemia is associated with systemic chronic inflammatory processes and increased oxidative stress [51, 52], both of which may contribute to lung cancer development [53, 54]. Literature suggests that females treated with anti-lipid medications, such as simvastatin and lovastatin, have a reduced risk of lung cancer [55].

These findings support a possible association between hyperlipidemia and lung cancer in females. Similarly, Female participants with hypothyroidism had a higher risk of lung cancer compared to those without hypothyroidism. However, the existing literature presents conflicting evidence, suggesting that hypothyroidism may have a protective role against lung cancer [56]. One potential reason for this discrepancy could be selection bias; our analysis focused solely on female lung cancer patients compared to healthy females, while other studies included both male and female patients. Interestingly, female smokers with hyperlipidemia had a higher risk (71%) of lung cancer compared to females with a smoking history (32%) and hyperlipidemia (57%) separately. In all races, the prevalence of metabolic disorders among non-Hispanic white females was the highest (Fig. 6). Conditions such as hyperlipidemia and hypothyroidism may increase their vulnerability to lung cancer compared to females from other racial groups. Further analysis of these conditions is necessary to reach a definitive conclusion. Regarding other common metabolic disorders, we did not find statistically significant association between hypertension or Type 2 diabetes and lung cancer. However, the literature suggests that both conditions may increase the risk of lung cancer [57, 58]. A small sample size could be one of the reasons for our findings.

Having a positive family history of cancer is a well-established risk factor for various cancers, including lung cancer in females [59, 60]. People with close relatives, such as parents, siblings, or children, who have had cancer may inherit genetic predispositions that increase their susceptibility to carcinogenesis at an early age [61, 62]. Figure 7 shows that 21.8% of participants with a positive family history were aged 60 or younger. After stratifying by race, it was found that Asian females more commonly had a positive family history of lung cancer (9.0%) compared to breast (7.5%) or ovarian cancer (1.5%), while in other races, a family history of breast cancer was more common. In this study, among the lung cancer patient group, 10.7% had a family history of lung cancer, 12.1% had a family history of breast cancer, and 1.9% had a family history of ovarian cancer(Table:1). Interestingly, within this group, smoking and a history of postmenopausal bleeding significantly increased lung cancer risk by 62% and 51%, respectively, compared to non-smokers or those without abnormal bleeding (p = 0.03). This suggests a possible synergistic effect between genetic predisposition and environmental exposure, such as prolonged smoking or hormone replacement therapy. Unexpectedly, females with a family history of breast or ovarian cancer showed protective associations against lung cancer compared to the control group, even after adjustment, which may be due to behavioral confounding, recall bias, or selection bias. However, when behavioral and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking), endocrine disorders (e.g., hypothyroidism), and reproductive events (e.g., postmenopausal bleeding) were included in the model, synergistic effects emerged that increased lung cancer risk among females. Specifically, females with a family history of breast cancer combined with either hypothyroidism or postmenopausal bleeding had a 76% and 82% higher risk of lung cancer, respectively. Further prospective studies are needed to explore these associations in detail.

This study has several key strengths. Leveraging the diverse All of Us Research Program database allowed us to include a broadly representative sample of American female lung cancer patients, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. The case-control design provided a cost-effective and time-efficient approach to simultaneously investigate multiple risk factors, including reproductive history, metabolic disorders, family history, and lifestyle behaviors. Importantly, this study focuses on non-traditional risk factors among females, moving beyond well-established smoking risks to explore gender-specific vulnerabilities. This addresses a critical gap in the literature, as many prior studies primarily compared males and females, potentially underestimating risk associations within specific subgroups of American women. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Some variables, such as race, reproductive history, and medical history, were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias. Additionally, there is temporal ambiguity regarding the measurement of exposure variables relative to lung cancer diagnosis. The All of Us dataset records both historical and current diagnoses, and age reflects the participant’s age at the time of survey completion rather than age at lung cancer diagnosis. Similarly, variables such as menopausal status, BMI, and comorbidities were measured at survey enrollment and may not reflect the participant’s status prior to lung cancer onset. This limitation may affect the interpretation of some associations, particularly for exposures that could change over time. Despite adjusting for known risk factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables—including environmental exposures, occupational hazards, and detailed dietary factors—cannot be excluded. Some subgroup analyses had small sample sizes, reducing statistical power and yielding wide confidence intervals. A potential limitation of this study was surveillance bias. Women presenting with PMB may have more frequent healthcare encounters, increasing the likelihood of diagnosing conditions such as hypothyroidism and hyperlipidaemia. As healthcare utilization intensity could not be quantified in our dataset, residual detection bias cannot be excluded. Future prospective studies with longitudinal data are needed to validate these associations and clarify temporal relationships across diverse female populations.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies key factors associated with lung cancer among American females, encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive history, metabolic conditions, family history, and their interactions beyond smoking exposure. The findings underscore the importance of recognizing female-specific risk patterns when designing targeted prevention and screening strategies. However, further prospective studies are essential to validate these associations and to better understand the underlying mechanisms across diverse female populations.
