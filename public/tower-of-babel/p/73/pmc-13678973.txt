CD73-LNPs promote antitumor T-cell immunity via amplifying TLR3-mediated immunostimulatory dendritic cell activation
Authors: Yuandong Xu, Fei Cao, Haowei Qiu, Yi Zhang, Yiting Wang, Zhen Xu, Yunru He, Ze-Xiu Xiao, Gao-Feng Zha, Jun Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundmRNA vaccines have emerged as highly promising therapeutic agents in cancer immunotherapy. Nevertheless, the current limited immunogenicity of target antigens and the lack of a robust systemic immune response remain significant challenges for their effective application. Background

Messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) vaccines have emerged as a promising platform for cancer immunotherapy, characterized by high potency, safe administration, rapid development, and cost-effective manufacturing [1]. These vaccines function by encoding tumor-specific antigens or costimulatory molecules that are translated into proteins by the patient’s cells—particularly antigen-presenting cells (APCs)—thereby eliciting robust antitumor immune responses [2–4]. Despite significant advances in cancer mRNA vaccine research, important challenges remain, including the poor immunogenicity of antigenic targets and the inability to induce rapid yet adaptable immune responses, which are likely required to suppress rapidly evolving cancers [5]. Identifying highly immunogenic antigens holds substantial potential to accelerate the clinical translation of mRNA vaccines.

CD73 is a membrane-bound ecto-5’-nucleotidase that catalyzes the extracellular conversion of adenosine monophosphate into the immunosuppressive molecule adenosine [6]. Its upregulation is frequently observed in multiple tumor types, including melanoma, ovarian cancer, and prostate cancer, contributing to adenosine accumulation within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and promoting immune evasion [7]. Beyond its enzymatic function, CD73 also plays important roles in cancer cell adhesion and migration [8]. Moreover, CD73 expression has been detected in various immune cell populations—including T cells, macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells—and its involvement in their immunosuppressive activities has been demonstrated [9–11]. Given these immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic effects, targeted inhibition of the CD73 pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Considerable efforts have been devoted to developing CD73-targeted therapeutics, including small-molecule inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and bispecific antibodies [6, 12]. However, none of these agents has yet progressed to clinical application.

In our previous work, we developed lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery that preferentially targets the spleen and draining lymph nodes (DLN) [13]. Building on this platform, the present study evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of CD73 mRNA vaccines (CD73-LNPs) in murine tumor models. CD73-LNPs elicited robust antitumor immune responses in vivo through antigen-presentation pathways, promoting the secretion of effector molecules such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and granzyme B (Gra B) by CD8+ T cells, and effectively inhibiting tumor growth in melanoma, cervical cancer, ovarian cancer, and prostate cancer models. Mechanistically, CD73-LNPs upregulated chemokine receptor expression on CD8+ T cells, enhancing their infiltration into the TME. Additionally, CD73-LNPs activated dendritic cells via stimulation of the toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) signaling pathway, thereby promoting dendritic cell activation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the strong immunogenicity of the CD73 protein and highlight the significant potential of CD73 mRNA vaccines for clinical translation.

Methods

Culture of cell lines

B16-OVA, TC-1, ID8, and RM-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Pricella, USA). All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Routine testing for mycoplasma contamination was performed using the MycAwayTM Plus-Color One-Step Mycoplasma Detection Kit (YEASEN, China).

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were generated by harvesting bone marrow from the femurs and tibias of C57BL/6J mice. After red blood cell lysis using ACK lysis buffer, the remaining cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 20 ng/mL recombinant granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, Peprotech, USA), and 20 ng/mL recombinant interleukin (IL)-4 (Peprotech, USA) to promote dendritic cell differentiation. Half of the culture medium was replaced every two days. Immature BMDCs were harvested on day six for subsequent experiments.

mRNA synthesis

The transcription of CD73 mRNA, E7 mRNA, PSMA mRNA and Luciferase mRNA was conducted in vitro, utilising T7 RNA polymerase (Megascript, Ambion, USA) and linearized plasmid templates. In the transcription reaction, methylated pseudouridine (m1Ψ)-5′-triphosphate (TriLink) was utilised as a substitute for the conventional uridine (UTP), thereby introducing modifications to the mRNA nucleotides. Subsequently, the synthesised mRNA was 5′-capped using 2′-O-methyltransferase (ScriptCap; CellScript, USA) and an m7G capping kit to enhance its stability and translation efficiency. The synthesis of mRNA was followed by a purification process, which involved the use of a cellulose column (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), with the aim of ensuring the removal of any impurities. The synthesis of mRNA was subjected to rigorous quality control measures. This was achieved by means of capillary electrophoresis, utilising the Agilent Technologies 5200 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). The integrity of the mRNA was assessed using the DNF-472-1000 kit and gel retardation method. The synthetic mRNA samples were stored at -80 °C until further use.

mRNA-LNPs preparation and characterization

The synthesis of the ionizable lipid (E12A1A3) and the detailed procedure for mRNA-LNPs preparation were described previously [13]. Lipids were dissolved in ethanol at a molar ratio of E12A1A3/DSPC (Avanti, China)/cholesterol (Avanti, China)/DMG-PEG 2000 (Avanti, China) of 50:10:38.5:1.5 to form the organic phase. The mRNA was dissolved in sodium citrate buffer to form the aqueous phase. The two phases were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:3, corresponding to a lipid-to-mRNA N/P ratio of 5:1, using a syringe pump-driven microfluidic chip device (INanoE, MicroNano, China) to facilitate nanoparticle assembly. The resulting formulation was centrifuged at 1200 × g for 10 min using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration membrane, washed thoroughly, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The preparations were stored at 4 °C. The particle size distribution was measured by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). The mRNA concentration and encapsulation efficiency were determined with the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA).

In vitro and in vivo cytotoxicity assessment of mRNA-LNPs

The BMDCs were exposed to different concentrations of mRNA-LNPs or PBS for 24 h. Cell viability was then measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (Beyotime, China). Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 to 1 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. After 24 h, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of mRNA-LNPs or PBS as a control. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and relative cell viability was calculated as a percentage of the control group using the following formula: Relative viability (%) = [(OD_treated − OD_blank)/(OD_control − OD_blank)] × 100.

For in vivo safety evaluation, serum samples were collected from tumor-free mice after CD73-LNPs administration. Biochemical parameters—including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate ALT (AST), creatinine (CREA), and CREA kinase (CK)—were measured to comprehensively assess hepatic and renal function.

Real-time PCR

Total cellular RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) was synthesized using the Hifair II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed with the Hieff UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Yeasen), and amplification signals were detected using a Bio-Rad real-time PCR system. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method and normalized to 𝛽-actin expression. Primer sequences used in this study were reported in previously and are listed in Table 1 [13].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)

Relative ELISA kits were procured from eBioscience to detect cytokines (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, and IL-12P70) released from BMDC culture supernatant.

Enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay for CD73-LNPs

IFN-γ production by splenocytes isolated from immunized mice was evaluated using an ELISpot assay, performed as previously described [13]. BMDCs derived from C57BL/6J mice were treated with CD73-LNPs for 24 h. T cells were then isolated from mouse spleens and co-cultured with mRNA-treated BMDCs at a 10:1 ratio (T cells: BMDCs) for 24–48 h. Following incubation, IFN-γ secretion by activated T cells was quantified using an IFN-γ ELISpot assay (Dakewe, China). Plates were pre-coated with an anti-IFN-γ capture antibody, and spot formation was visualized using a biotinylated detection antibody and a streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase conjugate, reflecting the immunogenic potential of CD73-LNPs.

Animal experiment

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sun Yat-Sen University (approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2025-002827). All experiments were conducted under specific pathogen-free conditions in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Gemmatech, and Tlr3–/– mice were obtained from Cyagen. Subcutaneous tumor models were established using B16-OVA, TC-1, ID8, and RM-1-PSMA cells at inoculation doses of 5 × 105, 5 × 105, 1 × 106, and 2.5 × 105 cells, respectively. Each treatment group comprised at least five mice. Immunization with mRNA-LNPs (0.5 mg/kg, intramuscular injection) was initiated when tumor volumes reached ≤ 100 mm3. Tumor size was measured every 2–3 days using calipers until the tumors reached the predefined endpoint of 1500 mm3. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Tumor volume = length × width2. Mice were euthanized if any of the following humane endpoints were reached: ≥ 20% body weight loss, inability to consume food, or tumor size ≥ 1.5 cm3.

Flow cytometry analysis

BMDCs, spleens, and lymph nodes were collected from tumor-bearing mice and analyzed by flow cytometry to evaluate immune cell populations and their functional status following different treatments, as previously described [14]. Blood samples were subjected to red blood cell lysis and stained using the same protocol as spleen specimens. Intracellular staining of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and Gra B in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells required fixation and membrane permeabilization before antibody labeling.

After staining, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the frequency and activation status of distinct immune cell populations. Data acquisition was performed using a Beckman Coulter flow cytometer, and analyses were conducted with FlowJo or CytExpert software. Surface marker expression levels were quantified to evaluate the contribution of different immune cell subsets to the immune response.

Quantification of adenosine by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry

Tissue samples were accurately weighed (0.1–0.5 g) and transferred into a 5-mL grinding tube, followed by adding 0.5/1 mL extraction solvent and homogenization. Finally, a 100 µL aliquot of tissue homogenate or serum was then mixed with 100 µL of a 2.5% perchloric acid and subjected to ultrasonication to precipitate proteins. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. Subsequently, 150 µL of the supernatant was transferred, neutralized with 15 µL of 1 mol/L NaOH, thoroughly mixed, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The sample must be transferred to a brown sample vial for subsequent instrumental analysis (Agilent HPLC 1200).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and data analysis for mouse tumors

For each scRNA-seq sample, subcutaneous tumors from three mice within the same group were pooled. Tumors were obtained by subcutaneously injecting 2.5 × 105 RM1-PSMA cells into 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. After CD73-LNPs treatments, tumors from the PBS and CD73-LNPs groups were individually processed and combined into a single-cell suspension. CD45+ immune cells were isolated by staining the suspension with anti-mouse CD45 for 30 min and sorted using a BD FACSAriaIII for downstream scRNA-seq analysis.

A total of 20 clusters were generated at a resolution of 0.6, and further identified into “CD4 T,” “CD8 T,” “NK,” “Macrophage,” “Monocyte,” “B cell,” “pDC,” “cDC1,” “cDC2,” “Neutrophil” and “unidentified” (not indicated) using the expression of published markers. A detailed analysis of the steps involved is provided in the literature [15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0, CA, USA). In comparing two or more groups, statistical analyses were conducted using two-sided Student’s t-tests with Welch’s correction or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc correction. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were performed with p-values calculated using the log-rank test (Mantel-Cox). Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant).

Results

Safety profile and in vivo immunogenicity of CD73 protein induced by CD73-LNPs

The preparation of CD73 mRNA vaccines (CD73-LNPs) used LNPs previously developed in our research, as depicted schematically in Fig. 1A. The in vitro transcription of CD73 mRNA was evaluated using capillary electrophoresis, confirming that the mRNA met quality control standards, with no signs of degradation or residual impurities (Figs. 1B and S1). We conducted a comprehensive characterization of the physicochemical properties of the CD73-LNPs. The average diameter of the CD73-LNPs complexes was determined to be 81.23 ± 0.58 nm, with a polydispersity index of 0.095 ± 0.017 (Fig. 1C; Table 2). Subsequently, we evaluated the translational efficiency of CD73-LNPs. Both Western blot and flow cytometry analyses indicated that the transfection of BMDCs with CD73-LNPs resulted in effective translation into CD73 protein (Figs. 1D–F and S2A). The cytotoxicity of CD73-LNPs in vitro was assessed using the CCK8 assay, which revealed that administration at a concentration of 10 µg/mL did not significantly affect the viability of BMDCs (Fig. 1G). Safety evaluations were conducted following CD73-LNPs administration to assess the mice’s tolerance to the therapy. Blood biochemical analyses revealed that key indicators of liver and kidney function, including alanine ALT, AST, CREA, and CK, remained within normal physiological ranges, suggesting the absence of hepatic or renal dysfunction (Fig. 1H). Histological examination (H&E) indicated that there is no evidence of tissue damage to the heart, liver, spleen, lung or kidneys (Fig. S2B). Having established that CD73-LNPs possess favorable characterization and safety profiles, the subsequent phase of the research assessed the immunogenicity of the CD73 protein. In healthy C57BL/6 mice, following four intramuscular injections of CD73-LNPs (5 µg per mouse), T cells from the spleen were co-cultured with BMDCs pretreated with CD73-LNPs. IFN-γ production was quantified using an ELISpot assay (Fig. 1I). The results demonstrated that BMDCs pretreated with CD73-LNPs exhibited a significantly enhanced production of IFN-γ compared to control BMDCs. This finding indicates that CD73-LNPs effectively activated specific immune responses, thereby underscoring their potential as tumor-associated antigen mRNA vaccines (Figs. 1J-K).

CD73-LNPs induced the maturation and activation of BMDCs

The efficient presentation of antigens by APCs and the subsequent activation of adaptive immune cells are crucial for the efficacy of mRNA vaccines (Fig. 2A) [16]. This study undertook a comprehensive investigation into the phenotypic modulation of BMDCs by CD73-LNPs. Phenotypic alterations in BMDCs were assessed 24 h post-transfection with CD73-LNPs. Given that innate immune signaling usually facilitates the expression of inflammatory cytokine genes to activate dendritic cells, we first evaluated cytokine gene expression in BMDCs by qPCR. The findings indicated that CD73-LNPs treatment significantly induced the expression of proinflammatory cytokine genes Il-12p40, Ifn-α1, Ifn-α4, and Ifn-β1 in BMDCs, with a milder induction observed for Il-1β, Il-6, and Il-12α (Fig. 2B). ELISA results corroborated the upregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12p70, and IFN-β protein levels in the culture supernatant from cells treated with CD73-LNPs (Fig. 2C). Consequently, it was indicated that CD73-LNPs induced proinflammatory cytokine expression in BMDCs. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that CD73-LNPs significantly upregulated the surface expression of costimulatory molecules, including CD80, CD86, MHC II, CD40, CD103, OX40L, and 4-1BBL on BMDCs, indicating their maturation and activation (Figs. 2D–H and S3). Additionally, CCR7 expression was upregulated (Fig. 2I), which is essential for the directional homing of dendritic cells to lymphoid tissues [17].

These findings suggested that CD73-LNPs can effectively enhance the expression of cytokines and costimulatory molecules in BMDCs, highlighting their potential to activate and mature dendritic cells for enhanced immunotherapeutic efficacy. Building upon these results, future studies will investigate the functional implications of these phenotypic changes in eliciting robust anti-tumor immune responses.

CD73-LNPs activated potent humoral and cellular immunity in vivo

mRNA vaccines are recognized for their ability to induce robust immune responses by activating both humoral and cellular immunity [2]. The roles of CD73-LNPs in the in vivo immune response were investigated based on the phenotypic modulation of BMDCs by CD73-LNPs. In tumor-free C57BL/6 mice, after two rounds of immunization, serum samples and spleen-derived T cells were collected for functional assessment (Fig. 3A). CD73 serves as the rate-limiting enzyme in adenosine metabolism; however, our findings indicated that CD73-LNPs did not significantly change serum adenosine levels (Fig. 3B). Notably, a decrease in serum CD73 protein concentrations was observed, accompanied by an increase in CD73-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentrations (Fig. 3B), suggesting that CD73-LNPs effectively elicited a humoral immune response.

As the largest secondary lymphoid organ, the spleen serves as a critical site for the initiation of immune responses by mRNA vaccines [18, 19]. Our observations indicate that CD73-LNPs significantly increased the proportion of CD3+ T cells in the spleen, particularly CD8+ T cells. In contrast, the proportion of CD11c+ dendritic cells decreased; however, their surface expression of CD80 and CD86 increased. Additionally, the proportion of F4/80+/CD206+ macrophages was reduced, suggesting a transformation of these macrophages. Non-significant changes were observed in the proportions of NK cells and B cells (Figs. 3C-D and S4-5). Given that CD73-LNPs were associated with increased serum concentrations of specific IgG, we further evaluated the proportion of activated plasma cells in the spleen. The results indicated a higher proportion of B220/CD138-positive plasma cells, indicating that CD73-LNPs facilitate B cell activation (Figs. 3E and S6). Furthermore, we observed that CD73-LNPs promote the activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in vitro (Fig. S7) and enhance the secretion of effector molecules, including IFN-γ, Gra B, and TNF-α, by CD8+ T cells in vivo (Figs. 3F–H and S8). Moreover, the transcription factor Eomes, known for its role in activating CD8+ T cells [20], was observed to have increased expression (Figs. 3I and S9). These results demonstrate that CD73-LNPs can effectively enhance both humoral and cellular immune responses in vivo without affecting serum adenosine concentrations.

CD73-LNPs improved immune cell infiltration in the TME and enhanced antitumor efficacy

It has been demonstrated that CD73-LNPs have a favorable safety profile and can effectively activate both humoral and cellular immune responses in vivo. We subsequently examined the antitumor efficacy of CD73-LNPs in subcutaneous tumor models, including B16-OVA (melanoma), TC-1 (cervical cancer), ID8 (ovarian cancer), and RM1-PSMA (prostate cancer). E7 is a specific mRNA vaccine therapeutic target for the TC-1 models [21], and PSMA is considered an effective target for prostate cancer treatment and has been validated in CAR-T and mRNA vaccines [22]. In this study, following administration of the four CD73-LNPs, a significant advantage in both tumor volume reduction and overall survival was observed in the aforementioned models (Figs. 4A–I and S10). Tumor volumes consistently remained below 500 mm³ after CD73-LNPs treatments, demonstrating the superior antitumor ability of CD73-LNPs. The complete response rates for the B16-OVA, TC-1, and ID8 models were 40% (2/5), 60% (3/5), and 100% (5/5), respectively. In the RM1-PSMA model, CD73-LNPs demonstrated significantly superior efficacy compared to PSMA-LNPs. In the TC-1 model, CD73-LNPs were as efficacious as the mRNA vaccine targeting the specific antigen E7.

Prostate cancer is characterized as an immunologically cold tumor, marked by a TME deficient in effective immune cell infiltration [23]. Surprisingly, CD73-LNPs have been observed to improve immune cell infiltration in prostate cancer TME, including CD45+ immune cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells (Figs. 4J and S11). Additionally, CD73-LNP has been indicated to facilitate the secretion of IFN-γ and Gra B by CD8+ T cells within the TME in a prostate cancer model (Figs. 4K-L and S12). Since CD73-LNPs were observed to achieve 100% complete remission in the ID8 model, we investigated the proportion of memory T cells present in the blood of ID8 model mice. The results indicated that CD73-LNPs significantly increased the proportion of CD44/CD62L-positive CD8+ T cells (Fig. S13).

These results indicate that CD73-LNPs enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration into the TME, effectively suppress tumor growth, and demonstrate promising antitumor efficacy across multiple tumor models.

CD73-LNPs activated CXCL/CXCR signaling to promote CD8+ T cell infiltration

Activation and recruitment of CD8+ T cells to the TME are essential for effective antitumor responses. In our study using the RM1-PSMA model, we demonstrated that CD73-LNPs enhanced immune cell infiltration into the TME and stimulated the secretion of effector molecules by CD8+ T cells. Nonetheless, the precise mechanism by which CD73-LNPs facilitated the recruitment of CD8+ T cells to the TME remained to be elucidated. To gain a deeper understanding of the impact of CD73-LNPs on T cells within the tumor immune microenvironment, we conducted scRNA-seq on CD45+ immune cells isolated from RM1-PSMA xenografts harvested from C57BL/6J mice treated with either PBS or CD73-LNPs. Following quality control and filtering, we successfully captured 12,327 cells, obtained their single-cell transcriptomes, and identified 21 transcriptionally homogeneous clusters using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) method (Fig. S14A). These clusters were annotated into 11 distinct cell types based on their transcriptional profiles and the SingleR package [15], including CD3+ T cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, macrophages, monocytes, B cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), conventional dendritic cell 1 (cDC1), cDC2, and neutrophils. Notably, the proportion of CD8+ T cells indicated the greatest increase. (Figs. 5A-B).

In our study, we employed t-SNE analysis to categorize CD8+ T cells into distinct subsets, including CD8+ effector T cells, exhausted CD8+ T cells, naïve CD8+ T cells, early-activated T cells, and tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells (Figs. S14B-C). Compared to mice treated with PBS, those treated with CD73-LNPs exhibited an increased population of effector CD8+ T cells and a decreased population of exhausted CD8+ T cells (Fig. S14A). Additionally, volcano plots of differentially expressed genes in CD8+ T cells between the two groups corroborated the upregulation of genes associated with CD8+ effector T cells, including Gzma, Gzmb, and Gzmk (Fig. S14D).

An analysis of intercellular communication networks revealed that CXCL-related signaling was significantly upregulated in mice treated with CD73-LNPs (Figs. 5C and S14E). The kinetics of chemokine expression in the TME, along with alterations in chemokine receptor expression on immune cells, are likely determinants of the immune cell composition within the TME [24]. Subsequently, we conducted Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses on the upregulated genes in intratumoral CD8+ T cells. The results indicated enrichment in pathways related to lymphocyte activation and migration, chemokine signaling, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Figs. 5D-E). Chemokines facilitate directed cell migration by binding to chemokine receptors (CXCR). Consequently, we employed flow cytometry to assess the expression of CXCR receptors, which facilitate the migration of CD8+ T cells in the blood, DLN, and spleen in RM1 model. The findings demonstrated that post-CD73-LNPs treatment, CXCR3 expression was upregulated on CD8+ T cells from the blood, DLN, and spleen; CXCR1 expression was upregulated on CD8+ T cells from the blood and spleen; and CXCR6 and CCR5 expressions were upregulated exclusively in blood CD8+ T cells (Figs. 5F–I and S15-16). In the ID8 model, it was also observed that CD73-LNPs upregulate the expression of CXCR3 on CD8+ T cells in the spleen and DLN (Fig. S17).

The findings presented herein substantiate that CD73-LNPs augment the migratory capacity of CD8+ T cells through the activation of the CXCL/CXCR signaling pathway. Furthermore, CD73-LNPs have been indicated to reduce the infiltration of exhausted T cells into the TME, underscoring their considerable therapeutic potential.

CD73-LNPs activated BMDCs through TLR3 signaling

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) initiate both shared and distinct signaling pathways that modulate immune responses [25]. Notably, among all TLRs, only the endosomal TLR3 does not trigger systemic inflammation and facilitates cross-priming of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells by dendritic cells [26, 27]. TLR3 activation triggers downstream signaling cascades involving NF-kB, mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and IRF3, leading to the expression of inflammatory mediators and cytokines (Fig. 6A).

We collected BMDCs treated with CD73-LNPs for 24 h and performed transcriptome analysis via RNA sequencing. Subsequent transcriptome analysis was conducted using RNA sequencing. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated robust replication within clusters and revealed significant differences between the CD73-LNPs treatment group and PBS control group (Fig. 6B). The volcano plot indicates that the expression of the genes associated with BMDC activation is significantly increased, including Ms4a6b, Slfn4, and Fcgr1 (Fig. 6C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of the Toll-like receptor signalling pathway, specifically TLR3 and TLR4 (Fig. 6D, and S18). qPCR experiments further confirmed that among the Toll-like receptors in mice, Tlr3 exhibited the most pronounced upregulation following treatment with CD73-LNPs (Fig. 6E). To ascertain whether the activation of BMDCs by CD73-LNPs was contingent upon the TLR3 signaling pathway, BMDCs were pretreated with small-molecule inhibitors targeting TLR3. Subsequently, the expression of cytokine genes and costimulatory molecules in BMDCs treated with CD73-LNPs was analyzed. The findings demonstrated that CU CPT4a, a TLR3-specific inhibitor, effectively inhibited the CD73-LNPs-induced production of cytokines (Il-1β, Il-6, Ifn-α4, and Ifn-β1) and costimulatory molecules (CD86, MHC II) in BMDCs (Figs. 6F–H and S19).

Subsequently, we collected BMDCs from Tlr3–/– mice and observed that treatment with CD73-LNPs significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of NF-κB, MAPKs, and IRF3 in wild-type (WT) BMDCs. However, this phosphorylation was substantially diminished in Tlr3–/– BMDCs (Figs. 6I-J). Additionally, we assessed the therapeutic efficacy of CD73-LNPs in subcutaneous tumor models of TC-1 and RM1-PSMA using Tlr3–/– mice. The tumor inhibitory effect of CD73-LNPs was nearly completely nullified in Tlr3–/– mice (Figs. 6K–N and S20). Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis of immune cell populations and proportions in the blood of WT and TLR3 knockout mice using flow cytometry, which revealed non-significant differences between the two groups (Fig. S21). These findings underscore the critical role of TLR3 in mediating the antitumor effects induced by CD73-LNPs. Collectively, the data strongly indicate that the antitumor efficacy of CD73-LNPs requires both effective antigen expression and activation of TLR3 signaling pathways.

Discussion

In this study, the potential of CD73 as a target for mRNA vaccines was investigated, with findings indicating that CD73 mRNA vaccines effectively inhibit tumor growth in murine models of melanoma, cervical cancer, ovarian cancer, and prostate cancer. Mechanistically, the function of CD73-LNPs was indicated to depend on TLR3 signaling in dendritic cells, leading to the upregulation of chemokine receptor expression on CD8+ T cells. Furthermore, a comparative analysis was performed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CD73-LNPs combined with a CD73 monoclonal antibody (anti-CD73) and the CD73 inhibitor APCP in a prostate cancer model. The results revealed that APCP exhibited the greatest ability to inhibit adenosine metabolism in both serum and the TME. However, CD73-LNPs were found to exert the most potent tumor-suppressive effect (Figs. S22A–G). These findings underscore the feasibility of using CD73 mRNA vaccines to treat the aforementioned cancers, highlighting a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

The efficacy of mRNA vaccine approaches demonstrates notable variability, as most exhibit limitations related to the immunogenicity of the antigenic targets, the lack of systemic immune responses, and an inability to induce the rapid, adaptable immune responses that are likely essential for the effective suppression of rapidly evolving cancers [2]. Tumor-associated antigens have been demonstrated to exhibit innate immune tolerance, a factor that contributes to their reduced immunogenicity [28, 29]. However, CD73, as a tumor-associated antigen, has been indicated to elicit potent antitumor immune responses. In the TC-1 model, the efficacy of CD73-LNPs was comparable to that of the TC-1-specific target mRNA vaccine E7-LNPs, with a CR of 60% (3/5). In the RM1-PSMA model, CD73-LNPs demonstrated significantly superior efficacy to PSMA-LNPs, despite PSMA being considered a prostate cancer-specific target. The results of scRNA-seq indicated a significant increase in the proportion of effector T cells in the TME, accompanied by a notable reduction in the proportion of exhausted T cells. The therapeutic efficacy of CD73-LNPs is likely due to dual mechanisms: activating antitumor immunity and mitigating the immunosuppressive state within the TME.

Following administration of CD73-LNPs, a notable enhancement in chemokine signaling and the infiltration of effector T cells within the TME was observed. Enrichment analysis further identified a significant enrichment of pathways associated with the activation and migration of CD8+ T cells. Chemokines have been documented to facilitate the recruitment of circulating CD8+ T cells into the TME through interactions with their specific receptors [30]. The following CXCRs and their corresponding ligands are recognized as key mediators in the migration and activation of effector T cells: CXCR3 (CXCL9, CXCL10), CXCR1 (CXCL1), CXCR6 (CXCL16), and CCR5 (CCL5) [31]. The results of this study demonstrated upregulation of all aforementioned receptors on the surface of CD8+ T cells in the bloodstream, with concurrent upregulation of CXCR3 expression on CD8+ T cells from the spleen and DLN. Although the CXCR3/CXCL9-CXCL10 axis is also involved in the migration of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [32], in this study, it predominantly facilitated the migration of effector T cells.

Moreover, this study demonstrated that CD73-LNPs activate TLR3 signaling in dendritic cells, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity. Unlike most vaccines, which require adjuvants to boost immunogenicity, mRNA inherently possesses immunogenic properties and acts as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern. It exhibits affinity for various TLRs, such as TLR3, TLR7, and TLR8, as well as intracellular sensors [3, 33]. These characteristics facilitate the swift initiation of innate immune responses, in conjunction with the activation of adaptive immunity, as the mRNA payload is translated into full-length proteins and subsequently processed into antigens [3]. Conversely, LNPs delivery systems can activate innate immune-related signaling pathways. For instance, the cationic lipids developed by Xia et al. have been demonstrated to activate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling in dendritic cells, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity [34]. Similarly, the lipids synthesized by Anderson et al. have been reported to activate the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway, thereby augmenting immune responses in vivo [35]. The mRNA delivery system used in this study consisted of LNPs previously engineered to induce cytokine secretion in BMDCs, although the precise mechanism remains to be elucidated. In this investigation, Luc-LNPs were observed to induce a moderate upregulation of Tlr3 expression in BMDCs, whereas CD73-LNPs demonstrated a more substantial upregulation of TLR3. This suggests that the activation of TLR3 by CD73-LNPs likely represents a synergistic interaction between the delivery system and CD73 mRNA.

This study has several limitations. First, although our results demonstrate that the antitumor efficacy of CD73-LNPs is largely dependent on TLR3 signaling, the precise mechanisms responsible for the loss of therapeutic efficacy in Tlr3-deficient mice remain to be further elucidated. Second, the immunostimulatory activity and translational relevance of CD73-LNPs have not yet been validated in human primary immune cells or clinical samples. Future studies will involve synthesizing human CD73 mRNA and optimizing LNP formulations for efficient delivery into human immune cells, particularly PBMC-derived dendritic cells, to further evaluate the clinical translational potential of CD73-LNPs.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the CD73 protein is highly immunogenic, and the CD73 mRNA vaccine has been indicated to safely and effectively induce antitumor immunity, suggesting significant potential for clinical translation.
