Exposure to air pollutants is associated with adverse changes in hemoglobin trajectories and an increased risk of anemia in children and adolescents
Authors: Keyang Zheng, Fengyun Zhang, Liting Chu, Yanting Yang, Lijing Sun, Yuting Huang, Shuangxiao Qu, Shenglei Huang, Wenjuan Qi, Dongling Yang, Chunyan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAir pollution is associated with anemia in children, however, longitudinal data supporting this association are insufficient.MethodsFrom 2019 to 2023, we conducted a dynamic cohort study involving 33,900 participants from 119 primary and secondary schools across 16 districts in Shanghai. We obtained the daily average concentrations of five air pollutants (PM2.5, O₃, NO₂, SO₂, and CO) based on school addresses using the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset to assess the exposure levels of the study subjects. We used latent class mixed models to determine the longitudinal trajectories of hemoglobin (Hb) and multinomial logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) between trajectory categories. Linear and logistic regression models were used to evaluate the associations between air pollutant exposure level and Hb and anemia risk, respectively, and Cox regression models was used for longitudinal validation. Interaction terms for gender, age, and body mass index (BMI) were used to explore the modifying effects of these factors.ResultsAt the baseline survey, 3,213 students (9.48%) were identified as anemic, with a higher proportion of girls (63.8%). During an average follow-up period of 3.16 years, 1,685 participants (4.9%) were observed to develop anemia. Children’s hemoglobin levels showed three different trajectory patterns throughout the study period. Exposure to PM2.5 [OR (95% CI) = 2.124(1.853, 2.433)] was associated with adverse trajectory changes characterized by “low hemoglobin”. Longitudinal analysis shows that exposure to PM2.5 [HR = 1.213, 95% CI: (1.068, 1.376)], NO2 [HR = 1.409, 95% CI: (1.254, 1.584)], and SO2 [HR = 1.268, 95% CI: (1.172, 1.372)] is associated with an increased risk of anemia. Stratified analysis showed that the adverse effects of PM2.5 exposure on anemia were more pronounced in younger or underweight children. The adverse effects of NO2 exposure on anemia were more significant in older or obese children. The association between SO2 exposure and the impact on anemia mainly appeared in male or younger children.ConclusionsLong-term exposure to air pollutants is associated with an increased burden of anemia in children and adolescents, and attention should be focused on vulnerable groups such as girls and underweight children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27895-z.Keywords: Air pollution, Anemia, Hemoglobin, Children, Adolescent

Background

Childhood anemia is a global public health problem that causes health and economic losses [35]. Anemia can impede children’s cognitive and physical development, diminish learning capacity and efficiency, and severe cases of anemia lead to an increased risk of death [6]. Presently, anemia prevention initiatives predominantly target menstruating women, children, and adolescents [40]. However, advancements in mitigating anemia rates have reached an impasse. At the same time, the role of exposure to environmental pollutants in the occurrence of anemia in children is becoming increasingly prominent [2, 43].

Current research shows an association between long-term exposure to air pollutants and an increased risk of anemia in children [18]. Initial studies have identified a notable association between the use of household biofuels in India and an increased incidence of anemia in young children, alongside diminished hemoglobin (Hb) levels in children aged 8 to 13 years [28, 32]. Furthermore, two cross-sectional studies conducted across multiple countries have similarly reported a positive correlation between the utilization of solid fuels and an increased risk of anemia in children under the age of five [1, 3]. Incomplete combustion of solid fuels can release a variety of harmful gases such as particulate matter (PM), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and carbon monoxide (CO) [45]. Subsequent investigations have concentrated on the impact of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) on the development of anemia in children, yielding consistent findings that link PM2.5 exposure to reduced Hb levels and an increased risk of anemia [7, 21, 26, 29, 31]. Chaudhary et al., pointed out that the influence of specific PM2.5 components on anemia in children surpasses that of the mass of PM2.5 [9]. Additionally, prenatal PM2.5 and CO exposure are associated with an increased risk of anemia in children from birth to 5 years of age [4, 16]. A study conducted in Iran showed that exposure levels of various air pollutants (PM10, ozone (O3), SO2, and CO) were negatively correlated with Hb levels in adolescents [33]. However, the majority of existing studies have employed cross-sectional designs, with only one retrospective longitudinal study investigating the relationship between air pollution and childhood anemia through an ecological lens [12].

Anemia in children exhibits a dynamic nature throughout the stages of growth and development, as the ideal hemoglobin concentration required to satisfy the physiological demands of the child is affected by variables such as gender, age, and other factors [41]. Studies have found that the rate of anemia in girls is usually higher than that of boys, especially after entering puberty, due to the loss of iron due to blood loss during menstruation, which increases the risk of iron deficiency anemia [13]. The incidence and degree of anemia generally decrease with age. Preschoolers are more likely to develop nutritional imbalances due to their rapid growth rate and higher nutritional needs than school-age children [27]. Studies have pointed out that compared with normal weight children, not only underweight children, but also overweight and obese children also have higher rates of anemia [20, 42]. There is a need for longitudinal studies to further elucidate the association between air pollution and anemia in children. Based on the above background, this study examined the trajectories of Hb in children and adolescents in a dynamic cohort and explored the association between air pollutant exposure and Hb levels and the risk of anemia. Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses to explore potential influencing factors, including gender, age, and BMI, in order to evaluate the moderating effects of these variables on the interplay between air pollution and anemia in children.

Methods

Study population

Based on the survey of common diseases among students in Shanghai, this study established a dynamic cohort in the city to investigate the relationship between environmental factors and common diseases among students. From 2019 to 2023, a total of 38,336 students aged 6 to 18 from 119 primary and secondary schools in 16 districts of the city were surveyed. The baseline survey included a physical examination to assess the participant’s anemia status, and a questionnaire to collect basic information and related behavioral factors. Subsequently, surveys were conducted annually to include a baseline survey of new participants and follow-up visits for included participants. The surveys are conducted every year from September to October. 4,436 participants were deleted due to missing data, and ultimately 33,900 participants were included in the study, of which 19,599 participants had at least one follow-up visit (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No.2022-13) with written consent from all guardians.

Flowchart for the inclusion of students from Shanghai 16 districts in this study from 2019–2023

Anemia measurement and assessment

Hb levels in fresh fingerstick blood were measured by a professional examiner using a Hb meter (HemoCue, HB201+, Sweden). Before blood collection, the finger ends of the middle or ring finger are sterilized and dried, the first 1 drop of blood is squeezed out and discarded after being punctured with a disposable lancet, and then 10–20 µl of blood is collected using a microcuvette. The blood collected from the cuvette is confirmed to be free of air bubbles and immediately placed in the analyzer for measurement, and the displayed results are recorded. The principle of the instrumental detection is the azide methemoglobin method, and the accuracy and stability of the results have been verified by the World Health Organization (WHO) [41]. For quality control, first, the instrument needs to be calibrated before measurement; Second, the sample holder of the instrument is cleaned at the end of each measurement to prevent contamination; Third, a parallel sample test was performed after every 20 samples tested to ensure stable results; Finally, the abnormal Hb measurement is retested to ensure that the measurement value is reliable.

Anemia is defined in terms of the Hb cut-off value recommended by the WHO. Specifically, Hb levels below 115 g/L in children aged 5 to 11 years, Hb levels below 120 g/L in boys and non-pregnant girls aged 12 to 14 years, Hb levels below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women over 15 years of age, and Hb levels below 130 g/L in men over 15 years of age were defined as anemia.

Air pollution exposure assessment

We collected data on five air pollutants (including PM2.5, O3, NO2, SO2, and CO) at a grid resolution of 1 km ×1 km using China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) (http://geodata.nnu.edu.cn). In short, the CHAP dataset is based on the spatiotemporal-extreme random tree model and artificial intelligence technology, and combines the high-precision ground air pollutant data produced by big data such as ground-based observation, satellite remote sensing products, atmospheric reanalysis and model simulation data. The methodology and validation of the model have been well described [37–39]. We matched the average daily air pollutant concentrations according to the school addresses and used the average air pollutant concentrations from the baseline survey to the end of follow-up as the individual long-term exposure levels. The average air pollutant concentration for each interval is calculated based on each student’s physical examination date as the exposure level for that period. In the longitudinal analysis, we also calculated the average level of air pollution exposure during the exposure window from the baseline survey to each follow-up.

Covariates

We used structured questionnaires to collect basic and related behavioral information of the study subjects, including child age (years), gender (male/female), ethnicity (Han/others), body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2), parental education (elementary school and below/junior high school/high school/junior college/undergraduate/master’s degree and above), number of family members, and family economic self-assessment (very poor/poor/fair a/good/very good). The gross domestic product (GDP, billion) of the area where the school is located is used to measure the economic level of the region (based on the data reported in 2020). The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) was used to quantify the degree of school greenery, with a spatial resolution of 250 m ⋅ 250 m (based on 2020 data). Behavior-related factors include 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous exercise per day (days/week), smoking behavior (yes/no), residential situation (school/home), recess areas (classroom/outdoors), daily outdoor activity time (< 1 h/1–2 h/2–3 h/≥3 h), and breakfast eating (every day/sometimes/never).

Directed acyclic diagrams (DAGs) (Figure S1) drawn from previous studies were used to identify various potential confounders. Finally, children’s age, gender, ethnicity, BMI, parental education, family economic status, regional economic level, physical activity, smoking behavior, and residential situation were selected as variables adjusted by the basic model.

Statistical analysis

For children who were followed up at least once, a latent class mixed model (LCMM) was used to identify the longitudinal trajectories of Hb levels, a process that has been described in previous studies [44]. In simple terms, we tested two to five categories of candidate models, each repeated 100 times to prevent local maxima. The number of trajectory categories of the model is selected according to the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The optimal number of trajectory categories should require an average posterior probability (APP) greater than 0.7, and the minimum proportion of each category should not be less than 5% of the total sample size.

Multinomial logistic regression models further examined the association between several air pollutant exposures and Hb trajectories during the study period. The cross-sectional relationship between air pollutant exposure and continuous Hb level and the occurrence of binary anemia was quantified by multiple linear regression model with robust standard error and binomial logistic regression model, respectively, expressed as regression coefficients (β) and odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The Cox proportional hazards model was used to perform a survival analysis of the association between air pollution exposure and anemia outcomes. We tracked anemia as an outcome, redefining the occurrence of anemia based on Hb data from children at each follow-up, taking into account age changes. Follow-up and baseline surveys are usually conducted from September to October each year, and the survival time of the study subjects is assessed using the interval from the baseline survey date to the date of event occurrence, measured in days. The level of air pollutant exposure during each interval period is calculated from the daily average concentration. Participants who were not followed up or were defined as anemic in the baseline survey were not included in the analysis. If a participant experiences an outcome event during the follow-up, their survival time and exposure level to air pollutants are fully recorded. If the participant does not experience the outcome event, or experiences transfer, loss to follow-up, or refusal to participate in follow-up before the outcome event occurs, their outcome will be defined as censored. For subjects who were censored or did not experience the outcome event before the end of the study, they are only considered as contributing to the time interval, recording the interval from baseline to the last survey, and using the average air pollution concentration during this period as their exposure level. The results are presented as risk ratios (HR) and 95% CI estimates for anemia in children after adjusting for covariates. All results were interpreted as estimates of the effect of increasing per quartile range (IQR) of air pollutant exposure levels.

We performed several sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of the results: (1) Model 1 was adjusted for the variables screened by the DAG, which included the multiple contaminants investigated, which was considered the base model. (2) The greenery around the school and students’ outdoor activities may affect the exposure level of air pollutants, and Model 2 adjusted NDVI, students’ recess areas, and daily outdoor activity time based on Model 1. (3) Model 3 further considered the factors associated with childhood anemia, including the student’s breakfast-eating behavior and the number of people living in the family, and the model was considered a fully adjusted model. (4) Considering that there may be multicollinearity between air pollutants, Model 4 independently examines the relationship between each air pollutant and Hb level and anemia risk on the basis of fully adjusting the model, which is a single pollutant model.

Gender, age, and nutritional status of children (as assessed by BMI) are considered to be potential contributing factors to anemia. We assessed the modifying effects of these potential influencing factors separately in a fully adjusted model using the cross-product terms of child gender (male/female), age group (6–11 years/12–14 years/≥15 years), BMI group (underweight/normal weight/overweight/obesity). The P-value of the cross-product term is considered the P-value of the interaction. The age groups are determined by reference to the age stratification that defines the criteria for anemia, and the students in several age groups basically correspond to the primary, middle and high school grades. BMI groups were determined based on the cut-off values for screening for malnutrition and overweight and obesity in school-age children and adolescents in China. In simple terms, underweight children are <5th percentile BMI of children of the same age and gender, normal weight is BMI from 5th percentile to < 85th percentile, overweight is BMI from 85th percentile to < 95th percentile, and obesity is BMI ≥ 95%. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.3; R Development Core Team). A P-value of < 0.05 for the two-tailed test was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

The ethnicity of the participants is mainly Han (97.6%), and more than half of their fathers (58.7%) and mothers (58.0%) have a bachelor’s degree or above, and 30.8% of the participants think that the family economic situation is “good” or “very good”. A total of 19,599 (57.8%) participants underwent at least one follow-up, with an average follow-up period of 3.16 ± 0.97 years. Hb trajectories in children and adolescents and their association with air pollution exposure

Overall, the LCMM identified three trajectories of changes in children’s Hb levels (Fig. 2A): (1) The “High hemoglobin” category (n = 3,115, 15.9%), (2) The “Medium hemoglobin” category (n = 12,608, 64.3%), and (3) The “Low hemoglobin” category (n = 3,876, 19.8%). LCMM identified three trajectories of Hb changes in male children (Fig. 2B) and two trajectories in female children (Fig. 2C). In the age-stratified analysis, LCMM identified three trajectories of Hb changes in children aged 6–11 (Fig. 2D) and those aged 15 and above (Fig. 2E), and two trajectories in children aged 12–14 (Fig. 2F). Table S3-S8 shows the relevant parameters for the number of optimal categories for hemoglobin trajectories. The different trajectories of Hb changes showed an overall upward trend throughout the study period. Participants in the “High hemoglobin” category had Hb levels significantly above the anemia cutoff, with a lower risk of anemia. Participants in the “Medium hemoglobin” category showed a slow upward trend in Hb levels, slightly above the anemia cutoff. The “Low hemoglobin” category had a relatively pronounced upward trend in Hb levels, but most participants in this category had Hb levels below or within the anemia cutoff intervals, indicating a higher risk of anemia.

Trajectories of change in hemoglobin levels in participants during the study period as determined by the latent class mixed model

The association between air pollutant exposure and participants’ Hb trajectories is shown in Table 2. The model used the “High hemoglobin” category as the reference category, and an increase in PM2.5 exposure levels per IQR was associated with an increased risk of Hb trajectories categorized as “Low hemoglobin” [OR (95% CI) = 2.124 (1.853, 2.344)] categories. Inconsistently, increased levels of O3 exposure were associated with a reduced risk of Hb trajectories categorized as “Low hemoglobin” [OR (95% CI) = 0.684 (0.599, 0.780)]. Increased levels of SO2 exposure were associated with a reduced risk of Hb trajectories categorized as “Medium hemoglobin” [OR (95% CI) = 0.868 (0.800, 0.943)] and “Low hemoglobin” [OR (95% CI) = 0.801 (0.729, 0.881)] categories. No significant association was observed between increased NO2 and CO exposure levels and the trajectory of changes in children’s Hb levels. The results of the analysis stratified by gender and age were generally consistent with the overall findings, except that CO exposure was found to be associated with an increased risk of being categorized as “Medium hemoglobin” [OR (95% CI) = 1.253 (1.127, 1.392)] in the 6–11 age group and a decreased risk of being categorized as “Low hemoglobin” [OR (95% CI) = 0.713 (0.608, 0.836)] in the 12–14 age group.

Association between air pollution and Hb levels and risk of anemia in children

The association between air pollutant exposure and Hb levels in children is shown in Fig. 3A, Table S9. When Hb was analyzed as a continuous variable, we found that Hb levels were negatively associated with PM2.5 and NO2 exposure and positively associated with O3 and CO. The fully adjusted model (Model 3) estimated that an increase in PM2.5 exposure per IQR was associated with a 0.678 g/L reduction in Hb levels in children and NO2 exposure was associated with a 1.281 g/L reduction in Hb levels. The increase in O3 and CO per IQR was associated with an increase in Hb levels of 0.979 g/L and 0.224 g/L, respectively. No statistical association between CO exposure and Hb levels was observed in the base model (Model 1) and the single pollutant model (Model 4). The effect estimation of the fully adjusted model was weakened compared with that of the single pollutant model but enhanced compared with the base model.

Multiple linear (A), logistic (B), and Cox (C) regression estimates of the effect of air pollutant exposure on anemia in children in a fully adjusted model. The model adjusted for child age, child BMI, child ethnicity, parental education, urban-rural type, physical exercise, household economic status, smoking behavior, residential situation, greening level, student recess areas, daily outdoor activity time, frequency of breakfast meals, and the number of family life members

When anemia was analyzed as a binary variable, the results were generally consistent with those of linear regression (Fig. 3B, Table S10). PM2.5 [OR = 1.251, 95% CI: (1.169, 1.340)] and NO2 [OR = 1.225, 95% CI: (1.130, 1.328)] exposure were observed in the fully adjusted model to be associated with an increased odds of anemia in children, while O3 [OR = 0.750, 95% CI: (0.708, 0.795)] and CO [OR = 0.845, 95% CI: (0.797, 0.896)] were associated with a reduced odds of anemia. Overall, the effect sizes of these associations changed across several sensitivity analyses, but their overall orientation remained consistent after extensive adjustments for various potential confounders.

Among 19,599 participants who were followed up at least once, 2,034 participants were defined as anemic at baseline and were therefore excluded, leaving 17,565 participants included in the Cox regression analysis. In this analysis, the study subjects had an average of 3.14 ± 0.98 follow-up visits [median (IQR) = 3.0 (1.0)], an average survival time of 2.63 ± 0.95 years [median (IQR) = 2.0 (1.0)], and 1,685 (9.6%) participants were observed to experience the endpoint event during follow-up. The results of the Cox proportional hazards model showed that increased levels of exposure to five air pollutants were statistically significantly associated with changes in the risk of anemia in children. The results of the Cox proportional hazards model showed that increased exposure levels to five air pollutants were statistically significantly associated with a change in the risk of anemia outcomes in children (Fig. 3C, Table S11). Specifically, in a fully adjusted model (Model 3), increased levels of PM2.5 [HR = 1.213, 95% CI: (1.068, 1.376)], NO2 [HR = 1.409, 95% CI: (1.254, 1.584)], and SO2 [HR = 1.268, 95% CI: (1.172, 1.372)] exposure during the study period were associated with an increased risk of anemia in children. Elevated levels of O3 [HR = 0.714, 95% CI: (0.631, 0.808)] and CO [HR = 0.899, 95% CI: (0.820, 0.986)] exposure are associated with a reduced risk of anemia outcomes in children. The fully adjusted model is generally consistent with the results of the base model (Model 1), but the effect size for each contaminant appears to be weakened compared to the results of the single pollutant model (Model 4). Notably, elevated levels of CO [HR = 1.124, 95% CI: (1.046, 1.208)] exposure in the single pollution model showed an association with an increased risk of anemia outcomes in children, which contradicted the results of other models.

Modifying effects of gender, age, and BMI in the association of air pollutant exposure with anemia in children and adolescents

We investigated the potential modifying effects of child gender, age, and BMI in the association between air pollution exposure and Hb levels and anemia in children (Fig. 4), with data presented in Tables S12-S20. In subgroup analyses stratified by child gender (Fig. 4A, Table S12-S14), PM2.5 exposure was associated with reduced Hb levels in males and females, but the gender-modifying effect was not significant (P interaction = 0.097). A stronger positive association between O3 exposure and Hb levels and a stronger negative association between NO2 exposure and Hb levels were observed in females compared to males (both P interaction < 0.001). In males, there was a significant positive correlation between CO and Hb levels, and SO2 exposure tended to be positively associated with Hb levels (both P interaction < 0.001). Logistic regression results showed that SO2 exposure was associated with a reduced odds of anemia only in male children (P interaction = 0.002). In the Cox regression analysis, an association between PM2.5 exposure and an increased risk of anemia was observed only in female children (P interaction < 0.001), while an association between CO exposure and a decreased risk of anemia was observed only in male children (P interaction < 0.001). Compared to female children, the association between exposure to O3, NO2, and SO2 and the risk of anemia is more pronounced in male children (both P interaction < 0.001).

Multiple linear, logistic, and Cox regression estimates of the effect of gender (A), age (B), and BMI (C) stratified air pollutant exposure on anemia in children in a fully adjusted model. The model adjusted for child age, child BMI, child ethnicity, parental education, urban-rural type, physical exercise, household economic status, smoking behavior, residential situation, greening level, student recess areas, daily outdoor activity time, frequency of breakfast meals, and the number of family life members

In subgroup analyses stratified by child age (Fig. 4B, Table S15-S17), PM2.5 exposure was only associated with decreased Hb levels only in children aged 6 to 11 years, and O3 was associated with increased Hb levels in children aged 6 to 11 and 12 to 14 years, but the age modification was not significant. The negative association between NO2 exposure and Hb levels was relatively weak in children aged 6 to 11 years (P interaction < 0.001). Inconsistent with results from other age groups, a negative association between SO2 exposure and Hb levels was observed in children aged 12 to 14 years (P interaction = 0.001), and a positive association between CO and Hb levels was observed only in children of this age group (P interaction = 0.014). In logistic regression analysis, PM2.5 exposure was observed to be associated with a reduced odds of anemia in adolescents over 15 years of age, contrary to the results for other age groups (P interaction < 0.001). NO2 exposure is strongly associated with an increased odds of anemia in children and adolescents aged 12 to 14 years and over 15 years than in children aged 6 to 11 years (P interaction < 0.001). The association of SO2 exposure with an increased odds of anemia was found only in children aged 12 to 14 years (P interaction < 0.001). Logistic regression and Cox regression analyses consistently showed that CO exposure was associated with a reduced risk of anemia in children aged 6–11 years and 12–14 years (P interaction = 0.029 and < 0.001, respectively). In addition, Cox regression analysis observed an association between PM2.5 exposure and an increased risk of anemia in children aged 6–11 years and 12–14 years (P interaction < 0.001). Significant associations between O3, NO2, and SO2 and the risk of anemia were observed only in children aged 6–11 years (both P interaction < 0.001).

Analysis of BMI stratification (Fig. 4C, Table S18-S20) in children showed that PM2.5 exposure was associated with reduced Hb levels in underweight and normal-weight children. The modification of BMI between O3 and NO2 exposure and Hb levels in children was not observed (both P interaction > 0.05). A positive association with elevated Hb levels was observed only in normal-weight and obese children (P interaction = 0.009). Logistic regression analysis showed that although PM2.5 exposure was only associated with an increased odds of anemia in normal-weight and overweight children, the modification of BMI was not significant (P interaction = 0.151). Except for underweight children, CO exposure was associated with a reduced odds of anemia in several other groups of children, and BMI modifications tended to be significant (P interaction = 0.052). The results of the Cox regression analysis showed that an association between PM2.5 exposure and an increased risk of anemia was only observed in children with normal weight, but the interaction was not significant (P interaction = 0.090). Except for underweight children, associations between O3, NO2, and SO2 exposure and the risk of anemia were observed in the other groups (both P interaction < 0.001).

Discussion

In this dynamic cohort study conducted in Shanghai, China, we identified several trajectories of changes in children’s Hb levels over the study period, and PM2.5 and NO2 exposure levels were associated with adverse changes in children’s Hb trajectories and an increased risk of developing anemia. These results were corroborated through cross-sectional analyses, longitudinal assessments, and multiple sensitivity analyses, yielding consistent conclusions. Stratified analyses revealed that SO2 exposure on anemia were found to be more significant in male children. In addition, exposure to PM2.5 and SO2 is more significantly associated with anemia in young children, while the adverse effects of NO2 exposure are more pronounced in older children. The association between PM2.5 exposure and adverse effects on anemia is more evident in underweight children, whereas the association between NO2 exposure and adverse effects on anemia is more significant in obese children.

The association between PM2.5 exposure and increased risk of anemia in children has been widely reported in several cross-sectional studies [17, 26, 31], This association is further corroborated by findings from our cohort study. A survey conducted among young children aged 0–59 months in India found that prenatal exposure to PM2.5, NO2, and SO2 was associated with an increased risk of developing anemia in early childhood, although the correlation between NO2 and SO2 was weak [16], and these results were similar to our findings. While our cross-sectional analyses did not demonstrate a significant association between SO2 exposure and hemoglobin levels or anemia risk in children, longitudinal analyses indicated such an association, suggesting that the effects of SO2 exposure may manifest over a longer duration.

There are also some inconsistent results with previous studies. A study of conducted on children under the age of five in South Africa indicated that both prenatal and childhood exposure to O3 and CO were associated with a higher prevalence of anemia [4]. Similarly, research involving 134 children and adolescents in Iran found that O3 and CO exposure were inversely correlated with Hb levels [33]. Recently, a study focusing on pregnant women in several low- and middle-income countries found that O3 exposure was associated with an increased risk of anemia among this population [36]. Subsequently, the research team conducted a study in adults in the highlands of China and found that O3 exposure was associated with increased Hb concentrations, revealing that the cause was O3 reducing oxygen supply, which in turn stimulates an increase in RBCs and Hb compensatory properties [15]. The impact of O3 on Hb levels appears to be contingent upon its concentration [23, 25]. At low concentrations, O3 can enter the bloodstream and generate free radical scavengers, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of red blood cells and providing a protective effect [5, 36]. In contrast, high concentrations of O3 may inflict oxidative damage on Hb [24]. Furthermore, O3 can specifically interact with the Hb of RBCs, selectively adsorbing onto the iron atom of the heme prosthetic group, leading to the oxidation of the central iron atom and the formation of orthohemoglobin, followed by oxidative fission of the heme ring [8]. A multi-country randomized controlled trial reported that CO exposure was associated with elevated Hb levels and a reduced prevalence of anemia in pregnant women [10]. However, elevated Hb levels due to CO exposure are not beneficial. When CO enters the bloodstream, it combines with Hb to form carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), which causes Hb to lose its ability to deliver oxygen, which causes the production of erythropoietin as well as the production of red blood cells from iron stores, ultimately resulting in anemia [2, 28]. Therefore, the impact of CO exposure on anemia in children should not only focus on the number of Hb, but also on changes in its oxygen-carrying capacity.

Research has demonstrated that air pollutants can induce systemic inflammation in children [14]. Prolonged inflammation is associated with a reduction in erythropoietin secretion, which subsequently hampers RBCs production and elevates the risk of anemia [30]. Certain constituents of particulate matter, including organic pollutants and heavy metals, may exert direct effects on Hb or indirectly contribute to anemia by interfering with iron metabolism [9, 18, 43]. Additionally, NO2 inhalation can provoke oxidative stress by disrupting the equilibrium of oxidant regulation [22]. Studies have shown that RBCs are a preferred target of oxidative stress in the blood, and dysfunctions in the ubiquitin-proteasome system may hinder the proteolytic degradation of damaged proteins within red blood cells and impair erythropoiesis, thereby facilitating the onset of anemia [11]. Furthermore, exposure to nitrogen oxides (NOX) may influence to the regulation of DNA methylation, which is closely linked to suboptimal growth and development in children [19]. While SO2 exposure has been shown to adversely affect children’s growth and development, there is a lack of adequate literature discussing the biological mechanisms associated with SO2 exposure and anemia in children [34]. The prevailing consensus suggests that the health effects of SO2 are related to inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and epigenetic changes, and further research is needed to elucidate the toxicological effects of SO2 exposure on the effects of anemia in children [16].

There are some advantages to our research. We identified several trajectories of change in childhood Hb in a dynamic cohort and comprehensively analyzed the longitudinal association between air pollutant exposure and anemia risk. Building upon the findings from the cross-sectional analysis results, we employed longitudinal analysis to further validate the reliability of these results. Additionally, we performed subgroup analyses to thoroughly examine the modifying effects of various factors related to the onset of anemia and to identify populations at heightened risk. Lastly, we endeavored to collect and adjust for covariate information to the greatest extent possible in order to mitigate confounding variables while preserving sample size. The consistency observed in the results across a series of sensitivity analyses underscores the robustness of our findings.

Our study is subject to several potential limitations. First, it was conducted in Shanghai, China, where air pollution levels are low compared to those reported in other similar studies, especially compared to low- and middle-income countries. Consequently, the findings may not be fully representative of broader contexts. Nevertheless, we identified that even low levels of air pollution significantly influence the prevalence of anemia among children and adolescents, offering valuable insights for regions characterized by lower air pollution levels or those that are more developed. Second, our method relied on school addresses to estimate air pollution exposure in study subjects, missing exposure assessments in households. Third, children’s nutritional status is an important factor in the development of anemia, and BMI is used instead of a more precise nutritional measurement (such as professional nutritional status assessment and nutrient level surveys) when we explore the potential modifying effects of children’s nutrition. Future research should aim to conduct a more thorough evaluation of children’s nutritional status to elucidate the various types and underlying causes of anemia.

Conclusions

In summary, our study confirms that under dynamic cohort conditions, long-term exposure to air pollutants PM2.5, NO2, and SO2 is associated with an increased risk of anemia in children and adolescents. The study also identified a range of susceptible populations, allowing for targeted interventions to be implemented. Air pollution remains a cause for alarm, and taking effective preventive measures can help reduce the burden of anemia in children and adolescents.
