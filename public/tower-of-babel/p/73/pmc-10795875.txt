Family Planning from a Male Perspective: Observations from a Community-Based Cross-sectional Study in Chennai, Tamil Nadu
Authors: SG Shruthee, V Deepanchakravarthi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Traditionally, family planning has been considered in the context of maternal and child health, although men play a dominant role in all matters related to reproductive decision-making. This study is carried out to assess the attitude and participation in family planning among currently married males and identify factors influencing the acceptance of male contraception. A cross-sectional study was conducted in Chennai in October 2018, which included 180 currently married men by systematic random sampling of eligible couples in the field practice area of Sanjeevarayanpet Primary Health Center (UPHC). Data was collected through a self-administered questionnaire in the local language and analyzed using SPSS version 16. Absolute and relative frequencies were estimated for descriptive data while correlates were tested for statistical significance using Pearson or Trend Chi-square tests. Study participants considered two children (82%) and spacing of at least 2 years (95%) as ideal, with spousal communication regarding family planning considered essential by a two-thirds majority. Among the 117 men who reported ever using contraception as a couple, 69% had used a condom vs. 42% and 30% for female spacing methods and tubectomy, respectively. Vasectomy, with nil acceptors, was considered acceptable by only 21% of men. Education, per-capita income, and spousal communication were seen to positively influence male contraceptive use. While married men show considerable commitment to family planning decision-making and practice, the stigma and misconceptions surrounding vasectomy have become ever more prominent. There is a need for the community and the health care system to work together to promote gender equity in family planning.

BACKGROUND

Traditionally, family planning has always been considered the domain of women; the underlying logic being: women get pregnant, most contraceptive methods are designed for them and it is easy to deliver family planning services in the context of maternal and child health. However, the International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) in 1994 changed the way family planning is viewed, from a strategy for population control to a means for individuals to exercise their right to reproductive self-determination. It has stressed the importance of the involvement of men in reproductive health “as clients, as partners and as agents of positive change.”[1] The National Population Policy (2000) in India reflects the changing scenario by emphasizing the importance of male participation in planned parenthood.[2] Promotion of male involvement in contraception is imperative as male reproductive health needs remain unaddressed by the existing reproductive health services which exhibit a high degree of provider bias.[3]

In a patriarchal society like India, men play a dominant role in all matters related to reproductive decision-making, but their share in the actual use of contraception continues to be low. As per the National Family Health Survey-4 (2015-16), the current use of male condom and vasectomy in India is 5.6% and 0.3%, respectively.[4] Use of male contraception is almost non-existent in Tamil Nadu at 0.8% and 0% for male condom and vasectomy, respectively.[4] Although this issue has been debated for quite some time, most studies done to study male involvement in contraception have captured the information from women, hence creating a bias. This study was conducted with the primary objective of assessing the willingness-to-use and ever-use of male methods of contraception and its correlates among currently married men whose wives were in the reproductive age group in Chennai.

METHODS

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the field practice area of Sanjeevarayanpet UPHC in Chennai in October 2018. Currently married men whose wives were in the reproductive age group (18 to 49 years) were the study participants. Assuming as per the NFHS-4 Tamil Nadu report,[4] that 67% of men agree they have a role in contraception, with absolute precision of 7%, at a 95% confidence level, the minimum sample size was calculated as 173. Hence, a final sample size of 180 was decided. Systematic random sampling was done using the eligible couples register maintained at the UPHC as the sampling frame. If any selected sample did not give consent to the study, the next eligible sample was chosen till the required sample size was reached.

Ever-use and willingness-to-use of male contraception defined, as a history of utilization at least once in the past or intention to use in the future when need is perceived, respectively, were the primary outcome variables. The data was collected by using a self-administered questionnaire in the local language after obtaining a written informed consent. The questionnaire was administered by the male investigator if the chosen study participant was illiterate. Data was analyzed using the statistical software SPSS version 16. Absolute and relative frequencies were estimated for descriptive data while correlates were tested for statistical significance using Pearson or Trend Chi-square tests as appropriate. A P value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Govt. Stanley Medical College, Chennai.

Ever-use of contraception

Among the 180 men, 158 (88%) men reported awareness of at least one contraceptive method, with 143 (82%) and 133 (74%) men considering spacing and limiting methods essential, respectively; while 119 (66%) men reported ever using contraception as a couple. Regarding the onus of the use of contraception in an ideal family, 43% said either spouse; while 33% believed contraception was women’s domain, the remaining one-third (34%) believed it to be the responsibility of the male spouse.

Ever-use of spacing methods was reported by 98 (55%) men, with male and female methods of temporary contraception utilized by 81 (46%) and 49 (28%) couples, respectively. Current use of spacing methods during last intercourse with a spouse was reported by 37 (20.5%) men, specifically male condoms (30), oral pills (4), and intrauterine devices (3). Tubectomy was the only practiced limiting method in this study population, adopted by 54 (30%) couples before or at the time of the study. Few (21%) men considered vasectomy an acceptable method of contraception. When probed regarding the decision-making behind opting for tubectomy as opposed to vasectomy, men cited stigma (30%), a belief that contraception was not their responsibility (23%), and fear of sexual weakness (20%) as the major reasons influencing their acceptance of vasectomy. Less common reasons included limited knowledge, lack of popularity of the method, and a belief that tubectomy was easier.

Family planning—decision-making

As regards the attitude toward family formation, 82% of the study participants considered two children as ideal, with few men opting for one (9.9%) or three (8.1%) children, respectively. While 65% of men preferred a spacing of three or more years between consecutive children as ideal, the majority of the remaining one-third (29%) believed that birth spacing of at least two years was essential for the well-being of the family. A two-thirds majority of the study participants considered consensual decision-making with spouse regarding limiting, spacing, use, and choice of contraception as ideal. However, while more than 70% of men were supportive of consensual decision-making with regard to family formation and expansion, 10% less men thought it necessary when deciding regarding contraception [Figure 1].

Also, correlates of positive gender dynamics such as the earning status of wife and spousal communication regarding family planning were analyzed for association with acceptance of male contraception. DISCUSSION

The present study reveals that a significant proportion (33%) of men believe contraceptive use in the context of marriage is the prerogative of the female spouse. This is similar to the state average of 34% as reported in the NFHS-4.[4] A study done in the neighboring state, Karnataka in 2015, however, shows a more favorable attitude among men in this regard, with only 16% of men having reported that “family planning is prerogative of females.”[5] Male methods predominate among the spacing methods ever used by the couples in the study; the nil acceptors of vasectomy in the present study, although precise estimation is limited by the small sample size, is corroborated by the findings of NFHS-5 (2019-2021) in Tamil Nadu,[6] which report current use of male condom and vasectomy at 1.8% and 0.1%, respectively. There exists a potential for information bias with regard to the reported use of female methods by the couples in the study, as its use is not readily apparent and information may have been withheld from the male spouse.

The positive attitude toward limiting and spacing of children observed in the study signifies the success of the effective communication strategies with respect to family planning employed both at the community and health system level. A positive gender dynamics is observed with respect to decision-making regarding all aspects of family planning at the household level, with consensual decision-making with spouse voted as ideal by the majority of the men in the study. In the study in Karnataka, 81% of men reported discussing with their spouse regarding family planning.[5] A study done by Char A (2011) in rural central India found that while conjugal communication predominated in decision-making to adopt reversible methods; mothers-in-law frequently intervened on when to adopt sterilization.[3] The possibility of social desirability bias influencing the above responses cannot be dismissed; however, a self-administered data collection tool with neutral non-biased questions was designed to particularly address this limitation.

The association between educational status and acceptance of male contraception is not surprising as educated men have better awareness and perception of health and are more likely to be proponents of healthy gender dynamics at the household level and beyond. This is corroborated by a study based on NFHS-II data, where better educated men had higher odds of using condoms, although this association was statistically significant only in Northern states like Bihar and Punjab.[7] By extension of the same logic and improved access to health services, men belonging to higher socioeconomic classes had understandably higher rates of acceptance and usage of male contraception and this is corroborated by similar studies.[7,8]

While exploring correlates of positive gender dynamics such as the financial status of the wife and spousal communication regarding contraception, both factors were significantly associated with the usage of male condoms. Spousal communication is cited as the most important factor having a positive influence on the use of male condoms by the study based on NFHS-II data, with Kerala having an adjusted odds ratio as high as 4.5 for the use of condoms among those who communicate with their spouse.[7] However, neither correlate was associated with willingness-to-use vasectomy. This could reflect the fact that vasectomy is plagued by myths and misconceptions that pervades both sexes, as ours is a nation that has still not forgotten its history of forced sterilizations in the 1970s.[9] A study by EngenderHealth in India discovered that women were equally or sometimes more concerned that vasectomy might result in a loss of interest in sex, and that convincing the spouse of a potential client greatly improved the acceptance rates of vasectomy among men.[10]

CONCLUSION

It is seen that more than two-thirds of married men in Chennai show substantial involvement in the decision-making and practices relevant to various aspects of family planning and modern contraceptive use. However, with only one-fifth of men even willing to consider male sterilization an acceptable limiting method and nil current utilization of the method in this population, gender-neutrality in family planning remains a distant vision. A two-pronged approach that simultaneously addresses the de-stigmatization of condoms and the re-popularization of vasectomy through a review of current educational campaigns is imperative.
