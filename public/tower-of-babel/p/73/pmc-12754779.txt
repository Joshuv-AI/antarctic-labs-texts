PexR is a noncanonical regulator of the peroxide stress response in bacteria
Authors: Eva Bastida-Martínez, Irene del Rey-Navalón, Naike Ye, Catherine L Drennan, S Padmanabhan, Montserrat Elías-Arnanz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCells combat peroxide stress using peroxiredoxins and catalases. The paradigmatic H2O2-sensing OxyR or PerR transcription regulators typically control their expression in bacteria. Here, we report our discovery of a noncanonical mechanism for H2O2-signaled regulation of peroxiredoxin ahpC and catalase katB genes in Myxococcus xanthus by PexR, an ATP-binding bacterial enhancer binding protein that acts as a dual-function repressor-activator. PexR, a dimer capable of higher-order oligomerization, binds to dyad-symmetry repeats upstream of ahpC and katB, activating their H2O2-induced σ54-dependent expression. Under peroxide stress-free conditions, PexR downregulates housekeeping σA-dependent ahpC expression. Deleting ahpC causes pleiotropy, and synthetic lethality when eliminated with katB or pexR, indicating PexR-mediated co-regulation of AhpC and KatB as critical for normal growth. We show that resting state PexR is autoinhibited by its N-terminal Zn2+/Fe2+-binding GAF (cGMP-specific phosphodiesterases, adenylyl cyclases, and FhlA) domain, which senses H2O2 and releases its bound metal to trigger PexR-activated σ54-dependent expression. Our genomic analyses reveal conservation of PexR and its regulatory elements and, likely, mechanism across Myxococcota, frequently co-occurring with OxyR or PerR, showcasing this phylum’s remarkable diversity of potential peroxide stress response regulatory mechanisms. Moreover, PexR likely operates in phyla beyond Myxococcota, and its discovery expands the toolkit for genetically encoded H2O2 sensors.

Introduction

Oxidation-reduction processes and their homeostasis are vital across all domains of life, but also the source of toxic, highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage cellular components [1, 2]. Reactions involving oxygen generate diverse ROS, such as singlet oxygen (1O2) or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which cause oxidative stress and signal general and specific cellular responses [1, 2]. To combat ROS, bacteria have evolved multiple ingenious strategies and interconnected sensory signaling-regulatory pathways to trigger, among others, expression of peroxiredoxins, like AhpC, and catalases to neutralize H2O2 [3–6]. In most bacteria, the archetypal H2O2-sensing transcription factors OxyR or PerR regulate the expression of defense regulons to counteract peroxide stress [5–9]. OxyR is widely distributed in Gram-negative and some Gram-positive bacteria and studied in Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinomycetes phyla, and PerR occurs primarily in Gram-positive Firmicutes bacteria; and while most species have either OxyR or PerR, some have both [7–9].

OxyR is a LysR-type transcriptional regulator (LTTR) with an N-terminal helix-turn-helix DNA-binding domain (DBD) and a regulatory C-terminal oligomerization domain that typically contains two conserved Cys residues for H2O2 sensing [5, 8–11]. Its hyperreactive peroxidatic Cys thiol Cp (C199 in the Escherichia coli OxyR prototype) senses H2O2 and is quickly oxidized to sulfenic acid, which can then form an intramolecular disulfide bond with a resolving Cys CR (E. coli OxyR C208) to modulate OxyR–DNA binding [5, 8–12]. OxyR functions as a dimer-of-dimers homotetramer and, depending on the bacterial species, can act as repressor, activator, or dual regulator under peroxide stress of many genes, notably those for AhpC and catalases, usually dependent on promoters recognized by the primary σ factor (denoted σ70 in E. coli or σA in the bacterium Myxococcus xanthus studied here) and defined by their −10 and −35 elements [7, 10–13]. OxyR recognizes relatively long (40–60 bp), not strongly conserved DNA sites, binding to multiple overlapping T-n11-A motifs (n = any nucleotide) as a repressor [14] and to four regularly interspaced motifs as an activator or dual-role regulator (ATAG-n7-CTAT-n7-ATAG-n7-CTAT consensus in E. coli) [15]. PerR, on the other hand, is a Fur (ferric uptake regulator) family member, with an N-terminal winged-helix DBD and a C-terminal dimerization domain containing a high-affinity structural Zn2+-Cys4 site, and an interdomain regulatory site in which a non-heme Fe2+ (or Mn2+) pentacoordinated to three His and two Asp residues is used for H2O2-sensing [16–18]. Under non-stress conditions, PerR binds to a Per-box AT-rich sequence of dyad symmetry (TTATAATAnTTATAA consensus) to repress target genes usually expressed from σA-type promoters [7, 19, 20]. PerR-bound Fe2+ senses H2O2 to generate hydroxyl radicals (OH•) via the Fenton reaction, and two of the three Fe2+ His ligands are susceptible to oxidation by OH• to 2-oxo-His [16–18]. This oxidation provokes loss of the bound Fe2+ and conformational changes in PerR to abrogate DNA binding and relieve repression of a large regulon that includes, as with OxyR, genes for AhpC and catalase [5, 16–18].

The soil bacterium M. xanthus is the best-studied member among the Gram-negative myxobacteria, now reclassified as the phylum Myxococcota [21, 22], which stand out in exhibiting various remarkable eukaryotic-like traits [4, 21–26]. Our studies on how light triggers M. xanthus carotenoid biosynthesis in response to 1O2 stress caused by light unmasked new photosensory and gene regulatory paradigms [4, 22, 27–29]. But little is known about how M. xanthus, an obligate aerobe, combats peroxide and other ROS. Its soil-dwelling, aerobic, predatory lifestyle would expose M. xanthus to diverse oxidative stresses. These can include ROS (1O2, H2O2, OH•, and also organic hydroperoxides and superoxide), reactive nitrogen species or RNS (nitric oxide/NO and peroxynitrite/ONOO⁻), sulfur species or RSS (hydrogen sulfide, per- and polysulfides or sulfanes), and chlorine species or RCS (hypochlorite) [1, 7, 30]. These stresses might arise endogenously from respiration, metabolic and other cellular processes, usually involving players like enzymes with metal (iron, copper) centers, or exogenously under oxic conditions via numerous agents like light (noted earlier), soil chemicals, metals, and predation [1, 7, 30, 31]. Here, we report that M. xanthus, which lacks bona fide OxyR or PerR homologs, instead employs a bacterial enhancer binding protein (bEBP) that we named PexR (for Peroxide Regulator) to sense peroxide and control the cellular response. The bEBPs are specialized activators required to initiate transcription by RNA polymerase (RNAP) holoenzyme associated with the alternative σ54 factor, which is a vital σ in M. xanthus [32, 33]. They typically comprise: a variable N-terminal regulatory (R) domain, with a role in signal sensing and in modulating bEBP activity; a highly conserved central activator AAA+ (ATPases Associated with diverse cellular Activities) oligomerization domain, responsible for ATP hydrolysis and activation of transcription by σ54; and a C-terminal helix-turn-helix DBD that binds to an upstream activator sequence (UAS) or enhancer site, often of dyad symmetry and located >50 bp upstream of σ54-dependent promoters [34]. In the absence of a specific signal, bEBPs are in a resting (inactive) state, generally as homodimers that can bind to the UAS and ATP [35]. The presence of more than one UAS and the binding of multiple bEBP dimers can facilitate oligomerization. Signal-induced ligand binding to the R domain, its phosphorylation, or protein–protein interactions triggers higher-order oligomer formation (to tetramers, hexamers, or heptamers), ATP hydrolysis, and interaction with RNAP-associated σ54 to drive open complex formation at its target promoters for transcription initiation [34, 36].

We show that PexR binds to ATP, can undergo oligomeric transitions, and is a dual-function repressor-activator that binds to dyad-symmetry UASs to activate σ54-dependent expression of peroxiredoxin ahpC and catalase katB genes under peroxide stress, and downregulate σA-dependent ahpC expression under normal growth conditions. Importantly, PexR-mediated co-regulation of AhpC and KatB is required for cellular viability. The PexR N-terminal metal-binding GAF (cGMP-specific phosphodiesterases, adenylyl cyclases, and FhlA) domain maintains PexR in an inactive resting state in the absence of stress, and senses H2O2 to release metal and activate PexR. Our genomic analyses indicate that the PexR regulatory mechanism in peroxide stress response is prevalent across Myxococcota and may also operate in other bacterial phyla. In sum, our study uncovers a new noncanonical H2O2-sensing regulatory mechanism that controls the peroxide stress response in bacteria.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, constructs, growth conditions, and materials

Chemicals, standards, and antibodies were from Merck or Fisher Scientific unless stated otherwise, and Strep-Tactin® XT Superflow® resin, columns, sample applicator, and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate antibody were from IBA Life Sciences. Myxococcus xanthus strains (Supplementary Table S1) were grown in the dark in liquid CTT (1% Casitone, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 8 mM MgSO4, 1 mM phosphate buffer) medium at 33°C with shaking at 300 rpm (growth monitored using the optical density at 550 nm OD550), or on CTT/1.5% agar plates supplemented with required antibiotics (kanamycin/Kan, 40 μg/ml; oxytetracycline or Tet, 10 μg/ml) and inducer (0.005–0.5 mM vanillate; 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside/IPTG) for conditional gene expression [37]. To test peroxide sensitivity by disk diffusion, a sterile Whatman filter paper disk spotted with 5 μl of H2O2 at a given concentration was placed in the center of a lawn of the M. xanthus strain on CTT-agar plates (generated by spreading 1 ml overnight cultures), incubated at 33°C overnight, photographed, and the inhibition zone diameter was measured (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/). To qualitatively assess cellular catalase activity, aliquots (100 μl) of liquid cultures (OD550= 0.8–0.9) in different test tubes were mixed with 100 μl each of 1% Triton X-100 and 30% (v/v) H2O2 incubated for 10 min at room temperature (RT), and the foam height assessed [38]. Pilot experiments indicated that 1 mM H2O2 inhibited M. xanthus cell growth, as also reported elsewhere [39], and treating cells grown in liquid CTT to OD550 of 0.8–1 with 0.5 mM H2O2 caused a drop in growth after 15 min. Hence, as required, cell cultures were subjected to a 10-min pulse with 0.5 mM H2O2, pelleted immediately, washed with fresh medium, and frozen until further analysis. Escherichia coli strains DH5α (plasmid constructions), BL21(DE3) or C2566H (protein overexpression), and BTH101 (bacterial two-hybrid analysis) were grown in lysogeny broth (LB) or terrific broth (TB) with antibiotic at a given temperature. Plasmid constructs were generated using standard protocols and verified by DNA sequencing. Plasmids for gene deletions, conditional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\rm P}_{van}$\end{document}- or PIPTG-inducible expression, or complementation analysis in M. xanthus contained a 1.38-kb or 1.31-kb M. xanthus genomic DNA for integration by homologous recombination at chromosomal sites without promoter activity [37]. Two-step allele exchange with KanR positive selection and galK galactose-sensitivity (GalS) negative selection markers was used to generate in-frame gene deletions in M. xanthus [29], and overlap extension polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or gene synthesis (GenScript) for mutations.

HyPer7 analysis of intracellular H2O2

WT and ΔahpC (−/+Pr1::hyper7) cells were grown to exponential phase in CTT, washed with TM buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 8 mM MgSO4), and resuspended in fresh TM to OD₅₅₀ ∼0.8–0.9. A 200 µl aliquot was transferred to a 96-well Nunc™ F96 MicroWell™ Black imaging plate (Fisher) to measure HyPer7 oxidation by recording fluorescence in a FLUOstar OMEGA plate reader (BMG Labtech) using 390 nm and 485 nm excitation (bandpass 12 nm) and 520 nm emission filters. The fluorescence ratio, F500/F400, was normalized relative to autofluorescence (AF) values in WT and ΔahpC cells not expressing Hyper7 and adjusted for OD600 using [(F500/OD600)–(AF500/OD600)]/[(F400/OD600)–(AF400/OD600)]. Fluorescence in M. xanthus was visualized by immobilizing 1 μl of cells from exponentially growing cultures on 1% agarose-TPM (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 8 mM MgSO4, 1 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.6) slices and viewing under a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope equipped with Plan Apo VC 100×/1.4 DIC objective, 495 nm excitation and 520 nm emission filters, and a Hamamatsu ORCA-AG CCD camera. Images were processed using Metamorph 4.5 (Universal Imaging Group).

Bacterial two-hybrid (BACTH) analysis and reporter lacZ expression

BACTH analysis was carried out using the E. coli system based on functional complementation of the Bordetella pertussis adenylate cyclase catalytic domain T25 and T18 fragments, which are inactive when physically separate but become active when each is fused to two proteins or domains that interact [40]. Interaction results in cAMP synthesis monitored by the expression of a reporter gene lacZ encoding β-galactosidase and is assessed qualitatively from blue colony color on LB/X-Gal (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-d-galactoside; 40 μg ml−1) plates. Coding regions of proteins or domains of interest were PCR-amplified genes and cloned into the BamHI and EcoRI sites of plasmids pKT25, pUT18, or pUT18C (Supplementary Table S1). Given pairs of the constructs were introduced into E. coli BTH101 (cya−) by electroporation, grown in liquid LB overnight at 30°C, and 5 μl spotted on LB/X-Gal plates, with interaction inferred from the blue color. Pairs with yeast GCN4 leucine zipper served as the positive control, and pairs with a construct lacking the fusion as the negative control. Reporter lacZ expression in M. xanthus was assessed quantitatively from β-galactosidase specific activity “β-gal activity” (nanomoles o-nitrophenyl β-d-galactoside hydrolyzed min−1 mg−1 protein) for samples from liquid cultures (OD550≈ 1.0) in a SpectraMax 340 microtiter plate reader (Molecular Devices), with data reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), n= 3.

Immunoblot/western blot analysis

Immunoblots of M. xanthus whole cell extracts expressing FLAG-tagged proteins were performed using cell pellets obtained from 1.5 ml cultures grown to OD550 ∼1 in CTT and suspended in 300 μl of buffer containing cOmplete protease inhibitor (Roche Applied Science), 1 mM each of benzamidine and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Sigma), and lysed with SDS/chloroform. After centrifugation, 20 μl of the supernatant was loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gel and subjected to electrophoresis, after which proteins were transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membranes (Millipore), and probed using monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 (F3165, Sigma) or, as loading control, monoclonal anti-RNAP β antibodies (8RB13, ThermoFisher), and the ECL Prime western blot detection system (Cytiva).

RNA isolation and purification

To isolate total RNA, cells were pelleted by centrifugation from 2–5 ml cultures grown in the dark at 33°C with shaking at 300 rpm in liquid CTT medium supplied with the antibiotic or inducer as required to OD550 ≈ 0.8–1, as described earlier. Cell pellets were suspended in 1 ml TRIzol™ (10 min, RT), vortexed (10 s) with 0.2 ml chloroform, incubated (10 min, RT), and centrifuged (18 000 × g/15 min/4°C). RNA recovered from the upper phase was mixed with 0.5 ml 2-propanol, precipitated (−80°C, 30 min), and centrifuged to remove the supernatant. The pellet was washed with 1 ml cold 75% ethanol, dried, resuspended in 86.5 μl RNase-free water, and treated with TURBO™ DNase (6 U/3 µl; Ambion) and 40 U (1 μl) Protector RNase Inhibitor (Roche) for 3 h/37°C to remove DNA. RNA was purified using PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit and eluted in 30–40 µl of RNase-free filtered water, concentrated, and purity checked in 1% agarose gel and in a NanoDrop™ ND-2000 spectrophotometer.

RNA-seq analysis

Purified RNA (n= 3 replicates for each condition; RNA integrity number/RIN > 8 in an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system) obtained as described earlier was used for prokaryotic RNA-seq analysis (Novogene-UK). After in-house quality control and prokaryotic directional messenger RNA (mRNA) library preparation, samples were used for paired-end 150 bp sequencing in an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System (>2 million read pairs per sample) with data quality control-bioinformatic analyses. Total RNA was treated to remove rRNA, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using random hexamer primers. This was followed by a second cDNA strand synthesis (dTTPs for dUTPs), end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, UTP-containing second strand cDNA removal, size selection, and PCR amplification. Libraries were quantified (Qubit, qPCR), the size distribution analyzed (Bioanalyzer), pooled (based on effective concentration and required data amount), and sequenced. Paired-end reads (raw data) processed to obtain clean reads were mapped to the Myxococcus xanthus DK1622 reference genome, with qualitative (Rockhopper, to analyze bacterial RNA-seq data) and quantitative analysis (FeatureCounts) of the number of reads mapped to each gene and normalized as FPKMs (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript sequence per Millions of base pairs sequenced). DEGs between two conditions were classified based on P-adjusted values <.05 using DESeq2 R package, with data reliability validated using raw data FASTQ files and Rockhopper. DEGs were manually filtered setting ±1.5-fold change (log2FC ≥ 0.585 or ≤−0.585) thresholds and P-adjusted value <.05. Data were represented as volcano plots using VolcaNoseR (https://huygens.science.uva.nl/VolcaNoseR/) and analyzed using GO (Gene Ontology) KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analyses (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/), BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/), and Conserved Domain Database (CDD; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/ wrpsb.cgi).

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR

Purified RNA (2 µg) incubated for 5 min at 65°C with 2.5 μM random hexamer primers (Promega) and 0.5 mM dNTP mix for primer annealing was reverse transcribed with 10 U/μl Superscript® IV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher) and 2 U/µl Protector RNase Inhibitor (Roche) in 20 μl total volume to obtain cDNA. For quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR), we used 10 ng cDNA and specific primer pairs (Supplementary Table S2) designed using Primer Express™ 3.0 (Thermo Fisher) to amplify ∼50–150 bp region within each transcript. Primers were checked for ∼100% RT-PCR efficiency using a standard curve of five 10-fold serial dilutions of cDNA and iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-Rad). Vegetatively expressed vegA was used as reference and absence of contaminant DNA was checked in an equivalent amount of non-reverse transcribed RNA. Reactions were done in triplicate in a StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using the 1-Step RT-PCR program cycle with no reverse transcription (95°C, 20 s/40 cycles at 95°C, 3 s/60°C, 1 min), and melting-dissociation curves were determined at 60°C–95°C/0.3°C min−1 and 95°C/15 s. Data were analyzed using Applied Biosystems software and reported as mean ± SD, n= 3.

5′ RACE (rapid amplification of cDNA ends) analysis

Total RNA purified from specific strains and specified growth conditions was reverse transcribed, directly or after XRN-1 5′→3′ exoribonuclease (New England Biolabs) treatment to remove partially degraded 5′-monophosphate RNA, using the 5′/3′ RACE 2nd Generation kit with Protector RNase Inhibitor present (Roche) and a primer hybridizing 700–750 bp downstream of the start codon of the gene. The cDNA product (purified with High Pure PCR Product Purification Kit; Roche) was tagged to 3′ poly(A) adaptor using terminal transferase (Roche), purified, and used as PCR template with oligo(dT)-Anchor and a cDNA-specific nested primer hybridizing 500–550 bp downstream of the start codon. The product was purified and PCR-amplified with Anchor primer and one hybridizing 280–350 bp downstream of the start codon, purified, analyzed in an agarose gel, and sequenced directly or after cloning in a carrier plasmid.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR

Cells grown in 50 ml CTT/40 μg/ml Kan/5 μM vanillate to OD550= 0.70 at 33°C were treated with rifampicin (25 μg/ml) for 30 min. They were pelleted by centrifugation, washed thrice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and crosslinked with 1% (v/v) formaldehyde in 10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.6) for 10 min at RT with shaking (100 rpm), then cooled in ice for 30 min, pelleted, washed twice with PBS, and stored at −80°C. The frozen cells were thawed, resuspended in 293 μl lysis buffer [100 mM NaCl/10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5/1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)/2.2 mg/ml lysozyme], and incubated for 30 min at RT with shaking (100 rpm), and further incubated at 37°C for 10 min after adding 343 μl ChIP buffer (167 mM NaCl/16.7 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.1/1.1% Triton X-100/1.2 mM EDTA/1× cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail). They were sonicated (15/30 s on/off cycles) in a Diagenode Pico Bioruptor to generate 0.2–0.4 kb fragments. The supernatant was isolated by centrifugation, and 20 μl was set aside as input sample. The rest mixed with 550 μl ChIP buffer/0.01% SDS was incubated for 30 min at 4°C with 40 μl monoclonal anti-FLAG magnetic agarose beads (Fisher) previously equilibrated with PBS and 0.01% SDS–ChIP buffer. Beads were washed with buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.1/2 mM EDTA/0.1% SDS-Triton X-100) at low (150 mM NaCl) and high salt (0.5 M NaCl), with 0.25 M LiCl in TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.1/1 mM EDTA) with 1% NP-40/1% sodium deoxycholate, and twice with TE buffer. DNA-bound protein was eluted in three 40 μl IP fractions using FLAG peptide (100 μg/ml) in PBS, pooled, and incubated with proteinase K (20 μg/μl) in 500 mM NaCl/70 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5/7 mM EDTA buffer for 2 h/45°C, then 65°C/6 h to disrupt crosslinks. DNA isolated using High Pure PCR product purification kit (Roche) and a similarly treated and purified input sample were analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Green reaction mix (Bio-Rad) and specific primers (Supplementary Table S2) in MicroAMP FAST optical 48-well reaction plates in a StepOne™ PCR system. Signal enrichment at each promoter was estimated as percent input and the ratio of promoter-specific to intragenic signal of the ChIP fraction relative to the input sample. Data are reported as mean ± SD, n= 3.

Protein purification and anaerobic reconstitution with iron

PexR and its domains were overexpressed with an N-terminal His6-SUMO-tag (PexR, PexRΔGAF/PexR186-531) or C-terminal Strep-tag (PexR GAF/PexR1-173 and mutants, GAF-AAA+/PexR1-444). Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) or C2566H freshly transformed with the overexpression plasmid grown in 10–50 ml LB/40–50 μg/ml Kan at 37°C to OD600 of 0.6–1.0 or overnight was added to 1 L fresh LB/Kan or TB/Kan and grown to OD600= 0.6 at 37°C/150–180 rpm in an Innova 44R Orbital Shaker (New Brunswick). After 1 h incubation at 4°C, it was grown overnight (~16 h) with 1 mM IPTG at 16 or 25°C to induce protein overexpression. Cells were harvested (8000 × g/10 min/4°C), flash-frozen into liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until use. For aerobic protein purification, thawed cells resuspended in 25 ml lysis buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5/2 mM β-mercaptoethanol or β-ME) with 0.1 mg/ml DNAse, one cOmplete EDTA-free inhibitor tablet, 1 mM each phenylmethylsulfonylchloride/PMSF and benzamidine were lysed in a French press (ThermoFisher). From the soluble fraction recovered by centrifugation (20 000 × g/45 min/4°C), His6-SUMO-tagged proteins were purified off TALON resin (2 ml, Clontech) and eluted with 150 mM imidazole as 2 ml fractions. Protein fractions checked by SDS–PAGE were pooled and digested with Ulp1 SUMO protease (100:1, 3 h, 16°C), diluted with 0 M NaCl buffer, bound to phosphocellulose (1 ml), and eluted with 800 mM NaCl buffer. Fractions containing pure protein were pooled and concentrated (Amicon Ultra-4, MWCO 30 kDa). Strep-tagged proteins were purified from the cell lysate supernatant off 1 ml Strep-Tactin XT4Flow resin (IBA Life Sciences) with 1 ml elutions using 50 mM biotin/buffer. Protein-containing fractions were pooled, concentrated (Amicon Ultra-15 MWCO 10 kDa), and purified using a Superdex™ 200 Increase 10/300 GL SEC column and AKTA HPLC unit (Cytiva). For reconstitution with Fe2+, cell lysis and protein purification were performed using buffers sparged with argon (1 h) and overnight equilibration at 4°C in an anaerobic chamber (MBRAUN). Cell pellets from 4 L TB resuspended in 100 ml lysis buffer with 2 mM each MgCl2 and DTT (GoldBio), 2 cOmplete EDTA-free inhibitor tablets, 1 mg/ml egg white lysozyme, and 0.2 μl/ml benzonase nuclease were sonicated (2 s on-off, 4-min cycle, 30% amplitude; Qsonica). Clarified lysate (28 000 × g, 50 min) was gently stirred with 1 mM Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O, filtered (0.45 μm pore size, 1 h), and loaded (flow rate 1 ml/min) onto a 5 ml Strep-Tactin XT 4Flow FPLC column pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer. After a 25 ml lysis buffer wash, the protein was eluted in 5 ml fractions with elution buffer (lysis buffer with 50 mM biotin), concentrated (Amicon 30 kDa MWCO), filtered with 0.22 μm centrifuge tube filters (Costar Spin-X), and purified off a HiLoad Superdex 200 16/60 SEC column pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer. The major eluted peak was collected, aliquoted, and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Aerobically purified PexR GAF and GAF-AAA+ domains were dialyzed in 3 kDa MWCO dialysis cassettes thrice each with 150 mM NaCl buffer without β-ME, pH 8.0, then with 10 mM EDTA/buffer to remove Zn2+, and then with EDTA-free buffer, concentrated to ∽13 mg/ml (Amicon Ultra 10 kDa MWCO), incubated with 10 mM DTT, reconstituted with Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O in the anaerobic chamber, and purified by SEC (Superdex 200 10/300 or HiLoad™ 16/600 GL column) with each step monitored by SDS–PAGE (4%–20% w/v). Purified protein concentrations were determined using absorbance at 280 nm and as extinction coefficients (ε280 in M−1cm−1): 26 930 (PexR), 16 960 (PexRΔGAF), 30 940 (PexR1-444-Strep), and 15 470 (PexR1-173-Strep and mutants).

Size-exclusion chromatography

Superdex 200 size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) columns were equilibrated with 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, and 2 mM β-ME in an AKTA or NGC (Bio-Rad) unit. Calibration curves obtained using aprotinin (6.5 kDa), cytochrome C (12.4 kDa), carbonic anhydrase (29.679 kDa), ovalbumin (43 kDa), BSA (66 kDa), blue dextran (2 MDa, to estimate void volume Vo), and vitamin B12 (1.35 kDa, to estimate bed volume Vt) were: logMr= 7.7145–0.1959(Ve) for Superdex 200 10/300 and logMr= 4.2379–0.0306(Ve) for Superdex 16/600 columns, with Mr= apparent molecular mass and Ve= elution volume. Purified protein (50–100 μM; 500 μl) was injected into the column, eluted at recommended flow rates, tracked by absorbance (280/235/220 nm), and peaks analyzed for Mr (from Ve) and by SDS–PAGE.

Metal analysis

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) 66Zn/56Fe analysis was performed in an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS system (Agilent Technologies) tuned with 10 μg/l Li/Ce/Co/Tl/Y optimized for mass sensitivity, resolution, etc. Purified protein (1 ml, 30–50 μM) was hydrolyzed (20 min/220°C/120 bar) with 3 ml nitric acid (65% Suprapur®, Merck), cooled, and diluted to 10 ml with ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ-cm). A 1 ml aliquot spiked with 400 µg/l Sc/Ga/Ge/Rh/Ir (internal standard to control plasma stability, drifting, and matrix effects) was injected, and data acquired in the collision cell in the quantitative spectrum mode (300 ms dwell time; 100 sweeps/replicate) for three replicates of independently purified samples to estimate average 66Zn/57Fe and standard error from eight-point linear MS calibration curves (R2 > 0.999) using an external multielement solution. Colorimetric assays were used to estimate: (a) Zn2+ with PAR, prepared zinc standards, and absorbance at 492 nm (A492; ε492= 71 500 M−1cm−1; KD= 0.71 × 10−12 M2 for Zn2+-PAR2; [41]); (b) Fe2+ with ferene, 3-(2-pyridyl)-5,6-bis(2-(5-furyl sulfonic acid)-1,2,4-triazine, iron standard (Specpure™; Fisher), and absorbance at 593 nm (A593; ε593= 34 500 M−1cm−1, KD= 1.26 × 10−15 M; [42]). PAR alone (100 μM) released Zn2+ from the 10 μM Zn2+-GAF domain, and KD was estimated for Zn2+-PexR GAF domain (P) binding using KD=([Zn-PAR2][P])/([Zn-P][PAR]2β2). β2= 1/KD for Zn-PAR2, Zn-PAR2 was estimated from A492 and the rest by mass balance from the total PAR and Zn-GAF domain [43]. Aerobically purified PexR GAF (10 μM) was exposed to 100 mM H2O2 (30 min, RT), and Zn2+ release was determined using 100 μM PAR and A492. Reconstituted PexR Fe2+-GAF-AAA+ (10 μM) was exposed to 100 μM H2O2 (30 min, buffer without DTT, anaerobic conditions, RT), dialyzed, and Fe2+ estimated using ferene and A593.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

DNA probes of ahpC, katB, and rlcC upstream segments (native or with specified mutations) were PCR-amplified using specific primer pairs with one 5′-end 32P-labeled T4 polynucleotide kinase (Takara) and purified using Sephadex G-25 DNA grade (Cytiva) and High Pure PCR Product Purification Kit (Roche). Each probe (1.2 nM, ∼13 000 counts per min) was incubated for 30 min/30°C with required concentrations of PexR or PexRΔGAF in 20 μl (80 mM potassium glutamate, 15 mM NaCl, 9 mM Tris–HCl, 15 mM HEPES, pH 7.9, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 200 ng/μl BSA, 1 μg sheared salmon sperm DNA non-specific competitor) and loaded onto 4% or 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gels (37.5:1 v/v acrylamide-bisacrylamide) pre-run for 30 min in 0.5× TBE (45 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 45 mM boric acid, 1 mM EDTA). After electrophoresis (2–2.5 h/200 V/30 mA/4°C), gels were vacuum-dried and scanned using Personal Molecular Imager™ FX with Quantity One 4.4 software (Bio-Rad).

Dithiobis(succinimidylpropionate)/DSP cross-linking

Purified protein (70 μl, 10 μM) in 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5 obtained by buffer exchange with Amicon Ultra-4 (10 kDa or 30 kDa MWCO) was mixed with freshly prepared DSP (Pierce) stock solutions in DMSO at 1:10, 1:25, or 1:100 protein:DSP ratio with MgATP (0.2 mM), as required, and incubated for 1 h at RT with gentle shaking. The reaction was quenched with 35 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5 (15 min), mixed with 4× loading buffer without β-ME, and 25 μl samples were subject to electrophoresis (2 h/200 V/30 mA) in 4%–12% Bis-Tris precast gels with MES buffer (Bio-Rad) and gels were stained with Coomassie Blue.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Samples were dialyzed thrice against isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 2 mM β-ME, 2 mM MgCl2). Buffer in the final step was used to prepare fresh ATP stock solutions (concentration from absorbance at 260 nm, ε260= 15 400 M−1cm−1). Protein (105–120 μM, 200 μl) or ITC buffer (for heat of dilution) in a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC system (Malvern Panalytical) cell was titrated with thirteen 2 μl injections of 1 mM MgATP at 150 s intervals with stirring (300 rpm) at 25°C. The mean of three independent titrations was fit to a single-site binding model using PEAQ-ITC analysis software to estimate the binding stoichiometry (N), dissociation constant (KD), and enthalpy (ΔH).

Cryo-EM sample preparation

In a 4°C MBRAUN anaerobic chamber, GAF-AAA+ protein from the main peak in a Superdex200 16/60 SEC run was collected and diluted to a concentration of 2.2 mg/ml in lysis buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM DTT). The protein was loaded into a chameleon sample vial and sealed in an airtight container for removal from the MBRAUN chamber and transport to the chameleon (SPT Labtech) instrument at the Cryo-EM Facility at MIT.nano. Protein in the vial was then immediately aspirated into the chameleon dispenser. Self-wicking 1.2 μm hole, 0.8 μm spacing holey-carbon grids (SPT Labtech) were glow-discharged at −12 mA for 85 s using the chameleon on-board glow discharger (SPT Labtech), and the cryo-EM specimen was prepared in 1-stripe mode using a dispense-to-plunge time of 54 ms (humidity 79%) and stored under liquid nitrogen.

Cryo-EM sample screening and data collection

Samples were screened on an FEI Talos Arctica G2 Cryo 200 kV transmission electron microscope equipped with a Falcon 3EC camera, and data were collected using a Titan Krios G3i 300 kV Cryo transmission electron microscope equipped with a Gatan K3 Direct Detector and Thermo Fisher Scientific EPU automated data collection software at the Cryo-EM Facility at MIT.nano. A dataset was collected at a nominal magnification of 130 000× in super-resolution mode and binned by 2 during collection, resulting in a pixel size of 0.654 Å. In total, 13 320 movies were collected with 50 frames, 50.44 e−/Å2 total exposure, and a defocus range of −0.25 to −2.00 μm. Data collection and 3D reconstruction statistics are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Cryo-EM data processing

A total of 13 320 collected movies were imported into cryoSPARC (v4.5.3) [44] (Supplementary Fig. S1). The frames of each dose-fractionated image stack were corrected for beam-induced motion using Patch Motion Correction, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters for each motion-corrected micrograph were estimated using Patch CTF Estimation. Micrographs with a CTF fit resolution greater than 8 Å were discarded using Manually Curate Exposures after which 12 713 micrographs (∼95% of total micrographs) were kept for downstream processing. Next, blob picker was used to pick 14 925 529 particles within the diameter range of 60–120 Å, and those with Normalized Cross Correlation (NCC) scores higher than 0.190 and local power scores lower than 424 were kept for extraction using a box size of 384 pix (Fourier cropped to 192 pix) and sorted into 200 2D class averages. Seven 2D classes containing 394 740 particles were selected and used as templates for template picking (120 Å particle diameter). The template picker yielded 13 561 535 particles that were filtered to keep particles with NCC scores higher than 0.270 and local power scores lower than 650. Particles were then extracted (384 pix, Fourier cropped to 192 pix) and the 10 460 532 that remained were 2D classified for two rounds (200 classes per round). Finally, a total of 387 128 particles were selected and used to generate two-class ab initio 3D models using no imposed symmetry (C1). Both classes showed an overall shape that is consistent with a PexR GAF-AAA+ dimer. Particles from class 1 (203 270 particles) were imported to RELION-5.0 [45] using csparc2star.py as part of the pyem v0.5 package [46] and an initial model was created and used for 3D classification (3 classes). Particles from the class with extra density (class 1, 11 753 particles) were discarded, and those from the rest of the two classes (191 517 particles) were used for the final 3D refinement, yielding a 7.85-Å resolution 3D reconstruction. A mask was generated and used to perform post-processing (map sharpening), yielding a 7.40-Å resolution 3D model (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). RELION-5.0 software suite, pyem v0.5, and 3DFSC programs were installed and configured by SBGrid [47].

Results

Myxococcus xanthus lacks bona fide OxyR or PerR homologs

To delve into the M. xanthus peroxide stress response, we first examined the large M. xanthus genome (9.3 Mb) for OxyR or PerR homologs. PSI-BLAST searches with E. coli OxyR as query yielded various M. xanthus LTTR proteins, but none among the six best hits (E-value < 1e−15; >80% query cover; 23%–34% sequence identity) had a Cys equivalent to the essential OxyR H2O2-sensing CP (C199), and only two (MXAN_1575, MXAN_1683) had a Cys that aligned with OxyR CR C208 (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Moreover, neighboring genes had no apparent antioxidant-related functions, suggesting that these LTTRs may have regulatory roles other than in the response to H2O2. The search with B. subtilis PerR as query yielded one hit with E-value < 1e−10, corresponding to a Fur family protein (MXAN_3702; E-value < 4e−22; >89% query cover; 28% sequence identity). However, the two PerR Asp residues (D85 and D104) involved in iron coordination are replaced by Glu in MXAN_3702, and the PerR Asn (N61) crucial for operator binding is replaced by Arg (Supplementary Fig. S3B), as in many non-PerR Fur proteins [19, 49]. Thus, it appeared unlikely that OxyR or PerR regulate the peroxide stress response in M. xanthus.

Deletion of ahpC provokes pleiotropic effects and enhances tolerance to exogenous H2O2

AhpC is a key guardian against endogenous H2O2 in most bacteria and is usually regulated by OxyR or PerR [5–9], but presumably not in M. xanthus, as noted above. We therefore analyzed the cellular role of its AhpC homolog (locus tag MXAN_1564) and its regulation. For this analysis, we generated an in-frame deletion of ahpC (ΔahpC) using a two-step allele exchange strategy in which a merodiploid with both wild-type (WT) and ΔahpC alleles is plated under conditions that select for haploid colonies retaining just one of the two alleles (see the “Materials and methods” section). Since M. xanthus exposed to light is subject to 1O2-induced photooxidative stress, which causes cells to change from yellow to orange-red due to carotenoid biosynthesis [4, 27, 29], cell growth was performed in the dark. Colonies with the ΔahpC allele correlated with a smaller size and, interestingly, had an orange color (Fig. 1A), compared to the larger yellow WT colonies. The ΔahpC cells grew slower and with more cellular debris than the WT in liquid media (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S4A), suggesting higher mortality, and exhibited plating defects (smaller, unevenly distributed colonies; and reduced plating efficiency, which required plating at high cell densities for colony formation; Supplementary Fig. S4B). The genetically encoded HyPer7 H2O2-specific sensor [50] indicated ∼5-fold more endogenous H2O2 in the ΔahpC strain than in the WT (Fig. 1C) and, in disk inhibition assays, the ΔahpC strain exhibited enhanced tolerance to exogenous H2O2 (Fig. 1D) accompanied by bubble formation, suggesting a compensatory induction of a catalase. We found that conditional ahpC expression at a heterologous site from a vanillate-inducible \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\rm P}_{van}$\end{document} promoter, used to complement deletions of genes including those essential in M. xanthus [37], did not rescue the WT behavior to H2O2 treatment (Fig. 1D) nor reverse the plating defects (Supplementary Fig. S4B), suggesting ahpC expression from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\rm P}_{van}$\end{document}is suboptimal for proper cellular function. By contrast, reintroducing ahpC at the same heterologous site with its upstream promoter-containing intergenic region (PahpC; 488 bp) rescued the plating defects (Supplementary Fig. S4B) and restored sensitivity to exogenous H2O2 (Fig. 1D), ruling out the possibility that the observed phenotypes are due to polar effects of the ahpC deletion on downstream genes. Pyruvate, a glucose metabolite that reportedly quenches H2O2 and reverses plating defects of ahpC mutants in some bacteria [51], also alleviated the high-density plating requirement of the M. xanthus ΔahpC strain (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

To further investigate the pleiotropic effects of deleting ahpC, we compared the transcriptomes of the ∆ahpC and WT strains, both grown in the dark. In the ∆ahpC strain, ∼25% of M. xanthus genome annotations were differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with ∼13% upregulated, ∼12% downregulated, and about half encoding hypothetical proteins of unknown functions (Fig. 1E). The log2 fold change (log2FC) was ≤5 for almost all downregulated DEGs, but >5 for many upregulated DEGs (Fig. 1E), some of which were validated by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. S5A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5B) linked some upregulated DEGs to DNA replication-recombination-repair and sulfur relay pathways and downregulated DEGs to primary metabolism. Consistent with the role of AhpC against peroxide stress, many upregulated DEGs are expected to be directly or indirectly related to oxidative stress, which is often linked to iron and sulfur metabolism [7–9] and protein damage from methionine and cysteine sulfur group oxidation [52]. Accordingly, upregulated DEGs included (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. S5, and Supplementary Table S4): (a) myxochelin siderophore biosynthesis and iron transport proteins, such as the catecholate siderophore TonB_dependent receptor CirA/MXAN_6911 [53]; (b) heme breakdown (heme oxygenase) and transport; (c) sulphur/methionine metabolism and redox state, like sulphur assimilation-transport-transfer and Fe-S cluster assembly proteins, methionine import, methionine sulfoxide reductases for oxidized methionine repair, and a gene (map) encoding an N-terminal methionine excision peptidase; (d) a gene (rclC) possibly linked to resistance against H2O2-derived hypothiocyanite [54, 55]. The carQRS operon, a key early regulatory locus in the photooxidative response, and various downstream regulatory and carotenoid biosynthesis genes [4, 22, 27–29] were also upregulated, which might explain the orange color developed by ∆ahpC colonies in the dark.

Importantly, the most upregulated gene (MXAN_4389) encodes a predicted periplasmic monofunctional heme catalase annotated as KatB (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S4). The only other known M. xanthus catalase, which is an essential cytoplasmic type II heme hydroperoxidase annotated as KatE (encoded by MXAN_6188; [39]), was not a DEG. Enzymatic properties of both M. xanthus catalases and their roles during vegetative growth, development, and predation were recently examined [31, 39], but regulation of their expression remains unknown, especially at a transcriptional level. The next most upregulated gene also occurs in the katB operon and encodes AnkB, an ankyrin repeat protein required for KatB activity [56]. Upregulation of katB-ankB in the ∆ahpC strain likely enables its observed tolerance to H2O2 (Fig. 1D), hinting at a H2O2-driven regulatory mechanism. Also upregulated, albeit relatively less than katB-ankB, were ahpD (MXAN_1563) of the ahpCD operon and dps (MXAN_1562) downstream, which, respectively, encode a reductase that works with AhpC [7–9] and iron-sequestering miniferritin Dps (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S4), suggesting that ahpCD-dps expression is also subject to H2O2 stress control. We noted that MXAN_1565, immediately upstream of and oriented opposite to ahpC, was also upregulated in the ∆ahpC strain. MXAN_1565 encodes a predicted bEBP that we named PexR, with an N-terminal GAF domain present in group III bEBPs and in many other signaling and sensory proteins in all kingdoms of life to respond to a variety of cues [34, 57]. We therefore investigated whether and how PexR is linked to the peroxide stress response in M. xanthus.

Lack of AhpC and PexR or KatB causes synthetic lethality

To understand the possible interrelationships among ahpC, katB, and pexR, we sought to generate strains with in-frame deletions of katB (ΔkatB) or pexR (ΔpexR), as well as the double-deleted ∆ahpC ΔkatB, ∆ahpC ΔpexR, and ΔkatB ΔpexR strains. We readily obtained the ΔkatB, ΔpexR, and ΔkatB ΔpexR strains, which exhibited neither the growth nor the plating defects observed for the ∆ahpC strain. By contrast, the same two-step allele exchange method failed to generate the ∆ahpC ΔkatB or the ∆ahpC ΔpexR strains, hinting that lack of AhpC together with either KatB or PexR is lethal in M. xanthus. Consistent with this result, we could obtain ΔahpC ΔkatB colonies if a Pvan::katB copy was expressed at a heterologous site. This ΔahpC ΔkatB Pvan::katB strain grew, albeit poorly, under permissive (with vanillate) but not under restrictive conditions (Fig. 2A). Likewise, expressing a Pvan::pexR copy (as PexR, or with N- or C-terminal FLAG epitope tag for immunoblot analyses), we could generate the ΔahpC ΔpexR strain (Fig. 2A).

Synthetic lethality when ahpC is deleted with either katB or pexR suggested a link between the latter two that, we reasoned, might be PexR-mediated katB upregulation under peroxide stress. To investigate this possibility, the ΔahpC ΔpexR Pvan::pexR-flag strain was grown in the presence of vanillate, which was then removed to halt pexR expression. Aliquots were analyzed at different times for katB expression, which decreased concomitantly with declining PexR levels (Fig. 2B), demonstrating that PexR mediates katB upregulation under endogenous H2O2 stress. We next checked the effects of exogenous H2O2. Exposing the WT strain to a 10-min pulse of H2O2 induced the expression of katB by ∼330-fold and that of ahpC by a lower ∼10-fold (Fig. 2C). The same 10-min exposure to exogenous H2O2 induced katB and ahpC expression in the WT to ∼230-fold and ∼3-fold higher levels, respectively, than in the ΔpexR strain; and expressing PexR or its FLAG-tagged versions in the ΔpexR strain restored H2O2-induced katB and ahpC expression to WT levels (Fig. 2D). PexR is therefore required to activate katB and ahpC expression in response to exogenous or endogenous H2O2 (ΔahpC strain). Lethality of the ΔahpC ΔpexR strain may therefore be the consequence of the combined effects of enhanced intracellular H2O2 levels, resulting from the removal of AhpC, together with an inability to induce katB expression.

PexR directly mediates σ54-dependent activation of katB and ahpC, and downregulates σA-dependent ahpC expression

In the ΔahpC strain, PexR may directly or indirectly activate katB expression and upregulate ahpD of the ahpCD operon and pexR. To examine expression regulation, we generated constructs with the entire intergenic regions upstream of katB, ahpC, and pexR (PkatB, PahpC, and PpexR, respectively) fused to a lacZ reporter and introduced them into different genetic backgrounds. In the WT, promoter activity was highest for PahpC::lacZ, negligible for PkatB::lacZ, and in between for PpexR::lacZ (Fig. 3A). All three promoter probes recapitulated their upregulation in the ∆ahpC strain inferred from the transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S4), with katB upregulated ∼400-fold (Fig. 3A), suggesting that the selected intergenic regions contain the necessary determinants for proper promoter control in response to peroxide. None of the probes exhibited increased promoter activity relative to WT in the ΔkatB strain (Fig. 3A) pointing to AhpC as the primary defense player against peroxide under normal growth conditions. Interestingly, expression from PkatB and PpexR was not significantly affected by deleting pexR, but that from PahpC increased ∼20-fold relative to the WT (Fig. 3A), indicating PexR negatively regulates ahpC expression under normal growth conditions.

To examine if PexR acts directly or indirectly at selected promoters, we used the Pvan::PexR-FLAG ΔpexR strain to perform ChIP-qPCR analysis (Fig. 3B). PexR enrichment was observed at PkatB, PahpC, and PpexR, but not at the promoter region of rclC (PrclC), which was also upregulated in the ΔahpC strain, indicating a direct effect of PexR on the expression of PkatB, PahpC, and PpexR, but not of PrclC. Inspection of the PkatB segment revealed sequences conforming to consensus −24 and −12 elements of σ54-dependent promoters (yTGGCACGrnnnTTGCw; uppercase: highly conserved; n: nonconserved; y: pyrimidine; r: purine; w: A, T; [34]; Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S6A). Both 5′-RACE analysis (Supplementary Fig. S6A) and mutation of the putative −24 or −12 regions (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S6B) validated the assigned σ54-dependent promoter. Upstream of this promoter we identified three tandem repeats (R1, R2, R3) of a perfect interrupted palindrome (CACCG-n6-CGGTG) and two pseudopalindromic repeats (R4, R5) (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S6A) that could constitute multiple binding sites for PexR. Notably, PrclC lacks such repeats of dyad symmetry, which can explain why PexR was not enriched at this promoter in the ChIP-qPCR analysis (Fig. 3B).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) revealed that purified PexR or PexRΔGAF (lacking the N-terminal GAF domain) binds to PkatB (Fig. 3E and F) but not, as expected, to PrclC (Supplementary Fig. S7A), which thus serves as a negative control. The ladder-like pattern of retarded bands with increasing protein concentrations at PkatB indicates stepwise formation of higher-order DNA–protein complexes. Simultaneously mutating the 5′-ends (R1–3mut5′) or 3′-ends (R1–3mut3′) of R1–R2–R3 abolished the high PkatB::lacZ activity in the ΔahpC strain, but such mutations in R4–R5 (R4–5mut5′, R4–5mut3′) had no effect (Fig. 3D). And in EMSA, mutations R1–3mut5′ or R1–3mut3′, but not R4–5mut5′or R4–5mut3′, in probe PkatB impaired binding to PexR (Supplementary Fig. S7B). R1, R2, and R3 dyad symmetry suggests that PexR (calculated monomer mass of 58.2 kDa) likely binds as dimers, but loss of signal precluded size-exclusion (SEC) analysis of its oligomeric state (see below). In sum, our data indicate that PexR is directly involved in activating σ54-dependent expression from PkatB under peroxide stress, with its UASs corresponding to the katB proximal repeats.

For PahpC, 5′-RACE with the WT identified a possible transcription start site (TSS) in a region where we assigned a putative σA-type promoter (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S8A). To experimentally validate this TSS, we fused a lacZ probe to PahpC_1, a shorter (255 bp) region, whose activities in both the WT and the ΔahpC strains were similar to those of the longer 488-bp PahpC::lacZ probe (Fig. 3H). Mutating the possible −10 or −35 promoter elements abolished PahpC_1::lacZ activity in the WT but did not affect its high activity in the ΔahpC strain (Fig. 3H), suggesting that ahpC expresses from the σA-type promoter under normal growth conditions and from another promoter under peroxide stress. Accordingly, 5′-RACE with the ΔahpC strain yielded a TSS downstream of a predicted σ54-dependent promoter (Supplementary Fig. S8A), which was validated by mutating its −24 or −12 regions (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. S8B). We identified at PahpC six pseudopalindromic repeats resembling those at PkatB, with a highly conserved 5′ CACCG but with mismatches in the 3′-end region (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S8A). Three (R1–R2–R3) are within PahpC_1, upstream of the ahpC σ54-dependent promoter and overlap with its σA-type promoter, while the other three (R4–R5–R6) are in the intergenic region (PpexR_1) close to the pexR TSS, with the stretch R5–R6 overlapping with a possible σA-type promoter (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Figs S8A and S9). Consistent with this, PexR was enriched at PahpC_1 and PpexR_1 in vivo (Fig. 3B) and exhibited binding to both promoter probes in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S10A), besides to full-length PahpC (Fig. 3E and F), suggesting that three such tandem repeats suffice for PexR–DNA binding, as observed at PkatB. Mutating only the 5′-end of R2 and R3 (R2-3mut5′), to leave the promoter elements intact (Supplementary Fig. S8B), provoked higher reporter expression in the WT and lower in the ΔahpC strain (Fig. 3H), and impaired binding to PexR in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S10B). The results are therefore in accord with PexR directly controlling ahpC expression, as a repressor of its σA-type promoter under normal growth but as an activator of its σ54 promoter under peroxide stress.

The GAF domain negatively controls PexR activity and is required for H2O2 sensing

The PexR AlphaFold (AF) structure (Fig. 4A) predicts a GAF domain linked to the central activator AAA+ domain via a long helix (residues 148–193) whose N-terminal half is part of the GAF fold (denoted the signaling helix, as it channels R domain signal sensing for allosteric activation of the AAA+ domain in some bEBPs [58]). A structurally disordered ∼45-residue linker connects the PexR AAA+ domain to the DBD. We tested the possible role in vivo of the GAF domain, which EMSA analyses indicated as dispensable for PexR–DNA binding in vitro, since truncated PexR lacking its GAF domain (PexRΔGAF) binds to probes PkatB and PahpC like full-length PexR (compare Fig. 3E and F). Whereas the strain expressing PexR-FLAG from Pvan in the ΔpexR background did not exhibit catalase activity (assessed qualitatively using the foam assay) under normal growth conditions regardless of the inducer concentration used, expressing PexRΔGAF-FLAG displayed catalase activity even at low inducer concentration (0.005 mM), despite being far less abundant than the full-length protein (Fig. 4B). Consistent with this, RT-qPCR analysis indicated that katB was expressed constitutively in the PexRΔGAF-FLAG strain and at ∼4-fold higher levels than in the PexR-FLAG strain exposed to exogenous H2O2(Fig. 4C).

Transcriptomic analysis of the ∆pexR Pvan::pexRΔGAF-flag strain with and without inducer further confirmed that deletion of the GAF domain leads to H2O2-independent expression of katB-ankB (Fig. 4D). This global analysis also revealed far fewer DEGs than those observed in the transcriptomic data for the ∆ahpC strain (compare Fig. 4D with Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S1), suggesting that the PexR regulon is rather small. Besides katB-ankB (and the vanillate-induced pexRΔGAF-flag), the rclC gene was also upregulated, but this is likely an indirect effect because PexR does not bind to PrclC in vivo (Fig. 3B) or in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Among the upregulated DEGs, ahpCD was not observed despite its direct regulation by PexR. The probable explanation is that ahpCD expression is already high without inducer in the ∆pexR Pvan::pexRΔGAF-flag strain, since the ahpC σA-type promoter is no longer subject to repression by PexR.

Expressing the FLAG-GAF domain from an IPTG-inducible promoter (PIPTG) in the ΔpexR Pvan::pexRΔGAF-flag strain downregulated katB expression, which persisted on exposure to H2O2 (Fig. 4C). Inability of exogenous H2O2 to counteract the inhibition of PexR activity by the GAF domain in trans could be due to katB expression and the consequent quenching of exogenous H2O2. This possibility was ruled out by performing the same analysis in a ΔpexR ΔkatB genetic background and monitoring ankB expression (Fig. 4E). The inhibitory action of the GAF domain in trans likely involves physical interactions between GAF and PexRΔGAF, which were indeed observed in bacterial two-hybrid analysis (Fig. 4F). Thus, the GAF domain inhibits PexR activity in the absence of H2O2, and proper signaling of H2O2 requires the domain to interact in cis with the rest of the protein.

A Zn2+/Fe2+-binding center in the PexR dimeric GAF domain determines H2O2-sensing

GAF domains typically use heme, nonheme iron, or iron-sulfur clusters bound to Cys, His, and Asp residues to sense reactive oxygen or nitrogen species [57]. ICP-MS analysis of aerobically purified PexR indicated binding to zinc and to iron, each at ∼0.3:1 metal:protein stoichiometry, but no metal in the constitutively active PexRΔGAF (Table 1). This metal analysis indicates that the GAF domain binds to zinc or iron, and AlphaFold3 (AF3) predicts that multiple amino acids that are capable of binding metals are located near each other in the GAF domain (Fig. 5A). We further confirmed metal binding with purified GAF-AAA+ (without the DBD) or GAF (with just this domain) PexR truncated variants.

PexR GAF-AAA+ was found to bind ATP with ∼1:1 stoichiometry (from ITC; Supplementary Fig. S11A), contained ∼0.6:1 zinc:protein and ∼0.2:1 iron:protein, and could be reconstituted with Fe2+ anaerobically (Table 1). Chemical cross-linking in vitro followed by gel electrophoresis revealed that GAF-AAA+ is primarily a dimer, with or without ATP, but coexists with possible tetramers and hexamers (Supplementary Fig. S11B), indicating that PexR can adopt higher-order oligomeric states. In SEC, GAF-AAA+ eluted with an apparent mass (Mr) of 145 kDa (Fig. 5B), compared to a value of 50.3 kDa calculated for the GAF-AAA+ monomer, suggesting elongated dimers. Additional support for a dimeric structure comes from AlphaFold3, which predicts a dimeric model for GAF-AAA+ with confidence scores of ipTM = 0.7, pTM = 0.72, and from single-particle cryo-EM analysis of anaerobically reconstituted Fe2+-GAF-AAA+, which has provided 2D class averages and a 3D cryo-EM map that are consistent with the dimeric AF3-predicted model (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Figs S1, S2, and S12). Although the resolution of the cryo-EM map is too low to assign any secondary structure, it reveals the overall shape and volume of Fe2+-GAF-AAA+ as dumbbell-shaped with two globular regions, one larger than the other, corresponding well with the AF3 dimer (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Figs S1, S2, and S12). Altogether, these data indicate that PexR is a dimeric σ54 activator capable of higher-order oligomerization and ATP binding, in line with PexR conserving the key bEBP determinants for ATP binding and hydrolysis, oligomerization, and σ54 contacts [34]. Hence, it is reasonable to assume that PexR shares the ATPase activity of typical bEBPs. Purified PexR GAF eluted in SEC with an Mr of 47 kDa (Fig. 5B), compared to a calculated GAF monomer value of 20 kDa, suggesting dimers, which was supported by chemical cross-linking data (Supplementary Fig. S11B). Furthermore, metal analysis yielded ∼0.8:1 zinc:protein and ∼0.13 iron:protein for the GAF domain, which could also be anaerobically reconstituted with Fe2+ (Table 1).

To investigate the role of metal binding by the GAF domain in PexR function, we probed the AF3 predicted metal binding pocket (Fig. 5A) by mutational analysis. Single Ala substitutions of H102, C122, D140, and H120 and the H102A-H120A double mutant activated katB expression even without exogenous H2O2, whereas single Ala substitutions of D108 (part of the predicted pocket), D111, or C144 (both away from the pocket) had negligible effects (Fig. 5D; immunoblots confirmed all mutants were stably expressed). Zinc contents were lower in the H102A, C122A, and D140A GAF variants and negligible in H120A (or H102A-H120A), and iron contents were essentially null (Table 1), confirming that these residues are important for PexR metal binding and suggesting that metal release leads to PexR activation. This finding was supported by colorimetric assays using 4-(2-pyridylazo)resorcinol/PAR or ferene to determine Zn2+ or Fe2+ content, respectively. With the GAF domain, PAR alone (dissociation constant KD= 7.08 × 10−13 M2) yielded the characteristic Zn2+-PAR absorbance peak at 492 nm, allowing a KD estimate of (0.27 ± 0.09) nM for Zn2+-GAF binding, and exposure to H2O2 released the rest of bound Zn2+ (Fig. 5E and F). Exposure of Fe2+-reconstituted GAF-AAA+ to H2O2 under anaerobic conditions also released the metal (Fig. 5F). Overall, the data suggest a molecular mechanism for regulating PexR activity in which H2O2 sensing leads to metal loss and relief of PexR autoinhibition by its GAF domain (Fig. 6).

Evolutionary conservation of PexR regulation of the peroxide stress response

To examine whether the PexR-based mechanism extends beyond M. xanthus, we searched for PexR homologs in 78 available genomes listed as NCBI reference genomes and in Minicystis rosea (not listed as a reference genome) in phylum Myxococcota, 55 in suborder Cystobacterineae (includes M. xanthus), 14 in Sorangiineae, and 9 in Nannocystineae (Supplementary Table S5). PexR homologs (overall sequence identities 47%–98%, ≥90% coverage; GAF domain identities 51%–98%) with the M. xanthus PexR H2O2-sensing, metal-binding residues conserved occur in most Cystobacterineae and Nannocystineae genomes analyzed, and in about half of the Sorangiineae genomes (Fig. 7A and B, Supplementary Fig. S13, and Supplementary Table S5). Most species have flanking, divergently transcribed pexR and ahpC, with conserved σ54- and σA-type ahpC promoters and three PexR-type UASs (Supplementary Fig. S14A) in intergenic regions typically <300 bp, with larger pexR-ahpC intergenic regions occurring only in M. xanthus and three other Myxococcus species (Supplementary Table S5). In myxobacteria where pexR flanks ahpC (as in M. xanthus), pexR may be transcribed from a σA-type promoter as leaderless mRNA (i.e. ≤5 nucleotides between TSS and start codon; [59]) (Supplementary Figs S9 and S14B). Our analysis revealed that KatE (which is essential in M. xanthus [39]) is conserved in most Myxococcota, and KatB with AnkB is largely restricted to Cystobacterineae and, when present, likely subject to PexR/σ54-dependent regulation (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Figs S13 and S14, and Supplementary Table S5).

Remarkably, in some myxobacteria, ahpC is flanked by a divergent oxyR (Vulgatibacter incomptus and Anaeromyxobacter, both in suborder Cystobacterineae; and Sorangium, in suborder Sorangiineae) or perR (Polyangium, suborder Sorangiineae) with another perR associated to a rbr gene encoding a rubrerythrin-like protein of the nonheme diiron center ferritin-type superfamily, which reduce H2O2 to water [60]. Like catalases, heme-based cytochrome c peroxidases (CCPs) detoxify H2O2 to water and are OxyR-regulated in many bacteria [61]. We found oxyR-ccp in two Sorangiineae (M. rosea and Chondromyces apiculatus). But in other members of this suborder (Chondromyces and some Polyangium) and in one Nannocystineae (Haliangium ochraceum), pexR flanks a divergently expressed ccp (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. S13, and Supplementary Table S5), suggesting PexR/σ54-regulated ccp expression. Many Cystobacterineae (but not M. xanthus or other Myxococcus) and a few Sorangiineae and Nannocystineae have an oxyR-katA-ankA cluster that encodes another monofunctional catalase KatA, suggesting OxyR-mediated regulation. This cluster in Cystobacterineae almost always coexists with PexR/σ54-regulated ahpCD and katB-ankB (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. S14, and Supplementary Table S5).

A search for PexR homologs beyond Myxococcota revealed ~60 hits (48%–92% identity, ≥90% coverage) in unclassified bacterial species of Acidobacteriota, Nitrospirota, Plantomycetota, and Pseudomonadota, with pexR often in the genomic vicinity of ahpC, rbr, or ccp (Supplementary Table S6). The only defined species belong to phylum Acidobacteriota, whose members are physiologically diverse, ubiquitous (especially in soils), and generally aerobic uncultivable Gram-negative bacteria. Two of these Gram-negative bacteria are in the order Acanthopleuribacterales (Sulfidibacter corallicola, Acanthopleuribacter pedis) and two others are in Holophagales (Geothrix rubra, Mesoterricola silvestris) (Supplementary Fig. S15). Thus, regulation of peroxide response genes by PexR/σ54 appears to operate beyond Myxococcota.

Discussion

Myxococcus xanthus and other myxobacteria with obligately oxygen-based lifestyles are inevitably exposed to peroxide, which they must combat for normal growth and survival, but their response to this stress remained largely unexplored. Peroxiredoxins and catalases are cellular bulwarks against peroxide stress, and their expression in bacteria is typically regulated by OxyR or PerR, which sense and transduce the H2O2 signal via redox-active Cys and iron-mediated His oxidation, respectively [5–9]. Our study unveils a noncanonical, OxyR/PerR-independent mechanism in M. xanthus orchestrated by PexR, the first bEBP that senses H2O2 to activate expression of a peroxiredoxin (AhpC) and a catalase (KatB) (Fig. 6). Two bEBPs in obligate anaerobes, Desulfovibrio vulgaris OrpR and Clostridium beijerinckii AdhR, act with σ54 in redox responses but neither function like PexR [62, 63]. OrpR activates σ54-dependent expression of cell division genes under anaerobic conditions and is inactivated by its N-terminal Per-Arnt-Sim (PAS) [4Fe–4S] cluster domain sensing increased redox potential [62]. AdhR, a σ54 activator of alcohol dehydrogenase genes under anaerobic or iron-replete conditions, becomes inactive upon sensing oxygen stress or iron limitation via an N-terminal nonheme Fe2+-binding GAF-PAS domain [63]. Escherichia coli bEBP RtcR, with an N-terminal CRISPR-associated Rossmann fold (CARF) cyclic oligonucleotide binding sensory domain, activates a σ54-driven RNA repair operon in response to peroxide stress, DNA damage, or nitrogen limitation, but does not sense H2O2 or control ahpC or catalase expression [64].

PexR is a bEBP with dual repressor-activator function. Thus, PexR binding to its UASs upstream of σ54-dependent promoters of ahpC and katB: (i) activates their expression under peroxide stress; (ii) downregulates an ahpC σA-type promoter in the absence of stress, presumably through steric occlusion of RNAP, since the UASs overlap with the σA-type promoter and TSS (Fig. 6). A bEBP acting as repressor is unusual but not unprecedented and generally involves inactive/resting state bEBP binding to UASs that overlap with σA-type promoters near the σ54-dependent promoter [65–67]. Expression of ahpC is notable even under uninduced conditions despite downregulation by PexR. This housekeeping ahpC expression ensures normal M. xanthus growth, since the ∆ahpC strain grew slower and exhibited plating defects and other pleiotropic phenotypes, probably because of the increased intracellular H2O2 levels in this strain. Unlike the ∆ahpC strain, those with pexR, katB, or both deleted appeared normal, indicating that AhpC is the primary scavenger of endogenous peroxide in M. xanthus under stress-free growth conditions. Deleting pexR or katB in the ∆ahpC strain, however, caused synthetic lethality, indicating that AhpC and its PexR-mediated co-regulation with KatB are critical in M. xanthus. KatB is a predicted periplasmic catalase, possibly explaining why the ∆ahpC strain is highly tolerant to exogenous H2O2, and why katB upregulation in this strain only partially offsets its cytoplasmic H2O2 surge and pleiotropic phenotype. Compensatory ahpC-catalase expression also features in various other obligate or facultative aerobic bacteria with OxyR- or PerR-regulated peroxide responses, but these remain viable despite simultaneous ahpC, catalase, and/or OxyR/PerR gene deletions [51, 68–72], suggesting that they may have more efficient and/or additional mechanisms against peroxide stress than M. xanthus.

Lack of AhpC results in altered expression of many genes in M. xanthus (Fig. 1E) that can be attributed to elevated intracellular H2O2 levels. H2O2 targets proteins with iron and iron-sulfur centers and generates highly reactive OH•, which inevitably damages cellular proteins and nucleic acids, and activates cellular protection/repair systems [6, 72]; and high katB expression entails strong demand for its heme cofactor. This may explain why in the ∆ahpC strain we observed upregulation of genes encoding systems for iron/heme homeostasis and uptake, as well as for protein repair/degradation and DNA repair/recombination. Also upregulated in the ∆ahpC strain were genes implicated in the M. xanthus photooxidative/1O2 stress response whose expression depends directly on the alternative extracytoplasmic function σ factor CarQ [4, 22, 27], revealing a link between the responses to 1O2 and to H2O2. Although the expression of many genes was affected in the ∆ahpC strain, our transcriptomics data with constitutively active PexRΔGAF (Fig. 4D) suggest that only a small subset of these, primarily katB-ankB and ahpCD, are directly controlled by PexR.

PexR binds to ATP and can undergo higher-order oligomeric transitions, like allosterically regulated bEBPs [34]. PexR’s N-terminal GAF domain is a dimeric H2O2 sensor and an intramolecular PexR inhibitor under uninduced conditions. Although the GAF domain can physically interact with the rest of the protein and mediate inhibition of PexR activity in trans, H2O2 signaling requires the GAF domain to be an integral part of PexR. This requirement implies that the signaling helix, which is part of the GAF fold and links it to the rest of PexR, must act in cis for proper signal transduction and allosteric activation of the AAA+ domain. The PexR GAF domain can bind to Zn2+ or nonheme Fe2+ and be reconstituted anaerobically with Fe2+. Conserved His, Asp, and Cys residues form a predicted metal-binding pocket in PexR GAF and single mutations of various of these residues affect PexR signal sensing, metal binding, and regulatory activity, as with the H2O2-sensing PerR and NO-sensing NorR GAF domain Fe2+ centers (Supplementary Fig. S16A; [16, 73, 74]), suggesting that the physiologically relevant metal in PexR may be Fe2+. If so, the Zn2+ in PexR could be a consequence of mismetalation, which is common in nonheme iron proteins because the affinity for zinc usually exceeds that for iron, and nonheme iron is readily oxidized and labile under aerobic conditions [75, 76]. A key feature of PerR H2O2 sensing is Fe2+-catalyzed His oxidation [16], but our attempts to probe His oxidation in PexR, using methods reported for PerR, have thus far been inconclusive. Hence, Zn2+ cannot be ruled out as the physiologically relevant metal in PexR. Reported Zn2+-based sensors such as Hsp33 and RsrA have high affinity (KD ∼10−17 M) Zn2+-Cys redox-active centers (four Cys in Hsp33, three in RsrA), which sense H2O2 by metal expulsion and disulfide bond formation [77]. Compared to Hsp33 and RsrA, the far lower affinity of PexR for Zn2+ (KD ∼10−10 M) would suggest greater sensitivity to H2O2. On the other hand, the PexR metal pocket contains only one Cys (C122), whose mutation affects H2O2 sensing in vivo and metal binding but whose redox partner, if any, is unknown. Future work, including structural elucidation of the molecular mechanism of PexR H2O2 sensing and activity, can resolve whether Fe2+ or Zn2+ is the relevant metal and its precise mode of action.

PexR, with an N-terminal GAF sensory domain, would belong to group III of the bEBP family, like NorR [34], with which it shares 33% overall sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. S16B). Both are autoinhibited in their resting states by their GAF domains that, despite only ∼19% sequence identity, have similar metal-binding pockets and can accommodate Fe2+ (Supplementary Fig. S16A). Furthermore, as observed with PexR, NorR binds to sites comprising three UASs, albeit with different sequences, but similarly positioned relative to one another and to the σ54-dependent promoter [78]. In both, the GAF domain senses and transduces a specific activating signal, H2O2 for PexR (based on the nature of the genes that PexR regulates), and NO for NorR. Hence, it is reasonable to propose that PexR and NorR are evolutionarily related but have been repurposed for different signal-specific σ54-dependent responses. Interestingly, although NorR evolved to sense NO, it can also be collaterally affected by H2O2, which has been shown to target the Fe2+ center in its GAF domain to modulate the NO response [79]. Likewise, the OxyR redox-active Cys and the PerR Fe2+ center, which evolved to sense H2O2, can react with NO, affecting their regulons [80, 81]. This reflects the shared ability of H2O2 and NO to target and modify transcription factors containing iron or redox-active Cys. Thus, PexR might also sense NO, like NorR, OxyR, or PerR. In addition, some other ROS, such as alkyl hydroperoxides, RSS like sulfanes, or RCS are reported to be sensed by OxyR or PerR [7, 30, 69, 83]. In our exploratory analysis with PexR, tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBuOOH or TBHP), but not the superoxide (O2−) generator paraquat, induced ahpC and katB expression in M. xanthus (Supplementary Fig. S17). This suggests that PexR likely senses TBHP besides H2O2, providing a springboard for future studies of inducer specificity. Despite the ability to sense other inducers, the high sensitivity of OxyR specifically to H2O2 and of NorR to NO inspired their use in the design of genetically encoded probes to sense H2O2 and NO, respectively [50, 82]. In this regard, our discovery of PexR may contribute an additional H2O2-sensing tool.

The distinct mechanisms and structural classes in peroxide stress response regulation represented by OxyR, PerR, and now PexR share some remarkable parallels with the three known regulatory strategies of RSS sensors, two of which are repressors of σ70-dependent expression, while the third, FisR, is a bEBP activator of σ54-dependent transcription [30, 84, 85]. Cupriavidus spp. FisR senses RSS via three R domain cysteines, although these are not generally conserved [30, 84, 85]. Diverse sensory-regulatory strategies might have evolved independently against RSS reflecting the need to detoxify various reactive derivatives of H2S and to exploit them in varied cellular signaling pathways [30], a scenario that may well apply to the emergence of the different mechanisms against peroxides analogous to those against RSS.

PexR-dependent regulation identified in M. xanthus represents a new paradigm broadly conserved in phylum Myxococcota, where 60 out of the 78 genomes analyzed contained a gene for PexR, associated with ahpCD in most cases and with ccp in some. It is therefore tempting to speculate that the pexR-ahpCD cluster was present in the Myxococcota ancestor and lost or subject to rearrangements in some members of the phylum. By contrast, the katB-ankB operon appears to have evolved specifically in the Myxococcota suborder Cystobacterineae, suggesting that the PexR-mediated σ54-dependent control might have originally emerged to upregulate ahpCD expression under peroxide stress and subsequently been co-opted to also upregulate katB-ankB expression and mount a more robust response against peroxide. Unlike M. xanthus, many other myxobacteria have OxyR associated with katA-ankA and co-existing frequently with pexR-ahpCD and katB-ankB. Interestingly, some myxobacteria, primarily in suborder Sorangiineae, are equipped with as many as two copies of PerR, one associated with ahpC and the other with rbr, sometimes co-existing with PexR or with OxyR linked to ahpC, katA-ankA, or ccp. This reveals a remarkable diversity of regulatory mechanisms in various species within just this single phylum. These diverse mechanisms might have been present in an early ancestor, with subsequent loss or divergence in extant myxobacteria, or alternatively, some might have been acquired by horizontal gene transfer. Our analysis thus reveals a complex evolutionary scenario in the occurrence of peroxide stress response regulatory mechanisms in phylum Myxococcota. The presence of multiple specific systems possibly endows these Gram-negative, predominantly obligate aerobic bacteria with a broader and more efficient response against peroxide stress and a fitness advantage. Finally, our analysis suggests that the PexR/σ54-dependent peroxide stress response regulatory mechanism might extend to less-characterized bacteria in phyla other than Myxococcota, such as in Acidobacteriota, Nitrospirota, Plantomycetota, and Pseudomonadota. Altogether, our study unmasks a new H2O2-sensing peroxide stress response regulatory mechanism, beyond the two existing OxyR and PerR paradigms in bacteria.
