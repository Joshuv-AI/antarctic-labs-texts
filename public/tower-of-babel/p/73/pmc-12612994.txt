Early stoppage of empirical antibiotic therapy in paediatric acute leukaemia with high-risk febrile neutropenia: a randomized, open-label, phase 3, non-inferiority trial
Authors: Santosh Kumar Chellapuram, Santosh Kumar Kn, Manraj Singh Sra, Rupak Kumar Giri, Deepam Pushpam, Atul Batra, Sreenivas Vishnubhatla, Shuvadeep Ganguly, Sameer Bakhshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEmpirical antibiotic therapy (EAT) is commonly continued until recovery of neutrophil counts in paediatric acute leukaemia with high-risk febrile neutropenia. This study assessed whether early discontinuation of EAT was non-inferior to the continuation of EAT until absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≥500/μL among children with high-risk febrile neutropenia.MethodsThis open-label, phase 3, non-inferiority, randomized trial was conducted between October 2018 and August 2024 and enrolled children aged 2–18 years with acute leukaemia experiencing high-risk febrile neutropenia during induction chemotherapy. Patients clinically stable and afebrile for ≥72 h on EAT, with ANC <500/μL and no documented infection, were randomized to either stop EAT or continue it until ANC ≥500/μL. The primary outcome was proportion of fever recurrence, with a non-inferiority margin of 15 percentage points. Secondary outcomes included antibiotic duration, hospital readmissions, use of third-line antibiotics, use of therapeutic antifungals, and mortality. This study is registered at Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2018/015994).FindingsOf 280 patients randomized, 278 were analysed (140 stop arm, 138 continuation arm). Median age (IQR) was 8 (5–14) years, with 34.2% being female. Fever recurrence occurred in 52.1% (73/170) and 47.1% (65/138) children in stop and continuation arms respectively. The difference in fever recurrence between the arms was 5.0 percentage points (90% CI: −4.8 to 14.8 percentage points), establishing non-inferiority of the stop arm. The median (IQR) days of EAT after randomization were shorter in the stop arm [4.2 (0–10) vs 11 (7.2–15); p < 0.0001]. Mortality, hospital readmission, third-line antibiotic use, or therapeutic antifungal use did not differ between the two arms.InterpretationEarly discontinuation of EAT in paediatric acute leukaemia with high-risk febrile neutropenia was non-inferior to continuation of EAT until recovery of ANC.FundingNone.Keywords: Febrile neutropenia, Paediatric leukaemia, Childhood cancer, Empirical antibiotic therapy, Antibiotic stewardship

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The 2023 update of the Clinical Practice Guideline by the International Pediatric Fever and Neutropenia Guideline Panel included a systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on strategies for managing febrile neutropenia in paediatric patients with cancer or undergoing haematopoietic cell transplantation. The review searched MEDLINE (including Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process & Other Non-Indexed Citations), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and PubMed in 2022. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration's tool. The review found 10 new RCTs, of which only 1 study included children with high-risk febrile neutropenia without documented infection and evaluated early discontinuation of empirical antibiotic therapy (EAT). A multicentre trial by Santolaya et al. including children with high-risk febrile neutropenia and documented respiratory viral infection found early antibiotic discontinuation non-inferior to continuation, though concerns remained about persistent viral detection and asymptomatic colonization. Consequently, the guideline reaffirmed the previous recommendation to continue EAT until evidence of marrow recovery in high-risk patients, citing the absence of direct trial evidence supporting early cessation in this subgroup. A new conditional recommendation permitted discontinuation of EAT at 48 h in afebrile, clinically stable patients with low-risk febrile neutropenia if blood cultures remained negative, even in the absence of marrow recovery. The guideline panel identified early discontinuation of antibiotics in high-risk febrile neutropenia as a critical knowledge gap requiring further investigation. To determine whether additional evidence had emerged, we repeated the literature search using the same strategy applied by the Guideline Panel in August 2025. No new RCTs involving children with high-risk febrile neutropenia were identified.

Added value of this study

This randomized, non-inferiority trial demonstrates that early discontinuation of EAT is non-inferior to continued therapy in children with acute leukaemia and high-risk febrile neutropenia after 72 h of defervescence and without bone marrow recovery. In contrast to existing guidelines that recommend continuing EAT until neutrophil recovery in high-risk febrile neutropenia, this study provides evidence supporting cessation of antibiotics after sustained clinical improvement. The discontinuation of EAT after 72 h of defervescence did not result in higher rates of fever recurrence, mortality, or escalation of antimicrobial therapy.

Implications of all the available evidence

The 2023 Clinical Practice Guideline identified the lack of evidence supporting early antibiotic discontinuation in high-risk febrile neutropenia as a key knowledge gap. This study directly addresses that gap by demonstrating the non-inferiority of early cessation of EAT in children with acute leukaemia and high-risk febrile neutropenia. Implementing early discontinuation in this setting has the potential to reduce antibiotic exposure and healthcare resource utilization without compromising patient safety.

Introduction

Febrile neutropenia is a common and potentially life-threatening complication seen in paediatric patients undergoing induction chemotherapy for acute leukaemia.1 The immunocompromised state caused by intensive chemotherapy blunts the typical inflammatory responses, making early fever often the only clinical clue of a serious underlying infection. Consequently, prompt initiation of empirical antibiotic therapy (EAT) is the cornerstone of management to reduce mortality due to febrile neutropenia.2

The latest Clinical Practice Guidelines conditionally recommend cessation of EAT in patients with low-risk febrile neutropenia who are clinically stable and have been afebrile for at least 24 h, even in the absence of evidence of bone marrow recovery, provided blood cultures remain negative at 48 h.3 However, the guidelines currently recommend continuing EAT in afebrile patients with high-risk febrile neutropenia until bone marrow recovery.3 Reflecting on this recommendation, a recent survey of clinicians managing paediatric febrile neutropenia found that the majority recommend continuing EAT until marrow recovery in afebrile patients with high-risk febrile neutropenia.4 Although continuing EAT until bone marrow recovery is recommended based on the assumption that it may reduce the risk of untreated infections during prolonged neutropenia, conversely, there are concerns that this approach may lead to unnecessarily prolonged antibiotic use in the absence of documented infection or persistent symptoms. Prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy is associated with increased antimicrobial resistance and longer hospital stays, both of which contribute to the financial and time toxicity of cancer treatment.5,6

Multiple randomized trials in adult patients with haematological malignancies have demonstrated that early discontinuation of EAT during febrile neutropenia is a viable strategy that increases antibiotic-free days without elevating the risk of adverse events or mortality.7, 8, 9, 10 While previous paediatric studies have shown that early discontinuation of EAT is non-inferior to continuation until bone marrow recovery in children with low-risk febrile neutropenia, evidence in high-risk febrile neutropenia among children remains limited.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 A multicentre randomized trial involving children with low and high-risk febrile neutropenia, who tested positive for respiratory viral pathogens and became afebrile after 48 h of EAT, compared early antibiotic discontinuation versus continuation until bone marrow recovery.12 Although this study demonstrated the non-inferiority of the early discontinuation strategy, factors such as persistent viral detection after clinical recovery and asymptomatic colonization raised concerns regarding routine viral testing to guide early antibiotic discontinuation.

To address this important evidence gap, we conducted a randomized, open-label, non-inferiority trial among children with acute leukaemia with high-risk febrile neutropenia during induction to evaluate whether early discontinuation of EAT among children with sustained clinical improvement before bone marrow recovery is non-inferior to continuation of EAT until bone marrow recovery.

Methods

Study design and participants

This investigator-initiated, open-label, phase 3, non-inferiority randomized controlled trial was conducted at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, between October 2018 and August 2024. The complete trial protocol, detailing the study design, eligibility criteria, and planned analyses is publicly available elsewhere.16 All authors had access to the data, verified its accuracy, and were responsible for the decision to submit the manuscript.

Patients aged 2–18 years with newly diagnosed or relapsed acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) or acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) who experienced febrile neutropenia during induction chemotherapy were eligible for enrolment. Febrile neutropenia was defined as a single oral temperature greater than 38.3 °C (101 °F) or a temperature greater than 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) sustained over 1 h, combined with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) below 500 cells/μL or expected to fall below 500 cells/μL within the following 48 h.17 Each patient was eligible for randomization only once and was ineligible for randomization for subsequent febrile neutropenia episode (if any) or at fever recurrence. Induction regimens for de novo and relapsed ALL were the ICiCLE-ALL-14 (Indian Collaborative Childhood Leukaemia group) protocol and the Medical Research Council UK-ALLR1 protocol or Berlin-Frankfurt-Munster (BFM) 90 protocols, respectively.18, 19, 20 Induction regimens for both de novo and relapsed ALL were administered on an outpatient/daycare basis. De novo AML was treated with AD (Cytosine arabinoside, Daunorubicin) protocol as an inpatient or ADE induction (Cytosine arabinoside, Daunorubicin, Etoposide) on an outpatient basis.21 Children with relapsed AML were treated with ADE induction.22

Eligible patients were required to be clinically free of infection with a negative blood culture and afebrile for at least 72 h while receiving intravenous EAT. Patients were considered afebrile if their axillary temperature remained below 37.5 °C (99.5 °F) for at least 48 h. Exclusion criteria included clinical, radiological, or microbiological evidence of infection, the requirement for third-line antibiotics, therapeutic antifungals, need for organ support during the treatment of febrile neutropenia. Additionally, patients with a history of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, diagnosis of HIV infection, or those with creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min were excluded. Patients diagnosed with mixed phenotypic acute leukaemia, acute promyelocytic leukaemia, blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm or Burkitt's leukaemia were also excluded.

All children who experienced febrile neutropenia during induction chemotherapy for acute leukaemia at our centre were screened for this study. The standard evaluation for evidence of infection during an episode of febrile neutropenia included blood culture, complete blood count, renal and liver function tests accompanied by a chest radiograph. Based on clinical suspicion, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, urine culture, and abdomen-pelvis ultrasound were also included.

EAT was initiated as per institutional protocol with dual antibiotics with at least one anti-pseudomonas agent.16 Patients received antibiotics according to a tiered protocol. First-tier antibiotics included cefoperazone-sulbactam, amikacin, or levofloxacin either as single agent or in combination. Second-tier agents included meropenem, teicoplanin, oral clindamycin, and oral linezolid. Third-tier antibiotics comprised piperacillin-tazobactam, colistin, tigecycline, aztreonam, daptomycin, intravenous linezolid, and ceftazidime-avibactam. Voriconazole or amphotericin B was administered for breakthrough fungal infections, while echinocandins, azoles, or amphotericin B was used for established fungal infections such as candidemia, sino-orbital mucormycosis, and fungal pneumonia (Supplementary Table S1).

Patients received treatment on an outpatient or inpatient basis based on discretion of treating physician. Both patients treated on outpatient and inpatient basis were eligible for randomization if they satisfy other inclusion/exclusion criteria. All patients were screened on alternate days for fever and with a complete blood count. Patients who were afebrile for 72 h on EAT (up to the second line of antibiotics) with no evidence of documented infection including negative blood culture and had an ANC of <500 cells/μL were screened for eligibility for this study based on the criteria described above.16

Randomization and masking

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the stop arm or the continuation arm using a permuted block randomization method with variable block sizes (4 or 8). Randomization was stratified by disease type (de novo ALL, de novo AML, or relapsed ALL/AML). To ensure allocation concealment, randomization was conducted by an investigator not involved in patient enrolment or outcome assessment. The treating physician was informed of the assigned treatment arm after randomization.

Procedures

In the stop arm, EAT was discontinued for all patients regardless of their ANC. In the continuation arm, consistent with current guidelines, EAT was continued for patients until the ANC reached ≥500 cells/μL. All patients received prophylactic antifungals, antivirals, and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis as indicated by the induction regimen throughout the study period. Prophylactic antibiotics targeting gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria were not permitted during the study. The use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) or pegylated G-CSF was allowed at the treating physician's discretion. Patients in both arms were followed up daily either physically or telephonically, with complete haemogram and renal/liver function tests conducted at least on alternate days or more frequently as per discretion of treating team. Blood product transfusions were permitted to maintain a haemoglobin level >7 g/dL and a platelet count >20,000/μL in the presence of fever or clinical evidence of bleeding; in the absence of these, the transfusion threshold for platelets was >10,000/μL.

In the event of fever recurrence, EAT was re-initiated in the stop arm. In the continuation arm, antibiotic regimens were modified according to the institutional protocol. The recurrence of fever was evaluated following the same standard of care used during the initial assessment of febrile neutropenia. The in-patient admission criteria were consistent across both groups and were applied in cases requiring prolonged intravenous antibiotic administration, use of third-line antibiotics, or requirement of organ support. At fever recurrence, no further attempts at antibiotic discontinuation were undertaken.

All patients were monitored daily until either day 35 of induction chemotherapy or until the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) reached ≥500 cells/μL, whichever occurred first. Temperature was recorded every 4 h in both study arms. For inpatients, temperature was documented by clinical staff, while for outpatients, it was recorded by parents or guardians using a pre-designed patient diary mirroring the frequency of inpatient monitoring (Supplementary Table S2). Temperature was monitored by a digital thermometer by documenting axillary temperature. For parents/caregivers, training was imparted for temperature monitoring by nursing staff, which is done as per routine institutional practice for all children, as part of outpatient management of febrile neutropenia, irrespective of trial. Anti-pyretics were used on “as needed” basis. In addition to temperature monitoring, data were collected on the duration of antibiotic therapy, hospital admissions, emergency visits, organ support, administration of blood products, use of growth factors, and mortality during the follow-up period. Adherence to the data collection protocol was ensured by daily physical or telephonic follow-up with the parents/guardians of the patients and the treating physicians.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the difference in the proportion of patients who experienced a recurrence of fever [defined as a temperature >38.0 °C (100.4 °F)] between the two arms during the follow-up period. Secondary outcomes included the duration of antibiotic therapy, mortality rate, hospital readmission rates, and the receipt of third-line antibiotics, therapeutic antifungals, blood products, and organ support.

The duration of antibiotic therapy was defined as the number of days antibiotics were administered after randomization until the ANC reached ≥500 cells/μL or up to day 35, whichever occurred earlier. Mortality was defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period.

Statistics

Based on a 2-month clinical audit conducted at the study site before study initiation, fever recurrence was estimated to occur in 36% of patients with febrile neutropenia who continued EAT until the ANC reached ≥500 cells/μL. A non-inferiority margin of a 15 percentage points higher recurrence rate in the stop arm compared to the continuation arm was deemed acceptable based on prior studies, which has been elaborated in already published protocol.9,10,23 To achieve 80% power at a one-sided alpha of 5%, a total of 250 patients (125 per arm) were required. After accounting for a 10% attrition rate, the final planned sample size was 280 patients (140 per arm).

In the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) analysis, all randomized patients who received the treatment were analysed in the groups to which they were originally assigned, regardless of the treatment received. In the per-protocol (PP) analysis, only patients who adhered to the treatment protocol of their randomized arm were included. Baseline clinical characteristics and self-reported demographics were summarized as counts (percentages) or medians (IQR). The difference in the proportion of patients experiencing fever recurrence between the stop and continuation arms was calculated, and the 90% confidence interval (CI) for this difference was estimated using the Miettinen–Nurminen method.24 To assess non-inferiority, the upper bound of this two-sided 90% CI (equivalent to one-side 95% CI) was compared to the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 15 percentage points.25 A forest plot was used to visualize the difference in the proportion of patients having fever recurrence between the arms for sub-groups of age, sex, diagnosis, ANC, and serum albumin. Subgroup analyses were pre-planned, although the final categorization of these subgroups was determined after data collection based on clinical relevance and distribution of participants. Categorical outcomes were compared between groups using the chi-square test. The duration of antibiotic administration was compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Hodges–Lehmann estimator was used to calculate the median difference in the duration of antibiotic therapy between the two arms. All reported p-values are two-sided, with a significance threshold of <0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Ethics

The study protocol was prospectively registered at Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2018/015994) and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IECPG-424) of the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi. The trial adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians of all participants, and assent was obtained from children aged 7 years or older.

Role of funding source

Results

We randomized 280 patients. Of these, 278 were included in the mITT analysis: 138 in the continuation arm and 140 in the stop arm (Fig. 1). One patient was excluded from each arm. In the continuation arm, one patient was later found to have an ANC count ≥500/μL at the time of randomization. In the stop arm, the patient was excluded because a delayed blood culture grew Staphylococcus hominis.

Primary outcome

Fever recurrence occurred in 52.1% (73/140) of patients in the stop arm and 47.1% (65/138) in the continuation arm, resulting in a difference of 5.0 percentage points (90% CI: −4.8 to 14.8 percentage points) (Fig. 2). Since the upper bound of this two-sided 90% CI was less than the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 15 percentage points, the stop arm was non-inferior to the continuation arm. In the PP population, the difference in fever recurrence between the arms was 5.4 percentage points (90% CI: −4.5 to 15.2 percentage points) (Supplementary Table S3).

Subgroup analysis showed consistent findings in most subgroups. However, a higher recurrence of fever was observed among patients with de novo AML in the stop arm compared to the continuation arm (71.1% (27/38) vs. 47.2% (17/36); difference: 23.8 percentage points, 90% CI: 4.9 to 41.2 percentage points) (Fig. 2).

Secondary outcomes

The median (IQR) duration of antibiotic administration after randomization, was significantly shorter in the stop arm [4.3 days (IQR: 0–10)] compared to the continuation arm [11 days (IQR: 7.2–15)] (p < 0.0001), as shown in Fig. 3. The median difference between the two arms was 6 days (95% CI: 5–7 days). Rates of hospital admission, use of third-line antibiotics, therapeutic antifungals, and mortality were not significantly different between the two arms (Table 2). The use of inotropes, mechanical ventilation, emergency visits, and transfusion of blood products also did not differ significantly. Granulocyte infusions and dialysis requirements were infrequent and comparable across groups (Table 2). Among patients with recurrent fever, blood cultures yielded microbiological growth in 9.6% (7/73) of the stop arm and 6.2% (4/65) of the continuation arm. Radiographically confirmed pneumonia was identified in 6.8% (5/73) and 1.5% (1/65) of patients with recurrence of fever, in the stop and continuation arms respectively.

In the stop arm, there were seven deaths among 140 patients (5%). Sepsis was the most common cause, reported in five patients, one patient died from haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis and one death was attributed to disease progression. In the continuation arm, there were eleven deaths among 138 patients (7.9%), all of which were attributable to sepsis; one patient had intracranial bleeding during sepsis as well. Among patients with mortality, culture-positive infections were documented in six deaths in the stop arm and four deaths in the continuation arm. In the stop arm, bacterial pathogens were identified in three patients (Escherichia coli [carbapenem-resistant], Klebsiella pneumoniae [carbapenem resistant], and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia) and Candida tropicalis in three patients. In the continuation arm, bacterial growth was detected in three patients (methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci in two, carbapenem resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa in one) and C. tropicalis in one patient.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the non-inferiority of early stoppage of EAT after 72 h of clinical stability and defervescence in paediatric patients with acute leukaemia during induction therapy and high-risk febrile neutropenia compared to the continuation of EAT until bone marrow recovery. Prior studies in children were largely limited to low-risk febrile neutropenia or with documented viral infections.11,12 Early discontinuation led to a significant reduction in the median duration of antibiotic use, without a corresponding increase in fever recurrence, mortality, hospital readmissions, use of third-line antibiotics, or therapeutic antifungal administration. The proportion of mortality during induction in both the arms were comparable and in fact lower than that reported in other large-scale prospective studies conducted among children with ALL and AML in India.21,26, 27, 28 These findings suggest that a shorter course of EAT can safely reduce treatment intensity and healthcare utilization for paediatric patients with high-risk febrile neutropenia, even in settings of high-infection burden and antibiotic resistance.26,27

The findings of this study are consistent with evidence from adult studies showing that early stoppage of antibiotics in adults with high-risk febrile neutropenia does not lead to higher rates of infection-related complications or mortality and allowed patients to experience more antibiotic-free days. Comparing our results with previous trials we observe that the rate of fever recurrence in our study was higher than that reported in a previous randomized trial evaluating early discontinuation of EAT among adults with high-risk febrile neutropenia, which is possibly related to increased burden of infection-related complications in our setting.9,10,24,25 Even then, the difference in EAT duration between study arms was greater in our study than in the How Long trial.9 The How Long study reported a 2.4-day difference in mean EAT-free days, whereas our study observed a median difference of 6 days in the duration of antibiotic administration. Comparatively, the duration of antibiotic administration and other secondary outcomes seen in our study are similar to those seen in a previous randomized trial involving children with high- and low-risk febrile neutropenia with documented respiratory viral infections.12

While the trial was not adequately powered to detect differences between the arms for the disease sub-groups, the sub-group analysis suggests that relative difference of early stoppage of EAT may differ by patient characteristics, leukaemia subtype and chemotherapy intensity and should be interpreted with caution, especially for subgroups like de novo AML.

By reducing the median duration of EAT by 6 days, early discontinuation can meaningfully contribute to antimicrobial stewardship efforts in paediatric oncology preventing emergence of antibiotic resistance and reduce antibiotic-associated adverse events. The reduction in antibiotic administration days can also lead to a reduction in the financial burden imposed by febrile neutropenia. The cost-effectiveness of early discontinuation of EAT is subject to future analysis of a sub-group of patients enrolled in this trial.29

This was the first adequately powered randomized control trial assessing the relative efficacy and safety of early discontinuation of EAT for paediatric patients with high-risk febrile neutropenia. The control arm of the trial reflects the current standard of care as endorsed by guidelines.3 Notwithstanding its strengths, this study has limitations. It was conducted at a single tertiary-care academic centre with strict inclusion/exclusion criteria which may limit the generalizability of its findings to all cases of high-risk febrile neutropenia. The open-label design, outpatient temperature monitoring and potential anti-pyretic use might have influenced outcome assessment. However, the primary outcome of fever recurrence is an objective and easily measured clinical event, minimizing subjectivity. Adverse events, specifically due to prolonged antibiotic administration, were also not documented in this study.

In conclusion, the early discontinuation of EAT in children with high-risk febrile neutropenia who have clinically improved is non-inferior to the standard strategy of continuing antibiotics until bone marrow recovery. These findings support a shift in the management of paediatric high-risk febrile neutropenia by minimizing antibiotic exposure and promote antibiotic stewardship in this vulnerable population. Further studies are needed to confirm the non-inferiority of early antibiotic discontinuation in different leukaemia subtypes and in patients who have undergone stem cell transplantation.
