Today’s flow, tomorrow’s subjective well-being: temporal dynamics in the Chinese educational context
Authors: Yiyan Li, Zhuofan Yang, Mei Xie, Jiaxi Lu, Alexander Scott English, Yanhui Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimal experience (flow) in academic study has been positively associated with student well-being; however, the precise day-to-day temporal ordering of this relationship remains to be empirically clarified. To advance this understanding, the present study examined within-person reciprocal associations between academic flow and subjective well-being among university students.MethodsA 21-day daily diary design was employed with 51 university students, yielding 1,006 valid daily observations and a compliance rate of 93.9%. Subjective well-being was assessed across three dimensions: positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction. Three Bayesian dynamic structural equation models were estimated to capture within-person dynamics, with daily sleep duration incorporated as a control variable.ResultsSignificant within-person autoregressive effects were identified for all study variables. Daily academic flow prospectively predicted positive affect and life satisfaction on the following day, whereas neither well-being component predicted subsequent flow, indicating a unidirectional rather than reciprocal association. No longitudinal association was observed between flow and negative affect.ConclusionsThese findings support the temporal precedence of academic flow in predicting daily subjective well-being, consistent with a eudaimonic perspective on student development. In practice, these results encourage educators and institutional practitioners to prioritize cultivating optimal learning experiences as a meaningful strategy to sustain student well-being in higher education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05203-z.

Background

University students navigate continuous academic demands, evaluations, and future uncertainties, making their daily emotional and cognitive states central to both well-being and academic success. These momentary experiences, rather than being trivial fluctuations, accumulate to shape broader adjustment patterns and psychological functioning. This perspective shifts attention from static traits to the dynamic micro-moments that structure students’ everyday lives. Flow experience, which is characterized by intense concentration, deep absorption, and intrinsic enjoyment during task engagement and arises under specific conditions including an optimal challenge-skill balance, clear goals, immediate feedback, and sustained attentional focus, has long been regarded as a central component of optimal experience and psychological well-being [1, 2]. Extensive research across educational, leisure, work, and digital contexts has consistently linked flow to positive affective states, intrinsic motivation, engagement, and life satisfaction [3–7]. Among university students, experiencing flow in academic activities has been associated with greater enjoyment of learning, enhanced engagement, and higher levels of subjective well-being [8–12]. Because academic activities occupy a substantial proportion of students’ daily lives, opportunities to experience flow may represent an important psychological resource for sustaining motivation and promoting healthy academic functioning. Understanding how these naturally occurring flow experiences relate to students’ well-being may therefore provide valuable insights for fostering both academic success and psychological health.

Subjective well-being is typically conceptualized as comprising both affective and cognitive components [13]. The affective component captures the balance between positive and negative emotional experiences, while the cognitive component reflects global evaluative judgments of life satisfaction [11, 14, 15]. While this two-dimensional framework is widely accepted, empirical studies often operationalize subjective well-being by distinguishing three components—positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction—to capture their potentially distinct antecedents and outcomes (e.g [16, 17]). This distinction is particularly important in dynamic models, as positive and negative affect, as well as life satisfaction, may follow distinct temporal trajectories and serve distinct roles in the flow–well-being process.

Notably, a growing body of empirical research has established that flow experiences are significantly associated with all dimensions of subjective well-being, including higher positive affect, lower negative affect, and greater life satisfaction [4, 11, 18, 19]. Cross-domain evidence from education, sports, leisure, and digital environments confirms that individuals experiencing higher levels of flow report greater happiness and life satisfaction [12, 20–22]. These findings align with broader perspectives in positive psychology, which emphasize engagement and intrinsic motivation as key pathways to well-being beyond dispositional personality traits [23, 24]. Within higher education, these associations are particularly significant because students’ well-being is closely intertwined with academic engagement, persistence, and successful adaptation to university life. Moreover, because flow frequently arises during learning activities, it may serve as a recurrent and meaningful source of positive psychological experience in this context. Understanding how flow contributes to students’ daily well-being may therefore not only advance theoretical knowledge of optimal psychological functioning but also yield practical insights for fostering academic success and supporting mental health in university settings. Elucidating the mechanisms by which flow shapes well-being in this population thus has clear implications for both educational practice and the promotion of student mental health.

Although robust evidence has linked flow to subjective well-being, less attention has been paid to how these experiences unfold over short timescales in everyday life. Understanding these short-term within-person dynamics carries both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, because flow is inherently a transient and context-embedded experience, its psychological benefits are most meaningfully examined at the timescale at which they actually occur. In practice, identifying how flow and well-being covary on a day-to-day basis can reveal critical windows during which targeted interventions may be most effective and inform evidence-based strategies for promoting student well-being in daily academic life. Moving beyond static associations is therefore not merely a methodological refinement, but a necessary step toward understanding how optimal experiences accumulate to shape longer-term psychological functioning.

Flow and positive affect

Positive affect—encompassing feelings of enthusiasm, vitality, and enjoyment—is theorized to share a mutually reinforcing relationship with flow. Flow, by virtue of its intrinsic reward and perceived mastery, is thought to amplify concurrent positive affect and to generate short-term emotional carry-over effects. Empirical studies among university students have shown that higher flow is associated with greater positive affect and happiness, supporting the affective payoff of engaging fully in meaningful tasks [3, 5, 25]. Similar patterns in learning and technology-mediated contexts confirm that flow co-occurs with enjoyable engagement [26–29]. At the same time, positive affect may serve as an antecedent of flow. Consistent with the broaden-and-build theory [30, 31], positive affect broadens attentional scope and cognitive flexibility; beyond this, it also fosters motivation, interest, and adaptive engagement [32–35], thereby creating conditions favorable to the onset of flow. However, the broaden-and-build pathway may not always translate into observable next-day flow, particularly in structured academic environments where flow onset is constrained by external task demands, fixed schedules, and performance pressures that operate independently of one’s current affective state. Under such conditions, the facilitating effect of positive affect on flow may be attenuated or rendered less detectable at the daily timescale.

Flow and negative affect

While positive emotions may facilitate flow, the emergence of this state is often hindered by negative affect, including distress, anxiety, and frustration. These aversive states may compromise executive control, increase threat monitoring, and reduce working memory capacity, thereby interfering with the sustained attentional engagement required for flow [36, 37]. In student populations, negative experiences frequently co-occur with academic pressures and are consistently associated with lower well-being [38, 39]. From this perspective, elevated negative affect may undermine focused immersion. Conversely, flow may serve an emotion-regulatory function; by narrowing attention to task-relevant cues and creating a sense of control, flow may temporarily buffer against negative feelings and reduce the salience of stressors [40, 41]. Evidence from work and digital environments suggests that disruptions to flow are associated with increased strain, implying that maintaining flow may protect psychological functioning [19, 42, 43]. In student samples, flow has also been positioned as a mechanism linking stress-related experiences to well-being outcomes, implying potential protective effects [4, 21, 44].

Flow and life satisfaction

Beyond these affective fluctuations, the impact of flow extends to life satisfaction, which reflects the cognitive-evaluative dimension of subjective well-being [14, 45]. Although traditionally viewed as stable, accumulating evidence indicates that life satisfaction is sensitive to daily experiences and perceived quality of functioning [15, 46]. In educational contexts, life satisfaction is not merely an outcome but also a predictor of adaptive engagement, suggesting that global evaluations may influence how students approach learning tasks [47, 48]. Flow has been linked to life satisfaction across domains; studies in leisure and sport show that experiencing flow during meaningful activities is associated with higher evaluative well-being [20, 49, 50]. In academic settings, flow co-occurs with positive appraisals of life quality, suggesting that frequent immersion in rewarding activities contributes to broader well-being [7, 22, 43]. Research on orientations to happiness proposes engagement as a key route to life satisfaction, reinforcing the conceptual link between flow and cognitive well-being [51–53]. Moreover, life satisfaction itself may shape subsequent engagement by influencing motivation, goal pursuit, and perceived meaning—processes that could support flow experiences in academic life [23, 47].

The present study

Despite extensive evidence linking flow and well-being, most research relies on cross-sectional or between-person designs, implicitly treating flow as a stable trait and limiting causal and temporal inference [54, 55]. This contrasts with flow theory, which conceptualizes flow as a situational and fluctuating experience varying across contexts and time [1, 8, 56]. Experience-sampling and diary studies consistently show substantial within-person variability, with daily changes in challenge, feedback, and perceived competence predicting fluctuations in flow [3, 57, 58]. Thus, trait-level approaches may obscure the dynamic processes through which flow co-evolves with well-being, particularly in everyday contexts such as university life [15, 18, 59]. Crucially, cross-sectional designs cannot determine temporal directionality—whether flow influences subsequent well-being, whether affective states facilitate flow, or whether reciprocal processes exist—nor can they capture how these dynamics unfold over time. A further gap concerns the multidimensional nature of subjective well-being. Although prior research confirms associations between flow and well-being, few studies simultaneously examine its distinct components—positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction—despite evidence that these components may follow different temporal patterns.

Addressing these issues requires intensive longitudinal data and within-person analytical approaches capable of modeling lagged and bidirectional effects [60]. Bayesian dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) provides such a framework, simultaneously disentangling between-person differences from within-person fluctuations and estimating autoregressive and cross-lagged effects—moving beyond the static associations that characterize most prior work [11, 61]. Thus, the present study employs a 21-day diary design combined with Bayesian DSEM to examine daily flow and three components of subjective well-being—positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction—in university contexts, with the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

Daily flow and positive affect exhibit reciprocal lagged effects, such that flow positively predicts next-day positive affect (H1a), and positive affect positively predicts next-day flow (H1b).

Hypothesis 2

Daily flow and negative affect exhibit reciprocal lagged effects, such that flow negatively predicts next-day negative affect (H2a), and negative affect negatively predicts next-day flow (H2b).

Hypothesis 3

Daily flow and life satisfaction exhibit reciprocal lagged effects, such that flow positively predicts next-day life satisfaction (H3a), and life satisfaction positively predicts next-day flow (H3b).

Method

Participants and procedure

Fifty-four university students in Southwest China were recruited via official university channels to participate in a broader diary project assessing positive psychological functioning and physical health behaviors. The current analysis draws on a 21-day subset (May 26-June 15, 2025) of this larger project, focusing specifically on the reciprocal relationship between academic flow and subjective well-being. All procedures for the broader project, including the present analysis, were approved by the Academic Ethics Review Committee of the Psychological Research and Counseling Center, Southwest Jiaotong University (Approval No. XL-20250506-0001). Prior to enrollment, informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were assured of the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw without any penalty.

Each evening, research personnel sent reminders at 7:30 PM and 10:00 PM, prompting participants to complete one diary entry before midnight via Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/YJaitnx.aspx), a reputable Chinese online survey platform widely used in academic research, accessible via QR code or direct URL. A grace period of up to 8:00 AM the following morning was permitted, with any submissions made after that time treated as missing data. Upon full completion, participants received a cash payment of 39 CNY.

Following data collection, three participants were excluded due to minimal compliance (submitting only 1, 2, or 4 diary entries, respectively), yielding a final analytic sample of 51 participants (18 males, 33 females; Mage = 19.49, SD = 2.13) from 19 academic departments across the university. The sample comprised 35 first-year undergraduates, 9 s-year undergraduates, 2 third-year undergraduates, and 5 graduate students, with 29 from urban and 22 from rural areas. These 51 participants contributed 1,006 valid daily observations over the 21 days, which is comparable to the number of observations reported in some prior studies employing DSEM [62, 63].

Measures

To minimize participant burden in repeated daily assessments, all core daily measures were assessed using single-item measures, following methodological recommendations for diary and intensive longitudinal research [58, 64]. Items were selected based on their psychometric performance in the original validated multi-item scales, as well as in prior studies that applied these measures in similar contexts. This approach allows repeated measurement of key constructs while maintaining data quality and participant engagement.

Daily academic flow experience

Daily academic flow was assessed using a single-item measure adapted from validated flow scales [65]. Each day, participants rated the statement, “Today, when I was studying, I was highly focused and fully immersed.” Responses were recorded on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater daily flow. This item directly operationalizes the core components of flow, namely concentrated focus and complete absorption in the activity [1], as applied to an academic context.

Daily positive affect and negative affect

Daily positive and negative affect were each assessed using a single item adapted from the Scale of Positive and Negative Experience (SPANE; [66]). Participants rated “Today, I felt pleasant” (positive affect) and “Today, I felt sad” (negative affect) on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = all the time), with higher scores reflecting greater positive and negative affect, respectively. These items were selected primarily for their semantic clarity in representing broad positive and negative affective states. Their psychometric performance in the original validation sample was acceptable (corrected item-total correlations: pleasant = 0.66; sad = 0.65; [66]), and both items have demonstrated good performance in our previous research [10].

Daily life satisfaction

Daily life satisfaction was assessed using a single item adapted from the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS; [67]). Participants rated “Today, I am satisfied with my life” on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater daily life satisfaction. This item carries one of the highest factor loadings in the original scale (λ = 0.83; [67]) and was further identified as the strongest indicator of life satisfaction in confirmatory factor analyses conducted in our previous research [10]. Its wording directly and unambiguously reflects the essence of the construct, making it well-suited for single-item measurement.

Covariates

Prior-night sleep duration was included as a time-varying covariate, as prior research has shown that sleep duration is associated with daily affect and well-being (e.g [68, 69]). In the daily questionnaire, participants were asked to self-report their nighttime sleep duration by responding to the question “How long did you sleep last night?” Participants entered their sleep duration manually in hours and minutes, which were subsequently converted to decimal format for data analysis (e.g., 7 h and 30 min was converted to 7.5 h). Age, gender, and residential location (urban vs. rural) were included as time-invariant covariates at the between-person level.

Data analytical strategy

Preliminary analyses were conducted in SPSS 27.0 to obtain descriptive statistics and to estimate within-person and between-person correlations, accounting for the nested structure of the data. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed in Mplus 8.3 to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to between-person differences and to support the use of multilevel modeling [70].

Primary analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.3. Given that the present study aims to examine reciprocal temporal dynamics between flow and well-being components across consecutive diary days while accounting for individual differences in these dynamics, DSEM was selected as it integrates time-series analysis, multilevel modeling, and structural equation modeling within a unified framework—enabling simultaneous estimation of lag-1 temporal dynamics, nested data structures, and reciprocal pathways across variables, capacities that conventional SEM or multilevel models alone cannot provide [60, 61]. A schematic example of the bivariate DSEM is presented in Fig. 1. To examine the unique temporal dynamics between flow and each well-being component while maintaining model parsimony, daily flow and each well-being component (positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction) were modeled in three separate bivariate DSEMs. Within each model, lag-1 autoregressive and cross-lagged effects with linear time trends were estimated to capture temporal carryover and reciprocal influences across consecutive days while maintaining the stationarity assumption [61]. To accommodate individual differences in daily dynamics, autoregressive effects, cross-lagged effects, and linear time trends were all specified as random effects, while daily sleep duration was included as a fixed-effect time-varying covariate. Person-mean sleep duration, age, gender, and residential location served as between-person covariates. All models were estimated via Bayesian MCMC (two chains of 5,000 iterations) with noninformative priors, with missing data handled under a missing-at-random assumption through full-information Bayesian estimation. Convergence was confirmed using PSR statistics (< 1.05; [71]) and inspection of the trace plot. Posterior means and 95% HPD credible intervals are reported for all parameters. As a robustness check, all models were re-estimated without covariates, with full results reported in the Supplementary Material; all results presented in the main text reflect the primary models with covariates included.

Day-to-day dynamics: flow experience and well-being

Flow and positive affect

The bivariate DSEM examining flow and positive affect converged successfully, with PSR values of 1.006. As shown in Table 2, flow (β = 0.184, 95% CI [0.087, 0.280]) and positive affect (β = 0.090, 95% CI [0.002, 0.175]) showed significant autoregressive effects. Flow positively predicted the next-day positive affect (β = 0.118, 95% CI [0.045, 0.189]), whereas the reverse effect was not significant (β = 0.013, 95% CI [-0.075, 0.119]), supporting H1a but not H1b.

Flow and negative affect

The bivariate DSEM for flow and negative affect also converged successfully (PSR = 1.004), with results presented in Table 3. Significant autoregressive effects were observed for flow (β = 0.195, 95% CI [0.083, 0.305]) and negative affect (β = 0.102, 95% CI [0.017, 0.193]). Neither cross-lagged effect reached significance (negative affect → flow: β = -0.001, 95% CI [-0.082, 0.082]; flow → negative affect: β = -0.073, 95% CI [-0.159, 0.007]), providing no support for H2a or H2b.

Flow and life satisfaction

The bivariate DSEM for flow and life satisfaction converged successfully (PSR = 1.010), and the results are presented in Table 4. Both flow (β = 0.159, 95% CI [0.066, 0.256]) and life satisfaction (β = 0.102, 95% CI [0.010, 0.184]) showed significant autoregressive effects. Flow significantly predicted next-day life satisfaction (β = 0.101, 95% CI [0.016, 0.191]), whereas the reverse effect was not significant (β = 0.079, 95% CI [-0.019, 0.195]), supporting H3a but not H3b. Discussion

The present study investigated the day-to-day reciprocal associations between flow experience and three core components of subjective well-being—positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction—using a 21-day diary design and DSEM. By explicitly modeling within-person temporal processes while separating them from stable between-person differences, this study extends prior flow research that has largely relied on cross-sectional or aggregated approaches [3, 18, 59, 60]. Situated in the context of Chinese higher education, where students’ daily academic lives are often characterized by intensive coursework, frequent evaluation, and sustained performance pressure, the findings provide a process-oriented perspective on how flow and well-being dynamically co-evolve in students’ everyday academic lives.

A first key finding concerns the temporal stability of flow. Although flow is theoretically defined as a situational and momentary experience, daily flow exhibited significant autoregressive effects across all models, indicating meaningful short-term carryover from one day to the next. This pattern echoes earlier diary-based evidence showing that flow fluctuates within individuals while remaining continuous across adjacent time points [8, 18, 56, 72]. In the Chinese academic context, this continuity may reflect the structured and cumulative nature of university study, in which learning tasks, schedules, and performance expectations tend to remain stable across consecutive days, thereby enabling the persistence of motivational and attentional states. Autoregressive effects were significant for positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction, consistent with prior research showing that these well‑being components exhibit short‑term temporal continuity [73–75]. Together, these findings indicate that although flow is transient, it—and the key components of subjective well‑being—are embedded within short‑term motivational and contextual continuity, reflecting the persistence of affective and evaluative states over time.

Second, flow prospectively predicted the next-day positive affect at the within-person level, whereas positive affect did not reliably predict subsequent flow. This unidirectional pattern supports theoretical accounts emphasizing the intrinsically rewarding nature of flow, which generates positive emotional states that extend beyond the immediate experience of task engagement [5, 12, 29, 30]. Within Chinese universities, where academic engagement is often shaped by external evaluation and performance demands, moments of flow may represent psychologically salient experiences that replenish positive affect and provide emotional relief from routine academic pressures. In contrast, the absence of a significant prospective effect from positive affect to subsequent flow suggests that positive emotions alone may be insufficient to produce flow experiences. Although positive affect can broaden cognition and enhance motivational readiness, flow typically requires the simultaneous presence of additional conditions, including an appropriate balance between perceived skills and task challenges, clear goals, immediate feedback, and sustained attentional engagement. These prerequisites may be particularly difficult to satisfy in Chinese university settings, where students often have limited autonomy in task selection or face difficulties due to structured curricula and assessment requirements. Consequently, feeling enthusiastic or happy on one day may not substantially alter the objective learning environment on the next day and therefore may not reliably translate into higher levels of flow. It is also possible that the facilitating role of positive affect emerges only over longer timescales or in conjunction with contextual factors, such as autonomy or perceived competence, that were not examined in the present study. By establishing temporal precedence, the present findings extend previous cross-sectional evidence linking flow with happiness and enjoyment by clarifying the direction of their day-to-day relationship [7, 43, 76]. Overall, the results suggest that, in daily academic contexts, flow functions primarily as an antecedent of positive affect rather than as its consequence.

In contrast, the relationship between flow and negative affect was non-significant in both directions, indicating no evidence of a prospective within-person association across consecutive days. Likewise, negative affect did not significantly predict subsequent flow experiences. Although previous studies have reported mixed findings regarding the association between flow and negative affect at the between-person level [19, 42, 77], the present results suggest that such associations may not extend to day-to-day temporal processes. One possible explanation is that negative affect among Chinese university students is influenced by relatively stable and externally driven stressors, including academic pressure, examination demands, and concerns about future development [78, 79], which may not be readily altered by transient fluctuations in flow experiences. These findings also underscore the possibility that the temporal dynamics linking flow and subjective well-being differ across affective domains, highlighting the importance of examining positive and negative affect as partially distinct processes rather than assuming symmetrical associations with flow [24, 45, 66].

Finally, flow and life satisfaction exhibited a unidirectional pattern. Daily flow prospectively predicted next-day life satisfaction, partially supporting Hypothesis 3: while H3a was supported, H3b was not. Contrary to our expectation of a reciprocal relationship, life satisfaction did not significantly predict subsequent flow experiences. This finding strengthens the evidence that flow operates as a driver rather than a consequence of positive psychological outcomes, and aligns with the “bottom-up” model of subjective well-being, which posits that specific positive experiences accumulate over time to inform broader life evaluations [80]. While previous studies have consistently linked flow to life satisfaction at the between-person level across educational, leisure, and work contexts [4, 20, 22, 81], the present findings provide preliminary longitudinal evidence suggesting that flow experiences precede global life judgments. The absence of the expected reverse effect—whereby life satisfaction would facilitate subsequent flow—warrants explanation. The absence of a significant reverse effect may reflect the context-specific and skill-dependent nature of flow experiences [25], which appear less susceptible to top-down influences from global life satisfaction judgments. Unlike positive affect, which operates at a proximal, immediate level, life satisfaction reflects a more distal, cognitively constructed evaluation of one’s life as a whole. Such global appraisals may have limited capacity to directly shape the moment-to-moment attentional and motivational states required for the onset of flow, particularly within structured academic environments where task demands and schedules are externally determined.

Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of distinguishing between different components of subjective well-being when examining the temporal role of flow. Flow appears to function as a robust emotional amplifier of positive affect, shows weak, non-significant relationships with negative affect, and predicts subsequent life satisfaction. By demonstrating that these relationships operate at the within-person level and situating them within the everyday academic realities of Chinese university students, this study provides a nuanced and temporally grounded understanding of how flow contributes to well-being. It advances flow theory by showing that flow operates as a dynamic within-person process rather than a static individual difference. Using DSEM, the findings reveal that the psychological benefits of flow unfold through temporal mechanisms, whereby daily flow prospectively drives positive affect and life satisfaction. This supports bottom-up well-being models and conceptualizes flow as an episodic experience embedded in daily life. Furthermore, differentiated patterns across affect and life satisfaction refine existing models, suggesting that flow selectively influences cognitive and affective dimensions through distinct temporal routes. From a practical perspective, the present findings suggest that the primary value of flow-facilitating interventions lies in enhancing students’ positive emotional experiences. The prospective effect of daily flow on the next day’s positive affect suggests that fostering flow in academic settings may be an effective strategy for promoting students’ day-to-day emotional well-being. According to flow theory, flow is most likely to emerge when individuals engage in tasks that provide an optimal balance between challenge and skill, involve clear goals, offer immediate feedback, and enable sustained attentional engagement. Therefore, particularly in Chinese university contexts where learning is often highly structured and academically demanding, educators may promote students’ positive affect by designing learning environments that incorporate these core conditions and, where appropriate, provide greater autonomy to further facilitate engagement. However, because flow did not prospectively predict negative affect, flow-based interventions alone are unlikely to address all aspects of well-being and may be most effective when complemented by stress management or emotion regulation strategies.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the sample comprised university students from a single Chinese institution, which warrants caution regarding the generalizability of the findings. Although parts of the discussion drew on contextual features of Chinese higher education, such as structured curricula and high academic demands, these interpretations should not be assumed to apply universally. At the same time, the prospective effect of flow on positive affect is consistent with flow theory and evidence from educational, occupational, and leisure settings across different cultural contexts, suggesting that this association may reflect a broadly applicable psychological mechanism. In contrast, the absence of a prospective relationship between flow and negative affect may be more context-dependent and could be influenced by relatively stable stressors characteristic of the sampled environment. Future studies should replicate these findings across diverse cultural and institutional settings to distinguish context-specific effects from more universal flow–well-being dynamics. Second, while the 1,006 valid daily observations from 51 participants over 21 days provide a sufficient data basis for multilevel time-series analysis, the relatively modest number of participants may limit the precision and stability of parameter estimates. Simulation work suggests that larger participant samples are generally preferable for improving estimation accuracy in such designs [82]. Increasing the number of participants in future studies would be a meaningful methodological improvement. Third, the observational diary design precludes causal inference; experimental or intervention-based studies manipulating flow-related conditions would help establish causal mechanisms. Fourth, all variables were assessed using single daily items, which, although commonly used in diary research to reduce participant burden and facilitate repeated assessments, may limit measurement precision. In particular, the positive affect item (“pleasant”) captures a relatively broad range of affective experiences, whereas the negative affect item (“sad”) reflects a more specific emotional state. Although “sad” was selected over the negation-based “unpleasure” to avoid linguistic ambiguity in the Chinese translation, this asymmetry may have introduced differential sensitivity in the measurement of positive and negative affect, and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, the study focused exclusively on short-term daily dynamics. Future multi-timescale research that incorporates behavioral or physiological indicators and examines moderators such as personality, academic demands, and social support would provide a more comprehensive understanding of flow–subjective well-being relationships.

Conclusions

Situated within the context of Chinese higher education, this study offers a process-oriented account of how flow experience and subjective well-being interact dynamically in students’ everyday academic lives. Moving beyond static, between-person approaches, this study shows that flow functions as a time-sensitive psychological process with differentiated effects across different components of subjective well-being. Daily flow reliably predicted subsequent positive affect and life satisfaction in a unidirectional manner, whereas its associations with negative affect were non-significant in either direction, suggesting limited influence on negative emotional states. By disentangling within-person dynamics from stable individual differences, this study advances understanding of flow as a dynamic and contextually embedded experience. Methodologically, the findings highlight the value of intensive longitudinal designs and dynamic structural equation modeling for studying well-being processes. Substantively, the results suggest that fostering flow in academic settings within Chinese universities may yield sustained benefits for students’ emotional experiences and overall life satisfaction.
