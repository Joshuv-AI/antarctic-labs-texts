Psychosis-predominant neuropsychiatric lupus in a severely malnourished adolescent: a diagnostic challenge
Authors: Manu Dwivedi, Muhammad Azaz Iftikhar Cheema, Zoha Ghuman, Asma Iftikhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) is a rare but severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus in adolescents, with psychosis-predominant presentations occurring in approximately 5-12% of pediatric cases. Such manifestations may precede or obscure classic systemic features, often resulting in diagnostic delays and initial misattribution to primary psychiatric or infectious etiologies. We present the case of a previously healthy 19-year-old female who developed acute psychosis characterized by paranoia, auditory hallucinations, disorganization, and suicidal ideation over five days. Immunosuppressive therapy was initiated with pulse-dose methylprednisolone, followed by intravenous immunoglobulin and rituximab. Early consideration of autoimmune etiologies and a multidisciplinary approach, including rheumatology, psychiatry, neurology, and nutrition, are essential to optimize outcomes in this vulnerable population. The patient's limited psychiatric response despite immunologic improvement underscores the delicate balance between prompt immunosuppression and the neuropsychiatric risks associated with corticosteroid therapy. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of psychosis-predominant NPSLE in adolescents complicated by nutritional and infectious factors, whilst emphasizing the importance of early recognition of autoimmune etiologies and multidisciplinary management for optimal outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, heterogeneous autoimmune disease marked by multi-organ involvement and significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [1-4]. Central to its pathogenesis is the production of autoantibodies against nuclear and cytoplasmic antigens, resulting in widespread inflammation and tissue injury [1-4]. SLE may affect almost any organ system, including the skin, kidneys, joints, hematologic system, and, notably, the central nervous system (CNS). CNS involvement, termed neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE), represents an especially severe facet of the disease spectrum.

Neuropsychiatric manifestations are a substantial and sometimes underrecognized complication of pediatric-onset SLE. Estimates suggest that up to 40% of children with SLE experience neuropsychiatric involvement, an incidence higher than that seen in adults [2-7] greater vulnerability is attributed both to the aggressive nature of childhood-onset lupus and the unique susceptibilities of the developing brain and immune system. The spectrum of neuropsychiatric presentations is broad, spanning seizures, psychosis, mood and anxiety disorders, cognitive dysfunction, headaches, cerebrovascular disease, movement disorders, and peripheral neuropathies [2,3,5,6,8].

Psychosis, while recognized, is a relatively uncommon manifestation among pediatric patients. Longitudinal studies and case series report that acute psychosis occurs in approximately 5-12% of children and adolescents with SLE, a lower rate than seizure disorders or cognitive dysfunction, but no less clinically significant [2,3,5,8,9]. The onset of psychosis or other severe psychiatric symptoms can be abrupt and may precede or overshadow typical systemic features such as rash, arthritis, or nephritis. In youth without a prior SLE diagnosis or classic autoimmune symptoms, this atypical presentation often results in initial consideration of primary psychiatric or infectious etiologies, causing delays in diagnosis [10-15].

The pathogenesis of NPSLE and lupus psychosis is multifactorial and incompletely understood. It is thought to involve genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors [3,4,6,16,17]. Autoantibodies, particularly those targeting neuronal, synaptic, and endothelial antigens, are central; increased blood-brain barrier permeability, driven by inflammatory cytokines, may facilitate their entry into the CNS. Anti-ribosomal P and anti-NMDA receptor antibodies are among the best-studied and have been implicated in both experimental models and clinical series in the development of psychiatric and cognitive manifestations [3,4,6,16,17]. Anti-ribosomal P antibodies, specifically, are associated with psychosis and movement disorders in pediatric SLE [4,17], thought to exert neurotoxic effects by disrupting synaptic function and promoting neuroinflammation.

In this context, the present report details a 19-year-old female whose first clinical manifestation of SLE was acute psychosis. Her case illustrates the unique diagnostic and management challenges, the additional confounding effect of nutritional compromise, and the complexity of therapeutic decision-making when immunosuppression, psychiatric symptoms, and physical health are tightly interwoven.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 19-year-old previously healthy female presented with a 5-day history of acute-onset psychosis, including paranoia, auditory hallucinations, insomnia, and severely reduced oral intake. Initial evaluation in the emergency department revealed an encephalopathic picture with marked disorganization and suicidal ideation, prompting broad differential considerations including infectious, autoimmune, nutritional, and primary psychiatric etiologies. The patient's recent history of treated ehrlichiosis and tick exposure raised concern for ongoing CNS infection, while her significantly low BMI (14.3 kg/m2) suggested potential nutritional contributions to her encephalopathy.

Initial management focused on medical stabilization and diagnostic evaluation. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated mild pleocytosis (WBC 17 cells/mm3) and elevated total protein (52.1 mg/dL), while serologic testing later revealed profoundly elevated anti-dsDNA (>300 IU/mL) and ANA (1:1280 titer), leading to a diagnosis of NPSLE.

Two MRI and two CT head scans were performed, all of which were negative for acute pathology. Neuroimaging did not reveal evidence of structural damage or infarction, further supporting the diagnosis of NPSLE rather than an infectious or vascular etiology.

Immunosuppressive therapy was initiated on hospital day 3 with pulse-dose methylprednisolone, followed by IVIG and rituximab. Psychiatric management was delayed until hospital day 15 despite early telepsychiatry consultation, reflecting the clinical prioritization of excluding medical etiologies before attributing symptoms to primary psychiatric disease. When antipsychotics (olanzapine up to 25 mg daily and valproate) were finally initiated, the response was suboptimal, raising questions about potential steroid-exacerbated psychosis. The patient's hypertensive episodes were managed with labetalol and diltiazem because anti-histone antibody positivity prevented hydralazine use.

While the patient's intake improved to 50-100% of meals during her hospitalization, her nutritional rehabilitation proved challenging due to the patient's reluctance to discuss eating behaviors. Discharge planning was complicated by the patient's low BMI, necessitating a specialized eating disorder program referral and precluding standard outpatient psychiatric follow-up. DISCUSSION

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity that often characterizes adolescent-onset neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE), particularly when psychosis is the initial and predominant symptom. Abrupt onset of paranoia, hallucinations, and marked disorganization in a previously healthy teenager may initially suggest a primary psychiatric illness, especially in the absence of classic SLE manifestations. Pediatric-onset SLE typically exhibits a more severe clinical course compared to adult-onset disease, with neuropsychiatric involvement both more common and potentially more severe in younger patients [1,2,7]. An additional diagnostic challenge is that neuropsychiatric symptoms may predate or obscure development of classic organ manifestations (i.e., nephritis, arthritis, or rash) that typically trigger evaluation for lupus [10-15]. Multiple reports highlight that affected patients may undergo extensive investigations for infectious, metabolic, toxic, or primary psychiatric causes before NPSLE is finally identified [10,11,13,15]. For example, Khormi and Hijazi [11] described a patient whose combination of seizures and psychosis ultimately led to an SLE diagnosis, while Feng et al. [10] reported cognitive dysfunction and white matter changes on neuroimaging as clues to autoimmune CNS disease after exclusion of infectious and metabolic causes. Shah et al. [13,14] further highlighted cases in which acute psychiatric decompensation preceded laboratory and clinical signs of lupus, sometimes by several months.

The pathogenesis of NPSLE – lupus psychosis, in particular – is under active investigation. Current understanding implicates autoantibodies (anti-ribosomal P, anti-NMDAR), immune complexes, and inflammatory cytokines, including type I interferons and interleukin-6, in neuronal dysfunction and CNS injury [3,4,6,16,17]. Anti-ribosomal P antibodies are especially associated with acute psychosis and movement abnormalities in young lupus patients, although sensitivity and specificity are not absolute and may vary by timing or assay [4,17]. Therefore, a broad diagnostic workup, including cerebrospinal fluid analysis, serologic evaluation for SLE and related diseases, and neuroimaging, is warranted in atypical psychiatric presentations.

Therapeutic management of NPSLE is complex. Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of acute therapy, targeting immune-mediated inflammation [2,3,17,18]. However, these drugs are well-known to provoke or worsen psychiatric symptoms, including mania, psychosis, and mood lability, particularly in pediatric patients [19,20]. Distinguishing lupus psychosis from corticosteroid-induced psychosis is often difficult, as both may present with similar psychiatric features during the acute illness, complicating assessment and treatment [19-21]. This overlap complicates assessment, timing of interventions, and therapeutic decisions, particularly in adolescents where rapid immunosuppression is essential but may exacerbate neuropsychiatric symptoms. For patients whose symptoms are refractory to corticosteroids or requiring steroid-sparing regimens, second-line agents such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil are often employed [2,3,17,18]. More recently, B-cell–directed therapies (e.g., rituximab) have been reported with some success in both adults and children suffering refractory CNS lupus [15,18]. Additional use of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and combination regimens have been described for severe cases, particularly those presenting with prominent psychosis and catatonia [15,18]. Our patient's improvement after rituximab and IVIG is consistent with these reports.

Comprehensive multidisciplinary care is vital, particularly when nutritional compromise or eating disorders complicate management [2,3,22]. Evidence suggests that autoimmune conditions and eating disorders may co-occur in adolescents, amplifying risk for neuropsychiatric and systemic complications [22]. Optimal management of adolescent NPSLE therefore requires collaboration among rheumatology, psychiatry, neurology, nutrition, infectious disease, and social work [2,3]. Early recognition and coordinated intervention are essential to minimize long-term morbidity and disability [6,9-15].

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the significant challenges in diagnosing and managing NPSLE in adolescents, especially when psychosis is the initial presenting symptom. The patient's clinical course highlights how psychiatric manifestations of lupus can closely mimic primary psychiatric disorders, infections, or nutritional deficiencies that often result in considerable diagnostic delays. A comprehensive laboratory evaluation, including serologic and cerebrospinal fluid analysis, is essential in adolescents presenting with acute psychosis of unclear origin, as these investigations can provide crucial evidence of autoimmune disease.

NPSLE management is often complicated by the urgency of immunosuppression, which needs to be balanced against the potential neuropsychiatric side effects of corticosteroids. For cases in which psychiatric symptoms are severe or refractory, B-cell–targeted therapies such as rituximab have been utilized in pediatric and adolescent patients, with variable outcomes. This patient's coexisting nutritional compromise and subsequent need for transition to an eating disorder program further highlight the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach involving rheumatology, psychiatry, neurology, and nutrition.

Timely recognition of NPSLE, especially when neuropsychiatric symptoms are the first sign, and appropriate inclusion of lupus in the differential diagnosis of acute adolescent psychosis are critical for optimizing patient outcomes. This case contributes to the growing body of evidence that psychosis may be the initial manifestation of lupus in youth and stresses the need for heightened clinical awareness during psychiatric evaluation. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for autoimmune causes in acute adolescent psychosis of unclear etiology.
