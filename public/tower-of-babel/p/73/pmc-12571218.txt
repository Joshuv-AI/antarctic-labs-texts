Nanoscale Characterization of Atomic Positions in Orthorhombic Perovskite Thin Films
Authors: M. Martirosyan, S. Passuti, G. Masset, J. Varignon, H. Chintakindi, J. Ghanbaja, S. Migot, E. Delacotte, A. Benedit‐Cárdenas, L. Pasquier, K. Dumesnil, L. Palatinus, W. Prellier, A. David, Ph. Boullay, O. Copie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe crystal structure determines many of the physical properties of oxide perovskites (ABO3) and only a tiny modification of the lattice structure causes major changes in the functional properties through the interplay among spin, orbital and charge orders. The determination of distortions and their associated symmetries is a valuable asset for understanding the structure properties relationship and guiding the design of epitaxial oxide heterostructures and correlated electronic states. Here, the in‐depth structural characterization of a 50 nm‐LaVO3 thin film grown onto (110)‐oriented DyScO3 by molecular beam epitaxy is reported. The heterostructure is investigated by means of X‐ray diffraction, high‐resolution and scanning transmission electron microscopies, scanning precession electron diffraction tomography and first‐principles calculations. LaVO3 crystallizes in the orthorhombic Pbnm space group and is constrained by the substrate, which imposes a growth along the [110] orthorhombic direction, over the 140 deposited unit cells. The mapping of the reciprocal space allows determining the orientation of the film and refining the lattice parameters. Using scanning transmission electron microscopy, the structure of LaVO3 is analyzed, focusing on the determination of the antipolar displacement of the rare earth. Additionally, 3D electron diffraction enables to resolve the atomic positions of all species within the film.

Introduction

Beyond the undistorted Pm3¯m cubic structure, the compounds with a perovskite structure can crystallize in various space groups. In particular, many transition metal oxides with the ABO3 perovskite structure adopt distorted variants of the ideal cubic Pm3¯m prototype to accommodate the size mismatch between A‐site cations (alkali, alkaline earth, or rare earth elements) and B‐site transition metals. To relieve the resulting strain from A–O and B–O bond length mismatches, these compounds most often crystallize in the orthorhombic Pbnm space group, characterized by cooperative rotations of the oxygen octahedra, though other distorted structures may also emerge depending on composition and external conditions. In the Pbnm space group, the cooperative BO6 octahedra rotations are described as a − a − c + tilt pattern according to Glazer's notation[ 1 ]: i.e., out‐of‐phase rotations along the [110] and [1¯10] directions and in‐phase rotation along the [001] direction. Due to the change in the A–O covalency, the rotations of the BO6 octahedra lead to a shift of A cations from their position in cubic symmetry. [ 2 ] All these atomic displacements resulting from structural changes contribute to the interplay between the charge, orbital and spin degrees of freedom of the electrons and give rise to complex orbital and spin ordered phases.[ 3 , 4 ]

Tuning the bond angles and lengths in epitaxially strained heterostructures is an efficient way for tailoring the functional properties of ABO3 systems and even promoting new properties otherwise absent in the bulk material.[ 5 ] These include the RVO3 series (R is a rare earth or yttrium). Indeed, the spins of the V3 + 3d electrons, occupying the t 2g orbitals, can order at a temperature higher than the bulk material, when PrVO3 is compressively strained on LaAlO3 (−2.9% lattice mismatch).[ 6 , 7 ] When grown on SrTiO3 (+0.1% lattice mismatch),[ 8 ] the tuning of the intra‐plane and inter‐plane magnetic superexchange interactions between nearest neighbors V3 + yields a non‐monotonous evolution of spin ordering temperature through uniaxial strain engineering.[ 9 ] It is also worth mentioning that LaAlO3 (R3¯c) and SrTiO3 (Pm3¯m) have very different tilt patterns, a − a − a − and a 0 a 0 a 0, respectively. This yields differences owing to the necessity to maintain the interface connectivity, as shown in LaNiO3 thin films,[ 10 ] and provides an additional leverage for property control.[ 11 ] Apart from this, the epitaxial strain has also an effect on the orbital transition, as in LaVO3 thin films[ 12 ] and YVO3/LaAlO3 superlattices.[ 13 , 14 ] RVO3 is a prototypical platform for investigating t 2g orbital physics, interactions with spin orders, and the couplings with structural distortions, such as relatively weak Jahn‐Teller (JT) distortions.[ 15 ] This JT distortion, consisting essentially in long and short V−O bonds length along the pseudo‐cubic directions,[ 16 ] allows lifting the degeneracy between the V t 2g levels. Hence, the two electrons per V3 + site are located either in the d xz or in the d yz orbitals, d xy being always occupied. As the temperature decreases, RVO3 undergo a structural phase transition from orthorhombic Pbnm to monoclinic P21/b.[ 15 , 17 ] Concurrently, the cooperative JT distortions promote a G‐type orbital ordering (GOO, see Figure 1 ). At low temperature, superexchange interactions stabilize then a C‐type antiferromagnetic spin ordering of the V spins (CSO, see Figure 1), i.e. ferromagnetic coupling of antiferromagnetic planes along the [001] direction of the Pbnm cell. Since controlling the properties of RVO3 is important for their potential applications, it is essential to delve into the structure of this compound deposited as thin films and to obtain reliable atomic positions for properties modeling.

Here, we report on the epitaxial growth of LaVO3 (LVO) by molecular beam epitaxy on (110)‐oriented DyScO3 (DSO) substrates and the in‐depth characterization of the film structure. In the literature, LVO thin films have been grown by pulsed laser deposition (PLD) on various substrates[ 18 , 19 ] and by MBE,[ 20 , 21 ] with a strong interest in the electronic[ 22 , 23 ] and optical properties.[ 24 , 25 ] The occurrence of hybrid improper ferroelectricity[ 26 , 27 , 28 ] in short‐period LaVO3/PrVO3 superlattices has been predicted with an electrical control of orbital orderings, natively coupled to the spin orders.[ 29 ] The proposed ferroelectric character of the superlattice is due to the coupling between VO6 rotations and R‐cation in‐plane anti‐polar motions (X5− mode) and between the JT distortions and the out‐of‐plane anti‐polar motions (X3− mode). In RVO3/R'VO3 superlattices, X5− and X3− motions become polar P xy and P z motions, respectively. However, this way to design ferroelectricity in otherwise non‐ferroelectric Pbnm systems requires the [001] orthorhombic direction to be along the growth direction.[ 29 ]

While compressively strained LVO is (110)‐oriented and multidomain when grown on (001)‐oriented SrTiO3,[ 21 , 30 , 31 ] tensely strained LVO is (001)‐oriented when grown on (110)‐oriented DSO by PLD.[ 32 ] Recently, the design of spin and orbital ordering patterns in YVO3 was achieved by growing 80 unit cells onto different surface orientations of an orthorhombic substrate, with specific octahedral rotations and cation displacements.[ 33 ] However, the demonstration of a competition between film/substrate oxygen octahedral connectivity and epitaxial strain, induced by the lattice mismatch in LVO grown on (110)‐oriented DSO, revealed that the film and substrate orientations can differ beyond a critical thickness of approximately 70 unit cells.[ 34 ]

Both LVO and DSO crystallize in the Pbnm symmetry (space group no. 62). The lattice parameters are a o = 5.555 Å, b o = 5.549 Å, c o = 7.849 Å for LVO,[ 17 , 35 ] and a s = 5.438 Å, b s = 5.714 Å, c s = 7.897 Å for DSO.[ 32 ] Based on the simple consideration of the pseudo‐cubic lattice parameters of bulk DSO and LVO, we can calculate the expected in‐plane biaxial strain ϵ experienced by the LVO film for different orientations given in the Pbnm crystal reference frame, i.e. the LVO [001] direction is parallel or perpendicular to the growth direction. For the latter, the [001] can be parallel or perpendicular to the DSO [001] direction. Hence, for LVO[110]‖DSO[110], we obtain ϵ001 = 0.61% (0.54%) and ε1¯10=0.43%(0.50%) for the LVO [001] direction parallel (perpendicular) to the in‐plane DSO [001] direction. For LVO[001]‖DSO[110] we have ε1¯10=0.54% and ϵ110 = 0.43%. Hence, these strain values leads us to anticipate the [001] axis orientation parallel to the growth direction, as it should minimize the strain energy. As will be shown below, however, the 50‐nm‐thick LVO film is found to be only (110)‐oriented.

Results and Discussion

We have first investigated the LVO possible phases by optimizing, with first‐principles simulations using density functional theory (DFT), the structure for two growth orientations and two possible magnetic orders (see Experimental Section). A schematics of the growth orientations that we have considered are presented in Figure 1a, along with a sketch of the orbital and spin orderings in Figure 1b,c, respectively. In Table 1 , we show the results of the geometry relaxations considering different spin orders. The ground state corresponds to the [110] directions of LVO and DSO being parallel. The corresponding magnetic ground state is CSO, in agreement with the literature.[ 15 ] However, GSO magnetic state is also possible, whose energy is only 1 meV f.u.−1 higher. These states are associated to different structure symmetries, CSO with P1¯ and GSO with P1121/m (c is the unique axis), corresponding to P21/b and Pbnm for the bulk material,[ 9 , 29 ] respectively. Although the [110]‐oriented LVO structure is the most stable, the small energy separation between the CSO and GSO magnetic states indicates actually two metastable states at 0 K, which are associated to different crystal structure symmetries.

We have also performed a symmetry mode analysis[ 36 , 37 ] of the structures with respect to a high symmetry Pm3¯m cell with a lattice parameter that agrees with the ground state volume. A pseudo‐cubic lattice parameter of 3.9367 Å is determined from the DFT calculations. The results are summarized in Table 1. At 0 K, the derived structures are made of the oxygen octahedral rotation modes (a − a − c 0 and a 0 a 0 c + in Glazer's notation),[ 1 ] the X5− mode and the cooperative JT modes (Q2+ and Q2−). In‐phase Q2+ mode favors a C‐type ordering (COO) of the V3 + occupied orbitals, while out‐of‐phase Q2− mode favors a G‐type ordering (GOO). For the [001] growth direction and in agreement with the Kugel–Khomskii mechanism,[ 38 ] the Q2− and the Q2+ dominate for the CSO and GSO spin orders, respectively. For the [110] growth direction, the out‐of‐phase and in‐phase JT modes dominate for the P1¯ (P21/b in bulk) and P1121/m (Pbnm in bulk) structure symmetries, respectively. This agrees with the corresponding CSO/GOO and GSO/COO spin/orbital states. Furthermore, the calculated results shown in Table 1 indicate also that the amplitude of the a − a − c 0 mode has decreased, while those of the a 0 a 0 c + and X5− have increased, when the growth is [110]‐oriented compared the [001]‐oriented one.

Guided by our theoretical investigation of a LVO film on DSO (we note that first‐rinciples calculations are performed below the structural phase transition temperature),[ 35 ] we have then deposited 140 unit cells of LVO onto (110)‐oriented DSO by MBE in a background pressure of distilled ozone (see Methods). A layer‐by‐layer growth mode, monitored by in situ Reflective High Energy Electron Diffraction (RHEED), and a stoichiometric incorporation of oxygen during the growth indicated a high crystalline quality of the film (see Supporting Information).

To get into the details of the growth of LVO on (110)‐DSO, we have investigated the film's microstructure by High‐Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM). The observations were performed on the LVO cross‐section taken along the [11¯0] zone axis of DSO (see Experimental Section). We show a representative HRTEM image in Figure 2a, where we observe a sharp interface (delimited by a white dotted and dashed line) between DSO (bottom) and LVO (top), whose thickness is around 53 nm, i.e., 136 unit cells, consistent with the RHEED and X‐ray diffraction (XRD) experiments (see Supporting Information). In Figure 2a, we show the electron diffraction patterns (EDP) of LVO and DSO, which exhibit reflections compatible with a Pbnm (2ap×2ap×2ap) [11¯0] zone axis pattern. The reflections highlighted in orange, distinct from those of the cubic perovskite Pm3¯m (a p ) subcell (highlighted in white), indicate the presence of a supercell associated with a doubling of the cell parameter along the [001] direction expected for the Pbnm space group. In the bulk material, this doubling is induced by the a − a − c + octahedral tilt pattern and the displacements of the rare earth. The doubling of the cell parameter along the [001] direction appears as a striped contrast perpendicular to the [001] direction, in the HRTEM image, both in LVO and DSO. Hence, the LVO film follows the orientation imposed by DSO with the epitaxial relationship being LVO[110]‖DSO[110] and LVO[001]‖DSO[001]. This observation agrees with our DFT calculations. In Figure 2b,c, we show a sketch of the bulk LVO atomic structure projected along the [1¯10] and [001] directions, respectively. The characteristic antipolar motion of the La cations along the [110] direction is observed, shifting alternatively upward and downward along the [001] direction (X5−). For this projection, the apparent distance between consecutive shifted cations is represented by the parameter Rdisp. In Figure 2d, we show an image captured by Scanning Tranmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) in High‐Angle Annular Dark‐Field (HAADF) mode. As this technique is most sensitive to heavy elements, we can actually visualize and map the antipolar La motions.[ 21 ] In order to highlight the atomic positions, we carried out a numerical analysis of the raw STEM‐HAADF image.[ 39 ] The atomic positions are extracted and compared with an undistorted reference, which is the average of the vertical and horizontal positions (see Experimental Section). The mapping of the La displacements and amplitudes on this projection allows the determination of both in‐plane and out‐of‐plane octahedral tilts, i.e., along the [001] and [110] directions, respectively. Focusing on the atomic displacement map in Figure 2e, we observe an alternating contrast in the [001] direction and across the substrate/film interface. This characterizes the X5− motion, which is present in both DSO and LVO, although the amplitudes are different. As the antipolar motion and oxygen octahedra rotation are coupled by a trilinear energetic term,[ 29 ] this also shows the continuity of the oxygen octahedra rotation across the interface. In Figure 2f, we show the amplitude of R cation displacement (Rdisp) along the growth direction, as a function of the distance from the interface. Owing to its small ionic radius, Dy3 + promotes high‐angle oxygen octahedra rotations in DSO, which in turn favor large Dy displacements, as we see below the interface in Figure 2f. Crossing the interface, the amplitude of the La displacement drops down to 20 pm after a region of ca. 3 unit cells where the LVO film experiences the effect of the larger structural distortions of the substrate. We note that this differs from the DFT calculations. As a comparison, we display in Figure 2g the case of LVO grown on (001)‐oriented SrTiO3 (STO) (see details in Ref. [21]), where no structural distortion exists at room temperature for STO. In this case, there should be no Sr atom displacement in the STO part. In Figure 2g, the displacement is not zero and gives then an estimation of the uncertainty which is close to standard deviations. Crossing the interface, the amplitude of the La displacement increases up to 30 pm over ca. 6 unit cells. The mapping of the R displacements and amplitudes, obtained from the [1¯10] Pbnm orientation, allows the determination of the in‐phase octahedral tilts, along the [001] direction as well. Over a short length‐scale close to film/substrate interface, the octahedra rotations gradually decreases or increases for DSO and STO, respectively. This shows the importance of the coherent connectivity of the oxygen octahedra at the interface that allows a octahedral rotational control,[ 11 , 28 ] exploiting the octahedra tilt amplitude and pattern mismatches, as well as the lattice mismatch. Based on this initial result, there is a clear difference between the (110)‐ and (001)‐oriented LVO phases separated by a switching plane reported in the literature[ 34 ] and the uniformly (110)‐oriented LVO film we show here, although the substrate is (110)‐oriented DSO for both cases. Driven by energetics, the transition to two‐phased film occurs beyond a critical thickness between 60 and 74 unit cells deposited by PLD.[ 34 ] However, we observe here a single (110)‐oriented LVO film with a thickness up to about 140 unit cells deposited by MBE. This underscore the influence of the deposition technique employed which, as the kinetics of the deposited species, along with the growth temperature and pressure,[ 40 , 41 ] collectively affect the growth mechanism and the balance between the lattice mismatch and interfacial octahedral connectivity.

Having characterized the structure of LVO at the atomic scale, we performed high‐resolution XRD (see Experimental Section) to determine how epitaxial strains affect the LVO structure on a larger scale. We show in Figure 3a–d series of reciprocal space maps (RSM) measured around the symmetric LVO (110), (220), (330), and (440) Bragg peaks, respectively. Figure 3e–h displays the asymmetric (332), (240), (332¯), and (420) Bragg peaks, respectively. This series of Bragg peaks, whose positions are compatible with a Pbnm cell, show that the LVO film (referred as F in Figure 3) was grown coherently on DSO (referred as S in Figure 3). The high crystalline quality of the film is attested by Laue fringes that are clearly seen for all film's peaks, whose width along q || is rather narrow. In an orthorhombic unit cell, the difference between the a‐ and b‐lattice constants appears as a difference in (240) and (420) Bragg peak position along q ⊥, as for DSO in Figure 3f,h. However, the peaks of LVO show almost identical positions, within our experimental accuracy. This is in good agreement with a LVO film experiencing a tensile stress leading to a ≈ b with the [110] direction remaining perpendicular to the surface.[ 9 , 42 ] To get further insights, we have also measured additionnal Bragg peaks displayed in Figure 3i–o: ‐ (111), (200), (202), and (204), respectively. Assuming the Pbnm space group, the (111) peak corresponds to one of the half‐order Bragg peak in pseudo‐cubic frame (Pm3¯m): (1201)p, (0121)p, (1012)p, and (0112)p (p stands for pseudo‐cubic). These peaks, originating from the quadrupled perovskite unit cell, depend on the orientation and the population of the domains present in the film. In the Figure 3i,j, we observe that only the (1201) reflection exists, while the others are absent. This means the LVO film consists in a single orientation variant, determined by the (110)‐oriented DSO substrate, which imposes its crystallographic orientation on a large scale. The structural coupling at the interface is facilitated by both LVO and DSO having the same Pbnm space group with similar structural distortions, beyond the mere consideration of the strain. From the absolute 2θ position of all Bragg peaks, we have refined the unit cell lattice parameters.[ 43 ] The 2θ position list and the unit cell parameters for a (110)‐oriented growth are summarized in Table 2 . We have first considered the Pbnm space group and we find the a‐ and b‐lattice parameters are shortened and almost equal. While ϵ110 describes a compressive strain, in agreement with the experimental value deduced from XRD (−0.61%, see Supporting Information), ϵ001 has increased and ε1¯10 is negative, compared to the expected value (see above). Thereby, considering the group‐subgroup relationship,[ 44 ] we have also calculated the unit cell parameters assuming a monoclinic space group. This introduces an additional degree of freedom, allowing the angle between the free a‐ and b‐axis to accommodate the strain, along with the a and b axis lengths.[ 30 , 42 ] We note that given the small difference between a and b the [110] direction could be slighty tilted by a small angle (2tan−1(a/b) ≈ 0.06°). Compared to the volume of the bulk material (242.1 Å3), it is equal to 241.3 Å3 and 243.1 Å3 for the orthorhombic and monoclinic structures, respectively. In Table 2, we find that the strain in the [001] and [1¯10] directions are closer to the expected value (see above), with γ different but close to 90°. A monoclinic cell agrees with an epitaxially strained LVO thin film. But, the strain along the [110] direction is now very different from the experimental value (−0.61%, see Supporting Information). As the monoclinic angle is very close to 90° due to the small number of accessible Bragg peaks, it remains difficult to distinguish between the P1121/m and Pbnm space groups, which seem to correspond to a strained and an unstrained scenario, respectively. We have thus deepened further the structure analysis of the LVO thin film.

To accurately determine the atomic structure of the LVO film, structural refinements using diffraction data, similar to those performed on bulk materials, would be a valuable asset. Over the past decade, several advances in 3D electron diffraction (3D ED) techniques[ 45 ] have enabled the structure analysis of crystalline domains in thin film smaller than 100 nm.[ 46 , 47 ] The determination of the LVO structure—across the film's thickness—requires to carry out 3D ED with an enhanced spatial resolution. To this end, we recently proposed to combine precession‐assisted 3D ED with a line scan along the growth direction.[ 48 ] This approach, known as scanning precession electron tomography (SPET), enables the collection of 3D ED data from multiple adjacent regions of interest (ROI) (see Experimental Section). The first step in SPET data analysis involved the extraction of a tilt series from the SPET data stack corresponding to the DSO substrate. This provides a determination of the calibration constant (value in Å per pixel) and a correction of the distortions caused by the microscope's optical system and the electron beam motion.[ 49 ] The DSO cell parameters are then used as an internal reference, enabling the lattice parameters of the LVO thin film to be determined more accurately.[ 48 ] The diffraction patterns from regions near the surface of the film (LVO top) and near the substrate/film interface (LVO bottom) were extracted to reconstruct the precession‐assisted 3D tilt series for both ROIs. During the tomography, the tilting was performed along the same direction, indicated by a black arrow in Figure 4a, in such a way that the probed film area was not shadowed by the substrate. The initial analysis identified an orthorhombic crystal system with a Pbnm space group and an (110)‐oriented LVO for both regions of the films. The refined a, b, c lattice parameters for the orthorhombic cell were determined as follows: 5.541(3) Å, 5.532(3) Å, 7.890(2) Å close to the surface (LVO top) and 5.539(3) Å, 5.538(3) Å, 7.890(2) Å close the film/substrate interface (LVO bottom). The unit cell volumes are 241.8 Å3 (near the surface) and 242.0 Å3 (near the interface), close to the bulk unit cell volume (242.1 Å3). a and b are 0.2–0.3% higher than those determined by XRD, while c remains strained to match the corresponding parameter of DSO, consistent with the LVO [001] direction lying in the substrate plane. Between the two ROIS, a and b lattice parameters change by 0.03% and 0.1%, respectively. However, this difference might be underestimated. We note that we have also considered the case of a monoclinic unit cell. Neverthless, the obtained very tiny deviation from 90° of γ may equally arises from underestimated uncertainties rather than an actual deviation in symmetry. Given the 15 nm diamater beam used in our SPED experiment, the measured cell parameters represent an average over the probed area and cannot capture stronger local variations, including any that may be present near the film/substrate interface.

We have ultimately reached the most valuable contribution of 3D ED in general, and SPET in this study, i.e., the quantitative crystallographic informations on the evolution of the atomic positions within the LVO film. To achieve an accurate structure refinement, we used the so‐called dynamical refinement procedure to account for both dynamical scatterings effects and the precession motion of the electron beam[ 50 ] based on precession‐assisted 3D ED data (see Experimental Section and Supporting Information). Comparing the atomic positions reported in Table 3 , the top and bottom LVO regions exhibit the same structure within standard deviations. The refinement also indicates no atomic vacancies, as suggested by the low values of the atomic displacement parameters Uiso. These results are consistent with the findings from STEM‐HAADF observations, which uses a smaller probe size and smaller scanning step size. In particular, from the full dynamical structure refinements, the displacement of the La3 + cations projected onto the [1¯10] direction was determined to be 22.7 pm, in excellent agreement with the displacements obtained from the STEM‐HAADF image analysis. We have also performed a symmetry mode analysis of the structures. The results are summarized in Table 3 for the experimental structures and as for the DFT calculated structures, the distortion modes are the a − a − c 0 and a 0 a 0 c + oxygen octahedral rotation modes, the X5− mode and the Q2+ and Q2− cooperative JT modes. The amplitude of the distortion modes are reduced as we move away from the 0 K calculated phases (see Table 1) and different from the bulk values. As for the bulk material, we observe that only the Q2+ mode is active at room temperature, reminiscent of a Pbnm space group (P1121/m in thin film). The oxygen octahedral rotation and JT amplitudes increase across the film thickness towards values close to the bulk materials (within our experimental accuracy). We have determined the oxygen octahedral rotation angles (see Figure 4b) which are reduced compared to the bulk materials (α = β = 8.800° and γ = 7.555°[ 35 ]). While the Q2− remains quenched across the thickness at a value lower than the bulk, the amplitude of Q2+ is 10 times higher at the surface compared to the interface and the bulk. The DFT calculations showed that GSO and CSO states are close in energy. Hence, the Q2+/Q2− imbalance between the top and the bottom of the film may favor the coexistence and/or a gradient of GSO/CSO ordered spin populations (and the associated COO/GOO) throughout the film thickness below the Néel temperature.[ 15 ] For example, the Néel vector associated to GSO and CSO may be rotated by using external stimuli such as strain fields in (110)‐oriented RVO3 heterostructures or susceptible to be manipulated by an electric or magnetic field. It seems the strain experienced by the LVO unit cell is two‐faced. On the one hand, our experimental results indicate indeed that the a, b, and c lattice parameters are constrained throughout the film thickness. On the other hand, the evolution of the octahedral tilt/rotation amplitude, of the Q2+ mode determined by SPET, and of the X5− mode determined by STEM‐HAADF suggest that the atomic positions relax within the constrained cell.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have presented a thorough structural investigation of a 50‐nm LaVO3 thin film grown on (110)‐oriented DyScO3. Both theoretical and experimental analyses demonstrate that the film adopts the bulk orthorhombic Pbnm space group while being epitaxially strained by the substrate, which enforces the (110) crystallographic orientation throughout the 140 deposited unit cells. Advanced characterization techniques enabled the precise determination of atomic positions within the perovskite film, allowing a detailed symmetry mode analysis to identify key structural distortion modes in the structure. Notably, the strained film should exhibit a lowering of symmetry to a monoclinic P1121/m structure on few unit cells at the interface, where oxygen octahedra tilts and antipolar cation displacements accommodate the epitaxial strain and connectivity constraints. Since spin‐orbital interactions in vanadates and related transition‐metal perovskites are closely linked to such structural distortions, obtaining reliable atomic‐scale structural information is crucial for accurate modeling of their electronic and magnetic properties. This combined experimental and theoretical approach highlights the strong interplay between lattice symmetry, strain, and functional properties in oxide thin films.

Experimental Section

First‐Principles Calculations. The first‐principles calculations were performed using density functional theory (DFT) with the VASP package[ 51 , 52 ] and employing the PBEsol functional. In addition, a U potential was considered on V 3d levels of 3.5 eV, entering as a single effective parameter.[ 53 ] This parameter was fitted in Ref. [29], providing, in this study, correct electronic, magnetic, and structural features for the LaVO3 ground state. The projected augmented wave (PAW) potential was used to model the core electrons.[ 54 ] In the calculations, a (2a, 2a, 2a) cubic cell was considered, which allows for the oxygen octahedra rotations and Jahn–Teller motions to develop. The energy cutoff was set to 500 eV and a 4 × 4 × 4 k‐point mesh was employed.

Thin Film Growth. The (110)‐oriented DSO substrates, provided by Crystec GmbH (a = 5.44 Å, b = 5.71 Å and c = 7.89 Å), were introduced in a MBE chamber (DCA Instruments) with a base pressure of 6× 10−11 mbar, equipped with reflection high‐energy electron diffraction (RHEED). The atoms were evaporated using high temperature Knudsen cells, whose atomic fluxes were calibrated using a quartz crystal microbalance before and after the deposition. The Pr, La, and V atomic fluxes were fixed to 1.5 × 1013 atom cm−2 s−1 with 5% accuracy. The growth conditions were optimized to obtain high quality single phase films. To get the proper oxygen stoichiometry, an oxidant gas (O3+O2) was introduced at a pressure fixed in the 5–7 × 10−7 mbar range in the chamber. During the growth, the substrate temperature was kept at 850 °C.

X‐Ray Diffraction. The crystallinity and the structure were characterized by X‐ray diffraction (XRD) with a PANalitycal X'pert Pro MRD diffractometer using monochromatic Cu Kα1 radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å). For high resolution reciprocal space mapping, the XRD measurements were performed using a triple axis crystal analyzer. It was pointed out that half‐Bragg's peaks were measured in skew diffraction geometry.

Transmission Electron Microscopy. The atomically resolved microstructure was determined by cross‐sectional high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and high angle annular dark field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). The cross‐section lamella were prepared by focus ion beam with Ga+ ion milling (FEI‐Helios Nanolab 600i) and observed with a double‐aberration corrected JEOL ARM microscope operated at 200 kV. The analysis of the pixel intensity of the STEM‐HAADF consisted in the determination of the nodes of a grid, each node corresponds to an atom. Then, the distances to the first neighbors were calculated yielding to the local average positions of the neighboring atoms. The atomic displacements were analyzed and quantified using the Python library Atomap[ 39 ] and also detailed in the Supporting Information of Ref. [55].

Scanning Precession Electron Diffraction Tomography. The electron diffraction analysis was performed on a cross‐section lamella using a JEOL F200 cFEG microscope working at 200 kV. Scanning Precession Electron diffraction Tomography (SPET) was carried out on the sample by scanning the electron beam (15 nm in diameter) in a direction perpendicular to the film/substrate interface. Every line scan was performed using the NanoMEGAS DigiSTAR unit on a 250 nm line going from the polycrystalline coating to the substrate with a step of 2.5 nm. The acquisitions were carried out in a tilt range of 75° with a tilt step of 1° and a precession‐semi angle of 1.2°. The diffraction patterns were acquired with an ASI Cheetah M3 512 × 512 CMOS hybrid pixel detector using the ACCOS GUI from ASI, each with an exposure time of 0.5 s. In view of this description, a SPET experiment actually corresponds to the acquisition of so‐called precession‐assisted 3D ED datasets on multiple regions of interest located along a line. Each 3D ED datasets was processed using PETS2[ 56 ] for indexation, cell parameters refinement and intensity integration. Subsequent structure refinements were carried out using the JANA2020 program.[ 57 ]
