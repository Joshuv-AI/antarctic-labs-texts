Mental health app boost my mood (BMM) as preventive early intervention for adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms
Authors: Linda Willems, Sanne P A Rasing, Dewi A M Heijs, Ad A Vermulst, Mireille J Huvenaars, Simone A Onrust, Daan H M Creemers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a common mental disorder. Detecting (sub)clinical depressive symptoms in adolescents at an early stage and offering a low-threshold early intervention can minimize the risk of serious and/or long-term depression. As such, a digital intervention can be a low-threshold preventive and early intervention. This study aims to examine whether the Boost My Mood (BMM)-app is a suitable preventive early intervention for adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms.MethodsThis naturalistic single-arm evaluation study (N = 50) was conducted in adolescents aged 16–21 with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the BMM-app was studied in relation to anxiety, worrying, stress, and sleeping problems. An exploratory objective was to determine whether positive expectations and social support are related to app use.ResultsThe study showed a significant decrease in not only depressive symptoms, but also anxiety, worrying and stress while using the BMM-app. Sleeping problems did not significantly decrease over time while using the BMM-app. The degree of use of the BMM-app and telling significant others about using the BMM-app were both not related to a decrease in depressive symptoms. The BMM-app was used significantly more when the adolescent had told relatives about their depressive symptoms.ConclusionsA digital intervention, such as the BMM-app, can be a low-threshold preventive and early intervention for adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. Beneficial effects of the BMM-app were reported on depressive symptoms as well as other aspects of quality of life, such as anxiety, worrying, and stress. Whereas several factors may have played a role in the current findings on depressive symptoms, there are reasons to assume that part of the reduction in symptoms could be attributed to the BMM-app. Although no causality can be assumed, this study is a first step in the implementation of preventive apps in mental health care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19666-5.Keywords: Depression, Adolescents, Early intervention, Digital intervention, Prevention

Background

Depression and adolescents

Depressive disorders, characterized by abnormal depressed mood or reduced interest in almost all activities [1, 2], are among the most common mental disorders in adolescents [3]. The largest increase in the prevalence of a first depressive episode is between 15 and 21 years of age. One in eight adolescents develops at least one depressive episode, lasting an average of six to nine months [1, 4, 5].

Depression during adolescence is strongly related to stress, worrying, sleeping problems, anxiety, dropping out of school, and increased incidence of smoking, drug abuse, and obesity [6, 7] and is one of the most important risk factors for suicidal behavior and death by suicide [8]. Depression during adolescence can have lifelong consequences in several domains [1, 9–13]. When intervening too late, the risk for nonresponse to treatment and thus chronic depression increases [1, 14]. Noyes et al. [15] propose that depression should be viewed as a spectrum disorder, due to the many similarities between subclinical depression and major depressive symptoms and the high risk for developing a major depressive disorder when subclinical symptoms are present. Therefore, it is important to recognize (sub)clinical depressive symptoms in an early stage and to intervene adequately during adolescence to positively influence the course of depression.

Research has shown that preventive early interventions based on evidence-based techniques, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), are effective in reducing and preventing depression among adolescents with (sub)clinical symptoms [1, 16]. These interventions are usually offered in a group setting and are cost-effective when offered by well-trained professionals and provided in close proximity to adolescents, such as high schools [11, 13, 17].

Despite these promising results, most adolescents with (sub)clinical depression do not receive any treatment or prevention [18]. Adolescents are difficult to motivate for participation in preventive interventions, and drop-out rates are high, limiting effectiveness [19]. Adolescents often do not feel the need to look for help, because they do not recognize their symptoms, are ashamed of their problems [11, 13, 20], have little confidence in regular care, or prefer to solve their problems independently [21]. Moreover, preventive interventions are typically organized within mental health care, which has a limited reach [22]. Further research on preventive early interventions for adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms can help us to define which methods are effective and meet the specific needs and preferences of adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms.

Digital intervention as preventive early intervention

Digital technologies, such as psychological interventions through internet programs (i.e., e-mental health) or smartphone applications (apps), can be a new solution to broaden and optimize access to health interventions. One of the first investigated e-mental health methods was internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy (iCBT), which has a similar effect on depressive symptoms in adults as face-to-face CBT if guided by a practitioner. Unguided iCBT, on the other hand, has a smaller but still significant clinical effect compared to a waiting list control group [23–25] and has an advantage in situations where access to traditional psychological interventions is limited or delayed [26]. When compared to a waitlist condition, iCBT, both guided and unguided, has also been found to be effective in treating depressive symptoms in adolescents under the age of 25 with enhanced depressive symptoms or a diagnosed depression (95% BI: 0.55–0.90) [27].

E-mental health has increasingly been available via smartphone apps. Advantages of app-based psychological interventions are the availability, accessibility, freedom of time and space, and flexibility in self-guidance. Almost all adolescents have smartphones and carry them around most of the day. This lowers the barrier to using psychological intervention apps installed on smartphones. Moreover, psychoeducation and treatment information can be accessed at all times via the app [26, 27].

The usage of a psychological intervention app depends on several factors: the app should be effective in reducing symptoms, have an appealing layout and content for the target group, should use automated encouragement [28, 29], and provide the user with choices and personalized interventions [30]. Many apps aim to reduce depressive symptoms and are designed for all ages [31], but feasibility studies focusing on the use of these apps to reduce depression symptoms in specific age groups remain limited. Christensen et al. [32] emphasize the importance of feasibility studies in assessing the suitability, acceptability, and effectiveness of digital interventions prior to implementing a mental health app in clinical practice. To our knowledge, there is no scientific evidence available yet for the suitability of a CBT-based app as a preventive early intervention for adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms.

To determine whether a CBT-based app is suitable, it is not only crucial that the intervention is effective but also that the app will be used by the target group. However, information is sparse on how to stimulate adolescents to use these apps. For example, there are mixed findings on the relation between dose-response (i.e., the impact of the degree of use on effectiveness) and the effectiveness of digital interventions in adults [33]. For the implementation, it is important to know whether adolescents need to be stimulated to increase the times they log in to the app or to spend as much time as possible on the app or whether these factors are not related to the effects. Hence, further research is needed.

Further research could focus on which factors play a role in the degree to which an app will be used. For example, the expectations of the user with regard to the impact of the app on reducing depressive and related symptoms, such as anxiety, worrying, stress, and sleeping problems, could influence the degree of use of the app. Previous studies showed that therapy compliance for chronic disease and parents’ expectations about psychotherapy for their child can be a predictor of therapy compliance and completion of the intervention [34, 35]. In other studies, it was found that (expected) confidence of adults with severe depression and/or anxiety in the effectiveness of e-health interventions predicted a better therapy outcome and a higher degree of use [36, 37]. However, the effect of a user’s expectation on the actual use of the app has not yet been investigated for app-based depression prevention for adolescents.

Another factor that could improve the use of the preventive early intervention app is social support. Commitment to psychological treatment for suicidal symptoms appears to be greater when significant others are informed about the symptoms [38]. Research by Grist et al. [26] confirms that parental support increases the effectiveness of a digital psychological intervention. It has not yet been studied whether telling significant others about screened (sub)clinical depressive symptoms and/or the plan to use the depression prevention app affects the degree to which the adolescent uses the app.

Current study

More scientific evidence for the suitability of a CBT-based app as a preventive early intervention for adolescents with (sub)clinical depression is needed. The Boost My Mood (BMM)-app was designed and developed by the Trimbos Institute (2017–2018). The BMM-app is an application for smartphones specifically focused on adolescents aged 16 years and older to reduce their (sub)clinical depressive and comorbid symptoms, such as sleeping problems, anxiety, worrying, and stress independently. This target group was chosen, because during this period the increase in depressive symptoms is greatest [39], meaning that the need for intervention is greatest in this age group. The Intervention Mapping roadmap [40] was used to tailor the app to the needs of this target group.

In this study, we examined whether the BMM-app is a suitable preventive early intervention for adolescents aged 16–21 with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. Our first aim was to study whether the severity of depressive symptoms decreases while using the BMM-app. Our secondary aim was to study whether symptoms related to depressive symptoms (stress, worrying, sleeping problems, and anxiety) changed while using the BMM-app. Furthermore, we studied whether the BMM-app was actually used by the adolescents (number of times used and duration) and whether this was related to a decrease in symptoms. Finally, we explored which factors are related to the use of the app. Specifically, positive expectations about the impact of the BMM-app and looking for social support by telling significant others about screened (sub)clinical depressive symptoms or using the app were considered possible factors that can increase the degree of use of the app. We expected a positive relation between sharing the existence of depressive symptoms and the plan to use the app with the degree of use.

Methods

Ethical aspects

The research proposal was examined and approved by the Medical Ethical Committee (METC) Utrecht (NL65545.041.18) and the Committee Scientific Research and Innovation GGZ Oost Brabant.

Design

This study was part of a larger research project to determine the usability and effectiveness of the BMM-app in three phases of the care trajectory: preventive early intervention, waiting list intervention, and relapse prevention. The current (N = 50) naturalistic single-arm evaluation (feasibility) study examined the app as a preventive early intervention. Importantly, the current study did not include an active control group (such as psychoeducation) or passive control group (no intervention), because an impression of usability had to be obtained in this initial stage of the developmental process before extensive effect research would be conducted.

Participants and procedure

Between February 2019 and December 2020, participants were recruited at five high schools in The Netherlands (region North-East Brabant). North-East Brabant has 674,380 inhabitants and is one of the 28 regions that directly commissions specialized mental health services at a local level in the Netherlands [41]. This region can be characterized as consisting of average households with median income, average socio-economic status, and average level of education for adolescents. Participants were (1) adolescents aged 16–21 with (2) (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. Adolescents were excluded from participation when they (1) were already receiving treatment or (2) had a high risk of suicidality.

Depressive symptoms and suicidality were measured with the Children’s Depression Inventory 2 (CDI-2), a self-report survey for ages 8–21 years [42, 43]. This survey contains 28 items about feelings or thoughts of the adolescents in the past two weeks that are scored on a 3-point scale with 0 “I am sometimes sad”, 1 “I am often sad”, and 2 “I am always sad”. Higher scores indicate more or more frequently recognized symptoms. Because screening is an early detection of (sub)clinical depressive symptoms, we chose a cut-off score of 10 (50th percentile) to identify adolescents with above-average depressive symptoms. The CDI-2 is a reliable standardized instrument that is representative of Dutch adolescents (α = 0.79) [43]. Given the inclusion criteria for age (16–21 years), only students in the final two years of higher general secondary education (in Dutch: havo) and pre-university education (in Dutch: vwo) were screened for depressive symptoms. Students and parents received written information and a consent form about the study and screening for (sub)clinical depressive symptoms.

In case a student showed a high risk for suicide (CDI-2 item 8 = 2), the student was invited for a meeting with a health care psychologist (in Dutch: GZ-psycholoog) or remedial educationalist within 48 h after screening. In accordance with the multidisciplinary guidelines for suicidal behavior, an estimation of suicide risk was made. Parents of students with a high risk for suicide were contacted and informed and, together with their child, they received advice and were referred to care when necessary.

Students with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms (CDI-2 ≥ 10) received verbal and written information about the outcome, and were invited to participate in this study. Participants returned a signed informed consent prior to the start of the study. After written informed consent was obtained, the participants completed the online questionnaires (T0; baseline) and were then instructed to use the BMM-app for at least four weeks. Push notifications were used to stimulate participants to use the app. After four (T1) and twelve (T2) weeks, the questionnaires were filled out again. Participants received 15 euros after completing the questionnaires at all three time-points.

Intervention

The smartphone app BMM is tailored to the needs of adolescents by means of Intervention Mapping, a method to effectively develop psychological interventions [40]. A structured step-by-step plan was used to develop the BMM-app. Based on the literature and interviews with therapists and clients, the app focuses on four main themes corresponding to the main symptoms of adolescents with depression: (1) the need for a better mood, (2) less stress, (3) worrying less, and (4) sleeping better. Each theme contains the following three components: psychoeducation; stories and tips of other adolescents who have had depressive symptoms; and CBT-based assignments, such as a journal or activity planner. Adolescents can use a self-test that advises them what to do or can make their own choice.

The app sends a push notification to encourage adolescents to keep using the app, to finish unfinished assignments, to praise their effort, to provide personalized suggestions for assignments or actions to make, and with the advice to contact a general practitioner when journal scores indicate worsening of symptoms. Furthermore, the BMM-app has a built-in monitoring system to store data while the app is being used. Participants gave their consent to collect these data.

Measures

Primary outcome measure

Depression severity: inventory of depressive symptomatology (IDS-SR)

The IDS-SR is a self-reporting instrument containing 30 items measuring depressive symptoms in the last seven days on a 4-point scale [44]. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. The IDS-SR has both excellent psychometrical properties (α = 0.94), concurrent and predictive validity and sensitivity to treatment effects [45].

Secondary outcome measures

Stress symptoms: perceived stress scale (PSS)

ontaining 14 items on a 5-point scale, the PSS is a self-report instrument measuring stress levels in daily situations [46]. Higher stress levels are represented by higher scores. The PSS is reliable (α = 0.85) and has sufficient concurrent and predictive validity for our target group [46].

Anxiety symptoms: state trait anxiety inventory (STAI)

he Dutch version of the STAI, zelfbeoordelingsvragenlijst (ZBV), was used to measure anxiety. Using 20 items, the STAI measures anxiety based on self-reporting on a 4-point scale with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety. The STAI (α = 0.92) and ZBV (α = 0.90) are both reliable tests for people aged 12 and older [47–49].

Worrying: penn state worry questionnaire (PSWQ)

The level of worrying was measured with the Dutch version of the PSWQ, a self-reporting instrument with 16 items on a 5-point scale [50]. The higher the score, the higher the level of worrying. Psychometric properties (α = 0.88; concurrent and predictive validity) are found to be good in college students samples [51].

Sleep: glasgow sleep effort scale (GSES)

The GSES is a 7-item self-reporting instrument measuring sleep effort and experienced sleeping problems in the past 7 days on a 3-point scale. Higher scores indicate more sleeping problems. Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.77) and concurrent and predictive validity are acceptable for people aged 18 and older [52]. The GSES has also been validated for adolescents aged 11–15 years [53].

Other measures

Expectations: the parent expectancies for therapy scale (PETS)

Expectations regarding the impact of the BMM-app were measured at baseline (T0) with the PETS. The PETS consists of 7 items on a 6-point scale, with higher scores indicating more positive expectations [54]. Psychometric properties for both the adult (α = 0.79) and child version (α = 0.87) are good [35, 55].

Social support

Prior to the start of the intervention (T0), adolescents were asked whether they had shared with significant others (parents/siblings, friends, partner, or care provider) that they had depressive symptoms or were planning to use the app.

Background variables

Sex, age, and educational level were registered at T0

App usage

Similar to the study of Mohr et al. [30], app usage was registered in two ways: (1) number of times logged in (i.e., the number of times the adolescent logged in to the BMM-app; when no activity was measured for more than 10 min, the user was automatically logged out), and (2) total duration of using the app (i.e., the total number of minutes the adolescent was logged in). Both were measured in three time slots: T0-T1 (weeks 1–4); T1-T2 (weeks 5–12); and T0-2 (weeks 1–12).

Statistical analyses

First, descriptive statistics of the participants and the means, standard deviations (SD), and correlations of the five outcome measures depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, worrying, stress and sleeping problems were calculated. Next, Latent Growth Curve Analyses (LGCA) [56] were used to investigate change over time for each of the five outcome measures. The statistical program Mplus (version 7.2) was used for these analyses [57]. Missing data were handled by using all available information in the data [58, 59]. Linearity between time (measured at week 0, after week 4 and after week 12) and scores on the outcome measure was assumed. The estimated parameters, based on a Bayesian estimation method, were the intercept B0 (starting value) and the regression coefficient B1 (course over time). To correct for possible effects of the background variables (sex, age, and educational level), these variables are used as time invariant covariates predicting B0 and B1. The estimated model fit is represented by the Posterior Predictive P-value (PPP-value), in which a PPP-value of 0.500 is ideal and PPP-values > 0.050 represent acceptable fit. A bad fit of the linear relation would indicate different changes over time between the first phase (1–4 week) and follow-up phase (5–12 weeks). In that case, t-tests for independent samples should be used instead of LGCA. The model fit in the current study was good (see Table 1). For outcome measures with significant values of B1, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated by multiplying B1 with 12 (the number of weeks) divided by the mean SD over the three time-points for that outcome measure. Here, values larger than 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represent small, medium, and large effects, respectively [60].

Furthermore, we described the number of times adolescents logged in and the time spent on the app both in the first four weeks (T1) and in the following eight weeks (T2). These variables were entered as time-varying covariates in the growth model (time-varying covariate at T1 as predictor of the outcome variable at T1 and time varying covariate at T2 as predictor of the outcome variable at T2) to test their effect on the measured outcomes at T1 and T2 and to examine whether the growth parameters are changed.

A path analysis (to address multiple dependent variables in a regression model) was used for exploratory purposes to investigate whether there was a positive relation between expectations about the app at baseline (PETS-score) and the degree to which the app was used with the two dependent variables “the number of times adolescents logged in” and “the time spent on the app”, both in weeks 1–12 [61]. Finally, a path analysis was used to explore whether the variables “sharing having depressive symptoms with significant others” and “sharing you will use the BMM-app” had a positive effect on the degree to which the app was being used.

Sample size and power

The sample size needed to obtain significant results in an LGCA with a power of 0.80 (N = 40) was calculated with a Monte Carlo study, as described by Muthén et al. [62]. Consistent with a review on iCBT for anxiety and depression in randomized controlled trials with an effect size of 0.72 (95% BI: 0.55–0.90) [27], we used a medium effect size of 0.50 (the lower boundary of the interval) to calculate the power. With these data (N = 40; medium effect size), we calculated a power of 0.80. The initial sample size of the current study was higher (N = 50), leading to a power of 0.87.

Participants

In this study, 220 of the 845 students who were screened for (sub)clinical depressive symptoms met the inclusion criteria. Sixty-eight students were interested in participation and received the information letter and consent form. The signed consent was returned by 50 students, who were then included in the study (see Fig. 1). In line with previously made agreements with participating schools, we collected and analyzed (demographic) data only from those who consented to participate.

Correlations between outcome measures

Correlations between all measures were calculated (see Additional file 1). Each outcome measure showed strong positive correlations across time. Furthermore, we found significant positive correlations of depressive symptoms with stress symptoms and anxiety symptoms. Significant positive correlations were also found of stress symptoms with anxiety symptoms and worrying and of anxiety symptoms with worrying. Interestingly, there were some negative correlations between sleeping problems at T1 and T2 and depressive symptoms.

Discussion

This study is a first step to further explore and implement depression prevention apps. We aimed to examine whether the BMM-app is a suitable preventive early intervention for adolescents aged 16–21 years with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. The results showed a significant decline in depressive symptoms (above medium effect), stress symptoms (medium effect), anxiety symptoms (medium effect) and worrying (small effect) during the period in which the BMM-app was used. Sleeping problems did not decrease significantly over time. Most adolescents did use the BMM-app, although the degree to which adolescents used the app did not significantly affect the reduction in symptoms over time. Both positive expectations about the app and telling loved ones about the app did not have a significant effect on the degree to which the app was being used. There was a significant positive relation between the degree of app use and telling significant others about the depressive symptoms.

The effect sizes of the outcome measures depression, stress, and anxiety are comparable with findings of a meta-analysis by Ebert et al. [27] on digital CBT-treatment for anxiety and depression among adolescents (d = 0.72) and even larger when compared to a meta-analysis on depression prevention programs (d = 0.22) [63]. Whereas the effect size of an intervention tends to be smaller when determined in comparison with a control group, the effect size of the natural course (comparable to a passive control group) is expected to be smaller than the effects of digital interventions. This assumption is supported by the studies of Garrido et al. [64], De Jonge-Heesen et al. [65], and Wijnhoven et al. [66] showing that for depression prevention, control groups have lower effect sizes than experimental groups. Thus, part of the large change in depressive symptoms over time when using the BMM-app could be attributed to the natural course, and part of the symptom reduction may be due to the BMM-app.

Our results showed a reduction in not only depressive symptoms, but also stress, anxiety and worrying. This is in accordance with a study by Hofmann et al. [67] and a meta-analysis by Birmaher et al. [10] indicating that CBT is effective for a broad scale of symptoms in addition to depression, including anxiety and stress. The found effect sizes of the outcome measures depression, stress, and anxiety are similar to the medium to above medium effect sizes for digital CBT-treatment for adolescents with anxiety and depression found in a meta-analysis by Elbert et al. (d = 0.72; 95% BI: 0.55–0.90) [27].

In contrary to the aforementioned mentioned reductions in symptoms, sleeping problems did not reduce while using the BMM-app. A study on the discriminant validity of the GSES showed that insomnia patients had a mean total GSES score of 7.06 (Broomfield & Espie, 2005). In the current study, participants scored even higher than insomnia patients did, as we found mean total GSES scores of 9.81, 10.30, and 10.55. Therefore, it is plausible that the prevention exercises in the BMM-app did not fully meet the needs of the participants and were not sufficient to lower the high levels of sleeping problems in this sample. Further, another explanation could be that the adolescents hardly ever used the sleep intervention component. In the current study, we did not specify which interventions were used how often. This is recommended for follow-up studies.

We did not find a relation between the degree of using the app and symptom reduction, meaning that there seemed to be no dose-response relation. The literature shows mixed findings on whether or not a dose-response relationship exists in depression interventions. Similar to this study, Donkin et al. [33] did not find a dose-response relation for an online intervention for depression. In contrast, a study by Poppelaars et al. [68] showed that minimal use of digital interventions by high school students with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms resulted in symptom reduction. Digital placebo, a phenomenon similar to drug placebo, described by Torous et al. [69], suggests that the latter finding might also be applicable to this study. This would mean that having access to a digital intervention aimed at reducing depressive symptoms might already lead to relief of the existing burden and to symptom reduction. A possible explanation for this is that access to a digital intervention after screening makes one realize that they themselves can do something about the symptoms.

Another alternative explanation for symptom reduction is that merely screening for (sub)clinical symptoms affects the symptoms. For example, De Jonge-Heesen et al. [65, 70], Poppelaars et al. [68], and Wijnhoven et al. [66] screened for (sub)clinical depressive symptoms similar to our study, showing that symptoms reduced when only the screening was performed. Screening is a form of awareness of one’s own mental health and increases the feeling of being allowed to have symptoms.

Mental health awareness might have also played a role in sharing symptoms with loved ones. Adolescents who told significant others about the symptoms were possibly more aware of their symptoms or possibly realized more that symptoms can be taken seriously, causing them to be more open to tackling their symptoms. This could explain why these adolescents used the app more than adolescents who did not tell significant others about their (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. In the literature, this awareness is described as part of mental health literacy [71], which, among other things, describes the importance of significant others to understand that early signs and symptoms could lead to more severe symptoms when untreated [72, 73]. Thus, in addition to the effect of CBT and screening, awareness itself might have contributed to symptom reduction. An explanation for why both “telling significant others that one will use the app” and “positive expectations about the impact of the app” were not related to app usage could be that these factors did not significantly contribute to raising awareness of having symptoms.

Overall, we assume that the decline in symptoms after using the BMM-app is partly due to the natural course, partly to the intervention itself, and partly to the increased awareness of the symptoms.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. A first strength is that it was conducted in “real-life”; instead of recruiting participants by letting students apply themselves, all students who met the inclusion criteria for age were screened. The burden of participating was limited because recruitment was performed at school during school hours. A second strength is the power; where a minimum of 40 participants was needed for sufficient power, 50 adolescents actually participated. Finally, a third strength of this study is that we did not merely look at a change in symptoms but also the degree to which the app was being used and factors that could affect the usage of the app. This provides more depth and insight into app usage and the feasibility of using the app.

This study also had some limitations. First, whereas this feasibility trial did not aim to investigate causality and therefore no control group was used in this study, it is uncertain whether the reduction of depressive symptoms over time is (partly) a result of using the BMM-app. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. However, the above medium effect size of the significant changes over time suggests that using an app aimed at reducing depressive symptoms in adolescents can be an effective preventive early intervention. Goldberg et al. [74] found a general pattern where effect sizes diminished when study designs became more rigorous. Nonetheless, before testing the effects in a rigorous design, we needed to gain more insight into the suitability and usability of mental health apps for adolescents, which was the main aim of this study. Here, it is important to mention that no iatrogenic processes occurred.

Furthermore, the benefit of an app is that it can be used continuously and whenever desired, in contrast to many interventions provided in a clinical setting [26]. In this study, adolescents used the BMM-app for 4 to 12 weeks. Due to the continuous availability of the app after the mandatory intervention period of 4 weeks, we cannot say with certainty whether the improvements in (sub)clinical depressive symptoms after the follow-up period were due to the continued use of the app or continued use of what was learned in the first four weeks. Furthermore, the sample of this study might not have been fully representative of the whole population (i.e., adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms). Participants might have been over motivated or disciplined, as only 23% of the adolescents who met the inclusion criteria participated. Additionally, similar to many other scientific studies, on average, the educational level of our sample size was higher than that of the population due to the inclusion criteria for age. The female-to-male ratio was skewed. It is possible that women aged 16–19 years are already more capable of metacognitive reasoning than men. However, this skewness is often the case in psychological research [75]. Finally, validation studies for the secondary outcome measures were mainly aimed at respondents aged 18 and older. This means that the age group 16–17 years is not represented in all validation studies mentioned.

Future research

Several recommendations for future research can be made. First, it is recommended to further examine the effectiveness of the BMM-app in reducing (sub)clinical depressive symptoms in a study with a more rigorous research design. For example, including a control group could provide more insight into the role of the BMM-app in reducing (sub)clinical depressive symptoms in adolescents. Moreover, to clarify whether the BMM-app prevents depressive symptoms in the future, it is recommended to include a follow-up with a longer period after the intervention. Furthermore, it is recommended to include a sample that is more representative of the population in terms of sex, age (for example, comparing adolescents aged 12–16 with adolescents aged 16–21 years), and educational level. Zelazo [71] described that metacognitive functioning (i.e., the ability to reflect on one’s own health and determine what is needed for health improvements) is less developed in younger children and practically educated people, making it plausible that screening and the BMM-app would be less appropriate for this group than for older and more theoretically educated people. Finally, assessing treatment satisfaction could provide interesting additional information about the effectiveness of the BMM app in reducing subclinical depressive symptoms.

Second, to benefit from the BMM-app, it is important that adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms learn about the app and decide to use the app. Wasil et al. [76] showed that whereas there are many apps intended to treat depressive symptoms in adults, very few are actually used. Therefore, it would be useful to examine the best practices to familiarize adolescents with the BMM-app and use the results to increase app use.

Conclusions

The findings of this study support the idea that the BMM-app is a suitable preventive early intervention for adolescents with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms. Moreover, we showed that depressive symptoms, stress, anxiety and worrying decrease in adolescents using the BMM-app. Importantly, due to the absence of a control group, we cannot conclude that using the app caused these changes. We assume that the decline is partly due to the natural course, partly to the intervention itself, and partly to the increased awareness of the symptoms. Therefore, the findings need to be interpreted with caution. We recommend stimulating adolescents to tell significant others about their symptoms, because this might lower the threshold to start using the app. Awareness of the symptoms through screening, seeking social support by telling significant others about the screened symptoms and realizing something can be done about the symptoms (digital placebo) may also play a role in reducing the symptoms. Furthermore, we suggest implementing mental health programs at schools to raise adolescents’ awareness of mental health, teach how mental health can be monitored, what symptoms indicate depressive complaints and what they can do to change this themselves. A digital intervention, such as the BMM-app can be a suitable tool for this purpose.
