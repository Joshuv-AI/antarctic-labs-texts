Severe rapidly progressing multi‑resistant polymicrobial surgical site infection after elective abdominoplasty: a case report
Authors: Alaa A Sultan, Abdullah Ahmed Alabbasi, Muhanad Saleh Alzahrani, Mohammed Naif Alsubhi, Abdullah E Kattan, Faris Alaa Sultan, Khalid Arab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse events following elective cosmetic surgery can raise concerns about patient safety, particularly in facilities with variable oversight of infection‑prevention practices. Severe early‑onset postoperative infections caused by multidrug‑resistant organisms may indicate system‑level failures in sterile processing or infection control protocols.Case presentationWe report a rapidly progressing polymicrobial multidrug‑resistant surgical site infection in a previously healthy woman seven days after elective abdominoplasty with liposuction and lipofilling. The patient experienced early clinical deterioration requiring multiple emergency debridements, intensive care admission, prolonged antimicrobial therapy, negative‑pressure wound management, and eventual skin grafting. Microbiology revealed evolving multidrug‑resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, ESBL‑producing Enterobacterales, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Klebsiella species. The severity, early onset, and unusual microbiological profile raised concerns for a possible sterilization or infection‑control breach at the original surgical facility.ConclusionEarly severe postoperative infections following elective procedures may signal underlying system failures rather than isolated clinical complications. Strengthening sterile processing oversight, improving infection‑control auditing, and ensuring timely escalation of care are critical to preventing similar events and safeguarding patient safety.Keywords: Abdominoplasty, Surgical site infection, Multidrug-resistant organisms, Patient safety, Sentinel event

Introduction

Abdominoplasty is a form of body contouring that removes excessive skin and fat from the abdomen and re-establishes the shape and tone of the abdominal muscles. Abdominoplasty is often performed with liposuction and/or autologous fat transfer to maximize aesthetic results [1]. The operation is one of the most popular plastic surgeries worldwide, with thousands of cases performed each year, indicating the acceptance and perceived safety of the procedure [1].

Although abdominoplasty is a widely accepted and popular cosmetic operation, there is a significant postoperative complication rate relative to many other cosmetic procedures. The majority of complications experienced by patients undergoing abdominoplasty are localized and include seroma, hematoma, wound dehiscence, and surgical site infection; systemic complications are much rarer [1]. Seroma has been described as the most common postoperative complication of abdominoplasty with previous meta-analyses demonstrating a wide variation in reported incidence, ranging from 5% to 43%, likely due to variability in the techniques used for the performance of abdominoplasties and prevention of seroma [2]. Recent global analyses suggest an average seroma rate of approximately 10.9%, emphasizing the importance of addressing seroma as a continuing challenge in abdominoplasty regardless of where it is being performed [3].

Advances in technique have significantly impacted reducing the risk of postoperative complications. The preservation of Scarpa’s fascia and utilization of progressive tension sutures to minimize dead space has been associated with reduced rates of complications such as seroma, infection and wound-related complications [4, 5]. Nonetheless, abdominoplasty, particularly when performed in combination with liposuction and fat grafting, involves extensive tissue disruption and creates large areas of potential fluid accumulation and infection [1, 4].

Venous thromboembolism represents a significant peri-operative risk in aesthetic surgery, and abdominoplasty has been identified as the aesthetic surgery procedure with the highest risk of postoperative venous thromboembolism (VTE) [6, 7]. While superficial surgical site infections are the most common infectious complication following abdominoplasty, severe or rare but potentially deadly soft tissue necrosis may occur [8, 9] and necessitate emergency surgical intervention.

While these complications represent serious risks for patients undergoing abdominoplasty, evidence exists that failures in instrument re-processing, lapses in sterilization and breaches in aseptic technique during cosmetic surgery may lead to outbreaks and/or clusters of severe postoperative infections in private surgical facilities [10–12]. Studies examining the risk of bacterial contamination on purportedly pre-sterilized surgical instruments demonstrate that system-level failures in sterilization protocols may result in increased risk of surgical site infection [12]. Studies and cluster investigations demonstrate that instrumentation contamination and breaches in aseptic technique may contribute to postoperative infection following both cosmetic and surgical procedures [10–12].

In this context, we describe a case of catastrophic polymicrobial surgical site infection following elective abdominoplasty with liposuction and lipofilling, complicated by multi-drug resistant organisms and required prolonged interdisciplinary care. This case highlights the possible consequences of suspected sterilization failure in aesthetic surgery, and emphasizes the need for rigorous infection control standards, validated instrument reprocessing, and early aggressive treatment to avoid unnecessary patient harm.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old previously healthy woman presented to the Emergency Department seven days after undergoing elective abdominoplasty with abdominal and axillary liposuction and flank lipofilling at a private hospital which was not the at the same hospital presented by the author, the reason to undergo abdominoplasty was purely aesthetic and pre BMI numbers was’nt rigestrated. She had no known chronic medical conditions and no reported drug allergies.The patient reported chest tightness, dizziness, abdominal pain, and a marked reduction in oral intake. On examination, she appeared acutely ill with borderline hypotension (blood pressure 97/61 mmHg). The abdomen was soft but tender over the lower abdominal region, and bilateral lower-limb edema was noted. Inspection of the operative site revealed active oozing, blistering, and areas suspicious for skin necrosis, raising concern for a severe postoperative soft tissue infection.

Initial laboratory investigations obtained at an outside facility demonstrated leukocytosis. Bedside ultrasonography suggested a possible fluid collection. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen showed diffuse subcutaneous edema, foci of subcutaneous emphysema, and a right lower-quadrant hematoma measuring 3 × 9.7 × 6.2 cm, without evidence of active bleeding or intra-abdominal collection. CT pulmonary angiography excluded a major pulmonary embolism.Given the patient’s clinical deterioration, wound findings, and radiologic features, she was admitted with sepsis secondary to a severe surgical site infection, with concern for deep soft tissue involvement and necrotizing features.

Due to a high index of suspicion for extensive soft tissue infection, the patient underwent urgent surgical intervention. Initial surgery on 22 May included abdominal exploration, evacuation of the hematoma, and extensive wound debridement.The clinical course necessitated a prolonged staged operative approach, including multiple second- and third-look explorations, serial incision and drainage procedures, repeated irrigation and debridement, and frequent dressing changes over several weeks. In total, the patient underwent nine operative debridements before wound approximation on postoperative day, followed by definitive abdominal wound debridement and split-thickness skin grafting Intraoperative findings consistently demonstrated extensive necrotic adipose tissue and purulent discharge involving the abdominal flap and lipofilling tracts, while deeper fascial and muscular structures remained viable.Following grafting, vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) therapy was continued, with minimal output (< 50 mL) and intact dressings. Lower-limb edema resolved 3 days postoperative day.

Microbiological cultures demonstrated a persistent and evolving polymicrobial infection. Early urine and wound cultures obtained grew pan-sensitive Pseudomonas aeruginosa and an extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)–producing Enterobacterales organism., Pseudomonas isolates demonstrated carbapenem resistance, with subsequent development of ciprofloxacin resistance in mid-June. Tissue cultures on 14 and 18 June confirmed persistent multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Later cultures grew Staphylococcus epidermidis and Klebsiella species. Screening for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was negative. Barrier precautions were implemented following identification of carbapenem-resistant organisms.

Empiric and targeted antimicrobial therapy was adjusted repeatedly in response to culture data and clinical progression. Initial broad-spectrum regimens included piperacillin–tazobactam, meropenem, linezolid, clindamycin, and daptomycin. Ciprofloxacin was administered briefly before discontinuation due to resistance. With the emergence of carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas, therapy was escalated to ceftolozane–tazobactam. Following sustained clinical improvement and achievement of adequate source control, Infectious Disease consultation recommended discontinuation of antimicrobial therapy, after which the patient remained clinically stable. Supportive management included VAC therapy, povidone-soaked dressings, chest physiotherapy, incentive spirometry, pneumatic compression devices, and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis with enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneously daily. Pain control evolved from intermittent intravenous opioids to patient-controlled analgesia following skin grafting, with adjunctive non-opioid analgesics.

Transient liver function abnormalities developed during hospitalization and were attributed to sepsis and possible drug-induced liver injury. Hemodynamic parameters remained largely stable, with occasional episodes of asymptomatic hypotension.

Multidisciplinary consultations were obtained. Psychiatry evaluated symptoms of emotional distress related to severe pain, without evidence of suicidal ideation. Dermatology managed a candidal outbreak associated with prolonged hospitalization. Gastroenterology was consulted for an eight-day ileus; imaging demonstrated colonic gaseous distension without mechanical obstruction, and conservative management was recommended. Over the course of hospitalization, the wound bed developed healthy granulation tissue, the split-thickness skin graft remained viable, VAC output remained minimal, edema resolved, oral intake improved, and pain was adequately controlled. Following cessation of antimicrobial therapy, the patient remained clinically stable, with no evidence of recurrent infection, new collections, or systemic deterioration.

Discussion

While many people consider tummy tucks to be relatively safe elective procedures, numerous reports have documented that they are one of the most complicated elective cosmetic surgeries due to their high rate of local complications, especially when performed with lipo or fat transfer [1]. Most complications are minor—seromas, wound dehiscences, or superficial infections—but some of these complications will progress to necrotizing or even lethal infections, which represent the extreme of the complication continuum [8, 9]. This report presents a dramatic example of how an otherwise uneventful cosmetic surgery on a previously healthy individual can quickly escalate into a catastrophic, life‑threatening polymicrobial surgical‑site infection requiring multiple hospitalizations, serial radical debridements, aggressive antibiotics, and subsequent reconstructive surgery [1, 8, 9].

In addition to the severity and rapid onset of the infection observed in this case, the presence of multiple, multidrug‑resistant microorganisms was unusual and suggests a larger system‑wide problem rather than a localized, idiosyncratic issue related to the patient or the surgical technique used. Specifically, there were no known host risk factors for developing a deep plane infection including diabetes, immunosuppression, obesity or smoking. However, the patient developed an infection within 1‑week of surgery and the infection involved both the liposuction and lipofilling tracts. From a safety standpoint, the sudden and severe nature of this complication represents what might be considered a “sentinel event” suggesting an underlying flaw in the operational aspects of care at an institution and would require an institutional review and correction process [13, 14]. While a definitive link between the type of bacteria found and contaminated surgical instrumentation could not be made retrospectively, the convergence of clinical indicators (early appearance of the infection, the presence of multiple, multidrug‑resistant microorganisms, and the location of the infection in deep tissue planes) support the likelihood of an error in either the preparation or sterilization of surgical instrumentation [10–15].

Multidrug-resistant infections composed of multiple microorganisms have become increasingly common in the context of complex surgical site infections, reflecting the growing global antimicrobial resistance crisis emphasized by international health agencies, particularly among individuals who have undergone multiple surgical interventions and/or prolonged antibiotic exposure [16–18].Commonly, gram‑negative rods such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are identified in these infections, and a significant portion of them exhibit resistance to multiple antibiotics, thus complicating empiric therapy and requiring culture‑directed therapy [16, 18]. Conversely, infections occurring after cosmetic surgery are usually monomicrobial and consist of skin flora. The identification of carbapenem‑resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa and extended‑spectrum beta‑lactamase (ESBL)–producing organisms in this case highlight the potential for prolonged treatment courses, serial debridements, and the selection of increasingly resistant pathogens in response to antibiotic pressure, thereby significantly increasing both morbidity and the complexity of therapy [8, 16]. In the broader patient‑safety literature, preventable postoperative infections—including those following low‑value or elective procedures—are increasingly recognized as emblematic, avoidable harms that demand system‑level prevention strategies [19, 20].

Contamination of equipment or a failure in the sterilization process should be given particular attention when considering the possible cause(s) of an infection that occurs in an unusual pattern, has an early onset, involves deep tissues, and includes uncommon microorganisms. For example, outbreaks of infections occurring after cosmetic procedures, particularly those caused by rapidly‑growing nontuberculous mycobacteria, have been repeatedly linked to failures in instrument reprocessing, defective autoclaves, and breaches in asepsis, particularly in private surgical centers [10, 11, 13, 14]. Studies examining contamination of presumably pre‑sterilized surgical instruments using observational methods have shown detectable levels of bacteria, thereby supporting the view that systemic problems contribute to a considerable number of avoidable surgical site infections [12, 15]. Recent analyses of medico‑legal claims and preventable postoperative events similarly underscore the contribution of process failures to adverse outcomes [19].

Both the specific techniques used to perform abdominoplasties and the combinations of procedures also influence the risk of infection. For example, extensive undermining of flaps, aggressive liposuction, and lipofilling result in large dead space areas and poorly vascularized tissue planes that increase the likelihood of hematoma, seroma, and bacterial proliferation if not properly managed [1, 4]. Techniques to reduce these risks include preserving Scarpa’s fascia and utilizing progressive tension sutures [4, 5]. Beyond technique, standardized infection‑reduction bundles and disciplined adherence to perioperative prevention measures have demonstrated meaningful reductions in SSI in general surgery populations and are relevant to aesthetic surgery programs aiming to minimize preventable harm [21]. Similarly, meticulous intraoperative wound irrigation practices and, in selected abdominal procedures, subfascial closed‑suction drainage have been associated with SSI reductions and may be applicable in complex body‑contouring contexts [22, 23].

Prompt recognition and aggressive surgical intervention remains the foundation of survival in severe postoperative infections and suspected necrotizing fasciitis. Delaying surgical intervention beyond 24 h after the diagnosis of suspected necrotizing fasciitis results in significantly higher mortality rates compared to immediate and radical excision of all devitalized tissue [24, 25]. Negative‑pressure wound therapy (NPWT) serves as a useful adjunct in the treatment of large, infected wounds of the abdominal wall by enhancing the formation of granulating tissue, reducing swelling, and facilitating subsequent delayed reconstruction [26–28].

Severe infections often lead to the development of systemic complications including drug‑induced organ damage, ileus, and venous thrombosis; therefore, providing comprehensive supportive care is necessary [16, 24]. The psychological effects of severe complications following elective cosmetic surgery are profound and infrequently reported. Depressive symptoms, feelings of guilt, regret, and passive suicidal ideation have been reported in patients who experience severe adverse consequences following cosmetic surgery, thereby highlighting the need for early psychiatric evaluation [29, 30]. Case reports from adjacent surgical fields further illustrate that preventable deep SSIs can necessitate major salvage procedures (e.g., panniculectomy) and prolonged rehabilitation, reinforcing the value of early escalation and structured prevention pathways [31].

This case further illustrates a sentinel‑level patient‑safety failure in which preventable harm following elective surgery leads to significant clinical, psychological, and resource‑related consequences. Although the exact cause cannot be conclusively determined, the early onset, polymicrobial pattern, and multidrug‑resistant nature of the infection raise legitimate concerns about lapses in sterile processing or infection‑control practices. Such failures—often hidden—may only become apparent when catastrophic harm occurs. The case underscores the need for standardized sterile‑processing audits, stronger regulatory oversight, clear accountability for infection‑prevention practices, and rapid escalation pathways for unusual postoperative infections [13–15, 19–21, 23]. Evaluating such events from a systems‑based patient‑safety perspective, rather than as isolated complications, is vital to preventing recurrence and promoting a culture of safety within both private and public surgical settings [19–21].

Conclusion

The sentinel event described here can serve as a paradigm for understanding how a preventable postoperative complication resulting from elective cosmetic surgery could be indicative of a patient safety issue that is systemic, rather than simply an incident with one surgeon or facility. The fact that the postoperative infection was early, caused by multiple microorganisms, and was multi-drug resistant indicates a potential failure in the system of sterile processing/infection control, which can occur in both community-based and hospital-based settings. Beyond the direct medical implications of preventable harm (e.g., prolonged length of stay, use of multiple disciplines, and considerable psychological impact), there are also indirect implications for all health services (e.g., increased costs associated with longer lengths of stay and multidisciplinary treatment). To avoid future sentinel events and protect patient safety, several key strategies exist (1) improving regulatory oversight of policies related to infection control, (2) establishing standardized audit processes to monitor sterile processing workflow, and (3) identifying and escalating unexplained postoperative infections sooner.
