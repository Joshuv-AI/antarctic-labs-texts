Mapping the Association of Cerebral Small Vessel Disease, Gray Matter Integrity, and Cognitive Function
Authors: Marvin Petersen, David Emskötter, Felix Leonard Nägele, Carola Mayer, Maximilian Schell, Jens Fiehler, Jürgen Gallinat, Simone Kühn, Raphael Twerenbold, Märit Jensen, Götz Thomalla, Bastian Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesCerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. While its impact on white matter is well documented, CSVD-related gray matter alterations are less understood. In this work, we aimed to investigate the association between CSVD burden, regional gray matter integrity, and cognitive function in a large population-based cohort.MethodsWe examined participants from the Hamburg City Health Study, who underwent multidomain neuropsychological testing and multimodal neuroimaging. A composite CSVD burden score was calculated by aggregating the white matter hyperintensity load, Fazekas score, perivascular space (PVS) count, and peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD). Advanced imaging techniques based on T1-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion-weighted MRI were leveraged to assess the integrity of cortical and subcortical gray matter, including the hippocampus. General cognitive ability (g) was derived by a principal component analysis of cognitive test scores.ResultsData from 2,603 participants (median age 65 years [interquartile range 14], 44.0% female) were analyzed. Higher CSVD burden was significantly associated with regionally specific gray matter abnormalities after family-wise error correction (family-wise error-corrected p value [pFWE] < 0.05): higher mean diffusivity and free water in cingulate, insular, and temporal cortices and basal ganglia; lower tissue fractional anisotropy in the anterior cingulate, insula, caudate, and hippocampus; lower T1/FLAIR ratio in the temporal cortex and hippocampus; lower thickness in the anterior cingulate, insula, and temporal cortex; and altered subcortical/hippocampal volumes. Aggregated indices reflecting these CSVD-related gray matter abnormalities were significantly associated with lower general cognitive ability (mean diffusivity: standardized β = 0.140, 95% CI 0.09–0.19, pFWE < 0.001; free water: standardized β = 0.183, 95% CI 0.13–0.23, pFWE < 0.001; tissue fractional anisotropy: standardized β = −0.097, 95% CI −0.13 to −0.06, pFWE < 0.001; T1/FLAIR: standardized β = −0.090, 95% CI −0.13 to −0.05, pFWE < 0.001; thickness/volume: standardized β = −0.051, 95% CI −0.09 to −0.01, pFWE = 0.026).DiscussionComplementing previous research that focuses on CSVD and white matter changes, our study highlights abnormal gray matter integrity as a possible link between small vessel pathology and cognitive disorders.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a relevant contributor to cognitive decline and Alzheimer and vascular dementia in aging populations.1 Given the current lack of therapeutic interventions, the identification of disease biomarkers is a key priority in CSVD research for converting advances in pathomechanistic understanding into effective prevention and treatment approaches.2-4 Brain MRI is at the forefront of these efforts, enabling the identification of structural brain alterations in all stages of CSVD. Established imaging markers range from altered diffusion measures at the microscopic level to white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) of presumed vascular origin as well as enlarged perivascular spaces (PVSs), observable in standard anatomic imaging sequences.5,6

Despite this progress, considerable gaps in understanding persist: most current imaging analyses in patients with CSVD investigate abnormalities in the cerebral white matter and their clinical consequences.7,8 Despite the known impact of CSVD on gray matter integrity,5 there is a notable lack of well-powered neuroimaging studies using a more diverse array of imaging markers capturing gray matter structural properties in CSVD.9-11 This includes markers of gray matter “microstructure,” reflecting microscale tissue properties such as cellular integrity, water diffusion, and myelination, and “macrostructure,” referring to larger scale morphological properties such as cortical thickness and subcortical volumes. Furthermore, most analyses focus on global measurements or those of predefined regions of interest, rather than examining the full range of gray matter areas, including the cortex, basal ganglia, and hippocampus.12 As a result, the relationship of CSVD imaging markers, regional gray matter microstructure and macrostructure, and cognition, specifically in early, preclinical stages of CSVD, remains unclear.

We, therefore, used data from the Hamburg City Health Study (HCHS), a large-scale epidemiologic study with extensive clinical and imaging phenotyping,13 to address this research need. Specifically, we integrated established CSVD imaging markers, detailed microstructural and macrostructural profiling of gray matter (cortex, subcortex, hippocampus) using multimodal MRI, and comprehensive neuropsychological assessments. Our primary aim was to characterize the relationship between CSVD burden and gray matter integrity, and subsequently, how these gray matter characteristics relate to cognitive function. We tested 2 main hypotheses: (1) higher CSVD burden, assessed using standard neuroimaging markers,5,6 is associated with regionally specific alterations in gray matter microstructure and macrostructure; (2) these CSVD-related gray matter alterations are associated with cognitive performance.

Methods

The methodological approach of this study is illustrated in Figure 1.

Methodology

(A) The analysis was based on cognitive and brain MRI data of the Hamburg City Health Study. (B) A CSVD score was computed by averaging the z-scored WMH load, Fazekas score, PVS count, and PSMD. (C) Imaging features of brain microstructure and macrostructure were derived based on T1-weighted, FLAIR, and diffusion-weighted images. (D) For cortical and subcortical areas, feature extraction was performed aggregating imaging features in regions defined by the Schaefer400x7 Atlas, Aseg Atlas, and Melbourne Subcortex Atlas.14,15 For the hippocampus, feature extraction was performed at the vertex level based on hippocampal surface reconstruction with HippUnfold.16 CSVD = cerebral small vessel disease; FLAIR = fluid-attenuated inversion recovery; HCHS = Hamburg City Health Study; PSMD = peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity; PVS = perivascular space; WMHs = white matter hyperintensities.

Study Population

The HCHS is a prospective, population-based, single-center cohort study in Hamburg, Germany. Its primary aim is to investigate risk factors of major chronic diseases in adults aged 45–74 years through extensive clinical and imaging phenotyping.13 Participants for the HCHS were recruited by drawing a random sample, stratified by age and sex, from the official inhabitant data files of the city of Hamburg.13 For this analysis, we examined a cross-sectional subset of HCHS participants who (1) had available brain MRI data suitable for our analysis and (2) were free of major neurologic conditions that could confound interpretation of CSVD-related changes (e.g., brain tumors and ischemic stroke), as determined by the neuroradiologic assessment.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The HCHS protocol was approved by the local ethics committee of the Landesärztekammer Hamburg (State of Hamburg Chamber of Medical Practitioners, PV5131). The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice, Good Epidemiological Practice, and the ethical principles described in the Declaration of Helsinki.17 Written informed consent, covering study participation and data usage, was obtained from all participants investigated in this work.

Cognitive Assessments

Clinical examinations including cognitive assessments were performed during a single comprehensive visit to the HCHS study center. Cognitive functions were assessed using the Animal Naming Test (evaluating verbal fluency), Multiple Choice Vocabulary Intelligence Test B (reasoning), Trail Making Tests A (information processing speed) and B (executive function), and Word List Recall Test (memory) of the extended version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease Neuropsychological Assessment Battery, as well as the Clock Drawing Test (visuospatial reconstruction).18,19 All tests were administered by a trained study nurse. To ensure that a higher value corresponds to higher cognitive performance across all tests, we inverted the Trail Making Test A and B results. Subsequently, z-scored cognitive test scores were dimensionality reduced by a principal component analysis. The first principal component (PC1) explaining the highest amount of variance was defined as a measure of general cognitive ability (g) following previous procedures.20

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing

MRI was conducted using a single 3-Tesla Siemens Skyra scanner. Owing to logistical requirements, the brain MRI scan was scheduled separately from clinical assessments (including cognitive testing) but was performed in temporal proximity (median 33 days after assessment [interquartile range (IQR) 53]). The scanning protocols follow those outlined in previous studies.17 For 3D T1-weighted anatomic images, a rapid acquisition gradient-echo sequence was used with the following sequence specifics: repetition time (TR) = 2,500 milliseconds, echo time (TE) = 2.12 milliseconds, 256 axial slices, slice thickness (ST) = 0.94 mm, in-plane resolution (IPR) = 0.83 × 0.83 mm. 3D T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images were acquired with TR = 4,700 milliseconds, TE = 392 milliseconds, 192 axial slices, ST = 0.9 mm, and IPR = 0.75 × 0.75 mm. For single-shell diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), 75 axial slices were acquired with gradients (b = 1,000 s/mm2) applied along 64 noncollinear directions with the following sequence parameters: TR = 8,500 milliseconds, TE = 75 milliseconds, ST = 2 mm, IPR = 2 × 2 mm, anterior-posterior phase-encoding direction, 1 b0 volume.17

MRI data were processed using preconfigured containerized pipelines, as described previously.21 The full documentation can be found on GitHub.22 Diffusion-weighted and T1-weighted images were preprocessed using QSIprep (version 0.14.2).23 DWI preprocessing included bias field correction; skull stripping; denoising; deringing, and correction of B1 field inhomogeneity, head motion, eddy currents, and susceptibility distortions.23,24 T1-weighted and FLAIR images underwent bias field correction and skull stripping.24

Computation of CSVD Scores

Following previous recommendations, we computed a summary CSVD score from 4 imaging markers representing CSVD burden: WMH load, Fazekas score, PVS count, and peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) (Figure 1B).6

WMH volume was quantified based on FLAIR images using an automated pipeline and normalized to intracranial volume to obtain the WMH load.21,25,26 The Fazekas score was assigned by trained neuroradiologists based on standard criteria.27 PVSs were automatically segmented on T1-weighted images using a validated filtering method.28 PSMD—a marker of diffuse white matter microstructural integrity—was calculated based on standard procedures adapted with nonlinear registration.29 Detailed methods for the computation of each marker are provided in the eMethods.

Missing values for individual CSVD measures were mean imputed. The final CSVD score was derived by averaging the z-scores of WMH load, Fazekas score, PVS count, and PSMD. Microbleeds and recent small subcortical infarcts were not included in the score because of limited data availability in this cohort. A Spearman correlation matrix of the CSVD score and its subcomponents is shown in eFigure 1.

To validate our composite CSVD score, we performed a confirmatory factor analysis as a sensitivity analysis.30 Using semopy (version 2.3.11),31 we specified a structural equation model, testing the hypothesis that a single latent factor (“CSVD severity”) underlies our 4 manifest imaging markers: WMH load, Fazekas score, PVS count, and PSMD. Model fit was assessed using standard indices, including the χ2 test, comparative fit index (CFI), and factor loadings.

Imaging Features of Gray Matter Microstructure and Macrostructure

Five measures representing different aspects of gray matter microstructure and macrostructure were computed (Figure 1C): thickness and volume representing brain morphology; T1/FLAIR ratio representing myelination; mean diffusivity representing bulk water diffusivity; free water representing volume of the extracellular compartment; and tissue fractional anisotropy representing directionality of water diffusion attributable to the tissue compartment.

These measures capture different aspects of gray matter tissue characteristics and morphology, making them potentially sensitive to various CSVD-related pathologic processes. Specifically, alterations in mean diffusivity and free water may reflect changes in tissue water content related to edema, inflammation, or cellular damage; alterations in tissue fractional anisotropy may indicate neurite disruption or disorganization; T1/FLAIR ratio changes can suggest altered myelin content; and thickness/volume changes typically represent macrostructural differences in morphology, for example, atrophy.

Intensity-based measures were derived from diffusion-weighted and anatomic MRI. Diffusion tensor imaging and free-water imaging were performed to obtain mean diffusivity, free water, and tissue fractional anisotropy maps.32,33 The T1/FLAIR ratio was computed by dividing normalized T1-weighted images by FLAIR images after registration to standard space.34 Morphometric measures (cortical thickness, subcortical volumes, hippocampal thickness) were derived from T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer (version 6.0.1) for the cortex/subcortex14,15 and HippUnfold (version 1.2.0) for the hippocampus.16

All gray matter measures were extracted for the following regions: cortical regions defined by the Schaefer400x7 Atlas14; subcortical regions including the caudate, nucleus accumbens, amygdala, pallidum, putamen, and thalamus; and vertices of the hippocampal mid-thickness surface derived using HippUnfold.16

More detailed methodology for image processing and feature extraction is provided in the eMethods.

Quality Assessment

Quality assessment included visual inspection of raw images and processed images based on quantitative outliers defined as measures exceeding 2 standard deviations from the mean of quality measures computed using QSIprep and mriqc.23 In addition, all HippUnfold outputs were visually inspected. Images of insufficient quality were excluded.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in python 3.9.1 leveraging Pingouin (version 0.5.4) as well as FSL's Permutation Analysis of Linear Models based on MATLAB version 2021b.35 Statistical tests were 2-sided, with a p < 0.05 as significance threshold. p Values were adjusted by family-wise error correction for multiple comparisons.

Regional Association Between CSVD Scores and Imaging Features

To investigate the association between CSVD burden and gray matter integrity, we conducted permutation-based testing for linear associations between the CSVD score and regional features of gray matter microstructure and macrostructure in a general linear model. Statistical tests were 2-sided (npermutation = 5,000), with a p < 0.05 as the significance threshold. To account for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted for family-wise errors. General linear models were adjusted for age, sex, and education. To obtain standardized β-coefficients, input variables were z-scored beforehand. As a result, β-coefficients and p values were obtained for each cortical and subcortical region as well as each hippocampal vertex, indicating the strength and significance of the CSVD score's linear association with respective imaging features for each region of interest.

Associations Between Imaging Features and Cognitive Functioning

To obtain a single aggregate measure per participant per feature, imaging features were averaged across cortical and subcortical regions with a significant association between the respective imaging index and the CSVD score. This means that any region demonstrating a significant correlation between the CSVD score and an imaging feature was included in the aggregation process for that specific feature. Before this, imaging features were averaged across hippocampal vertices to produce a single measurement for each participant for both the left and right hippocampus. Aggregate imaging scores were associated with general cognitive ability (g) using multiple linear regression adjusted for age, sex, and education.

Data Availability

HCHS data can be obtained by qualified researchers on reasonable request to the study's steering committee.

Regional Association Between CSVD Scores and Imaging Features

To investigate gray matter integrity differences in relation to CSVD burden, we performed a region-level analysis of the relationship between the CSVD score and imaging features of gray matter microstructure and macrostructure (Figure 3). The analysis revealed widespread, statistically significant associations after family-wise error correction (all family-wise error-corrected p value [pFWE] < 0.05), with standardized β-coefficients ranging between −0.2 and 0.2. Specifically, higher CSVD scores were related to regional differences in brain structural features—that is, higher mean diffusivity and free water in the lateral prefrontal, cingulate, insular, temporal, and occipital cortices as well as all subcortical structures; lower mean diffusivity bilaterally in the superior frontal and parietal cortices (Figure 3, A and B); lower tissue fractional anisotropy bilaterally in the anterior cingulate and insular cortices, caudate, thalamus, cornu ammonis and dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, and left amygdala; higher tissue fractional anisotropy bilaterally in the putamen (Figure 3C); lower T1/FLAIR ratio bilaterally in the temporal cortex and hippocampus as well as the right amygdala (Figure 3D); and lower cortical thickness bilaterally in the anterior cingulate, insular, and temporal cortices; lower volume in the nucleus accumbens; higher volumes in the caudate, putamen, and pallidum; and higher thickness in the subiculum and cornu ammonis of the hippocampus (Figure 3E).

Cognition Analysis of Imaging Indices

The approach for the analysis of cognitive data is illustrated in Figure 4A. To obtain a measure of general cognitive ability (g), cognitive test scores were subjected to a principal component analysis. The PC1 explained 35.0% of variance in cognitive data (Figure 4B), and loadings indicated that all cognitive test scores contributed to this variable (Figure 4C), confirming it as a measure of general cognitive ability. A Spearman correlation matrix of general cognitive ability (g) and the cognitive test scores is shown in eFigure 2. In multiple linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, and education, a higher PC1 score—that is, lower general cognitive ability—was significantly associated with higher mean diffusivity (standardized β = 0.140, 95% CI 0.09–0.19, pFWE < 0.001), higher free water (standardized β = 0.183, 95% CI 0.13–0.23, pFWE < 0.001), and lower tissue fractional anisotropy (standardized β = −0.097, 95% CI −0.13 to −0.06, pFWE < 0.001), lower T1/FLAIR ratio (standardized β = −0.090, 95% CI −0.13 to −0.05, pFWE < 0.001), and lower thickness and volume (standardized β = −0.051, 95% CI −0.09 to −0.01, pFWE = 0.036) (Figure 4D). Regression plots displaying the association between aggregate imaging indices and individual cognitive test scores results are presented in eFigure 3.

Sensitivity Analysis

The confirmatory factor analysis of the CSVD markers indicated an excellent model fit (CFI = 0.98, p(χ2) = 0.98), supporting the hypothesis that a single factor underlies the different markers. All 4 imaging markers loaded significantly and strongly onto this latent “CSVD severity” factor (standardized loadings: Fazekas score = 0.93, PSMD = 0.88, PVS count = 0.65, WMH load as reference). The resulting latent factor almost perfectly correlated with our original composite CSVD score (Pearson r = 0.98, a regression plot in eFigure 4), validating our use of the averaged z-score for subsequent analyses.
