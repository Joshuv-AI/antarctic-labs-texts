Population Pharmacokinetic Analysis of Glecaprevir and Pibrentasvir in Pediatric Patients ≥ 3 to < 18 Years of Age with Genotypes 1–6 Chronic Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) Infection
Authors: Neha Thakre, Denise Beck, Ahmed M. Saeed, Doerthe Eckert, Mong-Jen Chen, Wei Liu, Hoi Kei Lon, Nael M. Mostafa, Sven Mensing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMavyret, a combination medication of glecaprevir (GLE) and pibrentasvir (PIB), is approved to treat chronic and acute hepatitis C virus (HCV) (genotypes 1–6) infection in adults and pediatric patients ≥ 3 years old. To support approval, a two-part clinical trial was conducted in adolescents (≥ 12 to < 18 years) and children (≥ 3 to < 12 years) to identify the appropriate dosage and determine the efficacy and safety in pediatric patients.ObjectivesPopulation pharmacokinetic (popPK) analyses reported herein aimed to (1) characterize the pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters of orally administered GLE and PIB and the sources of PK variability in pediatric patients and (2) support the use of a body weight-based pediatric GLE/PIB dose ratio of 50/20 mg in HCV-infected children.MethodsPopPK models were built for GLE and PIB independently using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling and utilized data collected from 126 pediatric patients. Demographic, pathophysiological, and treatment factors were investigated for their impact on GLE and PIB PK.ResultsPopPK analyses of pediatric exposures confirmed comparable exposures to those of adults who achieved over 95% sustained virologic response (SVR12). The pediatric popPK model incorporated allometric body weight-based scaling and subsequently identified no covariate that significantly impacted GLE exposures. Similarly, no covariate beyond the specified allometric body weight scaling was identified to impact PIB exposures. The developed pediatric popPK models were able to describe the central tendency and variability of the data.ConclusionThese results supported the approval of body weight-based dosing regimens of GLE and PIB in children (< 45 kg) infected with any HCV genotype.Clinical TrialsNCT03067129.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01637-1.

Key Points

The pan-genotypic, 8-week hepatitis C virus (HCV) body weight-based dosing regimen and pediatric formulation of coadministered glecaprevir and pibrentasvir were approved for pediatric patients aged 3–17 years.This work supported the approval by demonstrating that drug exposures in HCV-infected pediatric patients receiving coadministered glecaprevir and pibrentasvir at weight-based doses in a pediatric formulation are comparable to those observed in HCV-infected adults who achieved over 95% sustained virologic response.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) treatment in adults has been the primary focus of the Global Health Sector Strategy on Hepatitis 2016–2021, which was initiated to eliminate the global threat of viral hepatitis by 2030 [1]. This initiative has accelerated the development, approval, and treatment access of short-duration, oral, and curative therapies of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) agents that have demonstrated high cure rates, minimal side effects, and short treatment durations in adults compared to prior interferon-based therapies [2]. In 2020, the Federation of International Societies of Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (FISPGHAN) released a position paper concluding that there was a strong consensus within the medical community to delay treatment for the estimated 3.5 million HCV-infected adolescents and children around the world until DAA therapies were approved for them and to avoid the use of interferon-based therapies in pediatric patients [3]. Today, multiple DAA combination therapies have been approved around the world for pediatric patients infected with any genotype of HCV [4].

DAAs are designed to target the non-structural (NS) proteins that are translated and processed from the RNA virus, resulting in the inhibition of viral replication [5]. DAAs inhibit the viral protease (NS3/4A), phosphoprotein (NS5A), or RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (NS5B) and are classified into non–pan-genotypic and pan-genotypic regimens. The pan-genotypic combination treatment regimen of 300 mg glecaprevir (GLE), an HCV NS3/4A protease inhibitor, and 120 mg pibrentasvir (PIB), an NS5A inhibitor, was developed for use in HCV treatment-naïve and treatment-experienced HCV genotype 1–6 (GT1–GT6) infected adult patients with compensated liver disease (with or without cirrhosis) [9–12]. GLE and PIB act synergistically and are co-formulated as Mavyret/Maviret (GLE/PIB), a once-daily (QD) oral fixed-dose combination drug product approved in 2017 for adults and in early 2019 for adolescents [9, 10]. In 2021, the oral pediatric formulation and dosing regimen of GLE/PIB was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) for use in children. Similar to adults (≥ 18 years of age), results with 12-week DAA therapy have demonstrated high sustained virologic response (SVR12) in adolescents (≥ 12 to < 18 years of age) and in children (≥ 3 to < 12 years of age) [4, 5, 8].

In HCV-infected adults without cirrhosis, steady-state GLE and PIB exposures (steady-state area under the plasma concentration-time curve from time zero to 24 h [AUC24]) are 4800 and 1430 ng·h/mL with maximum plasma concentrations (Cmax) of 597 and 110 ng/mL, respectively. The combination of GLE/PIB weakly inhibits cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) and 1A2 (CYP1A2) as well as uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) [9]. The population pharmacokinetics (popPK) of GLE and PIB have been extensively characterized across four phase 2 and six phase 3 studies in more than 2700 adult HCV-infected patients (with or without cirrhosis) across a range of studied dose combinations [15]. Prominent intrinsic or extrinsic factors included the effect of cirrhosis on GLE (160% higher exposure in patients with compensated cirrhosis) and the effect of coadministration with GLE on PIB, resulting in a threefold higher PIB exposure following administration of GLE 300 mg and PIB 120 mg [15, 16]. The results of the pharmacokinetics (PK), safety, and efficacy data from phase 2 and 3 studies in adult populations were used to guide the dosing regimens and protocol dosing design for the pediatric clinical study, DORA (NCT03067129).

Data from the DORA study demonstrated that GLE/PIB is a highly efficacious and safe treatment option for adolescents and children [13, 14]. In the DORA Part 2 study, non-compartmental analysis (NCA) was performed to assess the PK in children and was previously reported based on 38 patients enrolled into the intensive PK (IPK) portion of the study [14]. To expand our understanding of GLE and PIB PK in HCV-infected pediatric patients and investigate the sources of variability in patient exposures, we present here the popPK analysis of (1) HCV-infected adolescents who participated in the DORA Part 1 study [13] and (2) HCV-infected children who participated in the DORA Part 2 study [14]. The results of this work supported the approved dosing regimens and pediatric formulation of GLE/PIB in children with chronic HCV infection.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

The DORA study was a phase 2/3, non-randomized, open-label, multicenter study aiming to evaluate the PK, efficacy, and safety of GLE/PIB in adolescents and children with chronic HCV infection [13, 14]. The DORA study was divided into two parts, according to the QD oral formulation of GLE/PIB administered (see Table 1). In Part 1 of this study, the adult regimen of the QD, fixed-dose, co-formulated GLE/PIB (300 mg/120 mg) therapy was evaluated in adolescents (≥ 12 to < 18 years of age) who were able to swallow the adult co-formulated tablet (cohort 1). In Part 2, the GLE + PIB pediatric formulation (see Sect. 2.2) was evaluated in HCV GT1- to GT6-infected children, who were divided into cohorts based on age, which consisted of ages ≥ 9 to < 12 (cohort 2), ≥ 6 to < 9 (cohort 3), and ≥ 3 to < 6 (cohort 4) years. In Part 2, the initial dose ratio of GLE/PIB for children was 40/15 mg and was based on the patient's body weight and age band at screening (see Table 1). The initial GLE/PIB dose ratio was established through clinical trial simulations utilizing body surface area-normalized clearance data from adults. Doses within each pediatric age group were adjusted to align with multiples of a proposed sachet unit dose. Each cohort first enrolled patients into the IPK portion of the study to perform early assessments of exposure. IPK sample draws occurred on day 1 at 4 h after the first dose and at the week 2 visit, with blood samples taken immediately prior to the dose (0 h) and at 2, 4, 6, and 12 h after the dose. PK analysis from this first set of patients informed the necessary dose adjustment of GLE/PIB to a final dose ratio of 50/20 mg. The final dose ratio was confirmed in a second PK analysis. Once doses were confirmed, enrollment into the non-IPK safety/efficacy portions of that age group began. Patients were assigned to take 8, 12, or 16 weeks of treatment depending on their HCV genotype, prior treatment experience, cirrhosis status, and geographical location (Japan vs. non-Japan) in accordance with the approved GLE/PIB regimen for adult patients [9, 13, 14].

The DORA study was conducted in accordance with the International Counsel for Harmonisation guidelines, applicable regulations, and the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial protocol was approved by the independent ethics committee or institutional review boards for each site. All parents/guardians provided informed consent, and patients provided assent, where required, prior to any study-related procedures.

Formulation and Dosing

Adolescents participating in Part 1 were administered the adult QD oral treatment regimen, consisting of three fixed-dose combination tablets, each containing 100 mg GLE and 40 mg PIB (300 mg GLE and 120 mg PIB total dose). For patients participating in the IPK portion of Part 2, the study drug was dispensed as separate 15.67% GLE- and 8.25% PIB-coated granules (GLE + PIB). In the non-IPK portion of Part 2, a pediatric formulation, consisting of coated granules of GLE and PIB in one packet at a dose ratio of 50/20 mg, was dispensed and administered with soft food as the dosing vehicle for children. A summary of the initial and final doses for each age group is shown in Table 1.

Pharmacokinetic Sampling and Analytical Methods

Blood samples for determination of GLE and PIB plasma concentrations were obtained by venipuncture at the sampling time points shown in Table 1.

GLE and PIB plasma concentrations were determined with a validated assay under the supervision of the Drug Analysis Department at AbbVie [17, 18]. The lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) of the assays for both GLE and PIB was ~ 1.00 ng/mL.

Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling

Model Development

PopPK models were built for GLE and PIB independently using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling based on NONMEM 7.4.3 compiled with the GNU Fortran compiler (version 4.8.3). The first-order conditional estimation method with η–ε interaction (FOCE-INT) was employed for all model runs within NONMEM.

One- and two-compartment models were tested with and without the inclusion of a lag time in absorption. A body weight-based allometric scaling of apparent clearance (CL/F) and apparent volume of distribution of the central compartment (V2/F) is frequently used in pediatric studies [19–21]; thus, the effect of body weight (relative to the standard body weight of 70 kg) on clearance and volume was included in the structural model. To determine if children would benefit from body surface area group-based dosing, a body surface area-based allometric scaling (relative to a standard body surface area of 1.7 m2) of CL/F and V2/F was tested rather than using body weight. The difference in relative bioavailability (F1) between the adult and pediatric formulations, defined as the ratio of the pediatric to adult formulation, was parameterized and fixed at 0.8 and 1.2 for GLE and PIB, respectively, based on results from a phase 1 bioavailability study in adults.

Covariate effects were tested in a forward addition and backward elimination manner and included in the model in a multiplicative fashion. Continuous covariates (i.e., age and body surface area) were normalized to a reference value (median value of the study population) and included in the model with a power function. Categorical covariates (i.e., biological sex [male and female], race [white vs. others]) were tested with a multiplicative model to obtain the fractional difference of PK parameters between the tested categorical groups. Additional details regarding the covariate evaluation are provided in the “Supplementary Methods” section (see the electronic supplementary material).

Model Selection Criteria and Evaluation

Both GLE and PIB population models were developed, selected, and evaluated with a parsimonious approach as described previously [15]. The developed models were evaluated both ad hoc, during development, and after the model development was completed. Goodness-of-fit plots, visual predictive checks (VPCs), and bootstrap evaluations (1000 replicates) were used in model evaluation. To account for the different dose regimens across the cohorts, the simulated and observed concentrations were shown in prediction-corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC) plots [22]. Additional details regarding model evaluations are provided in the “Supplementary Methods” section (see the electronic supplementary material).

Model-Predicted Steady-State Exposures

Individual-predicted GLE and PIB exposures (AUC24) were derived in adolescents and children based on post hoc final model PK parameters. For patients with IPK sampling, individual-predicted exposures were compared to the observed individual GLE and PIB AUC24 based on NCA.

Simulations

Simulations were performed to investigate the impact of the body weight-based dosing on the steady-state GLE and PIB exposures. A simulation dataset was created by drawing 10,000 patients with body weight (wi) randomly sampled from a uniform distribution, wi ~ U(12, 45), reflecting the body weight range of children aged ≥ 3 to < 12 years. The final popPK models were used to simulate GLE and PIB PK concentrations for a continuous 14-day interval with QD body weight-based dosing over these 14 days and intensive sampling on the last day. The steady-state AUC24 of GLE and PIB for each simulated individual were calculated using the linear trapezoidal rule on the IPK samples on the last day. The distribution of AUC24 values for GLE and PIB in each body weight group corresponding to the respective doses was analyzed and compared to the target adult AUC24.

Results

Data Disposition and Exclusions

In Part 1, 48 HCV GT1- to GT4-infected adolescents were enrolled into cohort 1 of the study; one patient was randomized, not dosed, and excluded from this analysis [13]. Forty-four patients (94%; 44/47) received 8 weeks of therapy, and three patients (6%; 3/47) received 16 weeks of therapy. Seventeen patients participated in the IPK portion of the study, and data for three patients from the same study site were excluded from the analysis due to unusually low concentration PK profiles at the week 2 visit (see Fig. S1 in the electronic supplementary material). Day 1 values for these patients were within range of the other patients.

In Part 2, a total of 81 patients were enrolled; one patient was enrolled into cohort 4 but was not dosed. Eighty patients received study drug and were divided into cohorts 2, 3, and 4, respectively (Fig. S1). One patient (1/80) was partially dosed, discontinued from the study, and was excluded from these analyses. The first 17 patients enrolled into the IPK portion of the study in cohorts 2 (n = 6), 3 (n = 6), and 4 (n = 5) received the initial GLE/PIB dose ratio of 40/15 mg, which was based on the patient's body weight and age band at screening (see Table 1 and Fig. S1). Based on these data, the dose ratio was adjusted to 50/20 mg and confirmed in a second IPK analysis based on 38 patients (n = 13 in cohort 2, n = 13 in cohort 3, and n = 12 in cohort 4). The dose ratio of 50/20 mg was selected as the final dose ratio for the study (Fig. S1).

Overall, a total of 126 patients in the DORA study received at least one full dose of study drug (Table S1, Fig. S1). The median age of the patients in cohorts 1, 2, 3, and 4 were 14, 10, 7, and 4 years old, respectively. None of the patients in the DORA study had cirrhosis, only three patients (2.38%) had HCV-HIV coinfection. Approximately 56% were female, and only one adolescent patient from cohort 1 had mild impairment of renal function. The majority of patients (~90%) were treatment naïve.

The final pediatric GLE and PIB popPK models included all available data from both Part 1 (47 adolescents) and Part 2 (79 children) of the DORA study [13, 14]. In total, 693 GLE and 693 PIB plasma concentrations were utilized to build the popPK models. Plasma concentrations below the LLOQ were set to LLOQ/2, and the M6 method [23] was applied. Concentrations observed prior to the first dose (ten GLE and ten PIB concentrations) and subsequent samples below the LLOQ (two GLE and two PIB concentrations) were excluded from the analysis. Actual dosing times, recorded in patient diaries before scheduled PK visits, were used in the PK analysis where available. For doses without patient diary entries, dosing times of 8:00 a.m. were assumed for days without PK measurement. In cases where only sparse PK measurements were available, dosing times of 2 h after the PK sample were assumed. Dosing times were imputed when necessary for patients with IPK to be either 3 h before or after sample collection time, depending on the magnitude of the measured concentration.

Model Results

For GLE, a one-compartment PK model with first-order absorption and elimination adequately described the concentration–time data. The model was parameterized in terms of CL/F, V2/F, and absorption rate (ka). For PIB, a two-compartment PK model with first-order absorption and elimination, including a lag in absorption time, adequately described the concentration–time data. The model was parameterized in terms of CL/F, V2/F, ka, apparent inter-compartmental clearance (Q/F), apparent volume of distribution of the peripheral compartment (V3/F), and absorption lag time (ALAG1). In both models, ka was constrained to be greater than the elimination rate (ke = CL/V2) to avoid flip-flop PK. Body weight allometric scaling of CL/F and V2/F was treated as part of the structural base model. Using body surface area instead of body weight for allometric scaling led to analogous results. Since body weight is a more convenient measurement, it was kept as part of the base model. All structural parameters were estimated with adequate precision (relative standard error < 40%). Correlated random effects were included for CL/F and V2/F to describe inter-individual variability (IIV). A proportional error model was used to describe the residual error.

Covariates were tested on CL/F and V2/F, as appropriate, in stepwise covariate model building of the GLE and PIB popPK analysis. For GLE, race on V2/F was the only covariate identified as significant but was removed during backward elimination (P > 0.001); thus, the base and final models were identical. None of the tested covariates significantly affected GLE PK except body weight, which was included in the base model. None of the tested covariates significantly affected PIB PK. Additional information on the impact of body weight, age, and body surface area is provided in the “Supplementary Results” section. Discussion

The individual GLE concentrations were adequately described using a one-compartment PK model with first-order absorption and elimination. The individual PIB concentration–time data supported a two-compartment PK model with a lag time followed by first-order absorption and elimination. Structural models for GLE and PIB in pediatrics were similar to those reported in adults [15]. The pediatric formulations used in Part 2 of the DORA study were accounted for by parameterizing the F1 for both GLE and PIB according to a phase 1 study in adults comparing the adult and pediatric formulations. Body weight-based allometric scaling was applied to CL/F and V2/F for both GLE and PIB models.

Both final GLE and PIB model parameter values were robustly estimated, and pcVPCs indicated that the final models described the central tendency and the variability of the data reasonably. The individual model-predicted AUC24 values for patients with IPK sampling were also demonstrated to be comparable to NCA-calculated AUC24 values [13, 14].

The efficacy of the GLE/PIB combination has been established in HCV-infected adults, where the approved GLE/PIB 300 mg/120 mg formulation achieved exposures associated with high rates of SVR12 (> 95%). To support the extrapolation of efficacy from adults to pediatrics, it was critical to demonstrate that the proposed pediatric dosing regimens achieve comparable systemic exposures. Results from the DORA study confirmed that the model-predicted GLE and PIB exposures in pediatric patients following the proposed doses fell within the exposure ranges observed in HCV-infected adults receiving the GLE/PIB 300 mg/120 mg approved formulation [9, 15]. While the pediatric geometric mean AUC24 values were slightly higher than adult reference values (up to 77% for GLE and up to 44% for PIB), this difference is likely attributable to the small sample size in each pediatric dose group and the inherent high IIV in GLE and PIB exposures. Moreover, the model-predicted GLE and PIB exposure ranges in HCV-infected pediatric patients aged ≥ 3 to < 18 years encompasses the ranges observed in HCV-infected non-cirrhotic adults [9, 15].

To further confirm the appropriateness of the proposed final doses (see Table 1), PK simulations were performed by drawing virtual patients reflecting the body weight range of children aged ≥ 3 to < 12 years. These simulations demonstrated that the geometric mean AUC24 values are similar (≤ 32% difference) to the adult reference for both GLE and PIB. The simulated exposure distributions also closely matched the adult population, with about half of the simulated GLE AUC24 values and 80% of the simulated PIB AUC24 values lying within a 0.5- to twofold range around the respective median adult exposures.

This popPK analysis was integral to the regulatory approval of the GLE/PIB combination in pediatric patients aged ≥ 3 years by providing a quantitative framework to support dose selection and efficacy extrapolation. By demonstrating that the proposed pediatric doses achieve exposures comparable to those in adults, where efficacy and safety have been established, the analysis enabled extrapolation of the well-characterized adult exposure–efficacy relationship to the pediatric population. This approach aligns with regulatory guidance on pediatric drug development, which supports the use of PK bridging strategies when the disease course and drug response are expected to be sufficiently similar between adult and pediatric patients.

Although the number of patients in the DORA study, particularly in the IPK subgroups, was limited, as is typical for pediatric studies, the overlap of pediatric exposures with the established adult exposure ranges provides confidence in the extrapolation approach. The small sample size may have restricted the popPK model's ability to fully capture the effects of certain covariates on both GLE and PIB. However, the robustness of the popPK models and the demonstration that proposed doses achieve target exposures across the evaluated pediatric age range enable extrapolation of the data observed in the DORA study population to the broader pediatric population aged ≥ 3 to < 18 years.

Conclusions

In summary, all tested covariates (age, body surface area, sex, and race) did not significantly affect the PK of GLE or PIB in HCV-infected pediatric patients after including body weight-based allometric scaling of CL/F and V2/F as a structural covariate in the model. At the proposed doses, GLE and PIB exposures were similar between the HCV-infected pediatric patients and HCV-infected adults. The popPK analysis of pediatric patients reported here supported the approval of the GLE/PIB pediatric formulation and body weight-based dosing for children with chronic HCV.
