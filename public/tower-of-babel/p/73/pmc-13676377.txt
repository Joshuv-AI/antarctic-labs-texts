Relationship between Postoperative Neuropathic Pain and Clinical Scores in Patients with Cervical Ossification of the Posterior Longitudinal Ligament
Authors: Yuji Yokozeki, Masayuki Miyagi, Gen Inoue, Toshitaka Yoshii, Satoru Egawa, Kenichiro Sakai, Masahiko Takahata, Tsutomu Endo, Shunji Tsutsui, Masao Koda, Hiroshi Takahashi, Satoshi Kato, Kanji Mori, Hideaki Nakajima, Takeo Furuya, Satoshi Maki, Yoshiharu Kawaguchi, Norihiro Nishida, Kazuo Kusano, Hiroaki Nakashima, Shinsuke Ikeda, Masashi Takaso, Masashi Yamazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Postoperative neuropathic pain (NeP) is common after surgery for cervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (cOPLL) and may affect clinical outcomes. The PainDETECT questionnaire (PDQ) is a widely used tool for evaluating NeP, while the Spine PDQ (SPDQ) was developed to improve specificity for spinal disorder-related NeP. This study aimed to evaluate postoperative NeP using PDQ and SPDQ and to examine their associations with clinical scores. Methods A total of 234 patients (170 men and 64 women; mean age, 61.9 years) who underwent cOPLL surgery were analyzed and followed for more than 1 year across 13 institutions. NeP was assessed using PDQ (cutoff ≥13) and SPDQ (cutoff ≥0). Clinical outcomes included the Japanese Orthopedic Association Cervical Myelopathy Evaluation Questionnaire (JOACMEQ), the visual analogue scale (VAS), and the EuroQol 5-Dimensions 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L). Correlations between NeP scores and clinical outcomes were evaluated. Patients were stratified into four PDQ/SPDQ-based groups: Zone 1: PDQ−/SPDQ−, Zone 2: PDQ+/SPDQ−, Zone 3: PDQ−/SPDQ+, and Zone 4: PDQ+/SPDQ+, and clinical outcomes were compared among the groups. Results PDQ showed significant correlations with all clinical outcomes. SPDQ correlated with VAS, EQ-5D-5L, and the bladder function and quality of life (QOL) domains of JOACMEQ but not with other JOACMEQ domains. The PDQ+ group demonstrated worse scores across all domains than the PDQ− group. The SPDQ+ group had higher VAS scores and lower EQ-5D-5L and JOACMEQ QOL scores than that of the SPDQ− group, with no differences in other JOACMEQ domains. Zone 4 had the poorest prognosis, whereas Zone 3 showed higher VAS scores than Zone 1 but preserved JOACMEQ and EQ-5D-5L scores. Conclusions PDQ was associated with detecting pain, functional deterioration, and reduced QOL, while SPDQ reflected pain-related aspects only. Combined PDQ/SPDQ assessment and zone classification may stratify NeP severity and its clinical relevance in patients with postoperative cOPLL.

Introduction

Cervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (cOPLL) occurs when heterotopic bone forms in the posterior longitudinal ligament1). The resulting ossified lesion occupies the spinal canal, leading to spinal stenosis. In severe cases, this condition may result in motor and sensory dysfunction of the limbs, as well as bladder and bowel dysfunction. In such cases, patients usually require surgery. However, postoperative pain is often observed. Miyagi et al.2) reported that 51.7% and 40.4% patients with postoperative cOPLL had postoperative pain in the upper and lower extremities, respectively.

Pain is broadly classified into nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic categories. According to the International Association for the Study of Pain Task Force, neuropathic pain (NeP) is defined as “pain arising as a direct consequence of a lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory system”3). NeP is typically characterized by burning or electric shock-like sensations, tingling, or numbness and is often accompanied by sensory disturbances such as allodynia and hyperalgesia4). In spinal disorders, NeP is recognized as a major contributor to persistent pain after surgical intervention2,5). Accurate identification of NeP is essential for appropriate postoperative management because it is less responsive to standard analgesics and may considerably impact functional recovery and quality of life (QOL)2,6-8).

The painDETECT questionnaire (PDQ) is a simple, patient-based, easy-to-use screening tool used to investigate the prevalence of NeP9), allowing non-specialists to quickly identify potential patients with NeP4). However, PDQ may not have sufficient diagnostic utility in detecting NeP caused by spinal disorders10). To address this, the Spine PDQ (SPDQ) was developed as a valid screening tool10), and was constructed by calculating weighting coefficients for nine PDQ items to enhance specificity for spine-related NeP. Ikeda et al.11) previously reported a high prevalence of postoperative NeP after cOPLL surgery, evaluated using both PDQ and SPDQ. Their report also demonstrated that patients with poor preoperative Japanese Orthopedic Association scores may be at increased risk of postoperative NeP after surgery and should be monitored with greater care11). However, the relationship between postoperative NeP and clinical scores in patients with postoperative cOPLL remains largely unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the relationship between postoperative NeP and clinical scores in patients with postoperative cOPLL.

Materials and Methods

Patient population

This study was conducted as a multicenter prospective observational study, and this investigation represents a cross-sectional analysis of the postoperative data collected at a single time point. A total of 300 patients (213 men and 87 women; mean age, 62.0 years) who underwent surgical treatment for myelopathy secondary to cOPLL were initially enrolled, and patient-reported outcome measures were obtained at a single postoperative time point with a mean follow-up duration of 72.1±16.1 months after surgery. All participants prospectively completed a NeP questionnaire. Patients with preoperative neurological comorbidities or those who developed such conditions during follow-up, including individuals who underwent thoracolumbar spine surgery or had cerebrovascular disease, were excluded (57/300). An additional nine patients were excluded because of incomplete datasets, resulting in a final analytic study population of 234 patients (170 men and 64 women; mean age, 61.9 years).

Definition of NeP

NeP was assessed using two patient-reported outcome measures: the PDQ and the SPDQ. The PDQ, developed by Freynhagen et al.9), is a validated screening tool for detecting NeP components in patients with low back pain. PDQ scores of 13-18 are classified as “unclear,” and scores ≥19 are considered “positive” for NeP, with a probability exceeding 90%. In this study, NeP was defined as a PDQ score ≥13. The SPDQ, developed by Nikaido et al.10), was adapted from the PDQ to evaluate NeP in patients with spinal disorders. SPDQ scores are calculated by multiplying the PDQ score using a weighting coefficient for each of the nine items and by subtracting 12 points. An SPDQ score ≥0 was considered indicative of NeP in this study. In this study, both PDQ and SPDQ scores were derived from the same set of questionnaire responses using different scoring algorithms.

Measurements

The clinical scores of patients included the Japanese Orthopedic Association Cervical Myelopathy Evaluation Questionnaire (JOACMEQ) and the EuroQol 5-Dimensions 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L).

The JOACMEQ is a patient-administered instrument designed to assess disease-specific outcomes in cervical myelopathy and to evaluate disability, functional impairment, and overall health status12,13). The questionnaire contains 24 items divided into five domains: cervical spine function, upper limb function, lower limb function, bladder function, and QOL. Each domain is scored from 0 to 100, with higher values reflecting better function.

Pain and sensory symptoms were also quantified using a 0-100 mm visual analogue scale (VAS), where 0 represents “no pain or numbness” and 100 denotes “worst pain or numbness imaginable.” The VAS captured four symptom locations: neck/shoulder pain or stiffness, chest tightness, upper limb pain or numbness, and lower limb pain or numbness.

The EQ-5D-5L is a standardized instrument developed by the EuroQol Group to measure generic health status14). It evaluates five dimensions of health, including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, and generates a single utility index that enables comparisons across diseases and populations. In this study, health-related QOL (HRQOL) was assessed using the EQ-5D-5L, with the utility index calculated based on the Japanese population value set15). The EQ-5D-5L index ranges from 0 (death) to 1 (full health), with higher scores indicating better HRQOL.

Statistical analysis

Correlations between PDQ or SPDQ scores and various clinical scores were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. The correlation coefficients were classified as follows: r-values of 0.2-0.4, 0.4-0.7, and 0.7-1 were considered weak, moderate, and strong correlations, respectively. Subsequently, patients were divided into two groups according to the presence or absence of NeP based on PDQ or SPDQ cutoff values (PDQ≥13; SPDQ≥0). Patients with PDQ scores ≥13 were defined as PDQ positive (PDQ+), and those with PDQ scores <13 were defined as PDQ negative (PDQ−). Similarly, patients with SPDQ scores ≥0 were defined as SPDQ positive (SPDQ+), and those with SPDQ scores <0 were defined as SPDQ negative (SPDQ−). Clinical scores were compared between NeP-positive and NeP-negative groups. For comparisons, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify normality; normally and non-normally distributed variables were compared using the unpaired t-test and Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. Furthermore, patients were classified into four groups based on PDQ and SPDQ thresholds (Zone 1: PDQ− and SPDQ−; Zone 2: PDQ+ and SPDQ−; Zone 3: PDQ− and SPDQ+; and Zone 4: PDQ+ and SPDQ+) (Figure 1). Differences in clinical scores among the four groups were analyzed using Dunnett's t-test (two-sided), with each group compared with Zone 1 as the control. All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 29.0.2.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Classification of patients into four groups based on PDQ and SPDQ scores.

Patients were classified into four zones according to the results of the PDQ and SPDQ scores: Zone 1 (PDQ−/SPDQ−), Zone 2 (PDQ+/SPDQ−), Zone 3 (PDQ−/SPDQ+), and Zone 4 (PDQ+/SPDQ+). The cutoff scores used were 13 for PDQ and 0 for SPDQ.

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from our Institutional Review Board in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions.

Results

There were significant correlations between PDQ scores and all clinical outcomes, including JOACMEQ function scores, VAS scores, and EQ-5D-5L scores. Specifically, PDQ scores demonstrated weak negative correlations with cervical spine function, lower extremity function, and bladder function and moderate negative correlations with upper extremity function and QOL of the JOACMEQ. PDQ scores also exhibited moderate positive correlations with VAS scores for neck pain, chest tightness, upper limb pain or numbness, and lower limb pain or numbness. In addition, PDQ scores were moderately and negatively correlated with EQ-5D-5L.

SPDQ scores were significantly correlated with selected clinical outcomes. For the JOACMEQ domains, SPDQ scores showed no significant correlations with cervical spine function, upper extremity function, or lower extremity function. Weak negative correlations were observed with bladder function and QOL. Regarding the VAS scores, SPDQ scores demonstrated weak positive correlations with neck pain and chest tightness, a moderate positive correlation with upper limb pain or numbness, and a weak positive correlation with lower limb pain or numbness. SPDQ scores were also weakly and negatively correlated with EQ-5D-5L scores (Table 1).

No significant differences were observed between Zone 3 and Zone 1 in any JOACMEQ domains (Figure 2).

Discussion

In this study, PDQ correlated with all clinical scores for function, numbness, pain, and QOL. According to previous studies, patients with diabetic NeP experience sleep disorders due to pain, which may lead to anxiety, depression, decreased physical activity, and functional impairment in daily life16). Other studies have shown that patients with knee osteoarthritis who had NeP demonstrated significantly higher VAS scores and poorer physical function17). These findings suggest that the presence of NeP on the PDQ in patients with cOPLL after surgery may affect not only pain and numbness but also function and QOL. However, higher PDQ scores may reflect greater overall pain severity and multiple pain-related symptoms, in addition to NeP features, as indicated in previous validation studies involving PDQ9,16).

In contrast, SPDQ correlated with pain, numbness, and QOL but not with function. SPDQ was developed to address the limited specificity of PDQ for spinal disorder-related NeP10). It was constructed by calculating weighting coefficients for the nine items of the PDQ, emphasizing the specificity of NeP caused by spinal disorders. These results suggest that in patients with cOPLL after surgery, the presence of NeP on the SPDQ may affect pain, numbness, and QOL, while function may remain preserved. However, the number of clinical studies utilizing SPDQ remains limited, and its clinical applicability and interpretability have not yet been fully validated in diverse spinal populations.

In this study, most patients with NeP on the PDQ also had NeP on the SPDQ. Zone 2 had the fewest patients, with only nine cases (Figure 5). The SPDQ was developed by modifying the PDQ to screen for NeP caused by spinal disorders. A scoring system was devised by calculating weighting coefficients for the nine items of the PDQ. Specifically, numbness and tingling sensations were weighted eight and two times, respectively. Conversely, electric shock-like pain, pain from cold/heat stimuli, and pain from light touch were weighted four, three, and two times, respectively. These findings are likely due to the study population being patients who underwent cOPLL surgery, many of whom had spinal NeP. In addition, PDQ may capture NeP components originating from non-spinal sources, such as concomitant peripheral neuropathy, and because PDQ scores are known to correlate with pain severity and have a reported specificity of 84%, the possibility of false-positive PDQ results cannot be excluded.

In this study, Zones 2 and 4 (NeP present according to PDQ) showed poor clinical scores for both pain and function. Conversely, Zone 3 (NeP present according to SPDQ but absent according to PDQ) had poor VAS scores but preserved function. Zone 3 may represent patients with NeP features that were captured by SPDQ but not by PDQ. This pattern may reflect milder NeP, in which neuropathic characteristics are present but overall pain severity and functional impairment remain limited. Although no previous reports have examined PDQ and SPDQ together, these results clarify the difference in clinical significance between the two scales.

Therefore, SPDQ may serve as a screening tool for identifying spinal-origin NeP, whereas PDQ may be useful for evaluating more severe NeP associated with functional deterioration and reduced QOL. In this study, SPDQ positivity identified patients with pain and sensory symptoms despite preserved function, as observed in Zone 3. In contrast, PDQ positivity was associated with greater pain severity, functional impairment, and poorer QOL, particularly in Zones 2 and 4. Thus, simultaneous assessment using PDQ and SPDQ may help distinguish between the presence of NeP and its clinical severity, thereby reducing false-positive or false-negative interpretations when either tool is used alone. Using PDQ and SPDQ together may be beneficial for developing intervention strategies.

This study has some limitations. First, we did not account for the use of medications that may influence NeP. Neither the presence and type nor the dosage of pharmacological treatment was evaluated in the analysis. As these factors can affect pain perception, QOL, and functional outcomes, the absence of medication control may have influenced the observed associations between NeP classification and clinical scores. Future studies should incorporate detailed information on pharmacological treatments to clarify their impact, which may provide a better understanding of the relationship between therapeutic interventions and clinical outcomes in patients with NeP. Second, this study assessed postoperative NeP at a single postoperative time point. Therefore, we were unable to evaluate the trajectory of NeP or determine whether symptoms improved, persisted, or worsened over time. A longitudinal design involving repeated assessments would be necessary to clarify the natural history of postoperative NeP and its relationship with functional recovery and QOL. In addition, as the postoperative assessments were conducted at a single time point, we could not evaluate whether the clinical associations differed according to the timing of evaluation after surgery. Third, preoperative evaluation was not included in this study, which limited our ability to assess the degree of change in pain and function from baseline. Incorporating preoperative data would allow for a more comprehensive analysis of the course of NeP and the extent of postoperative functional improvement.

In conclusion, among patients who underwent surgery for cOPLL, NeP evaluated by PDQ was associated with pain, functional deterioration, and reduced QOL, whereas NeP evaluated by SPDQ was associated primarily with pain and QOL, without reflecting functional decline. Patients positive for both PDQ and SPDQ exhibited the poorest clinical outcomes, indicating multifaceted and severe NeP. Overall, these findings suggest that the complementary use of PDQ and SPDQ, as represented by the zone classification, may provide a useful framework for stratifying the severity and clinical relevance of postoperative NeP in patients with postoperative cOPLL.
