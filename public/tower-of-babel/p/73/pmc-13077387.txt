Global, regional, and national burden of chronic respiratory diseases among perimenopausal women, 1990–2021: an analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Lixiuyun Wang, Yafei Tang, Zixiang Ji, Guoqiang Yang, Jin Huang, Yonghong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerimenopausal women face a range of health issues, yet there is a lack of systematic overview regarding their pulmonary health. We aimed to assess the burden, risk factors, and cross-country inequality of chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) in perimenopausal women from 1990 to 2021.MethodsBased on the 2021 Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD), we assessed the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), prevalence rate (ASPR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life year rate (ASDR) for CRDs (1990–2021). Trends were analyzed using Joinpoint regression, correlations with Pearson’s method, and inequalities using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index (CIX). Risk factors for CRDs were analyzed from exposure data.ResultsIn 2021, CRDs were responsible for 2.11 million new cases, resulting in 3.97 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in perimenopausal women. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), with 1.1 (0.8–1.4) million incidence cases, had the highest ASIR and ASMR. With 14.1 (11.4–17.2) million prevalent cases, asthma had the highest prevalence among CRDs. The rates of interstitial lung disease and pulmonary sarcoidosis had slightly increased. The ASDRs of COPD, pneumoconiosis and asthma were negatively correlated with the socio-demographic index (SDI). There were significant health inequalities across 204 countries and territories. Particulate matter pollution is the greatest risk factor for CRDs.ConclusionsCRDs caused heavy burdens and deaths. Some risk factors (environmental pollution, high BMI and tobacco use) need to be urgently managed and improved, especially in areas with low and low-middle SDI levels. The correlation between elevated ASIR of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis and increasing of SDI needs to be given more attention.Keywords: Global Burden of Disease (GBD), perimenopausal women, health inequality, chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs)

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study focuses on the burden of chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) among perimenopausal women and analyzes health inequalities and risk factors.

What is known and what is new?

• The global burden of CRDs decreased from 1990 to 2021.

• The burden of disability-adjusted life-years for CRDs is heavier in low-socio-demographic index (SDI) regions.

• Tobacco, particulate matter pollution and high body mass index are the primary risks for CRDs in perimenopausal women. Household air pollution from solid fuels is the greatest risk factor in low SDI areas, while tobacco is the greatest risk factor in high SDI areas.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Developing prevention and control strategies for CRDs remains crucial. Attention should be given to household solid fuel use, body mass index management, and tobacco control.

Introduction

With the ovaries failing, women may experience a variety of mental and physical distress as they go through a special time in their lives, perimenopause. The transition from the reproductive period to the first year of postmenopause, termed perimenopause, occurs over several years and is characterized by substantial biological change (1). Significant fluctuations in sex hormones during this period can cause a variety of symptoms and a significant decline in the function of certain organs (2). A study of 21 centers suggested that perimenopause is associated with lower lung function and more respiratory symptoms (3). An analysis of the European Community Respiratory Health Survey revealed a more precipitous decline in lung function among transitional and postmenopausal women (4). In 2015, the United Nations established the Targets of Sustainable Development Goal 3 to reduce premature mortality from noncommunicable diseases by one-third by 2030 through prevention and treatment and to promote mental health and well-being (5). The treatment and prevention of this special population require wider social attention and understanding to better contribute to the realization of the relevant items of Sustainable Development Goal 3.

In 2019, chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) were the third leading cause of mortality, accounting for four million deaths. The most prevalent CRDs are chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma. Pneumoconiosis, interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary sarcoidosis pose significant public health challenges on a global scale (6). Although CRDs have been identified in global populations, there is a lack of epidemiologic evidence of CRDs in perimenopausal women, a population whose symptoms or illnesses are often overlooked and underestimated and whose complex physiologic changes require more deliberate prevention and treatment programs.

We used data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 to characterize the prevalence of CRDs (i.e. COPD, asthma, pneumoconiosis, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis) in menopausal women and risk factors. Notably, this is the first study of CRDs that targeted menopausal women and was based on the GBD 2021 study.

Methods

Data sources

The GBD2021 study expanded the data sources and modeled each CRD slightly differently The GBD2021 study provided comprehensive, up-to-date estimates of 371 diseases, injuries, and impairments and 88 risk factors in 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational areas, including 19,189 new data sources for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), 12 new causes, and other important methodological updates (7). Our study included the incidence, prevalence, deaths and DALYs of CRDs. Stratification of these indicators was performed through regional and socio-demographic indices (SDIs). The SDI was the geometric mean of 0–1 indices of total fertility rate (TFR) for those younger than 25 years old (TFU25), mean education for those 15 years old and older (EDU15+), and lag-distributed income (LDI) per capita. The geographic areas were categorized according to the SDI quintiles as high, high-middle, middle, low-middle and low. Details of the methodologies applied in GBD2021 have been thoroughly explained in previous studies (7,8). Data downloads and information on fatal and nonfatal estimates are available at the GBD 2021 website (VizHub-GBD Results and VizHub-GBD Compare). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Case definition

We defined perimenopause as 45–54 years of age on the basis of previous studies (3,4,9). In GBD 2021, instances of CRDs—COPD, pneumoconiosis (including bestosis, coal workers’ pneumoconiosis, silicosis, and other types of pneumoconiosis), asthma, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis—were identified on the basis of the International Classification of Diseases and Injuries-10 diagnostic codes (6) (Tables S1,S2).

Cross-country inequality analysis

The distributive inequality of the four CRDs burdens across countries were measured by the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the health inequality Concentration Index (CIX) (10). The SII was calculated by regressing the national DALYs rates for the corresponding age population on the SDI-associated relative position scale, defined by the midpoint of the cumulative range of populations ranked by the SDI. Heteroskedasticity was accounted for by the use of a weighted regression model. The CIX was calculated by numerically integrating the area under the Lorenz concentration curve, which was fitted using the cumulative fraction of DALYs and the cumulative relative distribution of the population ranked by the SDI (11). The concentration curve lies below the 45° diagonal from the bottom left to the top right (the so-called equality line) when health indicators are concentrated in advantaged areas and vice versa for disadvantaged areas (10).

Risk factors

In this study, we analyzed risk factors for CRDs during perimenopause in terms of DALYs, including particulate matter (PM) pollution (ambient PM pollution and household air pollution from solid fuels), ambient ozone pollution, non-optimal temperature (low and high temperature), occupational risks (occupational asthmagens, occupational exposure to asbestos, silica and occupational PM, gases, and fumes), behavioral risks (tobacco) and metabolic risks [high body-mass index (BMI)]. The specific calculation method has been described in detail in previous studies (12,13). To facilitate interpretation of the results, we described the percentage of risk factors for each of the two age groups in the GBD 2021 results.

Statistical analyses

The GBD study employed sophisticated modeling techniques to evaluate the burden of CRDs. The cause of death ensemble model, spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and a Bayesian meta-regression modeling tool, DisMod-MR V.2.1, were used to ensure consistency between incidence, excess mortality and cause-specific mortality (13). Age-standardized rates (ASRs) were calculated via the GBD global standard population. Point estimates are presented with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), and rates are reported per 100,000 people. 95% UIs were estimated via the 25th and 975th ordered values among 1,000 draws in each computational stage (6,8). The number of cases of morbidity, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs in perimenopausal women was obtained via direct summation of the corresponding numbers of women aged 45–49 and 50–54 years. In this study, the average annual percentage change (AAPC) was used to assess trends in age-standardized rates (ASRs) of incidence, prevalence mortality and DALYs for each cause. We used a joinpoint regression model to calculate the annual percentage change (APC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) and then calculated the AAPC on the basis of the trend in the APC. Joinpoint regression is a statistical method used to analyze trend changes in time series data by identifying “joinpoints”, splitting the time series into multiple segments, and calculating the APC for each segment (15). In our analysis, we used a log-linear model (Iny = xb) and selected the optimal number of connections via a Monte Carlo permutation test. The AAPC was a weighted average of the APCs. In the context of age-standardized indicators, an increase was indicated by both the AAPC value and the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeding 0, whereas a decrease was indicated by both the AAPC value and the upper bound of the 95% CI being less than 0. Conversely, a stable indicator was one for which the 95% CI of the AAPC was 0. This part of the statistical analysis was performed via joinpoint software (version 5.2.0, Joinpoint Regression Program) developed by the National Cancer Institute Division of Cancer Control & Population Sciences. Pearson correlation analysis was used to measure the strength and direction of the associations between covariates, and plotting was completed via the R program 4.4.2.

Results

Global level

According to the GBD Study 2021, the number of global DALYs attributable to CRD in perimenopausal women in 2021 was 3.97 million, with 2.11 million new incidence cases. There was a significant decline in the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) from 1990 to 2021 (AAPC =−2.21, 95% CI: −2.27 to −2.14) (Table 1).

In 2021, 34.5 thousand perimenopausal women died from COPD, with an ASMR of 16.8. A total of 19.7 thousand people died from asthma, with an ASMR of 7.63. A total of 3.3 thousand people died from ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, with an ASMR of 0.69, and 102 died from pneumoconiosis, with an ASMR of 0.04. A total of 1.1 million cases of COPD with an ASIR of 241.35 surpassed the rates for asthma (211.38; 125.31–315.51), 1.5 thousand cases of pneumoconiosis with an ASIR of 0.32, 27.6 thousand cases of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis with an ASIR of 5.99 (3.57–8.69). From 1990 to 2021, the ASIRs for the four CRDs (except for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, which had a slight upward trend) showed a downward trend, with asthma showing the most pronounced downward trend (AAPC =−1.91, 95% CI: −1.97 to −1.87) (Table 1).

Globally, from 1990 to 2021, the prevalence of asthma was consistently higher than that of COPD. In 2021, the ASPR of COPD was 2,527.03, that of asthma was 3,064.73, that of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis was 64.03, and the lowest ASPR for pneumoconiosis was 1.79. The ASPRs of the four CRDs showed a decreasing trend and were most significant for asthma (AAPC =−2.06, 95% CI: −2.08 to −2.03) (Table 1).

Region level

Notably, from 1990 to 2021, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR and ASDR for asthma exhibited a decreasing trend in all regions (in addition to the increasing trend in the ASPR in high-income North America). An increasing trend in the ASIR and ASPR was observed in five regions of COPD: Caribbean, Central Sub-Saharan Africa, high-income North America, North Africa and Middle East and Western Sub-Saharan Africa. The ASMR and ASDR of COPD in high-income North America were increased. For pneumoconiosis, only one region (Southeast Asia) of the ASIR exhibited an increase, the ASMR increased in four regions (Australasia, Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania and Tropical Latin America). For ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, eleven regions (Andean Latin America, Australasia, Caribbean, Central Latin America, Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa and Middle East, Oceania, Southeast Asia, Tropical Latin America, Western Europe) experienced an increase in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR and ASDR (Tables S3-S6, Figures S1-S4).

The top five regions for ASDR and the absolute AAPC value across the four CRDs are summarized in Table 2. In terms of the four CRDs, COPD accounts for approximately ninety percent of mortality and DALYs in East Asia, whereas ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis account for more than fifty percent of mortality in Andean Latin America. Nevertheless, in four African regions, asthma is the cause of more than 50% of mortality from four CRDs (Figure S5).

National level

In some countries in Africa and central Latin America, the data revealed a slight increase in the ASIR (AAPC >0) of CRDs (Table S7, Figure 1). In 2021, the United States of America, Nepal and Hungary were the three countries with the highest ASIRs and ASPRs of COPD. The lowest ASIRs of COPD were observed in Japan and Singapore, two countries located in the high-income Asia-Pacific region. In the United States of America, the ASIR and ASPR reached 402.84 and 3,659.9, respectively, whereas in Nepal, they reached 397.92 and 3,722.18, respectively. Papua New Guinea presented the highest ASMR and ASDR, at 50.86 and 2,281.84, respectively. Nevertheless, Morocco exhibited the most rapid growth in terms of the ASIR and ASPR. Saint Vincent and the Grenadines demonstrated the most rapid growth, whereas Guyana exhibited the most rapid growth in the ASDR (Tables S8-S11).

SDI levels and correlation of the SDI with age-standardized rates

According to the SDI classification, the ASIR and ASPR of COPD, asthma and pneumoconiosis have decreased. ASIR was elevated in all SDI classifications of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis except for the low SDI quintile. In 2021, the high SDI quintile level demonstrated a higher ASIR and ASPR for asthma, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis. Additionally, the low-middle-SDI quintile levels presented the highest ASIR and ASPR of COPD, whereas the middle-SDI quintile level presented the highest ASIR and ASPR of pneumoconiosis. The ASMR and ASDR for all CRDs were higher at the low-middle and low SDI quintile levels (Tables S3-S6, Figure S6).

Nationally, the burden of the four CRDs in 204 countries and territories was associated in some way with the SDI in 2021. Specifically, the ASMR and ASDR for COPD, asthma and pneumoconiosis were negatively associated with the SDI, whereas the ASIR and ASPR of asthma, pneumoconiosis and ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis were positively associated with the SDI. No significant correlation was found between the ASIR and ASPR of COPD, the ASPR of asthma and the ASMR and ASDR of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis and the SDI (Figure 2, Figures S7-S9).

Cross-country inequality analysis

For the four CRDs burdens, inequalities in absolute and relative SDI were detected. The DALYs disproportionately concentrated in countries with low SDI. The SII for asthma exhibited a shift from −456.0 in 1990 to −291.0 in 2021, whereas the CIX demonstrated a transition from −13.7 to −23 (Figure 3, A1,A2). This indicates that there was an excess of 456 DALYs per 100,000 between the countries with the highest and lowest SDI in 1990 and 291 in 2021. Similarly, the gap for COPD decreased from 533.2 to 386.1, with a CIX of −24.0 in 1990 and −19.8 in 2021 (Figure 3, B1,B2); the gap for pneumoconiosis decreased from 1.7 to 1.2, with a CIX of −13.3 in 1990 and −10.4 in 2021 (Figure 3, C1,C2); and the gap for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis changed from 5.0 to 3.78, with a CIX of 0.5 and −7.1 in 2021. Notably, the concentration curve for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis shifted from a position below the 1990 equality line to one above it in 2021 (Figure 3, D1,D2).

Risk factors

The study assessed the ASR of DALYs due to all attributable risks of CRDs. A decrease in the ASDR due to CRDs attributable to all risk factors from 1990 to 2021 was observed, except for ambient PM pollution (Figure S11). A decline in the ASDR attributable to household air pollution from solid fuels and tobacco was observed across regions. Conversely, the ASDR attributable to high temperature exhibited a notable increase in Central and Western Europe (Figure S12) whereas a similar trend was observed for low temperature in high-income North America. The ASDR attributable to asbestos exposure has increased in several regions, with tropical Latin America and Australasia demonstrating increases of more than 100%. Additionally, the ASDR attributable to ambient PM pollution increased in South Asia, whereas ambient ozone pollution exhibited varying degrees of increase across different regions (Figure S12).

In 2021, for perimenopausal women, PM pollution was the greatest risk factor for CRDs, especially household air pollution from solid fuels (Table 3, Figure 4A) and was evident in low and low-middle SDI quintile levels, followed by tobacco (Figure 4A, Figures S13,S14). Moreover, high BMI was one of the important risk factors and was most evident at high SDI quintile levels (Table S12, Figures S13,S14). The data demonstrated that, in addition to the aforementioned major risk factors, the risk of death from CRD was elevated at low temperatures relative to high temperatures. This disparity was particularly pronounced at the high SDI quintile level. Globally, with respect to COPD, household air pollution from solid fuels presented a greater risk than does that from tobacco (Figure 4B). The risk factors for asthma can be classified into three main categories, with high BMI being the most important. The risk factors for pneumoconiosis were all occupational, and the proportion of risk varies across regions, reflecting, to some extent, the variability of the disease across regions (Figure 4B).

Discussion

In this study, we used the latest GBD 2021 release to focus our perspective on perimenopausal women aged 45–54 years, describing their burden of CRD from 1990 to 2021. On a global scale, the number of mortality cases resulting from CRD has increased, yet the ASRs have decreased. In contrast to those of the other three diseases, the ASIRs of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis patients slightly increased, whereas the ASMR and ASDR remained stable. Cross-country inequality analysis revealed that perimenopausal women in low-SDI countries should have a disproportionately greater burden of CRDs.

The SDI was not correlated with the ASIRs of COPD and asthma; instead, it was strongly negatively correlated with the ASMRs of COPD and asthma. This finding reflects the significant impact of better healthcare resources on reducing the burden of these diseases in countries with higher SDIs. A review indicates that medications for treating asthma and COPD are difficult to obtain (access rates of 30–60%) and prohibitively expensive in most low- and middle-income countries (16). Between 40% and 70% of medical equipment and devices in low- and middle-income countries are broken, unused or unfit for purpose (17). Positive correlations between the SDI and the ASIR of pneumoconiosis and negative correlations between the SDI and the ASMR of pneumoconiosis were observed, but both correlations were at lower levels. There was a slight positive correlation between the ASIRs of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis and the SDI but not between the ASMRs and the SDI. ILD encompasses over one hundred subtypes, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary sarcoidosis, whose etiological mechanisms remain unclear. This poses significant diagnostic challenges for ILD, particularly in regions with inadequate medical resources. Advances in science and technology, coupled with the increased accessibility of diagnostic technologies, have improved the detection rate of these conditions, such as high-resolution CT scan (HRCT), ultrasonically guided fine-needle biopsy (EBUS), 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron-emission tomography and CT (FDG PET-CT) (18-20). Antifibrotic drugs can improve quality of life in patients with progressive pulmonary fibrosis but are prohibitively expensive. Lung transplantation remains the only viable treatment option for advanced fibrotic ILD, yet it is significantly constrained by technical limitations and donor lung availability (21). This may explain the elevated ASIR for ILD while the ASMR remains relatively stable.

From 1990 to 2021, a considerable decrease in the ASDR was observed due to CRDs attributable to all risk factors, except for ambient PM pollution in perimenopausal women. The ASDR from CRDs due to ambient PM pollution increased in South Asia, Africa and the low-SDI quintile. Low- and low-middle-income countries have shown relatively high concentrations of PM pollution due to a lack of legislation and/or adherence to air quality guidelines, a relatively high prevalence of coal power stations, and not meeting vehicle emission standards (22,23). The ASDR for COPD attributable to household air pollution from solid fuels was at its highest risk, especially in low- and middle-income countries. The Clean Cooking Alliance (CCA) is one of the most important global initiatives for universal access to clean cooking in low- and middle-income countries. In fact, the cost and infrastructure of cleaner fuels are recognized constraints that require corresponding national policy support (24).

In high- and upper-middle-income regions such as high-income Asia Pacific, America (high-income North America, southern Latin America), Europe and Australasia, tobacco continues to be the most important risk factor, followed by a high BMI. The World Health Organization (WHO)’s Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) entered into force in 2005. In 2008, the WHO launched the MPOWER package to complement the implementation of the FCTC. The achievements of these measures have been significant (25,26), but many challenges remain in implementing them in each country (27,28). Studies indicate that women over 40 who smoke face a higher risk of developing COPD (29), and women generally have poorer outcomes (30). As estrogen levels decline in menopausal women, tobacco use can also affect estrogen metabolism in the lungs, which can lead to even more adverse consequences, and women who smoke and have early natural menopause are at greater risk of malignant and non–malignant lung disease and mortality (31,32). Non-optimal temperatures are now receiving increasing attention, and there is a greater risk of CRDs from low temperatures. This finding is in line with those of previous studies, which suggest that low temperatures increase the risk of COPD and asthma (33,34). When preventing CRDs, the influence of low or changing temperatures should be considered.

A high BMI has been identified as the predominant risk factor for asthma. Since 2010, obesity has been linked to a greater risk of asthma in women than in men (35). With obesity on the rise, both the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force and the American Heart Association have issued recommendations and statements on the prevention, management, and treatment of obesity (36,37). It is imperative that clinicians, patients, and public health professionals engage in collaborative efforts to enhance the prevention and intervention of obesity (38).

To determine the influence of sex and age on the incidence of CRDs, a comparison was made of the incidence of the four diseases in male and female subjects, classified globally and at the SDI level in each age group at intervals of five years from the ages of 20–70 years. The findings indicated that by 2021, perimenopausal women worldwide, within the high- and high-middle-SDI quintile levels, presented a lower ASIR of asthma than other age groups did. The incidence patterns of asthma among men and women worldwide exhibit a U-shaped curve (Figure S15). A ten-year prospective cohort study demonstrated that the risk of developing asthma in postmenopausal women was significantly lower than that in premenopausal women (39). Estrogen influences asthma pathogenesis pathways, increasing its incidence, while androgens can reduce asthma incidence. With advancing age, changes in immune system function (immune senescence) lead to asthma shifting from an allergic/eosinophilic type to a non-allergic, neutrophilic type or severe form (40,41). Moreover, a notable surge in asthma cases was observed from 2020 to 2021 across most regions, particularly in high-income North America, as the COVID-19 pandemic increased the risk of developing asthma (42).

The numbers of deaths and DALYs associated with the four CRDs in East Asia were attributed mainly to COPD (approximately 90% of the total). This observation may be attributed to East Asia encompassing the most populous country, China. COPD in China accounts for one-quarter of the global COPD population (43). The ASDR for COPD in Papua New Guinea, and Nepal exceeded the standard deviation by more than 3 times. In the case of Nepal, studies (44,45) show a higher COPD prevalence in women than in men. Additionally, the ASDR for asthma in Papua New Guinea is also at the highest level. These findings correlate with low to moderate SDI levels, as well as the urgent need to strengthen public health policies and ensure adequate access to medicines in the region (46).

The ASDRs of pneumoconiosis in North Africa, the Middle East, Central Sub-Saharan Africa and southern Africa were significantly greater. In North Africa, Egypt, and in Southern Africa, Eswatini and Lesotho, in Central Asia, Kazakhstan, the ASDR for pneumoconiosis exceeded three times the standard deviation. The growing burden in the Sub-Saharan Africa region was chiefly attributed to increased desert dust due to climate change and rapid urbanization (47,48), along with generally low environmental awareness (49). Kazakhstan has a large industrial sector, and there is a dangerous exposure to suspended particulate matter in most cities, with heavy metal pollution detected in several cities (50). The results show occupational risk is the only risk factor for pneumoconiosis. In regions affected by this condition, effective resource allocation and the development of comprehensive occupational health legislation are paramount to reducing toxic exposure and ensuring that susceptible workers have access to adequate healthcare. In the high-income Asia Pacific region, although the ASMR of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis was not high, its proportion of deaths ranked second. Interestingly, the concentration curve for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis shifted from a position below the equality line in 1990 to the one above it in 2021. This change is related to improvements in diagnostic techniques, but also to unequal access to healthcare resources and the high cost of anti-fibrotic treatments (18-21). The GBD 2021 has not yet identified risk factors for ILD, but occupational and environmental hazards may increase susceptibility to the disease (51). Industrial development and urbanization progress in many countries, such as those in Africa, have environmental implications (47,48). This findings indicate that, whether in regions with low SDI levels or those with high SDI levels, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis need to be paid more attention to for public health management.

Limitations

However, the study also has several limitations that should be considered when the results are interpreted. First, the accuracy of the estimates may be affected by the quality and availability of data sources in various countries and regions. In addition, due to limitations in medical resources in countries with low and low-middle SDI levels, misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis occur, resulting in deviations from real data. These are especially the case, as ILD have numerous subtypes and are difficult to diagnose. Furthermore, GBD estimates are constrained by data sources, with incomplete baseline data in certain countries. Population estimates rely on model projections, and for regions with significant migration flows, GBD adjustments may be less effective. Additionally, the choice of statistical methods may lead to inaccurate estimates of data variance, and other uncertain covariates could introduce bias (8).

The GBD 2021 lacks individual-level data, so we define the average age of perimenopause worldwide as 45–54 years. This definition inevitably includes some women who are not in the perimenopausal stage or fails to cover all women in perimenopause, resulting in approximately 10–20% of women being misclassified (31,52). In addition, perimenopausal women tend to use hormone replacement therapy (HRT). Research in the United States (53) and South Korea (54) indicates that higher income correlates with higher rates of HRT use. A Swedish study revealed that the use of hormones during perimenopause can increase the risk of developing sarcoidosis, especially when systemic estrogen is used (aOR 1.51, 95% CI: 1.23–1.85) (55). A meta-analysis showed that HRT increases the risk of asthma: RR 1.41, 95% CI: 1.09–1.81 (56). Unfortunately, GBD 2021 is unable to assess the impact of HRT on the prevalence of CRDs. By the late 1990s, HRT use reached an all-time peak (57,58). In 1998, the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) raised awareness about the risks of HRT, resulting in a significant decline in its use over the following two decades (58,59). Therefore, we believe that from 1990 to 2021, the use of HRT had an impact on the incidence of CRDs in perimenopausal women, but this impact was likely minimal. Due to significant fluctuations in hormone levels among perimenopausal women, we recommend that physicians monitor estrogen levels and fully consider individual variations when prescribing medications for this population, thereby developing more personalized treatment plans.

Conclusions

Menopausal women have a complex impact on the respiratory tract and lungs due to significant fluctuations in sex hormones. From 1990 to 2021, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR and ASDR of CRDs among perimenopausal women declined, except for an increase in the ASIR of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis. Particulate matter pollution is the greatest risk factor for COPD in menopausal women. Attention should be given to the impact of low temperature on CRDs in high-income North America and Australasia, which is a risk factor that is easily overlooked. Tobacco use and high BMI in some regions, such as the Americas and Europe, have always been the most important risk factors for COPD and asthma, and there is still a need to strengthen the management and enforcement of related policies. Perimenopausal women should receive more attention under supportive policies. It is crucial to disseminate scientific knowledge to women during this period to ensure that they can pass through it in a healthy and smooth manner.
