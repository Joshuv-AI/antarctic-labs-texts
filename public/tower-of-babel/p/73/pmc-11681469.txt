Association Between Stroke Lesion Size and Atrial Fibrillation Detected After Stroke: An Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Markus G Klammer, Laura Reimann, Oskar Richter, Simone Lieschke, Helena Stengl, Simon Hellwig, Ramanan Ganeshan, Maximilian Schöls, Alexander Nelde, Christian Meisel, Kersten Villringer, Christian H Nolte, Matthias Endres, Jan Friedrich Scheitz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation detected after stroke (AFDAS) is considered to be a distinct entity influenced by cardiogenic and neurogenic factors. We hypothesized that patients with AFDAS have larger stroke lesions than patients without atrial fibrillation (AF) and with known AF (KAF).Methods and ResultsConsecutive patients with magnetic resonance imaging–confirmed acute ischemic stroke admitted to a university hospital between October 2020 and January 2023 were prospectively registered. We categorized patients as AFDAS, no AF or KAF upon hospital discharge. We manually segmented diffusion‐weighted imaging lesions to determine lesion volume. We analyzed 1420 patients (median age, 78; 47.2% women, median National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, 3; median hospital stay, 5 days). Of these, 81 had AFDAS (5.7%), 329 had KAF (23.2%) and 1010 had no AF (71.1%). Lesion volume was larger in patients with AFDAS (median, 5.4 mL [interquartile range, 1.0–21.6]) compared with patients with no AF and KAF (median, 0.7 [interquartile range,0.2–4.4] and 2.0 [interquartile range,0.3–11.1] mL, respectively; both P<0.001). Lesion volume was independently associated with AFDAS compared with no AF (adjusted odds ratio, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.20–1.58] per log mL) and KAF (adjusted odds ratio, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.07–1.41] per log mL). Patients in the highest lesion volume quartile (>6.5 mL) were more likely to be diagnosed with AFDAS compared with the lowest quartile (<0.22 mL, 13.6% versus 2.1%; adjusted odds ratio, 5.88 [95% CI, 2.30–17.40]). These associations were more pronounced when excluding 151 patients with nonembolic lesion pattern and similar when excluding 199 patients with KAF on oral anticoagulation.ConclusionsLarger stroke lesions were independently associated with AFDAS diagnosis during index stroke hospitalization highlighting a potential neurogenic contribution to AFDAS pathogenesis.Keywords: AFDAS, ischemic stroke, neurocardiology, stroke lesion volumeSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke, Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is an important cause of ischemic stroke worldwide. 1 , 2 Around 20% of patients with stroke have known AF (KAF) at the time of the index stroke. 3 In ≈25% of patients, AF becomes apparent only after stroke (AF detected after stroke [AFDAS]). 3 Recently, it has been discussed that AFDAS may represent a distinct disease entity with cardiogenic and neurogenic factors contributing to its pathophysiology. 4 Traditionally, AF is thought of as a symptom of atrial cardiopathy with contributing cardiogenic risk factors like increased atrial diameter, atrial fibrosis, heart failure, and, in some cases, arrhythmogenic myocardial scarring due to coronary artery disease. 5 More recently, neurogenic risk factors for AF have gained more attention, especially in patients with AFDAS. 4 , 6 It has been suggested that stroke itself may act as a neurogenic trigger for arrhythmogenesis even in an otherwise healthy heart but particularly in patients with known heart disease (so‐called stroke‐heart syndrome). 4 , 7

While cardiogenic risk factors for AFDAS are well characterized, 8 , 9 knowledge of neurogenic risk factors remains limited. Stroke lesions in the right insular cortex, a brain region known to modulate cardiac function, were associated with an increased detection of AFDAS. 10 , 11 Beyond lesion site, higher stroke severity on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Score (NIHSS) scale has repeatedly been shown to be associated with AFDAS. 12 Data on stroke lesion volume in patients with and without AFDAS, however, are scarce. Stroke lesion volume is a relatively straightforward and highly interpretable metric that can act as a proxy for stroke severity and may help to avoid common pitfalls like the infratentorial “blind spot” of the NIHSS.

In this study, we aimed to determine the association between stroke lesion volume and detection rate of AFDAS. Building upon the potential neurogenic pathophysiology of AFDAS, we hypothesized that patients with AFDAS have larger lesion volumes than patients without AFDAS and patients with KAF.

Methods

Transparency and Openness Promotion

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Cohort Selection and Inclusion Criteria

To derive our patient sample, we used the screening log of the recently completed CORONA‐IS (Cardiomyocyte Injury Following Acute Ischemic Stroke; NCT03892226) trial. 13 All patients admitted to a comprehensive stroke unit at a university hospital in Berlin, Germany, between October 2020 and January 2023 were included. We included all patients with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐confirmed ischemic stroke. We excluded patients with intracranial bleeding, nonstroke diagnoses, missing AF status, and low image quality on diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI). Ethics approval was granted by the Ethics Committee at the Charité Universitaetsmedizin Berlin (EA4/239/23). In Berlin, the State Hospital Act (Landeskrankenhausgesetz) allows for the retrospective evaluation of clinical routine data. All patients or their legal guardians sign the consent form upon admission to the hospital. In compliance with the State Hospital Act, it is not necessary to specify in advance how the data will be used.

Variable Definition

We defined variables of interest on the basis of available information in the patient medical charts and the hospital information technology system. No‐AF was defined as no evidence of AF either before or after index stroke. AFDAS was defined as newly detected AF nonevident in the admission ECG but diagnosed during poststroke short‐term cardiac monitoring (stroke‐unit monitoring and in‐hospital Holter monitoring). We lacked data on heart rhythm from the posthospital outpatient period. While the discussion on how to categorize heart rhythm in patients with stroke with previously unknown AF detected on 12‐lead admission ECG (sometimes termed ECG‐detected AFDAS) is still ongoing, we chose to include them in the KAF cohort according to recent literature and recommendations. 14 , 15 Thus, KAF was defined as AF known before index stroke or newly diagnosed on 12‐lead admission ECG. Atrial flutter detected after stroke was also defined as AFDAS. The categorization of AF status, determination of NIHSS and modified Rankin Scale scores, intravenous thrombolysis, endovascular treatment, cancer status, smoking status, and prior use of medications (oral anticoagulation [OAC] and statins) was carried out by screening patient discharge letters. MRI data were either extracted from existing clinical records (like Wahlund Score for white matter hyperintensities) 16 or, in the case of stroke pattern and lesion type (embolic versus lacunar), reassessed by a clinician with extensive stroke imaging experience (M.G.K.). Single‐lesion pattern was defined as a singular lesion. Scattered‐lesion pattern was defined as nonsingular DWI lesion in either right anterior, left anterior, or posterior circulation. Multiple‐territory pattern was defined as either bilateral anterior or anterior/posterior circulation DWI lesions. 17 We accounted for the presence of a fetal posterior cerebral artery. We defined lacunar lesions as singular, supratentorial, subcortical, or deep white matter DWI lesions <1.5 cm, following the international consensus definition. 18 We defined all other lesions as embolic. Information on ipsilateral internal carotid artery stenosis in patients with anterior circulation stroke was extracted from the quality control registry of the Association of German‐Speaking Stroke Registries (Arbeitsgemeinschaft Deutschsprachiger Schlaganfall‐Register), but no information on posterior or intracranial stenoses was available. Cardiovascular risk factors (arterial hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, history of myocardial infarction) were based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes in patient charts, which include known and newly diagnosed conditions. ECG monitoring time was extracted from stroke unit monitors. When unavailable for technical reasons, we approximated the variable using the hospital information technology system (89 cases). If additional Holter monitoring during in‐hospital stay was available, it was added to the overall length of ECG monitoring. Recorded laboratory parameters included high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs‐cTnT; fifth‐generation Roche ElecSys Assay, 99th percentile upper reference limit 14 ng/L), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and glomerular filtration rate according to the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula. If multiple measurements were available, we chose the earliest recorded value. To determine dynamic changes using serial hs‐cTnT measurements, we ensured that the interval between the first and second measurement did not exceed 24 hours. Acute myocardial injury was defined as dynamic hs‐cTnT change ≥20% increase or decrease between measurements with at least 1 value above the upper reference limit. 19 AFDAS prediction scores like CHA2D2S‐VASc 20 ; Age, Stroke Severity, NIHSS >5 12 ; and Cognitive Impairment, Hypertension, Age, Stroke, Enlarged LA, Reduced EF, and Prior Major Adverse Cardiovascular Event 21 were calculated from the variables obtained.

Stroke Lesion Segmentation With MRIcron

Stroke lesion volume was determined via DWI lesions on brain MRI. Stroke lesion segmentation was done semi‐automatically in MRIcron (Cocoa, version 1.0.20201102) 22 by investigators (M.G.K., S.L., L.R., and O.R.) who were blinded to the outcome. DWI Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine source images were converted into Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative files. B1000 images were chosen for lesion segmentation. After adjusting the contrast by roughly doubling the window, stroke lesion delineation was done slice by slice including every small section in scattered stroke lesions. Areas with hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic lesions were included in the lesion volume only if they were surrounded by infarcted tissue; ectopic hemorrhagic areas were not included into the lesion volume. Stroke lesion volume was then calculated in milliliters by MRIcron. Lesion segmentation was supervised by experienced stroke MRI specialists (K.V., R.G.). Interrater reliability was assessed among all 4 raters. The intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the consistency of ratings across raters. The obtained intraclass correlation coefficient value was 0.96 (95% CI, 0.94–0.99), indicating a high level of agreement among raters.

Statistics and Regression Modeling

Numeric variables were summarized as either means with SDs or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were summarized as percentages. We created 2 separate multivariable logistic regression models to test the association between stroke lesion volume (independent variable) and AFDAS status at discharge (dependent variable). Stroke lesion volume was entered into the model either as a log‐transformed continuous variable or as quartiles, with the lowest quartile set as the reference group. We compared AFDAS with no AF and AFDAS with KAF. Based on existing literature, we adjusted for age, 23 sex, 24 hypertension, 11 NIHSS, 11 log hs‐cTnT, 25 leukocyte count, 26 embolic lesion pattern, 27 and duration of cardiac monitoring. 3 In models comparing quartiles, we compared patients in the highest lesion volume quartile with all other quartiles, defining the lowest quartile as reference. In addition to these models, we also fitted a multinomial regression model with AFDAS development as reference. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with 95% CI. Statistical modeling was performed in R Studio 23.06.2 (“Mountain Hydrangea”) using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Graphs were generated in R Studio using the R package “ggplot2” (version 3.4.2).

Sensitivity Analysis

We performed 3 sensitivity analyses. First, we included only patients with an embolic lesion pattern, excluding all patients with lacunar stroke. 27 In a second sensitivity analysis, we restricted the KAF definition by excluding patients with KAF already on OAC before the index event. 28 This accounts for recent literature suggesting an association between OAC and stroke lesion volume 28 and stroke recurrence in patients with AF. 29 Finally, we also conducted an analysis excluding all patients with <48 hours of cardiac monitoring accounting for the fact that patients with small stroke lesions may have been discharged early, thus escaping in‐hospital AFDAS detection.

Results

Initial screening retrieved 2688 patients admitted to the stroke unit during the study period. We sequentially excluded 1268 patients due to final diagnosis other than ischemic stroke (n=919), unavailability of MRI imaging (n=329), insufficient imaging quality (n=9), and double or incomplete records (n=8 and n=3, respectively; Figure 1). A total of 1420 patients were included in the final analysis. Median age was 78 years (IQR, 68–83), 47.2% were women, median NIHSS upon admission was 3 (1–6). Median stroke lesion volume was 0.9 mL (IQR, 0.2–6.5; Table). After a median hospitalization of 5 days (IQR, 3–10.2), no AF was detected in 1010 patients (71.1%), and KAF was present in 329 (23.2%). The latter included 56 patients (3.9% of total cohort) with AF newly diagnosed on 12‐lead admission ECG. AFDAS was detected in 81 patients (5.7%; including 5 patients with atrial flutter).

In univariate comparison, patients with AFDAS had a significantly higher median NIHSS score compared with patients with no AF (Table). The proportion of prior OAC intake was higher in the KAF subgroup (60.5%) compared with no AF (2.9%) and AFDAS (0%). A total of 130 patients with KAF did not take OAC before the index stroke. Of all 3 groups, patients with AFDAS more frequently received intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular treatment, more often displayed dynamic hs‐cTnT changes, had the longest hospital stay, and the longest duration of poststroke cardiac monitoring including Holter ECG (Table). Regarding cardiovascular risk factors, patients with AFDAS showed similar proportions of arterial hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and prestroke statin use compared with patients with no AF. In patients with AFDAS, stroke lesion pattern was more frequently scattered (43.2%) and of embolic origin (93.8%) compared with patients with no AF (32.0% and 85.4%, respectively; both P<0.05). Nonembolic single lesions of presumed lacunar origin were more frequent in patients with no AF (14.6%) compared with patients with AFDAS (6.2%) and KAF (6.7%, both P<0.05). Stroke lesions in multiple vascular territories were least common in patients with no AF (30.2% versus 37.1% and 39.8% in AFDAS and KAF, respectively), but these proportions were not statistically different between groups. Characteristics of patients with AF newly diagnosed on 12‐lead admission ECG and AFDAS are shown in Table S1.

Median stroke lesion volume differed among the 3 groups. Patients with AFDAS displayed a larger median lesion volume (5.4 mL [IQR, 1.0–21.6]) compared with no AF (0.7 mL [IQR, 0.2–4.4]; P<0.001) and patients with KAF (2.0 mL [IQR, 0.3–11.1]; P<0.001; Figure 2). The density plot of stroke lesion volume in patients with AFDAS exhibits a notably flatter profile and broader distribution when compared with both the KAF and no‐AF groups (Figure 2). To determine the association of stroke lesion volume with AFDAS diagnosis at discharge, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis and adjusted for factors known to increase the likelihood of AFDAS. After adjustment, stroke lesion volume was independently associated with AFDAS diagnosis at discharge (aOR, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.20–1.58] per log mL; Figure 3A, top). When creating a regression model distinguishing between AFDAS and KAF, the association was weaker with an aOR of 1.22 (95% CI, 1.07–1.41 per log mL; Figure 3A, bottom; full models, Tables S2 and S3).

Next, we stratified patients into quartiles according to lesion volume (quartile 1, 0.01–0.22 mL; quartile 2, 0.23–0.94 mL; quartile 3, 0.95–6.46 mL; quartile 4, 6.50–321 mL). Compared with the lowest lesion volume quartile, patients in the highest quartile were more likely to be diagnosed with AFDAS (aOR, 5.88 [95% CI, 2.30–17.40] versus no AF; and aOR, 3.92 [95% CI, 1.53–11.53] versus KAF; Figure 3A top and bottom, respectively). We also created a multinomial regression model setting AFDAS development as the reference (Table S4).

In a first sensitivity analysis, we sought to investigate the association of stroke lesion volume and AFDAS in patients with embolic stroke (see Methods). After excluding all nonembolic strokes (n=308), the aOR comparing highest versus lowest volume quartile was similar to the main analysis (aOR, 6.24 [95% CI, 2.24–22.21]; Figure 3B, top).

In a second sensitivity analysis, we included only patients with KAF without OAC intake at the time of the index stroke (n=130; 199 with preexisting OAC excluded).The association between stroke lesion volume and AFDAS weakened slightly compared with the main regression model (aOR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.00–1.35], per log mL); the aOR comparing highest versus lowest volume quartile was similar to the main analysis (aOR, 3.28 [95% CI, 1.13–10.74]; Figure 3B, middle).

Finally, we tested the association by excluding patients with <48 hours of cardiac monitoring. The association between the log lesion volume and AFDAS diagnosis at discharge was similar to the main analysis when modeled against patients with no AF (aOR, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.16–1.62]; Figure 3B, bottom).

Discussion

In our analysis, we observed 4 major findings. First, larger stroke lesion volume was independently associated with AFDAS diagnosis during the index stroke hospitalization compared with no AF. AFDAS diagnosis at discharge was >5 times more likely in patients in the highest lesion volume quartile. Second, this association remained in sensitivity analyses excluding patients with nonembolic lesion type and short ECG monitoring duration. Third, patients with AFDAS had larger stroke lesions than patients with KAF. Fourth, this association remained after correcting for intake of OAC before the index stroke.

AFDAS is thought to be influenced by cardiogenic and neurogenic risk factors, but so far neurogenic risk factors have remained largely unexplored, and knowledge about stroke lesion size in patients with AFDAS is limited. 30 , 31 Currently, AFDAS is considered a separate entity from KAF with a lower prevalence of vascular risk factors, cardiovascular comorbidities, and structural heart disease. 4 In line with this, our study confirmed that patients with AFDAS have a lower burden of established cardiovascular risk factors compared with KAF, a trait they share with patients without KAF. Our study provides evidence that larger stroke lesion volume is associated with a higher risk of AFDAS. Overall, patients with AFDAS displayed the highest stroke lesion volumes, with a median >5 times higher than patients with no AF and more than double compared with patients with KAF. The overall stroke lesion volume was comparatively small in our cohort (0.9 mL), which possibly mirrors the fact that the vast majority of stroke imaging was done in a dedicated 3T stroke MRI with high sensitivity for small lesions as well as the fact that MRI (and not computed tomography) is the default imaging modality at our stroke unit. This included patients without persisting clinical deficits that would have otherwise been diagnosed as transient ischemic attack. This likely led to an overrepresentation of small strokes in our cohort. Nevertheless, our findings are consistent with previously published data from our department. 32 The small mean lesion volume was not due to a high proportion of lacunar strokes and‐small vessel disease in the no‐AF group (strokes with lacunar morphology were only 14% in patients with no AF), and the burden of white matter hyperintensities as assessed by the Wahlund Score was even slightly higher in patients with AFDAS. Crucially, the association between lesion volume and AFDAS remained after excluding all nonembolic strokes in the subgroup analysis (Figure 3B, top). In our analysis, we lacked detailed information on the stroke pathogenesis. Nevertheless, 87.8% of lesions were classified as embolic and only 12.2% as lacunar.

The difference in lesion volume between AFDAS and KAF supports the hypothesis that they are indeed separate entities and that a neurogenic AFDAS trigger seems possible. Importantly, this difference remained evident when including only patients with KAF without prior OAC, since prior effective OAC has been linked to smaller stroke lesion volumes and lower stroke severity. 28 , 33 OAC might be an important confounder in this discussion. It has been argued that the reported differences in stroke recurrence between patients with AFDAS and patients with KAF can largely be explained by the difference in OAC before the index stroke. 34 This calls into question whether KAF and AFDAS are clinically relevant and distinct prognostic entities. In opposition to this, our own subgroup analysis showed that stroke lesion volume is still strongly associated with AFDAS, even after the exclusion of all patients with KAF with prior OAC intake (see Figure 3B, middle). But the moment of detection might not be the best way of differentiating between AF types. Some suggested a different categorization of AF in patients with stroke, which can be based on AF burden or the temporal relationship between the first AF paroxysm and index stroke. 14 , 15 While our data do not provide any insight into stroke recurrence, our findings add to the ongoing discussion; for example, we can confirm differences in baseline characteristics of KAF and AFDAS. Our study also adds that stroke lesion volumes might be different between both entities. The comparatively low cardiac risk profile of patients with AFDAS has led to the hypothesis that the index stroke after which AFDAS is diagnosed may play a causal role by delivering a potent neurogenic trigger for arrhythmogenesis as part of a stroke‐heart syndrome. 35 One possible explanation could be that larger stroke lesions are more likely to affect areas of the central autonomic network such as the insular and cingulate cortex. Our finding that higher stroke lesion volume is associated with AFDAS provides some support for this hypothesis. So far, only stroke in the insular cortex could be linked to AFDAS development. 11 , 31 In our analysis, we lacked information on lesion site and involvement of the central autonomic network.

We can confirm previously published data that AF is newly diagnosed after stroke in ≈10% of patients during a hospital stay. 3 , 11 Of these, 40% were detected in 12‐lead admission ECG and 60% in cardiac monitoring and Holter ECGs. Notably, lesion size differed between these groups, with the former showing smaller lesion volumes than the latter (Table S1). Until recently, the definition of AFDAS included AF newly diagnosed on 12‐lead admission ECG (termed ECG‐detected AFDAS). There is, however, a growing consensus that the term AFDAS should be reserved for AF diagnosed during prolonged cardiac monitoring (PCM) after stroke, previously termed PCM‐detected AFDAS. 15 This is because PCM‐detected AFDAS might have different characteristics than ECG‐detected AFDAS. In support of this, Alvarado‐Bolaños et al found that patients with admission ECG‐detected AFDAS have a 5‐fold risk of recurrent ischemic stroke in a median follow‐up of 17 months compared with PCM‐detected AFDAS. 36 According to the authors, this may be explained by the overall higher age, higher prevalence of left atrial enlargement, and lower left ventricular ejection fraction of patients with ECG‐detected AFDAS compared with PCM‐detected AFDAS. Therefore, it is possible that patients with ECG‐detected AFDAS have subclinical AF at the time of index stroke (for characteristics of ECG‐detected AFDAS versus KAF versus PCM‐detected AFDAS, see Table S1). For these reasons, we decided to include ECG‐detected AFDAS in the KAF definition, although this choice may be controversial. Our observed difference in lesion volume between these 2 groups (4.5 mL for ECG‐detected AFDAS and 1.5 mL for patients with KAF) may be partially explained by OAC intake. Typically, patients with ECG‐detected AFDAS do not take OAC because in these cases AF is unrecognized by definition until admission.

Some AFDAS literature deals with the question of how to select patients for prolonged cardiac monitoring that might unveil AFDAS. This led to the development of numerous AFDAS risk scores like the Age, Stroke Severity, NIHSS >5 score, which accounts for stroke severity through incorporating the NIHSS. 12 While this score already performed well, we hypothesize that it could be improved by using stroke lesion volume instead. 37 Lesion volume is also an easily interpretable metric in a clinical setting. Our data show that patients with stroke with a lesion volume >6.5 mL have a >5‐fold increased risk of AFDAS, which could prompt the clinician to initiate prolonged cardiac monitoring. This is of clinical relevance for 2 reasons. First, it is still unknown how to best select at‐risk patients for PCM and considering stroke lesion volume might aid this selection. Second, current guidelines recommend OAC as secondary prevention for both KAF and AFDAS, even if AFDAS might carry a lower stroke recurrence risk than KAF. 38 To date, there are no clinical trial data suggesting that the long‐term efficacy of OAC for stroke prevention is attenuated in AFDAS detected on short‐term in‐hospital ECG monitoring compared with AF already known before stroke.

Several limitations of our study should be noted. First, we used retrospective data, which limit data availability regarding cardiogenic risk factors of AF like NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) or left atrial volume. We also lack data on specific stroke lesion site or AF burden. Second, this analysis was monocentric with European ethnicity. Third, AF status was determined at discharge with a median of 5 days after stroke. A proportion of patients without AF at discharge (≈15% within 6 months of the index stroke 3 ) may have still gone on to develop AFDAS at a later stage. Given the increasing time gap to the index stroke, these patients may represent a cohort with remote, low‐burden AF (also termed bystander AF) rather than neurogenic AF. 14 However, this hypothesis needs to be confirmed. Fourth, we included only patients with high‐quality MRI, which may lead to inclusion of smaller lesion volumes. Fifth, stroke lesion volume varies in the first days after stroke. 39 In our analysis, we could not account for this, since MRI time stamps were not systematically collected. Finally, current literature and guidelines agree that longer cardiac monitoring increases the likelihood of AF detection. 9 , 38 While we adjusted for duration of monitoring in our models, it should be noted that patients with small stroke lesions may have been discharged early, with PCM (and therefore possible AFDAS detection) occurring only in an outpatient setting.

In conclusion, our data show a strong association between stroke lesion volume and diagnosis of AFDAS. This finding adds to the still limited but continually growing number of neurogenic triggers for AFDAS and warrants a deeper exploration of stroke characteristics in patients with AFDAS. Additionally, our analysis has possible implications for selecting patients for prolonged cardiac monitoring.
