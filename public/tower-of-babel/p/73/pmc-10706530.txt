Prevalence and social determinants associated with oral cancer in Al-Baha Region of Saudi Arabia: A seven-year retrospective cohort hospital-based study
Authors: Abdullah Ali H. Alzahrani
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The region of Al-Baha area of interest is associated with risk factors; yet no research in the literature has explored oral cancer (OC) and its associated social determinants and risk factors in this region. The aim of this study was to access the prevalence of OC in the Al-Baha region and its association with the social determinants and risk factors in this population.Methods:A retrospective cohort hospital-based study was conducted in Al-Baha Region between January 2012 and December 2019. Data collected from all the patients diagnosed with OC were extracted. OC was assessed based on the hospital records, histopathological data, and Tumor, Nodes, Metastasis (TNM) staging aided in cancer assessment. Detailed demographic data associated with OC was collected using semistructured telephonic interviews. Risk assessment was reviewed based on population, exposure of interest, and outcome.Results:A total of 19°C patients out of 421 cancer patients were diagnosed. The mean age for OC in males was 59.72 ± 15.83 years and 69.14 ± 5.86 years in females. Oral squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue was the most with one case of metastasis. The average estimate per day smoking, E- cigarettes, water pipe smoking was 14.55 ± 7.15, 11.15 ± 3.55, and 0.52 ± 4.7, respectively, with males’ habits more than females. These contributes to the risk factors associated with socioeconomic factors.Conclusions:Al Baha region experiences different socioeconomic determinants with a lower prevalence of OC. The risk of OC is associated with various adverse oral habits, which can be prevented.

Introduction

Al-Baha region is situated in the southwest region of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The social determinants with familial dynamics associated with adverse habits in this region with oral cancer is the concern in primary prevention. The neoplasm which involves the oral cavity starts from the lip to the anterior pillar of the fauces known as oral cancer (OC).[1] OC is one of the ten most common cancers and is associated with delayed or improper detection with poor prognosis.[2] OC refers to malignancies in the mucosal lip, tongue, and oral cavity associated with alcohol consumption, exposure to tobacco, or both, and rarely by traumatic irritation of chronic nature and exposure to human papillomavirus.[3] Globally, OC is reported to be the tenth most diagnosed cancer, with an annual incidence of >300,000 cases. Incidence and mortality of OC are higher in developing countries in comparison with developed countries due to the combined effect of the aging of populations and the high levels of prevalence of cancer risk factors. As per the International Agency for Research on Cancer, around 18.1 million new cancer cases are diagnosed and 9.5 million people died from cancer worldwide.[4]

The risk factors associated with OC are smoking, alcohol, chewable tobacco (Tambaku), lifestyle, and social determinants. The social and lifestyle of the Saudi population was observed; in an adult population with 12.2% of current smokers, most of those smokers (74.1%) smoked 15 cigarettes daily. There is an increase in smoking from 12.2% in 2013 to 21.4% in 2018.[5] The use of waterpipe smoking (Shisha) is also a common practice seen in Arab countries. The harmful effect of charcoal, tobacco, and associated flavoring agents contributes to the carcinogenic effect.[6] The alternative to smoking that many Saudi youths developed was e-cigarettes. The myth of e-cigarettes being safe is hazardous to the tissues and carcinogenic.

The prevalence of OC in Saudi Arabia is in the rise from 1994 to 2015 along with an increase in the population. There is regional variation with more OC in Jazan, compared to the northern region. Studies on OC and its determinants associated with socio demography and risk factors in the Al Baha region are scanty. No study in the literature has explored OC and its associated social determinants and risk factors in Al-Baha region. It was thought that it might hence be important to explore and understand the prevalence, social determinants and risks associated with OC in this region. The aim of this study was to access the prevalence of OC in the Al-Baha region and its association with social determinants and risk factors in this population and to suggest appropriate preventive measures which can be initiated by the health care workers in primary care.

Materials and Methods

Study design and settings

The present study was based on the STROBE checklist[7] and designed to be conducted in three key stages. The first stage, a retrospective cohort hospital-based study, was conducted between January 2012 and December 2019 in Al-Baha Region. Participants were all the patients diagnosed with cancer, and cases related to OC were identified and then extracted. The main outcome measure was OC, which was assessed based on the diagnosis of hospital records, patients’ sociodemographic data, further histopathological data, and the TNM staging aided in cancer assessment which helped in outcome measurement. Moreover, information regarding oral cancer was obtained based on site, type of biopsy, and type of diagnosis.

In the second stage, a telephonic semistructured interview was conducted in study population. The semistructured interview comprised of details of education based on elementary school, bachelor’s degree, and higher education (masters, PhD or fellowship). The economic section of the interview comprised of monthly income and was categorized with low income (3000–6000 riyals), moderate low income (6001–10,000 riyals), moderate high income (10,001 to 20,000 riyals), high-income group (20,000 riyals above). Details of the lifestyle-associated adverse habits were collected, which includes average smoking per day, average e-cigarette per day, average waterpipe smoking (Shisha). Diet history was noted based on common food consumed by Saudi population, which includes consumption of meat, poultry, fish, and beverages.

The third and last stage of this study was a OC risk assessment review of the Saudi population which is associated with a discussion to find the probable evidence for risk assessment with various literature. Risk assessment was reviewed based on population, exposure of interest, and outcome. The descriptive part of determinants and distribution of oral cancer was accessed using the risk assessment with a review of oral cancer in the Saudi population. The risk assessment was done using the JBI samurai software.

Data collection, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The source of data was from several hospitals run by the Saudi Ministry of Health. The participants were Saudi nationals of the Al-Baha region. The average age (years), gender’s distribution, period of diagnosis, tumor type, type and site of biopsy, presence of malignancy, and diagnosis based on histopathology were considered in data extraction. Inclusion criteria for this study were all Saudi patients with OC of both genders with no restrictions on age, socioeconomic status, educational levels, and dietary habits. Patients who were non-Saudi or not living permanently in the Al-Baha region were excluded from the study sample.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval of this study was obtained from the Scientific Research Committee, Education, Training Center, and Academic Affairs, at the Saudi Ministry of Health, Al -Baha branch, Saudi Arabia (approval number: 20171105).

Variables, bias, qualitative, and statistical analysis

The study data was based on the hospital records, and the outcome of OC was associated with risk factors. The selection bias was reduced with efforts with inclusion and exclusion criteria and peer review of the selected data with other investigators. The observed data were analyzed statistically using the Chi-square test with a test significance of P value less than 0.05. Qualitative findings of the semistructured interviews were collected and categorized based on the theme analysis of socioeconomic factors, lifestyle practices based on adverse habits, dietary choices.

Results

The OC patients with mean age 62.63 ± 14.24 years, with minimum age 36 years up to 83 years. The average age for males and female with OC was 59.72 ± 15.83 and 69.14 ± 5.86 years, respectively. The age distribution of OC (n = 5, 26.31%) was in the age interval 70 to 79 years. An equally high number of cases was seen in the age group of 60–69 in OC (n = 5, 26.31%). Social determinants and risk factors from the patients’ interviews

A telephonic interview with a semistructured questionnaire gave a detailed socioeconomic information from the OC patients. Men were more affected (63.15%) than women (36.85%), but based on education level more women had passed elementary school (71.5%) in compared to men. The bachelor’s degree was honored in men (25%) more than women (14.2%). Men had obtained higher education levels (master, PhD, fellowship) more than women (17% and 14.2%), respectively. The economic factor comprised of monthly income showed that a 26.3% of the study participants had low-income level (3000–6000 Saudi Riyals), while a 42.10% of the participants had a moderate low income (6001–10,000 Saudi Riyals). Moreover, 15.8% of the participants for each of the moderate high-income level (10,001–20,000 Saudi Riyals) and high-income level (20,000 Saudi Riyals) were reported. Details of lifestyle-associated adverse habits were collected. The average smoking per day was 17.62 ± 7.85 in males and 11.48 ± 6.46 in women, while the average e-cigarette consumed per day in men was 12.65 ± 3.65 and 9.65 ± 3.45 in women. The consumption of waterpipe smoking (Shisha) per day average was 0.66 ± 5.65 and 0.38 ± 3.75 in males and females, respectively. Diet history revealed a typical Saudi culture of food with meat (89.5%), poultry (94.7%), and fish (79%). In compared to beverages, fresh juice (100%) and coffee (79%) were consumed more. Characteristics based on cohort study

The systematic meta-analysis of risk factors associated with OC in the Saudi population has shown smoking, shisha, or water pipe smoking being the risk factors for oral cancer [Table 1]. Patil S. et al.[6] reported a cohort study in the elderly population in association with OC and water pipe smoking. They concluded that the risk of head and neck cancer was associated with waterpipe exposure. While Qattan AMN et al.[5] revealed that gender, region, and marital status were socio-determinants associated with smoking. They found more smoking habits in the northern border in compared to other geographic location of Saudi Arabia with a prevalence of more smoking in unmarried. Moreover, Ahmad P. et al. 2021[13] indicated the cohort with the survival rate of patients with metastasis. Rodríguez-Molinero et al.[14], in their opinion, text has reported the influence of diet in the prevention of OC [Table 5].

Discussion

The prevalence of OC in the Al Baha region is less in compared to the global prevalence of 263,000 new cases, with mortality of 127,000 people.[15] Oral squamous cell carcinoma is on the rise, and it is an ulcer-proliferative lesion of the oral mucosa that affects the lips to the oropharynx cancer. Cancer therapy is associated with morbidities that hurt the quality of life, which extends throughout life. The male predilection for OC in the present study is in accordance with the data available worldwide. Hesham A et al. (2017) have shown similar male predilection in a 10-year retrospective analysis of oral cancer in the southern part of Saudi Arabia.[16] Alhazzazi (2017) and Alzahrani (2020) have also reported a male predilection for head and neck cancer in Saudi Arabia.[17,18] However, Saleh et al. (2017)[19] and Alshehri (2020) have reported a contrasting female predilection for oral cancer.[4] The male predominance may be attributed to their excessive exposure to the risk factors such as smoking and its associated oral unhygienic conditions and periodontal diseases.

The most common age of occurrence of OC in our study was in the sixth and seventh decades of life, with the average age being 62.3 years. Likewise, Alshehri (2020), Alharbi (2017), and Basha et al. (2019) have reported similar results.[4,19,20] AlZahrani (2020) has also reported a similar age distribution in head and neck cancer in Saudi Arabia.[18] As with many other types of cancer, the present study found a correlation between the occurrence of OC and age, with 52.6% of cases diagnosed during the age of 60–80 years. In the United States of America, the average age at diagnosis of OC is 62 years, and two-thirds of individuals with this disease are over the age of 55 years.[21] Ageing is accompanied by increased susceptibility to cancer-causing genetic maturations due to accumulated exposures to environmental and behavioral risk factors. We also found that squamous cell carcinoma was the most frequent histopathological type, in line with Alharbi (2017) and Alzahrani (2020) studies.[18,22] Most of the literature reports that over 90% of head and neck cancers are squamous cell carcinoma.[18]

The most common site of OC in our study was the tongue, followed by the hard palate and the lower lip. Al-Jaber et al. (2016) and Basha et al. (2019) have reported similar results in oral cancer trends noted in Arab countries and Saudi Arabia respectively.[20,23] Alharbi (2017) has also reported that the tongue was the commonest site of oral cancer, followed by the buccal area and floor of the mouth.[19] The present study results are also in accordance with the worldwide data.[24] One of the most important independent risk factors for the development of tongue cancer is heavy smoking, and cigarette smoke also contains known carcinogens, mainly nitrosamines, and polycyclic hydrocarbons.[24,25]

The present study showed that the maximum number of OC cases diagnosed were Stage 1 cancers. Similar results have been reported by AlZahrai (2020)[18] However, contrasting results have been revealed by Basha et al. (2019), Al-Jaber et al. (2016), and Alharbi (2017), who found most of the patients reported in late stages (Stages 3 and 4).[6,22,23] A possible reason for the early presentation of patients in our study may be due to the early appearance of symptoms such as oral mass and hoarseness of voice. However, the authors who have reported contrasting results believe that the presentation of patients in late stages could be attributed to the weakness of the educational and prevention program applied by the general physician in the peripheral hospitals. Another reason, unfortunately, might be that many of the patients may not have bothered to follow up for their further check-ups and treatment, leading to the progression of cancer into the advanced stages. The lifestyle of the Saudi population is associated with social gatherings, meat rich food, coffee, Saudi coffee, some other risk factors for OC like smoking, and consumption of tambak, and shisha (water pipe smoking). The present study reported an average smoking per day 14.55 ± 7.15, which was less in comparison to 18.02 ± 8 as per the study conducted by Qattan, A.M.N et al. The consumption of E cigarette by the study subjects per day was 11.15 ± 3.55, which was lesser in comparison to 15–16 puffs in a study conducted by Yingst J. et al.[26] The water pipe smoking per day average in the present study was 0.52 ± 0.3, which was lesser than with subjects smoked 140–260 shisha in a year.[6]

The Saudi culture is a good social gathering with diet-rich food; the present study reported meat (89.5%), poultry (94.7%), and fish (79%); this is in agreement with Nicola. E et al.,[27] who support the majority of Saudi population following local culture of food. Majority of the study subjects consumed fresh Juice (100%) and coffee (79%). The incidence of oral cancer in the present study was less in comparison to the global percentage; the reason may be due to increased consumption of coffee as reported by Miranda J et al.[11] in which they explain that coffee plays a role in protection against oral and pharyngeal cancer. Rodríguez-Molinero J. et al.[28] in their updated research paper reported the benefits of diet-rich micronutrients, such as omega 3 acids, polyphenols, and vitamins, which are found in fish and animal products which play the role of antiinflammatory, synergistic antioxidant and antiproliferative properties, and Saudi diet is rich in vegetables, fruits, fish, and animal products, which may play a role in reduced OC.

The studies associated with diet and OC are scanty, but few studies provide support the association between diet and pharyngeal cancer.[28] Almutairi et al.[29] reported the role of the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase rs1801131 variant in Saudis and the genotype role in fundamental protective effects against the diseases related to cigarette smoking. The limitations of this study were, firstly, the recall bias which was a drawback during the telephonic interview along with lack of few information regarding stress and marital status. Second limitation was the fact that the sample of OC exposure in the present study was low, which could be one of the barriers to generalizing findings. However, this study provides insight into the prevalence and incidence of OC besides exploring the social determinants such as age, gender, level of education, monthly income, and lifestyle associated with OC among the study population. The social determinants of OC are associated with family health, and this can be prevented by appropriate changes in the lifestyle which can be initiated by the health care workers when the patients come for a general health checkup.

Conclusions

The present study revealed a total 19°C with the majority of cases with squamous cell carcinoma followed by basal cell carcinoma. The most affected site was the tongue followed by the lip and cheeks. The least affected were the alveolar region and parotid gland with more cases in the T1 stage with one case metastasis. The social determinants assessment with OC showed the use of adverse habits, which includes smoking tobacco, E-cigarette, and water pipe smoking. The occurrence of OC was lesser in comparison to global population, with the reason being the diet and coffee consumption. The study gives an implication of reduction in morbidity and its association with mortality. Public health point of view, individual health, and family health can be improved with the prevention and control of diseases. Education and lifestyle changes are the key factors which can be initiated by intersectorial coordination with dentists, physicians, and other health workers. Further research with larger sample and a new scope or vision with bundle of studies to find the evidence and prevention of OC particularly in Al-Baha Region and among the Saudi population in general may be recommended.
