Association of niacin intake and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: findings from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Jing Zhou, Jun Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aims to explore the relationship between niacin intake and the prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) within a large, multi-ethnic cohort.MethodsA total of 2946 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were carefully selected based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants meeting the eligibility criteria underwent two dietary recall interviews, and niacin intake was calculated using the USDA’s Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS). Liver steatosis was diagnosed using a Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) of 248 dB/m, and MASLD diagnosis was based on metabolic indicators. Weighted multivariate logistic regression was utilized to analyze the correlation between niacin intake and MASLD prevalence, with potential nonlinear relationships explored through restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression.ResultsAnalysis of baseline data revealed that MASLD patients had lower niacin intake levels and poorer metabolic biomarker profiles. Both RCS analysis and multivariate logistic regression indicated a U-shaped association between niacin intake and MASLD prevalence. Specifically, there was a non-linear dose-response relationship, with the odds of MASLD gradually decreasing with increasing niacin intake until reaching a threshold of 23.6 mg, beyond which the odds of MASLD began to increase.ConclusionThis study confirms a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between niacin intake and MASLD prevalence within the diverse American population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20161-0.Keywords: MASLD, Niacin, NHANES, Cross-sectional study, RCS

Introduction

Niacin, a water-soluble vitamin, also known as Vitamin B3 or nicotinic acid [1]. Niacin functions in maintaining normal physiological functions, primarily obtained through dietary intake [2]. Foods rich in niacin include meats (especially red meat), poultry, fish, dairy products, nuts, whole grains, and legumes [3]. Maintaining an adequate intake of niacin is paramount for human health. Niacin plays a crucial role in the functioning of the immune system, participating in the production and functionality of white blood cells, aiding in resistance against infections, and maintaining the proper regulation of the immune system [4]. Furthermore, niacin is an essential component of enzymes required for DNA synthesis, crucial for maintaining the normal transmission of genetic information [5]. It also plays a key role in protein synthesis, cell division, and tissue repair [6].

In steatotic liver disease (SLD), abnormal fat accumulation is a major characteristic, highlighting the critical role of niacin in the normal regulation of fat metabolism [7, 8]. Therefore, niacin may influence the development of SLD by affecting fat metabolism. Additionally, niacin acts as an antioxidant, assisting in neutralizing free radicals and alleviating cellular oxidative stress [9]. The development of SLD is associated with oxidative stress, making the antioxidant properties of niacin positively impactful in slowing down the progression of SLD [10]. In the development of SLD, inflammation is a key factor, and research has suggested that niacin may provide protective effects against SLD by inhibiting inflammatory responses [10, 11]. However, excessive intake of niacin may impose a burden on liver function, leading to adverse effects [12]. High doses of niacin intake may even result in hepatotoxicity, manifested by abnormal liver function, including elevated liver enzymes, jaundice, and other symptoms. Therefore, attention should be paid to avoid excessive niacin intake to prevent adverse effects.

SLD is a comprehensive term covering various causes of fatty liver degeneration, of which nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is defined as a group of conditions where fat deposits accumulate in liver cells after excluding excessive alcohol consumption and other explicit liver injury factors [13–15]. This includes a spectrum from simple hepatic steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), with some patients eventually progressing to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [16]. NAFLD has become a global public health concern, and thus far, no specific therapeutic drugs have been approved for NAFLD. However, the field of hepatology is gradually recognizing that fatty liver disease not only coexists with other liver conditions but is also a multisystem disorder associated with metabolic dysfunction. The nomenclature and diagnostic criteria for NAFLD need to reflect this new understanding, avoiding the “exclusion” of diseases accompanying or comorbid with fatty liver conditions and instead “including” them.

European Liver Congress released a global expert consensus statement titled " A multi-society Delphi consensus statement on new fatty liver disease nomenclature.” Results showed that over 60% of respondents believed terms like “nonalcoholic” and “fatty” were stigmatized [17]. Subsequently, the three major hepatology journals (“Hepatology” in the USA, “Journal of Hepatology” in Europe, and “Annals of Hepatology” in Latin America) simultaneously published new naming rules: SLD was selected as an overarching term to encompass various causes of fatty degeneration [18, 19]. Cases without cardiovascular metabolic risk factors and with unclear causes of SLD are termed cryptogenic SLD. A new category called Metabolic Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (MetALD) is introduced to describe MASLD patients with significant alcohol consumption [20]. The concept of fatty liver hepatitis is retained, with NASH renamed Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH). In contrast to the former, the definition of MASH no longer requires the exclusion of concurrent liver diseases, emphasizing the significant role of cardiovascular metabolic risk factors, such as obesity and insulin resistance, in the occurrence and development of fatty liver disease.

Limited research has explored the association between the dietary intake of niacin and MASLD. We aim to examine the correlation between dietary intake of niacin and MASLD among large-scale multiracial population in the U.S.

Methods

Study population

NHANES is a nationwide survey dedicated to assessing the health status of all civilians in the United States through biennial assessments. Its primary objective is to gain a comprehensive understanding of contemporary disease patterns and offer insights for shaping public health policies. For this research, data from 9254 participants across ten consecutive NHANES cycles (2017–2018) were gathered. We clarify that we had no access to information that could identify individual participants during or after data collection. This study was approved by National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. The participants provided written informed consent to participate in the NHANES survey. Specific exclusion criteria were applied as follows: [1] participants aged below 18 (n = 3398); [2] participants without the data of intake of niacin (n = 1517); [3] individuals lacking ultrasound data (n = 259); [4] participants without the data of Alt or Ast (n = 230); [5] patients with MetALD or other combination aetiology (n = 843); [6] participants with cryptogenic SLD (n = 34); [7] participants with other specific aetiology SLD (n = 27). Following meticulous data screening, a total of 2946 participants were ultimately chosen for subsequent analyses. A comprehensive flowchart illustrating the recruitment process as Fig. 1.

Assessment of dietary intake of niacin

The dietary interview, referred to as What We Eat in America (WWEIA), is conducted for NHANES participants. All eligible NHANES participants undergo two 24-hour dietary recall interviews to disclose the types and amounts of foods they consumed in the 24 h prior to the interview (from midnight to midnight). All of the recalls were conducted in working. These interviews aim to gather information about the quantities and varieties of foods individuals ingested in the 24 h leading up to the interview, covering the time span from midnight to midnight. The first dietary recall is conducted face-to-face at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), while the second recall occurs through a telephone interview approximately 3 to 10 days after the initial recall. The FNDDS is employed to compute the nutrients and food components in all food items. The dataset containing total nutrient intakes serves as a concise record of the nutrient intake for each individual. In this study, participants’ daily niacin intake is determined by averaging their two dietary recalls.

Diagnosis of MASLD

The diagnostic flowchart for MASLD is illustrated in Fig. 2. In this study, the diagnosis of hepatic steatosis primarily relies on liver ultrasound. CAP of 248 dB/m was used as the cutoff value to diagnose liver steatosis with high sensitivity and specificity. Metabolic dysregulation is evidenced by the presence of at least two metabolic risk abnormalities. Patients with hepatic steatosis who have cardiovascular metabolic dysfunction and are not diagnosed with the following causes (1.excessive alcohol use; 2.hepatitis B/C virus infection; 3.pharmacological agents associated with steatosis (e.g.: amiodarone, methotrexate, tamoxifen, aspirin, ibuprofen, nrtis (nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors), protease inhibitors, valproic acid, carbamazepine, fluorouracil, irinotecan, glucocorticoids); 4.iron overload) leading to hepatic steatosis are classified as having metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD).

Covariates

Incorporating insights from existing literature and biological considerations, our goal was to encompass a wide array of covariates known to influence MASLD outcomes. Demographic information, including age, gender, race/ethnicity, education level, smoking history, and alcohol consumption, was collected through standardized questionnaires and face-to-face interviews. Thorough medical examinations were conducted by experienced healthcare professionals at the MEC. Ethnicity was categorized into five groups: non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, Mexican American, and other racial groups. Educational attainment was classified as below high school, high school, or above high school. Individuals who had smoked over 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, regardless of their current smoking status at the time of the interview, were considered smokers. Alcohol consumption was defined as consuming at least 12 drinks within the year preceding the survey. Body mass index (BMI), calculated by dividing weight in kilograms (kg) by height in meters squared (m²), served as the primary measure for assessing overweight and obesity: Normal Weight was defined as BMI: 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m²; Overweight was defined as BMI: 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m²; Obesity was defined as BMI: 30.0 kg/m² and above. Experienced clinicians, following standardized protocols, measured systolic/diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP). The final blood pressure value was derived from the average of three consecutive readings, taken at half-minute intervals. Laboratory tests, conducted according to standardized procedures, determined various parameters. Hypertension was defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis or measured blood pressure during physical examinations [21, 22]. Diabetes represented another significant confounder with potential implications for MASLD. Individuals with a previous physician or health professional diagnosis of diabetes were categorized as having diagnosed diabetes. Those lacking a diagnosed diabetes history but exhibiting HbA1c levels of 6.5% (47.5 mmol/mol) or higher, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher, or 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) plasma glucose levels of 200 mg/dL or higher (11.1 mmol/L), as determined by laboratory tests, were classified as having undiagnosed diabetes. Both individuals with diagnosed and undiagnosed diabetes were collectively considered as diabetic patients.

Statistical methods

Due to the intricate sampling methodologies applied in the NHANES survey, our analytical techniques incorporated sample weights tailored to specific research periods, ensuring precise calculations of health-related statistics. Continuous variables were represented by weighted means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions. To identify variations in baseline traits between participants with and without MASLD, student’s t-test and chi-square test were employed for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. For multiple testing, we apply the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to adjust the p-values for multiple comparisons and we have provided the details in the methods section. The intake of niacin was evenly stratified into quartiles, with the initial quartile (Q1) serving as the reference. To assess the association between niacin and MASLD, several multivariate logistic regression models were utilized, including an unadjusted model, and two adjusted models (Model I and Model II). Model I incorporated adjustments for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, while Model II additionally considered education levels, smoking, drinking, hypertension, and DM. The potential non-linear relationship between the intake of niacin and MASLD was explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with three knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles). In our study, we selected the number of nodes based on previous studies and statistics guideline entitled “Regression Modeling Strategies”, (https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-19425-7), which commonly recommend using three to five nodes for most epidemiological studies. When the number of nodes is 2, the resulting fitted curve is a straight line. Specifically, we chose to use 3 nodes, to provide a balance between model flexibility and the risk of overfitting. This approach allowed us to model the non-linear association between niacin intake and MASLD risk effectively while maintaining interpretability and robustness of the results. Subgroup analyses based on age, sex, BMI, diabetes, and hypertension were conducted to evaluate the stability of the niacin-MASLD association across various subgroups. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.6 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance set at a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

In the current study, we initially enrolled a total of 9254 participants from NHANES 2017–2018, the only one study circle containing liver ultrasound data. Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we ultimately enrolled 2946 eligible participants (Fig. 1). The average age of the entire study cohort was 37.2 years, with nearly half (47.92%) being male. A total of 1385 participants (4.0%) exhibited MASLD. Notably, those with MASLD tended to be older and exhibited higher prevalence rates of hypertension (53.02% vs. 24.26%), diabetes (27.08% vs. 4.70%). Associations between the intake of niacin with prevalence of MASLD

To explore the potential association between the dietary intake of niacin and the prevalence of MASLD, a comprehensive multivariate analysis was conducted, adjusting for variables such as age, gender, ethnicity, education levels, smoking, drinking, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus (DM). Employing restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, we found a U-shaped association between the intake of niacin and MASLD risk, characterized by a distinct non-linear trend (P for non-linear trend < 0.001). As the intake of niacin increased, the odds of MASLD exhibited a gradual decline until reaching an inflection point (23.6). After this threshold, the odds of MASLD gradually increased (Fig. 3). Additionally, participants were evenly distributed into quartiles based on the intake of niacin, and we found that the participants in Niacin-Q3 had the lowest MASLD prevalence rate. This was in accordance with the findings of RCS analysis that the U-shaped association between the intake of niacin with prevalence of MASLD (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis on the association of the intake of niacin and the prevalence of MASLD among different populations

We also conducted subgroup analyses of RCS among different populations, and the relationship between niacin intake and MASLD was found to be U-shaped in most of groups (Fig. 4A-E). It is noteworthy that the inflection points of this nonlinear relationship differ between males and females. In females, the inflection point is located at 18.8, while in the male population, the inflection point is at 26.7 (Fig. 4B). It is worth noting that in elderly patients over the age of 60 (Fig. 4A) and in patients with diabetes (Fig. 4D), the relationship between dietary niacin intake and MASLD is not nonlinear but linear. In other words, as the dietary niacin intake increased, the risk of MASLD gradually decreased.

Discussion

In the present large-scale cross-sectional analysis, we found that there was a non-linear U-shaped association between the intake of niacin and odds of MASLD. The dose–response relationship was non-linear in these ranges of dietary niacin intake. With the increase in dietary niacin intake, the odds of MASLD exhibited a gradual decline until reaching an inflection point (23.6 mg), after this threshold, the odds of MASLD gradually increased.

Niacin is present in many foods, especially those rich in protein. Niacin in the diet can be converted into niacinamide, which is its active form, within the body [23, 24]. The body can also synthesize niacin through tryptophan [25]. Niacinamide participates in various biochemical processes in the body, with its most crucial role being in energy metabolism. It constitutes components of coenzymes NAD (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) and NADP (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate), both of which play a key role in energy metabolism and redox reactions [26]. In recent years, more and more researchers focused on roles of niacin in prevention of various diseases. Zhao et al. conducted a NHANES based cross-sectional study to investigate the relationship between niacin intake and depressive symptoms. After adjusting for various factors, the study found a U-shaped association between niacin intake and depression in the general American population. This means that both low and high levels of niacin intake were associated with an increased risk of depression, while moderate intake showed a lower risk. This U-shaped pattern was consistent across different demographic groups, including men, Mexican Americans, White individuals, adults aged under 40, and those with a BMI under 30 [27–30]. As far as we know, this is the first study exploring the association of niacin and MASLD. We also found a U-shaped relationship between the intake of niacin and the prevalence of MASLD in this study. We found that with the increase in dietary niacin intake, the odds of MASLD exhibited a decline until reaching an inflection point (23.6 mg). This may be related to the biological functions of niacin, as its antioxidative stress and lipid-lowering effects could be reasons for its potential to reduce the risk of MASLD. Therefore, appropriately increasing the intake of niacin may be helpful in reducing the occurrence of MASLD. However, results also showed that the odds of MASLD significantly increased with the increase of intake of niacin after inflection point. To date, numerous studies have confirmed that excessive niacin can lead to certain liver toxicity. This may be related to the potential for excessive niacin intake to cause liver toxicity. Our research contributes to the control of niacin intake.

In this study, we focus on a new concept and a new disease, MASLD. This concept greatly aids in the precision and personalized treatment of fatty liver disease. The presence of metabolic dysfunction is a necessary condition for diagnosing MASLD, making it essential to study MASLD from a metabolic perspective. As of now, despite the recent introduction of the MASLD concept, numerous researchers have already conducted studies using the new definition and diagnostic criteria for fatty liver disease. Recently, through a retrospective national cohort study involving 940 histologically defined patients, covering the complete spectrum of MASLD, a secure, searchable, open resource (SteatoSITE) was established by Kendall et al. [31]. Despite numerous studies confirming the role of genetic susceptibility in fatty liver disease, dietary habits and lifestyle still play significant roles in this condition. Therefore, our current study explores the relationship between dietary niacin intake and MASLD.

Niacin can influence lipid metabolism, including regulating blood lipid levels and reducing serum triglycerides and cholesterol levels, which may contribute to alleviating the development of MASLD [32, 33]. One potential biological mechanism contributing to the positive impact of niacin in improving lipid profiles is its direct and non-competitive inhibition of diacylglycerol acyltransferase-2 in hepatocytes [34, 35]. This action delays fatty acid acylation, consequently lowering triglyceride synthesis. Niacin’s suppression of triglyceride synthesis leads to heightened degradation of ApoB, a key contributor to LDL-c synthesis within cells [36, 37]. This inhibition hinders the lipidization of ApoB, potentially affecting vesicle membranes, and results in the reduction of very low-density lipoprotein. Furthermore, niacin boosts the buildup of apoprotein A, the primary protein constituent of HDL-c particles, within hepatoma G2 cells [38]. As a result, it reduces the ApoB to apoprotein A1 ratio, potentially exerting a beneficial impact on the serum lipid profile. Oxidative stress plays a crucial role in fatty liver disease, as it can lead to lipid peroxidation, where fat molecules are damaged by oxidative reactions. This may result in the accumulation of fat within liver cells, forming lipid droplets and exacerbating the progression of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Additionally, oxidative stress can trigger inflammatory responses, causing inflammation in liver tissues [39–41]. Inflammation is closely associated with the development of NAFLD, as it can lead to the destruction and fibrosis of liver tissues [42, 43]. Niacin is an antioxidant that can help counteract the damage caused by free radicals, thereby mitigating inflammation and injury. In MASLD patients, insulin resistance is commonly present, which leads to the need for increased insulin levels to maintain normal blood glucose levels. Insulin resistance is closely associated with the development of MASLD, as it is linked to abnormal responses of fat cells to insulin [44]. Insulin serves as a crucial regulatory factor that can influence lipid metabolism. In the presence of insulin resistance, the response of adipose tissue to insulin diminishes, resulting in increased release of lipids. This may lead to excessive accumulation of intrahepatic fat, promoting the development of MASLD [45, 46]. Insulin resistance also impacts glucose metabolism, causing elevated blood glucose levels. Moreover, recent studies have shown that the gut microbiome significantly contributes to the synthesis and bioavailability of various vitamins, including biotin and niacin [47–49]. The microbiome’s ability to synthesize niacin and its precursors suggests that gut health could influence circulating niacin levels and potentially modify the association between dietary niacin intake and health outcomes [50–54]. We primarily focused on dietary niacin intake as assessed by two 24-hour dietary recalls, we acknowledge the emerging evidence indicating that microbiome-related niacin production may also play a role in determining niacin status and its effects on metabolic health. This area of research is particularly relevant as it may help to explain inter-individual variability in niacin levels and responses to dietary intake. Future research incorporating microbiome analysis could provide a more comprehensive understanding of niacin metabolism, including how gut-derived niacin contributes to overall niacin status and its potential role in the prevention or exacerbation of metabolic diseases, such as MASLD. Exploring the interaction between diet, the microbiome, and vitamin metabolism represents an important direction for further investigation. The association between insulin resistance and the occurrence and progression of MASLD is significant, as excess glucose can be converted into fat, contributing to hepatic accumulation. Studies suggest that niacin may contribute to improving insulin sensitivity, which is crucial for regulating blood glucose and lipid metabolism [55–57]. However, it is worth noting that excessive intake of niacin can lead to liver damage. Therefore, finding an appropriate intake of niacin is crucial. In this study, we utilized the RCS research method and found a U-shaped non-linear relationship between niacin intake and MASLD, with an inflection point at 23.6 mg, this may provide a reference for niacin supplementation intake. Of note, the current niacin intake in the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for adults is 14–16 mg/day. Obviously, there is a difference between the current RDA for adults and our study findings. The current RDA for niacin intake is based on studies aimed at preventing pellagra, a condition caused by niacin deficiency [58–60]. These studies primarily focused on ensuring adequate intake to prevent deficiency-related diseases, rather than optimizing intake for chronic disease prevention. Our study, however, explored the relationship between niacin intake and the risk of MASLD within a multi-ethnic cohort, revealing a U-shaped association where moderate intake up to 23.6 mg/day was associated with reduced MASLD risk, while intake beyond this level showed an increased risk. We believe that these differences highlight the need for further research to refine dietary recommendations, considering not only deficiency prevention but also chronic disease risk.

Our study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment and consideration in future research: [1] The cross-sectional design inherently limits our ability to establish causality between niacin and MASLD. Further validation is needed through longitudinal studies with prospective cohorts of US adults; [2] Despite efforts to include numerous covariates to control for confounding bias, MASLD is a complex disorder influenced by multiple genetic, behavioral, and environmental factors. Unidentified confounders may exist, impacting the pathogenesis and progression of hypertension, as not all relevant factors are explicitly documented in the NHANES database; [3] The use of a single 24-hour recall may not be the optimal method for calculating habitual dietary intake at an individual level due to day-to-day variations. The large number of participants in NHANES surveys may limit the practical application of more accurate options (e.g., multiple 24-hour recalls, food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)) in exploring the long-term link between dietary intake and hypertension. This issue should be addressed in future studies utilizing NHANES dietary information. Self-reporting is a convenient means of obtaining information about dietary intake and hypertension occurrence among NHANES participants. However, this method may introduce recall bias, and caution is warranted during the analysis and interpretation of the data.

Conclusion

Our study highlights a U-shaped association between the niacin intake and MASLD prevalence, with an inflection point at 23.6 mg. These results indicate that a moderate increase in niacin intake could potentially decrease the prevalence of MASLD. Further investigations and interventions centered around niacin consumption may provide valuable insights into the prevention and management of MASLD.
