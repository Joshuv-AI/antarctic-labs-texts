Facial Emotion Recognition Deficits and Electroencephalogram Alterations in Mild Behavioral Impairment
Authors: YongSoo Shim, Eun-Ye Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMild behavioral impairment (MBI) is a late-life neurobehavioral syndrome associated with increased risk of incident cognitive decline and dementia. Facial emotion recognition (FER), a core component of social cognition, is impaired across neurodegenerative disorders, yet its association with MBI remains underexplored. We examined cognitive/socio-emotional profiles and resting-state electroencephalogram (EEG) in individuals with MBI.MethodsA total of 120 older adults with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were classified into MBI (n = 57) and non-MBI (n = 63) using the MBI Checklist. Participants underwent comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological evaluation and neuropsychological assessments including Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Korean Facial Affect Battery (K-FAB subtests 1–5). Resting-state EEG was analyzed using an automated platform to derive sensor-level spectral power and source-level connectivity measures. Group comparisons used appropriate tests; K-FAB analyses were adjusted for age, education, MMSE, and Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS).ResultsThe MBI group showed greater functional impairment (higher CDR-SOB, p = 0.019) and higher depressive symptom severity (GDS, p = 0.004), with a trend toward fewer years of education (p = 0.090). Neuropsychological domain scores were broadly comparable, although the MBI group showed a trend toward lower frontal executive composite performance after covariate adjustment (p = 0.076). On K-FAB, the MBI group demonstrated significantly poorer affect selection performance (FAB4; adjusted p = 0.031), with additional trend-level differences in affect naming (FAB3; adjusted p = 0.084) and in negative emotion recognition (fear and sadness). Resting-state EEG findings indicated elevated Beta1–3 and gamma absolute power in the MBI group across multiple scalp regions (p < 0.05), as well as frequency-specific source connectivity differences, including widespread delta band hyperconnectivity, relatively stronger Alpha1 connectivity in the non-MBI group, and increased Beta1 connectivity within fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal circuits in the MBI group.ConclusionIn this clinic-based SCD/MCI cohort, MBI was associated with greater functional impairment and depressive symptom burden, selective FER deficits – particularly in affect selection – and distinct resting-state EEG spectral and connectivity patterns. These findings suggest that socio-emotional measures and EEG markers may provide complementary information for characterizing neurobehavioral alterations associated with MBI, although interpretations should remain cautious given the cross-sectional design and methodological constraints of automated EEG analyses.

Introduction

Mild behavioral impairment (MBI) is a neurobehavioral syndrome that emerges later in life, characterized by the persistent onset of new neuropsychiatric symptoms in individuals over 50 who do not have dementia [1, 2]. These symptoms must last for at least 6 months, signify a noticeable change from the person’s usual personality or behavior, and cannot be attributed to preexisting psychiatric or medical conditions. The International Society to Advance Alzheimer’s Research and Treatment – Alzheimer’s Association (ISTAART-AA) has developed diagnostic criteria that categorize MBI into five domains: decreased motivation, affective dysregulation, impulse dyscontrol, social inappropriateness, and abnormal perception or thought content [3, 4].

The importance of MBI as a transitional state lies in its ability to identify individuals at an elevated risk of developing dementia before cognitive symptoms appear [2, 3, 5]. Early identification could enable timely interventions and more effective management strategies. As a neurobehavioral marker, MBI may offer a complementary approach to traditional cognitive assessments in predicting future cognitive decline. By recognizing and evaluating these behavioral changes, clinicians might detect subtle signs of neurodegeneration earlier in the disease course [6], potentially leading to improved outcomes and more targeted therapeutic approaches. The connection between MBI and preclinical or prodromal dementia, particularly Alzheimer’s disease (AD), is a growing area of interest in neurodegenerative research [7–10]. Studies have shown that individuals with MBI are at a higher risk of developing cognitive impairment and dementia, including AD, compared to those without MBI [7, 11–13]. The presence of MBI symptoms may reflect underlying neuropathological changes associated with AD, such as amyloid-beta accumulation and tau pathology, even before significant cognitive deficits are apparent [8–10]. This suggests that MBI could potentially be used as a clinical marker for identifying individuals at increased risk of developing dementia, particularly AD, in its earliest stages. Longitudinal studies further demonstrate that MBI predicts higher rates of progression to dementia and is often present years before measurable cognitive decline [14, 15]. Thus, MBI has been proposed as a clinically relevant neurobehavioral syndrome that may help characterize individuals at increased vulnerability along the dementia continuum, although its predictive utility requires confirmation in longitudinal studies.

Facial emotion recognition (FER) is a fundamental aspect of social cognition, enabling individuals to interpret and respond appropriately to the emotional states of others [16]. This process involves analyzing facial expressions, which convey information about a person’s feelings, intentions, and mental state. The ability to accurately recognize and interpret facial emotions is crucial for successful social interactions, as it allows individuals to navigate social situations, empathize with others, and adjust their behavior accordingly [17].

FER impairment is a common feature across various neurodegenerative disorders [18–20], and this deficit can significantly impact interpersonal relationships and overall quality of life. Disorders such as AD, fronto-temporal dementia, Parkinson’s disease [19], and Huntington’s disease [20] have all been associated with varying degrees of FER deficits, though the specific patterns and severity may differ among these conditions [18]. The underlying mechanisms of FER impairment in neurodegenerative disorders are complex and multifaceted [21]. They may involve disruptions in neural networks responsible for processing emotional stimuli, including the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and temporal regions [22–25]. Additionally, cognitive decline, particularly in areas of attention, working memory, and executive function, can contribute to difficulties in accurately perceiving and interpreting facial expressions [26, 27]. As neurodegenerative diseases progress, these impairments often become more pronounced, further compromising patients’ social functioning and potentially leading to increased isolation and caregiver burden.

The association between MBI and FER represents a significant gap in current research. While MBI is recognized as a potential precursor to cognitive decline and dementia, its relationship with specific cognitive functions, particularly FER, remains understudied. This gap is particularly noteworthy given the importance of emotional processing in social interactions and overall cognitive health. Further investigation into this area could provide valuable insights into the early detection and management of cognitive decline. Understanding how MBI affects FER might reveal subtle changes in social cognition that occur before more apparent cognitive symptoms emerge. Additionally, exploring this relationship could lead to the development of more sensitive screening tools for early-stage cognitive impairment, potentially allowing for earlier interventions and improved patient outcomes.

This study investigates FER performance and resting-state electroencephalographic characteristics in individuals with MBI to better characterize socio-emotional and neurophysiological alterations associated with this syndrome in the context of neurodegenerative disease. The primary objectives are to quantify FER performance across discrete emotion categories and to characterize resting-state electroencephalogram (EEG) alterations – spectral power and functional connectivity – to identify candidate neural biomarkers of MBI. Because FER is central to social cognition, impairments in this domain may help explain the behavioral changes that define MBI. Examining how individual differences in FER relate to EEG-based indices of network function will clarify the neural mechanisms of emotion processing in MBI and highlight subtle preclinical alterations relevant to diagnosis and intervention.

Methods

Participants and Classification

Study participants were recruited from older adults who visited the memory clinic in the Department of Neurology at The Catholic University of Korea, Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital, for cognitive evaluation. Following comprehensive clinical evaluation, neurological examinations, laboratory tests, neuropsychological assessments, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), individuals diagnosed with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were eligible for inclusion. A total of 120 older adults with SCD or MCI were enrolled. Fifty-one were diagnosed with SCD according to the guidelines of Jessen et al. [28], and 69 were diagnosed with MCI based on the National Institute on Aging – Alzheimer’s Association criteria [29]. By Petersen’s criteria [30], participants with MCI had normal daily living activities but an objective memory impairment, which means less than 1.5 standard deviation from the norm in at least one memory test. Diagnosis of SCD was made when there was a complaint of memory decline without objective neuropsychological test results.

Participants were further classified into MBI (n = 57) and no-MBI (n = 63) groups using the MBI Checklist (MBI-C) [4, 31], which was administered to caregivers. MBI was defined according to the MBI-C score, with a threshold of 8.5 or higher in SCD [32] and 6.5 or higher in MCI [33]. Different MBI-C thresholds were applied for SCD and MCI groups based on prior validation studies, reflecting differences in baseline neuropsychiatric symptom burden across cognitive stages. The MBI-C is a valuable tool for classifying individuals into groups with and without MBI.

All participants completed clinical and neuropsychological assessments, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [34, 35], clinical dementia rating (CDR) [36], and comprehensive neuropsychological assessments across 5 domains (attention, language, visuospatial abilities, memory, and frontal executive functions) using the 2nd edition of Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery (SNSB-II) [37]. We assessed depressive symptoms using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [38]. Participants with conditions that could potentially lead to cognitive impairment, such as hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, syphilis, or a history of major psychiatric illness (e.g., major depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia), were excluded. We also excluded patients with MRI abnormalities, such as brain tumors, radiation injury, hippocampal sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis. Subjects with severe white matter hyperintensities or multiple (more than 5) lacunar infarctions, consistent with Erkinjuntti’s brain imaging criteria for subcortical ischemic vascular dementia [39], were also excluded. None of the participants had a history of previous stroke or focal neurological symptoms.

Participants who underwent qEEG and did not show an EEG abnormality were included in the present study. An EEG abnormality was defined as asymmetry of background activity, continuous θ-range slow waves, generalized or focal δ-range slow waves, or epileptiform discharges. Participants taking cholinesterase inhibitors, benzodiazepines, or antidepressants that could influence EEG rhythms were excluded. None of the participants were taking antiepileptic drugs.

Verbal informed consent for participation was obtained from all patients, regardless of cognitive status. For individuals with cognitive impairment, clinical evaluations were conducted in the presence of their legal guardians, and verbal informed consent was obtained from both the patients and their accompanying caregivers. This study was conducted as a retrospective analysis of clinical records.

Facial Emotion Recognition

To evaluate the FER ability, we administered a standardized and validated instrument known as the Korean Facial Affect Battery (K-FAB) [40]. The original version of the Florida Affect Battery (FAB) [41] was designed to assess the perception of facial and prosodic affect across various task demands, and it included 10 different subtests (comprising 5 facial, 3 prosodic, and 2 cross-modal subtests). However, the Korean version (K-FAB) exclusively on facial affect tasks includes subtest 1 (facial identity discrimination), subtest 2 (facial affect discrimination), subtest 3 (facial affect naming), subtest 4 (facial affect selection), and subtest 5 (facial affect matching). These tasks involved the recognition of five different emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, and neutrality).

EEG Data Acquisition and Analysis

All participants underwent a routine EEG recording in a dedicated examination room as part of the initial clinical assessment prior to the initiation of any AD-specific treatment (e.g., acetylcholinesterase inhibitors). EEG recordings were conducted on weekdays between 9:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. EEG signals were acquired under resting-state conditions with eyes closed using 19 scalp electrodes positioned according to the International 10–20 System (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T7, C3, Cz, C4, T8, P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, O1, and O2), referenced to linked ears. Data were sampled at 256 Hz with a high-pass filter at 1 Hz, a low-pass filter at 70 Hz, a notch filter at 60 Hz, and electrode impedances maintained below 5 kΩ. Vigilance was continuously monitored, and participants were alerted if signs of drowsiness were observed in the EEG traces. A total of 9–12 artifact-free epochs of 4 s were initially selected per participant. An independent investigator, blinded to the diagnostic and clinical grouping of participants, visually confirmed that the selected EEG segments were acceptable for subsequent analyses.

EEG preprocessing and feature extraction were performed using the iSyncBrain® (iMediSync Inc., Republic of Korea) (https://isyncbrain.com/) platform [42, 43], a proprietary cloud-based artificial intelligence (AI) EEG analysis system, without access to participants’ clinical characteristics or group assignment. Raw EEG data were uploaded to the platform, where preprocessing was conducted automatically on a centralized server. Offline preprocessing included high-pass filtering above 1 Hz, low-pass filtering below 45 Hz, and re-referencing to the common average. Transient artifacts were initially screened through visual inspection, after which stationary artifact components related to eye movements, muscle activity, or cardiac signals were removed using adaptive mixture independent component analysis [44]. In addition, the platform incorporates AI-based automatic denoising, which detects and removes artifact-related components in an automated manner. Investigators are able to review the AI-identified artifact components by examining their corresponding power spectra and scalp topographies (TopoMaps) within the analysis interface. Following automated denoising, at least 30 s (typically 36–48 s) of artifact-free EEG data was required for inclusion in the analysis. To ensure standardization across participants, the platform restricts analysis to a maximum of the first 4 min of clean EEG data, even when longer recordings are available.

Power spectral density was computed using fast Fourier transform with a frequency resolution of 0.25 Hz, utilizing the iSyncBrain auto-analysis system. Sensor-level spectral features were extracted for eight frequency bands: δ (1–4 Hz), θ (4–8 Hz), α1 (8–10 Hz), α2 (10–12 Hz), β1 (12–15 Hz), β2 (15–20 Hz), β3 (20–30 Hz), and γ (30–45 Hz). Absolute power, expressed in microvolts squared, was defined as the sum of component powers within each frequency band, while total power was defined as the sum of powers across all bands (1–45 Hz). Relative power was calculated as the ratio of absolute power in a given band to the total power and expressed as a percentage, reflecting the proportion of activity in that band relative to overall spectral power. Together, absolute and relative power provide complementary insights into both the intensity and distribution of brain activity across the different frequency ranges. For source-level analysis, standardized low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA) [45] was applied using the Colin 27 head model, segmented into 68 regions of interest (ROIs) according to the Desikan-Killiany atlas, to compare relative power values of ROIs [46] and connectivity (the imaginary part of coherency) [47] between ROIs. EEG coherence has been studied as a measure of brain connectivity [48], and the imaginary part of coherency has been introduced to avoid volume conduction artifacts [47]. We have estimated the functional connectivity at eight frequency bands (δ [1–4 Hz], θ [4–8 Hz], α1 [8–10 Hz], α2 [10–12 Hz], β1 [12–15 Hz], β2 [15–20 Hz], β3 [20–30 Hz], and γ [30–45 Hz]).

Statistical Analysis

Participants were classified into MBI and non-MBI groups based on their MBI-C scores. Demographic characteristics (age, sex, and years of education), diagnostic classification (SCD vs. MCI), apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, MMSE, CDR, SNSB domain scores, GDS, MRI findings, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, heart disease, stroke, and smoking), and K-FAB scores were compared between the two groups. Group comparisons were performed using independent t test or χ2 test, as appropriate. To control for potential confounding variables, comparisons of K-FAB scores between groups were adjusted for age, education, and MMSE score, and additionally for depressive symptoms as measured by the GDS. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All other statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 30.0 software program (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Quantitative EEG analyses, including power spectral density and sLORETA, were performed automatically using the iSyncBrain platform. Due to methodological constraints of the automated platform, subject-level covariate adjustment was not available for EEG outcomes. EEG analyses involved multiple electrodes, frequency bands, and connectivity pairs; therefore, EEG findings were treated as exploratory, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Of the 120 participants included in the study, 57 (47.5%) were classified into the MBI group and 63 (52.5%) into the non-MBI group (Table 1). There were no significant differences in age or sex distribution between groups. Participants with MBI tended to have fewer years of education compared with those without MBI (8.23 ± 4.26 vs. 9.67 ± 4.93, p = 0.090). Functional impairment was also greater in the MBI group, as indicated by higher CDR-sum of boxes (SOB) scores (1.73 ± 0.95 vs. 1.32 ± 0.93, p = 0.019). Depressive symptoms, as measured by the GDS, were significantly more severe among MBI participants (11.16 ± 7.50 vs. 7.33 ± 6.71, p = 0.004). MRI markers – including periventricular hyperintensity, deep white matter hyperintensity, medial temporal atrophy, lacunes, and cerebral microbleeds – did not differ significantly between groups. Vascular risk factors were largely comparable.

Neuropsychological Performance

Group comparisons of neuropsychological performance are presented in Figure 1. There were no significant differences between groups in attention, language, visuospatial function, or memory domains. Participants with MBI showed a trend toward lower frontal executive composite z-scores compared with the non-MBI group after adjusting for age, education, MMSE, and depressive symptoms (GDS) (−0.28 ± 1.12 vs. 0.10 ± 1.19, p = 0.076). This finding suggests that executive function may be relatively more impaired in individuals with MBI and may be associated with their behavioral and emotional symptoms.

Facial Emotion Recognition

On the K-FAB, after adjusting for age, education, MMSE, and GDS, the MBI group demonstrated significantly poorer performance than the non-MBI group, particularly in FAB4 (facial affect selection) (16.63 ± 2.65 vs. 17.78 ± 1.92, p = 0.031) (Table 2). Additionally, FAB3 (facial affect naming) also tended to be lower in the MBI group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (15.96 ± 2.67 vs. 16.75 ± 2.20, p = 0.084). Item-level analyses suggested a trend toward reduced recognition of negative emotions in the MBI group, including fear in FAB3 (F3, p = 0.053), sadness in FAB4 (S4, p = 0.057), and fear in FAB4 (F4, p = 0.068). Although some K-FAB outcomes showed nominal group differences in covariate-adjusted analyses, none remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons.

EEG Findings

As shown in Figure 2, participants in the MBI group demonstrated significantly elevated absolute power in the Beta1–3 and gamma frequency ranges across frontal (FP1, F8), central (Cz), occipital (O2), and temporal (T4, T5) electrodes (p < 0.05). Overall, these findings indicate increased high-frequency oscillatory activity (beta and gamma) in the MBI group at the sensor level. Alpha power did not differ significantly between groups, although alpha activity was numerically lower in the MBI group. Delta and theta power were numerically higher in the MBI group; however, these differences were also not statistically significant.

Scalp-level power spectra across frequency bands in MBI and non-MBI groups. Scalp-level power spectra across frequency bands, showing marked increases in beta and gamma activity in the MBI group compared with the non-MBI group. These findings suggest heightened cortical activation associated with behavioral dysregulation in MBI.

Source connectivity (sLORETA) analyses revealed frequency-specific patterns between groups (Fig. 3). In the delta band, the MBI group exhibited widespread hyperconnectivity. By contrast, Alpha1 connectivity was relatively stronger in the non-MBI group. In the Beta1 band, the MBI group showed increased fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal connectivity, consistent with the sensor-level observation of elevated high-frequency power. Collectively, these findings suggest that MBI status is associated with altered resting-state oscillatory power and frequency-specific network connectivity patterns, particularly involving fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal circuits.

Source-level connectivity differences between MBI and non-MBI groups. Visualization of source-level connectivity differences across frequency bands (delta, Alpha1, and Beta1) between MBI and non-MBI groups. The MBI group exhibited widespread hyperconnectivity in the delta band, reflecting nonspecific network recruitment and potentially reduced efficiency in attentional and executive control. In contrast, Alpha1 connectivity was stronger in the non-MBI group, suggesting preserved inhibitory control and emotion regulation. Increased Beta1 connectivity in the MBI group, particularly within fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal circuits, paralleled scalp-level findings of elevated high-frequency power and collectively indicated prefrontal network overactivation associated with deficits in regulatory control.

Discussion

Taken together, the present findings suggest that, within this clinic-based SCD/MCI cohort, individuals with MBI exhibit a distinct clinical and neuropsychological profile characterized by greater functional impairment and depressive symptom burden, with a trend toward fewer years of education and reduced executive performance. In socio-emotional processing, the MBI group showed poorer performance on FER tasks, particularly on affect selection (FAB4), with additional trend-level differences observed in affect naming (FAB3) and in the recognition of negative emotions (e.g., fear, sadness, and anger) [49, 50]. Resting-state EEG analyses further indicated group differences in oscillatory activity and frequency-specific connectivity patterns, including elevated beta and gamma power and altered Alpha1 and Beta1 connectivity within fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal circuits. Collectively, these findings highlight the clinical, cognitive, and neurophysiological features associated with MBI in this sample. These findings should be interpreted cautiously within the context of this clinic-based cross-sectional design.

MBI participants showed greater functional impairment, as reflected by higher CDR-SOB scores, despite broadly comparable performance across most cognitive domains. Executive function also showed a trend toward poorer performance in the MBI group, suggesting that frontal executive vulnerabilities may be relatively more prominent in individuals with MBI [51–53]. Together, these findings suggest that aspects of FER – particularly tasks requiring rapid labeling or selection of negative affect – may be relevant to the clinical characterization of MBI and may complement traditional cognitive screening. From a neurobiological perspective, these FER deficits may stem from dysfunction within temporo-limbic and prefrontal networks involved in salience detection, appraisal, and top-down regulation, consistent with prior studies [13, 54–56]. It should be noted that FER performance may also be influenced by factors beyond socio-emotional processing, including sensory deficits (e.g., reduced visual acuity), fatigue, and fluctuations in attention. Although these factors were not systematically assessed in the present study, they may have contributed to interindividual variability. Accordingly, FER findings should be interpreted cautiously, as unmeasured sensory- or state-related factors may have partially influenced task performance.

Because depressive symptoms were more prominent in the MBI group, mood-related effects represent an important potential confound. While FER analyses were adjusted for depressive symptoms, EEG analyses could not incorporate mood-related covariates due to platform constraints; therefore, EEG findings should be interpreted cautiously. At the scalp level, MBI participants exhibited elevated Beta1–3 and gamma power across multiple scalp regions. Although the present study did not include direct measures of arousal or affective state, similar high-frequency patterns have been reported in association with heightened cortical arousal and cognitive-emotional strain in prior literature [57–59]. Source-level analyses showed relatively lower alpha connectivity in the MBI group, which may be related to reduced inhibitory or emotion-regulatory network functioning as suggested in prior work [60]. In addition, the MBI group exhibited widespread delta band hyperconnectivity, along with increased Beta1 connectivity within fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal circuits [61, 62]. The diffuse delta hyperconnectivity may reflect nonspecific network coupling, potentially indicating inefficient network engagement. Taken together, these spectral and connectivity patterns may reflect network-level alterations associated with MBI-related executive and socio-emotional features, supporting the relevance of large-scale network dysregulation rather than focal deficits [63–66]. Overall, these EEG findings suggest frequency-specific network-level alterations associated with MBI status, but should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory given the methodological constraints of the automated platform.

Clinically, these findings carry several important implications. First, among individuals with SCD/MCI, MBI may help identify a subgroup with disproportionate executive and socio-emotional impairment, potentially reflecting greater clinical severity and functional vulnerability [67–69]. Second, FER tasks – especially those taxing rapid labeling and selection of negative affect – may provide additional information beyond standard cognitive tests and could help characterize socio-emotional dysfunction in pre-dementia populations [52, 70–72]. Third, EEG markers (e.g., elevated beta/gamma activity, altered Alpha1 connectivity, and network-level coupling patterns) are noninvasive and scalable measures that may be useful for phenotyping and longitudinal monitoring in future studies [73–76].

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding temporal trajectories, causality, or the predictive validity of FER and EEG abnormalities. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as associative rather than prognostic, and no conclusions regarding disease progression or risk prediction can be drawn. Second, participants were recruited from a single memory clinic, and referral/selection bias cannot be excluded; thus, the generalizability of these findings may be limited compared with community-based or population-based cohorts. Third, the relatively small sample size may have reduced statistical power and limits the robustness of subgroup comparisons. Fourth, AD biomarkers (e.g., amyloid PET or cerebrospinal fluid markers) were not available, preventing direct confirmation of underlying AD pathology. Fifth, FER performance may be influenced by unmeasured sensory- or state-related factors (e.g., visual acuity, fatigue, attentional fluctuations), which were not systematically assessed in this study. Several EEG-specific limitations should also be acknowledged. EEG analyses were performed using an automated proprietary cloud-based platform (iSyncBrain®), which does not support subject-level covariate modeling. As a result, potential confounders – particularly mood-related variables such as depressive symptom severity – could not be statistically adjusted in EEG analyses, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, EEG outcomes are inherently high-dimensional, involving multiple electrodes, frequency bands, and connectivity pairs; due to platform constraints and limited access to raw statistical outputs, comprehensive multiple-comparison correction across all EEG features could not be implemented. Accordingly, EEG findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Finally, interpretations of EEG alterations in terms of specific psychological or neurophysiological mechanisms (e.g., arousal, anxiety, or compensatory processes) are necessarily indirect, as the present study did not include direct measures of these constructs.

MBI was associated with frontal-predominant cognitive vulnerability, selective FER impairment for negative emotions, and an electrophysiological profile characterized by high-frequency hyperactivity with reduced alpha connectivity and strengthened fronto-parietal/fronto-temporal coupling. These convergent clinical, cognitive, and neurophysiological signatures support MBI as a clinically and biologically meaningful syndrome associated with late-life behavioral change and network-level neurophysiological alterations. Longitudinal studies incorporating disease-specific biomarkers are required to determine whether these measures have prognostic value for cognitive decline or neurodegenerative outcomes.
