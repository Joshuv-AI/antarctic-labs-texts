Cation-Assisted Surfactant Templating of DNA at the Air–Water Interface: New Insights into Nucleic Acid Surface Activity
Authors: Eshani Hettiarachchi, Vicki H. Grassian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA, a highly negatively charged polyelectrolyte biomolecule, shows negligible propensity for the air–water interface, as DNA prefers to remain fully hydrated in aqueous solutions. Previous studies have shown that cationic surfactants can complex with DNA at the air–water interface through electrostatic interactions and drive DNA to the interface. Here we show a new mechanism, involving DNA complexes to palmitic acid monolayers in the presence of divalent cations Mg2+ and Ca2+, suggesting that the interaction occurs through a divalent cation bridge that stabilizes DNA at the air–water interface. Interestingly, we find that the surface propensity of DNA depends on the base sequence due to differential nucleotide affinities for interfacial complexation. Oligomers with higher AT content were the most surface-active. Overall, this study provides new insights into DNA at the air–water interface under environmentally and biologically relevant conditions.

Understanding nucleic acids at the air–water interface is of interest for many reasons, some of which include enrichment of nucleic acids in aerosols, the use of DNA and RNA in environmental monitoring for species detection, horizontal gene transfer, human health implications, and expanding our understanding of the origins of life and early earth. − DNA is highly negatively charged and shows negligible propensity for the air–water interface. Instead, DNA prefers to remain fully hydrated in bulk aqueous solutions. Previously it was shown that cationic surfactants can complex with DNA at the air–water interface through electrostatics interactions. − The proposed mechanism for interfacial DNA complexation discussed here differs in that we show palmitic acid (PA), a common fatty acid surfactant found in sea spray and other atmospheric aerosols as well as in other environmental and biological systems, , forms a monolayer at the air–water interface that can interact with DNA in the presence of divalent cations (Mg2+ and Ca2+). This cation-assisted interaction between DNA and PA drives DNA to the interface for different DNA lengths including high molar mass salmon testes DNA and double-stranded DNA oligomers of shorter chain length including ATATAT, AGTGCT, and GGATCC and their complementary strands. This cation-assisted interaction between DNA and PA is reminiscent of that proposed for saccharides at the ocean-air interface which has been suggested to be the cause of saccharide enrichment in sea spray aerosols worldwide. − Additionally, divalent cations have been shown to enhance the adsorption of DNA on solid surfaces including clay mineral and biochar surfaces. − These interactions between divalent cations and DNA occur via both inner-sphere and outer-sphere complexation with either or both phosphate backbone and nitrogenous bases and are cation- as well as DNA sequence- and strand-dependent. ,,

Representative isotherms of PA monolayers on neat water (orange circles) and on 0.4 M MgCl2 at pH 4 (green curve, diamond markers) are shown in Figure (a). High inorganic salt concentrations were chosen as representative of marine aerosols which are enriched with cations such as Mg2+ and Ca2+. While aerosol pH varies from highly acidic to basic, pH 4 was chosen as the optimal condition for two reasons. First, the helical structure of double stranded DNA remains intact between pH 3 and below pH 9. Second, palmitic acid protonation on neat water occurs at these lower pH conditions. , Details of the sample preparation and experimental methods as well as a graphical representation of the Langmuir-trough and the measurements performed here are provided in the Supporting Information (see Figure S1). PA monolayers are fairly compressible and ultimately can be packed in a highly ordered structure at high surface pressures due to being a saturated single-chain surfactant molecule. Further increase in compression collapses this highly ordered structure. The isotherm of the PA monolayer shown in Figure on neat water reveals this behavior. Some differences in the isotherm were observed in the presence of Mg2+ in comparison to the isotherm on neat water and are in good agreement with similar differences seen in previous studies conducted with divalent cations. , In particular, the PA monolayer collapses at higher surface pressures due to increased surface tension in the presence of inorganic salts. ,, Additionally, the lift-off value for the mean molecular area (MMA) of a pure PA monolayer on a 0.4 M MgCl2 at pH 4 subphase is ∼ 21 Å2/ molecule, slightly larger MMA (∼19 Å2/ molecule) than neat water (∼19 Å2/ molecule). Such differences have been previously attributed to the cationic interactions with PA headgroups. At 0.4 M MgCl2 and pH 4, PA is deprotonated and Mg2+ is binding to the COO– headgroup in a 1:1 bidentate mode coordination. Most interesting, when a 0.2 mL aliquot of salmon testes DNA is added to the subphase at a final concentration of 8.8 nmol/L, the isotherm changes (purple curve, triangles). Most notable is that the measured MMA lift-off (∼27 Å2/molecule) for PA–Mg2+–DNA complex at 0.4 M and pH 4 MgCl2 subphase is noticeably larger, which is a well-known indicator for the presence of bulkier molecular complexes and/or penetration of DNA at the air–water interface after DNA injection, followed by rearrangement of the monolayer. −

There is a clear Mg2+ concentration dependence on the DNA interactions with the PA monolayer. For all MgCl2 subphase concentrations, in the absence of DNA, the initial surface pressure of the PA monolayer prior to DNA injection was kept at 10 mN/m. MMA at 10 mN/m for 0.05 M is ∼ 17 Å2/ molecule, whereas at 1.0 M it is ∼ 18 Å2/ molecule, and for all the other concentrations, the MMA falls between these values. Upon injection of DNA into the subphase containing Mg2+, to obtain an 8.8 nmol/L of final DNA concentration (see Supporting Information for additional details), a measurable increase in the MMA of the monolayer is observed. The MMA increases as a function of the Mg2+ concentration. The percent area increase of MMA is 3%, 8%, 10%, 15%, 23%, 28%, and 27% for 0.05 M, 0.1M, 0.2M, 0.3M, 0.4M, 0.6, and 1.0 M, respectively. The percent area increase in MMA as a function of Mg2+ concentration gradually increases and then plateaus toward higher Mg2+ concentrations (Figure (c)). Previous studies have shown that cation binding to the carboxylic acid headgroup of PA monolayers increases with cation concentration until a plateau is reached. This trend in cation concentration for Mg2+is also observed here with increase in the PA monolayer expansion in the presence of DNA in the subphase due to the cation-assisted interaction of DNA with PA at the surface. In contrast, there were no changes in the MMA lift-off for a PA monolayer on neat water following the injection of DNA was observed (see Figure S2). This confirms that DNA is not surface-active and does not complex to the PA monolayer in the absence of Mg2+.

Furthermore, there is a 10% increase in MMA when Ca2+ is present as shown in Figure (subphase CaCl2, pH 4), followed by fluctuations that eventually stabilize around a 3 – 4% increase suggesting more complex interactions, thus showing that Ca2+ also forms complexes between palmitic acid and DNA. However, there is less of an expansion in the monolayer compared to that of Mg2+. In contrast to interactions between Mg2+ and PA headgroups in a 1:1 stoichiometry, the CaCl2 interactions differ. Ca2+ primarily interacts with the PA carboxylate headgroup via 2Ca2+:1COO– bridging complexes. Similarly to Mg2+, under these conditions PA is expected to be deprotonated. Additionally, the binding affinity of Mg2+ to nitrogenous bases of DNA is higher than that of Ca2+, whereas binding of Mg2+ to phosphate is lower than that of Ca2+. ,,, With PA, the binding affinity of Ca2+ is higher than that of Mg2+. , Therefore, our results shed light onto a complex interplay between these various interactions governing DNA complexation at the interface and its surface propensity. Although the surface pK a of long-chain fatty acids has been proposed to be higher at the air/water interface, , PA remains deprotonated in the presence of Na+ cations at pH 4 as well due to long-range electrostatic interactions between the hydrated cation and the headgroup of PA. However, in contrast to divalent cations, in the presence of monovalent cations (Na+), the MMA of the monolayer decreased, indicating a monolayer destabilization and PA desorption from the surface due to changes in subphase. These data show that divalent cations stabilize the interactions of DNA with PA and thus control the surface propensity of DNA in these aqueous solutions.

Percent area increase, i.e., % increase in MMA, of PA monolayers held at 10 mN/m upon injection of DNA to the subphase containing chloride solutions of divalent cations (either Mg2+ or Ca2+). In the presence of monovalent cations (Na+), the mean molecular area of the monolayer did not increase; rather, it decreased indicating PA monolayer destabilization and PA desorption from the surface. Each experiment was run in triplicate, and values for the MMA lift-off and maximum percent area increase were less than 5%.

To further confirm these interactions, a sample was carefully collected from the air–water interface to mimic the collection and analysis of the sea-surface microlayer (see Figure S3 and associated text in Supporting Information for more details). These collected samples were then deposited onto Au-coated Si wafers and analyzed with contact-mode AFM-PTIR (atomic force microscopy – photothermal infrared) spectroscopy (Figures ) to detect evidence of the palmitic acid–Mg2+– DNA complex. This approach was taken to enhance the signal of the complex over the entire infrared region as interference from gas-phase water bands in infrared reflectance absorbance spectra (IRRAS) can sometimes mask weaker infrared absorption bands. Figure (a) shows the deflection and height images of the deposited film collected from the air–water interface and concentrated into a one μm substrate-deposited drop that is approximately 250 nm in height. The PTIR spectrum shows spectral signatures corresponding to both PA and DNA (Figure (b), Table S1). IR vibrational frequencies corresponding to COO– of palmitate at 1556 cm–1 for asymmetric stretching vibration indicating deprotonation of PA at the interface in the presence of Mg2+ were observed. This observation is consistent with previous studies conducted for palmitic acid with divalent cations. , Additionally, the symmetric stretching mode of COO– is observed at 1410 cm–1 indicating the presence of bidentate bridging complexes as the prominent species (Δυ of COO– asymmetric and symmetric stretching = 146 cm–1). ,, A peak at 1478 cm–1 was assigned to CH2 scissor mode of the alkyl chain. , A weaker feature at 1192 cm–1 is assigned to a combination of CH2 wagging, C–C skeletal vibrations, and C–O stretching vibrations. ,, The IR vibrational frequencies observed corresponding to the presence of DNA includes sugar phosphate backbone vibrations at 977 (C–C stretch), 1108 (PO2 – symmetric stretch), 1220 (PO2 – asymmetric stretch) and 1260 cm–1 (PO2 – asymmetric stretch), and nitrogenous base vibrations at 1424 cm–1 (CN), 1579 cm–1 (in-plane ring vibrations), and 1653 cm–1 (CO stretch). , Two vibrational frequencies for the asymmetric vibrational mode of the phosphate group are observed as water uptake from humidity allows both hydrated and dehydrated forms of DNA to be present. The asymmetric vibration of phosphate is highly sensitive to the hydration state, whereas the symmetric vibration is not. PTIR peaks corresponding to H-bonded OH and NH groups were observed near 3200, 3400, and 3550 cm–1. Additionally, a peak at 1323 cm–1 in the PA-Mg2+-DNA PTIR spectrum is assigned to symmetric deformation of methyl groups from thymine of DNA and possibly from PA as well. Moreover, there are strong C–H stretching modes observed at 2820, 2872, 2913, and 2927 cm–1 in the PTIR spectrum which are arising from both PA and DNA.

There are potentially differential interactions of individual nucleotides and nucleotide sequences within DNA that can impact the surface propensity of DNA at the air–water interface. Previous studies have shown base-specific interactions of DNA and mononucleotides on hard surfaces such as minerals. − To test whether there are differential interactions, smaller DNA oligomers of known base sequences and their complexation to a PA monolayer in the presence of 0.4 M MgCl2 at pH 4 were further investigated. The selected oligomers can demonstrate whether DNA sequence and %GC content matter in these interactions. Various genomes contain significantly different GC% content, e.g., salmon testes DNA: 41.2%; humans: ∼ 41%; Plasmodium falciparum: ∼ 19%; , Escherichia coli: ∼ 50%. Additionally, for longer strands of DNA, while the nucleobase sequence is distributed with pronounced variation, GC spikes can be seen in certain regions. Therefore, it is important to understand the selective and differential surface adsorption and surface propensities of different DNA segments.

Figure shows the same experiments done for salmon testes DNA but now for shorter oligomers of different sequencing, including ATATAT, AGTGCT, and GGATCC, and their complementary strands. We find that there is an inverse correlation of the %GC content for DNA oligomers and surface propensity. For ATATAT, AGTGCT, and GGATCC, the %GC content is 0%, 50%, and 66.6%, respectively. For the ATATAT sequence, with %GC of zero, there are clear increases in the MMA measured for palmitic acid monolayers upon injection of DNA into the solution and then an even greater increase upon a second injection of DNA. A second injection was proceeded to determine if more DNA could complex to PA at the surface. In contrast, when %GC is at the highest value of 66.6%, oligomers were unable to form a stable monolayer at the air–water interface as evidenced by a decrease in the percent MMA. The intermediate %GC of 50%, i.e., AGTGCT, does show surface propensity, as measured by an increase of the MMA of the palmitic acid monolayer, but the MMA increase is less than that for the ATATAT oligomer.

Percent area increase, i.e., % MMA increase, of PA monolayers held at 10 mN/m upon injection of oligomers to the subphase containing 0.4 M MgCl2 at pH 4. Oligomer complexation to PA monolayers was highest for oligomers with no GC content and decreased with increasing GC content (50% and 66.6%). Each experiment was run in triplicate, and values for the MMA liftoff and maximum percent area increase were all within less than 5%.

The enhanced surface propensity for ATATAT could arise from a number of factors including the relatively higher hydrophobicity of A and T bases compared to G and C. Additionally, while Mg2+ can bind to both G-C and A-T bases of DNA, interactions differ between bases, yielding different affinities at the air–water interface. For example, experimental evidence from infrared analysis of aqueous solutions suggests inner-sphere complexation of Mg2+ with ring nitrogen of guanine is apparent, whereas only weak metal-DNA interactions were seen for thymine. Therefore, it can be speculated that the weaker interactions of Mg2+ in solution with A-T allow these segments of DNA to be cation-bridged with PA, whereas G-C segments likely chelate with both charges of Mg2+, thus deriving the strong A-T preference in surface propensity. Such dependency on base sequence in DNA cation-bridging with PA monolayers along with the higher binding affinity for Mg2+ than Ca2+ (Figure ) suggests that molecular-level interactions among DNA, divalent cation, and the fatty acid monolayer may involve interactions not only with the phosphate backbone of DNA but also the bases themselves. Future studies will address these issues of the nature and energetics of sequence-dependent interactions, energetics, molecular organization, and mechanisms involved in these different interactions.

Overall, the current study not only expands our understanding of DNA behavior at the air–water interface through a cation-assisted surfactant templating model but additionally provides new insights into preferential interactions for some DNA sequences that can drive DNA to the surface. These interactions impact DNA mobility, transformations, and stability in aqueous aerosols and have potential implications for human health as well as the fate and transformations of DNA in the environment.
