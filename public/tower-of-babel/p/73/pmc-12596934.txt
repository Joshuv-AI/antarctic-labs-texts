ThiF‐Like Enzyme Chemistry in Primary and Secondary Metabolism
Authors: Keelie S. Butler, Anshul Rajput, Jonathan R. Chekan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ThiF‐like proteins are members of the widespread E1‐like enzyme superfamily. The eponymous ThiF enzyme was first described in thiamin biosynthesis as part of Escherichia coli's primary metabolism, and homologous proteins have been subsequently discovered in secondary metabolism. These ThiF‐like enzymes are united in their defining ability to perform nucleotidylation of a carboxyl group to generate an activated, electrophilic intermediate, a feature it shares with the structurally related ubiquitin‐activating enzymes. From here, an array of different nucleophiles are used across distinct biosynthetic pathways to yield diverse structural scaffolds. In this review, we discuss various ThiF‐like enzymes that perform nucleotidylation to facilitate a diverse array of interesting and rare chemistry on different types of substrates, as well as showcase some of the shared structural features.

Introduction

The E1‐like enzyme superfamily (IPR035985) is a conserved protein family found throughout the tree of life.[ 1 ] Using NTPs, these proteins nucleotidylate carboxylic acids, normally on protein or peptides, to enable downstream modification. E1 itself is part of the eukaryotic ubiquitin‐modification pathway where proteins are targeted for degradation upon addition of the small protein ubiquitin (Figure 1 ).[ 2 ] The pathway is composed of three key enzymes, E1, E2, and E3, wherein E1 uses ATP to catalyze adenylation of the C‐terminal glycine of ubiquitin.[ 2 ] This adenylated intermediate is then subject to nucleophilic attack by the thiolate of a conserved Cys in E1, generating a covalently attached ubiquitin through a thioester. Next, E2 performs a transthiolation reaction to capture the ubiquitin and is followed by E3, which ligates ubiquitin to the target protein, most often through the side chain of lysine (Figure 1). This process can be repeated multiple times to create polyubiquitin chains that designate proteins for degradation by the proteasome.[ 3 , 4 – 5 ]

E1 ubiquitin‐activating enzyme as part of the ubiquitination cascade.

Beyond this well‐characterized eukaryotic ubiquitylation system exists a complex evolutionary history of divergent enzymes and pathways. Defined as the ThiF family (PF00899) or ThiF/MoeB/HesA family (IPR045886), these proteins contain the same Rossmann fold and structural features as E1[ 6 – 10 ] but operate outside of eukaryotic ubiquitylation. Decades of study have revealed that these ThiF‐like enzymes are responsible for a wide range of chemical modifications, such as oxazolone or phosphoramidate formation. Moreover, they appear in completely disparate biosynthetic pathways suggesting that ThiF‐like enzymes are prone to neofunctionalization. Despite the array of structural scaffolds generated by ThiF‐like enzymes, they are unified mechanistically. As members of the E1‐superfamily, they utilize ATP or another NTP to nucleotidylate a carboxylic acid. This activated moiety is then primed for substitution by a wide range of nucleophiles. The resulting pathway products have been found to have important biological activities, such as enzyme cofactors or antibiotics. Notably, from a biosynthetic perspective, the types of reactions catalyzed by ThiF‐like enzymes show a range of interesting NTP‐dependent chemistry contained within natural products biosynthesis, making them intriguing enzymes for the discovery of undescribed transformations. Here, we review the chemistry of characterized ThiF‐like enzymes, emphasizing the biochemical details of the reaction and structural diversity that can be generated from a common nucleotidylated intermediate.

Primary Metabolism

ThiF‐like enzymes, including the eponymous ThiF, were first identified in primary metabolism. Responsible for the biosynthesis of universal enzyme cofactors or specific tRNA modifications, these ThiF‐like enzymes largely follow the mechanism of E1 in the ubiquitination pathway (Figur 1).

Thiamin

Thiamin pyrophosphate is a crucial component for all living organisms and is produced by most prokaryotes and some eukaryotes. As humans, we do not biosynthesize thiamin (Figure 2 ) and instead require it in our diets as vitamin B1. Containing a thiazole ring with a pyrimidine group, thiamin is used as an enzyme cofactor in a wide variety of biological and metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate metabolism and the Krebs cycle.[ 11 , 12 ] While decades of research have fully elucidated its multistep biogenesis, we will focus only on ThiF's role here.

ThiF and ThiS are involved in the early steps of thiamin biosynthesis and are needed for construction of the thiazole ring (Figure 2). Research conducted in the late 1990s by the Begley group noted similarities between enzymes in the thiamin biosynthetic gene cluster and those involved in the human ubiquitylation system. In particular, ThiF is a 27 kDa protein that shares amino acid sequence similarity to the ubiquitin‐activating enzyme E1, which catalyzes ATP‐dependent adenylation of the C‐terminus of ubiquitin (Figure 1).[ 13 ] Moreover, ThiS possessed a Gly–Gly C‐terminus, reminiscent of the erythrocyte ubiquitin. Together, these suggested a pathway wherein ThiF could adenylate the C‐terminus of ThiS to enable downstream formation of a thiocarboxylate (Figure 2). Indeed, heterologous expression of ThiFS showed they formed a stable complex and that ThiS could be adenylated in vitro as observed by mass spectrometry.[ 13 ] In E. coli, the ThiS adenylate then serves as a substrate for the sulfur transferase ThiI that uses persulfide to form an acyl disulfide with ThiS (Figure 2A).[ 14 ] Next, ThiF catalyzes a disulfide exchange reaction to release ThiI, thereby forming a covalent adduct between Cys184 of ThiF and the C‐terminus of ThiS.[ 15 ]

Structural studies of ThiF and ThiF–ThiS complex have been performed to further understand the adenylation and complex formation.[ 9 , 10 ] The ThiF–ThiS complex forms a heterotetramer, with one chain of ThiF binding one chain of ThiS. ThiF alone forms a homodimer, with the N‐terminal of one monomer interacting with the C‐terminal of the other to form one complete active site (Figure 2B). As it relates to the binding of ThiS to ThiF, there are a few main structural components. With regard to the ThiF, there is a ‘crossover loop’ that consists of residues 174–186. This loop is important for the ThiF‐ThiS protein–protein interaction and also contains the key Cys184 residue needed for acyl disulfide formation. In addition to this ThiF crossover loop, there is a group of four β‐sheets that interact with Ala35, Ile40, Leu58, and Phe60 of ThiS, and aids in complex formation with ThiF.

As a typical Rossmann fold, ThiF's ATP binding domain is well conserved and consistent with ubiquitin activating proteins (Figure 2B).[ 9 , 10 ] A glycine‐rich hydrophobic pocket binds the adenine and ribose portion of ATP. The α‐ and β‐phosphate groups interact with the side chains of Arg70, Gln71, Lys83, and Asp127, while the γ‐phosphate forms hydrogen bonds with Ser66 residues and forms a salt bridge with the Arg11 residue from the other ThiF monomer. When the C‐terminus of ThiS binds into the active site, the Ala64 residue of ThiS interacts with the Arg132 residue of the ThiF via hydrogen bonds to keep the C‐terminus in position for adenylation. From there, the carbonyl of the adenylated ThiS is activated by hydrogen bonding in the glycine‐rich pocket of ThiF for nucleophilic addition by ThiI persulfide.[ 9 ]

Molybdopterin

Another ThiF‐like enzyme is MoeB, which is found in molybdenum cofactor (Moco) biosynthesis. Overall, there are nine genes responsible for production of Moco, and the pathway can be broken down into three overall processes (Figure 3 ).[ 16 , 17 – 18 ] First is the generation of cyclic pyranopterin monophosphate (cPMP), then the synthesis of molybdopterin (MPT), and finally the addition of molybdenum into the MPT to form a molybdenum–MPT complex. MoeB, one of the nine genes, functions similar to ThiF. Analogous to thiamin biosynthesis, MoeB forms a heterotetramer complex with its target protein MoaD[ 19 ] and adenylates the Gly–Gly C‐terminus of MoaD in an ATP‐dependent manner (Figure 3).[ 20 ] Similar to ThiS in thiamin biosynthesis, MoaD then undergoes sulfuration, although evidence does not support that the MoeB is responsible for this sulfuration, but rather a sulfur transferase.[ 18 , 20 ] After the completion of this step, thiocarboxylate‐MoaD releases from MoeB and forms a complex with MoaE to finish the MPT synthesis (Figure 3). Overall, this MoeB–MoaD complex mirrors the ThiF‐ThiS pathway and mechanism, further showcasing the structural and functional similarities for these ThiF‐like enzymes.

tRNA Cyclic N6‐Threonylcarbamoyladenosine

Transfer ribonucleic acid (tRNA) plays pivotal role in primary metabolism because of its function in one of the most fundamental cellular processes, protein synthesis. Modifcations to tRNA can alter functions and improve fidelity, such as N6‐threonylcarbamoyladenosine (t6A) found in tRNAs that code for ANN.[ 21 ] However, t6A can be modified to an oxazolone form called “cyclic t6A” (ct6A, Figure 4 ).[ 22 ] Detailed studies across strains lacking ct6A identified TcdA, a ThiF‐like enzyme (also annotated as CsdL), as responsible for the dehydration of t6A to form ct6A.[ 22 ] Additionally, a TcdA crystal structure revealed that its ATP‐bound N‐terminal sub‐domain resembles the ATP‐bound Rossmann fold of ThiF‐like enzymes displaying 33% and 29% of amino acid sequence identity for MoeB and E. coli ThiF, respectively.[ 23 ] However, the C‐terminal sub‐domain of TcdA remained unrelated to any of these as it lacks the Zn‐binding cysteines, while in the case of MoeB and ThiF, four conserved cysteines are found to be a characteristic feature of a Zn‐binding domain in ThiF‐like proteins. Taking into account these similiarities with MoeB/ThiF and findings in previous study, the overall reaction of t6A dehydration and cyclization to ct6A likely takes place by first an ATP‐dependent adenylation of t6A followed by a thioester formation via the catalytic cysteine of TcdA (Figure 4). Further, the subsequent carbamoyl oxygen acts as a nucleophile in its tautomeric form and attack of this electrophilic thioester leads to the formation of a ring closed oxazolone structure, ct6A (Figure 4). It should be noted that the putative Cys nucleophile is not completely conserved in potential TcdA homologs, leaving open the possibility of direct cyclization instead of a covalent thioester intermediate. Beyond the oxazolone containing ct6A, a hydantoin version has also been observed, but a direct link to a ThiF‐like enzyme is not currently known.[ 24 ]

Secondary Metabolism: RiPP Pathways

Ribosomally synthesized and post‐translationally modified peptides (RiPPs) represent one of the most rapidly growing families of natural products with over 40 distinct classes described to date.[ 25 ] RiPPs are found in a variety of organisms, both prokaryotic and eukaryotic, and have been found to exhibit a wide array of bioactivities including anticancer and antimicrobial.[ 26 ] As the name suggests, the defining feature of RiPPs is their ribosomal origin followed by further enzymatic post‐translational modifications to generate their final bioactive forms (Figure 5 ). Specifically, the substrate for these pathways, the precursor peptide, is encoded for as a gene in the DNA and ultimately translated by the ribosome. In most cases, the precursor peptide can be demarcated into an N‐terminal leader peptide responsible for recruitment of the biosynthetic enzymes and a C‐terminal core peptide that is the site of modification. In some systems, a follower peptide exists C‐terminal to the core peptide. After modification, the leader is typically proteolytically cleaved from the core to yield the mature, bioactive natural product.

General biosynthetic route for RiPP natural products.

The structural and biosynthetic heterogeneity of RiPPs has resulted in multiple classification systems. In many cases, RiPP families are defined by a conserved structural feature, such as lanthipeptides (contain the class defining lanthionine linkage)[ 27 ] or lasso peptides (contain the class defining lasso 3D‐scaffold).[ 28 ] However, other classes were formed based on bioinformatic methods and are therefore defined by the function of a specific enzyme family. For example, the radical SAMs (rSAMs/RiPP‐RaSs),[ 29 , 30 ] P450s/biarylitides,[ 31 , 32 ] MNIOs,[ 33 ] and burpitides[ 34 ] each contain diverse chemical modifications but are unified by modification of a class defining enzyme. Decades of work on ThiF‐like enzymes indicate that they should be viewed as a RiPP superfamily with diverse modifications unified by the primacy of ThiF‐like enzymes in the biosynthetic pathway. In the following, we discuss each known ThiF‐like enzyme found in a RiPP pathway, organized by the type of modification it installs.

Phosphoramidates

Natural products containing phosphoramidate (N—P) bonds are rare in nature.[ 35 ] Instead, N‐P bonds are more often observed in proteins, such as in the phosphorylation of histidine.[ 36 , 37 ] This trend is due in part to the ease with which the N–P bond can be hydrolyzed. While they are uncommon in natural products, the phosphoramidates that have been successfully isolated and studied have shown interesting chemistry and applications with regards to bioactivity. For example, fosfazinomycin B is an antifungal compound with both a phosphoramidate and hydrazide (Figure 6A).[ 38 , 39 ] Others, such as agrocin 84[ 40 ] and dinogunellin[ 41 ] contain phosphoramidates formed by the attachment of a nucleoside. In each of these cases, the exact route of phosphoramidate installation is unknown. In contrast, the metalloprotease inhibitor phosphoramidon contains a phosphoramidate formed by the N‐phosphorylation of a dipeptide by a dedicated kinase (Figure 6).[ 42 ]

Phosphoramidate Bond Formation by ThiF‐Like Enzymes

A ThiF‐like enzyme has been documented as the modifying enzyme for the N–P bond installation for phosphoramidate nucleotide antibiotics. The most notable of these is microcin C7, a heptapeptide with adenosine monophosphate (AMP) attached at the C‐terminus (Figure 6A).[ 43 ] As a modified and intact peptide, microcin C7 has no bioactivity but is able to enter cells. It is not until it is inside the cell and proteolyzed to yield an aspartyl adenylate analog that it exhibits toxicity by inhibiting aspartyl‐tRNA synthetase, halting translation.[ 44 , 45 ] The biosynthetic gene cluster for microcin C7 consists of six genes:mccABCDEF (Figure 6B).[ 46 – 48 ] The precursor peptide (MccA) is a heptapeptide with the amino acid sequence Met‐Arg‐Thr‐Gly‐Asn‐Ala‐Asn, while the ThiF‐like adenylyltransferase (MccB) is the first enzyme to act on the precursor peptide. Detailed biosynthetic studies have shown that MccB uses two equivalents of ATP to create the phosphoramidate bond.[ 6 , 47 ] The first reaction carried out by MccB involves nucleophilic addition of the Asn7 carboxylate to the α‐phosphate of ATP to create the MccA‐AMP intermediate (Figure 6C). This adenylate intermediate then undergoes an intramolecular rearrangement when the amide nitrogen from Asn7 attacks the C‐terminal carbonyl, eliminating AMP and resulting in a succinimide intermediate. The succinimidyl nitrogen then attacks a second equivalent of ATP at the α‐phosphate, forming the characteristic phosphoramide bond. This succinimide ring is finally hydrolyzed by water through a regiospecific ring opening reaction, resulting in the peptidyl‐acyl‐N‐P‐adenosine MccB product (Figure 6C). Further, a SAM‐dependent enzyme MccD then transfers a 3‐amino‐3 carboxypropyl group (ACP) to the phosphoramidate oxygen. This product is finally decarboxylated by MccE to yield the mature microcin C7.[ 49 ]

MccB shares structural similarity with ThiF; specifically, the C‐terminus resembles the adenylation domain of prokaryotic E1 enzymes. With regard to mechanisms, however, ThiF and MccB differ. Both enzymes catalyze C‐terminal adenylation of the substrate, but as discussed previously, the MccB enzyme performs successive adenylation reactions in order to form the phosphoramidate bond. Additionally, the ThiF substrate (ThiS) is larger than 8 kDa, but the MccB uses a significantly smaller heptapeptide substrate.

To account for these differences, two X‐ray crystallography structural studies were completed on MccB to further understand its biochemical details (Figure 7 ).[ 6 , 7 ] MccB is found as a dimer, with each monomer composed of a ThiF‐like adenylation domain and an N‐terminal domain initially described as a ‘peptide clamp’.[ 6 ] Subsequent analysis and characterization defined this N‐terminal domain that composes most of the peptide clamp as a RiPP precursor peptide recognition element (RRE).[ 50 ] This RRE domain is widespread in RiPP biosynthetic pathways and responsible for binding the leader portion of the precursor peptide in most cases.[ 50 , 51 ] Within MccB, this peptide clamp domain consists of an RRE domain and a "crossover loop" structure observed in E1 enzymes. MccA docks in this pocket that is formed across the two monomers of MccB's dimeric structure (Figure 7A). Notably, N‐formyl‐MccA is the optimal substrate for MccB, and a separate structural study showed that the formyl‐group is accommodated by a pocket comprised of the C‐terminal residues of one monomer, Val240 and Ile243, while the N‐terminus residues of the other monomer, notably Gly23 and Glu26, form the other part of the pocket (Figure 7B).[ 7 ] The "crossover loop" is responsible for connecting the active sites of MccB, and the Tyr268 and Val264 residues interact with the MccA substrate.[ 7 ] In E1 enzymes, the Arg72 is important for ubiquitin substrate recognition, interacting with the crossover loop. Based on structural studies of MccB, Arg2 of MccA is analogous to the Arg72 in ubiquitin, interacting with residues of the MccB crossover loop, and with Tyr14 and Glu26 peptide clamp residues required for substrate binding and alignment (Figure 7B).[ 7 ]

Structure of MccB. A) Overview of dimeric structure. B) Crossover loop and MccA‐binding site. C) ATP‐binding site. Structures generated from PDB:3H5N and 6OM4.

Focusing on the active site, Tyr239 and Asp214 appear to be critical for the bifunctional activity of MccB (Figure 7C). Tyr239 interacts with both the ATP α‐phosphate and C‐terminus of MccA, while Asp214 coordinates a Mg2+. Mutation to either of these diminishes the ability of MccB to perform successive adenylation reactions. Compared to other ThiF‐like enzymes, D214 is observed at a corresponding position, but the Y239 residue is not. Instead, typical E1 enzymes have an Ala or Thr residue to orient the Gly–Gly C‐terminal of the protein substrate for adenylation.

Beyond E. coli's MccB, bioinformatic analysis and detailed biochemical studies have shown the presence and activity of several MccB homologs with their cognate substrates, such as ones from Bartonella washoensis, Helicobacter pylori, Lactobacillus johnsonii, and Streptococcus thermophilus. [ 52 ] Other divergent MccB enzymes that catalyze formation of alternative phosphoramidate bonds have also been identified (Figure 8 ). For example, in the Bacillus amyloliquefaciens gene cluster, the precursor peptide MccABam is a 19 amino acid peptide with the C‐terminal Asn as seen with the MccA from E. coli.[ 53 ] Curiously, MccBBam contains two domains, an N‐terminal ThiF‐like domain (MccBNTD Bam) and a C‐terminal methyltransferase‐like domain (MccBCTD Bam). The first reactions carried out by MccBBam are similar to the first steps of the original MccB. However, instead of utilizing ATP as a co‐substrate, MccBNTD Bam shows a preference for CTP (Figure 8). Next, MccBCTD Bam functions not as a methyltransferase, but a carboxymethyl transferase to modify the cytidine moiety using carboxy‐S‐adenosyl‐l‐methionine (cxSAM).

Microcin C7 (McC) analogs from B. amyloliquefaciens (McCBam) and Y. pseudotuberculosis (McCYps).

In another example, a Yersinia pseudotuberculosis biosynthetic gene cluster was found to combine aspects of both the original E. coli McC and B. amyloliquefaciens McCBam pathways.[ 52 ] The gene cluster consists of eight gene mccABCDE 1 E 2 SX Yps.[ 54 ] The precursor peptide, MccAYps, is 42 amino acids in length and has the C‐terminal Asn, while MccBYps is bifunctional with an N‐terminal ThiF‐like domain and a C‐terminal carboxymethyl transferase domain, similar to the MccBBam. Consistent with this observation, MccBYps was found to modify the C‐terminal Asn of MccAYps to generate the phosphoramidate using CTP followed by carboxymethylation (Figure 8). Notably, MccBYps was also found to accept ATP in vitro, indicating a level of promiscuity for some of these ThiF‐like enzymes.[ 52 ] The remaining steps to MccAYps maturation involve MccDYps and MccE1 Yps, which are hypothesized to act in a homologous nature to the MccD and MccE from E. coli and install the aminopropyl group.

Indolizidine N‐Heterocyles

Nitrogen heterocycles are one of the most abundant motifs observed in nature and make up the building blocks of life.[ 55 ] While nitrogen heterocycles are seen in a variety of scaffolds, one notable example in natural products is indolizidine. This system is composed of a five‐membered pyrrolidine ring fused with a six‐membered piperidine ring with a common nitrogen atom.[ 56 ] These are found in various plants, marine animals, and fungi displaying a plethora of biological activities.[ 57 ] Related, indolizidone is much rarer with the same core bicyclic indolizidine ring system, but a carbonyl group incorporated into any of the two rings (Figure 9A). Very few examples of these indolizidone containing alkaloids are reported[ 58 ] and little is known of their biosynthesis, as is the case with spirodactylone (Figure 9A).[ 59 ]

Indolizidone Bicyclic Scaffold Formation by ThiF‐Like Enzymes

The RiPP natural product pantocin A was first isolated in 2003 from Pantoea agglomerans, an important biocontrol agent that prevents the growth of fire blight causing Erwinia amylovora through inhibition of l‐histidinol phosphate aminotransferase.[ 60 , 61 – 63 ] Structurally, pantocin A is a small tripeptide (Figure 9A) that is highlighted by an unusual 3,5,6,7‐tetrahydroindolizine azabicycle. Identification of the biosynthetic genes using a cosmid library revealed a short four‐gene cluster (Figure 9B) composed of a precursor peptide (PaaP), ThiF‐like enzyme (PaaA), an α‐ketoglutarate dependent oxidase (PaaB), and an efflux pump (PaaC).[ 62 ] Curiously, comparison of the PaaA precursor peptide to pantocin A indicated that the three‐amino acid core of Glu–Glu‐Asn (E–E‐N) is present near the center of the sequence, an unusual feature of RiPPs. In vitro investigations revealed that PaaA (ThiF‐like) facilitates the formation of indolizidone bicyclic core of pantocin A via ATP‐dependent intramolecular cyclization through two dehydrations followed by one decarboxylation.[ 8 ]

Mechanistically, two potential biosynthetic pathways have been proposed for the PaaA‐catalyzed transformation (Figure 9C). One starts with the amide addition of peptide backbone to activated Glu16‐side chain to yield cyclic imide intermediate. This is subsequently followed by a Claisen‐like condensation with the enolate of Glu17‐side chain (pathway a). Alternatively, another pathway could follow the reverse order with the Claisen‐like condensation between the side chains of two adjacent Glu‐residues (Glu16 and Glu17) occurring first to yield an intermediate ketone that is subsequently attacked by the peptide backbone (pathway b). The same mechanism can be reasonably written starting from activation of Glu17, followed by its condensation as the first step. Eventually, both pathways would lead to enamine tautomers of the same bicyclic indolizidone core. Then, a subsequent decarboxylation of this enamine intermediate followed by oxidation catalyzed by PaaB and/or PaaA would yield the bicyclic pantocin A precursor. Finally, this resulting precursor would undergo to proteolytic cleavage to yield the final mature product, pantocin A (Figure 9C).

Since the pantocin A core (EEN) was found near to the middle of its precursor peptide (PaaP) sequence (Figure 9B), it was speculated that both the leader and follower peptide would be critical in the PaaA‐catalyzed transformation. To investigate this, truncated PaaP variants were synthesized and tested against PaaA.[ 8 ] The results highlighted that both leader and follower peptides are critically important for PaaA enzyme activity. While the N‐terminal peptide sequence was described to be essential for PaaA‐catalyzed post‐translational processing, removal of the follower sequence still permits the first condensation reaction to proceed. Later, mRNA display‐based technique was applied to acquire more insights into the PaaA–PaaP interaction, revealing a broad enzyme promiscuity.[ 64 ] Additionally, this work also proved to be insightful into the enzymatic timing of Glu‐residues modifications by the use of a 13C‐labeled Glu17‐PaaP. The resulting loss of the labeled residue suggested that Glu16 is the preferred initial substrate for adenylation followed by Glu17 cyclization and subsequent decarboxylation.[ 64 ]

While PaaA is in the same family as other ThiF‐like enzymes, these enzymes make modifications to different areas of a peptide. The MccB modifies the C‐terminus,[ 47 ] whereas PaaA modifies residues near the middle of the peptide, meaning that the active site must differ to allow room for the follower peptide sequence. Indeed, determination of the PaaA structure by X‐ray crystallography indicated that it shared significant homology with MccB: they both formed homodimers with an adenylation domain and an RRE peptide‐binding domain.[ 8 ] Upon further investigation, however, some differences in the domains were observed. The adenylation domain of PaaA had a larger active site cavity compared to MccB, likely to accommodate the follower region of PaaP. Additionally, the peptide‐binding domain was more disordered in PaaA than in MccB, but further experiments revealed significant PaaP–PaaA interaction with this region that induced stabilization.[ 8 ]

Thiolactones

Thiolactones are an abundant motif within the context of natural product biosynthesis with many of the resulting products displaying beneficial bioactivities.[ 65 ] Reflecting this diversity, numerous enzyme classes are responsible for the formation of thiolactones. For example, the thiolactone containing autoinducing peptide (AIP) natural products function as quorum sensing molecules,[ 66 ] with the thiolactone itself formed from a cysteine endoprotease (Figure 10A).[ 67 , 68 ] In contrast, the thioester in the fatty acid synthase inhibitor thiolactomycin is biosynthesized using a member of the CYP450 family (Figure 10A).[ 69 , 70 ] Beyond these, other examples include α/β‐hydrolases and thioesterases.[ 65 ]

Thiolactone Formation by ThiF‐Like Enzymes

Streptococci are rich in RiPP gene clusters under the control of quorum sensing‐based regulation. In particular, several studies have demonstrated that radical S‐adenosyl l‐methionine (RaS) enzymes found within these pathways are able to catalyze unusual side‐chain crosslinking reactions.[ 71 , 72 ] As part of their bioinformatic efforts, the Seyedsayamdost Lab identified a gene cluster of interest consisting of the quorum sensing regulatory genes, a precursor peptide (GrcA), a ThiF‐like enzyme (GrcB), two RaS enzymes (GrcC and GrcD), a standalone RRE (GrcE), and a transporter (GrcF) (Figure 10B).[ 73 ] In vivo reconstitution of the biosynthetic pathway and subsequent elucidation of the product by spectroscopic methods revealed the presence of three modifications: epimerization of Thr32 to l‐allo‐threonine by GrcC, thiolactone formation by GrcB, and dehydrogenation of His34 by GrcDE.

As this was the first time a thiolactone was shown to be formed by a ThiF‐like enzyme, the authors sought to probe its function in detail. Structurally, GrcB contains both an N‐terminal RRE domain and a C‐terminal adenylation domain similar to other ThiF‐like enzymes found in RiPP pathways. In vitro assays showed that the GrcB required ATP and Mg2+ for activity and that both unmodified GrcA or GrcC‐epimerized GrcA could serve as substrates.[ 73 ] GrcB appeared to have a slight preference for epimerized GrcA suggesting this is likely the authentic substrate. Mechanistically, it was hypothesized that GrcB would use ATP to activate the carboxylate side chain of the Glu35 residue in the precursor peptide to form an adenylated intermediate. This is identical to pantocin A biosynthesis. Then, the nucleophilic addition of the C‐terminal Cys39 thiolate to the Glu35‐AMP completed the formation of the thiolactone moiety via elimination of AMP (Figure 10C).

Cyclic Imide

Cyclic imide scaffolds are somewhat commonly found in natural products.[ 74 , 75 ] They are composed of two amides with a shared nitrogen and can exist as either small ring systems or large macrocycles across diverse natural products. For example, rebeccamycin is a well‐known naturally occurring indolocarbazole‐based cytotoxin containing a cyclic linkage formed by the combined action of a CYP450 and flavin‐dependent hydroxylase (Figure 11A).[ 76 ] In contrast, cycloheximide is a well‐studied member of the glutarimide‐containing polyketides which posses a cyclic imide bond installed with the help of an amidotransferase and specialized domains within the polyketide synthase (PKS) assembly line (Figure 11A).[ 77 , 78 – 79 ] Finally, succinamide and aleimide containing natural products are known to exist and can be produced as part of PKS pathways.[ 74 , 75 ] Notably, both microcin C7′s phosphoramidate bond and pantocin A's indolizidone scaffold formation proceed through a cyclic imide.

Cyclic Imide Formation by ThiF‐Like Enzymes

A recent bioinformatic study sought to characterize all the RiPP ThiF‐like enzyme chemistry in a subset of Lactobacillales (Streptococcus and Enterococcus).[ 80 ] Using a genome mining‐based effort based on MccB, the authors discovered many gene clusters containing ThiF‐like enzymes which could be grouped by conserved amino acid sequence features in the precursor peptides. Out of these, they analyzed a WWIII‐type biosynthetic gene cluster from Enterococcus faecium encoding a 21‐residue precursor peptide (EnfA), a ThiF‐Like enzyme (EnfB), and four transporters, while no peptidase was present (Figure 11B). Through an in vitro assay‐based approach, they demonstrated that this newly identified ThiF‐like enzyme EnfB catalyzes a reaction that results in the loss of water, resulting in a new molecule they named enterofaecin.[ 80 ] LC‐MS/MS and preliminary NMR studies suggested the formation of a macrocyclic imide between the C‐terminal Ile21 and the amide backbone of Ile19 (Figure 11C). Unfortunately, detailed NMR characterization and validation of the proposed structure was hindered by poor stability of enterofaecin. Alanine‐scanning mutagenesis of the WWIVI motif indicated that it could largely tolerate changes to this motif outside of two tryptophans.[ 80 ]

Indolylamide

Tryptophan modifications are common in natural product biosynthesis pathways, especially RiPPs and nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS)s. The indole ring can be readily derivatized due to the nucleophilic nature of the carbons and stabilization of radicals for oxidative reactions. For this reason, indole halogenation,[ 81 ] prenylation,[ 82 , 83 ] and crosslinking[ 84 ] reactions are all widespread in natural products. In rare cases, however, the indole nitrogen can serve as a nucleophile to form a new peptide bond, called an indolylamide. Very few examples are known to exist, with bulbiferamide[ 85 , 86 ] and psychrophilin B[ 87 , 88 ] natural products being the best described (Figure 12A). In both cases, the indolylamide has been shown to be installed by a specialized thioesterase or cyclization domain found as part of a NRPS.[ 88 , 89 – 90 ]

Indolylamide Formation by ThiF‐Like Enzymes

Inspired by the diverse chemistry displayed by ThiF‐like enzymes, our group sought to expand this protein family to uncover new chemical modifications in RiPP biosynthetic pathways. We used a global genome mining approach that search for all ThiF‐like enzymes from the NCBI database using the publicly available hidden Markov model for PF00899.[ 91 ] This was then screened for the presence of RRE domains to improve the likelihood that the ThiF‐like enzymes were found in RiPP pathways.[ 50 , 92 ] Using this pipeline, we identified a ThiF‐like biosynthetic gene cluster (ind) from the important human pathogen, Streptococcus pneumoniae. This ind cluster encodes a precursor peptide (IndA), a ThiF‐like enzyme (IndF), a peptidase (IndP), and a transporter (Figure 12B). Analysis of the IndA precursor peptide indicated a consensus NWYFI C‐terminal sequence which suggested possible macrocyclization through the C‐terminal carboxylate. Moreover, modeling with AlphaFold 3 indicated binding of the IndA leader to the RRE of IndF and threading of the C‐terminus into the active site near ATP. Heterologous expression of IndF and subsequent in vitro assays demonstrated the ATP‐dependent macrocyclization of IndA via formation of an indolylamide (Trp‐Ile) linkage.[ 91 ] Mechanistically, IndF is predicted to modify the C‐terminal carboxylate of isoleucine to afford an adenylated intermediate (Figure 12C). This is subject to nucleophilic addition by the indole nitrogen to yield the observed indolylamide macrocycle, the first example in a RiPP pathway and a new chemical space for a ThiF‐catalyzed reaction. While the bioactivity of the final product is unknown, its presence adjacent to multiple antibiotic resistance genes suggest a potential relevance to pathogenesis.

ThiF‐Like Domains for Peptide Binding

To this point, we have discussed the function of ThiF‐like enzymes that catalyze an array of different transformations in RiPP biosynthetic clusters. However, the two‐domain structure of RiPP ThiF‐like enzymes with an RRE and an adenylation domain has apparently been co‐opted into other RiPP biosynthetic clusters. Instead of performing a reaction with the C‐terminal adenylation domain, these ThiF‐like enzymes are recruited for their N‐terminal RRE domain to serve primarily a peptide binding function.[ 50 ] This is well described in linear azol(in)e‐containing peptide (LAP) biosynthetic pathways wherein a ThiF‐like enzyme forms a complex with a YcaO‐family cyclodehydratase to catalyze the formation of thiazoline and (methyl)oxazoline backbone heterocycles.[ 25 , 93 ] As part of this complex, the ThiF‐like enzyme is used to recruit the precursor peptide and modulate the activity of the heterocyclase. It does not catalyze a specific reaction.[ 94 , 95 – 96 ] Other examples that use the same scheme utilize a more distant member of the ThiF‐like enzyme family termed Ocin‐ThiF‐like.[ 97 ]

Secondary Metabolism: Non‐RiPP Pathways

In general, ThiF‐like enzymes have been found to modify peptides and proteins. As RiPPs are far from the only amino acid containing class of natural products, it stands to reason that ThiF‐like enzymes may be operational in other biosynthetic pathways, such as NRPSs.[ 98 ] However, there exists only a single validated example of a ThiF‐like enzyme found in the biosynthesis of a natural product outside of RiPPs to our knowledge. Whether this is a true representation of ThiF‐like enzyme biosynthetic abundance remains to be explored.

Oxazolone

Oxazolones, also sometimes known as azlactones, are oxidized five‐membered heterocyclic compounds containing a carbonyl, one oxygen, and one nitrogen.[ 99 , 100 ] They can exist as multiple isomers that vary in the location of the nitrogen and oxygen within the ring. In nature, the oxazolone scaffold is rare and its enzymatic synthesis in natural products was first observed in production of the RiPP methanobactin by the multinuclear nonheme iron‐dependent oxidative (MNIO) class of enzymes (Figure 13A).[ 33 , 101 ] Synthetically, the oxazolone core is used as an intermediate or observed in a few FDA approved drugs such as thozalinone (antidepressant).

Oxazolone Formation by ThiF‐Like Enzymes

In 2021, a bifunctional oxazalone‐forming ThiF‐like enzyme was discovered using a bioinformatic method for co‐occurring enzyme domains (CO‐ED).[ 102 ] The CO‐ED approach creates a network of proteins connected by multidomain similarities, which are then further annotated based on validated enzyme function from different databases (e.g BRENDA, MIBiG, or Uniprot). Using this approach, the authors focused on the oxzAB gene cluster found in Pseudoalteromonas rubra, which is composed of the N‐acyltransferase OxzA and the ThiF‐nitroreductase OxzB (Figure 13B). Isolation of the heterologous expression products of oxzAB from E. coli revealed the formation of multiple oxazolone‐containing metabolites derived from a fatty acid and tyrosine, named tyrazolones. Exploration of other clusters, such as one from Skermanella aerolata, found phenylalanine could be used in place of tyrosine to yield phenazolones. In vitro reconstitution of the tyrazolone pathway revealed that OxzA catalyzed the synthesis of N‐decanoyl‐l‐tyrosine in the presence of l‐tyrosine and decanoyl‐CoA. This served as a substrate for the ThiF‐nitroreductase OxzB. As this is a two‐domain enzyme, it is not clear which domain acts on the substrate first. With regard to the ThiF domain, the expected general mechanism uses ATP to adenylate a carboxylic acid of the N‐decanoyltyrosine which then undergoes cyclization to form the oxazolone ring‐containing nonyltyrazolone (Figure 13C).[ 102 ] At any point in the mechanism, the nitroreductase could catalyze an oxidation of the substrate. Time‐dependent analysis suggested that (E)‐nonyltyrazolone was the initial product but preferentially isomerized to (Z)‐nonyltyrazolone. Notably, this is the only ThiF‐like enzyme that acts on a small molecule and not a protein, peptide, or tRNA to our knowledge.

Summary and Outlook

This review has highlighted the ability of ThiF‐like enzymes to catalyze a notable variety of chemical modifications. Despite their product diversity, ThiF‐like enzymes are united by the use of an NTP to create a nucleotidylated intermediate as part of the catalytic mechanism. From this common activated intermediate, substitution by diverse nucleophiles including persulfide or amino acid side chains yields the variety of observed scaffolds. The products generated from ThiF‐like enzymes are not only found in the primary metabolism of enzyme cofactors but extend to the formation of bioactive natural products. Of particular note, pantocin A and members of the microcin C7 family of RiPP molecules are potent antibiotics.

The intriguing chemical diversity and bioactivity of ThiF‐like enzyme products make them exciting targets for discovery. This potential is supported by sequence similarity network analysis[ 103 , 104 ] which indicates that only a fraction of the chemical potential of this enzyme family has been characterized (Figure 14 ). However, typical genome mining efforts[ 105 ] are complicated by the fact that ThiF‐like enzymes are common in primary metabolism. Therefore, deeper bioinformatic methods are needed to identify target ThiF‐like enzymes for study, such as the fusion of ThiF‐like enzymes with RREs[ 91 ] or oxidases.[ 102 ] If successful, expansion of ThiF‐like enzyme chemistry could uncover new reactions and bioactive natural product scaffolds or open new opportunities for engineering of new molecules, drug scaffolds, and biocatalysts.
