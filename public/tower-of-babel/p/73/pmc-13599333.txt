Key challenges in communication across milestones of cancer care continuum: a systematic review
Authors: Viet Cuong Nguyen, Khoa Tran, Do-Quyen Thi Phan, Nguyen-Tuong Pham, Yao-An Shen, Chi-Long Chen, Tsai-Wei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective communication is essential in cancer care, significantly influencing patients’ emotional, psychological, and physical outcomes. As patients’ needs and goals evolve across different stages of the cancer journey, communication barriers also change. This systematic review aimed to identify key communication barriers between healthcare providers and cancer patients across the cancer care continuum.MethodsTwo reviewers systematically searched four databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science) to identify studies examining communication barriers between healthcare providers and cancer patients at specific stages of care. This review is registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251074785).ResultsA total of 139 articles were included and categorized into six distinct stages of the cancer journey. (1) During the screening phase, patients were reluctant to discuss or undergo cancer screening due to limited knowledge, the belief that cancer is unlikely in the absence of symptoms, and fear or anxiety about a potential diagnosis. (2) In the diagnosis phase, receiving cancer as ‘bad news’, combined with extensive information about treatment and prognosis, led to shock, emotional distress, and informational overload. (3) During treatment, patients found it difficult to discuss adverse effects, including fatigue, nausea, vomiting, and sexual health concerns. Many perceived these symptoms as normal indicators of treatment effectiveness and hesitated to burden their physicians. Female patients, in particular, reported difficulty discussing sexual health, especially with male physicians. (4) In the follow-up phase, limited consultation time contributed to insufficient discussions about long-term adverse effects, relapse risk, prognosis uncertainty, and individualized survivorship care planning. (5) In the recurrence phase, disclosing cancer recurrence was described by oncologists as particularly challenging, as patients often responded with anger, blame, or emotional withdrawal. (6) In end-of-life care, both patients and healthcare providers frequently delayed or avoided end-of-life conversations for various reasons.ConclusionsCommunication barriers between healthcare providers and cancer patients are multifaceted and stage-specific, arising from emotional, cognitive, and relational factors on both sides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02181-2.

Background

Communication is defined as the transmission of information through various channels, including verbal, written, graphical, or audiovisual formats. It permeates all aspects of life and is a fundamental component of cancer care [1]. Although communication serves as a central link between patients, their families, and healthcare providers, numerous challenges arise throughout the cancer care continuum [2]. Similar to other chronic diseases, cancer requires long-term engagement from both patients and healthcare professionals to optimize clinical outcomes [3]. Cancer is now widely regarded as a chronic disease; advances in early detection and therapeutic strategies have led to a growing population of cancer survivors who have completed curative treatment and require long-term follow-up care, including surveillance, prevention, and management of treatment-related adverse effects, and maintenance therapies [4].

Cancer care is a prolonged and complex trajectory, extending from diagnosis through treatment, survivorship, and in some cases, end-of-life care. Across this trajectory, patients experience distinct stages characterized by unique clinical and psychosocial features [5]. For example, during the diagnostic stage, patients are confronted with the reality of their diagnosis while navigating extensive information regarding treatment options, prognosis, and expected outcomes [6]. In contrast, the end-of-life stage pertains to patients with advanced cancer who may have a limited life expectancy ranging from hours to months [7].

The cancer journey is further characterized by an inherent unpredictability that affects patients, families, and healthcare providers, contingent upon cancer type, stage at diagnosis, and disease progression [5]. Consequently, patients at different milestones of the cancer trajectory have distinct communication needs and challenges.

Because of the complexity of the cancer journey, effective communication plays a vital role in fostering connections between cancer patients and healthcare providers, supporting patients throughout care, and achieving optimal clinical outcomes. However, communication is often hindered by multiple barriers that vary across specific milestones of the cancer journey. Therefore, this systematic review aims to identify key communication barriers between cancer patients and physicians and/or nurses within six specific milestones of the cancer trajectory: screening, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, recurrence, and end-of-life care.

Methods

Literature search

We searched for studies in the PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane databases. The keywords were provided in the supplementary information. The literature search was conducted up to June 3, 2025. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251074785).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Adult patients with a diagnosis of cancer or healthcare providers involved in oncology care; (2) Studies addressed barriers and/or challenges in communication between cancer patients and physicians or nurses; (3) Cancer patients categorized into at least one of six milestones, including screening, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, recurrence, and end-of-life care; (4) Studies were published in English.

Studies were excluded if they were: (1) Editorials, letters, reviews, conference abstracts, or study protocols; (2) duplicate publications; (3) studies without relevant data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators independently extracted the following data from the included studies in terms of (1) first authors, (2) time of publication, (3) study location, (4) method, (5) type of cancer, (6) number of participants, (7) milestone. The quality of the included papers was scored by the CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme) [8].

Data synthesis

A thematic synthesis was used to inductively analyze data extracted from the included studies [9]. The synthesis followed a three-stage approach: (a) line-by-line coding, (b) development of descriptive themes, and (c) generation of analytical themes. First, findings were coded line by line and categorized according to six predefined milestones along the cancer care continuum (screening, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, recurrence, and end-of-life care), with communication barriers between patients and physicians and/or nurses identified. Next, similar codes were grouped to form descriptive themes. Finally, analytical themes were developed by synthesizing patterns across themes and milestones to identify underlying mechanisms, contextual factors, and implications for improving communication. Microsoft Excel and RStudio version 4.4.1 facilitated data extraction, coding, and thematic development.

Results

Study selection

A total of 12,664 studies were identified from the four databases, which had 7,178 unique records. After selection, 315 articles were retrieved for full-text screening. Of these, 176 articles were excluded, resulting in a final total of 139 studies included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and quality assessment

The studies were published between 2000 and 2025 across various countries, including the USA, Australia, the UK, Canada, Italy, China, India, Germany, Korea, and Japan. The included studies focused on different cancer types, such as breast, lung, gastrointestinal, prostate, cervical, and hematological cancers. The study designs utilized cohort, cross-sectional, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies. Among these, qualitative studies were the most common design, accounting for 111 articles.

The CASP scores [8] for all included studies are presented in Table T2-T4. Among the 119 studies with qualitative (111 studies) and mixed-methods (8 studies) designs (Table T4), 104 studies (87.4%) had clear aims. Most studies used appropriate methodologies, accounting for 113 studies (95%), and the findings were considered valuable in 98 studies (82.4%). Among the 18 cross-sectional studies (Table T3), all had clearly stated research issues and appropriate methods; however, only 10 studies (55.6%) reported valuable findings.

Synthesis

Ten primary communication barriers across the six periods of the cancer journey were identified through the qualitative synthesis. Key communication barriers included reluctance to participate in cancer screening, poor understanding and distrust of screening and diagnosis, emotional distress and information misunderstanding at diagnosis, lack of patient-centered communication and shared decision-making, inadequate discussion of treatment options and adverse effects (including sexual health), anxiety and uncertainty related to prognosis, recurrence and disease progression, delayed end-of-life and goals-of-care conversations, difficulty in symptom management, limited discussion of non-standard treatment, religious or spiritual issues, and ineffective communication styles. Screening stage

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [10], screening means recognized as an undetected disease by test and examinations. Cancer screening is important reducing cancer mortality because it detects premalignant lesions and early-stage disease [11]. Nevertheless, communication barriers during the screening process remain common. A survey of 483 individuals regarding colorectal cancer screening found that 40.1% reported barriers [12].

Some patients do not discuss cancer screening with physicians because of limited knowledge about cancer screening [13–16] or the belief that they do not have cancer in the absence of symptoms [14, 16–19]. Despite an awareness of the benefits of screening, they may experience fear or anxiety about potential cancer diagnoses [12, 16, 17, 19]. In addition, some patients view screening procedures to be time-consuming, costly, uncomfortable, potentially invasive or harmful [12, 17, 20]. Feelings of embarrassment or shyness related to screening procedures involving sensitive anatomical areas, such as the breast or cervix, were also reported [17, 20].

In communication about cancer screening, some patients voiced skepticism toward physicians when recommendations or screening plans were provided [13, 21]. Others reported that physicians lacked time, did not listen to their concerns during shared decision-making, or used complex medical terminology when explaining cancer screening procedures [14, 15, 19, 21–23]. In particular, when screening results were ambiguous or abnormal, patients reported confusion, concerns about future care plans, and a need for further clarification [24–26]. Patients also reported experiencing physical and emotional discomfort when discussing sexual health-related screening, such as cervical cancer screening, HPV testing, HPV vaccination, and lifestyle issues, especially when the healthcare provider was of the opposite sex [27, 28].

Diagnosis stage

The diagnostic stage is considered the most challenging phase of the cancer journey for patients and their families, as it represents the first time they are confronted with a malignant disease. Approximately 30% of communication barriers occur during this stage [29].

Significant challenges emerged in the process of delivering bad news between healthcare providers and patients. Some healthcare providers avoided using the term “cancer” when providing diagnostic information [30–33], which may lead to misunderstanding or underestimation of the diagnosis. Another challenge concerns the recipient of bad news, as physicians differed in whether they disclosed information to the patient only, the family only, the patient first followed by the family, or the family first followed by the patient [30, 31, 34]. This variation reflects differences in cultural norms across regions and healthcare contexts, which often influence whether the patient or the family is the primary recipient of medical information.

Upon receiving a cancer diagnosis, patients experienced a complex spectrum of emotional responses, including shock, devastation, feelings of being ignored or unacknowledged, stress, uncertainty, anxiety, depression, shame, and guilt—particularly in HPV-related cancers—as well as a lack of individualized communication [20, 35–38]. These emotional reactions require timely recognition and appropriate management by healthcare providers. Emotional shock also acted as a barrier to understanding subsequent information related to treatment options, prognosis, and treatment-related toxicities following diagnosis [35, 37, 39–42].

As a result, patients frequently reported feeling overwhelmed, uncertain, unclear about their condition, and lacking sufficient time to comprehend the information provided. However, several physician-related communication barriers were also identified, including lack of language concordance, insufficient clarity and accuracy of cancer-related information, limited consultation time, overly general conversations, failure to respond to patients’ vague physical or psychological cues, lack of apology for mistakes, blunt delivery of bad news, and inappropriate attitudes or communication styles [34, 35, 38, 43–45]. These factors further compromised the effectiveness of bad news communication.

Given the substantial emotional burden and the large volume of information presented at the diagnostic stage, understanding patients’ information preferences is essential to enable healthcare providers to deliver sufficient, clear, and comprehensible information. Key informational needs reported by patients included financial concerns, treatment goals, quality of life, expectations regarding cure, prognosis, hope, disease management, emotional support, adverse effects, complementary and alternative medicine, and participation in shared treatment decision-making [36, 40, 45–54].

Awareness of patients’ preferred information enables physicians to identify relevant topics, recognize what patients already know, and organize the sequence of information delivery accordingly [35–37, 39, 45, 50, 55]. To better understand patients’ concerns at this stage, physicians should allocate adequate consultation time, ensure a quiet and private environment, and communicate with empathy [45, 46, 55, 56].

Ultimately, shared decision-making regarding care plans and treatment goals can be achieved between patients and physicians. However, some Asian patients reported confusion or uncertainty about their role in shared decision-making processes [50, 57].

Treatment stage

During the treatment stage, most communication centers on cancer treatment, including treatment options, adverse effects, outcomes, prognosis, and patients’ treatment preferences, with adverse effects being the most commonly reported concern (26.7%) [29]. Explaining treatment-related information is frequently complicated by patients’ limited awareness and knowledge of cancer, as well as differing beliefs and attitudes, particularly regarding complementary medicine, precision oncology, active surveillance, and novel therapies [32, 58–62]. Therefore, physicians are encouraged to use clear and understandable language, avoid excessive medical terminology, allow patients sufficient time to process information, and provide supplementary clarification when patients do not fully understand their treatment [63, 64].

The discussion of adverse effects constitutes another significant communication barrier, as patients frequently wish to discuss these issues with healthcare providers. However, in some cases, patients avoid discussing adverse effects with physicians or nurses. Some patients believe that adverse effects indicate treatment effectiveness; others fear wasting clinicians’ time or distracting them from primary treatment goals. Additionally, patients may worry that reporting adverse effects will lead to excessive medication use [60, 65–71] or disrupt cancer-directed therapy [65, 66, 68].

When adverse effects involve sexual and reproductive health, communication becomes particularly difficult for both patients and healthcare providers. Patients may perceive these effects as low priority or feel it is too early in the treatment course to address them. Feelings of shyness, embarrassment, and emotional discomfort further inhibit discussion, while some physicians hesitate to raise these topics due to concerns about patient discomfort. Additionally, some patients believe that sexual health falls outside the physician’s responsibility, and in cases of poor prognosis or urgent treatment needs, physicians may deprioritize discussion of sexual health issues [72–78].

Patients also expressed concerns regarding treatment outcomes and prognosis. However, when prognostic uncertainty or medical errors exist, effective communication becomes more challenging, as physicians must balance maintaining hope while avoiding unrealistic expectations or despair [60, 79–82].

Beyond providing essential treatment-related information, eliciting patients’ information preferences during the treatment stage helps healthcare providers understand patients’ existing knowledge and prevents patients from feeling overwhelmed or frustrated during communication [39, 42, 59, 60, 83]. The use of open-ended questions is an effective strategy to encourage patients to express their concerns and to reassure them that healthcare providers are willing to spend sufficient time addressing their needs.

Patients commonly reported preferences across three main domains: health-related quality of life (e.g., nutrition and daily activities), medical care (e.g., symptom management, warning signs, and follow-up plans), and prognosis (e.g., treatment outcomes and recurrence risk) [80].

At the conclusion of treatment-related communication, shared decision-making helps patients clarify their roles and responsibilities within the treatment plan. Patients expressed a desire to participate in shared decision-making when supported by clear guidance and explanations from physicians. However, high levels of anxiety may impair patients’ ability to engage effectively in decision-making processes [32, 39, 79, 81, 84, 85].

Follow-up stages

The follow-up stage refers to the period after patients have completed active cancer treatment and attend scheduled appointments with physicians for ongoing assessment, monitoring, or maintenance therapy. Despite completion of treatment, many patients continue to experience treatment-related adverse effects, particularly those affecting sexual health. For example, 12% of colorectal cancer patients in follow-up reported difficulties in discussing these issues [86]. However, these issues are often overlooked, with limited discussion during follow-up consultations due to time constraints, lack of physician-initiated communication, patients’ perception that these concerns are not a priority, sociocultural norms, feelings of embarrassment, and insufficient closeness or trust between patients and healthcare providers to discuss sensitive issues [86–94].

Beyond adverse effects, patients may continue to experience psychosocial distress related to their cancer experience. Nevertheless, some patients hesitate to disclose emotional distress with healthcare providers due to concerns about being prescribed additional medications or because physicians do not routinely inquire about emotional well-being during follow-up visits [95].

When discussing the future, patients in the follow-up stage often reported uncertainty and limited knowledge regarding signs of recurrence or genetic risk. They expressed a need for clear explanations, guidance, supportive information, and adequate time to understand these issues from healthcare providers [47, 86, 87, 96–98]. Addressing these needs may help patients overcome fear and worry and support informed decision-making during survivorship [87, 90, 91, 99].

Recurrent stage

The recurrent stage refers to the relapse or progression of cancer in patients. Cancer recurrence may be detected either when patients present to the hospital with new or worsening symptoms or during routine follow-up assessments. At this stage, patients and their families are confronted with “bad news” again, which may result in loss of hope, significant emotional burden, fears of imminent death, confusion about goals of care, emotional distress, blame toward the healthcare system, and difficulty participating in decision-making [39, 47, 48, 100–102]. For example, 4 of 12 patients with recurrent cancer were not ready to initiate end-of-life care discussions [102], and 6 of 30 patients with refractory malignancies demonstrated limited understanding of precision cancer medicine [103].

Despite advances in cancer treatment, physicians often feel uncertain when discussing prognosis at recurrence, as outcomes are frequently unpredictable. The shift in prognostic expectations from curative to incurable intent can trigger diverse emotional and psychological responses from patients [47, 48, 101, 103–105]. The recurrent stage represents an important opportunity to initiate discussions about end-of-life care [106]. However, many patients decline or postpone these conversations, leading to delays in end-of-life planning [102]. The underlying reasons for reluctance to engage in end-of-life discussions are addressed in the subsequent end-of-life stage.

End-of-life stage

The end-of-life stage refers to patients who are approaching the end of life and have discontinued treatments intended to cure or control their disease [107]. End-of-life care may be provided during the hours, days, or months preceding death and includes support for patients’ physical comfort, psychological and emotional well-being, spiritual needs, and practical concerns [108, 109]. In a survey of 74 patients with advanced cancer, more than 80% reported communication-related concerns during end-of-life discussions, including loss of hope, lack of emotional support, and insufficient listening from physicians [110]. Consequently, these findings highlight the importance of effective end-of-life communication in cancer care planning.

In practice, end-of-life discussions are frequently postponed or omitted among patients, family members, and healthcare providers when the patient’s condition becomes more severe, due to multiple barriers [111–115]. From the patient perspective, fear of death, emotional distress, and the perception that such discussions signify imminent death contribute to avoidance or refusal of end-of-life conversations. Patients may also perceive these discussions as threatening their hope, leading to denial or disengagement [116–119].

From the family perspective, relatives may struggle to accept poor prognosis or the possibility of death and may seek to protect patients from distressing or incomplete information. Disagreements among family members regarding whether and when to engage in end-of-life discussions further complicate communication [120–123].

From the healthcare provider perspective, physicians reported fear of negative patient reactions and concern that patients may be unable to cope with end-of-life information. Some physicians perceived end-of-life discussions as easier to initiate at very late stages of illness, when disease progression is more apparent and acceptance by patients and families is greater. In addition, physicians also described emotional difficulty in initiating these conversations, particularly when long-term therapeutic relationships had been established, and expressed concern that end-of-life discussions might diminish patients’ and families’ hope [121, 122, 124–127].

During end-of-life communication, patients and their families may experience a wide range of intense and often paradoxical emotions. These include emotional pain, shock, fear of death, anxiety, and disappointment [117, 128–130]. Healthcare providers must be prepared to recognize these emotional responses and adapt their communication with empathy to help patients and families cope with emotional burden. End-of-life conversations commonly encompass diverse topics, including goals of care, symptom management, fear of death, prognosis, treatment decisions, and existential concerns.

Changing goals of care from disease-directed treatment to end-of-life care is one of the most challenging aspects of communication for healthcare providers. The term “palliative care” may be frightening for patients, as it is often perceived as indicating imminent death or a lack of further treatment options, which can threaten patients’ sense of hope [50, 121]. Physicians therefore face the difficult task of balancing hope with realism. While palliative care primarily focuses on symptom management, quality of life, and support alongside disease-directed treatment, hospice care represents end-of-life care that emphasizes comfort-oriented management and quality of life rather than curative treatment [131]. Differences in goals of care and conflicting expectations among patients, family members, and healthcare providers can hinder the establishment of a consistent care plan. Exploring patients’ and families’ goals of care can help healthcare providers better understand their expectations. In addition, cognitive decline and physiological deterioration may impair patients’ ability to participate in shared decision-making. Physicians may also struggle to provide clear guidance due to uncertainty or incomplete information related to goals of care [39, 48, 112, 116, 117, 120, 121, 126, 127, 132–141].

Patients at the end-of-life stage often experience significant physical and psychological symptom burden, including pain, depression, dyspnea, and emotional distress. Many patients fear that suffering and pain will worsen as death approaches. Although patients often wish to manage symptoms at home, they face practical barriers such as limited caregiver support, restricted access to medications, and inadequate medical equipment [122, 130, 137, 142–144].

Beyond symptom burden, thoughts about death itself constitute a significant emotional challenge. Patients may wish to discuss their fear of death with healthcare providers but often do not know how to initiate such conversations [33, 100, 123, 145].

Alongside symptom management, patients frequently seek information about prognosis. However, family members may attempt to prevent prognostic discussions out of concern that such information could increase patient distress. In some cases, unclear professional roles or disagreement between nurses and physicians, or among different physicians, regarding who should discuss prognosis may further delay these conversations [112, 114, 133, 140, 146–150].

Even when patients understand their prognosis, some continue to explore non-standard treatment options, such as complementary and alternative medicine (CAM). Patients may ask about the potential benefits, risks, and adverse effects of CAM, while physicians often report limited knowledge or confidence in discussing these therapies [112, 151].

Healthcare providers also reported difficulty when patients raise religious or spiritual issues during end-of-life discussions. Physicians may avoid these topics due to lack of confidence, concern about addressing sensitive beliefs, or differences in religious perspectives between providers and patients [152].

End-of-life communication is inherently challenging for healthcare providers due to its emotional burden and the complexity of caring for patients approaching death. Enhancing effective communication skills is therefore essential to support high-quality end-of-life care. Several communication shortcomings were identified among physicians and nurses during end-of-life discussions. These included the use of overly general or impersonal language; lack of compassion, honesty, and empathy; insufficient active listening; failure to recognize patients as individuals; inadequate assessment of patients’ understanding; limited use of open-ended questions; lack of experience; statements suggesting that there was “nothing more to do”; and insufficient time devoted to patients and their families [110, 112, 114, 121, 122, 153–156].

Discussion

In cancer care, communication barriers vary across distinct milestones of the cancer journey. Both patients and healthcare providers must recognize and address these barriers to achieve effective communication and improve clinical outcomes.

During the screening stage, patients may be unwilling to discuss or undergo cancer screening, which can delay early cancer detection. Such delays often result in diagnosis at more advanced stages, leading to reduced survival and poorer treatment outcomes [157]. Healthcare providers should proactively initiate discussions about cancer screening with individuals who are eligible based on epidemiological risk, biological characteristics, medical history, and family history, clearly explaining the potential benefits, importance of screening, and limitations of screening tests, including cost, time burden, discomfort, and the risk of overdiagnosis. Balanced discussion of both the benefits and limitations of screening supports informed and shared decision-making regarding cancer screening. When patients decline or postpone screening, clinicians should explore the underlying reasons for refusal to identify specific communication barriers and develop tailored strategies to address them. Providing clear and accessible information regarding costs, screening procedures, possible discomfort, and recommended screening intervals may further support patient understanding and acceptance of screening tests [158].

At the diagnostic stage, breaking bad news and addressing its consequences represent a significant emotional burden for patients, their families, and healthcare providers. Several structured protocols such as SPIKES [159], ABCDE [160], BREAKS [161], SUNBURN [162], and Kaye’s 10-step model [163] provide a structured framework for the delivery of sensitive information. Among these frameworks, management of patients’ emotional responses is a critical component, as inadequate emotional support may lead to serious adverse consequences.

Poorly managed emotional distress may result in patients denying or rejecting the diagnosis, impairing their ability to understand medical information and participate in shared decision-making regarding future treatment plans. In severe cases, overwhelming emotional burden can erode patients’ sense of hope and may increase the risk of self-harm or suicide [164]. Compared with the general population, individuals with cancer are reported to have rates of depression up to two to three times higher [165], and suicide risk up to four times higher [166], and an estimated prevalence of anxiety disorders of around 10%, compared with approximately 6% in the general population [167]. Additionally, unresolved distress at diagnosis may have long-term negative psychological effects throughout the cancer trajectory. To effectively manage patients’ emotional reactions during the disclosure of bad news, several key strategies are recommended. These include preparing a quiet, private, and supportive environment; allocating sufficient time for the discussion; providing information gradually with clear warning cues; facilitating the ventilation of emotions; responding empathetically to these emotions; and reassuring patients that such reactions are common and that healthcare providers will continue to support them throughout the cancer journey [168].

During the treatment stage, shared decision-making is a major component of patient-centered communication, in which patients and clinicians collaborate to address clinical problems and develop appropriate treatment plans [169]. Successful shared decision-making requires several important elements. First, patients need to understand essential information regarding treatment benefits, adverse effects, prognosis, and treatment procedures while concurrently navigating the emotional burden of their diagnosis. Second, healthcare providers should communicate using clear and understandable language, actively explore patients’ preferences and concerns, and provide opportunities for bidirectional discussion and follow-up questions.

In the follow-up stage, patients often receive less attention from healthcare providers despite continuing to experience various difficulties, particularly long-term treatment-related adverse effects. However, these issues may receive insufficient attention for several reasons, including lack of physician-initiated communication and patients’ perceptions. Physicians and nurses should proactively inquire about and carefully monitor patients for adverse effects, providing timely interventions to help patients manage these symptoms. Effective adverse-effect management is associated with improved quality of life among cancer patients [170].

At the recurrence and end-of-life stages, end-of-life communication is a critical component of advance care planning and should not be postponed. These conversations enable healthcare providers to understand patients’ and families’ expectations while clarifying goals of care within the context of a shifting clinical prognosis. Discussions typically include prognosis, symptom management, patients’ preferences and values, and emotional concerns, all of which contribute to maintaining quality of life. Importantly, end-of-life communication serves as a preparatory process that supports patients and families in decision-making during acute or critical moments later in the disease trajectory [7, 171].

Collectively, these findings highlight the need for milestone-specific communication training and institutional support to facilitate the integration of effective communication practices into routine oncology care.

Conclusions

Given the complexity of the cancer journey and the substantial emotional burden experienced by patients and healthcare professionals, communication in cancer care is frequently challenged by multiple barriers that negatively affect clinical outcomes. Early identification and understanding of key communication barriers, along with their underlying causes at each milestone of the cancer journey, are essential for improving the quality of cancer care and patient-centered outcomes.
